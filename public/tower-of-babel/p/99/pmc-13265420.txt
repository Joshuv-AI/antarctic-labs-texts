T1 mapping in repaired tetralogy of Fallot: A marker of fibrosis or a mirror of hemodynamics?
Authors: Jef Van den Eynde, Alexander Van De Bruaene, Jan Bogaert
Published: 2026

The long-term course of patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot (rTOF) is increasingly defined not by residual lesions alone, but by the complex interplay between ventricular remodeling, hemodynamic burden, and extracardiac organ involvement [1]. Among the mechanisms proposed to drive adverse outcomes, myocardial fibrosis received particular attention [2]. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR), with its ability to non-invasively characterize tissue composition, has therefore become central to contemporary assessment of this population [3].

Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) has convincingly demonstrated the presence and prognostic relevance of focal replacement fibrosis in rTOF [4]. More recently, parametric mapping techniques—particularly native T1 and extracellular volume (ECV)—have been proposed as surrogate markers of diffuse interstitial fibrosis [5]. While these techniques provide important prognostic information, their biological interpretation remains complex and highly context dependent.

In this issue of the Journal of Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance, Ishikita et al. [6] report an elegant study exploring myocardial and hepatic T1 and ECV in a cohort of patients with rTOF. Their findings are clinically relevant and methodologically robust, but also raise important questions regarding the interpretation of parametric mapping as a direct measure of fibrosis.

Summary and strengths of the study

The authors studied 181 patients with rTOF, including a nested comparison of 40 patients with 20 matched controls. Both myocardial and hepatic T1 and ECV were elevated in rTOF. Right ventricular (RV) native T1—but not hepatic T1—was independently associated with adverse outcomes. These findings led the authors to conclude that diffuse myocardial fibrosis is a key determinant of outcome in rTOF, whereas hepatic fibrosis, although present, is not prognostically relevant. Correlations between myocardial and hepatic T1/ECV were interpreted as evidence for a systemic fibrotic process.

This study has several notable strengths. It includes a large, well-characterized cohort with outcome data, and the combination of myocardial and hepatic tissue characterization is innovative. The focus on RV tissue properties is particularly relevant given its central role in rTOF. Moreover, the findings are consistent with prior work linking myocardial T1/ECV with arrhythmias and adverse outcomes [2], [4], [7], [8], [9], reinforcing the clinical value of parametric mapping.

T1 and ECV: markers of fibrosis – or of something else?

Despite these strengths, the central interpretation of the study—that elevated T1 and ECV reflect fibrosis—merits further investigation.

It is well established that native T1 and ECV are influenced by extracellular matrix expansion, and thus correlate with collagen content in controlled settings [10]. However, they are not specific for fibrosis. Rather, they reflect the total extracellular space, which is influenced by multiple processes, including changes in total blood volume, edema, cellular hypertrophy, inflammation, and steatosis [11]. For example, myocardial T1 increases within several minutes after coronary occlusion—well before any fibrotic remodeling can occur—and this rapid rise, accompanied by parallel increases in T2, is most consistent with acute edema rather than fibrosis [12].

This lack of specificity is particularly relevant in rTOF, where loading conditions are abnormal and variable. Changes in volume load, filling pressures, and venous congestion are expected to directly influence hepatic and myocardial water content and extracellular volume, and therefore T1 and ECV, independent of fibrosis. Evidence from prior rTOF studies supports this interpretation in the myocardium. Chen et al. [9] demonstrated higher myocardial ECV in volume-loaded compared with pressure-loaded RVs, indicating sensitivity to the type of hemodynamic burden. Cochet et al. [2] reported lower myocardial T1 in patients who had undergone pulmonary valve replacement compared with those who had not, despite comparable LGE-defined scar burden, suggesting that diffuse T1 abnormalities are at least partly driven by loading conditions and may at least in part reflect reversible changes in interstitial water content, rather than fixed collagen deposition alone.

An even more clear pattern is observed in the liver. In the present study, hepatic T1 was higher in patients with moderate or severe tricuspid regurgitation compared to those with no or mild tricuspid regurgitation (623 msec [542–686] versus 570 msec [533–630], p=0.046), a finding that has also been observed in other populations with tricuspid regurgitation [13]. This parallel between myocardial and hepatic findings supports the notion that T1 might behave as a systemic marker of congestion-related changes.

Thus, while elevated T1/ECV may be consistent with fibrosis, they cannot be equated with fibrosis without corroborating evidence.

An illustrative case: reversibility of hepatic T1

The importance of hemodynamic context is illustrated by a case from our institution. A 68-year-old woman with rTOF presented with progressive dyspnea (New York Heart Association functional class III) and peripheral edema requiring escalation of diuretic therapy. CMR demonstrated severe RV dilation (RV end-diastolic volume 218 mL/m²), pulmonary homograft dysfunction (moderate pulmonary regurgitation and mild-to-moderate pulmonary stenosis with peak-to-peak gradient 16 mmHg on catheterization), and severe tricuspid regurgitation (regurgitant volume 96 mL, regurgitant fraction 82%). Hepatic T1 was elevated (799 ms), accompanied by a dilated inferior vena cava (IVC; 27.5 mm) with markedly reduced respirophasic variation (12%) (Fig. 1). T2 values were also elevated (60 ms), consistent with increased tissue water content. Right heart catheterization confirmed elevated filling pressures, with a right atrial pressure of 18 mmHg, mean pulmonary artery pressure of 37 mmHg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 27 mmHg. After multidisciplinary meeting, the patient underwent pulmonary homograft replacement and tricuspid valve repair, in conjunction with optimization of medical therapy. At 1-year follow-up, CMR demonstrated marked reductions in hepatic T1 and T2 (450 ms and 44 ms, respectively), alongside normalization of IVC size (14.5 mm) and respirophasic variation (49%) (Fig. 1).

Reversibility of hepatic T1 and T2 following hemodynamic correction in repaired tetralogy of Fallot. Representative images from a 68-year-old woman with repaired tetralogy of Fallot before (left) and 1 year after pulmonary homograft replacement and tricuspid valve repair (right). Pre-intervention imaging demonstrates elevated hepatic native T1 and T2 values, consistent with increased tissue water content, alongside a dilated inferior vena cava (IVC) with minimal respirophasic variation between expiration and inspiration. Following intervention, hepatic T1 and T2 values are markedly reduced, accompanied by normalization of IVC diameter and restoration of respirophasic variation. These findings illustrate the dynamic and reversible nature of parametric mapping abnormalities in the setting of right-sided volume overload and venous congestion

Such changes are difficult to reconcile with regression of established fibrosis, which is typically slow and incomplete [14], [15]. Instead, the rapid normalization of both T1 and T2 strongly supports a dominant contribution of congestion and tissue water content to the observed signal. This interpretation is consistent with prior work demonstrating elevated hepatic T1 in conditions characterized by right-sided heart failure—such as pulmonary hypertension, constrictive pericarditis, and right ventricular cardiomyopathy—where these abnormalities were closely associated with increased T2 and elevated right atrial pressures, supporting a congestive origin [16], [17], [18]. In this context, T2 mapping provides complimentary information on tissue water content, and a multiparametric approach integrating T1, T2, LGE along with clinical, functional, and hemodynamic data is essential to avoid overestimation of fibrotic burden and oversimplification of the underlying pathophysiology.

Conclusion

Parametric mapping has transformed our ability to non-invasively assess myocardial and hepatic tissue characteristics in rTOF. The study by Ishikita et al. [6] provides valuable insights and reinforces the prognostic relevance of these techniques. However, elevated T1 and ECV should not be interpreted as synonymous with fibrosis. Rather, they reflect a composite signal influenced by fibrosis, edema, congestion, and other factors. In a condition such as rTOF—where hemodynamic perturbations are central—this distinction is particularly important. The challenge moving forward is not to abandon parametric mapping, but to interpret it within its full biological and clinical context. Only then can we fully harness its potential to guide diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapy.
