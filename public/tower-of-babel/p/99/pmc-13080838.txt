Patent Foramen Ovale–Induced Hypoxemia Unmasked During Pregnancy
Authors: Amit Regev, Stephanie Golob, Bhavya Varma, Jodi Feinberg, Adam J Small, Michael Argilla, Sunil Saharan, Mina Owlia, Ashley Roman, Dan G Halpern
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Patent foramen ovale (PFO), a remnant of fetal circulation, may persist into adulthood when fails to close postnatally. While often benign, PFO may become clinically significant and symptomatic when exacerbated by various physiological alterations. Pregnancy leads to various hemodynamic changes, which can exacerbate an existing PFO into a right-to-left shunt, leading to aberrant blood flow and systemic hypoxia. This case report highlights a novel indication and timing for PFO closure during pregnancy.

Take-Home Messages

•Hypoxemia in pregnancy should always be considered abnormal and should prompt an elaborate investigation exploring cardiac, pulmonary, and hematological etiologies.

Case Presentation

A healthy 33-year-old female yoga instructor presented with significant dyspnea on exertion during her first trimester of pregnancy. She first noticed exertional dyspnea at 6 weeks of gestation. At 11 weeks of gestation, she presented to an outside hospital with exertional dyspnea and hypoxia. She presented to an adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) clinic at 12 weeks of gestation, where her vital signs were notable for baseline 99% oxygen saturation, with a drop to 85% oxygen saturation with walking around the clinic. Cardiac examination displayed regular rate and rhythm, and normal S1 and S2 without murmurs. Pulmonary examination revealed clear lungs without any crackles or wheezing. There was a suggestion of early clubbing. Abdominal examination was notable for gravid uterus.

The patient had a history of mild asthma with normal recent pulmonary function tests without any recent exacerbation and intermittent migraines. Her hemoglobin was 12.7 g/dL. Electrocardiogram displayed sinus tachycardia, normal axis, deep S in lead I, Q waves in leads III and AVF, and T-wave inversion in lead III. Echocardiogram displayed an interatrial septal aneurysm, normal biventricular systolic function, no significant valvular disease, no right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, and no pulmonary hypertension. Chest computed tomography angiography ruled out pulmonary embolism, pulmonary arteriovenous malformation, and parenchymal lung disease. Exercise stress echocardiogram including an agitated saline contrast injection at peak exercise displayed saline contrast crossing the interatrial septum within the first 3 cardiac cycles, yet only with exercise (Video 1). There was no obvious evidence of exercise-induced pulmonary hypertension. Further work-up with a transesophageal echocardiogram confirmed the findings of a PFO within an interatrial septal aneurysm, with right-to-left shunting.

Given the patient's active symptoms as well as risks to both fetus and mother of chronic hypoxemia during pregnancy and the increased risk of stroke in the setting of the hypercoagulable state of pregnancy, a multidisciplinary team including ACHD cardiologists, congenital interventional cardiology, and maternal fetal medicine obstetricians recommended PFO device closure. Procedural timing was planned at 18 weeks of gestation to avoid fetal radiation exposure during organogenesis in the first trimester, and extreme care was taken to minimize radiation exposure, limiting the fluoroscopy pulse rate when used while relying as much as possible on TEE imaging. At no time was the patient's abdomen or pelvis placed within the direct path of ionizing radiation. Pressure and oximetry data acquisition was minimized, both to decrease the radiation exposure as well as to minimize the duration of time under anesthesia.

Pulmonary vascular resistance was measured as normal. The PFO was occluded using a 25-mm Amplatzer cribriform septal occluder device (Abbott), with appropriate fetal shielding. The Qp/Qs before and after the procedure was 1:1. After the procedure, oxygen saturations improved and symptoms resolved. The patient was placed on aspirin and low-dose enoxaparin for the duration of the pregnancy and early postpartum. She did not have further desaturations for the remaining duration.

The patient delivered a healthy baby boy (3,120 g) via induced vaginal delivery at 39 weeks of gestation, without evidence of oxygen desaturation during delivery. Enoxaparin was discontinued 6 weeks postpregnancy, and aspirin was discontinued after 6 months. The patient continues to follow up routinely in the ACHD clinic, where she is asymptomatic and active, with normal oxygenation during rest and exercise.

Discussion

Normal physiologic changes during pregnancy, such as increased plasma volume and cardiac output together with decreased vascular tone, can unmask cardiac hemodynamic lesions.1 In this case, a previously unknown PFO produced symptomatic right-to-left shunting during pregnancy without evidence of pulmonary hypertension. PFO, an interatrial flap-like valve, remnant from embryonic life responsible to flow through the atria bypassing the lungs, is found with some degree of patency in 25% to 30% of the population and has been associated with cryptogenic stroke, paradoxical emboli, migraines with aura, and platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome (POS). Although its mechanism is elusive, classic POS refers to oxygen desaturation in an upright posture and improved by recumbency, which in some cases is the result of altered blood streaming into the right atrium targeting the fossa ovalis and opening the PFO. Examples of altered streaming include congenital or acquired anatomic distortions such as pericardial effusion or constriction, postpneumonectomy syndrome, giant aortas, and chest deformities.2,3 Nonstreaming causes of POS mostly refer to pulmonary shunt (eg pulmonary arteriovenous malformations, more commonly with cirrhosis) and other lung pathologies.2,3 Our patient did not exhibit classic POS, as the hypoxemia was triggered by exercise. Given no pulmonary hypertension or elevated right atrial pressures, we posit that the hemodynamic changes of pregnancy produced a unique streaming effect, which has similarities to that of the POS.

Pregnancy is a situation in which a person undergoes acute and substantial anatomic/hemodynamic changes in a short period, including 40% increase in plasma volume and pulmonary and systemic vascular resistance drop, in addition to pulmonary changes such as increase in minute ventilation and decrease in total lung capacity. Uterine enlargement along gestation further compresses the inferior vena cava, potentially reducing venous return and possibly distorting abdominal and chest anatomy. Previous reports of PFO-related complications during pregnancy have primarily focused on paradoxical emboli causing strokes.4,5 To our knowledge, this is the first description of a PFO-related shunt becoming symptomatic during pregnancy and for which the patient had an intervention.

The differential diagnosis of shortness of breath during pregnancy is challenging, as the normal physiological changes of pregnancy may result in dyspnea. This shortness of breath is in part induced by high levels of progesterone, as well as hypervolemia, relative anemia, hyperdynamic flow state, and increase in minute ventilation and tidal volumes.1 However, oxygen desaturations should always be regarded as pathologic. Hypoxemia can be the result of pulmonary (eg, pulmonary embolism, pulmonary arterial hypertension, parenchymal lung disease), cardiac (complex congenital heart disease, low cardiac output), hematological disorders, and erroneous measurement of oxygen saturation. Chronic cyanosis in pregnancy has been associated with low birth weights and decrease in number of live births below oxygen saturation of 90%.6 Drenthen et al7 reported a live birth rate of 12% when maternal oxygen saturation was ≤85%.

Current society guidelines for PFO closure are based on prospective trials showing benefit of closure after a stroke, as well as a conditional recommendation for PFO closure in the setting of POS, acknowledging low certainty of evidence.8 Our case displays both a unique indication for PFO closure (large right-to-left shunt during pregnancy producing cyanosis and increasing stroke risk) and a previously unreported timing for PFO closure (pregnancy, second trimester).

Conclusions

Pregnancy may unmask or promote acute hypoxemia due to right-to-left shunting through a PFO in the setting of profound hemodynamic changes, which may be safely treated with percutaneous device closure once pulmonary hypertension is refuted to resolve hypoxemia-related symptoms and avert risks of hypoxia to the developing fetus.
