Association between higher cerebrospinal fluid levels of phosphorylated tau and reduced depressive symptoms in patients with cognitive decline
Authors: Takashi Ozaki, Naoki Hashimoto, Hisashi Narita, Niki Udo, Shizuka Mito, Shin Nakagawa, Ichiro Kusumi, Takahiro A. Kato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimIn individuals with cognitive decline, depressive symptoms can affect disease progression and quality of life. One factor that may be associated with depressive symptoms is phosphorylated tau (p‐tau). However, the association between p‐tau and depressive symptoms remains controversial. The present study examined the relationship between p‐tau levels and depressive symptoms not only in terms of total scale scores of the 15‐item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS‐15) but also at the item level.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional survey of patients (n = 88) with mild cognitive impairment due to Alzheimer's disease (AD) or with AD dementia (ADD), who were strictly diagnosed by a board of psychiatrists and neurologists from Hokkaido University Hospital. All patients underwent lumbar puncture to obtain cerebrospinal fluid for the p‐tau assessment. We first investigated the association between p‐tau level and GDS‐15 total score. Next, we compared the p‐tau levels for each item using the GDS‐15 with binary responses “none (0)” or “present (1).”ResultsMultivariable regression analysis revealed that higher levels of p‐tau were significantly associated with lower GDS‐15 total scores (standardized coefficient [95% confidence interval] = −0.28, [−0.48, −0.09]). Furthermore, patients with higher p‐tau levels were less likely to have symptoms of GDS‐15 Items 2 (Dropped interests) (p < 0.001) and 14 (Hopeless) (p = 0.0030).ConclusionOur findings suggest an association between p‐tau accumulation and reduced depressive symptoms. However, future longitudinal studies are required to confirm whether p‐tau may be a predictive factor for depressive symptoms.

INTRODUCTION

Individuals living with cognitive decline are likely to experience depressive symptoms as a result of various biopsychosocial factors. 1 , 2 , 3 Depressive symptoms are considered common in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) through to the early stages of Alzheimer's disease dementia (ADD). 4 The prevalence of depressive symptoms is estimated to be between 20% and 60% among individuals with MCI 5 and ADD. 4 , 6 Depressive symptoms in individuals with cognitive decline can also affect their later life and are associated with subsequent cognitive decline, increased caregiving burden, and decreased activities of daily living (ADL). 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 In addition, such symptoms may be associated with poor interpersonal interactions and decreased social activities. 11

Many factors are associated with depressive symptoms in individuals with cognitive decline. 12 One of the most strongly associated factors is considered to be amyloid‐β. 13 , 14 , 15 Other biomarkers, such as phosphorylated tau (p‐tau), may also play a vital role. 16 , 17 Some previous studies have suggested that the prevalence of depressive symptoms is positively associated with p‐tau accumulation, whereas others have reported no such relationship. 13 , 14 , 15 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Conversely, we have previously demonstrated a negative association between the prevalence of depressive symptoms and p‐tau accumulation in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) 25 ; this finding was supported by a few similar studies. 26 , 27 In this previous study, we used the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q) to assess outcomes. Although the NPI‐Q is considered to be the best assessment for profiling the behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia, a more detailed evaluation is essential to fully understand the nature of depressive symptoms in individuals with cognitive decline. The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) is one of the most common scales worldwide for evaluating depressive symptoms, and has several unique characteristics that distinguish it from other similar scales. Specifically, the GDS does not contain somatic items that may be less valid in older adults, thus retaining its validity and reliability even when it is compared with the criteria for depression as per the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th edition. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 We therefore decided to use the GDS for the present study.

Herein, we examined the associations between CSF p‐tau levels and depressive symptoms (evaluated using the GDS) in patients admitted to our hospital for a medical checkup related to cognitive impairment. We also examined the relationship between p‐tau levels and GDS not only in terms of total scores but also at the GDS item level, to explore the patterns of associations between p‐tau and depressive symptoms in more detail. We hypothesized that higher p‐tau levels would be associated with lower GDS scores or prevalence and with only a few items of the GDS scores.

METHODS

Participants

Patients were recruited consecutively from referrals to Hokkaido University Hospital (Sapporo city, Hokkaido, Japan) for a medical checkup of cognitive impairments between 2012 and 2025. Inclusion criteria were as follows: aged 55–95 years, a new referral for the evaluation of cognitive complaints, and a diagnosis of (1) MCI due to AD or (2) ADD in accordance with the National Institute on Aging‐Alzheimer's Association 2011 criteria. 32 All patients were evaluated using the Clinical Dementia Rating scale (CDR), 33 and patients with CDR = 0.5, 1, or 2 were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who were diagnosed with intellectual disabilities, schizophrenia spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, drug abuse, solvent exposure, anoxic brain damage, traumatic head injuries, epilepsy, or other severe physical diseases that may influence cognition; patients with severe visual or hearing loss; patients who fulfilled any criteria for dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, or vascular dementia; patients who had previously received or were receiving anti‐amyloid antibody treatment; patients who fulfilled any of the clinical criteria for primary tauopathies such as Pick's disease, progressive supranuclear palsy, or corticobasal degeneration; and patients with a Hachinski ischemic score (indicator of vascular damage) ≥ 4. Trained neurologists participated in all diagnoses. The diagnosis of the underlying cause of dementia was made by trained psychiatrists (usually by two or more clinicians with >5 years of clinical experience) collaborating with a trained neurologist specializing in dementia in our hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Hokkaido University Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient or their family before enrollment in the study.

Sociodemographic data

Sociodemographic characteristics including age, sex, education status, place of residence, marital status (married, widowed, divorced, or never married), family structure (living alone or living with families), current employment, and comorbidities (hypertension, heart diseases, stroke, diabetes mellitus, or malignant tumor) were determined by interview. These demographic factors have either been reported as associated with depressive symptoms among older adults in a large cohort in Japan or are potentially modifiable risk factors for dementia. 34 , 35

Clinical assessment

We assessed depressive symptoms using the Japanese short version of the GDS (score range: 0–15) developed for self‐administrative surveys, which is one of the most commonly used scales in Japan. 29 , 30 , 31 The GDS was originally developed with 30 items that have binary responses of either “yes” or “no” for each item. 36 Its shorter version, with only 15 items (GDS‐15), was developed later to reduce the time required for GDS administration and avoid respondent fatigue, 37 thereby retaining its validity and reliability. 30

Patients were requested to respond to each item of the GDS‐15. For 10 of the items, a response of “yes” suggests the presence of depressive symptoms, coded as “1” point. The remaining five items are reverse items, and a response of “no” represents the presence of depressive symptoms, also coded as “1” point. We classified patients as “high GDS‐15” and “low GDS‐15” using a cutoff value of 5, according to previous literature from Japan. 34

Patients were also assessed using the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) 38 for cognitive function and the CDR 33 for dementia severity. Instrumental ADL (IADL) of patients were assessed using the Lawton IADL scale. 39 The Lawton IADL scale is widely used to assess ADL in clinical practice, and total scores have a possible range of 0–8.

We also checked whether patients were using any anticholinergic, antidepressant, antipsychotic, anxiolytic, and/or hypnotic drugs. We categorized anxiolytic and hypnotic drugs into one group as “benzodiazepines and its derivatives” because of the small sample size.

Biomarker assessment

CSF samples were obtained by lumbar puncture into the L3–L4 or L4–L5 intervertebral spaces with a 23‐gauge needle. A total of 12–15 mL of CSF was obtained from each participant in the morning (no fasting required) and placed into polypropylene tubes. The concentration of 181 p‐tau in CSF was measured in duplicate using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (Phinoscholar assay; Nipro Pharma Corporation, Osaka, Japan) and a plate reader (Infinite F50R; Tecan, Kawasaki, Japan), as described elsewhere. 25

Statistical analysis

For the demographic data, marital status was transformed into dichotomous variables as “married” or “not married,” similar to a previous report. 34 Comorbidities were also transformed into dichotomous variables as “any comorbidities” or “no comorbidities,” similar to a previous report. 40

To investigate the association between p‐tau and GDS‐15, we performed univariable regression analysis to identify the demographic factors associated with GDS‐15. Consequently, multivariable regression analysis was performed using the factors that were significantly different in the univariable analysis. As covariates, sex, age, education status, and MMSE were included in the multiple regression analysis because they were considered to be fundamental factors to describe depressive symptoms in this sample, similar to previous studies. 25 Because the sample size was relatively small, we performed bootstrapping multivariable linear regression to confirm the results. In the bootstrapping method, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to determine significance because this allowed us to evaluate the stability of the regression coefficients. When the 95% CI of a regression coefficient did not contain zero, we considered the variable to be significant. The number of bootstrap samples was set as 1000 to obtain a sufficiently accurate 95% bootstrap percentile. These analyses have been discussed elsewhere. 41

We then divided the participants into groups coded “0” and “1” points for each GDS‐15 item and compared the p‐tau levels of each group using Welch's t‐test. By examining each GDS‐15 item, we aimed to identify which depressive symptoms are significantly associated with p‐tau levels.

Multiple comparison tests were performed using the Bonferroni method, if necessary. To avoid multicollinearity, low correlations (i.e., correlations < 0.40) between the explanatory variables were included in the multiple logistic regression models by calculating Pearson's correlations between numeric variables, polyserial correlations between numeric and ordinal variables, and polychoric correlations between ordinal variables. Statistical differences were determined at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using statistical software 42 (R version 4.5.0; Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), along with the “boot” package (version 1.3‐31) for resampling procedures.

RESULTS

Demographic data and clinical characteristics of patients

Data were obtained from 88 patients with MCI due to AD (n = 42) or ADD (n = 46) (Table 1). The mean age of patients was 74.2 ± 9.3 years, 64.8% were women, and the majority were living at home; these findings resemble the characteristics of a previous study. 25 Regarding social factors, most patients were married (64.8%) and living with families (83.0%), which is similar to the findings from a previous epidemiological study in Japan. 34 Regarding clinical assessments, most patients had CDR scores of 0.5–1, suggesting MCI due to AD or mild ADD. However, 36% of patients had a GDS‐15 total score ≥ 5 (high GDS‐15), which is relatively high. Levels of p‐tau were similar to those in our previous study, 25 and the use of psychotropic drugs resembled that of a previous epidemiological study among individuals with dementia in Hokkaido, Japan. 43

Association between p‐tau and GDS‐15 total score examined by univariable linear regression and bootstrapping multivariable linear regression

In the univariable analysis, three factors were significantly associated with GDS‐15 total score: living alone, the use of antipsychotic drugs, and p‐tau level (Table 2). We therefore performed multivariable regression analysis using these three factors in addition to fundamental covariates such as sex, age, education status, and MMSE score. In this analysis, an increase in GDS‐15 total score was significantly associated with decreased p‐tau levels (standardized coefficient [95% CI] −0.28, [−0.48, −0.09]) and living alone (standardized coefficient [95% CI] = 0.23, [0.01, 0.45]).

Comparisons of p‐tau levels for each of the 15 individual items on the GDS‐15

The frequency of responses to each GDS‐15 item is shown in Table 3. The most common symptoms were GDS‐15 Items 13 (Energy), 10 (Memory), and 2 (Dropped interests). We compared the mean p‐tau levels between patients with and without each symptom item on the GDS‐15. For all items, patients who reported the symptom appeared to have lower mean p‐tau levels than those who did not. Welch's t‐tests revealed that the differences were significant for several items. Specifically, there were significant associations between higher p‐tau levels and the absence of GDS‐15 Items 2 (Dropped interests) (p < 0.001) and 14 (Hopeless) (p = 0.0030). We also observed a trend toward negative associations between p‐tau levels and GDS‐15 Items 3 (Empty) (p = 0.0065) and 8 (Helpless) (p = 0.0053).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we demonstrated an association between p‐tau and GDS‐15 scores among patients with MCI due to AD or with ADD. Accumulation of p‐tau was associated with a decrease in both GDS‐15 total score and the prevalence of specific GDS‐15 items. Although previous studies investigating the association between p‐tau and depressive symptoms have provided inconsistent evidence, 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 44 , 45 similar findings have primarily been reported by a US research group using Brazilian data and from Germany, 16 , 26 suggesting that the results of the present study may contribute to a more globally representative understanding of this association.

Here, we demonstrated that p‐tau accumulation was associated with a lower GDS‐15 total score. We have previously reported an association between higher levels of CSF p‐tau and reduced depressive symptoms in patients with cognitive decline. 25 The results of the present study therefore confirm and strengthen the findings of our previous investigation. At present, depressive symptoms among patients with cognitive decline are commonly evaluated using scales such as the GDS, 19 , 20 NPI‐Q, 18 , 45 and Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia. 13 , 15 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 44 However, most studies investigating the association between tau pathology and depressive symptoms have suggested positive correlations 18 , 19 or no associations, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 45 and only very few studies have reported negative associations, 25 , 26 , 27 meaning that the evidence is inconsistent. One review noted that these inconsistent results are likely caused by differences in study design as well as sample heterogeneity. 46 Notably, the results of a postmortem brain study suggest that specific stages of tau accumulation may be related to an increase or decrease in depressive symptoms, 16 thus partially supporting the findings of the present study. In addition, other than p‐tau accumulation, we identified that living alone and the use of antipsychotic drugs were associated with increased GDS‐15 scores. Note that, because antipsychotic drugs are often used as an adjunctive therapy to antidepressant drugs, the use of antipsychotic drugs may reflect the severity of depressive symptoms. 47 Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, 47 , 48 , 49 suggesting that our results are reasonable.

In the present study, p‐tau was significantly associated with GDS‐15 Items 2 (Dropped interests) and 14 (Hopeless), and had a trend toward associations with GDS‐15 Items 3 (Empty) and 8 (Helpless). Although the factor structure of GDS‐15 is relatively inconsistent, previous studies have demonstrated that the characteristics of GDS‐15 Items 2 (Dropped interests), 9 (Stay Home), and 13 (Energy) can be loaded onto a distinct factor that assesses “apathy” (the GDS‐3A subscale). 50 Other factors such as GDS‐15 Items 3 (Empty), 8 (Helpless), and 14 (Hopeless) may be loaded onto one factor that assesses “general depressive affect.” 51 In the present study, p‐tau accumulation may therefore be associated with diverse clinical features of depressive symptoms.

Caution is warranted regarding the potential explanations for why p‐tau accumulation was associated with decreased depressive symptoms in the current study. It is generally assumed that the accumulation of amyloid‐β is associated with the increase of depressive symptoms. 13 , 14 , 15 Therefore, a similar relationship might be expected between p‐tau and depressive symptoms. However, findings from our study and previous studies suggest the association between p‐tau and depressive symptoms may be even more complex, potentially involving fluctuations of symptoms. 16 , 46 Several hypotheses may account for the results. One hypothesis is related to the decrease in the awareness of depressive symptoms, and the other to changes in the clinical presentation of depressive symptoms.

The former explanation may be that mild depression in the early stage of AD may eventually diminish together with the accumulation of p‐tau. In a previous explanatory study from Europe, a negative correlation was observed between p‐tau and total GDS‐15 score, although no adjustments were made for confounding factors. 26 A previous neuropathological study suggested that, compared with earlier Braak stages, depression and anxiety may decrease as stages progress. 16 Another study suggested that the severity of depressive symptoms may decrease gradually regardless of medicine intake. 52 Notably, this decrease in depressive symptoms may occur simply because of a reduction in subjective complaints 52 and the progression of anosognosia, 53 because p‐tau particularly accumulates in brain hub regions that may be responsible for these cognitive functions. 54 Taken together, these findings suggest that the apparent reduction in depressive symptoms may be related to the decrease in the awareness of depressive symptoms.

The latter, more challenging explanation may be that as p‐tau accumulation progresses, the clinical features of depressive symptoms may change and become more diverse, which might lead to the appearance of decreased depressive symptoms. It is a common belief that depressive moods tend to be less prominent and depressive symptoms become more diverse among older adults. 3 In a positron emission tomography study examining tau accumulation in the brains of older individuals with depressive disorder and healthy older controls, extended tau deposition in cortical areas was reported to be associated with not only depressive symptoms but also psychotic symptoms. 55 These observations suggest that tau progression might alter the characteristics of geriatric depressive symptoms. Considering these findings, it may be that as tau accumulates, depressive mood and psychomotor retardation become unremarkable with the appearance of other diverse characteristics of geriatric depression, including psychosis. As such, the changes in symptom profile may result in lower scores on GDS‐15.

In addition to the hypotheses discussed above, a more complex model may also be assumed that depressive symptoms increase in the early stages of p‐tau accumulation and subsequently decrease as the disease progresses. For example, one previous study suggesting that depressive symptoms may follow an inverted U‐shaped trajectory in relation to cognitive decline. 46 If such a model is valid, our findings may represent only the latter phase of such trajectory. Nonetheless, the relationship between p‐tau and depressive symptoms among individuals with cognitive decline is not yet fully understood, the findings of the present study suggest that there is a significant association between these factors. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms of such an association.

The current study had several limitations. First, this was a cross‐sectional study, meaning that causal relationships were unable to be deduced. Second, because the patients were strictly diagnosed by well‐trained psychiatrists and neurologists, our sample size was small. It may thus be difficult to detect small effect sizes. Nevertheless, similar sample sizes have been used previously to evaluate the associations between p‐tau and depression. 25 , 26 Third, the data were extracted from a single center. However, referrals were received from various medical institutions, which increases the generalizability of our study. Fourth, although we extracted as many biopsychosocial factors (described in previous reviews and cohorts) as possible from our electronic medical records, there may be other covariate factors that affect the association between p‐tau and GDS‐15. Especially, we considered the use of psychotropic drugs as covariates, because psychotropic drugs may reflect the severity of depressive symptoms and could also influence depressive symptoms. However, further analysis regarding the types, dosages, and other pharmacological characteristics of such medications should be considered in future studies to more thoroughly address potential confounding effects. Finally, because of our limited dataset of amyloid‐β in CSF samples, we were unable to assess the association between amyloid‐β and GDS‐15; this should be considered in future research.

In conclusion, the present study investigated the association between CSF p‐tau and depressive symptoms (evaluated using the GDS‐15) in patients with MCI due to AD or with ADD. The accumulation of p‐tau was associated with a decrease in both the prevalence of specific GDS‐15 items and GDS‐15 total scores. Our results align with the previously reported negative relationship between p‐tau and depressive symptoms from Western countries, 16 , 26 making this finding relatively generalizable and of broader international relevance. However, future longitudinal studies are required to confirm our findings and assess the predictive value of p‐tau over the course of depressive symptoms in patients with cognitive decline.
