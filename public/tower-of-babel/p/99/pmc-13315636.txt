Lipid-based nanocarriers for antifungal drug delivery
Authors: Anna Staniszewska, Deepak Kala, Łukasz Kuryk, Aneta Borkowska, Swatantar Kumar, Monika Staniszewska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungal diseases constitute a rapidly escalating global health threat, causing an estimated 1.5–2 million deaths annually and affecting more than one billion individuals worldwide, particularly immunocompromised patient populations. The increasing incidence of severe systemic infections, severely limited therapeutic options, prolonged treatment regimens, and the rapid emergence of antimycotic resistance collectively underscore the urgent clinical need for innovative treatment strategies. Consequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) has highlighted sustainable investment in antifungal research and the targeted development of novel therapeutics as a primary global priority. Nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems, particularly advanced lipid-based nanocarriers, offer highly promising solutions to overcome current therapeutic limitations. Sophisticated vesicular systems have successfully demonstrated improved target-site accumulation, highly controlled drug release, enhanced tissue penetration (including deep ocular and cutaneous applications), and drastically reduced systemic toxicity in both preclinical and selected clinical settings. Furthermore, PEGylated and ligand-functionalized liposomes, such as DC-SIGN targeted amphotericin B formulations, significantly improve pathogen-specific binding and overall antifungal activity in rigorous experimental models. Emerging formulation strategies, including pH-responsive stealth liposomes and stimuli-sensitive systems adapted directly from oncological applications, suggest immense potential for maximizing antifungal selectivity and clinical efficacy. Additionally, advanced liposome-in-hydrogel hybrid matrices enable sustained therapeutic release, remarkably enhancing formulation stability while minimizing off-target systemic exposure. This comprehensive review provides an integrative analysis of nanostructured lipid carriers in antifungal therapy, detailing fundamental mechanistic principles, advanced formulation strategies, scalable preparation methodologies, and diverse clinical applications. Particular emphasis is strategically placed on highly deformable liposomal platforms explicitly engineered to overcome the structural limitations of conventional delivery systems. Ultimately, successfully bridging the formidable translational gap between experimental benchtop innovation and routine clinical implementation requires harmonized regulatory frameworks, scalable and reproducible manufacturing protocols, rigorous quality control, and substantially enhanced public and private sector collaboration. By synthesizing mechanistic pharmacological insights with realistic translational and regulatory perspectives, this work aims to advance the fundamental understanding of lipid-based antifungal delivery platforms and directly support the development of highly effective, clinically viable therapeutic approaches.Grapical abstract

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization's [153] fungal priority pathogens list, fungal diseases (FDs) represent a growing global health threat, responsible for approximately 1.5–2 million deaths annually [26, 153]. Each year, there are over 150 million severe cases and more than 1 billion superficial infections, disproportionately affecting immunocompromised individuals [26, 92]. The clinical management of FDs is highly challenging due to limited global access to care, the restricted efficacy of existing antimycotics, prolonged treatment regimens, poor patient recovery rates, and the alarming emergence of antifungal resistance [46, 153]. In response, the WHO has outlined strategic actions for public health professionals and scientists to enhance the global defense against fungal pathogens and mitigate resistance. A primary focal point of these strategies is the need for sustainable investment in antifungal research and the development of novel antimycotics [148]. In this context, nanotechnology has emerged as a powerful tool, offering significant advantages in pharmaceutical development to overcome current therapeutic limitations [11, 15, 40, 44, 64, 93].

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued comprehensive industry guidance in 2018 regarding liposome drug products, detailing regulatory requirements for their chemistry, manufacturing, controls, human pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and labeling [85, 142]. Structurally, liposomes are vesicles formed by amphipathic molecules, such as phospholipids. They consist of either a single bilayer (unilamellar) or a concentric series of multiple bilayers (multilamellar) that enclose a central aqueous core. Their therapeutic effectiveness stems from their exceptional versatility, safety, and biocompatibility. Notably, they offer targeted delivery, high loading capacity, and the unique ability to encapsulate both hydrophobic and hydrophilic molecules [15, 136]. Today, lipid-based nanocarriers rank among the most successful platforms for delivering poorly permeable drugs, effectively overcoming traditional delivery constraints in the management of superficial fungal diseases. Key clinical advantages include localized deposition in the epidermis, which is the primary active site of most superficial infections, along with a significant reduction in adverse effects, avoidance of hepatic first-pass metabolism, and excellent gastrointestinal tolerance [15]. Furthermore, the successful application of pH-responsive stealth liposomes in oncology underscores their broad adaptability [17]. Because fungal infections often present similar environmental triggers, translating these smart delivery systems into antifungal therapies holds immense potential to enhance drug selectivity and efficacy while simultaneously minimizing systemic toxicity [42].

Beyond traditional liposomal formulations, hydrogel-based carriers have garnered significant attention for their capacity to mediate localized and sustained drug release [43, 74]. Composed of three-dimensional networks of hydrophilic polymers, hydrogels effectively retain therapeutic agents directly at the infection site. This spatial confinement makes them particularly advantageous for managing superficial and mucocutaneous fungal infections [19, 125]. More recently, researchers have explored the integration of liposomes into hydrogel matrices. These advanced hybrid systems synergize the high drug-loading and targeting capabilities of liposomes with the structural stability and prolonged-release kinetics of hydrogels, maximizing therapeutic outcomes while curtailing systemic side effects [28, 78, 155]. Collectively, these ongoing advancements in lipid and hydrogel-based delivery platforms hold substantial promise for modernizing antifungal therapy across clinical disciplines. By enhancing drug bioavailability and reducing off-target toxicity, these approaches, bolstered by translational insights from oncology, provide a strategic framework to overcome antifungal resistance and ultimately improve patient care [74, 125].

The fundamental novelty of this review lies in its comprehensive analysis of nanostructured lipid carriers and liposomal systems for antifungal therapy, uniquely bridging mechanistic insights with translational and regulatory perspectives. We provide an in-depth evaluation of various antifungal drug-loaded liposomes, detailing their distinct mechanisms of action, preparation methodologies, clinical applications, and therapeutic advantages. Furthermore, this review specifically highlights the emerging role of deformable liposomes, which are strategically designed to overcome the permeation limitations associated with conventional liposomal formulations. Ultimately, this work seeks to consolidate contemporary understanding, address existing knowledge gaps, and establish a robust foundation to accelerate future research in advanced antifungal drug delivery.

Deformable liposomes

According to FDA guidance and established literature, liposomes are spherical artificial vesicles characterized by one or multiple concentric phospholipid bilayers that enclose a central aqueous core [8, 21, 48, 90, 103, 142]. In this structural arrangement, the polar head groups of the amphipathic molecules orient toward both the internal aqueous phase and the exterior environment. Therapeutic agents are subsequently intercalated within the lipid bilayers or encapsulated in the central aqueous compartments [142]. While conventional liposomes are highly effective, deformable liposomes offer significant structural advancements for topical applications (Fig. 1). These advanced carriers are also referred to as flexible, elastic, or ultra-deformable liposomes [79]. They are engineered by incorporating hydrophilic solutes or specific membrane-destabilizing edge activators, such as sodium deoxycholate, Tween, or Span, directly into the phospholipid bilayers.

These edge activators reduce interfacial tension to vastly increase bilayer flexibility. This unique property allows the vesicles to undergo reversible deformation without structural rupture or drug leakage [79]. Consequently, these flexible vesicles can squeeze through small pores and transport drugs deep into intact skin, moving along the transdermal hydration gradient via hydrophilic pathways [31, 33, 38, 103]. Depending on their specific composition, these specialized carriers are classified into several categories (Fig. 2). These include transfersomes (containing edge activators), ethosomes (containing ethanol), invasomes (containing terpenes), menthosomes (containing menthol), and transethosomes (incorporating both ethanol and edge activators). Compared to conventional liposomes, all these deformable vesicles offer higher entrapment efficiency, superior transdermal delivery, and reduced systemic exposure [103]. Their enhanced skin permeation results directly from high membrane elasticity and favorable interactions with stratum corneum lipids. As a result, they are extensively studied for topical antifungal therapies, particularly for delivering amphotericin B and azole derivatives to treat Candida infections. When evaluating these delivery systems, researchers rely on key performance metrics that include particle size, zeta potential, entrapment efficiency, and in vitro permeation studies using Franz diffusion cells.

Transfersomes

Transfersomes represent the first generation of elastic liposomes. Similar to conventional vesicles, they feature an internal aqueous compartment surrounded by a lipid bilayer [72, 81]. However, their unique structural advantage comes from the incorporation of a specialized edge activator directly into this bilayer. These activators typically consist of single-chain surfactants with a high curvature radius, such as sodium deoxycholate, Spans, or Tweens [157].

The addition of these specific surfactants deliberately alters the lipid membrane to impart exceptional elasticity and flexibility. This highly deformable nature enables transfersomes to squeeze through intact skin pores and biological barriers that are significantly smaller than the vesicles themselves [33, 35, 72, 79, 81]. Typically synthesized using the thin-film hydration method [103], these specialized lipid vesicles are highly preferred for topical and transdermal drug administration [99]. Despite their clinical advantages, transfersome formulations do present certain practical limitations. These challenges include high production costs, the inherently low purity of natural phospholipids, and a tendency toward slight chemical instability over time [99, 102].

Transfersomes facilitate skin penetration by compressing the intercellular sealing lipids of the stratum corneum [103]. Formulated from phospholipids and specific edge activators, such as sodium cholate, Tween, or Span, these vesicles achieve heightened membrane fluidity while strictly maintaining their structural integrity.

This critical feature enables reversible deformation without the premature release of their encapsulated cargo [103]. Within the scope of antifungal therapy, these highly adaptable carriers have been extensively studied for the targeted delivery of drugs like amphotericin B, fluconazole, and ketoconazole. By significantly improving tissue penetration, they enhance localized drug accumulation directly at the site of infection. This makes them particularly effective for the topical and transdermal treatment of fungal conditions, including infections caused by Candida species. Ultimately, these advanced deformable systems offer superior skin permeation, increased bioavailability, and lower systemic toxicity compared to conventional liposomes, establishing them as a highly promising therapeutic option for superficial and localized fungal infections [103].

Ethosomes

Ethosomes are elastic nanovesicles primarily based on phospholipids that contain a high concentration of ethanol, typically ranging from 20 to 45 percent, which significantly enhances tissue permeation. These versatile carriers can successfully entrap hydrophilic, lipophilic, or amphiphilic molecules [23]. They are typically produced through mechanical dispersion techniques, as well as hot or cold formulation methods, utilizing a carefully calibrated mixture of alcohol, cholesterol, phospholipids, surfactants, and propylene glycol [23, 94, 116]. In these specialized ternary systems composed of phospholipids, ethanol, and water, the ethanol acts as a powerful penetration enhancer. It simultaneously increases the flexibility of the lipid bilayer and disrupts the dense cellular organization of the stratum corneum to promote much deeper skin penetration.

The mechanistic action of ethosomes relies fundamentally on modulating membrane permeability and fluidity through a thermodynamic push and pull effect. As ethanol evaporates from the vesicular system applied to the skin surface, it creates a driving push mechanism that forces the vesicles deeper into the tissue. This physical process subsequently generates a pull effect that draws the encapsulated drug through the compromised stratum corneum, ultimately releasing the therapeutic agent directly at the target site [23, 80, 91, 94, 147].

Within antifungal therapy, these vesicles have been extensively investigated as advanced carriers for delivering azole derivatives such as fluconazole, ketoconazole, and itraconazole. Compared to traditional formulations, they consistently demonstrate improved clinical efficacy and substantially reduced systemic exposure. Ethosomes are particularly effective for the topical and transdermal delivery of treatments against Candida infections, offering both enhanced bioavailability and improved patient compliance. For instance, voriconazole loaded ethosomes prepared via the cold method have shown high entrapment efficiency and potent in vitro activity against Aspergillus species. This presents a highly viable strategy to reduce the severe side effects commonly associated with oral and parenteral administration [23, 91, 94, 116, 147]. Additionally, an ethosomal gel incorporating amphotericin B was successfully formulated and evaluated by Kaur and Maurya [83], further validating the broad antifungal potential of this delivery platform [83, 99].

Transethosomes (TELs)

Transethosomes (TELs) are advanced vesicular carriers that strategically combine the distinct advantages of both transfersomes and ethosomes. They contain a high concentration of ethanol alongside an edge activator to actively facilitate membrane passage [129]. These specialized delivery systems are formulated using a precise combination of phospholipids, surfactants, alcohol, permeation enhancers, and aqueous buffers.

Generally, TELs are synthesized utilizing the thin film hydration method, the cold formulation technique, or the ethanol injection process. Within these structures, the high ethanol content significantly enhances tissue permeation. Furthermore, a complex synergistic mechanism occurs between the incorporated surfactants, the ethanol, and the natural skin lipids. Consequently, these vesicles are highly effective for the dermal delivery of antifungal agents such as voriconazole. They exhibit exceptional elasticity, superior permeability, and targeted skin deposition directly resulting from the combined action of the ethanol and the edge activator [3, 129].

Menthosomes

Menthosomes represent a novel class of ultra-deformable vesicular carriers composed of menthol, phospholipids, and a specific edge activator such as cetylpyridinium chloride [55]. Scientists typically formulate these highly adaptable systems utilizing standard sonication techniques [54].

Upon topical application, these formulations significantly enhance transdermal penetration by directly altering the lamellar lipid bilayer structure of the skin. Mechanistically, the incorporated menthol acts to actively reduce hydrocarbon chain packing. This localized physical disruption alters the natural lipid microstructure and subsequently increases the overall fluidity of the intercellular lipids within the stratum corneum [151].

Invasomes

Invasomes represent an advanced phospholipid-based drug delivery system containing a synergistic combination of ethanol and specific terpenes. This unique chemical composition actively deforms the vesicles, thereby significantly enhancing the skin penetration of both lipophilic and hydrophilic therapeutic agents [53]. These specialized nanocarriers are typically formulated by utilizing thin-layer evaporation and vortexing techniques.

A prominent clinical application involves the delivery of dapsone (DPS), which is an established sulfone antimicrobial compound. When formulated as invasomes via the thin-film hydration method, the percutaneous penetration of dapsone is vastly improved, making the resulting formulation highly effective and ready for advanced topical use [53].

Comprehensive characterization of liposomes

The FDA guidance document [142]. provides a foundational framework for the rigorous characterization of liposome drug products. This regulatory framework specifically outlines essential evaluation parameters across two primary domains. The first domain encompasses the chemistry, manufacturing processes, and quality controls necessary to ensure structural bioequivalence. The second domain details stringent requirements for assessing human pharmacokinetics, systemic bioavailability, and clinical bioequivalence [142].

Beyond these regulatory guidelines, researchers must routinely evaluate these advanced vesicular systems in laboratory settings to confirm their overall therapeutic viability. Encapsulation efficiency (%EE)

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) represents the percentage of the entrapped therapeutic agent relative to the total amount of the drug utilized during the formulation process [103, 105]. The protocol outlines the comprehensive steps required to prepare and analyze vesicular suspensions to determine drug entrapment efficiency. The following protocols detail the preparation of the digestion mixture, homogenization, centrifugation, sediment lysis, absorbance measurement, and calculation of the entrapped drug concentration.

Preparation of the digestion mixture: Add an equal volume of the selected organic solvent (either 50% n-propanol or 0.1% Triton X-100) to the vesicular suspension. Incorporate 1 mL of 2.5% sodium lauryl sulfate into this mixture to aid in vesicle digestion. Homogenization: Use a homogenizer to briefly mix the solution, ensuring a uniform dispersion and complete lysis of the vesicles. This step is critical for the accurate measurement of the entrapped drug. Centrifugation: Transfer the homogenized solution into suitable centrifuge tubes. Centrifuge the samples at 20,000 rpm for 1 h at 4 °C. This step will separate the particulate sediment from the supernatant. Sediment Lysis: After centrifugation, carefully decant the supernatant without disturbing the sediment. Add methanol to the sediment to achieve lysis, facilitating the release of the entrapped drug into solution. Measurement of Absorbance: Once the sediment is lysed, use a UV–Vis spectrophotometer to measure the absorbance of the resulting solution at 252 nm. This absorbance corresponds to the concentration of the entrapped drug. Calculation of Entrapment Efficiency: EE% = (ED/TD) × 100; where ED represents the entrapped drug concentration, and TD represents the total concentration of the drug introduced into the system.

In a study by Pinilla et al. [112], researchers used membrane ultrafiltration with a Millipore Ultracel YM-10 filter (MA, USA) to determine the total allicin concentration, the main active component of garlic extract. They used high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with quaternary pumps and a diode-array detector, employing an XBridge C18 column (150 mm by 4.6 mm, 5-µm particle size) at an isocratic flow rate of 0.7 mL per minute for 15 min. Nayak and Saraswat [104] examined the encapsulation efficiency of oxiconazole-loaded liposomes, reporting values ranging from 48.85 ± 3.74% to 94.34 ± 3.09%. They found that using positively charged surfactants, such as stearyl amine, significantly increased the encapsulation efficiency of oxiconazole. Encapsulation efficiency is influenced by factors such as the chain length and hydrophilic head characteristics of the non-ionic surfactants, as well as the incorporation of sterols in the bilayer composition of the delivery carriers [16].

Morphology analysis

Advanced imaging techniques are essential for thoroughly evaluating the physical characteristics of liposomal carriers. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is routinely utilized to examine overall vesicle morphology and surface topography [45]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provides high-resolution assessments of structural features, precise carrier shape, and size distribution [9]. TEM protocol: (1) Pipette a small volume (typically 5–10 µL) of the liposome sample onto a clean 300-mesh formvar-coated copper grid, (2) Allow the sample to adhere for approximately 1–2 min, (3) Carefully add 25 g/L uranyl acetate to the grid containing the liposome sample, (4) Incubate for 1–2 min to enable negative staining, (5) Gently blot the grid with filter paper to remove excess uranyl acetate, (6) Allow the grid to air dry completely at room temperature, (7) Insert the prepared grid into the TEM and acquire high-resolution images to assess the structural features, carrier shape, and size distribution of liposomes. Analyze TEM images for structural features, including size and morphology.

CLSM protocol for skin penetration study: (1) Load liposome formulations with Rhodamine 6G (Rh6G) fluorescent probe at an appropriate concentration, (2) Apply the fluorescently tagged liposomal formulation onto the skin surface in a uniform manner, (3) Allow the formulation to remain on the skin for a predetermined amount of time to facilitate penetration, (4) Use the CLSM to scan the treated skin samples: set the excitation wavelength to 543 nm and the emission wavelength to 560 nm to visualize the distribution of Rh6G [121], (5) Collect images at various depths and layers of the skin to analyze the penetration of liposomal formulations. Ensure all imaging equipment is calibrated before use. Follow appropriate safety and handling protocols for all materials and samples used.

Determination of particle size (PS), polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential (ZP)

The particle dimensions and surface charge distribution of nanovesicles are routinely determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and a dedicated zeta potential analyzer [62, 112]. In a representative study, Pinilla et al. [112] measured vesicle size and the polydispersity index (PDI) using a Brookhaven system equipped with a He–Ne laser (35 mW) at a fixed scattering angle of 90°. The protocol: (1) Dilute the liposomal dispersions in purified water at a ratio of 1:100 to minimize multiple scattering effects. (2) Set up the Brookhaven system with the He–Ne laser. Ensure the laser operates at 35 mW and the scattering angle is fixed at 90°. (3) Input the prepared liposomal dispersion into the measurement chamber. (4) Start the measurement process and record the vesicle size and PDI. Adhere to safety guidelines while operating the laser and electrophoretic instruments.

Concurrently, the zeta potential (ZP) of the formulations is measured at room temperature to evaluate colloidal stability [104]. Prior to analysis, the liposomal dispersions are diluted in purified water, typically at a 1:100 ratio, to minimize multiple scattering. The ZP assessment is then performed by observing the electrophoretic migration of the vesicles within an applied electrical field using instruments such as the Zetasizer Nano ZS. To ensure statistical reliability and accuracy, the mean ZP value is typically calculated by repeating the analytical reading ten times [45, 114]. The protocol of measurement of zeta potential: (1) Use the Zetasizer Nano ZS for assessing zeta potential. (2) Place the diluted liposomal dispersion in the Zetasizer measurement cell. (3) Apply an electric field to observe the electrophoretic migration of the vesicles. (4) Record the ZP values. (5). Ensure that each measurement for ZP is repeated ten times to guarantee reliability and accuracy. (6) Calculate the mean ZP value from the repeated measurements. (7) Document the vesicle size, PDI, and mean ZP values along with any observations or deviations encountered during the experiment.

In vitro drug release study

It is evident that drug release kinetics act as a critical determinant of a delivery system's overall therapeutic efficiency; these evaluations are routinely conducted using a modified Franz diffusion cell [45]. In a standard protocol, a suitable dialysis membrane is securely positioned between the donor and receptor compartments. The formulated liposomes are introduced into the donor compartment, while the receptor compartment is typically filled with a physiological phosphate buffer at pH 7.4. To simulate human physiological conditions, the entire diffusion cell apparatus is maintained at 37 °C under continuous magnetic stirring, often at 200 rpm. At predetermined time intervals, specific aliquots are systematically withdrawn from the receptor compartment and analyzed by UV–Vis spectrophotometry to quantify cumulative in vitro drug release [45]. To understand the underlying mechanistic release pattern, the experimental data are subsequently evaluated using various mathematical models. For example, the in vitro release profile of a drug from a liposomal gel is frequently analyzed using Higuchi kinetics and the Korsmeyer-Peppas equation. In a representative evaluation by Nayak and Saraswat [104], their specific liposomal gel exhibited the highest coefficient of determination (R2) for the Higuchi model (R2 = 0.932). This statistical fit strongly indicates that the drug release process is fundamentally diffusion-controlled. Furthermore, their calculated release exponent values exceeded 0.85, which characterizes a super case II transport mechanism driving the release kinetics [104].

Skin penetration study ex vivo

Ex vivo skin penetration studies are routinely conducted to assess the permeation capabilities of vesicular drug delivery systems across biological skin barriers [103]. Researchers frequently utilize various animal tissue models for these evaluations, with fresh rat and porcine skin being the most common [103]. The protocol provides a structured approach to performing ex vivo permeation studies that can aid in the development of effective antifungal treatments: (1) Obtain excised dorsal skin and ensure it is properly cleaned and prepared for experimentation. (2) Mount the excised dorsal skin between the donor and receptor compartments of the diffusion apparatus. Ensure a secure fit to prevent leakage. (3) Apply the vesicular formulation directly to the epidermal surface located in the donor compartment. (4) Fill the receptor compartment with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), ensuring that it is at the correct volume to maintain sink conditions throughout the experiment. (5) Set the diffusion apparatus in a thermostatic water bath at 37 °C. Stir the PBS continuously to mimic human physiological conditions. Allow the system to run for 24 h. (6) At specified time intervals, carefully collect aliquots from the receptor compartment. For each aliquot taken, immediately replace it with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. (7) Analyze the aliquots using either high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) or UV–VIS spectrophotometry to quantify the amount of drug that has permeated through the skin. (8) Record the data for drug concentrations at each time point to evaluate permeation efficiency, release kinetics, and skin retention properties. (9) Compile the data for analysis. Determine the drug permeation profile and assess the performance of the antifungal formulation in comparison to desired benchmarks. Ensure all equipment and reagents are sterile to prevent contamination. Maintain a consistent temperature throughout the experiment. Follow safety protocols when handling biological materials and reagents [2, 41, 65, 103, 114]. These ex vivo approaches provide essential insights into the penetration efficiency, release kinetics, and targeted skin retention properties of novel antifungal formulations before conducting comprehensive in vivo evaluations [2, 41, 65, 114].

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is extensively utilized to characterize the physical and chemical transitions that occur in freeze-dried liposomes upon heating. To conduct this thermal stability evaluation, researchers employ a highly sensitive thermogravimetric analyzer. In a standard protocol, lyophilized liposome samples are continuously heated from 25 to 600 °C at a controlled rate of 20 °C per minute. This entire process is strictly maintained under a dynamic nitrogen atmosphere with a constant flow rate of 40 mL per minute to prevent the oxidative degradation of the lipids [22, 158].

Conversely, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) provides critical insights into the phase transition behavior of the lipid bilayers. For DSC studies, liquid vesicular samples are typically pre-concentrated by a factor of 1:3 utilizing a Millipore membrane filter to ensure an adequate thermal signal. These concentrated dispersions are then carefully sealed in standard aluminum pans. During the analysis, the pans are gradually heated from 30 to 100 °C at a steady rate of 10 °C/minute, again under a continuous nitrogen atmosphere to ensure absolute environmental consistency [22, 158].

The protocol for Thermal Stability Evaluation of Freeze-Dried Liposomes Using TGA and DSC: (1) Weigh a suitable amount of lyophilized liposome sample. (2) Place the sample in the TGA balance. Set the temperature program to heat the sample from 25 °C to 600 °C at 20 °C/min. Initiate the analysis under a dynamic nitrogen atmosphere (40 mL/min) to prevent oxidative degradation of lipids. Record the weight loss and temperature data throughout the heating process. Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC): (1) Prepare liquid vesicular samples and concentrate them by a factor of 1:3 using a Millipore membrane filter. Collect the concentrated sample in a clean container. (2) Seal the concentrated vesicular sample in standard aluminum pans, ensuring no leaks are present. (3) Place the aluminum pans in the DSC instrument. Set the temperature program to gradually heat the samples from 30 °C to 100 °C at 10 °C/min. Ensure that the heating occurs under a continuous nitrogen atmosphere to maintain environmental consistency. Collect heat flow data and analyze phase-transition behavior. (4) Data Analysis: For TGA, analyze the weight loss data to evaluate thermal stability and identify decomposition points. For DSC, interpret heat flow data to understand lipid bilayer phase transitions and thermodynamic properties. This protocol provides a systematic approach to assessing the thermal properties of freeze-dried liposomes, ensuring that temperature changes are accurately measured while maintaining sample integrity.

Antifungal activity studies

In vitro antifungal efficacy is routinely evaluated utilizing the standardized disc diffusion method [45]. The protocol for evaluating in vitro antifungal efficacy using the disc diffusion method [45]: (1) Prepare suspensions of both reference and clinical fungal strains in sterile water. Ensure that the fungal cultures encompass a range of yeasts and molds. (2) Using a Neubauer chamber, determine the concentration of the fungal suspensions. Standardize the inoculum to 1 × 10^6 spores/mL by direct microscopic counting. (3) Inoculate the surface of Sabouraud dextrose agar plates with the standardized fungal suspension. Ensure even distribution across the agar to obtain reproducible results. (4) Once the agar plates are inoculated, apply the specific liposomal formulations directly to the surface of the agar. (5) Incubate the inoculated agar plates at 28 °C for a duration of 24–48 h. (6) After the incubation period, measure the diameters of the zones of growth inhibition surrounding the discs using a standard millimeter scale. (7) Record the measurements to quantitatively determine the antifungal potency of the tested nanocarriers.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis is routinely performed to determine possible structural alterations within the lipid membranes. To achieve this, the FTIR spectra of the formulated liposomes and their individual structural components are systematically acquired utilizing an FTIR spectrometer. The analytical scanning is typically conducted within the mid-infrared region, specifically in the wave number range of 4000–400 cm−1, with a precise spectral resolution of 4 cm−1. Before this spectroscopic analysis, the liposomal samples must be completely freeze-dried to eliminate spectral interference from water molecules [112].

Fundamentally, infrared spectroscopy serves as a powerful analytical tool to assess the specific structural and conformational changes induced by various therapeutic compounds entrapped within the phospholipid bilayer [112, 139]. In a comprehensive study by Pinilla and colleagues [112], the researchers reported highly characteristic peaks in the FTIR spectrum of phosphatidylcholine and oleic acid liposomes encapsulating garlic extract. These distinct spectral signatures directly indicated the effective incorporation of the active extract into the nanovesicles. Furthermore, the authors observed no significant spectral shifts corresponding to the bilayer acyl chains, confirming that the structural integrity of the liposomes was successfully maintained during the encapsulation process [112].

Physical stability of liposomes under storage conditions

The author and colleagues assessed the physical stability of liposomal formulations through careful visual observation to detect any macroscopic signs of sedimentation or phase separation [56]. Fundamentally, the initial particle size and encapsulation efficacy of the nanovesicles significantly influence their long-term stability [25]. To conduct a comprehensive stability evaluation, researchers typically monitor the formulations over standardized intervals of 30, 60, and 90 days.

Following each specified storage period, critical parameters such as the residual drug content and the in vitro release profile are rigorously analyzed. These results are then directly compared with the baseline measurements recorded from the initial sample on day 1. In accordance with established regulatory guidelines, these longitudinal stability studies are routinely conducted under strictly controlled environmental conditions. Standard protocols require storing the samples either at 25 °C with 60 percent relative humidity (RH), or under accelerated testing conditions at 40 °C with 75 percent RH [56, 105, 125, 143].

Degree of deformability

The inherent deformability of the nanovesicles is quantitatively determined utilizing a standard extrusion method. During this procedure, the vesicular suspension is forcefully passed through an extrusion matrix equipped with specialized polycarbonate filters possessing defined pore sizes, typically ranging from 50 to 200 nm. Following this controlled extrusion process, the resulting size distribution and final vesicle dimensions are accurately monitored utilizing a dynamic light scattering instrument, such as a Zetasizer.

To mathematically standardize these physical characteristics, the overall degree of deformability (D) is calculated using the following established equation:

D = J (rv/rp)2, where J represents the total weight of the vesicular suspension that is successfully extruded through the polycarbonate filters, rv denotes the measured vesicle size following the extrusion process, and rp corresponds to the specific barrier pore size of the filter utilized [31, 33, 34, 58, 72, 81].

Histopathological study

Histopathological analyses are commonly conducted to evaluate structural or cellular changes in the skin after the topical application of liposomal formulations. According to the protocol [103]: (1) Topically apply the liposomal formulation to the designated area of the skin as per the study design. (2) After the appropriate period post-application, carefully excise the treated area of skin using sterile surgical instruments to avoid contamination. (3) Immediately immerse the excised tissue in a 10% neutral buffered formalin solution to ensure proper fixation. Allow the tissue to remain in the fixative for the recommended duration, typically 24 h. (4) Once fixed, use a microtome to section the tissue vertically at a precise thickness of 10 µm. (5) Mount the microtome sections on standard glass slides, ensuring proper orientation. (6) Stain the sections with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to established protocols to enhance visualization of cellular structures. (7) After staining, examine the slides under a high-resolution light microscope. Note epidermal integrity, vesicle penetration, and overall biocompatibility of the nanocarriers. (8) Record observations and perform a thorough analysis of the histopathological changes in the treated skin areas.

Liposome preparation methods

The thin-film hydration method (hand-shaking method or Bangham method)

The thin-film hydration method, classically known as the Bangham method, represents a foundational technique for liposome preparation. This procedure fundamentally involves dissolving phospholipids and cholesterol in a volatile organic solvent, evaporating the solvent to yield a uniform lipid film, and subsequently hydrating this film to generate multilamellar vesicles (MLVs). These initial MLVs can then be further processed into smaller unilamellar vesicles. The weighed phospholipids, the therapeutic drug, and specific edge activators are completely dissolved in a solvent mixture, typically methanol and chloroform at a 1:1 or 1:2 volume ratio, within a round-bottom flask [113]. The organic solvent is then carefully evaporated, usually via a rotary evaporator, leaving a homogeneous lipid film deposited on the inner wall of the flask. To ensure the complete removal of any residual solvent, the system is subjected to a vacuum. Following this purification step, the dry lipid film is hydrated using a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution at pH 7.4. This crucial hydration step is performed at temperatures ranging from 0 to 60 °C under normal pressure with continuous gentle agitation. The lipid vesicles are subsequently allowed to swell at room temperature. To achieve the desired size distribution, the resulting multilamellar vesicles are typically subjected to sonication to effectively reduce their size, after which the final formulation is stored at 4 °C to maintain stability. This classical method is highly versatile and is routinely utilized to formulate advanced carriers such as transfersomes and niosomal gels incorporating surfactants like Tween 60 [45, 114, 121]. For advanced size reduction and the preparation of highly uniform small unilamellar vesicles, researchers may also employ microfluidization. In this advanced processing step, two fluidized streams of the liposomal suspension move through a precisely defined microchannel and interact at ultra-high velocities within an interaction chamber [45]. In recent literature, Pinilla et al. [112] successfully utilized the thin-film hydration method to prepare their garlic extract liposomes. Similarly, Albash et al. [9] employed this precise technique to fabricate terpesomes loaded with fenticonazole nitrate. Furthermore, transethosomes (TELs) are frequently produced using this methodology by incorporating varying ethanol concentrations during hydration, followed by incubating the prepared suspension overnight to ensure complete vesicle hydration and formation [71].

Mechanical dispersion method

The mechanical dispersion method is a robust formulation technique frequently employed for the generation of specialized elastic vesicles. In this procedure, precisely weighed phospholipids at various concentrations are placed into dried round-bottom flasks and completely dissolved in a solvent mixture of chloroform and methanol. The organic solvent is subsequently evaporated under reduced pressure utilizing a rotary evaporator. This process yields a uniform, thin lipid film deposited on the inner wall of the flask. Unlike standard liposome preparation, this film is then hydrated using a carefully formulated hydroethanolic mixture that contains the therapeutic drug at specified concentrations [21, 58, 90, 101].

The unique presence of ethanol during this dispersion phase is critical for producing highly elastic vesicles. Highlighting its clinical utility, Maheshwari et al. [94] successfully utilized this mechanical dispersion method to formulate nanosized ethosomes with enhanced permeation characteristics. Furthermore, mechanical dispersion techniques are frequently applied as crucial post-processing steps following initial vesicle formation to significantly reduce vesicle size and improve overall sample homogeneity. Specifically, utilizing bath or probe sonication effectively produces highly uniform small unilamellar vesicles, while mechanical extrusion through specialized polycarbonate membranes yields liposomes with strictly defined and predictable size distributions [41].

Cold method

The cold method represents a mild and highly reproducible formulation technique commonly utilized to prepare specialized elastic carriers, such as ethosomes and transethosomes. This approach is particularly suitable for encapsulating thermolabile antifungal drugs, because it completely avoids elevated temperatures. In a standard protocol, as demonstrated by Shaji and Garude [116] for the production of ethosomes, soya lecithin is first dissolved in ethanol and subjected to continuous magnetic stirring to ensure complete solubilization and uniform molecular dispersion. In this system, the ethanol serves a critical dual role by acting as a primary lipid solvent while simultaneously enhancing both vesicle flexibility and subsequent skin permeation [116].

Following the initial lipid dissolution, supplementary components are carefully introduced. For instance, propylene glycol is frequently added to the alcoholic solution, which is then meticulously maintained at 30 °C (Fig. 3). This specific addition acts synergistically as a cosolvent, a humectant, and an additional permeation enhancer [116]. When formulating transethosomes (TELs), an edge activator, such as Tween or Span, is systematically incorporated into the alcoholic solution during the continuous stirring phase at 30 °C, as explicitly described by Garg and colleagues [67]. The integration of this specialized surfactant further increases the overall bilayer elasticity and inherent deformability by significantly reducing interfacial tension and strategically destabilizing the rigid lipid packing.

Once the organic phase is completely mixed, the targeted aqueous phase, typically a physiological buffer or sterile water, is added very slowly under continuous stirring. This precisely controlled addition triggers the spontaneous formation of the nanovesicles. To effectively reduce the initial particle size and drastically improve sample homogeneity, the resulting liposomal dispersion may be subjected to advanced post-processing techniques, such as sonication or mechanical extrusion [67]. Ultimately, the cold method remains highly advantageous because it is procedurally simple, strictly avoids thermal degradation, and efficiently entraps therapeutic agents, making it an ideal candidate for the large-scale production of stable vesicles designed for enhanced transdermal antifungal drug delivery.

Ethanol injection method

The ethanol injection method is a straightforward and highly reproducible technique that relies on the spontaneous formation of liposomes. Fundamentally, this process involves rapidly injecting a lipid solution, completely dissolved in a water-miscible solvent such as ethanol, directly into a continuous aqueous phase under constant mechanical stirring [41].

This robust fundamental mechanism is frequently adapted for more complex formulations. For example, in the specific preparation of transethosomes (TELs), accurately weighed phospholipids, the active therapeutic drug, and selected surfactants are initially dissolved in ethanol at a controlled temperature of 35 °C. Following complete lipid dissolution, the designated edge activator and the aqueous phase are carefully introduced to this organic mixture. This critical addition is performed under continuous stirring in a controlled environment to effectively prevent the premature evaporation of the ethanol [123]. While this method is highly advantageous for rapid vesicle generation and is easily scalable, researchers must be aware that it frequently necessitates additional downstream processing steps, such as dialysis or rotary evaporation, to adequately remove any potentially toxic residual solvent from the final formulation [41].

Hot method

The hot method serves as an effective alternative approach for the formulation of specialized vesicular carriers, specifically utilizing elevated temperatures to facilitate the uniform dispersion of lipid components. In a representative application of this technique, Bhalaria and colleagues [2009] successfully produced advanced ethosomal carriers. Briefly, their protocol involves carefully adding ethanol to an aqueous suspension of phospholipids. This primary mixture is subsequently subjected to continuous magnetic stirring while being strictly maintained at a temperature of 40 °C.

Following this initial thermal mixing phase, the resulting crude dispersion typically requires advanced mechanical treatment to achieve the desired vesicle size and optimal homogeneity. Therefore, the heated formulation is routinely subjected to specific post-processing steps, such as sonication and high-pressure homogenization. These rigorous mechanical forces ensure the complete formation of highly uniform, nanosized ethosomes that are perfectly suited for transdermal drug delivery applications (Fig. 3).

Vortexing method

The vortexing method is a rapid, straightforward technique primarily utilized for preparing multilamellar liposomes (MLVs) in small-scale laboratory settings [41]. Standard protocols involve dissolving phospholipids in an organic solvent, evaporating the solvent to form a thin lipid film, and hydrating this film with an aqueous buffer under vigorous vortex mixing to induce spontaneous MLV formation. Alternatively, a phospholipid ethanolic mixture can be vortexed and sonicated until completely clear, followed by the careful addition of phosphate-buffered saline under swirling [103]. Because vortexing inherently yields a remarkably broad vesicle size distribution, secondary size reduction techniques, such as sonication or mechanical extrusion through polycarbonate filters, are typically required [103]. While highly cost-effective, this limited control over final particle size generally restricts the method to preliminary research rather than large-scale clinical applications [41].

Drug delivery systems against fungal infections

Niosomes and Spanlastics

Modern approaches to targeted antifungal delivery prominently feature niosomes, which are versatile lamellar vesicles formed by hydrating a mixture of cholesterol and non-ionic surfactants. These carriers are specifically formulated for topical, oral, vaginal mucosal, pulmonary, and intranasal administration. Functionally, niosomes provide sustained medication release, significantly enhance the bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs, and protect active substances from premature degradation. They can efficiently encapsulate potent antifungal agents, such as ketoconazole or econazole. For instance, nystatin encapsulated within niosomes demonstrates reduced nephrotoxicity and hepatotoxicity in in vivo models, alongside increased therapeutic effectiveness against C. albicans. Furthermore, niosomal formulations of ketoconazole exhibit an extended duration of action, solidifying their promise for advanced antifungal treatments [106, 130].

Building upon this foundational technology, spanlastics represent highly elastic, modified niosomes that incorporate specific edge activators into their structural bilayer. This critical chemical modification vastly improves their membrane permeability, enabling highly effective cellular entry. Consequently, these highly deformable vesicles have been successfully employed for specialized applications, including ocular drug delivery and the targeted topical treatment of deep-seated fungal infections like onychomycosis.

Microemulsions and nanoemulsions

Microemulsions, consisting of oil and water stabilized by surfactants, are highly efficient carriers that significantly enhance drug solubilization and epidermal penetration. For instance, incorporating terbinafine hydrochloride or voriconazole into microemulsion systems markedly improves both their aqueous solubility and overall antifungal efficacy [130].

Nanoemulsions similarly offer exceptional kinetic stability and effectively transport substantial quantities of poorly soluble therapeutics into deeper skin layers. Demonstrating this, formulated nystatin nanoemulsions display vastly superior antifungal activity compared to the free drug. Furthermore, while structurally distinct from lipid emulsions, inorganic carriers like silicon dioxide nanoparticles are frequently utilized alongside these systems to further augment localized antimicrobial efficacy and maximize overall drug loading capacity [130].

Sterosomes

Sterosomes represent advanced vesicular nanocarriers that fundamentally differ from conventional liposomes by completely lacking phospholipids. Instead, they are engineered using monoalkylated amphiphiles combined with a remarkably high sterol content, primarily cholesterol ranging from 50 to 75 percent [106].

By combining these sterols with single-chain amphiphiles, such as stearylamine, cetylpyridinium chloride, or saturated fatty acids, in precisely defined ratios, researchers can generate highly ordered lamellar structures or large unilamellar vesicles. These structural modifications offer superior functional stability over traditional liposomes. A defining feature of sterosomes is their inherent pH sensitivity. This unique property enables the controlled, targeted release of therapeutics based on the protonation state of their amphiphilic components, allowing antimicrobial-loaded sterosomes to exhibit highly potent activity both in vitro and in vivo [106].

Ufasomes

Ufasomes are unique lipid vesicles composed entirely of unsaturated fatty acids, including oleic acid, sodium oleate, and linoleic acid, specifically designed for lipophilic drug encapsulation [88, 106]. They assemble into stable bilayer structures strictly within a pH range of 7–9, depending entirely on the dynamic balance between ionized and non-ionized fatty acid species.

Crucially, these vesicles exhibit strong fusogenic properties that significantly enhance membrane fluidity. This characteristic facilitates rapid lipid exchange with the stratum corneum, ensuring highly efficient drug release and deep epidermal penetration. Because of their substantial permeability enhancement, inherent cost-effectiveness, and strong affinity for hair follicles, ufasomes represent highly promising carriers for topical, transdermal, and intranasal therapeutic applications [106].

Novasomes are advanced vesicular carriers specifically engineered to overcome the stability limitations of conventional liposomes [10, 106, 118, 127]. Structurally, they feature a unique multilamellar architecture containing up to seven distinct bilayers, allowing for the highly efficient simultaneous encapsulation of both hydrophilic and lipophilic therapeutics.

By incorporating a precise blend of surfactants, cholesterol, and free fatty acids, novasomes synergistically combine the best structural characteristics of liposomes, ufasomes, and niosomes. This structural hybrid yields exceptionally high entrapment efficiency and superior intrinsic stability. Remarkably, these vesicles maintain their structural integrity across an extreme pH range of 2–13 and withstand broad temperature fluctuations. Their specialized channel-like bilayer structures facilitate controlled, prolonged drug release, while their easily tailorable surface charge enhances targeted interactions with specific biological tissues [106].

Clinical and preclinical applications of functionalized lipid-based nanocarriers in antifungal therapy

Clinically approved liposomal antifungals

Entrapping antifungal therapeutics within liposomes significantly improves their epidermal penetration while maintaining a distinct lack of immunogenicity [15]. To achieve sustained and controlled localized release, these antimycotics are frequently incorporated into specialized liposomal gel systems. Currently, amphotericin B (AmB) remains the only antifungal agent commercially available as a clinically approved liposomal formulation [15].

Compared to the conventional free drug, AmB liposomes exhibit a markedly superior safety profile, characterized by substantially reduced systemic toxicity and fewer adverse side effects. Furthermore, these nanocarriers demonstrate high stability in physiological saline and provide prolonged bioavailability [105]. The primary commercial formulations, specifically AmBisome® and Fungisome®, successfully encapsulate AmB to deliver these extensive clinical advantages over traditional, unencapsulated therapies [105].

Liposomal and lipid-based formulations of azoles and other topical antifungals

To overcome the inherent limitations of conventional topical therapies, such as poor skin permeation and localized inflammation, a wide array of synthetic antifungal agents has been successfully incorporated into advanced lipid-based nanocarriers. These formulations, ranging from traditional liposomes to highly deformable transethosomes, utilize specific edge activators, biopolymers, and lipid compositions to precisely target dermal, epidermal, and even posterior ocular layers. Lipid-based nanocarriers in antifungal vaccination strategies

Costa-Barborsa and colleagues [47] developed an advanced lipid nanodelivery system incorporating recombinant Candida albicans chitinase 3 (Cht3) to serve as a highly targeted potential vaccine against fungal infections. Their research demonstrated that utilizing a specific formulation of dioctadecyldimethylammonium bromide combined with monoolein significantly amplified the host immune response.

Crucially, encapsulating the Cht3 antigen within these specialized liposomes not only improved cellular uptake by antigen-presenting cells but also drastically enhanced the overall safety profile of the immunization protocol. The Cht3-loaded liposomes successfully induced a robust Th1 and Th17-mediated cellular immune response. This targeted activation led to elevated production of specific antibodies capable of recognizing both the native and recombinant forms of the Cht3 protein, firmly establishing this nanocarrier system as a highly promising platform for the future clinical development of antifungal vaccines [47, 106].

Niosomal systems for systemic and targeted antifungal delivery

Niosomal carriers present a highly effective strategy for the systemic delivery of potent antifungal agents, particularly for immunocompromised individuals. To specifically combat severe C. albicans infections in vulnerable neutropenic patients, advanced nystatin (NYS) niosomes have been successfully developed [57]. To optimize the performance of these carriers, researchers systematically modified the fundamental lipid composition by incorporating Span 60 or Span 40 alongside cholesterol. Furthermore, stearylamine and dicetyl phosphate were strategically integrated into the bilayer to generate specific positive and negative surface charges, respectively.

Crucially, formulations possessing neutral and positive surface charges demonstrated the highest overall encapsulation efficiency coupled with optimal sustained release kinetics. Ultimately, these advanced niosomal carriers significantly enhanced systemic antifungal efficacy. They successfully delivered elevated, targeted drug concentrations to vital organs while simultaneously mitigating the severe nephrotoxic and hepatotoxic effects typically associated with free nystatin administration in vivo [57, 106].

Fungal infections represent a severe global health challenge, affecting over 35 million individuals annually and resulting in more than 1.5 million deaths. While systemic pathogens such as Candida, Aspergillus, and Cryptococcus are major contributors to this high mortality, superficial cutaneous infections also demand innovative therapeutic strategies. To address these clinical challenges, advanced lipid carriers, specifically ufasomes, have been engineered to optimize targeted topical drug delivery. Liposomal systems for ocular fungal infections

Effective treatment of ocular fungal infections remains a profound clinical challenge due to the complex anatomy of the eye, inherently limited drug permeability, and the rapid physiological elimination of topically applied formulations [24]. Traditional eye drops notoriously suffer from low bioavailability and short precorneal residence times, factors that actively contribute to therapeutic failure and emerging antimycotic resistance [1, 97]. Consequently, developing advanced ophthalmic drug delivery systems is an urgent clinical priority.

Severe ocular mycoses, such as fungal keratitis, are predominantly driven by aggressive pathogens including Fusarium spp., Candida glabrata, and Aspergillus species. To combat these sight threatening infections, advanced nanovesicular carriers, encompassing liposomes, niosomes, and ethosomes, provide highly effective strategies for targeted ocular drug delivery [134].

Specifically, liposomal systems have demonstrated remarkable potential in overcoming intrinsic ocular barriers. By successfully encapsulating both hydrophilic and lipophilic antifungal agents, these highly biocompatible nanocarriers drastically enhance drug solubility, prolong corneal residence time, and actively drive therapeutic penetration into deeper ocular tissues. Ultimately, this controlled, sustained release profile maximizes localized antifungal efficacy while safely minimizing both local and systemic toxicity [97].

Targeted liposomal antifungal therapies

Ambati and colleagues [14] engineered an advanced targeted delivery system utilizing two recombinant DC-SIGN constructs (DCS12 and DCS78) created by fusing the carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) with distinct neck repeat regions. These highly specific constructs were successfully conjugated to a lipid carrier and incorporated into PEGylated, amphotericin B-loaded liposomes to form functionalized nanovesicles (DCS12-AmB-LLs and DCS78-AmB-LLs).

Comprehensive functional evaluations demonstrated that these DC-SIGN targeted liposomes exhibited significantly enhanced binding to the complex fungal exopolysaccharides of Aspergillus fumigatus, C. albicans, and Cryptococcus neoformans when directly compared to non-targeted controls. Specifically, the DCS12-AmB-LL formulation displayed superior receptor binding and potent in vitro fungicidal activity, significantly outperforming the DCS78 variant.

Subsequent in vivo studies utilizing murine models of both invasive candidiasis and pulmonary aspergillosis confirmed this enhanced clinical efficacy, revealing a substantial reduction in the overall fungal burden within target organs compared to conventional therapies. Ultimately, incorporating the DC-SIGN CRD significantly amplifies the targeted therapeutic effectiveness of amphotericin B liposomes, offering a highly promising strategy to minimize systemic toxicity and successfully eradicate drug-tolerant fungal pathogens [14].

Stealth liposomes in systemic fungal infections

To combat severe systemic infections, de Oliveira Kocerginsky and colleagues [50] evaluated the therapeutic efficacy and safety profile of ciclopirox olamine-loaded stealth liposomes (CPO-LipoS) against C. neoformans. Their optimized formulation exhibited highly uniform characteristics, featuring a mean vesicle size of 101.4 ± 0.7 nm, a precise polydispersity index of 0.307, and an exceptional drug encapsulation efficiency of 96.4 ± 0.9 percent.In rigorous in vivo testing, immunosuppressed murine models treated with CPO-LipoS (0.5 mg/kg/day for 14 days) demonstrated a complete absence of hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, ultimately achieving a remarkable 100 percent survival rate. Furthermore, the stealth liposomal formulation significantly reduced C. neoformans fungal burden across critical organs, including the spleen, liver, lungs, and brain [50]. Translational and regulatory challenges in liposomal antifungal therapy

Global landscape of liposomal clinical trials

To fully understand the translational trajectory of lipid-based nanocarriers, it is crucial to examine the current clinical development landscape of the 83,316 actively registered clinical trials globally, 511 of which are specifically investigating advanced liposomal formulations (Table 4). The therapeutic agents currently under rigorous clinical evaluation span a diverse array of medical applications, predominantly encompassing anticancer drugs, specialized analgesics, immunomodulators, and critical antifungal therapies.

Analyzing the clinical progression of these 511 targeted trials reveals a highly robust developmental pipeline. Currently, 121 candidates are successfully advancing through phase III testing, 236 are undergoing phase II efficacy evaluations, 120 are in phase I safety trials, and 6 remain in early phase I preliminary studies [103].

Limited clinical adoption of advanced deformable lipid nanocarriers

While conventional liposomal amphotericin B (AmB) has successfully navigated regulatory approval and is widely utilized in clinical practice [30, 109, 110], the translation of advanced, deformable lipid nanocarriers into the clinic remains severely limited [77]. As detailed in Table 5, nearly all registered clinical trials evaluate conventional liposomal AmB formulations, primarily AmBisome® or its direct bioequivalent products. Crucially, there are currently zero clinical trials examining deformable, ultra-elastic, ethanol-modified, or actively targeted liposomal antifungal systems.

This lack of active trials highlights a profound translational gap between preclinical innovation and actual clinical application. Several critical factors contribute to this barrier, including complex regulatory approval pathways, severe manufacturing bottlenecks associated with the large-scale production of advanced lipid nanocarriers, and substantial market hurdles such as exorbitant development costs and intense competition from established treatments [59, 110, 142, 150]. Furthermore, transparency remains an ongoing issue, as many completed trials fail to publicly report their findings (e.g., NCT00148148, NCT00386997, NCT06983665, NCT00177710). The vast majority of current studies strictly focus on pharmacokinetics and bioequivalence, strongly indicating an industry preference for utilizing established nanocarrier platforms rather than investing in novel formulation architectures [30, 59, 109, 142].

Regulatory oversight and approval barriers

Major regulatory agencies, including the FDA and EMA, maintain strict oversight over liposomal drug development. However, their existing guidelines are primarily tailored toward evaluating conventional liposomes [60, 142]. Consequently, these regulatory frameworks frequently lack specific evaluation pathways for highly deformable systems containing permeation enhancers or organic solvents such as ethanol.

Furthermore, advanced functionalized carriers frequently suffer from significant physical manufacturing challenges, notably poor batch-to-batch reproducibility and premature drug leakage during storage. These specific structural instabilities drastically increase overall development costs and routinely delay, or entirely halt, the regulatory approval process [7].

Manufacturing and scale-up challenges

The industrial-scale transition of highly deformable liposomes demands rigorous control over critical quality attributes, including final particle size, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency, and inherent membrane deformability [150]. Although advanced continuous manufacturing techniques, such as microfluidization, significantly enhance batch reproducibility, they simultaneously introduce substantial operational complexity [109].

Primary manufacturing hurdles involve maintaining precise vesicle elasticity, efficiently removing toxic residual solvents, preserving the delicate surfactant and lipid balance, and strictly adhering to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards [30]. Because minor manufacturing variations can drastically alter in vivo pharmacokinetics and clinical efficacy, implementing robust quality-by-design approaches is essential for successful commercialization [117].

Stability and long-term storage concerns

Guaranteeing long-term physical and chemical stability remains a critical bottleneck in the commercialization of highly deformable vesicles. Specific degradation pathways, including phospholipid oxidation, lipid hydrolysis, premature ethanol evaporation, and surfactant destabilization, can rapidly compromise structural integrity and cause catastrophic drug leakage during storage [100]. Unlike extensively characterized conventional liposomal amphotericin B, these novel ultra-deformable systems frequently lack comprehensive, long-term stability data. Consequently, executing exhaustive shelf-life evaluations is a mandatory prerequisite for securing regulatory approval and ensuring patient safety [30, 138, 150].

Economic and market barriers in antifungal drug development

The broader landscape of antifungal drug development is severely hindered by substantial economic and market barriers, including limited commercial incentives, a perceived niche market size, prohibitively high production costs, and the escalating global threat of antimycotic resistance [29, 63, 110, 153]. While highly targeted liposomal systems, such as DC-SIGN functionalized amphotericin B carriers, demonstrate remarkable promise in rigorous preclinical models, their translation into active clinical trials remains highly restricted [14, 109, 150]. Successfully bridging this formidable translational gap will require early and proactive regulatory engagement, the establishment of standardized preclinical evaluation protocols, and robust, sustained collaboration between public research institutions and private pharmaceutical sectors [110, 153].

Discussion

The escalating global burden of fungal infections, coupled with a severely limited arsenal of available antimycotics, urgently necessitates the development of advanced drug delivery platforms [145]. Liposome-based vesicular nanocarriers, particularly their highly deformable variants, offer a robust strategy to significantly enhance antifungal efficacy while simultaneously mitigating systemic toxicity [125]. Extensive research demonstrates that strategically altering vesicular composition directly improves both epidermal drug penetration and overall therapeutic outcomes [39, 44, 68]. Ultra-deformable liposomal systems, specifically transfersomes and ethosomes, are engineered to optimize topical therapies by maximizing skin penetration [23, 31]. However, these platforms frequently encounter significant translational hurdles, most notably physicochemical instability and the potential for severe localized skin irritation [125, 150].

Mechanistically, while transfersomes rely on the inclusion of specific edge activators to achieve mechanical deformability [31, 72, 81], ethosomes utilize high ethanol concentrations to actively fluidize stratum corneum lipids, a process that inherently increases the risk of epidermal irritation [94, 147]. Building upon these primary carriers, transethosomes synergistically combine ethanol with edge activators to achieve superior transdermal permeation in experimental models [116, 129]. Nevertheless, their highly complex structural composition can severely compromise batch reproducibility and long-term stability [125].

Similarly, invasomes incorporate specific terpenes to function as potent penetration enhancers [53]; however, terpene-induced irritation and significant formulation variability remain pressing clinical concerns. Ultimately, despite highly promising preclinical data, the definitive clinical utility of ultra-deformable vesicular systems remains uncertain. Successfully bridging this gap will require the rigorous, standardized evaluation of long-term safety, physicochemical stability, and in vivo therapeutic effectiveness [125, 150].

Advantages of different vesicular systems

Enhanced skin penetration and local bioavailability

Elastic vesicular carriers, such as transfersomes, ethosomes, invasomes, menthosomes, and transethosomes, penetrate the stratum corneum significantly more effectively than conventional liposomes [33, 103]. This superior penetration is driven by the strategic incorporation of specific membrane-destabilizing enhancers, which actively increase bilayer fluidity and disrupt rigid skin lipid packing [107, 125, 154]. Crucially, these targeted permeation enhancers allow the vesicles to undergo reversible deformation. Driven by transdermal hydration gradients, this flexibility enables them to squeeze through narrow intercellular lipid pathways without experiencing structural rupture or premature drug leakage [31, 33]. Consequently, these ultra-deformable carriers dramatically enhance drug accumulation directly at the infection site, making them particularly advantageous for delivering poorly soluble antifungal agents like itraconazole and ketoconazole (KET) [13, 23, 120].

Furthermore, while these systems are primarily engineered for dermatological and transdermal applications, highly adaptable carriers like transethosomes successfully deliver therapeutics across the vaginal mucosa [124]. Demonstrating this potent localized efficacy, recent research evaluating topical azole delivery for Candida infections revealed that advanced deformable carriers achieved an exceptional 97% cumulative release of KET over 8 h. Ultimately, these functionalized vesicles vastly outperform conventional liposomes by providing improved drug entrapment, maximizing local bioavailability, and significantly reducing off-target systemic exposure [31, 36, 77, 120].

Reduced systemic toxicity and improved safety

Vesicular carriers drastically mitigate the severe systemic exposure and subsequent dose-limiting toxicities, namely nephrotoxicity and hepatotoxicity, commonly associated with potent antifungal agents like amphotericin B (AmB), voriconazole, and nystatin [5, 57, 84]. By fundamentally altering pharmacokinetic distribution and restricting free drug circulation, lipid formulations preserve critical antifungal potency while enabling safely escalated therapeutic dosing profiles [5, 125].

This profound safety advantage is highly conserved across multiple administration routes. For instance, vesicular systems significantly reduce local tissue irritation and unwanted systemic absorption during specialized ophthalmic delivery [84]. Similarly, in vivo preclinical models demonstrate that the niosomal encapsulation of nystatin actively lowers organ toxicity through highly controlled release mechanisms [57].

Furthermore, integrating advanced targeted and stealth functionalization (e.g., PEGylation) maximizes this safety profile by prolonging systemic circulation time and actively directing the therapeutic payload strictly to the site of infection. This sophisticated targeting strategy, extensively leveraged in oncology to exploit specific pathological microenvironments, is highly translatable to optimizing and revolutionizing systemic antifungal therapy [12, 40, 140].

Versatility and adaptability

Vesicular systems demonstrate exceptional versatility, facilitating a wide array of targeted administration routes, including topical, transdermal, ocular, pulmonary, and systemic delivery [124]. Among these varied platforms, transethosomes represent a highly advanced structural architecture. By strategically combining ethanol, essential phospholipids, and specific edge activators, they achieve unprecedented membrane deformability. This unique structural synergy allows them to efficiently traverse the stratum corneum to deliver active therapeutics into significantly deeper tissue layers. Furthermore, these dynamic carriers can simultaneously encapsulate highly diverse payloads, ranging from small synthetic molecules and therapeutic proteins to complex phytochemicals, effectively enhancing the stability and transdermal penetration of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic substances [124].

Beyond basic encapsulation, advanced surface functionalization strategies, such as DC-SIGN modified liposomes, enable highly specific pathogen recognition, thereby maximizing antifungal selectivity and overall clinical efficacy [14].

Additionally, hybrid platforms, such as sophisticated liposome-in-hydrogel architectures, successfully integrate the targeted drug-loading capabilities of liposomes with the prolonged, sustained-release kinetics of polymeric hydrogels. Ultimately, these advanced combinatorial systems provide highly effective therapeutic solutions specifically tailored for managing severe chronic and recurrent fungal infections [27].

Despite their significant therapeutic advantages, the successful clinical translation of advanced vesicular carriers is heavily impeded by several critical formulation bottlenecks. These challenges primarily concern physicochemical instability, physiological degradation, and manufacturing reproducibility [95]. Translational and regulatory challenges

Scale-up and manufacturing

The successful industrial translation of vesicular nanocarriers demands advanced engineering, rigorous quality control, and substantial financial investment [8, 125, 150, 159]. Traditional lab-scale formulation methods, such as thin-film hydration and sonication, inherently struggle with scalability and frequently yield highly heterogeneous vesicle populations [8]. Because even minor compositional variations can drastically alter clinical outcomes, manufacturing consistency is absolutely vital. This requires extremely tight control over critical physicochemical parameters, such as particle size and encapsulation efficiency, to ensure strict compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards [66, 125, 150, 159].

To overcome these severe manufacturing bottlenecks, advanced fabrication techniques like microfluidization offer significantly greater parametric control, albeit with increased operational complexity and production costs (Fig. 4) [82, 98, 149]. Recently, innovative continuous manufacturing approaches have emerged to successfully bridge this translational gap. For instance, Phatak et al. [111] introduced in-line homogenization as a highly scalable, organic solvent-free method for fabricating structurally uniform, bilayered unilamellar vesicles. This continuous processing approach efficiently produced both niosome-like (NioTherms) and liposome-like (ThermoSomes) carriers loaded with either hydrophobic (posaconazole) or hydrophilic (dexamethasone sodium phosphate) therapeutics, representing a major advancement toward viable, commercial-scale nanocarrier production.

Regulatory considerations

Navigating the regulatory landscape for liposomal formulations requires exhaustive physicochemical characterization and rigorous bioequivalence demonstrations, strictly adhering to established Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidelines [141, 142]. The 2018 FDA guidance explicitly underscores the inherent complexity of liposomal products, which are heavily dependent on specific lipid compositions and precise manufacturing processes [143]. Consequently, regulatory bodies mandate the stringent control of critical quality attributes (CQAs), comprehensive stability testing, and meticulous evaluations of product comparability, as even minor process deviations can profoundly alter pharmacokinetics and toxicity [146].

These regulatory hurdles are particularly pronounced for highly complex formulations, such as polyphenol-loaded nanocarriers for psoriasis (PsO). Such systems frequently struggle with low aqueous solubility, severe batch-to-batch variability of botanical extracts, and a lack of robust human pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data, severely limiting their direct comparability with synthetic agents [52, 61, 115, 132].

Furthermore, the 2021 FDA guidance on Abbreviated New Drug Application (ANDA) submissions establishes a rigorous framework for demonstrating bioequivalence in complex generics. It explicitly dictates that relying solely on total plasma drug concentrations is insufficient. Instead, comprehensive analyses must accurately quantify the ratio of encapsulated to free drug fractions alongside detailed in vitro release kinetics [144]. While not specifically written for Biologics License Applications (BLAs), these foundational scientific principles remain highly applicable across licensing pathways.

Ultimately, this dual regulatory framework illustrates the immense difficulty of guaranteeing manufacturing consistency and in vivo performance. Consequently, advanced delivery systems, particularly highly deformable or ethanol-modified vesicles, face substantially greater regulatory scrutiny and approval barriers compared to traditional formulations like conventional liposomal amphotericin B [51, 137, 143].

Limited clinical translation

Currently, amphotericin B (AmB) remains the sole antifungal agent successfully translated into an approved liposomal formulation [5, 131]. The enduring clinical dominance of liposomal AmB, specifically AmBisome®, is rooted in its highly stable drug encapsulation, targeted delivery, and extensively validated pharmacokinetic profile. This established formulation offers proven broad-spectrum activity, standardized dosing regimens, and significantly reduced nephrotoxicity, which collectively command high regulatory and clinician confidence [5, 131].

In stark contrast, the clinical translation of novel antifungal nanocarriers is severely hindered by profound systemic barriers rather than a mere lack of technological innovation [110]. The inherently small antifungal market, which primarily serves immunocompromised populations, offers limited commercial incentives for pharmaceutical development. Furthermore, conducting clinical trials in this space is exceptionally costly and logistically complex due to the high diversity of target pathogens. The very existence of established, highly effective therapies like liposomal AmB inadvertently elevates the regulatory approval threshold for emerging treatments [110].

Consequently, while advanced lipid formulations demonstrate remarkable preclinical promise, they critically lack the long-term safety data, large-scale clinical trials, and standardized production protocols required to bridge this translational gap. Overcoming these entrenched commercial and regulatory obstacles will necessitate robust, collaborative research initiatives and sustained investment to directly support the World Health Organization (WHO) priorities for critical antifungal innovation [26, 153].

Economic and market barriers

Compared to major therapeutic areas such as oncology and cardiovascular medicine, the global antifungal market remains relatively niche, fundamentally limiting the commercial incentives required for large-scale investment in innovative drug delivery platforms [110]. Furthermore, exorbitant development costs, highly complex clinical trial logistics, and fierce competition from established standard-of-care therapies like liposomal amphotericin B collectively suppress industry motivation to aggressively pursue and commercialize next-generation vesicular formulations.

Conclusions

Lipid-based nanocarriers, particularly conventional liposomes and their highly ultra-deformable variants, represent a transformative frontier in the evolution of antifungal therapy [103, 125]. Established liposomal formulations of amphotericin B, such as AmBisome® and Fungisome®, have already revolutionized clinical care by significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy while fundamentally mitigating dose-limiting systemic toxicity [5, 131]. Building upon this established clinical success, advanced deformable vesicular systems, including transfersomes, ethosomes, and invasomes, actively optimize targeted drug delivery. By maximizing membrane elasticity, localized skin permeation, and overall drug entrapment efficiency, these dynamic carriers effectively overcome the profound limitations of traditional topical therapies [31, 79, 103].

Rigorous preclinical evaluations consistently demonstrate that advanced lipid nanocarriers drastically elevate the bioavailability and targeted antifungal activity of diverse antimycotics, ranging from azoles to polyenes, while simultaneously minimizing adverse physiological effects [15, 130]. Furthermore, highly functionalized liposomes show immense potential for executing pathogen-specific delivery and orchestrating advanced immunomodulatory strategies. However, actual clinical translation remains strictly confined to conventional, first-generation formulations. This stark reality is driven by formidable regulatory complexities, severe manufacturing bottlenecks, critical concerns regarding long-term physicochemical stability, and substantial economic market barriers [110, 143, 150]

Currently, there is a profound absence of registered clinical trials investigating ultra-deformable or actively targeted antifungal liposomes, highlighting a massive translational gap between experimental preclinical innovation and routine clinical practice. To successfully propel these next-generation therapies from the laboratory bench to the patient bedside, the scientific and regulatory communities must collaboratively establish standardized evaluation protocols, pioneer scalable continuous manufacturing processes, and aggressively foster sustained public and private sector partnerships. Only through this unified, multidisciplinary approach can the global management of severe fungal infections be truly modernized.

Future perspectives

To effectively manage the escalating global threat of fungal infections and successfully accelerate the clinical translation of advanced nanocarriers, the field must establish harmonized regulatory standards, implement robust quality-controlled manufacturing protocols, and prioritize early engagement with regulatory agencies [110, 143, 150]. Furthermore, actively strengthening public and private investment partnerships is essential to bridge the current translational gap.

Remarkable advances in broader nanomedicine, particularly within the highly developed fields of oncology and biomaterials, offer invaluable translational insights for next-generation antifungal drug delivery [125, 126, 138]. Moving forward, several highly promising technological approaches possess the potential to revolutionize this space. The development of smart nanocarriers, specifically stimuli-responsive vesicles, will allow for the active release of therapeutic payloads directly in response to specific pathological microenvironments [125]. Additionally, engineering advanced hybrid matrices, such as liposome-in-hydrogel systems, provides a sophisticated mechanism for the prolonged, localized, and sustained release of complex antifungal regimens [125].

To drastically improve targeted delivery to deeper epidermal and dermal layers, future methodologies must also integrate vesicular drug delivery with advanced physical permeation techniques, such as iontophoresis or sonophoresis [124]. Finally, coating ultra-deformable carriers like transethosomes with specific targeting ligands, including carbohydrates, will maximize pathogen recognition, enable diverse administration routes, and directly support emerging theranostic applications. Ultimately, sustainable financial investment and relentless translational research are the mandatory prerequisites required to successfully transform these highly experimental platforms into routine, highly effective clinical solutions.
