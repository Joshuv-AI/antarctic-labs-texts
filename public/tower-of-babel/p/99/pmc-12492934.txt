Biosynthesis of tauro-ursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Authors: Seonyun Moon, Michael J Smanski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) is a microbial bile acid known for its diverse yet still largely unexplored biological activities. Traditionally used in Asian medicine, TUDCA and the taurine-free form, ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), share similar therapeutic properties. Recent studies suggest that TUDCA is a promising lead compound for developing neuroprotective agents to prevent neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease and Huntington’s disease. However, sustainable and ethical sources for large-scale TUDCA production remain unavailable. To address this, we are engineering a yeast-based microbial platform capable of producing TUDCA via fermentation. Here we report strains capable of converting the more widely available primary bile acid, chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) into TUDCA. This was achieved by introducing heterologous genes enabling taurine conjugation and taurine biosynthesis, providing a sustainable and scalable approach for TUDCA production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02829-3.Keywords: Microbial bile acids, Metabolic pathway engineering, Genetic design optimization

Introduction

TUDCA is a major component of bear bile, traditionally used as a potent medicine in Asia for over 3,000 years [1]. It is a taurine-conjugated form of UDCA, sharing similar biological activities while exhibiting enhanced hydrophilicity due to the presence of the taurine group [2, 3]. Both TUDCA and its precursor, UDCA, are well known for their strong therapeutic effects and have long been used to support liver health. Notably, UDCA is the only FDA-approved treatment for primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC), a chronic autoimmune disorder that causes severe liver damage [4].

More importantly, recent studies have revealed the remarkable neuroprotective effects of TUDCA against multiple chronic neurodegenerative disorders, including Huntington’s disease (HD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [5–7]. These severe neurological conditions cause irreversible and fatal neuronal dysfunction, leading to behavioral and cognitive impairments [5, 8, 9]. As life expectancy increases and the elderly population grows, neurodegenerative diseases are becoming an increasingly significant societal concern. Despite substantial efforts in drug development, effective treatments are lacking. There remains a high demand more efficient therapeutic approaches to treat these devastating diseases.

TUDCA has demonstrated neuroprotective functions in different disease models of HD, PD, and AD by reducing the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates that induce cellular oxidative stress and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [5, 9–11], thereby inhibiting mitochondiral dysfunction and neuronal cell apoptosis [12–15]. These findings suggest that TUDCA is a promising lead compound for the development of novel neuroprotective agents.

Although TUDCA and UDCA share nearly identical biological functions, taurine conjugation slightly modifies the physicochemical properties of UDCA, making TUDCA a more physiologically active form to better function as a therapeutic agent. This modification reduces toxicity and increases the solubility of bile acids, leading to enhanced bioavailability compared to the unconjugated form [16]. Furthermore, TUDCA exhibits improved antioxidant properties compared to UDCA [17], which could further enhance its ability to alleviate cellular stress and prevent cell apoptosis. These advantages likely contribute to TUDCA being more commonly used than UDCA to investigate neuroprotective actions in animal models across most studies.

However, several limitations hinder drug development efforts for TUDCA. Only trace amounts of this compound are naturally produced in most animals, with UDCA or TUDCA constituting less than 3% of total bile acids in human bile [18]. Bear bile is known to be the only natural source that produces abundant amounts of TUDCA, comprising approximately 76% of total bile acids [1]. This has led to controversy due to the ethical concerns surrounding the sacrifice of large numbers of bears in bear farms for bile extraction. While chemical synthesis of TUDCA has been explored, it often relies on toxic reagents, such as diethyl phosphorocyanidate (DEPC), which pose environmental hazards [19]. Moreover, complete synthesis is particularly challenging due to the difficulty in replicating the complex ring structures of the compound.

To overcome these limitations, microbial and enzymatic approaches have been developed to produce TUDCA or UDCA from more readily available bile acids. Alternative bile sources from livestock, such as chickens, cattle, and pigs, contain lower amounts of TUDCA but are rich in primary bile acids like CDCA and its conjugates, which can serve as precursors for TUDCA production. More than 30% of human bile consists of CDCA [20], while over 80% of chicken bile is composed of CDCA or TCDCA [21]. CDCA and TCDCA can be converted into UDCA and TUDCA, respectively, through epimerization reactions catalyzed by microbial enzymes, 7- and 7-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases (HSDHs) [22].

Several groups have advanced microbial and enzymatic TUDCA production. The Wang group was the first to achieve 62.49% TUDCA conversion from TCDCA using an in vitro double-enzyme-coupled system [23]. Shi et al. engineered Escherichia coli for in vivo synthesis, producing approximately 1.61 g/L of TUDCA from TCDCA ( 49.8%) [19]. Xu et al. further improved TUDCA titers in E. coli by at least tenfold, optimizing the fermentation process with response surface methodology [24]. The Zhang group achieved kilogram-scale UDCA production from CDCA through large-scale enzymatic synthesis through a 500-L fermenter fermentation [25]. Additionally, the Wang group engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae to convert TCDCA in chicken bile powder into TUDCA using crude extracts [26] (Fig. 1).

Most previous studies have focused on optimizing the epimerization reaction by expressing the two responsible enzymes either in vitro or in vivo to convert TCDCA into TUDCA. However, in this study, we propose integrating taurine conjugation into the epimerization pathway to enable direct utilization of CDCA for TUDCA production. To achieve this, we expressed the human bile acid-CoA:amino acid N-acyltransferase (BAAT) gene to catalyze taurine conjugation [27]. We selected oxidoreductases that exploit endogenous pools of redox cofactors in yeast [28]. Furthermore, we engineered taurine biosynthesis for a sustainable taurine supply by incorporating two carp genes, cysteine dioxygenase 1 (CDO1) and cysteine sulfinic acid decarboxylase (CSAD) [29].

The resulting strain features three distinct functional modules: (i) Epimerization, (ii) Taurine conjugation and (iii) Taurine biosynthesis. To our knowledge, this is the first study of engineering sustainable taurine conjugation of bile acids in a microbial host and an important step towards engineering the complete biosynthesis of TUDCA via fermentation.

Results

UDCA epimerization

The model yeast strain S. cerevisiae was selected as a host organism because its production of sterols (e.g., ergosterol) will make it suitable for the eventual total biosynthesis of cholesterol-derived bile acids [30]. For this study, we focused first on building the metabolic capabilities in yeast for the processing of primary bile acid to bioactive microbial bile acids.

To engineer an epimerization module for UDCA production from CDCA, we expressed two microbial enzymes in yeast: 7-alpha-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (7-HSDH) and 7-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (7-HSDH). These enzymes catalyze the epimerization reaction by sequentially oxidizing and reducing CDCA to form UDCA (Fig. 2a). Various gut microbial species encode homologs of these enzymes with different cofactor preferences and catalytic activities [28]. Cofactor preference is particularly critical, as each homolog utilizes either NAD+ or NADP+, which have distinct intracellular concentrations and distributions in yeast. In the yeast cytosol, the oxidized form NAD+ is dominant in the NAD pool (i.e., [NAD+]>[NADH]), whereas NADPH is the predominant form in the NADP pool (i.e., [NADPH]>[NADP+]) [31, 32]. Thus, oxidation reactions are more favorable with NAD+-preferring homologs. Similarly, reduction reactions requiring NADPH as an electron donor are more favorable than NADH-requiring reactions. Based on this, we selected and expressed homologs from E. coli (K12) and Ruminococcus gnavus in yeast [33, 34].

UDCA production from CDCA. a Enzymatic epimerization reactions. b Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) chromatographs highlighting of UDCA conversion from CDCA. c UDCA production from varied CDCA feeding concentrations (0.0–2.0 g/L).

Engineered yeast strains were then subjected to fermentation with varying concentrations of CDCA to assess UDCA production. Both CDCA and UDCA were detected via mass spectrometry, with mass transitions of m/z 391.3 391.3 and retention times of 3.6 min and 1.8 min, respectively. When CDCA was supplemented in fermentation medium, we observed 93.0% convertion to UDCA (Fig. 2b). As the CDCA feeding concentration increased, a greater proportion of unconverted CDCA remained. Over 90% of CDCA was efficiently converted into UDCA at concentrations up to 0.25 g/L, while at the highest feeding concentration of 2 g/L, approximately 51% of CDCA was converted (Fig. 2c). UDCA production in these growth conditions, which yielded cell densities of approximately OD600 = 23.0, approached an asymptote of 1 g/L UDCA production. Fed-batch cultures in bioreactors capable of higher cell densities were not performed.

Taurine conjugation

Taurine conjugation requires a two-step process, (i) activating the carboxyl group as a coenzyme-A thioester, and (ii) amide bond formation via nucleophilic attack of the thioester by the amino group of taurine. To acheive this, we expressed one of two different acyl-CoA ligases (SLC27A5 from human or BaiB from gut microbe, Clostridium scindens) with BAAT from human [27, 35–37]. These enzymes catalyze the conjugation of CDCA or UDCA with taurine to form TCDCA or TUDCA (Fig. 3a). To compare the efficiency of taurine conjugation, we measured TCDCA titers in yeast cultures supplemented with 100 mg/L CDCA and 32 mg/L taurine, which is roughly a 1:1 molar ratio (Fig. 3b). The maximum TCDCA titer of ± 3.4 mg/L was achieved when the microbial CoA ligase, BaiB, was expressed.

TCDCA titers with different CoA ligases. a Enzymatic steps required to convert CDCA to TCDCA. b TCDCA production with different acyl-CoA ligases and culture supplementations. c LC-MS (selected ion) chromatographs of acyl-CoA ligase experiments.

BAAT is known to show promiscuity towards the amine-containing substrate [38, 39]. One possible explanation for the low titer of TUDCA relative to fed substrate concentrations is intracellular competition for the amino-containing substrate. Indeed, we observed glyco-UDCA (GUDCA) at 20 mg/L despite no glycine supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 1). TCDCA/TUDCA and GUDCA were identified via mass transitions of m/z 498.3 80 and m/z 448.3 74, respectively, with retention times of 2.37 min (TCDCA), 1.47 min (TUDCA), and 1.46 min (GUDCA).

Taurine biosynthesis

In addition to supplementing taurine exogenously, we engineered yeast strains to biosynthesize taurine by expressing Cdo1 and Csad from Cyprinus carpio [29]. These enzymes convert cysteine into taurine, providing an endogenous source of taurine for TUDCA biosynthesis (Fig. 4a). Taurine production from intracellular cysteine was confirmed by LC-MS, with yields of 2–3 mg/L. Hypotaurine was detected only at low levels, likely due to its spontaneous conversion into taurine (Fig. 4b). Taurine and hypotaurine were detected with mass transitions of m/z 80.0 80.0 and m/z 64.0 64.0, respectively, with retention times of 1.7 min (taurine) and 1.8 min (hypotaurine).

Taurine and hypotaurine production in engineered yeast strains. a Biosynthetic pathway from cysteine to taurine. b LC-MS (selected ion) chromatographs of yeast expressing enzymes Cdo1 and Csad or an empty vector control. Standards of hypotaurine and taurine show similar retention times but unique m/z ions, which were used to distinguish between the two molecules.

When taurine biosynthesis was integrated with the two other modules, epimerization and taurine conjugation, the engineered strains were able to synthesize TUDCA from CDCA (100 mg/L) without the need for external taurine supplementation. Notably, TUDCA production reached approximately 20.5 mg/L, which was four times higher than that achieved with external taurine feeding only (Fig. 5).

TUDCA production with different mechanisms of taurine provision. LC-MS (selected ion) chromatographs of TUDCA standards or cell culture extracts with no taurine feeding (left), external taurine feeding (center), or in vivo taurine supply (right). CDCA was fed to all of the groups. The recombinant strains at right are designed to produce taurine in vivo.

Discussion

Our study successfully demonstrated the biosynthesis of UDCA and its taurine-conjugated form, TUDCA, in yeast, achieving proof-of-concept TUDCA production at 15–20 mg/L. Notably, this is the first study to engineer yeast for the complete in vivo synthesis of UDCA from CDCA by expressing epimerization genes, achieving gram-per-liter-scale bioconversion. There are several advantages to biomanufacturing TUDCA in yeast versus bacteria. Yeast naturally produce sterol lipids that can be redirected towards cholesterol biosynthesis with a small number of genetic modifications [30, 40]. Additionally, a yeast platform minimizes the risk of endotoxin presence in the final product. While the Zhang group previously attempted yeast-based TUDCA synthesis their approach ultimately relied on crude cell extracts for in vitro bioconversion, distinguishing it from our fully in vivo system [25]. Despite the structural constraints of the yeast cell wall and subcellular compartmentalization, our engineered strain achieved in vivo UDCA titers comparable to those reported in E. coli. Furthermore, the significant production of UDCA, despite external CDCA supplementation, suggests that both CDCA and UDCA can diffuse freely across the yeast cell wall, indicating no apparent structural limitation on transport.

A key innovation of this study is the successful expression of the human BAAT gene in yeast, enabling the conjugation of bile acids with taurine or glycine. Previous efforts have focused on engineering biotransformation either between conjugated bile acids (e.g., TCDCA to TUDCA) or between unconjugated bile acids (e.g., CDCA to UDCA). However, to our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of a microbial host engineered to convert an unconjugated bile acid into its conjugated form. The ability to synthesize TUDCA in yeast marks a significant advancement in developing scalable approaches for conjugated bile acid production, which could be valuable for structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies of UDCA. Amino acid conjugates of UDCA, including TUDCA, hold significant therapeutic potential, and further optimization of this pathway could facilitate the development of novel bile acid derivatives with enhanced bioactivity.

We observed much great activity with the acyl-CoA ligase BaiB compared to SLC27A5. Given that BAAT localizes to the cytosol, it is likely that BAAT has more direct access to BaiB, which is also cytosolic, compared to SLC27A5, which localizes to the ER or cell membranes. This highlights the importance of co-localizing pathway enzymes to enhance metabolic efficiency.

Despite these achievements, a major challenge remains: the conversion efficiency of TUDCA was significantly lower than that of UDCA. While UDCA production exceeded 1 g/L with CDCA feeding, TUDCA titers remained at approximately 20 mg/L. Several factors may account for the lower efficiency of TUDCA production. The additional steps required for taurine biosynthesis and conjugation may be less efficient than the CDCA-to-UDCA epimerization module and could constitute rate-limiting steps.

In addition, as much as 20 mg/L of GUDCA was also produced without any glycine supplementation. This unintended side reaction could represent another significant factor contributing to the reduced yield of TUDCA, as it may compete for substrate or enzymatic activity. This possibility can be further explored by comparing TUDCA titers produced by diverse BAAT homologs from multiple species, as some - such as those from certain primates and rodents - are known to exclusively utilize taurine over glycine [41, 42]. It would be worthwhile to investigate whether such exclusive taurine specificity results in higher TUDCA production. Such comparative studies may also enable tuning of product selectivity, TUDCA versus GUDCA, by employing BAAT homologs with differing substrate preferences for taurine or glycine.

An additional contributing factor that may be of interest for future investigation is the impact of taurine conjugation on membrane permeability. Our results suggest that the epimerization module functions efficiently, as evidenced by high UDCA yields (Fig. 2). However, when TCDCA was supplied instead of CDCA, no detectable TUDCA production was observed, which contrasts with findings from previous studies, such as Ji et al., who demonstrated the in vitro conversion of TCDCA to TUDCA by 7- and 7-HSDHs [23]. This discrepancy suggests that the hydrophilic taurine moiety may significantly hinder membrane permeability. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that taurine itself exhibits very low membrane permeability due to its hydrophilicity, which may also contribute to the poor permeability of TCDCA and TUDCA [43]. Taurine is known to cross cellular membranes only via active transport, not passive diffusion [43, 44]. Although its permeability across the yeast cell wall has not been fully characterized, it is likely that taurine is transported into or out of yeast cells through endogenous amino acid transporters, whose efficiency and kinetics for taurine remain unknown [45]. Our data in Fig. 5 further support this hypothesis. The strain relying on external taurine feeding produced lower levels of TUDCA than the strain engineered with a heterologous taurine biosynthetic pathway. In our experiments, the feeding strain was supplemented with 6.3 mg/L of taurine, while the biosynthetic strain produced 2–3 mg/L in vivo (Fig. 4). Despite the higher absolute amount of taurine in the feeding group, TUDCA production was lower, indicating that externally supplied taurine is not efficiently taken up by yeast, likely due to limited membrane permeability. Thus, once TUDCA is synthesized intracellularly, it may become trapped within the yeast cell and not be secreted into the extracellular space due to its low membrane permeability, ultimately limiting overall TUDCA production. To further validate these hypotheses, future studies should directly assess the permeability of taurine, TCDCA, and TUDCA by quantifying their intracellular concentrations following external supplementation.

In parallel, additional studies could explore engineering strategies to overcome spatial limitations and improve TUDCA production efficiency. Potential approaches include relocating the TUDCA biosynthetic pathway outside the limited intracellular space, such as by expressing transporter proteins to facilitate TUDCA secretion, secreting all pathway enzymes to drive extracellular biosynthesis, or displaying the enzymes on the cell surface. Utilizing crude cell extracts for bioconversion is another possible strategy. Additionally, as reported in previous studies, further optimization of the fermentation process could substantially boost TUDCA yields by implementing process improvements, such as media composition tuning and bioreactor parameter adjustments, to enhance production efficiency [24].

In summary, this study represents the first successful attempt to engineer yeast for the biosynthesis of conjugated bile acids. By integrating taurine conjugation with epimerization, we have established a novel microbial platform for TUDCA production from CDCA. While current titers remain limited due to spatial constraints, further optimization of biosynthetic pathways and fermentation strategies could unlock the full potential of yeast as a scalable system for producing UDCA derivatives. These findings provide a strong foundation for future research into microbial bile acid biosynthesis, with promising applications in drug discovery and therapeutic development.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and media components

Authentic standards of bile acids—CDCA, TCDCA, and GUDCA—were purchased from Cayman Chemical Company (Ann Arbor, MI), whereas UDCA and TUDCA were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) and AstaTech (Bristol, PA), respectively. Taurine and hypotaurine were sourced from EMD Millipore (Burlington, MA) and Cayman Chemical Company, respectively. LB Broth (Luria-Bertani), Miller, for E. coli growth was obtained from Becton, Dickinson and Company (BD) (Franklin Lakes, NJ). Yeast cells were grown either in rich media (YPD, yeast extract-peptone-dextrose) media for cell growth and fermentation or in minimal media (SD, selective drop-out) for auxotrophic selection. YPD media contains 10 g/L of yeast extract, 20 g/L of peptone and 20 g/L of glucose whereas SD media is made of 6.7 g/L of yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, 20 g/L of glucose, supplemented with dropout mix. Yeast extract and peptone were purchased from BD and VWR International (Radnor, PA), respectively. Glucose and components for SD media were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Microbial strains and growth conditions

S. cerevisiae CEN.PK 2a strain was used to construct TUDCA-producing yeast. Engineered strains were grown in the shaking incubator at 30 C and at 250 rpm. For fermentation, cells were cultured in 5 mL of YPD media overnight and transferred to fresh 50 mL of YPD in the next day, followed by four days of incubation. E. coli JM109 strain was purchased from Promega (Madison, WI) (Cat. No. L2005) and was used for plasmid cloning. E. coli strains were grown in LB media at 37 C and at 250 rpm.

Plasmid construction and yeast engineering

The multi-gene plasmids were constructed by Golden Gate assembly through a series of building steps: coding DNA sequence (CDS) vector assembly, cis-regulon assembly with regulatory elements, and final multi-gene cluster assembly [46]. Genes were redesigned to remove internal restriction sites and synthesized by Twist Bioscience or Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The MoClo Yeast Toolkit was purchased from Addgene and used to create genetic constructs based on Golden Gate assembly [46]. Promoters and terminators of yeast origin were used, and distinct regulatory elements were assigned to each gene to prevent sequence deletions caused by homologous recombination in yeast. Most promoters and terminators were sourced from the MoClo Yeast Toolkit, while several terminators characterized by Curran et al. (2013) were synthesized and used [46, 47]. Promoter strengths were determined according to the data presented in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1 of Lee et al. (2015) [46]. Restriction enzymes were purchased from New England Biolabs (NEB) (Ipswich, MA), and T4 ligase was sourced from Promega. Engineered plasmids were transformed into chemically competent E. coli JM109 strain following the protocol outlined by Promega [48]. TUDCA-producing gene clusters were inserted into plasmid backbones containing either the URA3 or LEU2 auxotrophic marker, along with flanking homology arms to ensure correct integration into the yeast genome. The 7- and 7-HSDH genes were integrated at the native, non-functional URA3 locus, thereby restoring URA3 function for auxotrophic selection. Similarly, the taurine conjugation and biosynthesis module genes—BAAT, BaiB, SLC27A5, Cdo1, and Csad—were integrated at the LEU2 locus to restore LEU2 function. Plasmids were linearized by NotI digestion before being transformed into yeast cells. The lithium acetate/single-stranded carrier DNA/PEG method of yeast transformation was used [49].

LC-MS/MS analysis

Bile acid production in yeast was examined through liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. The product titers were quantified by comparing the corresponding peak area to a calibration curve generated with commercially available authentic standards [50]. Bile acids were extracted from 50 ml of yeast fermentation cultures as described in the protocol published by Krautbauer and Liebisch in 2018 [51]. Extracted samples were injected into the LC-MS (Ultra LC-triple quadrupole MS, Agilent) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source to identify the peaks of bile acid products. The solvent system consisted of mobile phase A (90% water:10% methanol (v/v)) and mobile phase B (100% methanol). Both solvents contained 10 mM of ammonium acetate. The LC gradient was programmed as follows: the mobile phase started at 80% solvent A for 0.05 min, followed by a stepwise linear decrease to 53% A at 0.1 min, 49% A at 2.0 min, and 28% A at 4.5 min. The flow rate during this gradient was maintained at 0.5 mL/min. After the run, the column was washed with 100% solvent B for 0.5 min and re-equilibrated with 100% solvent A for 0.6 min. Injection volume was 5 uL. Mass transitions for each compound were selected in negative ESI mode. Collision energy was set to -10V [51].
