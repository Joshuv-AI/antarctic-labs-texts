In-situ observation of phase separation dynamics for immiscible aqueous solution within ultrasonic field

Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Immiscible alloys composed of both soft and hard metallic phases have great potential for industrial applications as structural and functional materials [1], [2], [3], [4]. Unfortunately, this kind of alloy generally separates into two immiscible liquids when they are cooled into the miscibility gap, and the large density difference between the two liquids leads to the formation of severely segregated or even layered structures [5], [6]. Hence, many efforts have been devoted to exploring reliable technological ways to suppress the macro- and micro-segregation of immiscible alloys [7], [8], [9].

High-energy ultrasound has proved to be an effective methodology to fabricate immiscible alloys with uniform structures for a long time [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. It dates back to the 1930s that Schimid et al. [12] applied 10 kHz ultrasound to successfully disperse Pb and Cd liquid droplets into Al-rich alloy melts. Several decades later, Feng et al. [13] fabricated a microstructure with uniformly distributed fine Pb particles by introducing high-intensity ultrasound into the miscibility gap of Al-Pb alloy. In recent years, our group has successfully suppressed macro- and micro-segregation of more types of bulk immiscible alloys, such as Cu-Sn-Bi [14], Al-Sn-Cu [15], Al-Sn-Si [16], Al-Cu-Bi [17] and Al-Bi [18] by power ultrasound. Nevertheless, due to the opacity of metallic alloys, direct observation on the liquid phase separation process within ultrasonic field is limited, leading to that the exact mechanism is still controversial. To overcome this problem, Yamamoto et al. [11], [19] used water-gallium transparent immiscible system to demonstrate that bubble collapse in the liquid–liquid interface was responsible for the emulsion of Ga droplets. Similar conclusions were obtained through a water–oil system by Orthaber et al. [20] and Perdih et al. [21]. However, most of the published researches have focused on investigating the interaction between bubbles and liquid–liquid interface, but the interactions between acoustic cavitation/streaming and secondary liquid droplets, which greatly affect their migration, fragmentation, and the final morphology and distribution features remain unclear.

In present work, by using high-speed imaging technology, the liquid phase separation process of water (H2O)-20 %succinonitrile (SCN) organic transparent solution within ultrasonic field was investigated. Various H2O-rich droplet evolution modes were dynamically detected, and the ultrasound-induced droplet fragmentation mechanisms were elucidated on the combination of the experimental observation and the calculated sound field and flow field. The comparative solidification experimental results of Al-30 %Bi immiscible alloy indicated that power ultrasound showed very similar effects on the separation process of monotectic solution and monotectic alloy systems.

Experimental procedure and numerical simulation

Experimental procedure

The 2D solidification cell is shown schematically in Fig. 1(a), which contains a glass chamber with a length × width × height size of 12.5 × 5 × 45 mm, a titanium-alloy sonotrode in a size of 3 × 8 mm, and a pair of heating copper sheets with a thickness of 0.2 mm. Planes ABCD and EFGH represent a longitudinal section of the glass chamber and an observation area with a size of 5 × 8 mm, respectively. Fig. 1(b) shows the phase diagram of H2O-SCN system. During the experiment, the H2O-20 %SCN organic transparent solution prepared by 99 % purity succinonitrile and distilled water was melted through water bath heating, stirred thoroughly until it mixed uniformly, and kept isothermally at 330 K for 5 min. After that, this solution was injected into the glass chamber by using a syringe with a capacity of 5 ml. Meanwhile, the temperature of the left hot- and right cold-side copper sheets can be maintained at 320 and 300 K respectively by resistance heating and coupled with the digital temperature controller. The sonotrode, coupled with an FS-1800 N ultrasonic transducer with 20 kHz driving frequency and 12 μm amplitude, was inserted into the solution 5 mm away from the liquid level. Finally, a Photron Fastcam SA-Z high-speed camera was used to record the process of liquid–liquid phase separation on plane EFGH with an image acquisition rate in the range of 5,000 ∼ 100,000 frames per second.

2D geometric diagram of solidification cell used for in-situ observation device.

The metallic immiscible alloy solidification experiments with and without ultrasound were also carried out as a comparison. During the experiment, 152 g weight Al-30 %Bi immiscible alloy prepared by 99.99 % Al and Bi elements was contained in a Φ 30 × 35 mm graphite crucible, which was then melted and held isothermally at about 1445 K for 5 min to form a homogeneous liquid. After that, a 20 kHz ultrasonic wave with 22 μm amplitude, generated by VCX-1600 ultrasonic transducer, was introduced into the liquid alloy through a Φ 26 mm niobium-alloy sonotrode for 5 mm depth until the end of liquid phase separation. After cooling the alloy samples to room temperature, they were sectioned longitudinally along the central axis, polished, and characterized by an FEI Sirion 200 scanning electron microscope (SEM).

It should be noted that the above two experiments were independent and used different samples, container shapes and scales, and ultrasonic devices.

Static and ultrasonic evolution of secondary droplets

Static growth process of secondary droplets

Fig. 3 displays the nucleation, collision, coagulation, and migration of secondary H2O-rich droplets under static state, where the dark grey background and light grey circles represent SCN-rich phase matrix and H2O-rich phase droplets, respectively. This picture is divided into three areas by yellow dashed lines: Area Ⅰ (the cold-side), Area Ⅱ (transition area), and Area Ⅲ (the hot-side). H2O–rich phase droplets nucleate from Area Ⅰ, grow through solute diffusion and occur collision and coagulation under the action of interfacial tension and Brownian motion. It is impossible to directly observe Brownian motion in this special scale because the spatial resolution of Fig. 3 is only 14.56 μm/pixel. However, it can be speculated that at the early nucleation stage in Area Ⅰ, these tiny droplets in the black fog are undergoing Brownian motion. The Brownian motion weakens as these droplets approach the micrometer-scale and is replaced by the Marangoni effect induced by temperature gradient. Collision and coagulation continue to occur in Area Ⅱ during this period until these droplets finally aggregate into large droplet clusters.

Ultrasonic cavitation induced secondary droplets deformation and fragmentation

Fig. 4 shows a deformation and migration process of H2O-rich droplet D1 triggered by the collapse of nearby cavitation bubble B1. In Fig. 4(a), bubble B1 with a radius of 11 μm locates in the upper left corner, near two spherical droplets D1 and D2 with radii of 4 and 6 μm. After several acoustic wave circles, bubble B1 collapses violently to its minimum with a radius of 1 μm, resulting in droplet D1 changing from previously spherical to threadlike and accelerating its migration to the lower right corner, while droplet D2 is almost unaffected and still remains spherical with a migration velocity of about 6 mm/s, as seen in Figs. 4(b) and (c). Fig. 4(d) shows that B1 collapse increases rapidly the migration rate of droplet D1 from 6 mm/s to 192 mm/s within 0.3 ms. However, due to the duration of bubble collapse and the resulting physical effects being extremely transient (＜10 μs) [34], droplet D1 decelerates quickly to 5.6 mm/s within 1.78 ms and its morphology recovers to a spherical shape.

Effect of a collapsing bubble on secondary droplet migration and morphology within ultrasonic field: (a) t = 0 ms; (b) t = 0.05 ms; (c) t = 0.68 ms; (d) droplet D1 migration rate versus time.

The phenomenon in Fig. 4 indicates that whether the droplets are affected by bubble collapse depends on the bubble-droplet distance. Here, a dimensionless parameter d* = d/Rmin is used to characterize the distance, where d is the shortest distance between the bubble center and the droplet surface, and Rmin is the minimum radius during bubble collapse. It is found from 15 observation experiments of cavitation induced droplet fragmentation that the dimensionless distances d* between the bubble and the droplet are less than 20, as shown by the orange dot in Fig. 8(c). Hence, the bubble-droplet interactions are categorized as strong interaction and weak interaction. The weak interaction is defined as when the dimensionless distance between the bubble and the droplet exceeding 20 (d*>20). In this case, the bubble cavitation only leads to the transient deformation and accelerated migration of the droplet. And the case where the dimensionless distance is less than 20 (d*<20) is defined as strong interactions, in which case the bubble collapse stimulates the droplet to fracture and form a liquid ejection containing numerous sub-droplets. Three strong interactions with d* = 15, 3 and 1 are discussed respectively in the following.

The following discusses the fractured mechanism of H2O-rich droplets driven by bubble collapse in detail. According to the Refs. [35], [36], [37], when bubble collapse takes place near a rigid boundary, the bubble experiences asymmetric collapse and generates a high-speed microjet towards this rigid boundary. However, when the bubble collapses in an unconstrained surrounding, a high-pressure wave is generated from the bubble center and propagates into the surrounding liquid, thus forming the shock wave. Since there is no rigid boundary near the bubble in the experiment, we consider that the migration and fragmentation of secondary phase droplets in Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7 are induced by shock wave. Acoustic streaming stimulated secondary droplets fragmentation

Figs. 10(a)-(c) illustrate a dynamic interaction between acoustic streaming and a millimeter-sized H2O-rich droplet. Under the action of acoustic streaming, an initially spherical droplet becomes unstable and is then stretched into an ellipsoid D6 with 5 mm in length and then keeps deforming until a neck is formed in its middle, as presented in Fig. 10(a). In Fig. 10(b), the neck thins gradually and the master droplet D6 eventually produces a smaller droplet D7 from the tip of the elongated end of the prolate droplet. After then, the end of the remaining D6 becomes more slender and increasingly pointed. Similar to D7 formation, the rupture repeats three times on the remaining droplet, forming several small spherical sub-droplets D7-D11 in a string-beads distribution, as presented in Fig. 10(c).

It can be seen that the minimum critical radius is about 1.07 mm, which indicates that the shear flow field produced by acoustic streaming is sufficient to break millimeter-sized or even larger H2O-rich droplets into several near-spherical sub-droplets.

Immiscible alloys always present heavy segregation after conventional solidification due to the large density difference between two immiscible phases, which deteriorates their physical properties and restricts their industrial applications [4], [44]. In order to obtain superior wear resistance, the refined soft phase should be uniformly distributed on the continuous hard matrix phase [45], [46]. In this work, it is found that ultrasonic cavitation and acoustic streaming can break up coarse secondary phase droplets into fine droplets, and our previously published work proved that acoustic streaming could also suppress the sedimentation of secondary phase droplets induced by Stokes migration [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. Hence, power ultrasound effectively refines secondary phase droplets and enhances uniform structures, which improves wear-resisting properties and mechanical properties of immiscible alloys. These positive effects of ultrasound provide a new way for the efficient fabrication of immiscible alloys.

Ultrasonic modulation of phase separation for Al-30 %Bi immiscible alloy

The Al-30 %Bi immiscible alloy was chosen as a control group to verify the in-situ observational results. Their solidified microstructures under static and ultrasonic conditions are given in Fig. 11, where the white and black phases are Bi-rich particles and Al-rich matrix, respectively. As shown in Fig. 11(a), many spherical or near-spherical Bi-rich particles are uniformly distributed on the Al-rich matrix under static condition, which is similar to Fig. 3. However, the morphology of Bi-rich phase particles after ultrasonic solidification in Fig. 11(b) is modulated to several new forms such as tadpole- like, threadlike, gourd-like and string-beads shapes, which are in good agreement with the morphology of H2O-rich droplets in Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 10. Moreover, many fine particles gather near the non-spherical particles, which may be formed by the dispersion of numerous nanometer-sized sub-droplets after master droplets fracturing. It should be noted that although the interfacial tension of water-SCN (1.0 × 10-3N/m [31]) is much smaller than that of Al-Bi (48 × 10-3N/m [11]), their Laplace pressure are smaller than the pressure induced by shock wave. Hence, the effects of ultrasonic cavitation on Bi-rich droplets and H2O-rich droplets are similar, which can also be inferred from the similar morphology between H2O-rich droplets and Bi-rich droplets after ultrasonic processing. In fact, many investigators [47], [48], [49] have also used this transparent aqueous solution to study the liquid phase separation process, though the physical properties of the two systems are different. Therefore, the in-situ observation of transparent immiscible solutions can be used to predict the liquid phase separation mechanism of immiscible alloys induced by acoustic cavitation/streaming.

Conclusions

In this study, a high-speed in-situ observation technology and numerical simulation method were used to investigate the dynamic evolution of secondary droplets during the liquid–liquid phase separation of immiscible solution within ultrasonic field. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

(1)During the liquid phase separation of H2O-20 %SCN immiscible solution, the secondary H2O-rich droplets present spherical or near-spherical under static condition. When power ultrasound is introduced, these droplets are dynamically modulated into several novel forms.

During the liquid phase separation of H2O-20 %SCN immiscible solution, the secondary H2O-rich droplets present spherical or near-spherical under static condition. When power ultrasound is introduced, these droplets are dynamically modulated into several novel forms.

(2)Calculated results show that the shock wave generated by bubble collapse far exceeds the Laplace pressure of spherical droplets, resulting in the fragmentation of micron-scale H2O-rich droplets. As the distance between the droplets and the collapsing bubble increases, the volume of ejected sub-droplets decreases, and the morphology of those fractured droplets evolves from threadlike to gourd-like shapes and finally into elongated tadpole-like appearances.

Calculated results show that the shock wave generated by bubble collapse far exceeds the Laplace pressure of spherical droplets, resulting in the fragmentation of micron-scale H2O-rich droplets. As the distance between the droplets and the collapsing bubble increases, the volume of ejected sub-droplets decreases, and the morphology of those fractured droplets evolves from threadlike to gourd-like shapes and finally into elongated tadpole-like appearances.

(3)Numerical simulation demonstrates that the acoustic streaming generates shear force exceeding the surface tension of H2O-rich droplets, which stretches, splits and finally fractures the millimeter-sized or even larger H2O-rich droplets into several sub-droplets with string-beads shape distributions.

(4)The secondary droplet distribution characteristics in H2O-20 %SCN solution are in good agreement with those in the ultrasonically solidified Al-30 %Bi immiscible alloy, indicating that the acoustic cavitation and acoustic streaming may play the same roles in liquid phase separation of different immiscible systems.
