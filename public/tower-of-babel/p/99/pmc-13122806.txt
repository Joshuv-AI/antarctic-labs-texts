Viperin weakens IFN-I-induced immune activity by facilitating STAT1 degradation through E3 ligase UBE4A
Authors: Ying Miao, Yukang Yuan, Liping Qian, Yong Yu, Qun Cui, Tingting Zhang, Renxia Zhang, Qian Zhao, Zhize Li, Anning Miao, Yanou Li, Jun Wang, Sidong Xiong, Hui Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Viperin is considered as an antiviral protein known to directly target a variety of viruses. However, whether and how Viperin affects virus infection by targeting intracellular immune signaling remain unexplored. Here, we reveal that Viperin inhibits type-I interferon (IFN-I) antiviral immune signaling by degrading STAT1. We found that IFN-I upregulates the ubiquitin E3 ligase ITCH to degrade UBR5, while Viperin subsequently recruits another ubiquitin E3 ligase UBE4A to promote STAT1 ubiquitination and degradation to attenuate IFN-I signaling. Moreover, the multifunctional interfering peptide VS-IP1 can block Viperin-mediated STAT1 degradation, thus enhancing IFN-I antiviral immune function. This study reveals that Viperin is a suppressor of IFN-I immune signaling, which could renew understanding of the biological function of Viperin, and provide a strategy for enhancing clinical IFN-I therapeutic efficacy.

Introduction

Upon virus infection, the host's innate immune system is activated to prevent virus invasion and replication (Zhang et al., 2022). One of the main features of the host's innate immune response to viral infection is the production of type-I Interferon (IFN-I), although a few viruses such as Coxsackievirus-B3 (CVB3) have been reported to be almost unable to induce IFN-I production. IFN-I is currently recognized as a broad-spectrum antiviral protein (Duncan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Samuel, 2001). It does not directly target viruses or kill viruses, but activates IFN-I signals after binding to its corresponding receptors. IFNs can activate different signaling pathways by binding to different receptors, plays a variety of biological functions (Ivashkiv and Donlin, 2014). The classical signaling pathway mediated by IFN-I mainly refers to the JAK-STAT pathway, which is composed of the Janus kinase family (JAK) and signal transducers and activators of transcription family (STAT) (Banerjee et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2023). IFN-I induces the expression of IFN-I-stimulated genes (ISGs) such as IFIT1, Viperin and PKR, which ultimately exert a range of biological functions. Some of the ISGs are gradually gaining attention due to their efficient antiviral functions. Therefore, focusing on these ISGs and studying their mechanisms in the antiviral function will undoubtedly provide direct and effective help in exploring how to enhance the signal strength and antiviral function of IFN-I.

More than 300 ISGs are known to perform a variety of biological effects including antiviral functions, and several ISGs have been reported to suppress viruses directly (Der et al., 1998; Ren et al., 2023). Among these ISGs, the antiviral protein Viperin (virus inhibitory protein, endoplasmic reticulum associated, interferon-inducible) has attracted increasing attention in recent years due to its extensive and significant antiviral functions. Viperin (also known as cig5, RSAD2) is composed of 361 amino acids and has a molecular weight of 42 KD (Hinson and Cresswell, 2009a, 2009b; Shaveta et al., 2010). Viperin can be directly induced by type I, II and III-IFNs in many cells (Stirnweiss et al., 2010; Yuan et al., 2022). Viperin can also be effectively induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and poly I:C, as well as a variety of virus infection (Fitzgerald, 2011; Olofsson et al., 2005). These factors indirectly induce Viperin expression through the production of IFNs. However, some viruses such as HCMV and VSV, can directly induce Viperin expression by activating IRF1/3 (Helbig and Beard, 2014; Severa et al., 2006). Viperin has been considered to have a broad spectrum of antiviral functions, but whether and how Viperin affects virus infection by regulating intracellular immune signaling is still unknown.

Ubiquitination is one of the important pathways for cellular protein degradation. The functional diversity of ubiquitination is derived from its ability to form eight different structural and functional polymers, that is, the ubiquitin group is linked by seven lysine residues or by the amino terminus. Among them, the roles of Lys 48 and Lys 63 linked polyubiquitination in cell signaling have been well studied. Ubiquitination is a three-step enzymatic cascade reaction, including ubiquitin activating enzyme E1, ubiquitin conjugating enzyme E2, and ubiquitin ligase E3. Ubiquitination is reversible and deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) can reverse the ubiquitination process and remove the ubiquitin from proteins. More than 600 ubiquitin ligases E3 exist in mammals and regulate the ubiquitination of a variety of proteins (Glickman and Ciechanover, 2002). The mechanism by which the ubiquitination modification of Viperin regulates the IFN-I signaling pathway remains unclear.

In this study, we found that Viperin is able to inhibit IFN-I-induced immune signaling, thus attenuating IFN-I-induced antiviral immune activity. Mechanistically, IFN-I-induced Viperin protein can promote STAT1 ubiquitination and degradation, which is independent of UBR5, a reported ubiquitin E3 ligase degrading STAT1 during CVB3 infection. Further studies revealed that during IFN-I signaling, IFN-I upregulates the ubiquitin E3 ligase ITCH to degrade UBR5, while Viperin subsequently recruits another ubiquitin E3 ligase UBE4A to degrade STAT1, thus attenuating IFN-I immune signaling. More importantly, we developed an interfering peptide, VS-IP1, to block Viperin-STAT1 interaction without inhibiting the antiviral activity of Viperin itself, which eventually significantly enhances IFN-I-induced antiviral immunity.

Our previous studies have shown that during CVB3 infection Viperin employs UBR5 to promote STAT1 degradation (Yuan et al., 2025). However, we noticed that UBR5 knockout did not affect either the changes of STAT1 protein levels during IFN-I signaling (Fig. 1(C)) or IFN-I-induced ISGs expression (Fig. 1(D)), suggesting that UBR5 could play a marginal role on IFN-I signaling. As a matter of fact, we noticed that UBR5 was rapidly downregulated during IFN-I signaling (Fig. 1(C)). In our previous research, we reported that UBE4A and UBR5 are the E3 ubiquitin ligases of Viperin (Yuan et al., 2020, 2025). Here, we noticed that in addition to ubiquitin E3 ligases UBR5, UBE4A knockout also blocked Viperin-mediated downregulation of STAT1 to some extent (Fig. 1(E)). We further screened UBE4A, UBE3A and UBE3C reported in our previous studies (Yuan et al., 2020), and found that UBE4A is another potential ubiquitin E3 ligase of STAT1 (Fig. 1(F)). Subsequently, we observed that STAT1 interacts with UBE4A in the presence of Viperin. Notably, this interaction was abolished in Viperin-deficient (Viperin−/−) cells (Fig. 1(G) and (H)).

Given that the ISGs mRNA levels were upregulated in Viperin−/− MEF cells (Fig. 1(A) and (B)), we next analyzed whether Viperin could affect the activation of the ISRE promoter. Our results showed that overexpression of Viperin significantly decreased IFN-I-mediated ISRE activity (Fig. S1B). Conversely, Flag-Viperin overexpression downregulated the protein levels and mRNA levels of multiple ISGs such as PKR, IFIT1, ISG15, ISG54, Mx1 and SOCS1 (Fig. S1C–H). Studies have shown that IFN-I treatment fails to induce Viperin protein expression in epithelial cells, while IFN-I robustly upregulates Viperin levels in fibroblasts, revealing a cell type-specific differential regulation of Viperin (Yuan et al., 2020). We prepared the primary lung fibroblasts and primary lung epithelial cells from Viperin+/+ and Viperin−/− mice. The results showed that the ISGs mRNA levels in Viperin−/− epithelial cells were comparable to those in Viperin+/+ epithelial cells (Fig. S1I). Different from this result, the VSV-induced ISGs mRNA levels in Viperin−/− fibroblast cells are higher than those in the Viperin+/+ fibroblast cells (Fig. S1J). This is because there is no expression of Viperin proteins in epithelial cells during virus infection. Furthermore, we found that Viperin dose-dependently down-regulated the mRNA levels of ISGs induced by VSV (Fig. S1K).

Next, we observed that endogenous STAT1 was upregulated during virus infection (Fig. S1L). However, virus infection downregulates the level of exogenous Myc-STAT1 (Fig. S1M), as the exogenous expression of Myc-STAT1 is not subject to the transcriptional regulation of IFN-I induced by the virus. Notably, endogenous STAT1, as an ISG, can be transcriptionally upregulated by IFN-I signaling during viral infection. This compensatory upregulation of STAT1 expression may counteract the Viperin-mediated suppression of STAT1 protein levels.

UBE4A promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of STAT1

In Stat1−/− cells, Viperin no longer reduced ISGs mRNA expression induced by IFN (Figs. S2A and S1B), suggesting that Viperin-mediated downregulation of ISGs is dependent on STAT1. In addition, Viperin overexpression downregulated cellular STAT1 protein levels in various types of cells (Fig. S2C and S2D). Consistently, overexpression of Viperin inhibited the protein level of p-STAT1 (Fig. S2E).

Studies have shown that Viperin can directly target and inhibit a broad spectrum of viruses (Gizzi et al., 2018). Consistently, when we used the viruses to infect cells for 24 h, we found that the viral RNA levels were significantly inhibited by overexpressing Flag-Viperin (Fig. S2F). Thus, we speculated that the direct inhibition of the viruses by Viperin could overcome Viperin-mediated inhibition of IFN-I antiviral immunity. However, cells were infected with the viruses for 2 h, and the results showed that Viperin overexpression did increase cellular susceptibility to virus infection (Fig. S2G). In addition, the viral RNA levels were higher in Viperin+/+ MEF cells than in Viperin−/− cells (Fig. S2H and S2I). The viral growth curve analysis showed that during the early stage of infection, Viperin promotes viral replication, and in the late stage, it suppresses the viruses (Fig. S2J). This is because Viperin's direct antiviral activity may compensate for its suppressive effect on IFN-I-mediated antiviral immunity. Collectively, these findings suggested that Viperin inhibits cellular IFN-I antiviral immunity and leads to higher susceptibility of the cells to virus infection.

To investigate whether UBE4A regulates STAT1 protein levels and IFN-mediated signaling, we utilized Ube4a−/− cells. Our results demonstrated that genetic ablation of UBE4A enhanced STAT1 protein expression in response to IFN-I stimulation (Fig. 1(I)). To explore the roles of UBE4A in regulating IFN-I-induced signaling and antiviral function, we treated Ube4a+/+ and Ube4a−/− cells with IFN-I. The results showed that the ISG proteins and mRNA levels were much higher in Ube4a−/− cells than in Ube4a+/+ cells (Fig. 1(J) and S2K). Further studies showed that overexpression of UBE4A downregulated STAT1 in Viperin protein-sufficient but not in Viperin protein-deficient cells (Fig. 1(K) and (L)). UBE4A knockdown attenuated STAT1 ubiquitination in Viperin-expressing stable cells but not in Viperin protein-deficient cells (Fig. S3A). Furthermore, we found that UBE4A overexpression promoted STAT1 ubiquitination in Viperin-expressing stable cells (Fig. S3B). However, the inactive UBE4A-PA (P1030A) mutant lost the ability to both promote STAT1 ubiquitination (Fig. S3C) and reduce STAT1 protein levels (Fig. S4A). Importantly, knockdown of UBE4A by shUBE4A attenuated Viperin-mediated downregulation of STAT1 protein levels (Fig. S4B). Moreover, knockout of UBE4A inhibited Viperin-induced STAT1 downregulation during IFN-I treatment (Fig. S4C and S4D).

Moreover, during infection with RNA viruses (VSV, H1N1, SeV) and DNA viruses (HSV-1), knockout of UBE4A promoted IFN-I-mediated antiviral function (Fig. 1(M)–(O) and S4E). Taken together, our findings suggested that UBE4A interacts with STAT1 in the presence of Viperin and in turn promotes STAT1 ubiquitination and degradation, thus inhibiting IFN-I signaling and antiviral activity.

Viperin-STAT1-UBE4A binding rescues Viperin and promotes STAT1 degradation

We further explored how Viperin protein interacts with STAT1 and UBE4A. Our previous study reported that UBE4A could bind to the SAM domain of Viperin (Yuan et al., 2020). Thus, we further determined the detailed domain of Viperin that interacts with either UBE4A or STAT1. We found that UBE4A mainly interacts with the 4th part (4#) of the SAM domain of Viperin (Fig. 2(A), left). Interestingly, deletion of the SAM-4# domain of Viperin abolished STAT1 binding (Fig. 2(A), right), suggesting that STAT1 also binds to the SAM-4# domain of Viperin. Thus, we hypothesized that STAT1 could competitively inhibit the effect of UBE4A on Viperin. In our previous reports, we have demonstrated that Viperin undergoes K6-linked ubiquitination induced by cellular UBE4A (Yuan et al., 2020). Next, we generated a series of STAT1 deletion mutants (Fig. 2(C)). We found that deletion of amino acids 704-750 of STAT1 (STAT1-ΔC1) completely abolished Viperin binding (Fig. 2(C)), suggesting that Viperin could mainly interact with the C-terminus of STAT1. In addition, our above data demonstrated that it is difficult to detect the interaction between endogenous UBE4A and endogenous STAT1 in Viperin protein-deficient cells, while UBE4A can interact with STAT1 in the presence of Viperin. Thus, we further analyzed the UBE4A-STAT1 interaction in cells with Viperin expression. We found that Flag-UBE4A can interact with Myc-STAT1, and deletion of amino acids 1-324 of STAT1 (STAT1-ΔN1) largely inhibited UBE4A binding (Fig. 2(D)), suggesting that UBE4A could mainly bind to the N-terminus of STAT1. The interaction results help understand our aforementioned finding that UBE4A can promote STAT1 ubiquitination in the presence of Viperin. Together, these findings suggested that Viperin proteins produced in cells can interact with STAT1 and then UBE4A can bind to STAT1; this interaction protects Viperin from UBE4A-mediated ubiquitination, but results in STAT1 ubiquitination and degradation by UBE4A (Fig. 2(E)).

IFN-I promotes ubiquitination and degradation of UBR5

We observed from Fig. 1(C) that UBR5 was able to be rapidly downregulated by IFN-I. To further validate this result, we stimulated HT1080 and A549 cells with IFN-I. The results showed that UBR5 was gradually downregulated by IFN-I (Fig. 3(A)). However, the mRNA levels of UBR5 were not affected by IFN-I stimulation (Fig. 3(B) and (C)). Thus, we speculated that IFN-I could regulate UBR5 levels at the protein level. Consistently, overexpression of Flag-UBR5 was also degraded by IFN-I in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 3(D)). Furthermore, we explored how IFN-I affects UBR5 proteins. We first used cycloheximide (CHX) that is a protein synthesis inhibitor to observe UBR5 protein degradation. The result showed that the degradation rate of UBR5 was faster when treated with IFN-I (Fig. 3(F)). When treated with proteasome inhibitor MG-132, the degradation of exogenous Flag-UBR5 and endogenous UBR5 mediated by IFN-I was significantly inhibited (Fig. 3(G) and (H)). However, the degradation of endogenous UBR5 by IFN-I was not significantly inhibited when treated with either the lysosomal inhibitor methylamine (MA) or the deubiquitinating enzyme inhibitor PR619 (Fig. 3(I) and (J)). Taken together, these results demonstrated that IFN-I could degrade UBR5 through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway but not the lysosomal pathway.

We next examined UBR5 ubiquitination levels at different times of IFN-I treatment. We found that the ubiquitination levels of UBR5 increased with the duration of IFN treatment (Fig. 3(K)). It is well known that the types of K48-ub and K63-ub chains mainly mediate the degradation of proteins (Ding et al., 2019; Krappmann and Scheidereit, 2005; Weber et al., 2016). Thus, we analyzed the ubiquitination type(s) of UBR5 regulated by IFN-I. The results showed that IFN-I treatment promotes K48-linked but not K63-linked polyubiquitination of UBR5 (Fig. 3(L) and (M)). Taken all together, these findings suggested that UBR5 undergoes K48-linked polyubiquitination and degradation during IFN-I signaling.

Further studies demonstrated that UBR5 interacts with ITCH in cell lines, and also in mouse primary tissues (Fig. 4(E) and (F)). To further investigate the role of ITCH in regulating the levels of UBR5 and IFN-induced signaling, we constructed Itch−/− cells (Fig. S6A). We found that the protein levels of UBR5 were higher in Itch−/− cells than in Itch+/+ cells (Fig. 4(G)). Moreover, overexpression of ITCH significantly reduced the protein level of UBR5 (Fig. 4(H)). However, the mRNA levels of UBR5 were unaffected by overexpression of ITCH (Fig. 4(I)). In addition, the lysosomal inhibitor MA can not abolish ITCH-induced UBR5 reduction (Fig. 4(J)).

We further found that UBR5 was degraded at a slower rate in Itch−/− cells, whereas overexpression of ITCH promoted UBR5 degradation (Fig. 4(K) and (L)). We next found that UBR5 ubiquitination was almost undetectable in Itch−/− cells (Fig. 4(N)), while overexpression of ITCH significantly increased the ubiquitination levels of UBR5 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4(O)). Collectively, these findings suggested that IFN-I upregulates ITCH expression to induce UBR5 protein degradation.

In the last decades, peptide therapy has become an emerging market in drug treatment. Compared to antibodies or large-molecule proteins, peptides have smaller molecular weights and simpler structures, making them less likely to trigger immune rejection and easier to metabolize by the body, resulting in higher safety. In our previous study, the Viperin-STAT1 interaction was blocked by designing a Viperin-STAT1 interfering peptide 1 (VS-IP1) (Yuan et al., 2025). Previous studies have shown that Viperin protein is expressed at low levels in epithelial cells, and its induction by IFN-I is relatively weak (Yuan et al., 2020). When IFN-I was not used, VS-IP1 did not work because Viperin protein was not present (Fig. 5(A) and (B)). The interfering peptide VS-IP1 enhances host antiviral ability in vivo

We further explored the effects of VS-IP1 on the host IFN-I signaling and antiviral response in vivo. C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with either the vehicle (PBS, n = 5) or VS-IP1 (n = 5) for 12 h and then infected with VSV (1 × 108 PFU per gram mouse, i.p) for 3 days. We observed that during virus infection VS-IP1 treatment resulted in higher STAT1 protein levels in mouse spleen tissues, compared with the PBS control group (Fig. 6(A)). Consistent with STAT1 up-regulation, the expression of the representative ISGs, including PKR and IFIT1, were significantly enhanced in the spleen tissues of VS-IP1 treatment, as compared with the Ctrl group (Fig. 6(A)). However, STAT1 protein levels and the ISG (PKR) protein levels had no dramatic changes in mouse lung tissues which cannot produce Viperin protein during virus infection (Fig. 6(B)), suggesting that VS-IP1 upregulated STAT1 and ISG expression dependently on the presence of Viperin. As a consequence, the levels of VSV-encoding protein (VSV-G) and viral RNA in the organs of VS-IP1 treatment group were much less than those of the Ctrl group (Fig. 6(A) and (C)). Moreover, mIFNβ-induced expression of the representative ISGs, including Ifit1, Isg54, Isg15, Isg20 and Mx1, was significantly enhanced in the VS-IP1 treatment group (Fig. 6(D)). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of mouse lung and spleen tissues revealed more pronounced pathological changes in the control group compared to the VS-IP1-treated group (Fig. 6(E)). Following VS-IP1 treatment, the mRNA levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β in the spleen and lung tissues were reduced compared to those in the control group (Fig. 6(F) and (G)). In addition, VS-IP1 treatment mice showed lower mortality when challenged with viruses, as compared with the Ctrl group mice (Fig. 6(H)). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that IFN-I-induced gene Viperin can inhibit IFN-I-induced ISGs expression. Mechanistically, Viperin promotes the degradation of STAT1, a key node protein in IFN-I signaling pathway. Under the stimulation of IFN-I, STAT1 can be phosphorylated and activated, and form the ISGF3 complex with p-STAT2 and IRF9 to enter the nucleus, thereby promoting the expression of ISGs, including Viperin. We found that Viperin can recruit two ubiquitin E3 ligases, UBR5 and UBE4A. During CVB3 infection, Viperin can recruit UBR5 to promote STAT1 degradation. However, in response to IFN-I stimulation, the expression of ITCH is upregulated, which can interact with UBR5 and result in the ubiquitination and degradation of UBR5. Subsequently, Viperin degrades STAT1 by recruiting another ubiquitin E3 ligase, UBE4A. Importantly, our studies demonstrated that the multifunctional interfering peptide VS-IP1 inhibits the degradation of STAT1 by interfering with Viperin-STAT1 interaction but not Viperin-mediated antiviral activity, thereby improving IFN-I-induced antiviral immune function.

The IFN family consists of three main types: type I (IFN-I or IFN-α/β), type II (IFN-γ), and type III (IFN-λ) IFNs(Zhang et al., 2008). Among them, the type of IFN that mainly plays a role in the antiviral function of the immune system is type-I IFN, and the clinical use of interferon is mainly recombinant type-I interferon preparations, including α2a, α2b and α1b (Crow and Casanova, 2024; Zhao et al., 2023). IFN can activate different signaling pathways by binding to different receptors, both STAT1 and Viperin are essential components of the IFN-I signaling pathway. IFN-STAT1 signaling activates the transcription of hundreds of genes, some of which have antiviral functions (Tolomeo et al., 2022). Therefore, the regulation of STAT1 protein expression is important for regulating antiviral function. Here, we found that overexpression of UBE4A was able to degrade STAT1 protein levels in the presence of Viperin. Thus, this study revealed a delicate negative regulatory mechanism by which IFN-I signaling is inhibited. the negative feedback mechanism of Viperin helps prevent excessive IFN-I responses and maintain immune homeostasis; however, it could also potentially promote the persistence of certain viral infections.

Our recent study confirmed the interaction between Viperin and STAT1, demonstrating that CVB3-induced Viperin promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of STAT1 in a UBR5-dependent manner. However, this UBR5-mediated regulation of STAT1 protein was not observed during IFN-I signaling. In this study, we found that in response to IFN-I stimulation, the expression of ITCH is upregulated. ITCH interacts with UBR5, leading to the ubiquitination and degradation of UBR5. Under these conditions, UBE4A is subsequently recruited and plays a role in inducing the ubiquitination and degradation of STAT1. Unlike the response to IFN-I treatment, during CVB3 infection, the CVB3-encoded 3C protease cleaves UBE4A and restores Viperin protein expression. Consequently, CVB3 infection utilizes UBR5 to promote the degradation of STAT1. These findings indicated that IFN-I and CVB3 can employ different mechanisms to regulate STAT1 levels, which in turn leads to different biological outcomes.

In summary, this study reveals a new role of Viperin as a suppressor of IFN-I immune activity. Moreover, combining these two studies, we demonstrated that the interfering peptide VS-IP1 is able to not only restrict CVB3-induced cardiac dysfunction but also enhance IFN-I-induced antiviral immune activity. It indicates that VS-IP1 is multi-functional and has pharmaceutical potential in the field of treating CVB3-induced cardiac dysfunction and antiviral function.

Materials and methods

Mice

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Soochow University. Mice were maintained and bred in special-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in the Experimental Animal Center of Soochow University (the ethics approval number: 202307A0236). 6-8 weeks old mice were used for most experiments. Animal care and use protocols adhered to the National Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals. All protocols and procedures for mouse studies were performed in accordance with the Laboratory Animal Management Regulations with approval of the Scientific Investigation Board of Soochow University.

Primary cells from mice

For the mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), Viperin−/− embryos were obtained from the pregnant 13-day mice. Briefly, the embryos were cut into pieces and digested in 0.05% trypsin/EDTA with 100 units of DNase I, and then cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 units/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The primary cells were isolated from mouse tissues, which were cut into small pieces and digested by the erythrocyte lysis buffers. After centrifugation, the cells were collected and then cultured in RPMI medium until further experiments.

Cell culture and maintenance, transfection

HEK293T, A549, HT1080, Vero, and RAW264.7 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were cultured in DMEM (HyClone) supplemented with 10% FBS (GIBCO, Life Technologies), 100 units/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The cells were free of mycoplasma contamination for the described experiments. All transient transfections were carried out using LongTrans (Ucallm).

Expression constructs and reagents

Myc-tagged mouse Viperin was a nice gift from Dr. Zhenghong Yuan (Fudan University, China). Flag-tagged human Viperin was a nice gift from Dr. Chunfu Zheng (Fujian Medical University, China). Myc-tagged human Viperin (WT and the deletion mutants) and Flag-HA (FH)-tagged Viperin were generated using PCR amplification from Flag-tagged human Viperin. Flag-UBE4A was kindly provided by Dr. Wei Li (Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences). Flag-STAT1 and Myc-His-tagged human STAT1 deletion mutants were generated using PCR amplification from Myc-His-tagged STAT1-WT. All plasmids were confirmed by DNA sequencing. Cycloheximide (CHX), MG132, methylamine (MA) and other chemicals were purchased from Sigma. PR619 was purchased from Selleck.

Western blot

Equal amounts of total proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with either 5% skim milk or 5% BSA for 0.5 h at room temperature, and probed with the corresponding primary antibodies, followed by the respective HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit (Bioworld) secondary antibody. After washing three times with PBST, the membrane was visualized with the ECL Prime (Thermo Scientific). The antibodies with indicated dilutions were as follows: Viperin (Abcam, ab107359, 1:1 000), Flag (Sigma, F7425, 1:5 000), STAT1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 8826, 1:5 000), Myc (Abmart, M20002, 1:5 000), Tubulin (Proteintech, 66031-1-Ig, 1:5 000), PKR (Cell Signaling Technology, 3072, 1:1 000), IFIT1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 14769, 1:1 000), UBR5 (Affinity, AF0268, 1:1 000), HA (Sigma, ab9110, 1:5 000), UBE4A (Santa Cruz, sc-365904, 1:1 000), Ubiquitin (Ub) (Santa Cruz, sc-8017, 1:500), 48ub (Cell Signaling, #4289, 1:500), 63ub (Millipore, #05-1308, 1:500), ITCH (Selleck, F0891, 1:1 000), H1N1 (HA) (Sino Biological, #11684-T56, 1:1 000) and VSV-G (Abcam, ab1874; 1:2 000).

TCID50 assay

For viral titer quantification, cells were infected with VSV for 24 h. Supernatants were harvested, serially diluted in DMEM, and viral titers were determined by TCID50 assay. This was performed by plating the dilutions onto Vero cell monolayers in 96-well plates, with titers calculated via the Spearman-Karber method.

CHX chase assay

To determine the half-life of UBR5, HT1080 cells were transfected with Flag-UBR5. At 36 h post-transfection, the cells were treated with either DMSO or CHX (50 μg/mL) for the indicated time periods. Cells were then harvested and analyzed by Western blotting. The degradation half-life was determined by measuring the remaining protein levels at specific time points. The striped images of UBR5 and Tubulin were imported into Image J. The pixel count of all the strips was measured, and then the percentage of UBR5 that remained was calculated in GraphPad Prism software.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Fresh tissue samples were fixed in formaldehyde fixative solution for at least 24 h. Following fixation, the tissues were removed from the fixative, and the target areas were trimmed flat using a surgical scalpel in a fume hood. The trimmed tissues were then placed into embedding cassettes along with corresponding labels and subjected to dehydration through a graded ethanol series. Subsequently, the tissues were cleared and infiltrated with paraffin wax, embedded in paraffin blocks and sectioned at a standard thickness with a rotary microtome. For staining, the sections were first immersed in hematoxylin solution for 3-5 min, followed by differentiation in hydrochloric acid solution and bluing in ammonia water. The sections were then dehydrated through a gradient of 85% and 95% ethanol before being counterstained with eosin Y solution for 5 min. Finally, the stained sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were captured under an optical microscope (Model: NIKON Eclipse SI; Imaging System: NIKON Digital Sight DS-FI2).

Statistical analysis

Comparison between different groups was analyzed using the two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons between multiple groups in a single factor. Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001; NS, not significant. The protein band density was quantified with Image J.
