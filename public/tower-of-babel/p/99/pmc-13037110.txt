Insight into sonography specialists’ knowledge concerning grayscale and doppler artifacts: across-sectional survey study in Sudan

Introduction

Ultrasound is a common imaging modality to diagnose several diseases because it is safe and portable. Due to rapid advances in technology, the imaging quality of various ultrasound procedures has significantly improved over the last few years. Despite these developments, the probability of encountering artifacts while imaging remains [1].

Image artifacts are frequently detected in both B-mode and Doppler ultrasound, despite optimal technique and scanning parameters, and can lead to confusion for the interpreting physician. Certain artifacts could be avoided and arise from improper scanning processes. The inherent physical restrictions of the modality produce others. Understanding US artifacts needs a fundamental knowledge of the physical properties of the ultrasound beam, its propagation through material, and the principles of image processing. Ultrasound artifacts are usually noticed, and expertise is required to avoid misdiagnosis. In some circumstances, specific artifacts can provide useful diagnostic information. For example, some artifacts may be suggestive of specific disorders [2, 3]. Some artifacts can be minimized by altering the position of the transducer and the machine settings. As for the rest, medical professionals must be aware of their mechanisms and the probability of relevant inaccurate diagnoses [4].

Artifacts can be identified routinely in clinical sonography. They are present in grayscale B-mode imaging, color Doppler imaging and spectral pulsed Doppler imaging. The predominant distortions can be comprehended fundamentally by analyzing the configuration of the focused ultrasound beam, the interaction of sound with the body tissue, and the assumptions regarding the spatial allocation of reflected echoes. Artifact are logical and arise due to the conditions necessary for image production not entirely aligning with reality. They arise from neglecting the actual size of sound lobes (slice-thickness artifacts and bow artifacts, range ambiguities) and from various forms of mirroring exhibiting distinct appearances [5] Sonographic devices determine depth by measuring “the time interval of round-trip echo travel”, presuming a linear and singular trajectory from the transducer to the reflector and back [6].

Probe flaws and failures can cause some ultrasonic artifacts such as disrupted reverberation; thus regular equipment quality checks are necessary [7]. Doppler abnormalities include motion, twinkling, reverberation, noise, or blooming, grayscale ultrasound artifacts are frequently caused by incorrect focal zone adjustment, ultrasound signal attenuation, changes in sound speed across tissue resistance, and sound refraction. When the probe is not orthogonally aligned to the scanned area, ultrasound artifacts appear, producing less-than-ideal image quality and sometimes incorrect diagnoses [8]. A sonographer’s capability involves recognizing the basic physical principles of ultrasound, distinguishing prevalent US artifacts, and making recommendations for adjusting the imaging procedure, which plays a vital role in good image interpretation [9].

Understanding of ultrasound grayscale and Doppler artifacts is crucial for precise image interpretation and clinical decision-making. Artifacts can either mimic or obscure true anatomical structures and pathological findings, leading to potential misdiagnosis if not properly recognized. Furthermore, some of them aid in the diagnosis of certain pathological conditions, such as twinkle, enhancement, and shadowing which help in identification of microcalcifications, simple cysts, and stones., respectively [9] (Fig. 1).

Sonography education in Sudan and some of the surrounding Middle Eastern countries is conducted at the Diploma, Bachelor’s, and postgraduate level and incorporates both theory and practice in ultrasound physics, instrumentation, image optimization and clinical use. Across most of these curricula, the common grayscale and Doppler artifacts described, such as acoustic shadowing, reverberation, mirror imaging, aliasing, blooming, and twinkling, are at some point introduced in the physics and clinical scanning courses. Thus, disparities in educational focus, clinical experience, and ongoing professional development may lead to discrepancies in awareness levels across radiology practitioners. This is supported by a previous study finding which reported considerable disparities in artifact awareness within professional categories, with participants employed in public institutions and hospitals exhibiting higher average knowledge scores. The educational and institutional diversity underscores the significance of this survey in objectively evaluating sonographic professionals’ understanding of grayscale and Doppler artifacts and in pinpointing areas that may require focused training initiatives [10].

The focus of this study is to objectively evaluate the sonographers knowledge of grayscale and Doppler artifacts and to identify some of the gaps that can be filled by further educational interventions.

There is a lack of studies in the literature concerning the assessment of sonographer’s knowledge of Doppler artifacts; this is considered the first study in Sudan to provide insight into sonographers’ knowledge about both grayscale and Doppler artifacts. Knowledge of these artifacts, including how to identify, manage, and interpret them, is crucial for accurate diagnosis and image interpretation.

.

Methodology

A descriptive cross-sectional study design was used. An electronic self-administered survey was distributed among sonographers between May and June 2025, to determine their awareness about ultrasound artifact (grayscale and Doppler). The sample size (n) was estimated utilizing the formula for sample size with an infinite (unknown) population (n = P (1-P) Z2/ d2) with a margin error (d) of 0.05, Z value of 1.96 (for a 95% confidence interval), and a population ratio of 0.5, yielding a determined sample size of 385 respondents. Google Forms analytics indicated that the survey link was accessed/viewed 384 times (“reach”), and 152 participants completed the questionnaire and were included in the analysis, corresponding to a completion rate of 39.5% (152/384) among those who opened the form.

Data collection

Data were collected using a self-administered online questionnaire developed by the authors and distributed via Google Forms (https://forms.gle/kvqbCfLwjSgFseFC8 ). It is distributed via social media such as WhatsApp and Facebook groups, which are widely used by sonographers in Sudan for professional communication, education, and networking. These groups included licensed sonographers and sonographic specialists working in both governmental and private healthcare settings. To reduce the likelihood of duplicate responses, the Google Forms settings were adjusted to limit submissions to one per device and browser session, and participants were directed to complete the questionnaire solely once. The dataset was examined for potential duplication by analyzing response timestamps, answer patterns, and completion durations to detect unusually similar or repeated items. No identical or dubious duplicate responses were identified during the data cleansing process.

Before distribution a pilot study was conducted among 15 respondents to assess the validity and reliability of the questionnaire. The resulting Cronbach’s alpha was 0.830, indicating a good value of internal consistency. Additionally, the questionnaire was reviewed and an academic consultant sonographic specialist with more than fifteen years of experience before its distribution through social media (WhatsApp and Facebook).

Statistics and data analysis

The data was analyzed using SPSS version 27 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and DATAtab statistical online calculator 2025 (https://datatab.net). Descriptive and inferential statistics were used. Mean and median were utilized for the score of knowledge of grayscale and Doppler artifacts, and for total score. Participants’ familiarity with artifact was assessed by eleventh questions (7 of them for Doppler artifact and 4 for grayscale, each of which had a correct answer assigned a value of one (1) and an incorrect response assigned a value of zero (0). Weighted average score was intended for the score of knowledge of gray scale, Doppler, and total (Summing the mean for all questions in both domains). Prior to correlation analysis, the normality test was implemented for the mean score of knowledge. The results showed that the degree of knowledge was abnormally distributed, with a p-values (Kolmogorov–Smirnov, p < 0.001 for grayscale and for doppler, and < 0.05 for total score of knowledge; Shapiro–Wilk, p < 0.001). Nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess the significance of the difference in mean rank between genders, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis H test was utilized to appraise the differences in rank among different age groups, education levels, and years of experience. Moreover, the chi-square test is used to judge the correlation between demographic characteristics and questions deal with practice.

Discussion

This study assessed the knowledge of sonographers regarding most common grayscale and Doppler ultrasound artifacts while clinical scanning. The artifacts the study focused on included acoustic shadowing, reverberation, mirror image, acoustic enhancement, aliasing, blooming, twinkling, and flash artifacts, broadening, and the scatter. These specific artifacts were chosen, as they are encountered in daily scanning, and ultrasound practitioners are expected to identify and describe them in the context of image quality and/or diagnostic value, especially in the routine applications of ultrasound in the abdomen, obstetrics and gynecology, kidneys, and superficial structures. With the aim of evaluating knowledge that is clinically and image quality oriented, the current study focused on these artifacts, and aims to evaluating the participants’ knowledge of more obscure artifacts that are encountered in the routine clinical practice.

Some Ultrasound artifacts are beneficial for diagnosis and they are utilized instinctively to differentiate tissue. For instance, acoustic enhancement posterior to an anechoic structure confirms the diagnosis of a simple cyst. Similarly, acoustic shadowing distal to hyperechoic structure leads to the diagnosis of stones or calcification. Additionally, the appearance of twinkle in color Doppler confirms the diagnosis of stones. If unidentified, ultrasound artifacts can lead to serious misdiagnoses. Identifying these artifacts is vital, as they can provide insights into tissue composition and support the diagnosis [6, 11–13].

The current study found good knowledge and awareness of grayscale and Doppler artifacts. It was found that the knowledge of grayscale artifacts was a higher score (77.2%) than in Doppler artifact (73.1%), which was similar to a study conducted by Gameraddin et al. in the Western region of Saudi Arabia. They found good knowledge concerning grayscale ultrasound artifacts among sonographers (mean 0.77) [10].

The sonographic specialists in this study showed a good understanding of the causes of acoustic shadowing, including the strong reflection from bone or calcification mentioned by 88.8%, the factors that cause mirror image artifacts (81.1%), the appearance of reverberation artifacts (71.1%), and the majority of them were aware that the enhancement artifact is caused by low sound wave attenuation (67.1%). In contrast, Alsaedi et al. reported that 85%, 71%, 73%, 69%, 54%, and 53% of the respondents accurately identified the following artifacts: ring down, side lobe, reverberation, mirror, acoustic shadowing, and acoustic enhancement [14]. On the other hand, Gameraddin et al. reported that 91% of the participants aware of acoustic shadowing artifacts, and 82.9% identified the cause of reverberation artifacts. Furthermore, they reported that the awareness of contact artifacts, acoustic shadowing, and reverberation artifacts was average [10].

In the present study, no significant differences were observed in ultrasound artifact knowledge across demographic characteristics such as age, years of experience, gender, and education level. This finding differs from the report by Alshamrani et al. and Alsaedi et al., who demonstrated a significant association between age and years of experience and awareness of renal grayscale ultrasound artifacts [14, 15]. The discrepancy may be explained by differences in study scope and design. While Alshamrani et al. focused on a specific ultrasound application (renal imaging) and a defined subset of artifacts, the current study assessed awareness of commonly encountered grayscale and Doppler artifacts across multiple clinical applications, including abdominal, obstetric–gynecological, renal, and superficial imaging. Furthermore, this contradiction might be attributed to differences in the characteristics of the participants, as in this study, only sonographic specialists were included, with the majority of them holding an M.Sc. in diagnostic ultrasound.

Concerning Doppler artifacts, the respondents demonstrated average knowledge, with insignificant differences in the degree of knowledge with demographic characteristics. Aliasing is the predominant Doppler artifact that arises when the velocity range surpasses the scale that depicts it. It appears on the screen as a reversed flow with a color shift, leaving no black or no flow space between. In the Doppler spectrum imaging, the highest frequencies are taken off at the top and presented below the baseline, pointing upwards. The velocity scale is restricted by the pulse repetition frequency (PRF), which is the frequency of pulses per second that the transducer can broadcast and receive. Aliasing is crucial for detecting high velocity flow in stenosed arteries or arteriovenous fistulas. To avoid this, increase the PRF or scale, shift the baseline in spectral Doppler, increase the Doppler angle, or use a low-frequency transducer [16]. The majority of the respondents were aware that aliasing is a common Doppler artifact. On the other hand, a significant number of them are unable to determine that increasing PRF is the factor that reduces aliasing.

The majority of the respondents aware of the cause of blooming artifact, which is a Doppler color phenomenon manifesting as color, overflowing posterior to the margins of the blood vessel, making them as larger and obscuring the vessel wall [16].

A significant number of the respondents (68.4%) understand that the flash artifact, which can be observed in spectral Doppler as a symmetric small signal upper and below the baseline, is most noticeable during systole. It is helpful since “color bruit,” which appears as a color mosaic in the tissue nearby a vessel with turbulent flow due to vascular tissue vibration; it can be reduced by decreasing the motion [16].

Twinkling artifacts are mosaics of rapidly shifting red and blue colors in a powerful reflector with color Doppler. Machine settings influence the artifact’s appearance and is likely caused by phase jitter, a narrow-band noise. Surface roughness expands the noise spectrum, and with a rough surface like a kidney stone, the high-amplitude signal manifests as random motion [16–18]. Most of the respondents correctly identify the calcification as one of the main causes of twinkle artifacts.

Most respondents correctly identified that adjusting the angle of insonation is one factor to reduce mirror artifact, which arises in Doppler systems when an image of a vessel and a source of Doppler shifted echoes are repeated on the other side of a powerful reflector, possibly misinterpreting the duplicated vessel as a new vessel [19, 20]. It was found that 69.7% of the respondents understand the broadening Doppler phenomenon caused by turbulent flow. The ability to discriminate and address correctable artifacts is crucial for refining image quality and care of patient [12, 21, 22]. Continued education and training in determining Doppler and US artifacts are mandatory to enhance ultrasound diagnostic accuracy and performance.

Although the study provides valuable insights into sonographic specialist’s knowledge about grayscale and Doppler artifacts, the small sample size confines the results’ generalizability to a larger population. Furthermore, using surveys introduces potential biases similar to those found in self-reported data. The questionnaire’s design may also have restricted the participants’ responses. Also, inequality distribution number of participant across different demographic characteristic is one contributing factors for determination of the impact of these characters on knowledge of artifacts.

Limitations

The study has some limitations need to be considered. The generalizability of the results to the sonographers of Sudan or other areas may be limited due to the sample size (n = 152) and the overall participation rate (39.5%) was not large enough. There is also the potential for self-reporting bias to affect the data, primarily because participants completed self-administered online surveys, and this may not accurately capture the participants’ practical knowledge or performance of the tasks in the clinical setting.

The unequal distribution of study participants in demographic variables, like gender, years of experience, and education level, may have impacted the study’s statistical power to identify significant relationships. Moreover, the study’s cross-sectional design entails knowledge and awareness assessment in one instance and, therefore, permits no causal assessments.

The focus of the questionnaire was limited to certain grayscale and Doppler artifacts, which may have precluded the inclusion of several ultrasound artifacts that participants encounter in their practice. Therefore, we recommend the use of larger, multi-center samples and the inclusion of tools for objective assessments like direct observation or simulation-based testing.

Conclusion

Good knowledge concerning grayscale and Doppler artifact was noticed among Sudanese sonographic specialists. In grayscale, the respondents demonstrate higher knowledge about causes of acoustic shadowing and mirror artifact. Furthermore, in Doppler, the respondents demonstrate higher knowledge about the aliasing as the commonest artifact, the Doppler artifact produced by calcification, and about the contributing factor to the blooming artifact. The score of knowledge was higher in grayscale than in Doppler artifact, with no impact of demographic characteristics on the mean rank of knowledge.

Recommendations

Implementation of regular webinars, workshops, and specialized courses focused on ultrasound artifacts. The integration of separate module contains all details about gray scale and doppler ultrasound artifacts in all training programs. Also, activation of using the ultrasound simulators which represents the artifact scenarios and the correction options in a safe environment with negative risk to the patients. Furthermore, utilize the periodic discussions of a real cases which contains artifacts affected the diagnosis in each department or health care facility. Also, we recommended the collaboration with an expert sonographer, or radiologist, or physicists to share their knowledge about ultrasound artifacts and their management’s.
