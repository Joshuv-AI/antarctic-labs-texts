Decoding the lung-brain axis in influenza: from pulmonary infection to central nervous system injury
Authors: Xindan Liang, Xiaoqi Wang, Zhibin Wang, Chunjing Chen, Ling Li, Fangguo Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Influenza viruses are respiratory pathogens of the family Orthomyxoviridae. Among the four recognized types, influenza A virus (IAV) is the primary cause of seasonal epidemics and pandemics. Continuous antigenic drift facilitates immune evasion, whereas occasional antigenic shift drives the emergence of novel hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) subtype combinations. Although influenza is primarily characterized by respiratory manifestations, it can also cause severe central nervous system (CNS) complications, including encephalitis, collectively referred to as influenza-associated encephalopathy (IAE), which is associated with poor clinical outcomes and remains frequently overlooked. The neurotropic potential of influenza viruses remains controversial. Although vaccination and antiviral therapies effectively reduce influenza-related morbidity and mortality, specific preventive and therapeutic strategies for influenza-associated CNS complications remain limited. This review synthesizes current evidence on the neuroinvasive characteristics of influenza viruses, including susceptibility factors, viral determinants, host responses, pathogenic mechanisms, therapeutic strategies, and prognostic indicators. Particular emphasis is placed on the mechanisms underlying CNS injury, including both direct viral invasion and indirect injury mediated by dysregulated immune and inflammatory responses. By integrating these emerging mechanistic insights, this review proposes a lung–brain axis framework to explain the bidirectional communication between pulmonary infection and neurological injury during influenza. This framework provides an updated perspective on the pathogenesis of IAE, identifies potential therapeutic targets, and may facilitate future studies aimed at improving early diagnosis, risk stratification, and the development of mechanism-based interventions.

Introduction

Influenza viruses cause seasonal epidemics and global pandemics, with approximately 1 billion infections occurring worldwide each year, including 3–5 million severe cases and 290,000–650,000 deaths, according to the World Health Organization. While influenza is widely recognized for its respiratory manifestations, its potential to cause severe central nervous system (CNS) complications, including encephalitis, myelitis, meningitis, seizures, Guillain-Barré syndrome, and cerebellar ataxia, has received less attention despite their often poor prognosis [1–3].

Influenza viruses belong to the family Orthomyxoviridae and are classified into four antigenically and genetically distinct species: influenza A, B, C, and D viruses. Influenza A, B, and C viruses are known to infect humans [4]. Influenza C virus usually causes mild disease and has been less extensively studied. Consequently, current research has focused primarily on influenza A virus (IAV), including human subtypes such as H1N1, H1N2, H2N2, and H3N2; avian-origin variants such as H5N1 and H7N9; and influenza B virus (IBV). IAV and IBV infections commonly present with fever, chills, headache, cough, sore throat, anorexia, and myalgia [4]. In some cases, however, they may also induce CNS pathology and neurological symptoms, collectively referred to as influenza-associated encephalopathy (IAE), with most studies focusing on IAV [1, 5–7].

IAE has been reported in association with pandemic, seasonal, and zoonotic influenza viruses. Historical cases can be traced to the 1957 Asian flu (H2N2) [8], the 2009 global pandemic (pH1N1) [9], the 1998 Japanese winter outbreak [10], the 2019 Nepalese seasonal outbreak [11], and occasional outbreaks caused by animal-origin IAV such as H7N9 [12]. IAE occurs predominantly in young children, particularly those younger than 5 years of age [13]. Elderly individuals, pregnant women, and immunocompromised patients are also at increased risk of CNS complications because of their physiological or immune status. These complications are associated with high mortality and impose a substantial public health and socioeconomic burden.

Following the COVID-19 pandemic, the neuroinvasive potential of respiratory viruses has attracted increasing global attention. In recent years, numerous epidemiological studies, retrospective studies, and case reports have investigated IAE [14–16]. According to their clinical and neuroradiological characteristics, IAE comprises several clinical syndromes.Acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE), the most severe subtype, is characterized by bilateral thalamic lesions and is associated with high rates of neurological sequelae and mortality.Acute encephalopathy with biphasic seizures and late reduced diffusion (AESD) typically presents with biphasic seizures and delayed diffusion restriction predominantly involving the subcortical white matter on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Mild encephalitis/encephalopathy with a reversible splenial lesion (MERS) is characterized by a reversible lesion within the splenium of the corpus callosum (SCC) and generally has a favorable prognosis. Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is characterized by reversible vasogenic edema predominantly affecting the parieto-occipital regions. Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) is an encephalitic disorder associated with spinal cord demyelination and characterized by multifocal white matter lesions following influenza infection [3, 17, 18]. Traditionally, highly pathogenic avian influenza viruses (HPAIVs), such as H5N1 and H7N9, have been recognized as neurotropic, whereas seasonal and pandemic IAV strains are generally considered to have limited intrinsic neurotropism. However, the neurotropic potential of influenza virus remains unclear. Debate persists regarding whether influenza viruses should be classified as neurotropic or non-neurotropic and whether both types can cause CNS manifestations. The structural determinants underlying these differences also remain uncertain. Furthermore, whether CNS manifestations induced by different influenza virus subtypes result primarily from productive viral replication or from abortive infection with non-replicative protein production remains incompletely understood. The mechanisms underlying IAE remain unclear, and substantial gaps in knowledge persist.

At present, the treatment of IAE primarily focuses on antiviral therapy targeting the underlying infection and supportive management of respiratory and neurological symptoms. Although seasonal influenza vaccination reduces the risk of influenza infection and its associated neurological complications [27], no disease-specific therapeutic strategies have been established for IAE, posing a substantial clinical challenge. By analogy to the gut-brain axis the concept of the lung-brain axis has been proposed to explain how pulmonary infections may remotely affect the brain. In this review, we summarize the current literature to clarify the mechanisms, recent advances, and current therapeutic approaches related to influenza-associated neurological manifestations through the lung-brain axis, thereby providing a reference for future studies aimed at the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of IAE.

Neurotropism of influenza viruses

Influenza viruses exhibit varying neuroinvasive properties and are generally categorized as neurotropic or non-neurotropic. Interestingly, both types can cause neurological complications, indicating that a viral strain's ability to enter the CNS and directly infect neurons does not solely determine its potential to induce neurological effects. A long-standing debate persists regarding whether influenza viruses directly invade the CNS, the mechanisms underlying such invasion, and whether certain influenza viruses acquire the ability to invade the CNS under specific conditions. These questions remain incompletely understood and require further investigation.

Neurotropic influenza virus

Certain influenza viruses exhibit neurotropic properties and are capable of invading and replicating within the CNS, with whole viruses, viral RNAs, or viral antigens being detected in the CNS or CSF. HPAI H5N1 and H7N9 viruses, both zoonotic pathogens, have been linked to CNS disorders. HPAI H5N1 can penetrate the CNS, triggering innate immune responses characterized by neuroinflammation, microglial activation, and increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [38]. In a fatal pediatric case, infectious H5N1 virus was isolated from the CSF, indicating direct CNS infection [39]. Another child with H5N1 encephalitis complicated by obstructive hydrocephalus had detectable viral RNA in the CSF despite minimal respiratory symptoms [34]. Human autopsy studies have demonstrated the presence of H5N1 viral antigen and RNA in multiple brain regions, including the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, brainstem, and hippocampus, accompanied by neuronal degeneration and inflammatory responses [35]. By contrast, evidence for direct CNS infection by other HPAI subtypes, such as H7N9 and H5N6, is derived primarily from animal models or limited postmortem observations. Avian H7N9 viral RNA persists at elevated levels in mouse brain tissues, with infectious virus isolated from brain filtrates, potentially leading to severe CNS damage [40]. In H7N7-infected mice, infectious viral particles have been identified in multiple brain regions, including the midbrain, thalamus, and medulla, indicating efficient viral replication within the brain [41]. Postmortem analyses have revealed the presence of H5N6 viral nucleoprotein (NP) in brain tissues [37], whereas in vivo studies confirm that HPAI H5N6 can infect, replicate, and disseminate throughout the CNS [42]. These findings indicate that specific influenza viruses can effectively infect and propagate within the CNS.

Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to human IAE. Murine models are valuable for investigating viral replication, neuroinflammation, and host immune responses; however, mice are not natural hosts for human influenza viruses and differ from humans in sialic acid (SA) receptor distribution, viral susceptibility, immune responses, brain anatomy, and disease manifestations [43, 44]. Therefore, CNS replication observed in mice may partly reflect host adaptation, inoculation route, viral dose, or strain-specific neurovirulence rather than mechanisms that universally occur in human disease. Ferrets more closely resemble humans in respiratory tract physiology, clinical manifestations, and SA receptor distribution and are therefore widely used to investigate influenza pathogenesis and transmission [45, 46]. However, ferret models also have important limitations. Current studies have primarily focused on viral transmission and neuroinvasion, whereas systematic investigations of blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction, neurovascular injury, and immune-mediated mechanisms remain limited. In addition, the limited availability of species-specific reagents and genetically modified ferret models constrains mechanistic studies [47]. Therefore, although ferrets provide an important translational model for influenza pathogenesis, they do not fully recapitulate the complex pathophysiological features of human IAE. Moreover, different animal models may yield distinct findings. Most experimental infection models rely on intranasal or intratracheal inoculation of a large volume of viral suspension under anesthesia, which does not fully mimic natural exposure. Alternative inoculation approaches that more closely resemble natural infection are currently being explored [48].Overall, although animal models have provided valuable insights into influenza neurotropism and neuropathogenesis, their findings should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to human IAE. Integration of evidence from human CSF, postmortem brain tissue, and well-characterized clinical cases remains essential for validating the clinical relevance of these experimental observations.

Non-neurotropic influenza viruses

Non-neurotropic influenza viruses primarily replicate in respiratory epithelial cells and rarely invade the CNS; however, they can indirectly affect neurological function. Seasonal influenza viruses (H1N1 and H3N2) are generally classified as non-neurotropic. Nevertheless, they may induce neurological dysfunction through interconnected indirect and conditional direct mechanisms. First, it is widely recognized that non-neurotropic influenza viruses cause neurological complications primarily through inflammatory mediator storms or immune response dysregulation, rather than by directly infecting the CNS [41, 49]. In a study by Jurgens et al. [50], 4–6 week-old mice intranasally inoculated with H1N1 (A/PR/8/34) exhibited cytokine-mediated cognitive and behavioral impairments at 7 days post-infection (dpi). Allison Y Louie et al. [51] intranasally inoculated C57BL/6 J mice with PR8 influenza virus and observed no significant reduction in oligodendrocyte numbers or myelin protein loss but noted a stress-induced state and remodeling of the myelin lipid profile. These findings indicate that non-neurotropic influenza viruses can substantially alter white matter structure and function through inflammatory and immune-mediated mechanisms. Second, inflammatory disruption of the neurovascular unit may create permissive conditions for limited viral entry or viral protein expression in selected CNS-associated cells. Some studies have reported that even non-neurotropic influenza strains may infiltrate the brain and cause neurological disorders. For instance, viral particles were detected in postmortem brain tissues from patients infected with H3N2 [52, 53]. Recent research has identified infectious virus and viral RNA in the brains of mice with H1N1 PR8-induced brain edema (IVE), together with viral protein accumulation in the brains of IVE mice and patients with IAE [54]. However, it remains unclear whether the findings observed in 3–4-week-old mice are directly applicable to adult animals. The extent to which non-neurotropic influenza viruses can directly invade the CNS under special conditions, such as altered cytokine profiles or changes in immune status, and whether factors such as sex, age, or other host characteristics influence this process, remains a subject of debate.

Replication-dependent and replication-independent mechanisms of IAV-induced CNS injury

The traditional view holds that productive replication of IAV within the brain primarily drives the occurrence and progression of CNS manifestations. Numerous studies have investigated whether influenza viruses replicate in CNS cells, directly damage the CNS, or trigger inflammatory responses and immune dysregulation, thereby facilitating further viral dissemination. For example, influenza virus replication in the brain has been associated with more intense and prolonged microglial activation, adversely affecting cognitive performance [41].Following mutation, the ability of H9N2 to invade brain tissue correlates with its replication competence in cerebral tissues [55]. However, recent evidence suggests that CNS injury may also occur in the absence of productive viral replication. In this context, it is important to distinguish productive infection, which generates infectious progeny virions, from abortive or non-productive infection, in which viral entry and limited viral transcription or protein synthesis occur without completion of the full replication cycle. Current evidence indicates that influenza viruses entering brain endothelial cells (ECs) and undergoing subsequent translation may trigger IAE. Observations in IVE mice and patients with IAE have shown that IAV enters ECs, promoting viral nucleoprotein (NP) accumulation in ECs and the brain parenchyma without significant viral proliferation, ultimately leading to cerebral edema and severe neurological symptoms [54]. These findings indicate that viral transcription and translation, even in the absence of productive replication, may be sufficient to trigger endothelial dysfunction, cerebral edema, and severe neurological symptoms. Therefore, the pathogenic effects of IAV in IAE may arise not only from productive replication within CNS tissues but also from abortive infection.

Central nervous system barrier

The brain barrier system consists of specialized metabolic, physical, and transport barriers between the cerebral blood vessels, CSF, and brain parenchyma. It comprises the endothelial BBB, blood-CSF barrier (BCSFB), and CSF-brain barrier (CSFBB), all of which are crucial for maintaining homeostasis of the brain microenvironment [56]. This system not only supplies nutrients and energy to the CNS but also protects the CNS microenvironment from toxic substances, pathogenic invasion, and fluctuations in blood components [57].

Blood–brain barrier

The BBB is formed between the brain tissue and circulating blood as part of the neurovascular unit and represents the most highly regulated and selective barrier in the body [58]. Rather than serving solely as a passive anatomical barrier, the BBB functions as an active interface involved in viral sensing, immune signaling, and neurovascular homeostasis. Brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs), connected by tight junctions (TJs), adherens junctions (AJs), and gap junctions (GJs), form the structural basis of BBB selectivity. Pericytes, which exhibit a jagged morphology, envelop approximately 37% of the EC surface. Both cell types are embedded within the basement membrane. Astrocytic endfeet encase approximately 99% of the combined EC and pericyte surfaces (Fig. 1) [59]. In the context of influenza infection, these cellular and junctional components are directly relevant to neuroinvasion. Experimental studies have shown that influenza viruses can impair brain endothelial function and disrupt TJs, thereby increasing BBB permeability and facilitating CNS involvement [60, 61]. These findings suggest that IAE may arise not only from direct viral invasion of neural tissues but also from disruption of BBB integrity. In addition to TJs and ECs, the endothelial surface glycocalyx (ESG) has recently been recognized as an important luminal component of the BBB. The ESG is composed primarily of proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and glycolipids and may contribute to vascular barrier stability. Experimental studies have shown that glycocalyx disruption can impair BBB integrity and increase vascular permeability in non-influenza neurological disease models [62, 63]. During influenza infection, viral hemagglutinin primarily mediates viral attachment through host glycans, including sialylated receptors, and recent studies have also discussed potential interactions between H5N1 and respiratory glycocalyx-associated glycosaminoglycans [64]. However, direct evidence that influenza virus infection or influenza-associated encephalopathy causes structural damage to the brain endothelial glycocalyx remains lacking. Therefore, whether influenza-induced alterations in the cerebral ESG contribute to BBB dysfunction in IAE should be regarded as a hypothesis that warrants further experimental investigation.

Blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB)

The CSF is primarily synthesized and secreted by the choroid plexus (CP), which facilitates brain development and maintains physiological functions. The CP is the principal component of the BCSFB and is located within the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles of the brain, where it forms an interface between blood and CSF (Fig. 1). The remaining components of the BCSFB are formed by arachnoid barrier epithelial cells and specialized ependymal cells in regions such as the circumventricular organs. Compared with the BBB, BCSFB is relatively permeable, facilitating transchoroidal transport between blood and CSF through several structural features: (1) ECs of the capillary loops constitute the first cellular barrier between the blood and CSF, and their characteristic fenestrations permit efficient diffusion of molecules up to 800 kDa [65–67]; (2) the extensively interdigitated basolateral membranes and densely packed apical microvilli of CP epithelial cells significantly increase the surface area available for molecular exchange; and (3) the CP exhibits the highest regional blood flow rate among all brain structures, approximately 4 mL·g⁻1·min⁻1 [68].

Cerebrospinal fluid-brain barrier (CSFBB)

The CSFBB forms a barrier between the CSF and the brain tissue within the ventricles and subarachnoid space (SAS) (Fig. 1). In the ventricles, the CSFBB consists of a single layer of ependymal cells [69, 70], connected primarily by AJs near their apical surfaces. GJs have also been reported within the ependymal layer [70, 71], forming extracellular spaces of 2–4 nm [72], that permit the passage of macromolecules, including proteins of various molecular weights [73, 74] and tracer molecules [72, 75]. In vivo studies have shown that, after weaning in mice, the CSFBB permits size-independent diffusion of molecules up to 70 kDa [76]. Although these permeable extracellular spaces exist, the ventricular membrane also functions as a regulatory barrier that limis diffusion between the CSF and brain parenchyma while modulating substance transport and metabolism.

Influenza virus directly invades the CNS: pathways connecting the lung and the brain

Influenza viruses can invade the CNS through either hematogenous or neuronal pathways. In the hematogenous route, viral components reach the cerebral vasculature and cross the BBB or BCSFB to enter the CNS. In the neuronal route, peripheral nerves, including the olfactory, trigeminal, and vagus nerves, are initially infected, followed by axonal transport into the CNS through motor protein–mediated retrograde and anterograde transport within neurons [77].

Hematogenous route

Respiratory viruses may enter the CNS through the BBB and BCSFB via transcellular or paracellular routes or through the "Trojan horse" mechanism, in which infected leukocytes migrate across these barriers. Alternatively, viruses may directly damage barrier cells (Fig. 2). Although several mechanisms have been proposed for how influenza viruses cross the BBB and BCSFB, direct experimental evidence remains limited. In some cases, direct damage mediated by viral virulence factors has been implicated. In most instances, however, barrier permeability is regulated by multiple mediators, including vasoactive substances, inflammatory mediators, and lipid mediators [78], and is influenced by factors such as inflammatory responses, cytokine storms, and immune status. Disruption of barrier structural integrity

The physical barrier function may be compromised either through direct damage to ECs, pericytes, astrocytes, and TJs by influenza viral virulence factors or through indirect mechanisms such as cytokine storm induction and immune response activation, ultimately facilitating viral invasion into the CNS. The principal mechanisms underlying influenza virus transit across the BBB involve alterations in the expression and phosphorylation of TJs and AJs between ECs (Fig. 2A), disruption of the basement membrane, and destruction of the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 2B), with transient TJ relaxation playing a major regulatory role. In vitro studies using human cortical microvessels endothelial cells/D3 (hCMEC/D3) cells infected with H7N9 revealed no effect on cell viability but demonstrated altered cell morphology and downregulation of key junctional proteins, including claudin-5, occludin, and VE-cadherin [60], resulting in impaired BBB integrity, increased permeability, and enhanced viral dissemination. In an HPAI H7N1-infected chicken model, viral RNA appeared in the blood at 18 h post-infection (hpi), with preferential infection of BMECs by 24 hpi, followed by disruption of zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and Evans blue leakage, confirming BBB dysfunction and permitting viral particles and serum proteins to enter the brain parenchyma [61]. Following neurotropic H7N7 infection in mice, direct viral invasion and the associated inflammatory response increased BBB permeability, facilitating viral entry into the brain parenchyma. In contrast, the non-neurotropic H3N2 strain has been shown to disrupt the BBB through peripheral inflammation, resulting in microglia activation and cytokine release within the brain [80].

Nevertheless, substantial BBB disruption is not consistently observed across different experimental models of IAE. The extent of BBB impairment varies considerably depending on the viral strain, host species, disease severity, and experimental conditions. In the HPAIV models described above, marked BBB leakage accompanied by the loss of TJs has been demonstrated. In contrast, MERS is thought to be predominantly associated with cytotoxic edema and inflammation-mediated reversible injury rather than overt structural BBB disruption [81, 82].Furthermore, host genetic susceptibility, including pathogenic variants in RANBP2 and CPT2, may increase neuronal vulnerability to inflammatory and metabolic insults, thereby influencing the severity of IAE [83, 84]. Consequently, the occurrence of IAE may not necessarily correlate solely with the extent of structural BBB disruption.

Transcellular permeation

Transcellular transport across the brain barriers occurs primarily through receptor-mediated transport and transcytosis, and influenza viruses may traverse these barriers by transcellular transport (Fig. 2C). SA residues on N-linked glycoproteins act as attachment factors for viral binding, although they do not directly mediate endocytosis. Their absence impairs viral binding and internalization [85]. Metabotropic glutamate receptor 2 (mGluR2), a seven-transmembrane N-linked glycoprotein belonging to the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily [86], binds to viral HA and functions as an endocytic receptor that mediates clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) of influenza viruses across the BBB and BCSFB into the brain parenchyma. mGluR2-knockout mice exhibit increased resistance to multiple influenza subtypes, with significantly reduced viral replication in both pulmonary and cerebral tissues [87].

Immune cell trafficking

ECs provide an effective barrier against the entry of influenza virus particles into the CNS, while permitting the transmigration of infected immune cells, including monocytes, neutrophils, and natural killer (NK)/T cells. The "Trojan horse" mechanism provides an alternative route by which viruses can cross the BBB and BCSFB to reach the brain parenchyma (Fig. 2D). When viruses infect immune cells, CNS entry may occur via endothelial transmigration of these infected cells. This mechanism has been described for viruses such as West Nile virus [88], Enterovirus A71 [89], and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [90, 91]. However, direct evidence that influenza viruses enter the brain through infected immune cells remains lacking.

Nevertheless, Several observations suggest that this possibility cannot be completely excluded.The highly pathogenic H5N1 strain (A/HK/483/97), isolated during the 1997 Hong Kong outbreak, efficiently infects and replicates in primary human alveolar macrophages [92]. Human macrophages and dendritic cells (DC) are also susceptible to H1N1 and H5N1 infections [93, 94]. Both seasonal influenza strains (NC/99 and Tx/91) and pandemic strains (the 1918 Spanish flu and 2009 Cal/09) can infect DCs, with seasonal strains replicating efficiently [95]. Nevertheless, several studies have reported that influenza virus infection in these immune cells may be abortive, whereby viral entry and initial gene expression occur but replication is halted before completion, ultimately preventing the production and release of infectious viral particles [96, 97]. The factors determining whether influenza virus replication in immune cells proceeds to viral assembly and release remain unclear. Nonetheless, influenza viruses possess the capacity to infect immune cells and may potentially complete replication. Although H3N2 does not directly invade the CNS, it activates peripheral immunity, induces interferon-γ (IFN-γ) release, stimulates glial cells to express inflammatory chemokines, and promotes immune cell infiltration into the CNS. Exposure to influenza virus may also increase immune cell migration across the BBB and BCSFB [80]. Therefore, although leukocyte-mediated CNS entry remains biologically plausible, it should be regarded as a speculative and unproven route of influenza virus neuroinvasion rather than an established mechanism.

Neural route

Respiration is an autonomic process involving gas exchange between the respiratory tract and the external environment, and is tightly regulated by the nervous system [98, 99]. Various types of neurons, including olfactory, trigeminal, vagus, and sympathetic nerves, project into the respiratory tract and form synapses within different respiratory regions [100]. Following respiratory infection, influenza viruses may gain access to the CNS through peripheral neural pathways, although the supporting evidence is stronger for HPAIV than for seasonal influenza viruses.

Invasion via the olfactory nerve

The olfactory nerve serves as the gateway to the brain. When odorant molecules bind to ciliary receptors on olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs), action potentials are generated and transmitted along the olfactory fila to the olfactory bulb and subsequently via the olfactory tract to the telencephalon [101, 102] (Fig. 3A). In murine models, following intranasal inoculation with HPAI H5N1, viral antigens were detected in the glomerular and mitral cell layers of the olfactory bulb by 5 dpi, with subsequent dissemination to the frontal lobe. In H1N1/PR8 infected mice, viral antigens were abundantly detected in the olfactory bulbs of sham-operated controls but were significantly reduced in olfactory nerve-transected mice, highlighting the olfactory nerve as the principal route of CNS entry [103]. In ferret, multiple influenza subtypes including H5N1, H1N1, and H7N9 have been isolated from the olfactory bulb, and patterns of CNS invasion through the olfactory mucosa have been observed during H5N1 infection [104]. Human autopsy data remain limited, with current evidence derived primarily from case reports [102]. Although OSNs are susceptible to direct viral infection, fatal encephalitis is uncommon because microglial provide an innate defense against neuroinvasive nasal infections by cross-presenting antigens to non-cytolytic antiviral T cells [105]. This olfactory neural pathway, which connects the respiratory tract to the brain, enables influenza viruses to bypass conventional barriers such as the BBB and BCSFB, facilitating direct CNS invasion and potentially resulting in encephalitis or meningitis. Invasion via the trigeminal nerve and sympathetic nerve

In addition to the olfactory pathway, peripheral nerves have been proposed as potential routes for influenza virus entry into the CNS. The trigeminal nerve innervates the nasal mucosa and provides direct anatomical connections between the respiratory tract and the brainstem [107] (Fig. 3B). Although the olfactory nerve is a well-established pathway for IAV entry into the CNS in both ferrets and humans, the inefficient replication of the 1918/H1N1 virus in the olfactory bulb, together with the presence of viral antigens in the trigeminal ganglion and brainstem, suggests that alternative peripheral neural routes, including the trigeminal nerve, may contribute to viral transmission from the respiratory tract to the CNS [108]. Similarly, HPAIV strains, including AIV-H5N3 [109] and A/HK/97 (H5N1) [110], have been detected in the trigeminal ganglion following intranasal infection, supporting the possibility of transneuronal spread to the CNS.

Sympathetic neurons innervating the respiratory tract originate from the superior cervical ganglion (SCG), stellate ganglion (SG), and thoracic sympathetic ganglia (TSG; T2-T4 segments), projecting to the upper and middle trachea, lower trachea, and lungs, respectively [111]. Following intranasal infection with a neurotropic, mouse-adapted lethal dose (LD₅₀) of A/Hong Kong/483/97 (HK483) strain, viral antigens and RNA were detected in the trigeminal, superior cervical, and pterygopalatine ganglion [112]. These findings suggest that trigeminal and autonomic neural pathways may contribute to influenza virus neuroinvasion. However, the relative contribution of these peripheral nerves to CNS infection remains to be fully established.

Invasion via the vagus nerve and vestibulocochlear nerve

The vagus nerve is considered another potential route for influenza virus transmission from the respiratory tract to the CNS (Fig. 3B). Experimental evidence supporting this pathway is derived primarily from murine models [112, 113]. Most vagal sensory neurons respond to diverse mechanical and chemical stimuli, which may increases their susceptibility to viral neuroinvasion and enable the transmission of viruses, peripheral immune signals, and inflammatory mediators to the brain. In mice, intranasal administration of a neurotropic influenza virus infects both the respiratory tract and the CNS via the peripheral nervous system, particularly the vagus nerve. In a unilateral vagotomy model inoculated intranasally with influenza virus (24a5b), viral antigen appeared in the vagal ganglion on the vagotomized side 2 days later than on the intact side, providing direct evidence for transneuronal transport from the respiratory mucosa to the CNS via the vagus nerve [113]. However, evidence from other animal models is less consistent. In ferrets intranasally infected with wild-type HPAI H5N1 virus, viral antigen was detected predominantly along the olfactory pathway, whereas no viral antigen was identified in the vagus nerve or nodose ganglion, suggesting that the vagus nerve was not a major route of neuroinvasion in this model [114]. These findings indicate that the relative contribution of the vagus nerve to influenza virus neuroinvasion may depend on viral strain, host species, and experimental conditions. Further studies are required to clarify its role across different influenza virus subtypes and animal models.

The vestibulocochlear nerve may represent another potential neural route, although supporting evidence remains limited (Fig. 3B). Intranasal instillation of 10⁶ TCID₅₀ of clade 1 H5N1 virus (A/Vietnam/1203/04) in ferrets resulted in viral antigen detection within the Eustachian tube epithelium at 1 dpi and in the cochlea and vestibulocochlear nerve by 5 dpi, suggesting viral spread from the inner ear to the CNS [114].

Extracellular vesicles

Exosomes are nanosized extracellular vesicles (30–200 nm) of endosomal origin that carry proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, including small RNAs, and mediate intercellular communication in both physiological and pathological processes [115, 116]. Although the precise role of exosomes during influenza virus infection remains unclear, a dual-color exosome labeling strategy has enabled simultaneous visualization of exosomal membranes and luminal cargo, providing insight into their dynamic interactions with influenza viruses. Influenza virus infection has been shown to increase exosome release from host cells, and exosomes exhibit marked co-localization with viral particles in the extracellular space. In addition, exosomes contain complete IAV genomes and viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) complexes and may contribute to the intercellular transfer of viral components while modulating host immune responses [117] (Fig. 4). Because exosomes can cross the BBB, they have attracted considerable interest as drug delivery vehicles [118, 119]. However, current evidence supporting exosome-mediated influenza virus transport into the CNS is derived primarily from in vitro studies and experimental animal models, and its role in influenza neuropathogenesis remains hypothetical. Direct evidence demonstrating that exosomes mediate viral neuroinvasion or brain infection in humans is currently lacking. Therefore, although exosome-mediated transport represents a plausible mechanism contributing to IAE, its clinical relevance and translational significance require further validation in vivo.

The core of the lung–brain axis in influenza is not merely attributable to direct viral dissemination but rather to excessive activation of immune cells induced by pulmonary infection, which leads to uncontrolled overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and subsequent systemic amplification of inflammation [120]. Cytokine storm hypothesis: pulmonary inflammation as the upstream driver of brain injury

The innate immune system responds to influenza virus infection by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, interferons, and chemokines. When this response becomes dysregulated, it may result in systemic inflammation and tissue injury, commonly referred to as a cytokine storm [120]. Unlike direct viral neurotoxicity, which depends on viral entry into CNS cells followed by viral gene expression or replication, cytokine storm–mediated CNS injury is primarily driven by peripheral immune activation and inflammatory signaling. Influenza virus first activates innate immune recognition in respiratory epithelial cells, alveolar macrophages, and pulmonary vascular ECs, subsequently generating large amounts of inflammatory factors and chemokines through pathways such as TLR/RIG-I/NF-κB/IRF, particularly IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, CXCL8/IL-8, CCL2/MCP-1, and CXCL10/IP-10 [121].

Importantly, this systemic inflammatory cascade is not merely a byproduct of infection but serves as a long-distance signaling network capable of conveying pathological information from peripheral organs to the CNS. When the cytokine storm enters the systemic circulation, it can cross the BBB and affect the CNS, producing neurological symptoms [122]. Numerous clinical studies have also confirmed that patients with IAE exhibit significantly increased concentrations of cytokines in both serum and CSF, including interleukins (IL-6, IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-10), chemokines (CXCL10 and CCL2), TNF-α, and interferon (INF-γ) [123]. Among these, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α exert direct neurotoxic effects at high concentrations. They can impair vascular endothelial integrity, increase BBB permeability, and ultimately contribute to cerebral edema and a spectrum of CNS manifestations, associated with poor clinical outcomes [124]. Although substantial research has focused on the pathogenic roles and therapeutic targeting of certain cytokines, the contributions of others, such as IL-1β, IL-2, and IL-15, remain relatively underexplored.

MMP-9 and TIMP-1: a structural pathway linking pulmonary inflammation to BBB disruption

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, whereas tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) are their endogenous inhibitors that help maintain BBB integrity [125]. An imbalance in the MMP-9/TIMP-1 system has been recognized as an important feature of influenza-related neurological complications and may increase BBB permeability through extracellular matrix degradation and disruption of tight junctions, thereby potentially contributing to the development of IAE. Clinically, Ichiyama et al. [126] reported that serum MMP-9 levels and MMP-9/TIMP-1 ratios were elevated in patients with IAE, particularly in those with poor prognosis, indicating a potential association between this proteolytic imbalance and disease severity. In addition, clinical studies of viral encephalitis have shown that elevated MMP-9 is closely associated with BBB dysfunction, brain inflammation, and severe neurological outcomes [127]. Mechanistically, influenza virus infection activates peripheral innate immunity and neutrophil/monocyte–macrophage responses, thereby increasing host-derived MMP-9 secretion. Elevated MMP-9 can degrade type IV collagen within the basement membrane of the BBB, increase BBB permeability, and promote the influx of plasma cytokines and complement into the brain parenchyma, ultimately contributing to IAE [126]. In vitro, influenza virus infection of cultured cells induces MMP9 production while exerting limited effects on TIMP-1 expression, leading to an increased MMP-9/TIMP-1 ratio. In IAE with a poor prognosis, inflammatory networks (such as IL-10) may secondarily increase TIMP-1 expression, but this increase is insufficient to counteract the overall destructive effect of MMP-9 [126]. The influenza-induced inflammatory mediators described above, including IL-6 and TNF-α, can activate intracellular signaling pathways such as NF-κB, JAK/STAT, leading to downregulation of TJ proteins (occludin, claudin-5, and ZO-1), upregulation of adhesion molecules (ICAM-1 and VCAM-1), and increased permeability of the brain microvascular endothelium [128, 129]. At the same time, the expression of matrix metalloproteinases, particularly MMP-9, is upregulated, further degrading extracellular matrix components and compromising BBB integrity [130]. This process constitutes a functional reprogramming of the BBB, transforming it from a neuroprotective interface into a pathway that permits infiltration of inflammatory signals and immune cells.

Neuroimmune activation: secondary neuroinflammatory response

Pulmonary infection can induce an inflammatory state within the brain through systemic immune signals even in the absence of direct viral invasion of the brain parenchyma. This process primarily involves microglia and astrocytes. An autopsy study of eight patients with IAE [131] showed apoptosis of neurons and glial cells accompanied by marked microglial activation. Notably, microglial accumulation was more prominent in TUNEL-positive brains than in TUNEL-negative brains, suggesting that microglial activation is closely associated with apoptotic brain injury in IAE. Animal studies have provided further evidence. Sadasivan et al. [132] reported that following intranasal infection with the non-neurotropic A/CA/04/2009 H1N1 strain, there was no clear viral colonization in the brain, but significant Iba1-positive microglial activation occurred in the substantia nigra and hippocampus. Düsedau et al. [133] further demonstrated that low-dose respiratory infection with non-neurotropic H1N1 can induce a significant peripheral immune response, transient BBB disruption, and microglial activation.

Astrocytes, which are essential for maintaining BBB integrity and regulating inflammatory responses, also undergo functional alterations following stimulation. [134] directly stimulated astrocytes with the peripheral inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, and found that these cytokines upregulated aquaporin-4 (AQP4) expression, thereby promoting cerebral edema. Other studies [135] have shown that inhibition of the activated JNK/ERK signaling pathway attenuates IAV-induced neuronal loss and microglial activation in the hippocampus and cortex. HMGB1 and microRNAs: fine layers of cross-organ inflammation regulation and barrier homeostasis

Beyond cytokines and chemokines, additional regulatory factors may amplify the progression from systemic inflammation to BBB dysfunction and neurological injury. High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), a prototypical damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), plays a critical role in severe influenza. Evidence indicates that anti-HMGB1 antibodies markedly attenuates pulmonary microvascular hyperpermeability and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [139]. In the CNS, HMGB1 promotes BBB disruption and neuroinflammation through TLR4/RAGE signaling and can further enhance MMP-9 expression, thereby increasing vascular permeability and compromising barrier integrity [140, 141]. Although direct evidence in IAE remains limited, the established role of HMGB1 in inflammatory amplification and BBB dysfunction suggests its potential contribution to influenza-associated CNS injury.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and play essential roles in immune homeostasis, inflammatory signaling, and neurovascular function [142–144]. Previous studies have reported that hsa-miR-4657 and hsa-miR-342-3p are significantly downregulated in patients with severe IAE, suggesting their close association with inflammatory and immune responses [145]. Although direct evidence of miRNAs in the influenza lung–brain axis remains relatively limited, their role as upstream regulators and signal amplifiers is increasingly recognized. Influenza virus infection induces widespread alterations in host miRNA expression, many of which regulate innate immune and inflammatory signaling pathways [146]. Several miRNAs regulate endothelial activation, cytokine production, and BBB integrity by modulating TJs and inflammatory signaling pathways.For example, miR-155, a well-characterized pro-inflammatory miRNA, is induced by inflammatory stimuli and impairs endothelial barrier function under neuroinflammatory conditions [147]. Consistently, aberrant miRNA profiles have also been associated with infection-related encephalopathies and pathways linked to inflammation, MAPK signaling, and neuronal injury [148]. Collectively, these findings suggest that dysregulated HMGB1 and miRNAs expression not only amplifies systemic inflammatory signaling but also contributes to BBB dysfunction and neuroinflammation, thereby representing a potential molecular link between peripheral immune activation and CNS injury in IAE.

Excitotoxicity: the central execution phase from systemic inflammation to glutamate dysregulation

Another key terminal mechanism underlying the lung-brain axis is excitotoxicity. In IAE, particularly in AESD, accumulating evidence implicates disruption of the glutamate-glutamine cycle [149–151]. AESD typically presents with biphasic seizures and delayed diffusion restriction in the subcortical white matter, often described as a “bright tree appearance” [152]. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) studies in AESD have shown elevated glutamate/glutamine complex signals in the acute stage and dynamic disruption of the glutamate–glutamine cycle, supporting excitotoxic neuronal injury in vivo [149, 153].

Mechanistically, early seizures may induce excessive presynaptic glutamate release. Under physiological conditions, astrocytes clear extracellular glutamate through excitatory amino acid (AA) transporters and convert glutamate to glutamine via glutamine synthetase, thereby maintaining glutamate homeostasis. Experimental studies have shown that inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α can inhibit astrocytic glutamate uptake [154]. Meanwhile, studies of HIV-associated neurological injury have further demonstrated that TNF-α-mediated loss of astroglial glutamate transport can drive excitotoxic neuronal injury [155]. When extracellular glutamate exceeds the buffering capacity of astrocytes, astrocytic swelling and cytotoxic edema occur, contributing to restricted diffusion. Excess extracellular glutamate further activates N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, causing Ca2⁺ overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and postsynaptic neuronal injury [149, 156]. Although direct evidence specifically linking influenza infection to astrocytic glutamate transporter dysfunction remains limited, the MRS findings in AESD, together with established mechanisms of inflammation-induced astrocyte dysfunction and NMDA-mediated excitotoxic neuronal injury, support excitotoxicity as a relevant terminal pathway in IAE.

Glutamate excitotoxicity may also contribute to other IAE-related syndromes, although the strength of evidence differs among subtypes. In ANE, current evidence suggests that excitotoxic injury is more likely a secondary consequence of cytokine storm, BBB disruption, cerebral edema, and metabolic dysfunction than a primary pathogenic driver [157, 158]. In MERS, transient diffusion restriction in the splenium of the corpus callosum is generally attributed to cytotoxic or intramyelinic edema. Emerging evidence indicates that inflammatory cytokines may impair astrocytic glutamate uptake, resulting in extracellular glutamate accumulation and subsequent neuronal stress, thereby contributing to the development of reversible splenial lesions [81, 159]. Thus, excitotoxicity appears to be most strongly established in AESD but may represent a shared downstream pathway linking astrocyte dysfunction, edema, and neuronal injury across multiple IAE phenotypes.

Metabolic dysfunction

Influenza virus can remodel host metabolic networks to support viral propagation, and disruption of mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation has emerged as an important metabolic feature of influenza-associated encephalopathy [160]. Rather than being viewed solely as either a primary pathogenic driver or a downstream consequence, mitochondrial dysfunction should be considered a bidirectional pathogenic amplifier in IAE. On the one hand, pre-existing or genetically determined defects in mitochondrial β-oxidation may increase host susceptibility to severe neurological injury. Clinical observations further reveal that fatal or severe IAE cases frequently exhibit impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation, reflected by an increased serum acylcarnitine ratio [(C16:0 + C18:1)/C2], and thermolabile variants of carnitine palmitoyltransferase II (CPT II) have been identified as genetic risk factors for IAE [161, 162]. Consistently, mice with impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation develop viral invasion of brain capillaries and progressive cerebral edema following intranasal infection with non-neurotropic H3N2 influenza virus [163].

On the other hand, mitochondrial dysfunction can also arise secondarily from systemic inflammation during IAE. Cytokine storm, particularly through TNF-related pathways, can induce mitochondrial respiratory failure and promote apoptosis of neurons and glial cells. Elevated serum cytochrome c levels in patients with IAE further indicate the involvement of systemic inflammation–induced mitochondrial stress in multi-organ injury [164]. As mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is essential for neuronal ATP production, impaired mitochondrial metabolism may lead to lactate accumulation, energy depletion, neuronal instability, and ultimately severe neurological dysfunction [165]. Therefore, metabolic dysfunction in IAE is best interpreted as both a predisposing vulnerability and a downstream consequence of inflammatory injury, forming a feed-forward loop in which mitochondrial impairment, inflammation, and energy failure mutually reinforce disease progression. This mechanism may be particularly relevant in children and individuals with underlying metabolic susceptibility.

Critical factors influencing the pathogenesis of IAE

The pathogenesis of IAE is influenced by three major components: viral, host, and environmental factors. Viral determinants such as the cleavage mechanisms of HA cleavage sites and mutations in viral structural components influence neuroinvasive capacity and tissue tropism. Host-related factors, including age, immune status, medical history, dominant or recessive genetic mutations, and the expression of brain-specific proteins, significantly influence the development of IAE. Environmental factors that coexist with both the virus and the host can substantially affect disease onset, progression, and prognosis. External stimuli are transmitted to the CNS after being received by the human body, eliciting various responses and thereby constituting a critical element influencing CNS susceptibility to influenza virus infection.

Virus-related factors

Influenza virus attachment to host cells is primarily mediated by SA (Fig. 6A), yet its distribution in the brain remains unclear. Notably, infections have been observed in cells lacking SA receptors, and SA utilization depends not only on α−2,3 and α−2,6- linked SA. For instance, α−2,8-linked SA [166] and even SA-independent mechanisms have been reported [167]. The cleavage and mutation mechanisms of the HA site

HA, a homotrimeric class I fusion protein located on the influenza viral envelope, mediates receptor binding and membrane fusion and is a key determinant of host range, tissue tropism, and pathogenicity. HA is synthesized as an inactive precursor, HA0, which must be cleaved by host proteases into HA1 and HA2 to render virions infectious [168] (Fig. 6B). Therefore, the structure of the HA cleavage site directly influences viral activation, extrapulmonary dissemination, and potential CNS involvement. The cleavage site is located within a surface loop of the stem domain, with variations in its AA sequences.

Most human seasonal influenza viruses contain a monobasic HA cleavage site that restricts viral replication primarily to the respiratory tract. In contrast, HPAIVs, particularly H5 and H7 subtypes, often acquire a multibasic cleavage site (MBCS), enabling HA activation by broadly distributed furin-like proteases and thereby promoting systemic dissemination, including potential involvement of extrapulmonary organs such as the CNS [169]. This feature is clinically relevant because human H5N1 infections with neurological manifestations have been reported, and H5N1 viral RNA or infectious virus has occasionally been detected in CSF or brain tissue, supporting the possibility of direct CNS involvement in severe cases [170].Experimental studies further support the role of HA cleavage-site architecture in neurovirulence. Removal of the MBCS significantly reduces H5N1 replication efficiency in CNS cells, though replication is not entirely abolished [171].

The insertion of four amino acids (KRTA) at the cleavage site enhances extrapulmonary dissemination and virulence in HPAIV. The H7N9 GZ8H002 strain, which already contains the KRTA insertion, also carries K526R and M535L mutations. K526R is associated with increased viral replication, whereas M535L may enhance polymerase activity. Together, these mutations enhanced viral replication and proliferation, thereby further strengthening its neurotropicism [40].

Similarly, single AA substitutions at the HA cleavage site can alter cleavage efficiency, replication, pathogenicity, and neurovirulence. For example, introduction of a serine (S) residue at position P6 of the HA cleavage site altered the phenotype of the SY08 H5N1 strain, a non-pathogenic virus that is normally restricted to the respiratory tract in mice. Following the G325S mutation, the virus became detectable in the spleen and brain, leading to a significantly increased pathogenicity and neurotoxicity [172]. Likewise, substitution of tyrosine at position P2 of the HA cleavage site enhances cleavage by the serine protease plasmin, thereby increasing neurovirulence of the A/WSN/33 virus in mice [173]. In addition to the cleavage site, mutations at specific AA residues within HA influence viral adaptation, host range, and tissue tropism. The K222E mutation in H5N1 HA reduces binding affinity for α2,3-linked SA, resulting in restricted viral spread to the lungs and decreased brain infectivity in mice [174]. Overall, alterations in the HA cleavage site are closely associated with viral neurotropism and potential CNS involvement. However, most mechanistic evidence originates from animal studies, and the specific contribution of HA mutations to human neurological disease remains to be established through further clinical and virological investigations.

Other mutations in the influenza virus structure

The IAV genome contains eight single-stranded RNA segments, with the eighth segment encoding the multifunctional non-structural protein 1 (NS1), which regulates viral replication and antagonizes host antiviral responses. Mutations or deletions in NS1 are associated with increased IAV virulence [175, 176]. NS1-truncated viruses (NS80 and NS80ad) derived from A/WSN/33 (WSN) reduce the expression of neurofunction-related proteins (IGFBP-2, CHI3L1, MPO, LDL, etc.) in the brains of infected mice, potentially attenuating influenza-associated neurological damage [177]. NS1 binds to the host cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor 30 kDa (CPSF30) to suppress the expression of antiviral factors. The A/HK/156/1997 (H5N1) strain lacks the consensus phenylalanine (F) and methionine (M) residues at positions 103 and 106 of NS1, weakening NS1-CPSF30 interaction. Restoration of these residues enhances viral virulence and promotes severe brain infection and inflammation, as demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo models [178].

Another virulence factor, polymerase basic protein 1-frame 2 (PB1-F2), a 75–90 AA accessory protein, induces host immune and epithelial cell death while modulating inflammatory responses [179, 180]. However, systematic investigations of its neurological functions remain scarce. Direct injection of recombinant PB1-F2 into the mouse hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) impairs cell proliferation and oligodendrocyte development, disrupts myelination, suppresses the expression of key synaptic proteins (BDNF, PSD95, and CaMKII), and compromises synaptic plasticity [181]. These findings provide valuable insights for identifying novel therapeutic targets for IAE. (Fig. 6C). Overall, NS1 and PB1-F2 may contribute to the neuroinvasive and neuropathogenic potential of IAV. Elucidating the mechanisms by which these viral factors promote CNS injury may improve risk assessment and facilitate the identification of biomarkers and therapeutic targets for IAE.

Host-related factors

Whether influenza virus infection triggers IAE depends on factors such as age, ethnicity, sex, comorbidities, and family genetic history, with varying probabilities of disease onset. Epidemiological studies investigating these factors remain insufficient, and further investigation into their common pathogenic mechanisms may inform prevention and treatment strategies.

Epidemiological risk factors and genetic susceptibility

Host-related factors contributing to IAE can be broadly divided into epidemiological risk factors and genetic susceptibility mechanisms. Epidemiologically, IAE predominantly affects pediatric populations, likely due to their immature brain and immune systems. From December 2023 to January 2024, seven children in Tuscany, Italy, were diagnosed with influenza A (H1N1pdm09) complicated by encephalopathy [27]. Between 2013 and 2015, 54 cases of IAE were identified at two tertiary children's hospitals in Australia, accounting for 7.6% of influenza-related hospitalizations [182]. Data collected between 2015 and 2020 from 49 U.S. children's hospitals showed that among 29,676 children hospitalized with influenza, 2,246 experienced neurologic complications, with increased risk observed in males, children of Asian ethnicity compared with non-Hispanic white children, and those with pre-existing neurological conditions [183]. These findings suggest that pediatric age and underlying neurological disease are important clinical risk factors, although demographic associations should be interpreted cautiously because they may be influenced by healthcare access, surveillance patterns, and population structure.

In addition to epidemiological factors, inherited susceptibility may contribute to severe IAE, particularly ANE. ANE has been linked to autosomal dominant missense mutations in the Ran-binding protein 2 (RANBP2) gene, with mutation carriers exhibiting increased susceptibility [83]. A case of recurrent ANE following IAV infection has been reported in a genetically predisposed family carrying a RANBP2 mutation [184]. Ribonuclease inhibitor 1 (RNH1), a cellular protein containing leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domains, may influence immune regulation when its functional structure is impaired. Whole-exome sequencing identified biallelic RNH1 variants in eight previously healthy children who developed acute encephalopathy during sudden viral infections (influenza or rhinovirus) or febrile illnesses, resulting in deleterious effects on RNH1 protein function and expanding the known genetic spectrum of ANE [185]. Additionally, variants in the carnitine palmitoyltransferase II (CPT II) complex, specifically [1055 T > G/F352C] and [1102G > A/V368I], are associated with autosomal recessive inheritance and exhibit a thermolabile phenotype and mitochondrial energy instability during hyperthermia, potentially representing the predominant genetic basis of Japanese IAE [186] (Fig. 7A). Expression of host brain tissue-related proteins

Influenza neurotoxicity depends not only on viral mutations but also on host proteins in brain tissues. Fragile X mental retardation protein (FMRP), which is abundantly expressed in the brain, promotes neuronal replication of neurotropic AIV such as H9N2, H5N1, and H10N7 by binding viral NP to support RNP assembly and nucleocytoplasmic transport, thereby leading to extensive viral replication within the CNS [55]. PGRMC1, a membrane-bound protein that is also highly expressed in brain tissue, exhibits neurotrophic properties and participates in various cellular regulatory processes. Following H5N6 infection, transcriptomic analysis revealed significant downregulation of PGRMC1 in mouse brain tissue. Functional experiments further demonstrated that PGRMC1 restricts H5N6 replication by enhancing RIG-I–mediated IFN-β signaling, whereas PGRMC1 knockdown attenuates this antiviral response and facilitates viral replication in CNS-derived cells [42] (Fig. 7B).

Furthermore, TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP‑43), a ubiquitously expressed RNA-binding protein, interacts with multiple influenza viral mRNAs (NP, PB1, PB2, PA, HA, etc.) to support their splicing, stabilization, and translation [187]. It also plays a critical role in brain ECs by maintaining BBB integrity [188]. While TDP-43 enhances viral invasion, it simultaneously supports BBB stability. This apparent contradiction raises the hypothesis that TDP-43 may be recruited by influenza viruses, potentially compromising its native functions in ECs and consequently weakening BBB function. This hypothesis warrants further investigation.

Surrounding environmental factors

Environmental enrichment (EE) may represent a potential modifier of neuroinflammatory responses following influenza infection, although its direct relevance to human IAE remains unproven. In a mouse model of intranasal A/PR/8/34 (H1N1) infection, EE housing attenuated hippocampal neuroinflammation and improved cognitive performance compared with the standard environment (SE) [189]. Similar protective associations have also been reported in experimental neurotropic viral encephalitis, in which EE was associated with reduced CNS viral invasion or enhanced viral clearance [190]. Therefore, these findings suggest that host environmental and behavioral factors may influence neuroimmune responses during viral infection and warrant further investigation in IAE.

Recent advances in diagnosis and therapeutic strategies

Diagnostic consensus

Although no universally accepted diagnostic criteria for IAE currently exist, diagnosis is generally based on laboratory-confirmed influenza infection in conjunction with acute neurological dysfunction and the exclusion of alternative etiologies. Among these, ANE represents the most severe neurological complication of IAE. Recently, a consensus case definition for ANE has been proposed, characterized by acute neurological deterioration accompanied by neuroimaging findings of bilateral symmetrical thalamic lesions, with possible involvement of the brainstem, cerebellum, and cerebral white matter [192].

Several laboratory parameters have been explored as prognostic markers and predictive model variables rather than definitive diagnostic markers. In children with influenza-associated ANE, elevated serum procalcitonin and increased CSF protein levels have been reported as early indicators associated with disease severity [15]. However, these biomarkers should be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with clinical presentation, virological confirmation, and neuroimaging findings, as they are not specific diagnostic criteria for IAE.

Therapeutic strategies

Currently, no specific therapy is available for IAE, and clinical management primarily relies on symptomatic and supportive treatment, including antiviral therapy, immunomodulation, antiepileptic therapy, and neuroprotective interventions. Because influenza infection can rapidly progress to severe neurological impairment or even death, early recognition and timely intervention are critical. Intensive neurological monitoring and multi-organ supportive care are essential for patients with severe disease. Although no standardized evidence-based guidelines have been established, high-dose corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), plasma exchange, and targeted immunotherapies have been widely employed. However, the optimal therapeutic strategy for IAE remains to be determined.

Vaccination

Influenza vaccination represents a key public health strategy for preventing IAE. However, evidence specifically linking vaccination to a reduced risk of IAE remains limited and is derived mainly from observational reports, surveillance data, and retrospective analyses rather than randomized controlled trials. Case reports and national surveillance data indicate that most pediatric IAE cases occur in unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated children. Only 16% of vaccine-eligible patients with IAE reported to the CDC in the U.S. had received the influenza vaccine [193]. A study by Yang et al. [13], together with warnings issued by an Italian research group regarding pediatric influenza encephalitis [27], demonstrated a strong association between vaccination and a reduced incidence of neurological complications. These findings suggest an association between under-vaccination and severe neurological complications but cannot establish direct causality.

Stronger evidence is available for the protective effect of influenza vaccination against severe influenza outcomes in children. In a case–control study of U.S. children admitted to pediatric intensive care units(PICU), Ferdinands et al. reported that influenza vaccination was associated with a 74% reduction in the risk of PICU admission due to severe influenza illness [13], with similar findings reported by Frankl et al. [194]. Likewise, a multicenter test-negative study by Olson et al. [195] found that vaccination reduced critical and life-threatening influenza illness in children, although the magnitude of protection varied according to viral strain and antigenic match. Therefore, vaccination should be regarded as a well-supported strategy for reducing severe influenza disease, whereas its protective effect against IAE specifically remains supported mainly by indirect and observational evidence.

Since 2010, the CDC and the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices have recommended annual influenza vaccination for all individuals aged ≥ 6 months to prevent influenza and its complications [196]. However, vaccination coverage among children remains suboptimal in many countries and has declined in recent years [13, 197]. In Italy, influenza vaccination is provided free of charge to individuals aged ≥ 60 years and other high-risk populations. Since 2020, vaccination has also been recommended for children aged 6 months to 6 years. However, coverage remains below the European Union target of 75%. Vaccination coverage in the general population decreased to 18.9% during the 2023/2024 season and increased only slightly to 19.6% in 2024/2025, compared with 20.2% in 2022/2023 [198]. These findings support continued public health efforts to improve vaccine uptake, particularly among children, while also highlighting the need for prospective studies to determine whether influenza vaccination directly reduces the incidence or severity of IAE.

Antiviral therapy

The Protocol for diagnosis and treatment of influenza (2025 version) [199] recommends that critically ill patients, including those with IAE, should receive prompt antiviral therapy, because early intervention can significantly improve clinical outcomes. Oseltamivir is the most commonly recommended antiviral agent. It inhibits neuraminidase activity, thereby preventing the release and spread of newly formed virions. This recommendation is based mainly on evidence from randomized controlled trials in uncomplicated influenza, observational studies in hospitalized or critically ill patients, and expert consensus rather than controlled trials specifically targeting IAE. For hospitalized patients, the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines recommend oseltamivir as the preferred antiviral agent, although they acknowledge that direct evidence in severe disease is limited [200]. Observational cohort data suggest that early oseltamivir treatment may reduce mortality or shorten hospitalization in critically ill patients with influenza, but these findings remain susceptible to confounding [201]. In contrast, meta-analyses of randomized trials in outpatients have not consistently demonstrated a reduction in hospitalization [202], indicating that the clinical benefit may depend on disease severity and timing of treatment.

Evidence supporting oseltamivir for IAE is considerably weaker. Most available data are derived from case reports, case series, or retrospective analyses. The IDSA guidelines for the management of encephalitis recommend considering oseltamivir in cases of influenza-associated encephalitis [187]. In patients with ANE, standard-dose oseltamivir appears to have limited efficacy, whereas higher-dose regimens may provide potential benefits [13, 203]. A population-based retrospective cohort study involving children aged 5–17 years found that oseltamivir treatment during influenza episodes was associated with a lower risk of serious neuropsychiatric events requiring hospitalization, including seizures, altered mental status, and encephalitis [204].It remains unclear whether antiviral treatment for influenza affects the development or progression of IAE, but empirical antiviral therapy should be initiated early during the influenza season.

Immunomodulatory regimens

High-dose glucocorticoid pulse therapy is widely used in IAE. However, its clinical benefit remains uncertain [203, 205, 206]. Corticosteroid therapy may also lead to severe adverse effects, including hypercoagulability and serious opportunistic infections [197, 207], and the survival benefit observed in some studies has not reached statistical significance. Several studies have reported that methylprednisolone administration within 48 h after the onset of neurological symptoms does not improve long-term outcomes [208]. Conversely, other studies have suggested that immunomodulatory therapies may provide clinical benefits and improve prognosis in selected patients. The apparent discrepancy among studies may be partly explained by differences in treatment timing, disease subtype, and baseline severity. Retrospective studies suggest that corticosteroids administered within 24 h after neurological symptom onset may be associated with better outcomes in selected patients with ANE without brainstem involvement, whereas this association is less evident in patients with brainstem lesions or more fulminant disease [209]. Thus, further studies are warranted to evaluate the effectiveness of early pulse corticosteroid therapy in specific clinical subtypes.

IVIG is the most commonly used immunotherapeutic approach after corticosteroid therapy for inflammation-related acute encephalopathy. It exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting immune cell activation and cytokine production, but evidence regarding its impact on improving prognosis remains inconsistent [210]. Taken together, current evidence does not support the routine use of corticosteroids or IVIG in all patients with IAE. Future prospective studies are needed to identify the patient populations most likely to benefit from different immunotherapeutic approaches and to determine the optimal timing and indications for these interventions.

Anti-cytokine therapy

Anti-cytokine therapy is considered a rational treatment for IAE. Tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor antagonist, significantly reduces inflammatory responses. Some studies suggest that its early combination with corticosteroids may improve outcomes in severe IAE [211], but its effects on mortality and long-term neurological prognosis require further verification.

Plasma exchange

Plasma exchange can rapidly remove circulating inflammatory mediators, thereby counteracting cytokine storm, and is considered a therapeutic option for critically ill patients with IAE. Case reports have demonstrated that plasma exchange can lead to rapid clinical improvement [212]. A retrospective cohort study further indicated that in-hospital mortality was significantly lower in the plasma exchange group than in the non–plasma exchange group, although no significant difference in long-term mortality was observed between the two groups [213].

Hypothermia therapy

Hypothermia therapy exerts neuroprotective effects by suppressing cerebral edema and inflammatory responses and has shown potential benefits in acute encephalopathies such as ANE [214]. Evidence from case reports and retrospective studies suggests that targeted temperature management can significantly improve clinical outcomes and reduce the incidence of seizures in patients with acute encephalopathy. However, adverse effects associated with hypothermia, including bradycardia, hypotension, and coagulation abnormalities, require further optimization [215, 216].

Other therapies

For refractory status epilepticus, sedative agents such as midazolam, propofol, or barbiturates may be administered for seizure control [217]. The ketogenic diet may also serve as an adjunctive treatment for seizure control [210]. In addition, animal studies have shown that anti-elastase agents and histone-modifying enzyme inhibitors can alleviate cerebral edema and inflammatory responses in mouse models of IAE, suggesting their potential as therapeutic strategies for IAE [218].

Prognostic indicators

Elevated D-dimer levels, sex, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, interleukin-6 (IL-6), the proportion of CD4+ T cells, the proportion of NK cells, the CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio, and brainstem involvement have all been identified as predictors of poor prognosis in IAE [219–221]. Among these, IL-6 concentration, the percentage of CD4+ T cells, the percentage of NK cells, and the CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio have predictive value for mortality in children with IAE, and the combined use of these four indicators provides better predictive performance than any single marker alone. Elevated serum aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and ferritin levels are also prognostic indicators of severe influenza [222, 223]. A study by Shafran et al. showed that elevated liver enzymes were associated with disease severity and mortality risk in patients with influenza (OR 4.344; 95% CI, 2.218–8.508) [223]. Mean serum ferritin levels were significantly increased in patients with severe disease, and multivariate analysis indicated that elevated ferritin increased the risk of severe outcomes in patients with influenza by fivefold [222]. In addition, certain neuroradiological features, particularly extensive MRI abnormalities, as well as changes in procalcitonin and albumin levels, have been associated with disease severity [224, 225]. Independent risk factors for progression of IAE include seizure frequency, the timing of the first seizure during the febrile period, tachypnea, elevated procalcitonin levels, decreased albumin levels, and a reduced CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio [15]. Conclusion and future directions

Influenza viruses can induce neurological symptoms and may invade the CNS, causing CNS lesions. Despite rapid advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies, the causative agent is identified in fewer than half of clinical cases. From a public health perspective, influenza is generally characterized by mild respiratory manifestations, primarily involving the throat and lungs, and its potential to affect the CNS is often underestimated. Currently, no targeted vaccines or specific therapeutic interventions are available for IAE. The mechanisms by which these respiratory pathogens invade the CNS remain incompletely understood. Various mechanisms that may facilitate influenza virus invasion of the CNS have been identified, including the release of numerous inflammatory mediators and immune dysregulation that disrupt BMEC and TJs, receptor-mediated transcytosis, and the potential to cross the BBB and BCSFB through a "Trojan horse" mechanism. The olfactory, trigeminal, sympathetic, and vagus nerves represent additional potential routes that bypass these barriers. Evidence from both postmortem human studies and animal models supports these neuroinvasive pathways. This review explores both common and less frequently reported routes of CNS invasion and presents current mechanistic hypotheses.

Several critical questions remain unresolved, including how to determine the dominant pathway when multiple routes are involved during infection with the same viral strain, whether host-specific tropism exists among different species, and whether universal neuroinvasive mechanisms can be identified across influenza virus variants. In the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, increased research attention has been directed toward influenza viruses as another clinically important respiratory pathogen. Given their continuous evolutionary capacity and the potential for CNS complications that may lead to poor prognosis or fatal outcomes, elucidating the mechanisms of neuroinvasion and identifying susceptible populations are essential for developing effective preventive measures and therapeutic strategies.

Future studies should move beyond descriptive observations and address several specific priorities. First, currently available animal models vary considerably in viral strains, inoculation doses, infection routes, and host backgrounds, making it difficult to directly compare neuropathological findings across studies. Establishing standardized experimental models would facilitate mechanistic investigations and therapeutic evaluation. Second, clinically relevant biomarkers should be validated in well-characterized patient cohorts, including inflammatory cytokines, endothelial injury markers, BBB integrity markers, and CSF or serum viral signatures. Third, treatment remains largely supportive. Although neuraminidase inhibitors reduce viral replication, convincing evidence for CNS-targeted therapies that preserve BBB integrity or attenuate neuroinflammation remains lacking. Finally, integrating virology, neuroimmunology, neuroimaging, and multi-omics approaches may facilitate the identification of susceptible populations and help determine whether common mechanisms of neuroinvasion exist across different influenza virus variants. Such efforts will be essential for improving the early diagnosis, risk stratification, and targeted intervention of IAE.
