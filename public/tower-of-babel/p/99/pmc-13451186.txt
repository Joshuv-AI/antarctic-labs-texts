Mechanical and durability investigation of concrete made with natural, recycled, and secondary recycled coarse aggregate
Authors: Mingming Zhang, Xiaojia Ji, Weifeng Zhu, Tingting Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe application of recycled concrete (RC) in the construction industry has yielded significant economic and social benefits in recent years. However, as RC structures reach the end of their service life, they themselves become a new source of demolition waste. To investigate the recyclability of secondary recycled aggregate (RRA) and to compare the performance of secondary recycled concrete (RRC) with that of RC, this study systematically examines the content influence of secondary recycled coarse aggregate (RRCA) on the overall performance of concrete. The analysis focuses on crack resistance, mechanical properties, and durability, with varying replacement ratios of recycled coarse aggregate (RCA) and RRCA as the main variables. Furthermore, the mechanisms underlying performance changes are explored in relation to microstructural characteristics. The results indicate that increasing the replacement ratio of both RCA and RRCA leads to a reduction in concrete strength. However, at equivalent replacement levels, the strength of RRC is comparable to that of RC. At a 100% replacement ratio, the 28-day splitting tensile strength and compressive strength of RRC are only 3.3% and 3.1% lower, respectively, than those of RC. Nevertheless, due to the higher water absorption and porosity of RRCA compared to RCA, concrete incorporating RRCA exhibits a higher cracking tendency and inferior crack resistance. In addition, its permeability resistance and frost resistance are significantly lower than those of RC. Overall, the crack resistance, impermeability, and frost resistance of RRC with a 50% RRCA replacement ratio (RRC50) are comparable to those of RC with 100% RCA replacement (RC100). Microstructural analysis reveals that, at the same recycled aggregate content, the number of transition pores in RRC is significantly greater than in RC, while the total area of gel pores and macro pores shows little difference. This suggests that transition pores have a limited effect on strength but play a critical role in reducing impermeability and frost resistance. Moreover, a greater number of micro cracks and loosely distributed hydration products are observed in RRC specimens, which further explains their inferior crack resistance, impermeability, and frost resistance compared to RC. In summary, although the application of RRC is feasible to a certain extent, it is recommended that the replacement ratio of RRCA should not exceed 50%.Keywords: Recycled concrete, Secondary recycled concrete, Recycled coarse aggregate, Crack resistance, Durability, MicrostructureSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The rapid pace of urbanization and infrastructure development has led to the demolition of a large number of obsolete buildings and transportation systems, generating massive quantities of construction and demolition waste1. The inadequate management and indiscriminate landfilling of such waste not only consume valuable land resources but also pose serious environmental risks. Consequently, improving the recycling efficiency and reuse of construction waste has become a critical research priority in contemporary civil engineering2. Against this backdrop, recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) has attracted increasing attention as a green, low-carbon, and sustainable construction material. The use of RAC reduces dependence on natural aggregates, lowers material costs, and facilitates the recycling of waste concrete3,4. In addition, it contributes to mitigating environmental pollution, including soil and water contamination5. Accordingly, the utilization of RCA6–9, recycled fine aggregate (RFA)10–12, and recycled powder (RP)13–15 in concrete production has become a major research focus in recent decades. Numerous studies have investigated methods to enhance the performance of recycled materials. For instance, Chen et al.6,8, Zhang et al.7, and Poon et al.9 conducted comprehensive studies on the modification and performance improvement of recycled coarse aggregates. Similarly, Feng et al.11 and Rifa et al.12 experimentally evaluated the performance enhancement of recycled sand concrete. Investigations into recycled powder activation were carried out by Guan et al.14 and Kaptan et al.15. Furthermore, Li et al.16, Silva et al.17, and Branston et al.18 explored interfacial transition zones and shrinkage behavior in recycled aggregate systems. Overall, substantial progress has been made in improving the performance and expanding the application of RAC, leading to notable advancements in its practical use19,20. These studies have significantly promoted the safe and efficient utilization of RAC in modern construction21.

The application of recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) has become increasingly mature and widespread, delivering considerable economic and social benefits22,23. However, these successfully implemented RAC structures inevitably face a challenge similar to that of conventional concrete structures at the end of their service life. Once decommissioned, RAC structures generate a new stream of construction and demolition waste, thereby reintroducing the same disposal and environmental concerns associated with earlier generations of concrete waste. This raises a critical question regarding the management of end-of-life RAC: whether such materials should be discarded or further recycled. Addressing this issue has become an urgent research priority, particularly in the context of advancing circular economy principles within the construction industry. From a resource efficiency perspective, we have to admit that the best way to reuse these waste recycled concrete is to prepare RRA (which refers to crushing recycled concrete and preparing it into recycled aggregate again24 and RRC, as shown in Fig. 1. However, it is more noteworthy that compared with recycled aggregate, RRA has more old slurry layers attached to the surface, more developed micro cracks, lower density, and its physical properties are further deteriorated compared to RRA. These characteristics mean that concrete made with RRCA may be significantly affected in terms of workability, strength, and durability compared to RAC. Whether these RRC with inferior performance can be widely accepted and applied like RAC is a puzzling question. This requires not only attention to the treatment and optimal utilization of these waste recycled concretes but also the safety and durability of RRC after its application in actual projects. Consequently, a comprehensive investigation into the impact of the RRCA replacement ratio on concrete performance, together with the disparities in mechanical behavior and durability between RRAC and RAC, is of substantial theoretical and practical significance for realizing multi-cycle recycling of buildings waste and advancing the sustainable development of RAC.

Source and treatment of secondary recycled aggregates.

To address the aforementioned issues, this study aims to investigate the effects of RRCA replacement ratios on the performance of concrete and to elucidate the underlying microstructural mechanisms responsible for the differences between RAC and RRC. In this work, varying replacement ratios of RCA and RRCA were employed as the primary experimental variables. The influence of RRCA on crack resistance, mechanical properties, and durability of concrete was systematically evaluated. In addition, the mechanisms governing performance evolution were analyzed in conjunction with nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) techniques. The findings of this study are expected to provide a scientific basis for material design and engineering applications of RAC and RRC, as well as to support the development of relevant technical specifications. Furthermore, this work contributes to promoting the efficient and multi-stage recycling of construction and demolition waste.

Method and mix

A total of nine concrete mixtures were designed with varying replacement ratios of RCA and RRCA. Based on previous studies4,25,26, the dosage of the superplasticizer was adjusted to achieve a target slump in the range of 160–200 mm for all mixtures. The RCA used in this study exhibited relatively low 24 h water absorption; therefore, its water uptake during mixing was assumed to be negligible, and no additional water was introduced for RCA containing mixtures. In contrast, RRCA showed significantly higher 24 h water absorption and moisture content. Accordingly, an additional water content was considered to compensate for water absorption by RRCA. This additional water was calculated as 90% of the water absorption capacity of RRCA under saturated surface-dry (SSD) conditions4,27. It should be noted that this absorbed water was not included in the effective mixing water. The detailed mix proportions for all concrete mixtures are presented in Table 2, where the dosage of the water-reducing agent is expressed as a percentage of the total cementitious material.

Strength test

The compressive strength and splitting tensile strength of 100 mm concrete cube specimens were tested at 7 and 28 days of curing in accordance with GB/T 50081–201929. For each mixture, three parallel specimens were tested, and the average value, reported to an accuracy of 0.1 MPa, was taken as the representative strength. If the deviation of either the minimum or maximum value from the median exceeded 15% of the median value, the outlier was excluded and the median value was adopted. When both extreme values deviated by more than 15% from the median, the test results for that batch were considered invalid and discarded.

Electric flux value and frost resistance

The frost and chloride ion permeability resistance of concrete were evaluated according to the rapid freeze–thaw method and electric flux method specified in GB/T 50082–200928. For the frost resistance test, prismatic specimens (100 × 100 × 400 mm) were subjected to cyclic freezing and thawing using an SF-300 concrete rapid freeze–thaw tester. For the chloride ion permeability test, cylindrical specimens (50 mm × 100 mm) were tested. Prior to testing, all specimens were cured under 20 ± 2 °C and 95 ± 5% RH for 28 days. The frost resistance of specimens was assessed by measuring the mass and dynamic elastic modulus loss (or relative dynamic elastic modulus (RDME)). Prior to freeze–thaw cycling, the initial mass and dynamic elastic modulus of each specimen was recorded. Which needs to be re-measured after each 25 freeze–thaw cycle, surface residues were removed, surface moisture was wiped off, and visible damage was inspected. All inspections and measurements were performed promptly to minimize moisture loss. Specimens from the same batch were kept covered with a damp cloth during testing to maintain consistent moisture conditions.

NMR and SEM tests

The NMR analysis was performed using a MacroMR12-150 H-I nuclear magnetic resonance microstructure analyzer, and the cylindrical specimens of electric flux test were used. Following curing, the cylindrical specimens were subjected to water saturation and subsequently sealed in plastic film to prevent moisture loss prior to testing. The detailed testing procedures followed the methodology described in Refs4,25., as shown in Fig. 4.

Test equipment and step of NMR and SEM.

The microstructural features of RC and RRC were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). After the compressive strength tests, fragments approximately 0.5 × 0.5 cm were extracted from the specimens and immersed in anhydrous ethanol for 7 days to terminate cement hydration reaction. The samples were subsequently vacuum-dried at 60 ± 5 °C to constant weight, and examined under SEM at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. The test apparatus is shown in Fig. 4.

Figure 6 shows that all concrete specimens exhibit rapid cracking development and growth between 2 and 14 h. Between 14 and 24 h, the cracking area begins to grow slowly, and then gradually stabilizes. The first stage primarily encompasses the phase of concrete initial to final setting, during which most cement hydration completes. During this phase, a significant amount of water is consumed due to the rapid cement hydration, resulting in the most significant increase in concrete shrinkage during this phase. This significant shrinkage is a primary factor in the pronounced cracking of concrete during this phase. Moreover, the ongoing hydration of cementitious components releases considerable heat. Owing to the inherently low thermal conductivity of concrete25,26, the internally generated heat tends to accumulate and is not easily dissipated to the exterior surface. This results in a large temperature difference at a given section, causing the concrete to expand internally due to heat, generating compressive stress in the concrete internal, while generating tensile stress in the concrete contracts externally. This temperature stress is another major cause of concrete cracking during this stage. During Stage II, the progression of autogenous shrinkage in the concrete gradually decelerates as the rate of cement hydration diminishes. Simultaneously, its ability to resist deformation increases with the concrete hardens, gradually increasing its strength and crack resistance during Stage II and slowing its cracking.

Figure 5 shows that t with different coarse aggregate (CA, including RCA and RRCA) replacement ratios follows the following order: C0 > RC50> RC100 = RRC50=RRC100. Compared to specimen C0, the t of RC50, RC100, RRC50, and RRC100 specimens cracking accelerated by 42.9%, 57.1%, 57.1%, and 57.1%, respectively. With increasing CA replacement ratio, the t of concrete significantly accelerates, and at the same replacement ratio, the t of RRC is shorter. With increasing CA replacement ratio, the number and maximum length of crack in RC and RRC are similar, but the maximum crack length, the a and b all show an increasing trend. When the CA replacement rate is the same, the cracking effect of RRC is more significant than that of RC. When the CA is 50% and 100%, the area of RC specimen cracks increases by 4.2 and 5.1 times respectively compared with C0, and the area of the RRC specimen cracks increases by 5.4 and 7.8 times respectively. As the CA content increases, the crack width, crack length, and crack area in the recycled concrete will become larger. This can be attributed to the presence of a residual mortar layer on the RCA and RRCA, which possesses high water absorption and abundant micropores and microcracks. These microstructural defects act as pathways for moisture transport4, increasing both capillary and gel porosities within the concrete matrix. As a result, the evaporation of free and capillary water is facilitated, leading to a higher likelihood of crack initiation and propagation4.

More importantly, the cracked area of RRC50 was 22.6% higher than that of RC50, whereas RRC100 exhibited a 44.1% increase compared with RC100. In addition, the maximum length and cracked area of RRC specimens were markedly higher than those of RC specimens. These findings reveal that RRC possesses weaker crack resistance at equivalent replacement levels. This deterioration can be primarily attributed to the higher water absorption (see “Materials” section) and the increased presence of micro defects-namely, cracks and micro pores in RRCA relative to RCA, which collectively promote crack development in the concrete at early age. These research findings are highly consistent with literature4,25,26.

Strength

The splitting tensile strength (fst) and compressive strength (fc) test values of concrete with different recycled coarse aggregate content are shown in Figs. 7 and 8. The fc and fst of RC and RRC generally show a downward trend with increasing CA replacement content. When the CA reaches 100%, the strengths of both RC and RRC are lowest. Compared with natural aggregate concrete, the 7 d and 28 d splitting tensile and fc of RC decrease by 21.4%, 16.7% and 12.4%, 7.9%, the 7 d and 28 d was splitting tensile and fc of RRC decrease by 25%, 19.4% and 17.8%, 12.5%, respectively. The test data show that the fc and fst of RC and RRC concrete decrease with increasing CA replacement content. This is because, RC and RRC have higher water absorption and crushing properties, more interfacial areas, micropores, and microcracks compared to natural aggregates, resulting in lower concrete strength than natural aggregate concrete. Increasing the CA content increases defects in the concrete, reducing the fc of RC and RRC. Electric flux

Figure 9 presents the electric flux values of RRC and RC samples. Electric flux is commonly employed to evaluate the chloride ion penetration. The higher values indicate increased chloride ion transport and, consequently, lower resistance to chloride ion passed. In contrast, lower electric flux values correspond to improved chloride ion passed resistance. Compared to natural concrete, when the CA replacement ratio was 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, the electric flux values of RC increased by 6.3%, 44.5%, 54.7%, and 60.9%, while the electric flux values of RRC concrete increased by 23.4%, 52.3%, 77.3%, and 89.1%, respectively. It can be seen that the penetration resistance of both RC and RRC decreased with CA content.

Electric flux.

When the CA replacement ratio was 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, the RRC electric flux values increased by 16.2%, 5.4%, 14.7%, and 17.5% compared to the RC. Although the strength values of the RC and RRC specimens were similar when the RCA and RRCA content was the same, the RRC electric flux values were significantly greater than those of the RC specimens. This is attributed to the higher crushing index, water absorption, interfacial area, and number of micro cracks in the RRCA than in the RCA. While the tiny cracks and pore defects in the RRCA appear small enough to have little impact on concrete strength, it is important to note that these extremely small micro cracks can increase the probability of capillary connectivity within the concrete, significantly increasing the chloride ions passed and significantly reducing the penetration resistance to chloride ion of RRC. This phenomenon is also very similar to the conclusions in references30.

Frost resistance

Figure 10 shows the trends in mass loss and RDEM for RRC and RC specimens after freeze-thaw cycles. It should be noted that the 3 specimens of the RRC100 specimen were incomplete after 150 freeze-thaw cycles, making it impossible to measure their mass loss and RDEM. Figure 10 shows that with increasing CA replacement content, the concrete mass loss rate significantly increases, while the RDEM significantly decreases. Due to the surface of the RCA and RRCA often contains an old mortar layer, which has high water absorption and high porosity. The pores of these old mortars easily absorb moisture during freeze-thaw cycles, which high water absorption rate causes the RCA and RRCA to become saturated with water, generating large expansion stresses during freezing and causing micro cracks to expand. Furthermore, the uneven moisture content of recycled and natural aggregates creates regions of varying freezing rates within the concrete, which can easily lead to the rapid development of micro cracks within concrete due to stress concentration. These two factors result in the frost resistance of RCA and RRCA being lower than that of NCA. As the replacement rate of CA increases, the decline in concrete frost resistance becomes more pronounced.

Electric flux.

When the CA replacement rate is the same, the RRC specimens experience significantly greater mass and elastic modulus losses than the RC specimens. When the CA replacement rate is 50% and 100%, respectively, the RRC specimens experience a mass loss of 16.7% and 22.0% greater than the RC specimens after 125 cycles, and a 5.3% and 13.3% greater elastic modulus loss than the RC specimens after 125 cycles. This is because the mortar layer’s proportion in the RRCA aggregate increases, further increasing its porosity and water absorption. This micro pore micro cracks and degradation effect becomes more pronounced during freeze-thaw cycles, and its impact on concrete performance becomes even more significant.

As the CA replacement ratio increases, the T2 spectrum area of RC and RRC concrete increases significantly, and the peak shifts of T2 spectrum to right. This indicates that as the CA content increases, the pores within the concrete become larger, and the porosity increases4,15,17. This is because recycled coarse aggregate contains numerous micro cracks and pores, which can expand further under shrinkage stress. Increasing the RCA and RRCA content leads to a further increase in the porosity. This is why the concrete’s cracking, impermeability, and frost resistance all decrease significantly with the RCA and RRCA content. On the other hand, the RCA and RRCA contain a certain amount of pre-absorbed water, which does not appear to be completely consumed (either through hydration reactions or through migration and evaporation). A small amount remains within the pores of the concrete at 28 days of age. This may be another important reason for the significant increase T2 spectrum area (especially the transition pores) of RRC and RC with increasing CA replacement. The above phenomenon has also been reported similarly in other literature33,34.

As shown in Fig. 11c and d, the first peak area of the T2 spectrum of the RRC sample (at the transition pore location) is significantly larger than that of the RC sample, while the second and third peak areas of the two samples are similar. This is due to the fact that RRCA has higher crushing index, water absorption rate, interface area, and number of micro cracks (inherent defects or existing porosity in RRC aggregate) than RCA. Theoretically, RRCA absorbs more water during mixing or high-pressure saturation water absorption, resulting in the larger first peak area of the T2 spectrum of the RRC sample. The second and third peak areas likely originate primarily from particle accumulation pores and residual pores (new pores) resulting from insufficient new hydration products. Since the amount and quality of cementitious materials, fine aggregate, and water used are identical, the RRC and RC specimens show similarities. This confirms the validity of our aforementioned test results while the tiny cracks and pore defects in RRCA appear sufficiently small to have little impact on concrete strength, these microcracks can increase the probability of capillary pore connectivity within the concrete, reducing its crack resistance, impermeability, and frost resistance.

High-magnification (1000×) SEM observations indicate that abundant hydration products are present in all three groups. This is likely attributed to the relatively high cement content used in this study, where sufficient cement paste contributes to the formation of a relatively dense concrete matrix. Careful comparison reveals that the morphology and distribution of microcracks within the cement matrix constitute one of the most pronounced differences among the three groups. As shown in Fig. 12, the hydration products in the C0 specimen appear relatively loosely arranged, and the bonding between calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and calcium hydroxide (CH) is not well developed (Fig. 12a). Although the overall morphology of hydration products in the C0 specimen does not indicate a highly compact microstructure, the interfacial region appears relatively dense, and no obvious microcrack development is observed within the cement paste.

For RC100 and RRC100 specimens (as shown in Figs. 13 and 14), although the hardened cement paste is relatively dense and neat, numerous micro cracks and loose accumulations of hydration products are present. We speculate that these micro cracks may have developed from existing pores in the RCA or RRCA (e.g., interfacial defects, micro pores, and micro cracks). The shrinkage stress differential generated during cement hydration further amplifies the original defects in the RCA or RRCA, promoting the interconnection and further development of these micro pores and micro cracks. Furthermore, these loose hydration product hillocks likely arise from the continued hydration reaction of some unhydrated active particles (including cement or other active powders) in the RRCA. Although it’s not yet directly proven that these loose deposits degrade the mechanical and durability properties of concrete, microstructural evidence suggests that cracks in these areas are more likely to propagate. Therefore, it’s clear that these excess and peculiar loose deposits do degrade the density of the cement matrix. Furthermore, it’s noteworthy that the RRC100 specimens exhibit significantly more micro cracks and loose hydration product accumulations than the RC100 specimens, primarily contributing to the RRC100 specimens’ poorer crack resistance, impermeability, and frost resistance compared to the RC100 specimens.

Conclusions

To promote the recycling of RRC, the effects of different replacement ratios of RCA and RRCA on the mechanical properties of RRC were systematically investigated. Nine groups of RC and RRC specimens with replacement ratios of 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% were prepared, and their crack resistance, mechanical strength, impermeability, and frost resistance were evaluated. In addition, pore structure characteristics and microstructural features were analyzed. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

RRCA exhibits a higher water absorption capacity, more microcracks, and a more porous structure than RCA, leading to an increased cracking risk and reduced crack resistance of the resulting concrete. At the same replacement ratio, RRC shows inferior crack resistance compared to RC. In particular, the crack resistance of the RRC50 specimen is slightly lower than that of the RC100 specimen.

The RRCA and RCA leads to a reduction in concrete strength. However, the strength of RRC is comparable to that of RC when RA content is the same. Specifically, the 28 d fst and fc of RRC concrete are only 3.3% and 3.1% lower than those of RC specimens at equivalent replacement levels, respectively.

The RRCA and RCA leads to a reduction in concrete strength. However, the strength of RRC is comparable to that of RC when RA content is the same. Specifically, the 28 d fst and fc of RRC concrete are only 3.3% and 3.1% lower than those of RC specimens at equivalent replacement levels, respectively.

For RRC, the electric flux, freeze–thaw elastic modulus reduction, and mass loss are significantly higher than those of RC at the same replacement level. Notably, the permeability and frost resistance of the RRC50 specimens are slightly inferior to those of the RC100 specimens.

Although the studies have demonstrated the potential application value of RRCA and RRC, the use of a constant water–cement ratio and controlled curing conditions may limit the generalizability of the results to more variable field conditions. Further research is still required to investigate variable water–cement ratios or curing conditions, other long-term durability properties, and validation under field conditions.
