Significance of 3-dimensional cardiac anatomical axis as the predictor of low QRS amplitude of subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator
Authors: Yukihiro Uehara, Nobuhiko Ueda, Shumpei Mori, Takanori Kawabata, Kohei Ishibashi, Tsukasa Oshima, Toshihiro Nakamura, Satoshi Oka, Yuichiro Miyazaki, Akinori Wakamiya, Kenzaburo Nakajima, Tsukasa Kamakura, Mitsuru Wada, Yuko Inoue, Koji Miyamoto, Takeshi Aiba, Kengo Kusano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSMART Pass (SP) deactivation is associated with inappropriate shock from a subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (S-ICD). A low-amplitude subcutaneous electrogram (S-ECG) is a risk factor for SP deactivation; however, the cause of the low-amplitude of S-ECG is unclear.ObjectiveWe aimed to investigate the relationship between the cardiac anatomical axis and S-ECG amplitude.MethodsWe retrospectively investigated 73 patients who underwent S-ICD implantation between 2016 and 2021 using computed tomography data. The cardiac anatomical axis was defined as the line connecting the summit of the inferior pyramidal space, where the atrioventricular node is supposed to be located, and the apex of the left ventricle. We assessed the relationship between rightward, horizontal, and ventral rotations and the amplitude of the S-ECG in each vector. The primary en d point was SP deactivation.ResultsThere were significant correlations between rightward rotation and amplitude of the primary vector (R2 = 0.09, P = .002), rightward rotation and amplitude of the secondary vector (R2 = 0.12, P < .001), and ventral rotation and amplitude of the secondary vector (R2 = 0.06, P = .02). Ten patients experienced SP deactivation. The optimal cutoff value of rightward rotation for SP deactivation was 27° (area under the curve, 0.69; sensitivity, 0.90; specificity, 0.49; P = .02). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with rightward rotation > 27° had a higher risk of SP deactivation (log-rank test, P = .02).ConclusionThe cardiac anatomical axis and S-ECG amplitude are significantly correlated. Rightward rotation is a risk factor for SP deactivation.Keywords: Subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, Inappropriate shocks, SMART Pass deactivation, Cardiac anatomical axis, Amplitude of subcutaneous electrogram

Introduction

Subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (S-ICDs) reduce lead-related complications compared with transvenous ICDs, although there are no differences in efficacy and safety between the 2.1,2 Despite these results, inappropriate shocks (IASs) remain to be resolved; a recent study showed that the IAS rate was 3.1% in 1 year.3 The causes of which are classified as cardiac, including supraventricular tachycardia and T-wave oversensing (TWOS), and non-cardiac oversensing, including myopotential and electromagnetic interference. The study by Thomas et al4 demonstrated the utility of 12-lead electrocardiography in S-ICD screening, revealing that large T waves were identified as a primary cause of oversensing. The SMART Pass (SP) algorithm was developed to avoid the risk of IAS of S-ICD caused by TWOS, and SP-reduced IAS.5 Because a high-pass filtering may attenuate QRS amplitude and lead to undersensing of potentially lethal ventricular arrhythmias, the SP status is deactivated when small QRS amplitude (<0.25 mV) with 2 long intervals >1.4 s or asystole (>10 s) occurs to avoid to undersense R wave. Recently, SP deactivation was associated with IAS.6,7 Although a low subcutaneous electrogram (S-ECG) is a risk factor for SP deactivation, the precise mechanism of a low-amplitude S-ECG is unknown.

A previous report using computed tomography (CT) demonstrated that the electrical and anatomical axes were significantly correlated8; however, little is known about the relationship between cardiac rotation and S-ECG amplitude or SP deactivation. We hypothesized that if the axis of the implanted S-ICD was different from the cardiac anatomical axis, the amplitude of the S-ECG would be low, which could cause SP deactivation. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the relationship among cardiac rotation, S-ECG amplitude, and clinical events.

Methods

Patient population

Among the 115 cases of S-ICD implantation between 2016 and 2021, we retrospectively investigated 73 cases using tomography data. The indications for ICD implantation were based on the Japanese Circulation Society guidelines.9 Patients passed the screening test with at least 1 sensing vector in all positions, including supine, sitting, standing, and right supine, before S-ICD implantation. Baseline clinical data, including age at implantation, sex, underlying heart disease, body mass index (BMI), and electrocardiographic and administrating drugs, were collected.

Device implantation

The S-ICDs were implanted under general anesthesia in a standard manner, as previously described.10 EMBLEMTM S-ICD SYSTEM models A209 and A219 (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Massachusetts) were used as generators. A pulse generator was placed posterior to the midaxillary line to achieve a low PRAETORIAN score.11

Device setting

An automatic setup was used to select the optimal setting vector after the S-ICD implantation. Among the patients who could exercise, a treadmill exercise test was performed to select the sensing vector of the appropriate QRS/T wave and create a template for S-ECG during exercise after S-ICD implantation. In contrast, the sensing vector was automatically determined at every clinical visit for patients who could not perform the exercise test. The conditional zone was set to 200 bpm, and the non-conditional zone was set to 250 bpm.

CT and the cardiac anatomical axis

Chest CT images were obtained pre- or post-operatively with or without contrast. The CT scan was performed in the supine position, aligning the center of the body with the center of the gantry. The cardiac anatomical axis was determined by connecting the summit of the inferior pyramidal space, where the atrioventricular node is supposed to be located,12 to the apex of the left ventricle. We identified the vector of the cardiac anatomical axis, decomposed it into 3 planes, and determined the rotation using the following methods.13 First, the 3-dimensional coordinates of the left ventricular apex (XLVA, YLVA, ZLVA) and inferior pyramidal space (XIPS, YIPS, ZIPS) were calculated. The vector of the cardiac anatomical axis was then determined using the 2 coordinates and decomposed into 3 rotation angles: rightward, horizontal, and ventral rotations (Figure 1). We also assessed the angle between the inferior vena cava (IVC) and interventricular septum (IVS). The IVS angle was defined as the angle between the vertical line drawn from the center of the IVC at the level of the ostium of the coronary sinus and the line drawn from the center of the IVC tangential to the right ventricular site of the IVS (Supplemental Figure 1).14

Assessment of 12-lead and subcutaneous electrogram

Twelve-lead and S-ECGs were assessed during the first visit after S-ICD implantation. All S-ECGs were performed with the patient in the supine position. The S-ECG amplitude of the selected vector was measured by reading the scale of the printed waveform. The 12-lead QRS and S-ECG amplitudes were defined as higher R and S waves in leads I, II, and III.

PRAETORIAN score

The PRAETORIAN score is calculated with a bidirectional chest radiograph in 4 steps.11 In step 1, the amount of fat tissue between the coil and sternum is determined on the lateral chest radiograph. In step 2, the position of the generator in relation to the midline is assessed using a lateral chest radiograph. Step 3 evaluates the amount of fat tissue between the nearest point of the generator and the thoracic wall on the postero-anterior chest radiograph. The final step, step 4, subtracts 40 points for patients with a BMI ≤25 kg/m2 and a score ≥90 points. The final score classifies the risk of conversion failure during defibrillation testing into 3 categories: low risk (<90 points), intermediate risk (≥90 to <150 points), and high risk (≥150 points).

SP deactivation

The primary end point of this study was SP deactivation. Events were detected at the outpatient clinic using the LATITUDETM remote monitoring system.

The SP algorithm is a high-pass filter that reduces cardiac signal oversensing.3 It is set to deactivate at the instance of a low-amplitude S-ECG (<0.25 mV) with 2 long intervals >1.4 s or periods of asystole (>10 s). The SP status was checked at every outpatient clinic using the LATITUDE remote monitoring system. When the SP was deactivated, we manually set SP again.

Inappropriate shock

IASs were defined as those delivered for any rhythm other than ventricular fibrillation or tachycardia. The causes of IASs were classified as cardiac, comprising T-wave or P-wave oversensing, or non-cardiac, comprising oversensing to noise. Events were detected at the outpatient clinic using the LATITUDE remote monitoring system.

Statistical analyses

Categorical data are presented as counts and percentages. Continuous data are presented as means ± standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. Circular–linear correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between rotation angles and the amplitudes. Circular–circular correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between rotation angles and IVS angle. The cutoff value of rightward rotation in relation to SP deactivation was evaluated using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to predict the primary end point. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to compare the SP deactivation events. All tests were 2-sided, and values of P < .05 were considered significant. Univariable analyses were first performed to identify independent predictors of the primary end point, and predictors with a significance level of <5% were included in the multivariable models. Multivariable analyses were performed using Cox regression models, and statistical significance was set at P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical approval

Rotation angles and amplitude of the 12-lead and subcutaneous ECG

The mean rightward, horizontal, and ventral rotation angles were 29.5 ± 9.5°, 58.8 ± 11.6°, and 43.3 ± 13.9°, respectively. The amplitudes of S-ECG were 1.6 mV (IQR, 1.0–2.4 mV) (primary), 1.5 mV (IQR, 0.9–2.0 mV) (secondary), and 1.0 mV (IQR, 0.8–1.2 mV) (alternate) (Table 1). There was a significant correlation between rightward rotation and (90° – IVS angle) (R = 0.521, P < .001). The regression analysis yielded the following equation for rightward rotation using 90° – IVS angle as the predictor: rightward rotation = 9.125 + 0.534 × (90° – IVS angle).

Correlation between rotation angles and amplitude of subcutaneous ECG

There were significant correlations between rightward rotation and amplitude of the primary vector (R2 = 0.09, P = .002), rightward rotation and amplitude of the secondary vector (R2 = 0.06, P < 0.001), and ventral rotation and amplitude of the secondary vector (R2 = 0.06, P = .02) (Figure 2). The rotation angle and SP deactivation

During the median follow-up period of 780 days, 10 patients (13.7%) experienced SP deactivation and 7 patients (9.5%) experienced IAS. The ROC curves demonstrated that the optimal cutoff value of rightward rotation for predicting SP deactivation was 27° (area under the curve, 0.69; sensitivity, 0.90; specificity, 0.49; P = .02). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with rightward rotation >27° had a higher risk of SP deactivation (log-rank, P = .02) (Figure 4). The baseline characteristics between the rightward rotation ≤27° and >27° groups are shown in Table 2. There were no significant differences in the PRAETORIAN scores between the 2 groups. Multivariable analysis showed that the rightward rotation was an independent predictor of SP deactivation (hazard ratio [HR], 1.08; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.01–1.17; P = .04) (Table 3).

Discussion

Main findings

The main findings of this study were as follows: there were significant correlations between the rightward rotation and the amplitude of the primary or secondary vector and between ventral rotation and the amplitude of the secondary vector. Furthermore, the amplitude of the S-ECG and rightward rotation were independent predictors of SP deactivation.

SP deactivation, low amplitude of S-ECG, and inappropriate shock of S-ICD

Previous studies have demonstrated a higher risk of IAS with S-ICDs in patients with SP deactivation. Monkhouse et al7 showed SP deactivation was associated with increased risk of IAS (HR, 5.36; 95% CI, 2.37–12.13). This study reported that SP deactivation was due to low-amplitude R waves (94%) and sensing vector changes should be strongly recommended. Although the exact relationship between SP deactivation and IAS remains unknown, it has been speculated that TWOS after SP deactivation may cause IAS. In addition, we previously reported that myopotential oversensing might be another cause of IAS after SP deactivation.6 These phenomena could be due to the auto-gain control for a low QRS amplitude, which caused overall survival of T wave or myopotential. SP deactivation due to low QRS amplitude is an indicator of IAS.

To the best of our knowledge, no study has focused on identifying the factors that predict low amplitude. In this study, rightward rotation was found to be an independent predictor of SP deactivation. It has been suggested that the cardiac anatomical axis can affect the S-ECG amplitude and cause SP deactivation. There was no significant difference in the risk of IAS between patients with and without rightward rotation in this study (Supplemental Figure 2). This may be due to the relatively low incidence of IAS and small study cohort.

Cardiac anatomical axis and the amplitude of subcutaneous electrogram

In this study, we showed significant negative correlations between rightward rotation and the amplitudes of the primary and secondary S-ECGs and between ventral rotation and the amplitude of the secondary S-ECG. A previous study using CT demonstrated a significant correlation between the cardiac anatomical axis and the QRS axis.8 The amplitude of bipolar ECGs is sensitive to direction, and it decreases when electrode pairs are oriented obliquely to a propagating wavefront, and the bipolar amplitude is minimal when bipolar pairs are orthogonal to the wavefront.15,16 Our study suggests that as the S-ICD was implanted in a posterior position to achieve a low PRAETORIAN score, the QRS axis deviates from the primary and secondary vectors if the heart rotates rightward or ventral, reducing the S-ECG amplitude of the primary and secondary vectors.

The potential utility of the cardiac anatomical axis in improving S-ICD eligibility

Our study demonstrates that the cardiac anatomical axis influenced S-ECG amplitude, and it may be a useful factor to consider when assessing S-ICD eligibility. Specifically, anterior repositioning of the lateral lead may improve eligibility in cases with rightward or ventral anatomical orientation. Wang et al17 reported that 40% of 101 adult patients with congenital heart disease were ineligible for S-ICD, and patients with tetralogy of Fallot passed right-sided screening more often than left-sided screening. This study suggests that repositioning of the lead electrodes may enable S-ICD eligibility in certain cases. In contrast, our study shows that in patients with rightward and/or ventral cardiac rotation, anterior repositioning of the lateral chest electrode may increase QRS amplitude and improve S-ICD eligibility. These rotations can be evaluated on axial CT images and lateral chest radiographs, respectively. Therefore, they are suitable for consideration as S-ICD screening methods. Reassessment of S-ICD eligibility by anterior repositioning of the lateral chest electrode (ie, the expected implantation site of the generator) may be useful in patients with rightward and/or ventral cardiac rotation who were initially deemed ineligible during screening. Due to unknown impact on defibrillation threshold, it is reasonable to assess the anterior repositioning of the lateral electrode, ensuring it does not exceed the midline, and to consider screening failure for patients who do not pass screening unless the electrode exceeds the midline.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and the sample size was relatively small; therefore, further investigations are required to assess the association between the anatomical axes and low QRS amplitude. Second, this study did not assess the relationship between the generator position and cardiac anatomy. Third, the primary end point of SP deactivation—rather than IAS—represents a soft end point, and the small cohort size inherently limits its interpretation. Finally, no association was found between the cardiac anatomical axis and the IAS. However, this may be due to the small number of IAS cases, and further validation with a larger sample size is required.

Conclusion

The cardiac anatomical axis and S-ECG amplitude are significantly correlated with the rightward rotation and amplitude of the primary or secondary vector, and the ventral rotation and amplitude of the secondary vector. Rightward rotation is an independent predictor of SP deactivation. In patients with more rightward or ventral rotation of the heart, it may be better to consider the appropriate implantation site of the S-ICD based on the cardiac anatomical axis.
