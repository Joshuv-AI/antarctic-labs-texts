Integrative QSP Modeling of Cancer Stem Cells in Triple‐Negative Breast Cancer: Impacts on Tumor Immunity and Response to Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors
Authors: Junsheng Xue, Weizhe Jian, Tianyu Wang, Zhujun Jiang, Xinyu Hou, Qirui Yang, Tianyu Bao, Lei Cai, Enze Gan, Chen Liu, Tianyan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer stem cells (CSCs) contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment through complex mechanisms and tumor‐immune interactions. However, the key determinants of CSC characteristics in driving tumor progression, immune suppression, and response to ICIs remain unclear and require systematic investigation. This study developed a quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) model covering various CSC properties, thereby capturing the temporal dynamics and CSC‐immune interactions in triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC). Using the unified longitudinal dataset of tumor growth, CSC frequency, and immune cell dynamics that we obtained from BALB/c mice bearing wild‐type or Cd274‐knockout 4T1 cells under various inoculation conditions, which provides multi‐dimensional insights into CSC‐related biology, the QSP model was calibrated and validated. Simulations and sensitivity analysis indicated that TNBC tumors with strong stemness exhibited significantly accelerated tumor growth and reduced infiltration of cytotoxic immune cells such as cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and natural killer (NK) cells. These were associated with CSCs' enhanced self‐renewal capacity, stemness maintenance, secretion of transforming growth factor beta (TGF‐β) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and PD‐1/PD‐L1–mediated immunosuppression. ICIs showed minimal efficacy in tumors with enhanced stemness, which was also linked to the aforementioned characteristics. Both the administration sequence and initiation timing of ICIs differentially influenced the therapeutic outcomes. These findings elucidate the roles of CSCs in TNBC progression, tumor immunity, and ICI efficacy while identifying the key underlying CSC characteristics, suggesting the potential value of assessing CSC biomarkers or abundance before ICI treatment and support the development of ICIs and anti‐CSC combination therapies.Keywords: cancer stem cell, immune checkpoint inhibitor, quantitative systems pharmacology, triple‐negative breast cancer, tumor immunology

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become a breakthrough for tumor treatment, which can leverage the immune system to attack tumor cells, potentially leading to complete response and long‐term survival [1, 2]. However, the low response rates of ICI monotherapies for most tumor types have posed significant challenges to their clinical application [3]. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are implicated as key players in this process. Omics studies based on clinical samples have established that high tumor stemness is generally associated with weaker immune infiltration and worse efficacy of ICIs in most solid tumors [4, 5]. Existing studies have shown that CSCs are a small proportion of cancer cells with self‐renewal and differentiation abilities within the tumor, which may also serve as one of the key components that affect tumor immunity [6, 7]. Transforming growth factor beta (TGF‐β), interleukin 10 (IL‐10), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secreted by CSCs could reshape various immune cells toward pro‐tumor phenotypes, inhibit antigen presentation, and infiltration of cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) into the tumor tissue [6, 7]. Additionally, CSCs could inhibit CTL activity by expressing high levels of immune checkpoints such as programmed cell death protein 1 ligand 1 (PD‐L1) [7, 8]. Therefore, targeting CSCs could be promising to reverse immunosuppression and improve the ICI efficacy. However, a quantitative understanding of how CSCs influence tumor progression, immunity, and ICI efficacy is still lacking.

While various mechanisms have been proposed above, existing research on CSCs has predominantly focused on isolated aspects. This makes it difficult to distinguish which mechanisms are most critical and to what extent they contribute to the observed phenotypes [7]. Consequently, an integrative approach that simultaneously evaluates multiple mechanisms is urgently needed. During tumor progression and treatments, there would be complex dynamic changes for CSCs and differentiated tumor cells (DTCs) [9]. The extensive interactions among CSCs and other tumor components could also lead to complex changes in their abundance and status over time [9]. Quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) model, as a mechanism‐based mathematical modeling approach, can simultaneously integrate biological data including but not limited to molecular, cellular, and tissue‐organ levels, allowing for integrative research [10]. Currently, only a few mathematical models have incorporated CSCs. For these models, simplified mechanisms of CSCs were employed [11, 12], and in many cases, there was a lack of sufficient data for model validation [11, 13, 14], particularly longitudinal data on immune cells and CSCs. Hence, a QSP model for tumor immunity that incorporates more CSC mechanisms and offers a more comprehensive investigation of CSCs remains essential.

Triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer, whose poor prognosis is largely attributable to a strong invasive and metastatic tendency, in which CSCs are likely to play a crucial role [15, 16]. Pembrolizumab (targeting PD‐1) and atezolizumab (targeting PD‐L1) have been approved for TNBC treatment, either as monotherapies or in combination with chemotherapy [2]. ICIs targeting CTLA‐4 are also under clinical investigation [1]. However, the response rates to ICIs remain low in TNBC. These characteristics make TNBC an ideal model for an integrated study of CSCs.

Therefore, in this study, we developed a QSP model for TNBC that mechanistically integrates multiple CSC properties and describes the intensive CSC‐tumor immune interaction, an aspect overlooked by existing models. To enhance the model's credibility and reliability, we utilized tumor‐bearing mice for data collection, a strategy that enables convenient tissue sampling and longitudinal monitoring, thereby generating a dataset that provides multi‐dimensional insights into CSC‐related biology for model calibration and validation. Simulations based on this QSP model provide quantitative evidence for the critical roles of CSCs in TNBC progression, immune suppression, and ICI response.

Materials and Methods

Overview of the CSC‐Immune QSP Model

The QSP model was established using SimBiology within MATLAB 2022a (MathWorks, Natick, MA). Although the basic form and principles of some equations were adapted from the immuno‐oncology QSP platform developed by Aleksander S. Popel's group [17], extensive modifications were made and a novel framework was developed from the ground up. This enables a more detailed integration of multi‐faceted CSC biology and a more granular description of tumor‐immune interactions and the tumor cell's role in shaping the microenvironment. Based on the concept of the cancer‐immune cycle [8], the model incorporated key tumor immunity compartments, including lymph node (LN), lymph transport system (LTS), blood vessel (BV), and tumor (T), as well as a peripheral compartment (P) representing other physiological structures (Figure 1).

CSC immune mechanisms and QSP model. (A) Known mechanisms of CSCs hindering the cancer‐immune cycle. (B) QSP model schematic diagram. Arrows indicate migration or promotion, and dots indicate inhibition.

It was assumed that tumor cells consisted of CSCs and DTCs, both of which possess self‐renewal capabilities. CSCs could differentiate into DTCs to sustain rapid tumor growth, while a small proportion of DTCs could de‐differentiate back into CSCs [9]. Additionally, a feedback mechanism regulated the CSC frequency [9]. The innate immune system, including natural killer cells (NKs), tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs), dendritic cells (DCs), and TAMs, was initially activated by tumor cell debris. The adaptive immune cells, such as CTLs, helper T cells (Th1s), and regulatory T cells (Tregs), could exert anti‐tumor or immunosuppressive functions [8]. The CSCs and DTCs could directly or indirectly interact with immune cells through cytokines and cell synapses, while tumor immunity could also shape the stemness of tumor cells.

The model has described the differences between CSCs and DTCs in the following aspects. First, CSCs are usually smaller than DTCs [9], thus VCSC and ACSC were set lower. Second, CSCs are mostly in a dormant state, with a lower self‐renewal rate (kngCSC) than DTCs [9]. Third, CSCs resist NK and CTL killing through autophagy and anti‐apoptosis [18], resulting in lower keffCTLCSC and keffNKCSC values. Fourth, CSCs express lower levels of MHC‐I molecules, making them less recognizable to CTLs, and IFN‐γ is less effective in inducing MHC‐I expression on CSCs [7]. These differences are reflected in parameters like koutMHC, kIFNgMHC and IFNg50,MHC. Fifth, CSCs have lower immunogenicity compared with DTCs [7], with less antigen released per unit of CSC debris. Sixth, CSCs exhibit higher levels of PD‐L1 [7, 19], leading to an elevated PDL1CSC/ACSC ratio compared with PDL1DTC/ADTC. Seventh, CSCs secrete inhibitory cytokines such as TGF‐β and VEGF at higher rates than DTCs [6, 7], contributing to stronger immunosuppression. Finally, when PD‐L1 on CSCs and DTCs forms equal amounts of complexes with PD‐1 on CTLs and NK cells, CSCs exert stronger inhibitory effects [7, 19], reflected by higher PD150,CTL_DTC and PD150,NK_DTC values than CSCs.

For QSP model calibration and validation, we used allografts derived from 4T1 and 4T1‐PDL1KO cell lines and their enriched CSCs, with parameters informed by literature and calibrated manually. Parameter sources are broadly categorized as follows. First, certain parameters (e.g., target molecule expression on tumor cells, checkpoint levels on immune cells) are adopted directly across species, as they reflect cellular traits rather than species differences. Second, kinetic parameters for rapid processes (e.g., ligand‐receptor or MHC–peptide binding) are not allometrically scaled. Third, physiological parameters (e.g., murine blood/lymph volume) are replaced with species‐specific values. Fourth, we assumed immune cell size is conserved across species and used human data. Finally, allometric scaling was applied to slow, lifespan‐related processes (e.g., immune cell lifespan and trafficking rates), using Equation 3 shown in the Supporting Information.

Model Simulations

To study the impact of CSCs on tumor growth and immunity in relation to ICI efficacy, we integrated the PK and mechanisms of clinically used ICIs (αPD‐1 and αCTLA‐4) for TNBC into the CSC‐immune QSP model, and calibrated the related parameter based on the tumor growth data. αPD‐1, αCTLA‐4, and their combination were evaluated at 10 mg/kg (i.p., Q3D), a dose commonly used in animal studies [20].

Simulations were conducted using sundials solver. The absolute tolerance and relative tolerance were set to 1e‐9 and 1e‐6, respectively. For most simulation scenarios, the simulation time was set to 50 days. This was based on observations in animal experiments, where 5 × 104 4T1 cells were inoculated into BALB/c mice, the mice generally did not survive over 45 days.

Sensitivity Analysis

The Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) method was adopted to generate distinct sets of parameter values within the specified ranges for each parameter under investigation. Simulations were conducted based on each sampled parameter set, and partial rank correlation coefficients (PRCC) were calculated to quantify the strength and direction of correlations between the investigated parameters and the model outputs during simulations.

Bridging Model Simulation to Clinical Phenomena

RNA‐seq data of 173 TNBC samples were obtained from the TCGA database. The tumor‐infiltrating level of CTL was calculated using the CIBERSORTx method [21], and the stemness score of each tumor was computed based on literature [22]. Spearman correlation analysis was then performed. In addition, 2000 virtual individuals were generated by LHS method, and ROC curves were plotted to predict ICI response using biomarkers such as CSC frequency, intratumoral immune cell densities, and parameters in Table S5.

Statistical Analysis

The simulation data were processed and stored using Excel 2019 and R 4.2.2. Differences in parameters, simulation outputs, and the 95th percentile confidence intervals of tumor growth inhibition (TGI) were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test, implemented in the stats package of R 4.2.2. Statistical differences of experimental data between two groups were analyzed by Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test (for non‐normally distributed data) using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Results

The QSP Model Shows Acceptable Predictive Performance on Tumor Volume, CSCs, and Immune Cells

The model predictions aligned well with the observations under different calibration and validation scenarios, indicating an acceptable predictive performance (Figure 2B–E). The model also effectively captured the phenomenon that innate immune cells peaked earlier than adaptive immune cells. 4T1‐PDL1KO tumors showed markedly higher CTL levels and lower Treg and CSC frequencies than 4T1 tumors. PD‐L1–deficient CSCs were more susceptible to CTL and NK cell killing, leading to slower tumor growth, suggesting the importance of the PD‐1/PD‐L1 pathway in tumor progression and immune suppression (Figure 2E). Apart from the tumor volume, our experimental results also demonstrate that the inoculation of 4T1‐PDL1KO CSCs leads to a significant increase in the intratumoral CTLs (data not shown), further confirming the robust predictive capability of the model.

Initial CSC Frequency Significantly Affects Tumor Immunity in TNBC

To investigate the impact of CSCs on TNBC progression and tumor immunity, we simulated tumor growth and immune cell dynamics under varying initial CSC frequencies while maintaining a constant initial tumor cell number. The initial CSC frequency had a limited impact on tumor growth (Figure 3A). As the initial CSC frequency increased, the densities of innate and adaptive immune cells markedly decreased. The levels of anti‐tumor immune cells (NK cells, CTLs, and M1 TAMs) and immunostimulatory cytokines (IL‐2 and IFN‐γ) were significantly decreased, whereas immunosuppressive cytokines (TGF‐β and VEGF) showed marked elevation, collectively indicating a suppressed tumor immune microenvironment (Figure 3B–K). Regardless of initial CSC frequencies, all groups reached convergence by Day 30 due to differentiation equilibrium, followed by sustained increase (Figure 3L).

Key CSC Characteristics That Affect Tumor Growth and Immunity in TNBC

To further identify which CSC characteristics significantly affect TNBC progression and tumor immunity, sensitivity analysis was conducted on CSC‐specific parameters in the QSP model, and evaluated by PRCC values between parameter values and maximum model outputs. As shown in Figure 4A, the CSC self‐renewal rate (kngCSC), the de‐differentiation capacity of DTCs into CSCs (Rdc), and the secretion rate constant of VEGF by CSCs (ksecVEGFCSC) were positively correlated with tumor volume, suggesting that CSC proliferation not only directly contributes to tumor growth but also indirectly supports it by angiogenesis promotion. In addition, weaker CSC‐mediated CTL inhibition via the PD‐1/PD‐L1 pathway (PD150,CTL_CSC) was associated with smaller tumor volume, indicating that CSCs can also influence TNBC progression by modulating CTL activity.

(C) The influence of CSC properties and host immune strength on tumor growth, CSC frequency, M1/M2 ratio, intratumoral cell densities of NK cells, CTLs, and Tregs, intratumoral concentration of IL‐2. As for the correlations between CSC parameters and immune metrics (Figure 4A), stronger TGF‐β and VEGF secretion abilities of CSCs were associated with reduced infiltration of NK cells and CTLs, while TGF‐β also promoted the accumulation of immunosuppressive cells like M2 TAMs. When CSCs exhibited weaker PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibition of NK cells, both NK cell density and frequency increased, resulting in enhanced tumor‐killing capacity and DC maturation. Larger PD150,CTL_CSC led to higher CTL infiltration, reduced Treg levels, and elevated mature DC (mDC) density. Moreover, larger kngCSC and Rdc were both associated with reduced infiltration of most immune cell types, favoring the formation of a cold tumor.

To further investigate the roles of CSC‐expressed PD‐L1 on TNBC progression and immunity, we conducted simulation with PDL1CSC set to 0 while maintaining DTC properties unchanged. The absence of PD‐L1 on CSCs slightly slowed tumor growth and markedly activated immunity, with these effects being consistent regardless of initial CSC frequencies (Figure 4B).

TNBC Tumors With Prominent CSC Features Exhibited Significantly Accelerated Growth and Suppressed Immunity

Next, we assessed the impact magnitude of these key CSC features: kngCSC, Rdc, ksecTGFbCSC, ksecVEGFCSC, PD150,NK_CSC, PD150,CTL_DTC were selected. Different host immune strength was also simulated by adjusting the initial numbers of naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (nCD40 and nCD80) in the LN compartment (Table S3).

As shown in Figure 4C, although the initial CSC frequency was only 5%, the tumor with enhanced CSC properties (StemHigh) grew significantly faster than StemLow tumor and showed markedly reduced anti‐tumor immune cells and cytokines. Although host immune strength did modulate T cell infiltration, its influence was weaker compared with intrinsic tumor cell stemness. Simultaneously, RNA‐seq analysis of 173 clinical TNBC samples showed a significant negative correlation between tumor stemness score and CTL infiltration level, which is consistent with the trend predicted by the model simulation (Figure 4D).

CSC Accumulation in TNBC Tumors Is Mostly Dependent on Their Intrinsic Characteristics

We further investigated tumor microenvironmental regulation of CSC accumulation. We simulated cold and hot tumors, which differed in tumor cell immunogenicity, immune cell infiltration capacity, immunosuppressive cytokine levels, and immune checkpoint expression (Table S4).

Despite identical initial tumor cell numbers and CSC frequencies, the cold tumor exhibited significantly accelerated growth, lower infiltration of both innate and adaptive immune cells, and higher levels of immunosuppressive cytokines (Figure 5A–J). The CSC frequency in the hot tumor was slightly higher than in the cold tumor at various time points. However, due to the smaller tumor volume in the hot tumor, the CSC number in the cold tumor was significantly higher at late stage (Figure 5K,L).

We calculated the PRCC values between model parameters and maximum CSC frequency or number. The proliferation and differentiation‐related parameters of CSCs and DTCs (kngCSC, kngDTC, Rcd, Rdc, FACT) showed significant correlations with CSC frequency. TGF‐β and PD‐L1‐associated parameters modulating the differentiation equilibrium also modestly influenced CSC frequency (Figure 5M). As for CSC number, the most influential parameters were kngCSC, kngDTC, and Rdc. Besides, the growth and decay rate constants for the tumor's maximum carrying capacity (kngmax and kdeathmax) were also strongly correlated. VEGF and TGF‐β exhibited limited influence on CSC frequency compared with the aforementioned parameters (Figure 5N). These results suggest that CSC accumulation in TNBC is dominantly determined by the tumor cell‐intrinsic properties, with relatively limited influence from the tumor microenvironment.

Leveraging the virtual individuals in Figure 4C, we evaluated the efficacy of αCTLA‐4, αPD‐1, and their combination therapy. Under the same host immune strength, ICIs exhibited minimal effects on both tumor growth and CTL infiltration in the StemHigh individual, while the ICI efficacy significantly enhanced in the StemLow individual (Figure 6B–D), directly suggesting the negative impact of CSCs on ICI efficacy in TNBC. Even in the ImmuneHigh individual, tumors with high CSC stemness showed poor responses to ICIs (Figure S4). The differences in ICI efficacy are more dependent on the intrinsic properties of CSCs rather than their initial frequencies (Figure S5).

Sensitivity analysis was further conducted to identify key CSC characteristics that influence ICI efficacy. kngCSC, ksecVEGFCSC, ksecTGFbCSC, Rdc, PD‐L1 molecule number per CSC (PDL1CSC), and PD150,CTL_CSC all significantly affected the tumor growth inhibition achieved by ICIs (Figure 6E). The effect of PD150,NK_CSC on tumor growth inhibition was relatively minor. The efficacy of αCTLA‐4 was more dependent on weak CSC‐mediated suppression of CTLs (PD150,CTL_CSC), while αPD‐1 relied more on the intrinsic cytotoxicity of CTLs against CSCs (keffCTLCSC), which may come from their different mechanisms of action (Figure 6E). For the immune cell densities post‐ICI treatment, stronger CSC properties, as reflected by the parameters above, were associated with lower levels of anti‐tumor immune cells (Figure 6E), which could lead to the formation of an immune desert microenvironment and contribute to resistance.

LHS was performed using the parameters in Table S5 to generate 2000 virtual individuals, who received αCTLA‐4, αPD‐1, or combination therapy. The TGI was calculated from tumor volumes at Day 50 in the treatment and control groups. The individuals were then classified into responders (TGI equal to or larger than 60%) and non‐responders (TGI less than 60%). Individuals with lower kngCSC showed greater TGI, while kngDTC did not differ significantly between the two groups. Responders also exhibited significantly lower Rdc, while Rcd showed less difference, suggesting that compared with DTCs, CSCs may even play more critical roles in ICI treatment outcomes. ksecVEGFCSC and ksecTGFbCSC significantly influenced ICI efficacy. Stronger CTL cytotoxicity against CSCs and DTCs (keffCTLCSC, keffCTLDTC) was associated with improved TGI. Similar trends were observed with αCTLA‐4 and αPD‐1 monotherapies (Figure 7A, Figures S6 and S7). These virtual individuals were further stratified into high and low subgroups according to CSC‐related parameter values to compare TGIs. kngCSC and Rdc had the most pronounced impact on ICI efficacy (Figure 7B and Figure S8).

We further explored differences in CSC frequency, immune cell densities, and cytokine levels between responders and non‐responders. Pre‐treatment CSC frequency was predictive of ICI treatment outcomes (Figure 7C). Individuals with higher CSC frequency showed significantly lower TGI. This indicator continued to distinguish treatment outcomes during both early and late treatment stages. Among innate immune cells, higher densities of NK cells, mDCs, and TAMs were associated with better ICI response, while the M1/M2 ratio showed relatively small differences between groups. In the late treatment phase, responders exhibited significantly higher CTL and Treg densities. Additionally, the responder group showed elevated levels of anti‐tumor cytokines such as IFN‐γ and IL‐2, and reduced levels of TGF‐β (Figures S9–S11).

Using the virtual individuals generated based on parameter sampling in Table S5, we assessed the predictive power for treatment response to αCTLA‐4 or αPD‐1 by ROC analysis. As shown in Figure 7D, for αPD‐1 treatment, univariate analysis identified self‐renewal rate (kngCSC) and CSC frequency as top predictors (AUCs of 0.721 and 0.716, respectively), outperforming DTC‐related metrics. kngCSC and CSC frequency can also predict the response for αCTLA‐4 (Figure 7D). When focusing on clinically measurable biomarkers rather than specific mechanistic parameters, the CSC frequency may also emerge as an indicator for predicting patient responses to ICIs (Figure 7E). These findings suggest that both CSC‐ and immune‐related indicators hold promise as biomarkers for predicting ICI response.

ICI Dosing Sequence and Treatment Timing Critically Modulate Tumor Growth, Immunity, and CSC Frequency

Finally, investigation of ICI dosing regimens suggested that sequential αPD‐1 followed by αCTLA‐4 administration significantly enhanced TGI and CTL density compared with reverse sequence at matched total doses (Figure S12A), outperforming even higher‐dose monotherapies. Concurrent αCTLA‐4/αPD‐1 administration showed superior efficacy when initiated early. However, stronger ICI‐induced tumor suppression correlated with elevated CSC frequency, suggesting therapeutic benefit may require combining ICIs with anti‐CSC strategies. In addition, early intervention significantly improved the therapeutic outcomes of αPD‐1 compared with late‐stage treatment, while this was less pronounced for αCTLA‐4 (Figure S12B,C).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a QSP model incorporating CSC features and mechanisms identified in previous studies to detailedly describe the interactions between CSCs and tumor immunity. After multi‐perspective calibration using 4T1 and 4T1‐PDL1KO cell lines and their CSC‐enriched allograft models established from CSC‐enriched spheroids, we conducted simulations to quantitatively investigate the roles and mechanisms of CSCs in TNBC progression, immunity, and ICI efficacy.

Currently, there are very limited mathematical models incorporating both CSCs and tumor immunity components. Some models employ oversimplified assumptions that only characterize the transition between CSCs and DTCs while neglecting critical immune cells and cytokines [11, 12], making it inadequate for the description of the complex tumor‐immune interactions. Some models are purely theoretical without experimental data [13], raising concerns about the reliability of extrapolations and predictions. A QSP model includes CSCs and evaluates combination therapy of ICIs and anti‐angiogenic agents [23]. However, this model primarily focused on tumor vasculature and stromal cells under hypoxic conditions, but failed to distinguish CSCs from DTCs, specify CSC‐immune interactions, or validate its findings with experimental data on CSCs. Furthermore, current tumor immunity models generally lack the collection and utilization of temporal data on key immune cell populations, which may lead to significant parameter identifiability issues and subsequent uncertainty in simulation‐based predictions.

Our model refines the mechanistic details of tumor immunity, incorporating elements such as NK cells, a defined antigen uptake limit for DCs, competition between Tregs and CTLs for IL‐2, distinct tumor recognition mechanisms by NK and CTL cells (MHC‐dependent or independent), and the influence of tumor cells on the immune microenvironment. It also differentiates between CSCs and DTCs in terms of cell volume, proliferation rate, immunogenicity, secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, expression of immune checkpoints, and the suppression of NK cells and CTLs via the PD‐1/PD‐L1 pathway [6, 7, 9, 18, 19] (Table S1). This allows for a detailed description of the interactions between CSCs and tumor immunity. By leveraging the sampling flexibility of animal experiments, we collected longitudinal, multi‐dimensional data on tumor volume, immune cell frequency, and CSC frequency within a unified experimental framework to calibrate and validate the model, thus overcoming the clinical limitations of infrequent tumor sampling in patients due to ethical and procedural constraints. Therefore, we consider that this study has contributed a relatively detailed QSP model of tumor immunity.

Based on collected longitudinal data, CSC‐ and immune‐related parameters were calibrated using the workflow in Figure 2. The calibrated model predicted tumor growth after inoculation of 5 × 104 CSCs from 4T1 or 4T1‐PDL1KO cells, with predictions broadly consistent with experimental data. However, tumor growth for 4T1‐PDL1KO CSCs appears overestimated (Figure 2E). In this study, the tumor growth model structure features an initial slow‐growth phase, meaning volume differences often emerge later. Our data collection window ended around 26 days post‐inoculation, capturing mainly the early, slow‐growth period. Prior experience shows CSC‐derived tumors can remain dormant before rapid expansion [24], consistent with the need for CSCs to differentiate into proliferative DTCs. And the model parameters are constrained by multiple simultaneous data sets, limiting perfect fit across all data, though the model captures key trends.

The simulations showed that not all CSC properties can contribute to significant change in TNBC growth, tumor immunity landscape and response to ICI treatment, properties such as kngCSC, Rdc, ksecTGFbCSC, ksecVEGFCSC, PD150,NK_CSC, and PD150,CTL_CSC may reflect the key underlying mechanisms, especially the first two characteristics (Figures 3A, 6 and 7). CSCs exhibit aberrant activation of Wnt/β‐catenin, Notch, and Hippo pathways, enhancing their proliferation [25]. A study quantified stemness using a 7‐gene expression signature for clinical TNBC samples [22], in which genes like TOP2A, PAXX, and SDC1 are functionally linked to CSC proliferation and stemness maintenance [26, 27, 28], consistent with our model predictions. Furthermore, analysis of RNA‐seq data from 173 patients in the TCGA database revealed a significant negative correlation between the stemness score and CTL levels (Figure 4D), also aligning with the trend predicted by our model.

Moreover, simulations suggested that CSCs exert a markedly negative impact on ICI efficacy, where CSC‐intrinsic properties play critical roles. A study based on clinical samples of 21 solid tumor types found that stronger stemness was generally associated with lower intratumoral infiltration of immune cells such as CTLs [4]. A scRNA‐seq analysis spanning 17 cancer types also revealed a robust link between cancer stemness and resistance to ICIs [5], both align with our simulation results. A key finding is that CSC frequency did not differ significantly between the simulated hot and cold tumors, despite substantial differences in their immune cell profiles, which indicates that CSC frequency is determined primarily by the tumor cells' intrinsic proliferative and differentiation/de‐differentiation dynamics, not by the immune microenvironment (Figure 5). Our results also showed that stronger TGI was associated with a higher CSC frequency (Figure S12). Consequently, therapies targeting only the immune microenvironment may be inadequate to control CSCs, supporting the rationale for combining ICIs with direct anti‐CSC agents, which remain underexplored. Virtual individual analysis indicates that CSC frequency may also serve as an biomarker to aid in predicting ICI response (Figure 7D), highlighting the need to determine CSCs in clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, CSCs may exhibit distinct phenotypes depending on their spatial location within the tumor [29, 30], which could be addressed in future work using partial differential equations or agent‐based models. Moreover, stromal cells such as cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) can interact with CSCs and promote resistance and immunosuppressive microenvironment [31, 32, 33], which could be integrated into the QSP model. While we have calibrated the CSC‐related parameters in the model using multidimensional data, complete alignment with clinical data remains challenging due to the model's complexity, potential parameter unidentifiability, and the inherent heterogeneity of clinical data, including diverse prior treatments and a lack of standardized CSC measurement. Perhaps subsequent simplifications of the model may reduce parameter unidentifiability. Despite these limitations, the model captures key CSC‐related clinical trends, as shown in Table S6.

Currently, many small‐molecule inhibitors, bispecific antibodies, antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs), and CAR‐Ts targeting CSC‐specific markers are being explored [25, 34]. With the continued accumulation of clinical data on CSCs and the incorporation of RNA‐seq profiles, we believe this model can provide a foundational framework for extrapolating to clinical settings and for designing virtual clinical trials of anti‐CSC drugs, enabling target selection, dosing regimen optimization, and identification of predictive biomarkers.
