Validity and reliability of the Turkish translation of the heart health self-efficacy and self-management scale in patients with chronic heart disease
Authors: Emine Tunc Suygun, Naciye Vardar Yagli, Hakan Suygun, Melda Saglam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background The self-management and self-efficacy skills of people with chronic heart disease affect their health outcomes by determining their lifestyle or health behaviors. It is important to evaluate disease-specific self-efficacy and self-management levels to increase treatment compliance in individuals with chronic heart disease. The aim of the study was to translate the Heart Health Self-Efficacy and Self-Management (HH-SESM) scale into Turkish and test its validity and reliability.Methods A total of 250 patients with chronic heart disease were included. The self-efficacy and self-management components of the HH-SESM scale were translated into Turkish (HH-SESM/T) in compliance with international recommendations. The HH-SESM, Self-Care Management Processes-Guarding (SCMP-G), and General Self-Efficacy (GSE) scales were applied to measure the participants' self-management and self-efficacy levels. Construct, criterion, and content validity; internal consistency; and test–retest reliability were analyzed.ResultsAccording to the confirmatory factor analysis, indexes and factor loadings were sufficient. While there was a high positive relationship between the self-management score and the SCMP-G score (ρ = 0.796,p < .001), a moderate positive relationship was observed between the self-efficacy score and the GSE score (ρ = 0.693, p < .001). The content validity index for both components was sufficient. The Cronbach’s alpha values for self-efficacy and self-management were 0.79, and 0.81, respectively. The intraclass correlation coefficients for self-efficacy and self-management were 0.92, and 0.97, respectively. Test and retest scores were similar for the two components.ConclusionsThe Turkish version of the HH-SESM scale is valid and reliable for the assessment of self-management and self-efficacy in patients with chronic heart disease. The ability of the HH-SESM scale to evaluate both self-efficacy and self-management constructs simultaneously may provide clinicians with a holistic perspective.Trial registrationClinical Trials.gov ID: NCT05939297 Registration Date: 21/06/2023

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) is considered a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide due to its high prevalence. Cardiovascular diseases, especially CHD, are the most common noncommunicable diseases [1, 2]. Among the aims of the United Nations 2030 Sustainable Development Goals is to reduce premature deaths caused by noncommunicable diseases by one third [3].

Individuals’ lifestyles determine or affect the likelihood of contracting many diseases and the progression of their diseases [4, 5]. The importance of lifestyle changes to control risk factors in the prevention, treatment, and management of CHD is emphasized by the American Heart Association and the European Association of Cardiology [6–8]. Unhealthy behaviors such as poor diet, sedentary lifestyle, smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption increase the risk of coronary events and disability-adjusted life years [5].

The self-management and self-efficacy skills of people with CHD affect their health outcomes by determining their lifestyle or health behaviors [9–11]. The concept of self-management in chronic disease management refers to the daily tasks that people must consciously undertake to improve their health outcomes [12]. In addition, physical, psychological, and social limitations caused by CHD negatively affect individuals’ self-efficacy perceptions and reduce their quality of life. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s motivation to take action to successfully conform with recommended health behaviors and is indicative of their personal confidence that they can maintain this [12]. Deterioration in individuals’ self-efficacy perceptions after CHD makes compliance with treatment and disease management difficult [6].

It is important to evaluate disease-specific self-efficacy and self-management levels in order to increase individuals’ compliance with treatment, participation in the disease process, and cooperation with healthcare personnel after CHD [6, 8]. The Heart Health Self-Efficacy and Self-Management (HH-SESM) scale is used to evaluate together the concepts of self-efficacy and self-management, which are prominent in the management of coronary heart disease [12]. The HH-SESM scale, which was originally developed by Mares et al. in two languages (Arabic and English), has subsequently been culturally adapted into Chinese [12, 13]. There are different general self-efficacy and self-management scales whose Turkish versions have previously been studied for validity and reliability. However, these scales are not specific to CHD patients. In addition, they evaluate either self-efficacy or self-management. One such scale, the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE), measures an individual’s overall level of competence in coping with challenges. However, it does not include disease-specific self-efficacy behaviors. This scale may be inadequate in determining the level of self-efficacy in CHD-specific behaviors, such as diet, exercise, and medication compliance, in heart patients [14]. Similarly, the Self-Care Management Scale for Chronic Illnesses-Guarding (SCMP-G) is a general measure that assesses chronic patients’ self-care behaviors (e.g. managing the effects of the disease on daily life). However, it focuses solely on the self-management process and does not assess the patient’s self-efficacy in performing these behaviors [15].

In the literature, it is emphasized that the level of self-efficacy is an important source of motivation in self-management, including self-care [16]. Due to this interaction between self-efficacy and self-management, evaluating both parameters together will strengthen chronic disease management. Using a scale that can evaluate two constructs simultaneously allows the clinician to see at a glance any discrepancies in individuals’ self-confidence (self-efficacy) and health behavior implementation (self-management). In this way, the intervention can be tailored to the individual by assessing self-efficacy, self-management, or both. There is no scale in Turkish that evaluates together and in detail the concepts of self-management and self-efficacy, which are important factors affecting chronic disease management in patients with CHD. The aim of the present study was to adapt the HH-SESM scale into Turkish and investigate its validity and reliability in patients with CHD.

Methods

Translation

Permission to translate the HH-SESM scale into Turkish (HH-SESM/T) was obtained by e-mail from Maria Mares, who developed it. Ethics committee approval for the study was received from Karamanoglu Mehmetbey University Ethics Committee (Approval number: 04–202 L/04). We followed the procedure outlined in the guide for translating and culturally adapting patient-oriented scales by Beaton et al. [17]. Initially, two native Turkish speakers with fluency in English translated the HH-SESM scale into Turkish independently; one of them was informed about the scale, while the other did not. The people who translated the scale into Turkish then convened and merged the two translations. Two sworn translators with good mastery of Turkish, who were native English speakers, re-translated the scale from Turkish into English. One translation was created by combining the two Turkish to English translation scales. A translation committee consisting of one physiotherapist, one nutritionist, one cardiologist, and two sworn translators compared the source and backward versions of the scale in order to adapt it to the Turkish language and evaluate its cultural adaption and then the initial draft of the Turkish translation was created. The final HH-SESM/T scale was submitted to five experts to assess its content validity. Thereafter, to determine the degree of comprehensibility of the scale, a pilot study was conducted with 30 CHD patients. All patients indicated that every of the scale were comprehensible and thus the final version of the Turkish version was created.

Participants

CHD patients between the ages of 18–70 years who admitted to the cardiology clinic of Karaman Training and Research Hospital were included in the study. The inclusion criteria were having CHD for at least 6 months, being native Turkish speakers, age > 18 years, and being willing to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria included not fully completing out the provided scales and having a serious psychiatric disease, such as major depression or panic disorder. The participants provided written informed consent stating to their voluntary participation in the study.

One method of calculating the sample size for scale adaptation studies is to include participants numbering at least 10 times the total number of questions [18, 19]. According to this rule, data collection was initiated with the aim of including at least 120 patients, which is ten times the 12 questions. In order to reduce the possibility of losses during data collection, a higher sample size was targeted. In the beginning, the current study included 257 patients. Seven patients were excluded: five withdrew from the study and two were unable to fill out the scales. The study was completed with 250 patients.

Assessments

The participants’ age, height and weight, body mass index (BMI), sex, level of education, employment position, duration of hypertension illness, and comorbidity were recorded along with their physical and demographic characteristics. To measure the self-management and self-efficacy levels of the participants, the HH-SESM, SCMP-G (self-protection), and GSE scales were applied [14, 15].

HH-SESM

This was developed by Maria Mares et al. [12]. It can evaluate the concepts of self-efficacy and self-management together and consists of 12 items specifically prepared for CHD patients. Since the scale is short, it is easily filled out and is practical to use. The questions concern behaviors regarding physical activity, diet, smoking, alcohol, weight management, medication compliance, and psychosocial factors [12]. Both English and Arabic were used during the development of the scale. The response format for each structure is created differently. Responses for the self-efficacy component range from [1] “not confident” to [4] “very confident,” while those for the self-management component range from [1] “rarely/never” to [4] “always” [12]. The score of the self-efficacy and self-management components is determined by summing the scores obtained from the items. The scores for the answers given for both the self-management and self-efficacy components can be interpreted as follows: ≥36 points indicates high, between 24 and 35 sufficient, and ≤ 24 low self-efficacy and low self-management [12].

SCMP-G

This scale consists of 35 items. The Turkish adaptation study of the scale was conducted by Hançerlioğlu and Aykar [15]. The scale has self-protection and social protection subgroups. The self-protection subdimension comprises items 2, 6, 8, 11, 15, 18, 19, 20, 22, 23, and 25–34 and social protection subdimension items 1, 3–5, 7, 9, 10, 12–14, 16, 17, 21, 24, and 35. It is a 5-point Likert scale with each item scored from 5 (Strongly Agree) and 1 (Strongly Disagree) points. A minimum of 35 and a maximum of 175 points can be obtained from the scale. For the self-protection subdimension, the minimum is 20 and the maximum is 100 points; for the social protection subdimension, the minimum is 15 and the maximum is 75 points. Low scores from the scale indicate that the self-care management power is not good and vice versa [15].

GSE

This scale was developed in Germany by Matthias Jerusalem and Ralf Schwarzer in 1981. The Turkish adaptation study of the scale was conducted by Aypay [14]. When the scale was originally designed, it had 20 items. However, in 1981, it had revisions and there were only 10 items left. The GSE scale is scored using a 4-point Likert-type system. The scale has been translated into many languages and validity and reliability studies have been conducted [20]. The scale tests the belief of different circles in their ability to cope with new and difficult tasks. It has two subdimensions: “Effort and resistance” and “Ability and confidence”. The score on the GSE scale ranges from 4 to 40. High scores for items indicate high levels of general self-efficacy [14].

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using normality graphs and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Depending on the distribution, continuous variables in the physical and demographic data are given as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (minimum-maximum). Categorical variables, such as sex, are expressed in numbers (%). The comparisons between two independent groups in terms of scale scores were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Since HH-SESM/T scale consists of two components, self-efficacy and self-management, validity and reliability analyses were conducted separately for the two components.

In our study, the validity of the HH-SESM/T was tested via construct, criterion, and content validity analyses. In the construct validity analysis, whether the items were sufficient to explain the original structure of the scale was established using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The scale items’ residual correlation and modification indices were analyzed, and the parameters that needed to be introduced to improve the model’s fit were identified. New models were created by adding the highest modification index parameters for the loaded items of the same dimension one at a time without changing the scale’s original structure. Consequently, χ2 and degree of freedom (df), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), comparative fit index (CFI), goodness of fit index (GFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) were used as recommended by Kline [21] to assess model fit. χ2/df < 3, CFI ≥ 0.90, TLI ≥ 0.90, GFI ≥ 0.90, and at most RMSEA < 0.08 were used as criteria for good (or at least acceptable) fit [22]. Using nonparametric bootstrap with 1000 replications, the consistency of the fit indices was examined. The bootstrap results were shown with 95% confidence intervals (CI). In the criterion validity analysis, the relationships between the self-management and self-efficacy scores and the SCMP-G and GSE scores, respectively, were evaluated with the Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ). For content validity, the HH-SESM/T scale was assessed by five experts (one nutrition and diet specialist, two physiotherapists, and two cardiologists). In calculating the content validity index (CVI) of the scale, the 12 items were scored by the experts as “not appropriate = 1”, “a little appropriate (items/expressions should be required to be put in an appropriate form) = 2”, “quite appropriate = 3”, or “very appropriate = 4” [23]. Recommended acceptable CVI values are Item CVI > 0.78 and Scale CVI > 0.80 [24].

The reliability of the HH-SESM/T scale was examined via item analyses, calculation of Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, and test–retest reliability analysis. For each component, the internal consistency reliability was computed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. A coefficient ≥ 0.80 indicates high reliability, between 0.60 and 0.79 medium reliability, and < 0.60 low reliability [25]. Test–retest reliability was calculated using two-way ANOVA for the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and 95% confidence intervals of the ICC.

CFA was performed using IBM SPSS Amos software. 95% confidence intervals based on bootstrap for fit indices were obtained using the R package lavaan [26]. IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 was used for the other statistical analyses and calculations. The statistical significance level was set at p <.05.

To improve model fit, modification indices were examined and suggested interitem relationships were added to the model. When the fit measurements obtained from the final model in the CFA of the self-efficacy component were examined, it was seen that the CFI, TLI, and GFI values were greater than 0.90 and the RMSEA was lower than 0.08. According to these findings, the fit measurements of the components are acceptable (Table 2).

Content validity

In both components, the CVI of one item (item 12) was 0.80, while the CVI of the other items (items 0–11) was 1.00. The CVI of the components was 0.98 and this value was sufficient since it was higher than 0.80.

The median self-efficacy and self-management scores were 44 (min–max: 41–46) and 43 (min–max: 38–45) for the participants exercising and 38 (min–max: 35–41.50) and 36 (min–max: 33–40) for the participants who did not exercise, respectively. The individuals who did exercise had significantly higher both scores than the non-exercising group (p <.001 for both comparisons).

Discussion

In order for a scale to be used in another culture with a language different from the one it was written in, translation and adaptation are required. In the present study, by following international methods recommended in scale adaptation studies, first the language and content validity of the scale was ensured and then psychometric evaluations were made. As a result of the evaluations, it was determined that the HH-SESM/T scale is a valid and reliable scale for Turks with CHD.

The HH-SESM scale was developed in English. In addition, during the development phase, the scale was simultaneously translated into Arabic, as it is the second most widely spoken language in the work environment after English. More recently, a cultural adaptation and validation study of the scale has also been conducted in Chinese [13].

In our study, the validity of the HH-SESM/T scale was examined via construct, criterion, and content validity analyses.

In the present study, the unidimensional structures of the components of the scale were evaluated with CFA. All fit indices except the RMSEA value of the competency component (borderline low) were at an acceptable level, indicating that internal construct validity was achieved. Since CFA was not performed in the original study and there were no other studies on adaptation of the scale, we were unable to compare our findings. Upon examining the parameter estimates derived from the model, it was observed that all of the items in the competence component and all of the management component’s factor loadings were significant, with the exception of item 11, which deals with people’s ideal weight status. This may be due to the fact that even if the respondents have high self-management scores, they experience overweight problems, as obesity is common in the Turkish population. Nevertheless, this item was used because excess weight is considered a modifiable risk factor in people with CHD, its removal did not cause a significant increase in the total Cronbach’s alpha value (0.79 vs. 0.79) and to ensure that the original structure of the scale was retained. The findings of our study supported the construct validity of the HH-SESM/T scale and showed that it is valid for use in Turkey. Neither the original validation study nor the Chinese adaptation included CFA [13].

Furthermore, the criterion validity of the scale was assessed in our study by examining the relationships between the GSE and SCMP-G scores and the self-management and self-efficacy scores. Accordingly, while the self-management score and the SCMP-G score showed a high positive correlation, the self-efficacy score and the GSE score showed a moderate positive correlation. These relationships show that the scale has criterion validity. Furthermore compared to the non-exercising participants in our study, the exercisers had higher self-management and self-efficacy scores. This may be due to the fact that participants who exercise are more conscious of their health behaviors.

The adaptation of the HH-SESM scale into Turkish was carried out with careful attention to conceptual and cultural relevance. While self-efficacy and self-management are universally recognized constructs, their expression and perceived meaning may be shaped by sociocultural factors. In Turkey, for instance, a physician-centered approach to care remains common, which may influence how patients view autonomy in health-related behaviors [27]. Additionally, cultural tendencies such as fatalistic health beliefs and varying levels of health literacy may influence individuals’ health behavior attitudes [28]. These considerations were addressed during the expert evaluation phase to ensure that each item was both culturally appropriate and conceptually equivalent. CVI results supported the appropriateness of all items, similar to the findings reported in the Chinese adaptation study [13]. Still, future research may be valuable in exploring how these sociocultural characteristics influence the use of self-report tools across different segments of the Turkish population.

In the reliability analysis of the original scale, Cronbach’s alpha values for internal consistency were 0.81 for self-management and 0.83 for self-efficacy [12]. In the Chinese adaptation study, Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.72 for self-management and 0.77 for self-efficacy [13]. In our study, internal consistency, item, and time-invariance analyses were included in the reliability analysis.

The self-management and self-efficacy components both have good internal consistency, which is similar to the findings of the original HH-SESM scale. In our study, item–total score correlations for both the self-management and self-efficacy components were within a sufficient range and generally similar to those of the original scale [12, 25]. The ICCs we obtained showed that the scale’s time-invariance characteristic was excellent. Since ICCs were not calculated in the original study, we were unable to make a comparison [12].

Limitations

The research was carried out in a hospital in Karaman Training and Research Hospital. For more robust evidence, it is strongly recommended that the scale be administered in multiple centers and with a larger sample. Another limitation of this study is the study’s sample had a notable gender imbalance, with 71.6% male participants, aligning with epidemiological trends that coronary heart disease is more common in men [29]. Since the primary aim of the study was to examine the validity and reliability of the scale, subgroup analyses based on gender were not conducted. Nonetheless, given evidence that health self-management behaviors can vary by gender, future research with a more balanced gender representation should explore gender-specific dynamics and further evaluate the scale’s performance across different subgroups.

Conclusion

In this study, the HH-SESM/T scale was found to be valid and reliable in patients with CHD. It can be widely used to determine the self-management and self-efficacy levels of CHD patients regarding their health behaviors. It can also be used as a pretest and posttest to evaluate the results of behavior change interventions to include individuals in disease management. In addition, scales that can separately assess self-efficacy and self-management in the cardiac population often focus separately on behaviors such as physical activity, smoking cessation, or weight management. This limits their ability to holistically measure various lifestyle behaviors recommended for the management of cardiovascular patients and reducing cardiac risk. The HH-SESM scale eliminates this limitation and facilitates holistic evaluation.
