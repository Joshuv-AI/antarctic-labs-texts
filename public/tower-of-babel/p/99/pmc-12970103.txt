Managing pleural infections: a nationwide survey of respiratory physicians in Singapore
Authors: Ken Junyang Goh, Glenn Khai Wern Yong, Dipika Agrawal, Jonathan Jia Jun Wong, Clare Fong, Si Ling Young, Brian Lee Wei Chua, Qiao Li Tan, Wei Shyuan Siow, Kiran Sharma, Imran Bin Mohamed Noor, Wui Mei Chew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPleural infection remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality, with a significant healthcare burden. Despite growing research and guideline recommendations, real-world management continues to vary widely.MethodsAn anonymous cross-sectional survey evaluating current practice was conducted among respiratory physicians across all public hospitals in Singapore. The survey examined key aspects of management, including pleural fluid diagnostics, chest drain management, surgical referral and intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy (IPFT).Results95 respondents (58% response rate) completed the survey. Only 36.8% routinely inoculate pleural fluid into both plain specimen bottles and blood culture bottles, and up to 22% used plain tubes for pH analysis. Opinions on pH-guided chest drain insertion were divided: 50.6% would always consider pH, while 43.1% did not find it useful. IPFT was regarded as first line treatment following failure of standard care (antibiotics and chest drainage) by 63.2%, while 30.5% would only administer IPFT when surgery was deemed unsuitable. Most respondents (81.0%) had used a reduced dose of tissue plasminogen activator, with 12.6% doing so for all patients. The most common reduced dose was 5 mg (73.7%), largely driven by perceived reduction in bleeding risk (64.2%). Once-daily IPFT dosing was reported by 35.8%, primarily due to logistical constraints and safety concerns related to after-hours administration. Management practices, including pleural fluid sampling for microbiology and pH, indication and timing of surgery or IPFT, and IPFT dose adjustment, varied substantially between hospitals and between individual physicians within the same hospital, with no clear association with respondent seniority. Overall key challenges cited included variation in practice (48.4%), limited after-office hours expertise (44.2%) and limited surgical support (37.9%).ConclusionsSignificant variation exists in pleural infection management across Singapore. Strengthening multidisciplinary collaboration, establishing dedicated pleural services and high-quality trials are needed to optimise care pathways.Cite Now

Introduction

Pleural infection remains a significant clinical challenge, associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Over 80 000 new cases are diagnosed annually in the UK and USA,1 with rising incidence across all age groups, particularly among the elderly.2 3 Despite diagnostic and therapeutic advances, mortality rates remain high at 10–20%, with 12-month mortality approaching 35%.46 The healthcare burden is also considerable; in the USA, costs exceeded US$1 billion in 2016, with an average hospitalisation cost of US$38 500 and an average stay of 14 days.7

While developments such as intrapleural tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and deoxyribonucleic (DNase) therapy have improved outcomes, overall prognosis remains suboptimal. Furthermore, significant heterogeneity in clinical practice persists worldwide,8 despite the availability of multiple guidelines.911 In Singapore, there are no existing national guidelines or consensus statements to advise on the management of pleural disease. This study aimed to evaluate real-world management of pleural infections by respiratory physicians in Singapore, to identify areas of variation, uncertainty and potential areas for clinical standardisation and future research.

Methods

An anonymous cross-sectional survey was conducted between July and August 2025 among respiratory physicians and trainees across all public hospitals in Singapore. The 40-item online questionnaire, developed by study team respiratory physicians with expertise in pleural disease, covered pleural fluid diagnostics, chest drain management, antibiotics use, surgical treatment and intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy (IPFT).

Eligible participants were respiratory consultants and trainees (senior residents/registrars) from nine public hospitals. Two remaining public hospitals (women’s/children’s and psychiatric hospitals) were excluded. Invitations and reminders were emailed to 164 physicians. The study protocol was submitted to the institutional review board (SingHealth Centralised IRB 2025–0418). Documented informed consent was waived and the study was exempted from formal review, as it was an anonymous survey with minimal risk. Responses were analysed descriptively using SPSS (V.29, IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Results of the survey are provided in figures 1–4 and in the supplementary material (online supplemental figures S1–S16). Survey results stratified by seniority (senior resident, consultant and senior consultant) and the nine hospital sites (A–I) are illustrated in the supplementary material (online supplemental figures S16–S33, respectively).

Survey questions exploring pleural fluid diagnostics.

95 respondents (response rate 58.0%) completed the survey, 60 (63.2%) consultants and 35 (36.8%) trainees, representing all nine hospitals (online supplemental figure S1). Approximately half (52.6%) had ≥5 years of specialist experience, and most (91.6%) managed ≥five pleural infection cases annually (online supplemental figure S2). Most respondents (75.8%) reported having a pleural lead or pleural team in their institution, though only 22.1% and 24.8% ‘always’ and ‘often’ referred cases to these services, respectively (online supplemental figure S3).

Pleural fluid diagnostics

Figure 1 shows the responses for questions directed at pleural fluid diagnostics and analysis. For pleural fluid gram stain and culture, 12 (12.6%) respondents used plain specimen bottles only, 48 (50.5%) inoculated pleural fluid into blood culture bottles, and 35 (36.8%) used both. Most respondents (76.8%) would collect pleural fluid in heparinised blood gas syringes for pH measurement, while 21 (22.1%) used plain bottles. Most (51.6%) reported pleural fluid pH analysis with blood gas analysers in their institution, 12.6% with a laboratory pH metre and 32.6% were unsure of their institutional methods. About half (49.5%) always considered pleural fluid pH in chest drain insertion decisions. In contrast, 38.9% often did not rely on pleural fluid pH and 4.2% did not consider it useful, preferring other clinical or imaging criteria.

There were no clear differences in these practices when the results were stratified by seniority of respondents (online supplemental figures S16–S18). However, when stratified by hospital sites (online supplemental figures S24–S27), there were significant differences in the collection of pleural fluid for bacterial cultures (61.9% inoculated in both plain specimen and blood culture bottles in site G, vs 6.2% in site H and 0.0% in site D) and pH measurement (100.0% collection of pleural fluid in a heparinised blood gas syringe in sites A, D and H, compared with 30.0% and 0.0% in sites F and C, respectively). Significant heterogeneity in practice also appeared to exist within some hospitals—the proportion of respondents who preferred collection of pleural fluid in blood culture bottles, plain specimen bottles or both was almost evenly distributed in hospital sites E and F (online supplemental figure S24).

Management of chest drains

Most (85.3%) respondents preferred 12–14 French drains for pleural infections, which were usually inserted bedside (72.6%), and less commonly in a dedicated procedural room or ward space (27.4%) (online supplemental figure S4). There were clear differences in practice between hospitals, where almost all respondents in hospital sites G and I reported performing chest drain insertions in a dedicated procedural room, compared with none of the respondents from all other hospital sites (online supplemental figure S33). After-hours chest drain insertions were mostly performed by respiratory registrar/senior residents (40.0%), followed by junior residents or medical officers under senior supervision (12.6%) (online supplemental figure S5).

Views on small-volume saline flushes for chest drains varied: 28.5% supported flushing for small bore drains (≤10 French), 20.0% when draining pus and 11.6% for septated or loculated effusions (online supplemental figure S6). 20 (21.1%) respondents advocated routine flushing for all patients, while 34.7% reported no evidence to support the practice. External suction was used when drainage output was poor (43.2%) or for loculated effusions (11.6%), but nearly half (47.4%) felt there was no supporting evidence for suction (online supplemental figure S7).

Antibiotic therapy

Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid was the most common empirical choice for community-acquired pleural infection (92.6%) (online supplemental figure S8). Infectious disease consultation was ‘rarely’ and ‘never”’ sought by 50.5% and 18.9% of respondents, respectively (online supplemental figure S9). Antibiotic therapy was typically 4 weeks (57.9%) or 6 weeks (31.6%) (online supplemental figure S9).

Role of surgery and IPFT

When considering surgical treatment (for patients fit for surgery), most respondents (58.9%) would refer for surgery after failure of standard care and IPFT; 15.8% favoured early routine referral, while 29.5% would refer all patients with septated or loculated effusions (figure 2). Most respondents (63.2%) considered IPFT as first-line treatment when standard care failed and 30.5% regarded it as a second-line treatment—to be used only when surgery was unsuitable or unavailable (figure 2). Only 6.3% supported IPFT as routine first-line treatment for all patients. Almost all respondents (98.9%) preferred tPA/DNase for IPFT (online supplemental figure S10). Regarding the timing of escalation for surgery or IPFT when standard care had failed, 6.3% would intervene within 12 hours, 27.4% within 12–24 hours, 36.8% within 24–48 hours and 17.9% within 48–72 hours (figure 2). Most respondents (54.7%) reported ‘rarely or almost never’ using prognostic tools such as the RAPID (renal, age, purulence, infection source and dietary factors) score, followed by ‘sometimes’ (28.4%), ‘frequently’ (8.4%) and ‘almost always’ (2.1%); 6.3% were unfamiliar with these tools (online supplemental figure S11).

There were no clear differences in the perceptions of the role of IPFT (online supplemental figure S19) and the timing of IPFT or surgical referral (online supplemental figure S20) when survey results were stratified based on the seniority of respondents. However, a larger proportion of trainees (40.0%) reported frequently or almost always using validated scoring systems (eg, RAPID score), compared with consultants (2.8%) and senior consultants (3.1%) (online supplemental figure S21). When stratified by hospital sites, all respondents from site B and most from sites F, G and H (80.0%, 90.5% and 81.3%, respectively) reported employing IPFT as a first line treatment option, compared with sites D, E and I (22.2%, 30.0% and 16.7%, respectively) where most respondents viewed IPFT as a second line treatment option only when surgery is not suitable or unavailable (online supplemental figure S28). On-site thoracic surgery services are available at hospital sites D, F and G. A larger proportion of respondents from hospital sites B, F, G and I (100.0%, 80.0%, 83.3% and 100.0%, respectively) would initiate IPFT or refer for surgical intervention within 48 hours of chest drain insertion, compared with sites D and H (40.0% and 56.2%) (online supplemental figure S29).

Dose attenuation of tPA/DNase

Reduced tPA dosing was common. 38 (40.0%) respondents prescribed attenuated doses for some (<50%) patients, 11 (11.6%) for the majority (50–79%), 16 (16.8%) for most (≥80%) and 12 (12.6%) for all patients, while 15 (15.8%) consistently prescribed a 10 mg dose (figure 3). The most common reduced dose used was 5 mg (73.7%), followed by 2.5 mg (11.6%) (figure 3). The primary reasons cited for attenuating tPA dosing were to reduce bleeding risk (64.2%) and evidence supporting equivalent efficacy at lower doses (46.3%) (figure 3). Most respondents would prescribe a reduced tPA dose for patients with ongoing anticoagulation therapy (68.4%), patients with uraemia or advanced chronic kidney disease (67.4%) and patients with cirrhosis or acute liver failure (56.8%); almost half would prescribe a reduced dose for patients with ongoing antiplatelet therapy and patients >65 years of age (49.5% and 41.1%, respectively) (online supplemental figure S12). For patients with risk factors for bleeding and required IPFT, approximately two thirds (66.3%) of respondents used attenuated dosing of tPA, and 21.1% combined this with once daily administration (online supplemental figure S13).

Regarding the frequency of prescribing a reduced tPA dose, there were no significant differences in responses when the survey results were stratified by seniority of respondents (online supplemental figure S22). However, a larger proportion of respondents from hospital sites C, H and I (100.0%, 93.8% and 83.3%, respectively) reported using a reduced dose of tPA for the majority of patients who required IPFT, compared with hospital sites B, D, E, F and G (6.7%, 33.3%, 22.2%, 20.0% and 28.6%, respectively) (online supplemental figure S31).

Dosing frequency of tPA/DNase

Regarding dosing frequency, 49.5% of respondents generally administered tPA/DNase two times a day, 35.8% once daily and 12.6% once daily in patients with risk factors for bleeding (figure 4). One respondent used a two times a day dose on weekdays and once daily dose on weekends. When asked about reasons for not administering a two times a day dose, the most common responses were logistical and safety issues due to limited after-hours supervision (44.2%), and that a once daily dosing is adequate for most patients (17.9%) (figure 4). Only one respondent cited prohibitive financial cost as a reason. There were no clear differences in responses when stratified by seniority of respondents (online supplemental figure S23). However, a much larger proportion of respondents from hospital sites B and F (93.3% and 90.0%) reported a preference for a once daily frequency, compared with none (0.0%) of the respondents from hospital sites A, C, D, E, H and I (online supplemental figure S32).

Administration of tPA/DNase

During office hours, tPA/DNase was most commonly administered by junior residents or medical officers familiar with IPFT (55.8%), followed by respiratory registrars or senior residents (18.9%) and junior doctor under direct senior supervision (16.8%) (online supplemental figure S14). Regarding after-hours IPFT, 47.4% reported that administration of tPA/DNase was performed by junior residents or medical officers familiar with IPFT, while 27.4% reported that after-hours IPFT was not recommended or practised in their institution (figure 4). Most (82.1%) respondents would administer concurrent tPA/DNase, while 16.8% used a sequential approach (online supplemental figure S14).

Challenges in the management of pleural infection

The main challenges identified in pleural infection management were significant variation in clinical management (48.4%), inadequate expertise or limited after-office hours support (44.2%), limited access to surgical support (37.9%) and poor microbiological yield to guide treatment (26.3%) (online supplemental figure S15).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive insight into real-world management of pleural infection in Singapore. Respondents were well distributed across experience, seniority and hospitals, offering a representative overview of national practice. Our findings demonstrate considerable variation across multiple domains of care and highlight key areas for standardisation and further research.

A consistent theme was significant variability in pleural fluid diagnostics. The microbiological yield of pleural fluid and blood cultures in pleural infection remains low (30–40%).12 13 However, approximately two thirds of respondents sent pleural fluid in plain bottles or blood culture bottles alone, despite evidence that inoculation in blood culture bottles (in addition to standard culture) improves yield by approximately 20%.9 14 Similarly, many were uncertain how pleural fluid pH was analysed, with some institutions appearing to rely on pH metres rather than blood gas analysers. Collection of pleural fluid in plain tubes further compromises accuracy due to air exposure.15 Close collaboration between clinical and laboratory services is therefore essential to standardise pleural fluid collection (blood gas syringes, blood culture bottles) and analysis (blood gas analysers). Our results also illustrate that hospital-specific practices, rather than seniority (or experience) of the managing physician, appeared to explain more of the variation in management, particularly with regard to how pleural fluid was collected for microbiological or pH analysis. Nevertheless, significant heterogeneity also existed within some hospital sites—suggesting that these variations in practice are influenced by both hospital and individual physician preferences.

Despite guideline recommendations,9 the use of pleural fluid pH to guide chest drain decisions was highly variable: similar proportions reported using it routinely versus considering it unhelpful. While pleural fluid pH remains the best predictor for chest drainage,16 its utility may be limited by measurement inaccuracy, intrapleural variability across different locules17 and the lack of point-of-care pH testing. Nevertheless, in such situations, pleural fluid glucose (<3.3 mmol/L) is a useful surrogate biomarker.9 Novel biomarkers related to fibrinolysis, including soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor and plasminogen activator inhibitor, are inherently related to pathophysiological mechanisms and show promise for prognostication but have yet to make their way into clinical practice.18 19 Further research is needed to enhance diagnostic and prognostic precision.

Practice was more consistent regarding chest drain size with most respondents preferring small bore chest drains (≤14 French), supported by Multicenter Intrapleural Sepsis Trials (MIST-1) data showing equivalent efficacy and less pain compared with large-bore drains.20 21 However, other aspects of chest drain management varied widely. In MIST-1, drain patency was maintained with 30 mL saline flushes three times a day.21 Retrospective data also suggest that chest drain blockage can occur in up to 9% with small bore chest drains (≤12 French), and regular small-volume (20 mL every 6 hours) flushes may reduce blockage rates.22 Nonetheless, in our study, only one-third of respondents supported routine flushing, while another third believed that evidence to support this practice was insufficient. Current British Thoracic Society guidelines provide general chest drain management advice but do not specifically recommend saline flushing,9 and concerns remain about potential drain dislodgement or infection with frequent manipulation—which may explain this wide variation in practice. It is also important to distinguish between low-volume saline flushing (to maintain patency) and high-volume irrigation (250 mL three times a day) used as a therapeutic alternative to IPFT.23 Evidence and consensus for external suction is unclear; outside thoracic surgery, its main indication is managing large air leaks with inadequate flow through existing chest drains. Despite this, almost half of the respondents reported considering suction when drainage output was low. In our view, the priorities in chest drain management should be (1) maintaining drain patency and (2) prompt assessment for IPFT or surgery when drainage and clinical response are inadequate.

Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid was the most common empirical choice for community acquired pleural infections, reflecting local microbiological prevalence and resistance patterns.24 Broad spectrum coverage remains appropriate, with up to 80% of pleural infections being polymicrobial in nature.25 Reported antibiotic durations (4–6 weeks) align with current recommendations, although the optimal duration should ultimately be guided by clinical response.9 26 Most physicians managed these cases independently, rarely consulting infectious disease specialists, suggesting confidence in respiratory-led management.

While tPA/DNase was the preferred intrapleural therapy, considerable variation was observed in its relationship to the indication and timing of surgery, reflecting ongoing clinical equipoise and differing institutional norms.10 27 Most respondents favoured IPFT as first line treatment for inadequate drainage and failure of standard care, yet nearly a third preferred surgery as the initial option, and a similar proportion would refer all patients with septated or loculated effusions for surgery. No randomised trials have yet defined optimal timing or patient selection for surgery. Rather, it is generally accepted that patients with organised effusions or significant pleural thickening are unlikely to achieve full recovery and lung re-expansion without surgery, and delayed intervention is associated with poorer outcomes and higher thoracotomy conversion rates.28 29 The key challenge, therefore, lies in identifying patients who would benefit from early surgery, acknowledging that pleural infection may progress without a distinct transition into the organised phase (stage 3) of empyema. Conversely, an argument against routine surgical referral is that many patients do improve with usual care—reflected in the recent MIST-3 feasibility randomised controlled trial where half of the patients randomised to surgery did not receive the allocated treatment, mostly due to clinical improvement.30 The ongoing MIST-4 trial comparing early video assisted thoracoscopy surgery with IPFT will shed more light on the comparative benefits of each strategy and may reduce uncertainty in decision making.

Interestingly, the indication and timing of surgery and/or IPFT appeared to vary significantly between hospital sites, where surgery as a first line treatment option and the timing for surgery appeared to be favoured in some hospitals, and the timing for surgery and/or IPFT (>48 hours after chest drain insertion) was preferred in others. Of note, these differences did not appear to correlate with the availability of on-site thoracic surgery services. Like pleural fluid diagnostics and analysis, significant heterogeneity in individual practice within most individual hospital sites was still present. The RAPID score is a simple and readily applicable prognostication tool that has been well validated for predicting mortality in pleural infection.31 32 However, its role in routine clinical care—particularly in guiding treatment decisions such as surgical referral—remains undefined. This perhaps contributes to the overall limited adoption of the score as reflected in our survey. The ongoing RAPTOR-f (RAPID driven treatment of pleural infection) feasibility randomised trial, which compares RAPID-guided treatment versus standard care, should provide useful insights. Regardless, early escalation—within 48 hours—to IPFT or surgery or non-responders remains critical, as delays in both modalities are associated with worse outcomes.33

A contentious area of IPFT is the optimal effective dose of tPA. Our study found that attenuated or reduced dosing is common practice, with nearly half of the respondents prescribing ≤5 mg routinely, mostly to mitigate perceived bleeding risk. This practice aligns with findings from an international survey, where 73% of respondents reported prescribing a 5 mg tPA dose.8 Considerable variability is also evident within Singapore, with two cohort studies reporting that 11.1% and 90.5% of patients received reduced starting doses of tPA.24 33 In the latter study, higher starting doses were more commonly prescribed to patients with fewer comorbidities and a lower prevalence of chronic kidney disease or anaemia—suggesting that physicians individualise dosing based on perceived bleeding risk.33 Similar to the timing of surgery or IPFT, while the use of reduced tPA dosing appears to be influenced by hospital practice (rather than seniority of the managing physician), significant variation in individual practice (plausibly due to variation in perceived bleeding risk) was still seen within most individual hospitals. However, evidence that dose reduction decreases bleeding is lacking. A large retrospective multicentre study of 1833 patients reported clinically significant pleural bleeding in 4.1% of patients, with no difference in bleeding rates between patients receiving a standard (10 mg) dose and reduced doses.34 Nonetheless, the practice of dose reduction has been encouraged by smaller prospective studies reporting treatment success rates of up to 90% with starting doses of 5 mg or even 2.5 mg.35 36 Future randomised studies will be needed to establish whether reduced dosing is truly non-inferior in efficacy, or safer in terms of bleeding risk. Determining optimal dosing across diverse populations will also be essential in future studies, particularly with growing evidence that East Asian populations have higher risks of major bleeding events and rates of bleeding complications from anti-thrombotic therapy compared with Caucasians—a phenomenon commonly referred to as the ‘East Asian paradox’.37 38 The mechanisms for this phenomenon are not well understood, but are likely to be complex and multifactorial, including differences in demographics (lower body weight) and genetic factors influencing drug metabolism.37 38

With regard to the frequency of IPFT dosing, 36% of respondents use once-daily regimes, and another 13% adopt this approach in the presence of bleeding risk factors. In an international survey on the use of IPFT for pleural infections by Lau et al, 18% of respondents reported using a once-daily dosing for IPFT.8 The increasing use of a once-daily regime is perhaps encouraged by relatively high treatment success rates exceeding 90% in retrospective studies, although with variations in dose and concerns with selection bias.39 40 Another contributing factor is practical and logistical inconvenience with two times a day dosing—most respondents reported that after-hours administration was discouraged or unsupervised, and the most commonly cited reason for once-daily dosing was logistical and safety concerns after-hours. While concurrent dosing may reduce manpower demands, these findings underscore the importance of structured training for IPFT administration and safe handling of chest drains. Alternative strategies include two times a day dosing during office hours (eg, 9:00 and 416:00) to maintain efficacy while minimising after-hours procedures. Indeed, this also underscores the need for future research to shed light on optimal dosing and personalised dose adjustments in pleural infection.

The key challenges identified—significant variation in practice, limited after-hours expertise and logistical barriers to IPFT—highlight the need for further research to inform practice, greater multidisciplinary collaboration and capacity building. Several limitations should be acknowledged. Private sector physicians were not surveyed, although most of the healthcare in Singapore is delivered through public institutions. Survey data may not fully reflect clinical practice due to recall or response bias, and respondents may have had greater interest or expertise in pleural disease. There was also a limited number of respondents (<5) in two hospital sites and therefore may not accurately represent institution practice and physician preferences in those hospitals. This survey also did not explore the role of thoracentesis or medical thoracoscopy, which may be relevant options for selected patients.41 42

Conclusion

Despite advances in pleural infection management, clinical practice in key aspects of management remains highly variable in Singapore. Significant variation in these practices was observed not just across hospitals, but also between individual practice in the same hospital. These observations are perhaps explained by evolving evidence, differing resource availability and variable interpretation of best practice between hospitals and individual physicians. As such, there remains a pressing need for strengthening multidisciplinary collaboration, conducting high-quality trials and developing dedicated pleural services, which are essential next steps towards well-informed and evidence-based management that can improve patient outcomes. This study also highlights the need for the development of national guidelines and consensus regarding pleural disease management in Singapore.
