Rotational stability of two toric intraocular lenses with different design: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Jair Maldonado Aparicio, Gerardo Rafael Ricárdez González, Melissa Janeth Reynoso Muñoz, Daniela Pulido London, Leire Irusteta Jiménez, Azyadeh Camacho Ordonez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRotational stability is a critical determinant of the refractive performance of toric intraocular lenses (IOLs), as even small degrees of postoperative rotation may compromise astigmatic correction. This study aimed to compare the rotational stability of two toric IOLs with different designs.MethodsA retrospective comparative study was conducted including eyes that underwent cataract surgery with implantation of either a monofocal toric Ankoris IOL or a TECNIS ZCT toric IOL. Postoperative IOL rotation was evaluated at 1 day, 1 week, and 1 month after surgery. Rotational stability was assessed using the absolute value of rotation, regardless of direction. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of eyes with clinically significant rotation (> 10°) and the need for surgical repositioning. Statistical analyses were performed according to data distribution.ResultsA total of 61 eyes were included, with 31 eyes in the Ankoris group and 30 eyes in the TECNIS ZCT group. Background

Cataract surgery is one of the most frequently performed surgical procedures worldwide and has evolved from a vision-restoring intervention into a refractive procedure aimed at achieving high levels of postoperative visual quality and spectacle independence [1–3]. In this context, residual refractive error—particularly corneal astigmatism—remains a relevant limiting factor for optimal uncorrected visual acuity following surgery.

Corneal astigmatism greater than 1.00 diopter is present in approximately 30–39% of patients undergoing cataract surgery, while higher degrees (> 3.00 D) occur in a smaller but clinically significant proportion [4]. Uncorrected astigmatism has been associated with reduced uncorrected visual acuity, increased dependence on spectacles, and decreased visual quality. Several surgical strategies have been developed to address corneal astigmatism at the time of cataract surgery, including corneal incisions on the steep axis, limbal relaxing incisions, peripheral corneal incisions, and toric intraocular lenses (IOLs), with toric IOL implantation currently representing one of the most predictable and effective approaches [4, 5].

The refractive success of toric IOL implantation is highly dependent on postoperative rotational stability. It is estimated that for each degree of IOL rotation away from the intended axis, approximately 3.3% of the astigmatic corrective effect is lost, and rotations greater than 10° may result in a reduction of nearly one-third of the intended correction [6, 7]. Clinically significant rotation has been reported in 2.8–6.7% of cases, occasionally requiring secondary surgical repositioning [4, 8]. Consequently, even modest differences in rotational behavior between toric IOL designs may have meaningful clinical implications, particularly in eyes with moderate to high astigmatism.

Several factors have been proposed to influence rotational stability, including capsular bag anatomy, surgical technique, and IOL material and design characteristics. Previous studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated variability in rotational outcomes among different toric IOL platforms, suggesting that lens design may play a relevant role in postoperative stability.⁹–¹¹ However, comparative data on the rotational performance of newer toric IOL designs remain limited, particularly in real-world clinical settings and specific populations.

The Ankoris monofocal toric IOL is manufactured on a hydrophobic acrylic platform with a plate-haptic design, featuring a 6.0-mm optic and an overall diameter of 11.40 mm. It incorporates ultraviolet (UV) and blue light filters, with a refractive index of 1.46, an Abbe number of 58, and an A-constant of 118.95. The available power range includes spherical powers from + 6.00 to + 30.00 diopters and cylindrical powers from 1.50 to 6.00 diopters. Implantation requires a Medicel Accuject injector system and an incision size greater than 2.0 mm. In comparison, the TECNIS ZCT toric IOL is also composed of a hydrophobic acrylic material but features a C-loop haptic design, with a 6.0-mm optic and an overall diameter of 13.00 mm. It includes a UV filter, with a refractive index of 1.47, an Abbe number of 55, and an A-constant of 118.8. The available power range spans from + 5.00 to + 34.00 diopters for spherical power and from 1.00 to 8.00 diopters for cylindrical correction. This lens is compatible with the TECNIS Simplicity delivery system and Platinum series injectors [9–10] (Fig. 1).

Design and characteristics of the Ankoris and TECNIS ZCT IOLs. Ankoris (left): hydrophobic acrylic, plate-haptic; TECNIS ZCT (right): hydrophobic acrylic, C-loop; both differ in optical properties and power ranges

The purpose of this study was to compare the postoperative rotational stability of two monofocal toric IOLs with different designs—Ankoris and TECNIS ZCT—using a retrospective comparative approach. Rotational behavior was evaluated at multiple postoperative time points during the first month following surgery, with the aim of determining whether differences in absolute rotation and variability could be observed between both toric IOL models.

Despite the growing body of evidence on toric IOL rotational stability, comparative data involving newer or less extensively studied lens designs remain limited, particularly in real-world clinical settings. In addition, many studies focus on single postoperative time points or report average rotational values without addressing variability or directional behavior. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to perform a direct, single-center comparison between two toric IOLs with different designs—Ankoris and TECNIS ZCT—evaluating not only absolute rotation but also its temporal evolution, variability, and direction during the early postoperative period.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective, observational, comparative study was conducted at a tertiary referral ophthalmology center. Medical records of patients who underwent cataract surgery with implantation of a monofocal toric IOL between March 2025 and June 2025 were reviewed. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Participants

Eligible participants included patients aged 18 to 80 years who underwent uncomplicated phacoemulsification with implantation of either a monofocal toric Ankoris IOL or a TECNIS ZCT toric IOL and had complete postoperative follow-up data. Only one eye per patient was included in the analysis.

Exclusion criteria comprised intraoperative complications, significant ocular trauma, zonular instability, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, collagenopathies (including Marfan syndrome and homocystinuria), ectopia lentis, prior vitreoretinal surgery, intravitreal drug administration, IOL exchange procedures, and loss to follow-up.

Surgical procedure

All surgeries were performed using standard phacoemulsification techniques by either senior ophthalmology residents in their fourth year of training (R4) or attending anterior segment surgeons at the institution. Preoperative toric IOL power calculation and axis planning were conducted according to institutional protocols. All patients presenting with corneal astigmatism greater than 1.25 diopters underwent corneal tomographic evaluation using a Scheimpflug-based imaging system (Pentacam; OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH). IOL power and axis calculation were performed using the Barrett Toric Calculator. The surgical eye (right or left) was selected based on poorer visual acuity and greater lens opacity.

Preoperative corneal axis marking was performed manually with the patient in an upright position to minimize cyclotorsion. A 2.2-mm clear corneal incision was performed at the 135° meridian using a Mendez degree gauge ring. Intraoperative alignment of the toric IOL was achieved using the reference marks located near the optic edge and optic–haptic junction, following the manufacturer’s recommendations. No intraoperative complications were recorded in the included cases.

Postoperative assessment and outcomes

Postoperative IOL alignment was evaluated at 1 day, 1 week, and 1 month after surgery using slit-lamp examination by aligning the slit-lamp beam with the IOL axis markings, patients were pharmacologically dilated with one drop of tropicamide/phenylephrine hydrochloride (0.8%/5.0%) in accordance with institutional protocols, all measurements were confirmed by the same attending physician from the department. The postoperative axis of the toric IOL was compared with the intended intraoperative axis. IOL rotation was calculated as the absolute difference, in degrees, between the planned axis and the observed postoperative axis.

The primary outcome was absolute IOL rotation at each postoperative time point, regardless of rotational direction. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of eyes with clinically significant rotation (> 10°), the presence or absence of any rotation (0° vs. > 0°), the temporal behavior of IOL rotation, and the need for surgical repositioning. An IOL rotation greater than 10° from the intended axis was defined as the threshold for considering surgical repositioning. For descriptive purposes, rotational direction (clockwise or counterclockwise) was also recorded and analyzed.

Size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using the pwr.anova.test function from the pwr package in R. A significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 80% were assumed. An effect size of (f = 0.40) was selected based on evidence suggesting that differences in toric IOL design and haptic configuration may result in clinically relevant differences in postoperative rotational stability. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported pooled mean absolute rotations ranging from approximately 1.0° to 3.3° across different toric IOL models and from 1.8° to 2.8° across haptic designs, demonstrating measurable variability attributable to lens design [11–13].

Given that rotational misalignment progressively reduces astigmatic correction and that a misalignment of approximately 5° is generally considered clinically relevant, we considered a between-group difference of approximately 2° in postoperative rotation to be clinically meaningful.

In the absence of direct comparative studies between the Ankoris and TECNIS ZCT lenses, a large effect size (f = 0.40) was considered a reasonable and conservative assumption for estimating a feasible sample size. Under these assumptions, a minimum of 28 eyes per group was required.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As rotational data did not follow a normal distribution, results were primarily reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), with means and standard deviations provided for descriptive purposes. Comparisons of absolute IOL rotation between the Ankoris and TECNIS ZCT groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables, including the presence of rotation and the need for surgical repositioning, were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Rotational behavior was analyzed across postoperative time points (1 day, 1 week, and 1 month). Absolute rotation values were used for inferential analyses, while signed rotation values were evaluated descriptively to assess potential directional bias. 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.0.2 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), with the packages tidyverse, ggplot2, rstatix, psych, and pwr.

Results

A total of 61 eyes were included in the analysis, of which 31 belonged to the Ankoris group and 30 to the TECNIS ZCT group. IOL rotation was recorded considering both magnitude and direction (clockwise or counterclockwise). For the assessment of rotational stability, the absolute value of rotation was used, regardless of direction.

Discussion

Rotational stability of toric IOLs is a critical determinant of postoperative refractive success, as even modest degrees of misalignment may substantially reduce astigmatic correction. In the present study, both toric IOLs demonstrated clinically acceptable rotational stability during the first postoperative month; however, the TECNIS ZCT lens exhibited lower rotational magnitude and less variability compared with the Ankoris lens at 1 month. These findings suggest that differences in lens design may influence postoperative rotational behavior, with potential implications for refractive predictability.

In addition to confirming previously reported trends in toric IOL rotational stability, the present study provides novel insights by evaluating a less extensively studied lens model in a real-world clinical setting and by incorporating a detailed temporal and variability-based analysis of rotation.

The temporal pattern of rotation observed in this study is consistent with previous reports indicating that the majority of toric IOL rotation occurs during the early postoperative period, followed by progressive stabilization. Factors such as capsular bag remodeling, early capsular contraction, and the interaction between the IOL haptics and the capsular bag are believed to contribute to this early rotational behavior. In the present series, both IOLs exhibited a similar stabilization trend over time; however, the TECNIS ZCT lens demonstrated lower dispersion of rotational values throughout follow-up, suggesting a more consistent interaction with the capsular bag [12].

Several studies have highlighted the influence of IOL material and haptic design on rotational stability. Meta-analyses and comparative studies have reported that hydrophobic acrylic toric IOLs with specific haptic configurations tend to exhibit lower rates of postoperative rotation and reduced need for surgical repositioning [12, 13]. The lower variability observed with the TECNIS ZCT lens in the current study aligns with these findings and may be related to its C-loop haptic design and overall lens diameter, which could promote enhanced capsular bag fixation. In contrast, limited real-world data are available regarding the rotational performance of newer plate-style toric IOL designs, particularly in routine clinical practice.

Although ocular biometric parameters such as axial length, anterior chamber depth, and capsular bag size have been proposed as potential factors influencing rotational stability, our exploratory analysis of cases with significant rotation did not reveal a consistent pattern. This may be related to the limited number of cases; however, it suggests that factors beyond standard biometric measurements—such as IOL design or capsular bag–IOL interaction—may also play an important role.

An additional strength of this study is the evaluation of rotational direction. Analysis of signed rotation demonstrated no preferential clockwise or counterclockwise rotational tendency in either IOL group, supporting prior observations that rotational direction is largely random and not systematically influenced by lens design [7]. This finding reinforces the use of absolute rotation as the most clinically relevant parameter for assessing toric IOL stability and justifies its selection as the primary outcome measure in the present analysis.

Although the proportion of eyes with rotation greater than 10° and the need for surgical repositioning were low in both groups, all cases requiring repositioning occurred in the Ankoris group. While this difference did not reach statistical significance, it is consistent with the greater rotational variability observed with this lens. From a clinical perspective, even small differences in rotational behavior may be particularly relevant in eyes with moderate to high astigmatism, where minimal rotation can translate into meaningful refractive error.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design, including potential selection bias and a relatively short follow-up period. Long-term follow-up is warranted, as IOL alignment may change secondary to capsular contraction and fibrosis, and rotational stability may continue to evolve beyond the early postoperative period. An increased sample size is warranted to minimize the statistical imbalance in the distribution of operated eyes (right vs. left). As surgical selection was based on poorer visual acuity and greater lens opacity, a higher proportion of right eyes was included. Although rotational behavior was evaluated at multiple postoperative time points, formal repeated measures analysis was not performed. A longitudinal statistical approach, such as a mixed-effects model or Friedman test, could provide additional insight into within-eye temporal changes and inter-individual variability. Future studies with larger sample sizes may benefit from incorporating these methods. Additionally, surgeries were performed by both senior residents and attending surgeons, which may introduce variability related to surgical technique; however, this also reflects real-world clinical practice. Future prospective studies with longer follow-up and larger sample sizes may help further elucidate the impact of lens design on long-term rotational stability. Despite these limitations, the present study provides valuable comparative data on the early postoperative rotational behavior of two toric IOL designs in routine clinical use.

Conclusions

In this retrospective comparative study, both toric IOLs demonstrated clinically acceptable rotational stability during the first postoperative month. Among eyes demonstrating measurable postoperative rotation, the TECNIS ZCT lens exhibited lower rotational magnitude and less variability compared with the Ankoris lens at 1 month of follow-up, nevertheless, both lenses exhibited comparable clinical performance. Absolute rotation proved to be the most clinically relevant parameter for evaluating toric IOL stability, as no preferential rotational direction was identified. These findings may assist surgeons in toric IOL selection, particularly in cases where refractive predictability is critical.
