Impact of the Raqeeb narcotic controlling system on tramadol prescriptions in surgical patients: A pre-post implementation study
Authors: Yahya Almarhabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Tramadol hydrochloride, a Schedule III opioid, is the primary take-home narcotic for postoperative pain in Saudi Arabia, with a misuse prevalence of 11.4%–20%. On February 1, 2024, the Saudi Food and Drug Authority implemented the Raqeeb electronic monitoring system for real-time prescription tracking to curb abuse while maintaining legitimate access.Objectives:To evaluate Raqeeb’s impact on tramadol prescribing in surgical patients and compare outcomes with international prescription drug monitoring programs (PDMPs).Methods:Retrospective cohort study of all adult patients (≥18 years) receiving tramadol prescriptions at a tertiary hospital in Saudi Arabia (April 2023 to June 2025), divided into pre-Raqeeb and post-Raqeeb periods. All tramadol prescriptions (acute postoperative and chronic/non-surgical indications) were included. Monthly rates were normalized; comparisons used t-tests and chi-square tests.Results:Monthly tramadol prescription rates in surgical patients decreased 43% (from 10.6 to 6.1 prescriptions/month). Mean dispensed quantity increased from 17.0 ± 7.6 to 21.5 ± 12.9 tablets (P = 0.003). Prescriptions issued by the surgical team fell from 86% to 48% (P < 0.001); prescriptions linked to a recent surgical procedure declined from 67% to 51% (P = 0.033). Anorectal procedures dropped 81%, while breast surgery increased 117%. Mean patient age rose from 50 ± 16 to 56 ± 15 years (P = 0.012). Repeat prescriptions remained stable (33% vs. 29%).Conclusion:Raqeeb achieved a 43% reduction in tramadol prescriptions among surgical patients—exceeding typical PDMP postoperative reductions (5%–30%)—with a shift toward older patients and chronic indications, without apparent barriers to legitimate access.

Introduction

Opioid analgesics such as tramadol hydrochloride—a centrally acting synthetic agent combining mu-opioid receptor agonism with serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibition—provide effective relief for moderate to severe pain, particularly in postoperative settings where they offer advantages in tolerability over pure mu-agonists.[1] Despite these benefits, opioid prescribing has fueled a global crisis, with surgical procedures responsible for initiating persistent use in 6-10% of previously opioid-naïve patients.[2] This leads to dependence, overdose, diversion, and substantial economic burdens, including billions in annual healthcare costs from prolonged recovery and complications.[3] In surgical practice, factors like tradition, patient expectations, and limited adoption of alternatives perpetuate high-volume dispensing, heightening community-level harms.[4]

In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, tramadol is the leading take-home opioid for surgical discharge, valued for its oral administration and perceived safety.[5] National surveys indicate misuse prevalence of 11.4%–20%, often through self-medication or unregulated access, with higher rates among younger demographics. Common surgical indications, including minor anorectal and orthopedic procedures, contribute significantly to volumes.[6] The Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) addressed this through Raqeeb, launched nationwide on February 1, 2024, as an electronic platform requiring prescriber verification, 90-day patient history review, and automated alerts for controlled substances.[7]

Internationally, similar electronic monitoring has yielded variable outcomes. United States Prescription Drug Monitoring Programs (PDMPs) achieve 5%–20% overall opioid reductions, with surgical cohorts experiencing 12%–36% declines in some mandates, though enforcement varies.[8] Australia’s SafeScript reduced high-dose prescribing by 15%–25%, while the UK’s Electronic Prescription Service focused on traceability with modest abuse impacts.[910] Middle Eastern systems in the UAE and Egypt reported 10%–18% tramadol decreases. Common challenges include potential under-prescribing and shifts to illicit sources.[11]

Evidence on Raqeeb remains limited, particularly in surgical contexts. This study investigates its effects on prescribing in surgical patients. The primary objective is to assess changes in tramadol prescription volume and proportion among surgical patients. Secondary objectives encompass evaluating shifts in dispensed quantities, prescription settings (Outpatient Department [OPD], Inpatient-Service, and Emergency Room [ER]), repeat prescriptions, surgery categories, and patient demographics, while benchmarking against regional and international programs to guide future policy.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective pre-post cohort study was conducted at a tertiary referral hospital in Saudi Arabia. All tramadol prescriptions (tablet form) issued to adult patients (≥18 years) from April 1, 2023, to June 1, 2025, were extracted from the hospital’s Electronic Controlled Drug System (CDS). Raqeeb implementation on February 1, 2024, defined the cutoff, with pre-period and post-period. The longer post-period allowed assessment of sustained effects, reflecting data availability up to the study endpoint and capturing long-term adaptation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Adult patients (≥18 years)—All tramadol tablet prescriptions dispensed during the study period, regardless of indication (acute postoperative pain, chronic/non-surgical pain, oncology-related, or other).

Exclusion criteria

Patients <18 years of age—tramadol injections or other formulations—prescriptions canceled before dispensing.

Data source and preparation

Data were collected from the hospital’s Electronic CDS, ensuring comprehensive capture of tramadol dispensings. Cleaning standardized formats (e.g., dates and categories) without imputation beyond minor fixes for consistency. Surgical procedures were categorized into eight predefined groups (anorectal/colorectal, breast surgery, biopsy/excision, orthopedic/spine, vascular/interventional, gastrointestinal/hepatobiliary, thoracic/pulmonary, and urological) using keyword matching of the free-text procedure and diagnosis fields. Prescriptions issued by the surgical team in which no specific procedure was clearly documented were excluded only from the subgroup analysis of surgery category distribution.

Variables included demographics (file number, sex, date of birth for age calculation, financial coverage), prescription (date, quantity tablets, prescriber type, setting “OPD, Inpatient-service, ER,” repeat flag), and clinical (diagnosis, surgery category).

Outcomes and benchmarking

The primary outcomes were prescription volume, expressed as monthly rates to account for period differences, and the proportion of prescriptions involving surgical patients. Secondary outcomes included mean quantity dispensed, prescription settings “OPD, Inpatient-service, ER,” repeat prescription rates, distribution of surgery categories, and patient demographics (age, sex, coverage type). Benchmarking compared volume reductions to PDMPs systematic reviews, focusing on overall opioid declines (typically 5%–20%) and postoperative cohorts (12%–36% in high-enforcement settings), using published meta-analyses for direct surgical parallels.[891011]

Statistical analysis

A prescription-level analysis was performed as the primary approach (all dispensed tramadol prescriptions were included as independent observations). Because some patients contributed multiple prescriptions, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by randomly selecting one prescription per patient to verify that clustering at the patient level did not materially alter the results. Analyses were performed using Python 3.12 with pandas for data management and scipy.stats for inferential testing. Descriptive statistics summarized continuous variables as means ± standard deviations (SD) with medians and ranges where appropriate, and categorical variables as frequencies and proportions. Inferential comparisons between pre- and post-Raqeeb periods employed independent t-tests for continuous outcomes (e.g. quantity dispensed, age) and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical outcomes (e.g. prescriber type, surgery categories, settings). Prescription rates were normalized by dividing total prescriptions by months in each period to mitigate unequal durations. Subgroup analysis focused on surgical patients, with surgery category distributions tested for independence. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed), with effect sizes reported as Cohen’s d for t-tests and Cramér’s V for chi-square tests to quantify practical importance.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of King Abdulaziz University. (Date: 2025-11-25, No: 475-25).

Results

The dataset comprised 203 tramadol prescriptions involving 181 unique patients during the 26-month study period. The pre-Raqeeb phase included 106 prescriptions (average 10.6 per month), while the post-Raqeeb phase covered 97 prescriptions (average 6.1 per month).

Patient demographics demonstrated comparability between groups in terms of sex distribution (48% male pre-Raqeeb vs. 42% post-Raqeeb, χ² = 0.89, df =1, P = 0.346) and coverage type (80% insurance pre-Raqeeb vs. 81% post-Raqeeb, χ² = 0.04, df =1, P = 0.841). However, post-Raqeeb patients were significantly older, with a mean age of 56 ± 15 years compared to 50 ± 16 years pre-Raqeeb (t = −2.52, df =201, P = 0.012, Cohen’s d = 0.38), potentially indicating a focus on chronic rather than acute cases [Table 1].

Overall prescription trends revealed a 43% decline in monthly rates post-Raqeeb. Mean quantity dispensed rose from 17.0 ± 7.6 tablets to 21.5 ± 12.9 tablets (P = 0.003). Prescribing patterns shifted markedly after Raqeeb implementation. Prescriptions issued by the surgical team decreased from 86% (91 / 106) to 48% (47 / 97) (χ² = 32.1, df = 1, P < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.40), while the proportion dispensed in the OPD rose from 15% (16 / 106) to 30% (29 / 97) and inpatient prescriptions declined from 72% to 54% (χ² =6.78, df =2, P = 0.034, Cramér’s V = 0.18). Repeat prescriptions remained stable at 33% (35 / 106) pre-Raqeeb versus 29% (28 / 97) post-Raqeeb (χ² = 0.19, df =1, P = 0.661).

Clinical indications also changed substantially: pre-Raqeeb prescriptions were predominantly for acute surgical conditions (e.g. hemorrhoids, n = 18), whereas post-Raqeeb prescriptions showed a clear shift toward oncology-related and chronic-pain indications (e.g. breast cancer: 6 cases pre-Raqeeb vs. 19 cases post-Raqeeb) [detailed in Table 2].

Of the 138 prescriptions issued by the surgical team, 120 (87%) had a clearly documented surgical procedure that permitted categorization into eight predefined groups. The remaining 18 prescriptions lacked specific procedural details and were therefore excluded from the surgery category distribution analysis only. Among these 120 fully documented prescriptions, the distribution of surgery categories changed significantly after Raqeeb implementation (χ² = 28.4, df =7, P < 0.001). Notably, anorectal/colorectal procedures decreased by 81% (from 51% to 14% of surgical prescriptions), whereas breast surgery increased by 117% (from 8% to 27%) [Table 3].

Benchmarking against published PDMPs showed that the 43% overall reduction and the 44% relative decline in surgical team prescriptions achieved with Raqeeb are substantially larger than the reductions typically seen in mandatory PDMPs (usually 5%–20% overall and 12%–36% in postoperative/high-enforcement cohorts).[891011]

Discussion

The introduction of the Raqeeb electronic monitoring system on February 1, 2024, was associated with a pronounced and sustained 43% reduction in monthly tramadol prescription rates at our tertiary center. The most striking change was the sharp decline in prescriptions issued by the surgical team (from 86% to 48% of all tramadol prescriptions) and in prescriptions linked to a recent surgical procedure. These findings suggest that Raqeeb successfully curtailed low-value postoperative tramadol dispensing—particularly for minor procedures such as anorectal surgery—while oncology-related and chronic-pain indications became relatively more prominent.

The observed increase in mean dispensed quantity per prescription, rise in outpatient prescribing, older patient age (mean increase of 6 years), and stable repeat prescription rates collectively indicate that access for patients with legitimate higher-need chronic pain (especially in oncology) was preserved.[1213] This pattern aligns with the intended stewardship effect of mandatory real-time monitoring systems and is consistent with national efforts to reduce tramadol misuse among younger individuals.[7]

When benchmarked against international PDMPs, the magnitude of change achieved with Raqeeb appears notably larger. Systematic reviews of mandatory PDMPs report overall opioid prescribing reductions of 5%–20% and postoperative or high-enforcement reductions of 12%–36%.[8] Regional tramadol-specific initiatives in the UAE and Egypt have shown 10%–18% declines[1011] and Australia’s SafeScript achieved 15%–25% reductions in high-dose opioid prescribing.[9] Raqeeb’s 43% overall reduction and 44% relative decline in surgical team prescribing, therefore, exceed most published PDMP effects,[811] likely reflecting its mandatory prescriber verification and real-time alerts.

Strengths of this study include its surgical-specific focus with detailed procedural categorization, integration of benchmarking for policy relevance, an extended post-implementation observation period (16 months) to capture maturation effects, and robust effect-size reporting.

Limitations include the single-center retrospective design, which limits generalizability; inability to definitively attribute causality due to possible unmeasured confounders; absence of patient-centered outcomes (pain control, satisfaction, misuse incidents); lack of data on potential diversion to illicit sources; and unequal pre- and post-period durations (mitigated by monthly rate normalization).

Clinical implications include the need to promote multimodal analgesic protocols (e.g., NSAIDs, regional blocks) for minor surgery while continuing to support appropriate tramadol use in oncology and chronic-pain settings through Raqeeb.[14] Caution is required to avoid over-restriction that might drive undertreatment or illicit substitution, as observed in some PDMP settings.[15]

Multi-center prospective research incorporating pain metrics and misuse surveillance is recommended.

Conclusion

The Raqeeb system was associated with a substantial and sustained 43% reduction in tramadol prescribing, particularly in the postoperative setting, with a marked shift away from minor surgical procedures toward older patients and oncology/chronic-pain indications. These changes exceed many international PDMP postoperative reductions, indicating effective stewardship in surgical patients. However, caution is advised to avoid over-restriction that might compromise legitimate access or encourage illicit alternatives.
