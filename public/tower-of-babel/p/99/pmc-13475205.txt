The physiology of leptin in avians: A review
Authors: Michelle Wright, Joonbum Lee, Kichoon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leptin, discovered in 1994 in ob/ob and db/db mutant mice, is a key regulator of energy balance, appetite, and multiple physiological systems including immunity and reproduction. While its function in mammals is well-established, leptin’s presence and role in birds remain controversial due to differences in gene sequence, tissue expression, and signaling mechanisms. Avian leptin exhibits a GC-rich sequence, predominates in autocrine and paracrine signaling, and maintains conserved receptor-binding residues, supporting functional activity despite low systemic levels. Experimental studies show leptin influences feed intake, immune function, and reproduction, though conflicting results have fueled ongoing debate. In this review, we discuss evidence supporting and questioning avian leptin function and highlight emerging gene-editing approaches, including CRISPR/Cas9-mediated manipulation of leptin and its receptor in poultry, as powerful tools to clarify its functional dynamics in vivo, resolve longstanding uncertainties, and potentially enhance poultry growth and production efficiency.

Introduction

The identification of the leptin hormone in 1994 was a major milestone in understanding energy homeostasis in mammals. This peptide hormone was initially characterized through experiments using the ob/ob and db/db mutant mouse models (Coleman, 2010). These mutant mice both experience severe obesity, excessive feed intake, and diabetes, yet the genetic mechanisms leading to these similar phenotypes were shown to differ. The critical distinction between the two models lies in their capacity to either produce or detect the leptin hormone. Ob/ob mice were found to have a mutation in the obesity (ob) gene, causing a lack of the leptin hormone in the body, whereas db/db mice lack the receptor due to a mutation in the diabetes (db) gene (Coleman, 2010). These findings established leptin as a key regulator of mammalian energy balance and provided the foundation for investigating leptin’s function in other systems as well as across other vertebrate taxa.

Since the discovery of leptin, many additional functions in other body systems have been identified, including essential actions in the immune and reproductive systems. In mammals, leptin acts as a crucial immunostimulatory molecule, increasing the function and proliferation of most immune cell types (Pérez-Pérez et al., 2017). In the reproductive system, leptin acts as a marker for the onset of puberty as well as assisting in follicular development through direct and indirect mechanisms by affecting the secretion of major reproductive hormones, including gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (Agarwal et al., 2008).

While ample research in mammals has long since characterized leptin's function as a satiety factor as well as its role in other systems, the hormone's presence and function in other vertebrates, especially avians, has long been debated. This controversy stems from significant differences in gene sequence, tissue expression patterns, and protein structure when compared to mammals. Moreover, technical challenges, such as the high guanine-cytosine (GC) content of the avian leptin gene, have posed a challenge in characterization of the leptin gene in many bird species including chicken and quail.

Discussion

Mammalian leptin

Leptin is a peptide hormone encoded by the obesity gene and predominantly produced by white adipose tissue in mammals (Sweeney, 2002). It was later discovered that the hormone is also produced in brown adipose tissue and the placenta, albeit at significantly lower levels of expression than in white fat (Trayhurn et al., 1999). Several factors regulate the expression of the leptin hormone from these tissues, with adiposity and caloric intake being two of the most significant. The relationship between leptin and adiposity is relatively straightforward, as increases in adiposity are directly associated with elevated leptin expression and circulating concentrations (Trayhurn et al., 1999). This correlation reflects leptin’s role as an indicator of energy storage within the body. In contrast, the relationship between leptin and caloric intake is more complex, involving intricate metabolic and hormonal regulation. Both glucose and insulin influence leptin expression, with insulin acting as a key signal that stimulates leptin synthesis in adipocytes. Overall, studies have shown that leptin concentrations tend to rise in response to elevated glucose and insulin levels, linking nutritional status to leptin-mediated energy homeostasis (Considine, 2001).

Leptin receptors, encoded by the diabetes gene, are most abundantly expressed in the hypothalamus, with lower levels of expression detected in various other tissues (Mercer et al., 1996; Sweeney, 2002; Trayhurn et al., 1999). The next two highest-expression tissues, the leptomeninges and the choroid plexus, are also found in the brain (Mercer et al., 1996). Leptin receptors are also expressed in the kidneys, adrenal medulla, pancreas, placenta, gonads, and adipose tissue of mammals, albeit at much lower levels than in the hypothalamus and other brain regions (Trayhurn et al., 1999). Multiple isoforms of this transmembrane receptor exist due to alternative splicing of the receptor mRNA, with at least six variants identified: four short forms (Ob-Ra, Ob-Rc, Ob-Rd, and Ob-Rf), one long form (Ob-Rb), and one soluble form (Ob-Re) (G.-H. Lee et al., 1996; Sweeney, 2002). The membrane-bound isoforms of the leptin receptor differ mainly in the structure of their intracellular domains, which determine their ability to participate in leptin signaling. All forms of the receptor contain a Janus-family tyrosine kinase (JAK) domain, while the long form, Ob-Rb, possesses additional signal transducers and activators of transcription (STAT) binding sites (Sweeney, 2002). Together, these domains form the JAK-STAT pathway, a major cell signaling route that transmits extracellular leptin signals to the nucleus to regulate gene expression. Because the short receptor isoforms lack the STAT binding sites, they have far less signaling capability than the Ob-Rb receptor, which is essential for normal leptin function (Sweeney, 2002). While the soluble form of this receptor is not membrane bound and thus has no direct role in leptin signaling, it likely functions to regulate the amount of the hormone present in circulation (Sweeney, 2002).

Leptin’s main function of regulating feed intake and energy expenditure is primarily achieved through its interactions with the neuroendocrine system. This regulation involves a network of hypothalamic pathways that respond to endocrine signaling by the leptin hormone. There are many ways leptin carries out this role, although it primarily acts through hypothalamic neuropeptide Y (NPY), glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1), corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART), and the pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) system (Trayhurn et al., 1999). Leptin’s regulatory action on NPY expression is distinct from that of the other neuropeptides in this list. It functions by decreasing the expression of NPY, a neuropeptide known to increase feed intake and decrease energy expenditure. In contrast, leptin signaling increases the expression of GLP-1, CRH, CART, and POMC, which together work to reduce feed intake and promote higher energy expenditure. Through these coordinated interactions, leptin plays a key role in maintaining energy homeostasis within the body (Trayhurn et al., 1999).

Although the primary role of leptin is the regulation of energy homeostasis through its function as a satiety factor, the hormone also exerts significant effects on several other physiological systems. Among these, the reproductive and immune systems are particularly influenced by leptin activity. Within the immune system, leptin has been shown to enhance the function of nearly all immune cell types, acting as an immunostimulatory molecule (Pérez-Pérez et al., 2017). Interestingly, when leptin concentrations decrease due to malnutrition, this immune-enhancing effect is diminished, which likely contributes to the immunosuppressive state commonly associated with malnutrition (Trayhurn et al., 1999). In the reproductive system, leptin plays an essential role in the onset of puberty and the regulation of follicular development. These effects are mediated, in part, through stimulation of GnRH secretion, which in turn regulates the release of LH and FSH. Additionally, leptin has been shown to directly stimulate the secretion of LH and FSH (Agarwal et al., 2008).

Avian leptin

Controversy. Although leptin has long been recognized as a key hormone regulating satiety and energy homeostasis in mammals, its presence and functional significance in poultry have remained subjects of debate. This is due to the many differences that exist between mammalian and avian leptin, including differences in the genetic sequence itself. In poultry, the leptin gene is notably GC-rich compared to its mammalian counterpart. This elevated GC content, characterized by extensive G/C stretches within the gene, not only distinguishes it from the mammalian sequence but also complicates analysis of the gene, as such regions are difficult to amplify using PCR and other methods (Farkašová et al., 2016). Despite this, the leptin gene has been sequenced in many different avian species, the first of which were zebra finch and falcons (Huang et al., 2014; Prokop et al., 2014). Additionally, variation in the gene sequence subsequently leads to substantial differences at the amino acid level. Comparative studies have reported that chicken and human leptin share only about 25% similarity in the amino acid sequence of the protein (Farkašová et al., 2016). Despite these pronounced differences in both the nucleotide and amino acid sequences, the overall three-dimensional structure of the hormone appears to be more conserved. Structural analyses have revealed over 50% similarity in protein conformation between human, duck, and chicken leptin, suggesting that key functional domains are maintained across species (Seroussi et al., 2016).

Along with differences in the structure of the hormone, the signaling function of leptin also differs significantly between mammals and poultry. In mammals, leptin is primarily produced by adipocytes and acts through endocrine signaling by binding to receptors in the hypothalamus. In contrast, in poultry, leptin is expressed not only in adipose tissue but also in the kidneys and heart, albeit at relatively low levels. Interestingly, the cerebellum, cerebrum, hypothalamus, pituitary, and adrenal gland represent the major leptin-expressing tissues in birds (Seroussi et al., 2016). The distribution of leptin receptors closely parallels this expression pattern, with receptor levels often exceeding those of leptin itself in the same tissues. The presence of both the hormone and its receptor, together with the elevated receptor expression, strongly suggests that leptin in poultry primarily functions through autocrine or paracrine signaling rather than the endocrine route observed in mammals (Seroussi et al., 2016). Unlike mammals, in which alternative splicing generates six leptin receptor isoforms (Ob-Ra–Ob-Rf) with distinct signaling properties, the structural diversity of the avian leptin receptor remains poorly characterized. The chicken LEPR was originally cloned as a functional long receptor capable of activating the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and while alternative transcripts have been suggested, no functionally distinct short receptor isoforms or soluble LEPR comparable to mammalian Ob-Re have been conclusively identified (Adachi et al., 2008; Liu and Sharp, 2007). Consequently, whether poultry possess receptor variants capable of attenuating leptin signaling through dominant-negative activity or ligand sequestration remains unknown. Because of these differences, despite numerous studies examining leptin in avian species, its physiological role remains a topic of considerable debate within the scientific community.

In support. Despite ongoing controversy surrounding avian leptin, numerous studies support its existence and functional role in energy homeostasis. At the molecular level, investigations have focused on the hormone’s ability to bind its receptor and the receptor’s capacity to respond. Although avian and mammalian leptin and leptin receptors differ substantially in amino acid sequence, key hydrophobic, polar basic, and polar acidic residues within the binding pocket are conserved, enabling critical ligand–receptor interactions. For example, comparison between human and peregrine falcon leptin shows only two amino acid differences in the hormone and one in the receptor within this crucial region, with additional residues proposed to compensate and maintain binding interactions (Prokop et al., 2012). Beyond binding, the receptor’s signaling capability has also been examined. In vitro studies indicate that the avian leptin receptor can activate the JAK–STAT pathway, mirroring the well-characterized signaling mechanism observed in mammals (Adachi et al., 2008; Prokop et al., 2012). Studies have also been performed to investigate the functional role of leptin and its receptor in regulating energy balance and growth. One approach has been to examine the direct effects of leptin administration on feeding behavior, providing insight into its role in appetite regulation. Across multiple species, including several different breeds of chickens, administration of exogenous leptin consistently leads to a reduction in feed intake through activation of the JAK–STAT pathway, demonstrating its role as a potent appetite-suppressing hormone (Denbow et al., 2000; Kuo et al., 2005; Lõhmus et al., 2003). Additionally, studies have shown that leptin maintains homeostasis through interactions with AMPK to induce autophagy in a tissue-specific manner. This mechanism allows leptin to play an autocrine/paracrine role in metabolic stress and cellular survival of certain tissues including the hypothalamus and liver (Piekarski et al., 2018).

Beyond leptin’s primary role in energy homeostasis and feed intake regulation, its additional functions in the immune, reproductive, and endocrine systems have provided further support for its presence and significance in avian species. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments conducted on poultry have demonstrated consistent results: administration of leptin promotes the proliferation of T-cells (Lõhmus et al., 2004). This response mirrors the leptin-mediated immunostimulatory effects observed in mammals. Furthermore, leptin has been shown to influence the reproductive systems of birds, with studies reporting significant effects on folliculogenesis in post-hatch chicks. These effects occur both through direct action on the ovary and indirectly by modulating the secretion of other hormones (Ahmadi and Ohkubo, 2022, 2025; Shaikat et al., 2021). Additionally, administration of leptin has been shown to advance the onset of puberty in chickens, a finding consistent with its reproductive effects in mammals (Paczoska-Eliasiewicz et al., 2006). In addition to post-hatching treatment of leptin, in ovo administration of recombinant mouse leptin to embryonic quail significantly changed growth and development regulatory hormones, namely, decreased thyroid hormone T3 level on day of hatching and T4 level during post-hatching growth phase (Lamosová et al., 2003). Concomitantly, embryonic leptin administration resulted in early hatching with increased body weight and improved growth rate, suggesting regulatory functions of embryonic leptin for growth and development of birds before and after hatching.

In denial. While substantial evidence supports the existence of avian leptin, there also remains research that questions the hormone’s existence and function. One of the earliest and most influential sources of controversy arose from issues surrounding the initial sequencing of the leptin gene. Early studies reported that the chicken leptin gene shared 97% sequence homology with that of the mouse (Taouis et al., 1998). Subsequent investigations, however, revealed that no gene within the chicken genome has such similarity to the mouse leptin gene, suggesting that the earlier results were likely due to sample contamination (Friedman-Einat et al., 1999). Although numerous, more reliable leptin gene sequences have since been identified in many avian species, the uncertainty and debate sparked by these early inaccuracies, as well as studies based on these findings, continue to influence acceptance of the hormone (Friedman-Einat et al., 2014).

Studies performed in vivo on avian species have also produced results that support the denial of leptin’s existence in birds. Interestingly, while many studies involving the administration of exogenous leptin have reported the expected effect of decreased feed intake, others have shown no response. Experiments using mouse-derived leptin as well as synthetic chicken partial leptin have both shown no significant effect on food or water intake, thereby challenging leptin’s proposed role as a satiety factor in these cases (Bungo et al., 1999; Sims et al., 2017). These inconsistent findings likely reflect more than simple receptor incompatibility. Chicken leptin shares only approximately 25% amino acid sequence identity with mammalian leptin, although both retain a conserved four-helix cytokine fold (Farkašová et al., 2016; Prokop et al., 2014). Structural modeling has demonstrated that while several residues directly involved in receptor binding are conserved, numerous amino acid substitutions surrounding the binding interface are predicted to alter ligand-receptor affinity and receptor activation. Consequently, heterologous mammalian leptin may exhibit reduced binding affinity or fail to induce the conformational changes required for efficient JAK-STAT signaling through the avian receptor (Prokop et al., 2012, 2014). Similarly, synthetic chicken partial leptin peptides lack the complete tertiary structure necessary for optimal receptor engagement (Sims et al., 2017). These structural differences provide a plausible biochemical explanation for the conflicting physiological responses reported following administration of mammalian or synthetic leptin preparations and highlight the importance of studying authentic avian leptin and its endogenous receptor.

Future directives

While there has been ample evidence both confirming and denying the existence of the leptin hormone in avians, continued research is essential to understand these conflicting findings and bring resolution to the debate surrounding avian leptin. One aspect of avian leptin that is not fully understood is how the hormone binds to its receptor. Clarifying the existence and physiological significance of avian LEPR splice variants represents an important future research direction, as receptor diversity may contribute to tissue-specific leptin sensitivity and could partially explain the inconsistent physiological responses reported following exogenous leptin administration. Furthermore, while the genetic sequence of leptin has been characterized in many avian species, several critical species, including many breeds of chickens, have yet to be sequenced in their entirety. Numerous early sequencing attempts of leptin in chickens have resulted in failure or nonauthentic sequences (Friedman-Einat et al., 1999; Ninov et al., 2008). In recent years, while some studies have successfully sequenced portions of the leptin gene in various chicken breeds, a breakthrough was made through the identification of the full leptin coding sequence in Silkie chickens (Farkašová et al., 2016; Seroussi et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2023). Although, we are still missing full leptin sequences from major chicken breeds such as commercial broiler and layer chickens. The difficulty in obtaining these sequences is primarily attributed to the high GC content of the leptin gene, which creates a difficult landscape for accurate sequencing. Advancements in sequencing methods for such regions could not only facilitate the successful sequencing of avian leptin genes but also allow the characterization of other GC-rich genes across species.

Beyond experimental approaches involving leptin administration or in situ analyses of avian leptin and receptor structure and function, one avenue that remains largely unexplored in the study of avian leptin is gene editing. Recent studies have shown that leptin receptor genes can be efficiently edited in both chicken and turkey cells in vitro using CRISPR/Cas9 delivered via adenoviral vectors (Wright et al., 2026). These findings lay the groundwork for applying gene-editing strategies in vivo in chickens and turkeys using adenoviral vectors, allowing previously inaccessible studies on the functional dynamics of the leptin receptor (Lee et al., 2019, 2022). While administration of leptin has produced varied results both supporting and refuting the existence of avian leptin, these findings may arise from a variety of factors. Findings that question the existence of avian leptin do not necessarily indicate the absence of the hormone itself but may instead reflect an inability of the hormone to effectively bind to its receptor or, in some cases, methodological limitations. Gene knockout studies targeting either the leptin or leptin receptor genes could provide clearer insight, potentially yielding results comparable to those observed in ob/ob and db/db mouse models. If such results were obtained, the original leptin experiments that established its function in mammals could be replicated in avian species, ultimately confirming the presence or absence of the hormone in birds. Although these approaches may be time extensive, they could also offer practical benefits. For instance, previous studies involving leptin receptor knockout in fish resulted in animals that were larger and heavier than their wild-type counterparts (“Japan Embraces CRISPR-Edited Fish, 2022). If similar outcomes were achieved in poultry, this could produce birds that reach market weight faster and have greater body weight than current commercial breeds, not only confirming the existence of avian leptin but also creating a more efficient production bird.
