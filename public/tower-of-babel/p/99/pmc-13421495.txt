Performance analysis of a biomass gasification combined cycle power plant using rice husk and sugarcane bagasse with circular utilization of ash in green concrete
Authors: Samia Parvez, Syed Shakil Afsar, Ibadur Rahman, Mohd Parvez, Sagar Paruthi, Nazia Parvez, Ashok Kumar Yadav, Osama Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomass gasification is an emerging area for sustainable power generation, by utilizing readily available agricultural residues to address energy demand while reducing environmentally harmful waste disposal. The current study presents a theoretical design for a 1 MWe combined cycle power plant that uses rice husk and sugarcane bagasse as potential feedstocks. The study models the parameters by employing Python software, in which the gasification process is modelled using air-steam blending over a temperature range of 1273–1473 K. The produced syngas powers an integrated gas turbine–steam turbine configuration, achieving a predicted net energy efficiency of 39.15% for rice husk, 36.89% for sugarcane bagasse and power output of 1.05 MWe under optimal conditions. The gasification model produced normalized wet syngas yields of 2.57 kg syngas/kg biomass for rice husk and 2.45 kg syngas/kg biomass for sugarcane bagasse, corresponding to dry syngas yields of 1.77 and 1.61 kg/kg biomass, respectively, with equivalent dry volumetric yields of 2.10 and 1.56 Nm³/kg biomass. Additionally, generated rice husk ash (RHA) and sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) from the gasification process are further utilized to partially replace cement in the creation of sustainable concrete. The hybrid concrete showed favourable results, improving compressive strength by 12.6% and 8.2% for 10% RHA and SCBA cement replacement in a 28-day analysis, respectively, compared to conventional concrete. Furthermore, these results support the viability of the plant in agriculture-dependent countries such as India, with levelized cost of electricity readily available at $0.08–0.10/kWh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57320-9.Keywords: Energy efficiency, Exergy efficiency, Biomass gasification, Green concrete, Waste ash utilizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

In recent years, countries in tropical regions with high annual agricultural production have generated large quantities of agricultural residues which are often disposed of through unsuitable methods. This presents major difficulty with respect to waste management and environmental preservation. In particular, India, a country with rich agricultural resources, generates a large amount of biomass waste, with the total yearly biomass waste produced estimated to be more than hundred million metric tonnes1,2. Rice husk and sugarcane bagasse represent the two most significant agricultural residues, produced in large quantities by majority of agricultural-based regions. Rice husk production is estimated to be around 20–30 million metric tonnes annually, while sugarcane bagasse production is approximated to be around 75–95 million metric tonnes of residue produced each year3. The environment suffers considerably from improper disposal and handling practices of these residues. Often these wastes are burned in open fields which release harmful toxics into the atmosphere such as particulate matter, unburnt hydrocarbons, and greenhouse gases. This in turn results in poor air quality which is considered as the primary reason for increased respiratory ailments and global climate change4,5.

Recent popularity for sustainable and cleaner fuels has diverted the attention to biomass utilisation. Among these, biomass gasification has emerged as a sustainable waste management option for agricultural waste. Biomass is partially oxidized under oxygen-limited conditions and produces a combustible gas termed syngas or producer gas. This syngas can be subsequently used to generate electricity in engines or gas turbines, thereby reducing environmental pollution and providing useful energy from waste. At the same time, the world market for biomass gasification is estimated to grow substantially, at an expected compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of approximately 21.5% between 2023 and 20286–8, as India strives to meet its renewable energy goals. Biomass gasification has several benefits such as low cost, renewable nature, readily available and easier conversion in other fuels capability9,10. Recent studies have also confirmed that thermochemical upgrading of agro-byproducts can improve their fuel characteristics, thereby increasing their suitability for thermal power plant and biomass-to-energy applications11. Waste ash of these biomasses can be used as green concrete in building also as shown in previous literature survey12–15.

In past multiple investigations explored the potential of biomass residues in integrated power plants that generate energy by biomass gasification. Pellegrini and Junior16 performed a parametric analysis to model gasifier performance. Using exergy analysis in their study, irreversibility is found to be the primary source of inefficiency. Results showed that adding a gasifier to a sugarcane mill would greatly increase the total efficiency of both the gasifier and the entire system and result in significant energy savings. In another study, Srinivas, et al.17 developed a thermodynamic equilibrium model that was used to estimate the performance of a pressurized circulating fluidized bed biomass gasifier and predict the composition of syngas from it. Thermodynamic analysis was employed to calculate other variables of interest, indicating that by lowering values of both the RAFR and SFR resulted in the generation of syngas of a higher heating value, thereby increasing the overall exergy efficiency. Kalita and Baruah18 investigated biomass gasification using thermochemical technique for creating Fischer-Tropsch fuels and thermal power. The equilibrium model made use of rice husk prediction analysis for reaction kinetics, emphasizing that variations in biomass feedstock parameters have a significant impact on gas composition and system performance. Parvez and Khan19, developed a thermodynamic model of different efficiency measures to analyse a biomass gasification plant using principles from both the first and second laws of thermodynamics. The results of their analyses showed that the combustor produced the greatest amount of energy destruction while the gas turbine was next followed by the steam turbine and the heat recovery steam generator (HRSG).

Bamisile et al.20 examined the multigeneration performance of a novel energy system, by comparing thermodynamic efficiencies with two different biomass inputs. Their results showed the rice husk achieving 34.83% energy efficiency and 12% exergy efficiency whereas, sugarcane bagasse-based system demonstrated better performance, reaching 68.29% energy efficiency and 21.85% exergy efficiency. In order to assess and rank agro-waste biomass for pyrolysis while taking sociological, economic, and environmental issues into account, Howari et al.21 used an AHP weighted TOPSIS based model. With a closeness value of 0.9, sawdust placed highest among the six feedstocks assessed based on eight parameters, while apple bagasse came in second at 0.8. The difficulties of biomass gasification in present energy scenarios were examined by Cortazar et al.22, who emphasized the process’s potential for producing heat, power, and biofuel. High tar levels can lead to pipeline clogging, corrosion, catalyst deactivation, as well as environmental and health risks. In a recent study, Awasthi et al.23 recognized rice husk and sugarcane bagasse as important biomass sources for heat and power generation, and they presented agricultural leftovers as useful resources for both energy and material production. Paudel et al.24 reviewed 343 papers from the past 23 years to analyze thermochemical conversion of agricultural crop residues into biofuels and value-added products. Results showed rapid research growth, with pyrolysis and biochar as the dominant process and product, and a recent shift toward sustainability, circular economy, process optimization, and diversified applications.

In addition to producing energy, biomass gasification yields substantial solid leftovers in the form of ash, such as sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) and rice husk ash (RHA), both of which have high silica contents25. The sustainability of biomass-based energy systems depends on the efficient use of these ashes. Because of their high reactive silica concentration, RHA and SCBA have strong pozzolanic capabilities, according to recent concrete study26. Because of these qualities, they can be used as additional cementitious materials27–30. In addition, their partial substitution of cement contributes to a reduction in the total amount of cement used, which lowers carbon emissions and promotes more environmentally friendly building methods31–34. RHA and SCBA have been employed as additional cementitious materials in concrete applications by a number of studies.

Secondary hydration processes and the ensuing densification of the microstructure are mostly responsible for these enhancements35–38. Most studies focus either on the use of ash in concrete or on the production of biomass energy. Integration of both elements into a single framework is the subject of very few studies. Consequently, there is a glaring research deficit in using a circular economy strategy to connect biomass gasification systems with sustainable building materials. By converting agricultural waste into energy and sustainable building materials, this integrated strategy lowers carbon emissions and promotes sustainability. In order to get over these restrictions, combined energy and exergy assessments have been performed for assessing the theoretical performance of energy systems, especially the thermodynamic cycles for combined cycle power plants while simultaneously making use of ash for green concrete production39–42.

This methodology further indicates how entropy generation can be minimized in each cycle component so as to improve and enhance overall thermodynamic performance for the proposed cycle. To further understand the effects of different features of a combined cycle system based on biomass gasification, additional study was done utilizing energy and exergy performance indicators. To interpret different patterns among important parameters and the relationships between different properties inside the system, theoretical results were analyzed and graphical representations were used. System description of 1 MWe biomass gasification combined cycle (BGCC)

The analysis begins by analysing a 1 MW electrical (MWe) biomass gasification plant which makes use of the agricultural by products for initial power generation. Figure 2 depicts the schematic representation of a 1 MW electrical (MWe) BGCC power plant where biomass feedstocks such as rice husk and sugar cane bagasse are converted to synthesis gas (syngas) in the gasifier using compressed air obtained from the compressor at state point 2a and a quantity of steam taken from state point 9. After passing through a tar and char removal unit (state point 4), the syngas enters the combustion chamber to be combusted with excess air supplied (state point 2b). Before being expanded into the gas turbine for generating electricity, the excess air is specifically regulated so as to achieve a desired GTIT at its entrance (state point 6). The exit of the high-temperature exhaust gases from the gas turbine (state point 7) is directed into the HRSG for producing steam that will be sent to the ST engine (state point 8) for generating additional electricity. Finally, the exhaust of the ST is cooled in the condenser (state point 10) while the remaining hot exhaust from the HRSG (state point 13) is used for hot water applications. Fluidized bed gasifiers are used in this study to biomass resources. Because of its superior mixing and high heat transmission capabilities, which guarantee uniform bed conditions, fluidized bed gasifiers are beneficial. High-quality combustion is accomplished even at relatively low combustion temperatures, leading to a carbon burnout rate of 99–100%43.

Development of sustainable concrete

In order to produce sustainable concrete, the ashes produced by the biomass unit RHA and SCBA were utilized as an additional cementitious ingredient46,47. To create a circular economy concept for the renewable syngas, biomass gasification first uses agricultural leftovers. The silica-rich ashes that are left over after energy extraction are used to partially substitute cement in concrete48,49. The purpose of this analysis is to assess the mechanical performance of concrete that uses SCBA and RHA independently at different replacement amounts.

Materials

The contemporary study employs ordinary Portland cement (OPC) in accordance with the requirements of IS 12,269 established standards50. In terms of fineness, the cement has a value of 320 m²/kg, and its specific gravity was found to be 3.15. The coarse aggregates were crushed granite with a maximum size of 20 mm, while fine aggregate consisted of sand obtained from Badarpur area with a fineness modulus equal to 2.65. The specific gravities of both fine and coarse aggregates were found to be 2.62 and 2.74, respectively, meeting the minimum limits according to requirements set forth in IS 383:201351. Water used for the mixing and curing processes was potable. RHA and SCBA were both generated from the gasification unit at a biomass power plant. The gasification process took place using a fluidized bed reaction under controlled air to steam environments with temperatures maintained between 1273 K and 1473 K throughout. The ashes produced from high-temperature, low-oxygen processing are primarily comprised of amorphous silica phases which are known for their high pozzolanic reactivity52. After collection, these ashes were cooled down to room temperature and processed further to enhance their uniformity and reactivity while sieving them through a 75-µm sieve in order to remove un-combusted carbon and coarse impurities. These are eventually ground in a laboratory ball mill to reduce the size of the particles and increase their specific surface area which will improve their reactivity53. Figure 4 shows the particle size distribution curves for Cement, RHA, SCBA, fine aggregate and coarse aggregate. Both RHA and SCBA have a fineness similar to that of ordinary Portland cement with improved packing density of particles. This further results in a denser microstructure, making it more compact54,55. The LOI value of RHA was 4.2%, which is within the commonly acceptable limit of < 6%, whereas SCBA showed a slightly higher LOI of 6.8%, indicating a greater amount of residual unburnt carbon. This higher LOI may reduce the effective pozzolanic reactivity of SCBA compared with RHA and partly explains the relatively lower compressive-strength improvement observed for SCBA-based concrete36. The lower strength gain of SCBA concrete compared with RHA concrete can also be associated with its higher LOI value. A higher LOI reflects residual carbonaceous matter, which may absorb mixing water, reduce effective binder hydration, and weaken particle–paste interaction. Therefore, although SCBA contains reactive silica, its higher LOI of 6.8% limits its pozzolanic efficiency compared with RHA, which has a lower LOI of 4.2%38. Compressive strength methodology

The composition of concrete quarts adopted a M30 grade concrete design as established by IS 10262:2009 guidelines56. A consistent water/binder (w/b) ratio of 0.45 has been maintained for all samples thus ensuring that any differences observed would reflect the performance of the cement while comparing them. Also, a mechanical performance of the concretes is determined by replacing the cement partially with RHA and SCBA.

Cubical moulds measuring 150 mm × 150 mm × 150 mm were used for the casting of concrete samples (as shown in Fig. 5a). In order to maintain statistical reliability and consistency; three samples were prepared for each mix ratio for both 7 and 28 days. Immediately following casting; the samples were settled on vibrating tables (as shown in Fig. 5b); to remove excess air and ensure uniform density. After a period of 24 h; the samples were released from their initial moulds; and placed into curing tanks at 27 ± 2 °C; until their specified test ages (as shown in Fig. 5c, before testing for compressive strength using a calibrated compression testing machine; following the standard in IS516:195957; at a loading rate of 140 kg/cm²/min (as shown in Fig. 5d). The resultant compressive strength results were determined to be the average of three samples; and their variability determined from standard deviation calculations. Results varied less than 5%; indicating good repeatability and reliability of the testing procedure used.

Results and discussion

The present study evaluates the performance of a 1 MWe BGCC power plant using rice husk and sugarcane bagasse as feedstocks. The analysis examines the influence of gas turbine inlet temperature (GTIT) and steam turbine inlet pressure (STIP) on overall energy and exergy efficiencies. Component-wise exergy destruction is also quantified to identify the major sources of irreversibility within the gasifier, combustion chamber, HRSG, turbine units, condenser, and feed pump. In addition, the ash generated from biomass gasification is assessed as a supplementary cementitious material for sustainable concrete production58. The predicted syngas composition, heating value, and cycle-performance indicators are compared with independently reported values to validate the thermodynamic model and confirm the consistency of the present results.

Influence of gas turbine inlet temperature on energy efficiency

Figure 6 displays the relationship between GTIT and biomass-fuelled combined cycle power plant energy efficiency. As GTIT increases for both rice husk and sugarcane bagasse systems, there is a near-linear increase in energy efficiency. However, at all GTIT levels, rice hulls produce higher energy efficiencies as shown in the Fig. 6 above than do sugarcane bagasse systems.

Effect of GTIT on energy efficiency.

This difference is due to the higher operating temperatures of the gasifiers and the increased value of LHV of the syngas produced when converting rice husks to syngas. Thus, more total energy recovers during conversion. Sugarcane bagasse systems, in comparison, operate at a lower LHV, resulting from lower temperature gasifiers, and therefore have a lower energy conversion efficiency.

Impact of GTIT on exergy efficiency

As shown in Fig. 7, exergy analysis results indicate that biomass fuel types experience an increasing trend in exergy efficiency as gas turbine inlet temperatures (GTIT) increase similarly to energy efficiency (Et) as GTIT increases. However, exergy efficiencies are consistently lower than Et for both rice husk and bagasse. Since the fuels used have greater exergy content than energy content, this discrepancy is due to thermodynamic irreversibility’s present and system losses associated with the operation of the turbine generator set. More importantly, although rice husk has higher exergy efficiency than bagasse through all test conditions, the observed exergy efficiencies of both fuel types follow the same trend as average Et results. Therefore, exergy can be equally classified as an indicator of facility performance because it quantifies how effectively the facility utilizes available energy and minimizes thermodynamic losses incurred by operating conditions.

Effect of steam turbine inlet pressure on energy efficiency

The effect of STIP is evaluated by varying the pressure of superheated steam entering the steam turbine at state point 8 from 30 to 70 bar, while the condenser pressure was fixed at 0.06 bar. For the thermodynamic state-point table, a base-case STIP of 50 bar was selected. Accordingly, state point 8 represents the steam turbine inlet, state point 10 represents the turbine exhaust at condenser pressure, state point 11 represents condensate leaving the condenser, and state point 12 represents pump outlet/feedwater entering the HRSG at approximately the same pressure level as state point 8.

The relationship between energy efficiency vs. STIP can be seen in the following Fig. 8. The results indicate that increasing STIP results in lower energy efficiency, this is due mainly to the reduced steam mass flow rate produced within the HRSG at higher pressure levels compared to lower points. A lower amount of generated steam leads to lower power generation from the steam turbine, thus reducing overall cycle efficiency. Rice Husk operation at an STIP of 50 bar will generate an energy efficiency of 36.76%, while sugar cane bagasse operation will generate energy efficiency of 34.88%. This demonstrates the influence of operating pressure on system performance.

Effect of STIP on energy efficiency.

Effect of steam turbine inlet pressure on exergy efficiency

Figure 9, depicts the changes in exergy efficiency concerning STIP. As STIP increases from 30 to 70 bar, exergy efficiency decreases slightly for both biomass cases, following the same declining tendency observed for energy efficiency. For rice husk, exergy efficiency decreases from approximately 32.9–32.1%, while for sugarcane bagasse it decreases from approximately 30.9–30.1%. This confirms that the influence of STIP is comparatively weaker than that of GTIT, but higher steam pressure still reduces the useful steam-cycle contribution because of reduced steam generation and increased irreversibility in the HRSG–steam turbine section.

Effect of STIP on exergy efficiency.

The STIP trend should not be interpreted using the GTIT-based exergy-efficiency range. In the present model, GTIT directly increases the gas turbine work output and improves the quality of heat supplied to the HRSG, leading to a clear increase in exergy efficiency. In contrast, increasing STIP at fixed GTIT and condenser pressure reduces the effective steam mass flow generated in the HRSG and increases the temperature mismatch during heat recovery. Therefore, the steam turbine contribution decreases marginally, and the overall exergy efficiency shows a slight downward trend with STIP.

Exergy destruction of each component in the system

Exergy analysis measures exergy destruction and pinpoints the exact location within the overall energy loss of a system, whereas an energy analysis only uses the conservation of energy principle to determine energy losses. The availability of exergy allows for a better understanding of how to optimize design parameters and improve overall system performance through better knowledge of where and how much inefficiencies exist within a system. Figures 10 and 11 show the distribution of the exergy destruction within each component of the combined cycle system using rice husk and sugarcane bagasse as fuel. It can be seen that the combustion chamber has the largest percentage of exergy destruction (30.79% for rice husk and 32.31% for sugarcane bagasse). The reason for this is that the temperature difference between the combustion zone and the incoming air is large, and also that the combustion process is irreversible in nature. The HRSG represents the 2nd major source of exergy destruction and accounts for 19.48% of the exergy destroyed for rice husk and 16.55% of the exergy destroyed for sugarcane bagasse. Heat transfer across large temperature differences is the reason for the large entropy generation resulting in these losses. The gasifier represents the 3rd major source of exergy destruction with 9.95% of exergy destroyed for rice husk and 12.98% for sugarcane bagasse. The complex thermochemical reactions taking place during gasification, which include simultaneous oxidation and reduction processes, are responsible for the high level of exergy being destroyed by the gasifier. Overall, the system achieves exergy efficiency values of 32.62% for rice husk and 30.65% for sugarcane bagasse. Based on these results, the combustion chamber, HRSG and gasifier are the primary sources of irreversibility within the system. Therefore, any effort to reduce exergy destruction by improving the performance of these three components will have a significant impact on the overall performance of biomass-based power generation systems and promote sustainability through enhanced efficiency.

Exergy distribution in the combined power plant cycle

Figures 12 and 13 illustrate exergy distribution in the combined power plant cycle using rice husk and sugarcane bagasse. The useful work reaches 32.62% for rice husk and 30.65% for sugarcane bagasse. Further, exergy destruction for rice husk of about 63.85% whereas the maximum exergy destruction of about 65.7% for sugarcane bagasse. Exhaust exergy loss remains low (3.53–3.65%) when switching fuels from rice husk to sugarcane bagasse. Rice husk shows slightly lower total exergy destruction than sugarcane bagasse because of the combined influence of its fuel chemistry, higher biomass LHV, syngas composition, gasifier reaction characteristics, and heat-recovery behaviour within the BGCC configuration, rather than oxygen content alone. The difference in exergy destruction between rice husk and sugarcane bagasse cannot be attributed to elemental oxygen content alone. Exergy destruction in a BGCC plant is generated by several coupled mechanisms, including chemical irreversibility during gasification and combustion, heat transfer across finite temperature differences in the HRSG, pressure losses, moisture evaporation, ash-related inert loading, and the quality of syngas supplied to the combustor. Although sugarcane bagasse has higher oxygen content, the present results are more strongly linked to the complete thermodynamic behaviour of the fuel–gasifier–combustor–HRSG system. Rice husk has a higher biomass LHV, different CO–H₂–CH₄–CO₂ distribution, and lower gasifier exergy destruction, which improves the useful-work fraction of the cycle. Sugarcane bagasse, despite producing syngas with a comparable LHV, shows higher total exergy destruction because its fuel composition and product-gas distribution increase irreversibility in the gasifier and combustion chamber. Therefore, the lower exergy destruction for rice husk should be interpreted as a combined cycle-level effect rather than a single fuel-composition effect.

Sustainable concrete performance

Figure 14 shows the compressive strength values of concrete mixtures containing different proportions of RHA and SCBA. These results show that RHA has more pozzolanic activity than SCBA due to the fact that RHA contains more amorphous silica and has a finer particle size distribution. At 28 days, using RHA at replacement rates of 5%, 10%, and 15% increased the compressive strength of the concrete by 7.9%, 12.6%, and 3.9%, respectively. The increase in strength due to the addition of RHA is primarily attributable to the formation of additional C–S–H gel through secondary hydration reactions, which results in a denser microstructure and improved ITZ. The addition of SCBA resulted in increases in compressive strength of 5.8%, 8.2%, and 2.4% when used at 5%, 10%, and 15% replacement levels, respectively. Performance was significantly influenced by the amount of reactive silica contained within the pozzolans and the non-combusted carbon found in the SCBA; therefore, the use of these materials at lower rates, such as 5–10%, would be advantageous in terms of compressive strength without creating any major negative impacts on structural integrity. The significant increase in the compressive strength of RHA concrete has been attributed to both the high silica reactivity present in RHA as well as the large surface area available for surface area reactions between the cement particles and the reactive silica of RHA, which leads to the formation of additional C–S–H gel through pozzolanic reactions59,60.

Influence of incorporation of different dosage of ashes on the compressive strength of concrete.

In addition, the reactive silica contained within SCBA is known to react with the calcium hydroxide generated during the cement hydration process, producing additional binding thus aiding in strength development61–64. Improvements in volume density of concrete as a result of the use of SCBA as a partial replacement for cement are also significant factors leading to increased performance of concrete when used alone or in combination with RHA65.

Comparison and validation of present model with previous studies

To strengthen the reliability of the developed thermodynamic model, the validation was carried out in two stages. First, the predicted syngas composition was compared with independently reported experimental/literature gasification data under a comparable air/steam gasification basis. Second, the BGCC performance indicators, including energy efficiency, exergy efficiency, and total exergy destruction, were compared with the closest available thermodynamic study having similar cycle configuration and biomass feedstock basis. The operating conditions considered in the present model include a compressor pressure ratio of 12, GTIT of 1273–1473 K, condenser pressure of 0.06 bar, pinch point temperature of 30 K, steam turbine pressure range of 30–70 bar, and 85% isentropic efficiencies for compressor, gas turbine, steam turbine, and feed pump. These assumptions are provided in Online Appendix 2, while the biomass properties, syngas composition, and thermodynamic state-point data are reported in Online Appendices 3, 5, and 6. Therefore, validation is reported not only as numerical comparison but also through percentage error, mean absolute error, and RMSE (Table 1).

Conclusions

The energy and exergy analyses of the proposed combined-cycle power plant provide the impact and influence of key operating parameters on system performance and component irreversibility’s. A comparative assessment was conducted by varying the STIP and gas turbine inlet temperature. The results indicate that the gas turbine inlet conditions play a dominant role in determining overall plant efficiency. Among the considered biomass fuels, rice husk demonstrates superior performance compared to sugarcane bagasse, primarily due to the generation of higher-quality syngas. Additionally, the utilization of waste ash in green concrete contributes to enhanced environmental sustainability by promoting effective waste management and resource reuse.
