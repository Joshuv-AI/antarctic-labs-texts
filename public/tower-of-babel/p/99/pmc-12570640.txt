Comparative evaluation of hyaluronic acid and injectable platelet-rich fibrin in palatal wound healing: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Seval Ceylan Şen, Özlem Saraç Atagün, Gülbahar Ustaoğlu, Erkan Özcan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to compare the effects of locally applied hyaluronic acid (HA) and injectable platelet-rich fibrin (I-PRF) on patient morbidity, oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), and healing of palatal donor sites, palatal tissue consistency, complete epithelization (CE), and color match (CM).Materials and methodsSixty-seven patients undergoing free gingival grafting (FGG) were randomly assigned to three groups in an examiner-masked clinical trial. Test group 1 received a 0.8% HA gel-impregnated gelatin sponge, test group 2 received an I-PRF-impregnated sponge, and the control group received a sterile sponge. Pain and burning were assessed via a visual analog scale (VAS) on days 3, 7, 14, and 21. CM was evaluated on days 3, 7, 14, 21, 28, and at months 1 and 3. CE and Landry Wound Healing Index (WHI) were recorded on days 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28.ResultsTopical application of hyaluronic acid (HA) and injectable platelet-rich fibrin (I-PRF) significantly reduced postoperative discomfort and improved complete epithelization (CE) compared to the control group (p < 0.05). HA showed better outcomes than I-PRF in wound healing and morbidity, while I-PRF also significantly reduced postoperative pain (p < 0.05). Palatal tissue thickness at the 1st and 3rd months differed significantly among groups (p = 0.001), and OHRQoL scores decreased significantly from day 7 to day 14 in all groups (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe adjunctive use of HA or I-PRF with gelatin sponge is more effective in minimizing discomfort and enhancing healing at palatal donor sites than sponge use alone.Clinical relevanceOptimizing healing and minimizing patient discomfort at palatal donor sites are crucial for improving outcomes in free gingival grafting procedures. This study demonstrates that the adjunctive application of HA or I-PRF significantly enhances soft tissue healing and reduces postoperative morbidity. Particularly, HA was found to be more effective in promoting wound healing and comfort. These findings support the clinical use of HA or I-PRF as simple, biocompatible adjuncts to improve patient experience and surgical outcomes in periodontal procedures.Clinical trial numberNCT06867055https://www.ClinicalTrials.gov 03/04/2025‘Retrospectively registered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07183-9.Keywords: Gingival grafting, Platelet-rich fibrin, Hyaluronic acid, Pain, Wound healing

Introduction

Free gingival grafting (FGG) is regarded as the most employed mucogingival surgical technique for augmenting the width of keratinized gingiva, owing to its surgical simplicity, predictable clinical outcomes, and suitability for application over extensive areas [1]. Following FGG, the palatal donor site undergoes secondary intention healing, which can substantially influence postoperative recovery and the patient’s quality of life [1, 2]. Various products are utilized to enhance quality of life and accelerate wound healing, including (i) low-dose herbal products, (ii) laser treatments, (iii) platelet concentrates, (iv) hemostatic agents, (v) ozonated oil, (vi) antiseptics and antibacterial agents, (vii) hyaluronic acid, and (viii) bioactive materials Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a high-molecular-weight, naturally occurring polysaccharide that has gained increasing significance in the field of dentistry, in addition to its widespread applications across various biomedical disciplines [3]. HA and its derivatives have been investigated, either as monotherapies or in combination with various adjunctive agents, for their potential to enhance palatal wound healing by modulating proliferative activity, tissue remodeling, and angiogenic processes [1, 4]. Systematic reviews and clinical studies have further demonstrated that HA can accelerate periodontal wound healing, stabilize blood clots, and promote osteogenesis, thereby improving probing depth reduction and clinical attachment gain [5]. In oral and maxillofacial surgery, HA has been shown to reduce inflammation, enhance recovery after procedures such as implant placement and third molar extraction, and support bone regeneration [6]. It has also been utilized in the management of temporomandibular disorders, where intra-articular injections help decrease inflammation, enhance lubrication, and stimulate tissue repair [7]. Collectively, these findings underscore the broad therapeutic potential of HA across both periodontal and maxillofacial regenerative interventions, offering a robust biological and clinical rationale for its use.

Injectable platelet-rich fibrin (I-PRF) is an improved liquid formulation of traditional PRF. Recent studies have shown that I-PRF can induce higher TGF-β, PDGF release, as well as mRNA expression and a significant increase in type I collagen gene expression [8, 9]. Clinical studies have provided promising data indicating that I-PRF can be utilized in gingival recession surgeries, thickening thin gingiva, and accelerating healing in connective tissue surgeries and palatal wound healing [10–13].

Both HA and I-PRF have shown promising results in gingival tissue regeneration and wound healing, particularly in procedures involving palatal donor sites in FGG. Given these advancements, it is crucial to evaluate and compare the effects of these two treatment modalities on the post-surgical healing process [4, 13].

Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the effects of locally applied HA and I-PRF on patient morbidity, oral health-related quality of life, and palatal donor site wound healing, specifically with respect to tissue consistency, CE, and CM.

Material method

Study design and patient selection

This study was structured as a randomized, single-blind, parallel-group clinical trial and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Gulhane Training and Research Hospital (Approval No: 2023/87). The study was also registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06867055). Participants were selected from individuals referred for free gingival grafting (FGG) for gingival augmentation at the Department of Periodontology, Gulhane Faculty of Dentistry, University of Health Sciences, between May 2023 and April 2025. Procedures were explained to the participants, and all of them signed informed consent forms before participating in the study. All procedures performed in this study comply with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committees, as well as the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older, systemically healthy, not under medications that could interfere with healing, and without any history of coagulation disorders, strong gag reflexes, or hypersensitivity to anti-inflammatory drugs. Those with systemic diseases such as diabetes or hypertension, undergoing radiotherapy or chemotherapy, or with a history of smoking or alcohol use were excluded. Pregnant or lactating women, participants with inadequate oral hygiene, and those failing to comply with scheduled follow-up visits were also excluded.

Randomization and sample size

Before undergoing surgical procedures, all participants received professional oral hygiene instructions and, if necessary, scaling and root planning to ensure optimal oral health before surgery. They were then randomly allocated into three groups using a computer-generated randomization list created via the “List Randomizer” tool (https://www.random.org.lists).

. The sample size calculation was performed using the G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2) [14]. Based on the standardized effect size (f = 0.7375) calculated from the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain scores reported in a previous study [15], and considering a significance level of α = 0.05 with a statistical power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 20 participants per group. To compensate for an anticipated 30% attrition rate, the final sample size was increased to 26 participants per group.

Patients were unaware of their group assignments. During the study period, four patients from the control and HA (Gengigel) groups, and three from the I-PRF group, were excluded due to missed follow-up visits, resulting in 22, 22, and 23 participants in each group, respectively. Surgical procedures

Before surgery, an individual acrylic stent (Essix PLUS, Dentsply Sirona Deutschland, Germany) was made to protect the palatal area. All surgical procedures were performed by the same expert periodontologist (SCS).

The palatal sites were anesthetized using articaine hydrochloride 80 mg/2 ml + epinephrine bitartrate 0.02 mg/2 ml (Maxicaine Fort, Vem Ilac, Istanbul, Turkey). Immediately before surgery, palatal thickness (PT) was evaluated 5 mm apical to the mucosal margin in the donor area of the first or second premolar using a 15-endodontic reamer. The distance between the tip of the reamer and the rubber stopper was measured with a digital caliper (Absolute Solar Caliper Series 500, Mitutoyo, Aurora, IL.).

The graft size was determined using sterile aluminum foil templates matching the recipient area. FGG was harvested from the hard palate with the technique of Sullivan and Atkins [16], later modified by Miller [17]. The donor site extended from the distal aspect of the canine to the mesial aspect of the first molar, avoiding the greater palatine foramen and maintaining at least a 3 mm distance from the gingival margin to preserve anatomical integrity. Using the periodontal probe (PCP-UNC 15, Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL.), the length and height of the graft were measured to calculate the total area of the graft. The graft thickness was measured at the center of the graft during surgery using an endodontic reamer, a silicon stopper, and a caliper. The average graft thickness was 1.5–2 mm.

In the control group, a sterile gelatin sponge (Spongostan, Ethicon, USA) was sutured to the palatal donor site using 4/0 silk sutures (Sterisilk 4/0, SSM Medical Equipment, Turkey).

In the first test group, I-PRF was prepared by collecting peripheral blood into sterile 11-mL plain plastic tubes (16 × 100 mm; Beliver Industrial Estate, Plymouth, UK). The blood samples were centrifuged at 2700 RPM for 3 min (relative centrifugal force [RCF-max] = 509.53 × g) [10, 18] using a centrifuge with a rotor radius of 101 mm and a fixed angle (Nuve NF 200, Nuve Industrial Materials Manufacturing and Trade Company, Ankara, Turkey) at room temperature. The I-PRF at the top of the tube was collected using a syringe and transferred to a metal bowl, where it was allowed to polymerize for 20–25 min. In the I-PRF group, the freshly prepared I-PRF was immediately applied dropwise onto a sterile gelatin sponge (Spongostan, Ethicon, USA) using a sterile syringe and allowed to sit for 5 min. The I-PRF–impregnated sponge was then sutured onto the palatal donor site with 4/0 silk sutures (Sterisilk, SSM Medical Equipment, Türkiye).

In the HA group, a sterile gelatin sponge was thoroughly saturated with 0.8% polyvinylpyrrolidone sodium hyaluronate gel (Gengigel®, Prof, Ricerfarma SRL) for 5 min and sutured to the palatal donor site with 4/0 silk sutures.

Postoperative care

In all groups, the acrylic stent was placed in the relevant area for three days for postoperative protection. No antibacterial or antimicrobial agent was recommended for postoperative use. Only 500 mg of paracetamol-containing analgesics (Parol 500 mg, Atabay, Istanbul, Turkey) were prescribed. Patients were directed to consume only soft foods during the first week and to avoid any mechanical trauma. The upper teeth were not brushed until the acrylic stent was removed; following its removal, gentle brushing of the vestibular and palatal surfaces of the teeth was recommended.

Palatal sutures were removed after 7 days in all groups.

All healing data were evaluated by a single-blind expert periodontologist (OSA). Follow-up visits were scheduled on the 3rd, 7th, 14th, 21 st, and 28th days postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Skewness-Kurtosis analysis. Since the data followed normal distribution, parametric statistical methods were employed. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

To compare continuous measurements across the study groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. If a significant difference was identified, the Duncan post hoc test was applied to determine the specific groups responsible for the difference. For intra-group comparisons of OHRQoL scores between days 7 and 14, paired t-tests were used.

Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0 for Windows).

Discussion

The most challenging situation for patients after FGG surgery is the healing of the palatal donor site, which is left to secondary healing. Numerous clinical trials are investigating techniques to enhance palatal wound healing and improve patient comfort [13, 22, 26, 27]. Following graft harvesting, the open wound at the donor site typically requires two to four weeks to heal. Furthermore, it has been noted that palatal wound remodeling takes at least nine weeks to appear histologically complete [26]. Patients often experience significant discomfort during this period, particularly in the early stages of healing [27]. The morbidity associated with this process and delayed wound healing in the palate remains a significant concern.

Various dressing materials can be used to protect the wound in the palatal donor site, making it more comfortable, aiding the re-epithelialization process, and protecting the palatal connective tissue from physical and chemical insults as well as colonization by resident oral microorganisms [28]. This study compared the efficacy of HA and I-PRF impregnated with a collagen sponge for this purpose. We also evaluated the results by applying HA and I-PRF to the palatal area to accelerate healing in the palatal donor area, increase patient comfort, and reduce pain.

While numerous studies have evaluated wound healing in the palate following FGG, only a limited number have specifically investigated the efficacy of HA in this context. According to our results, HA application resulted in the most pronounced clinical improvement across all evaluated time points, including enhanced CE, improved tissue CM, and reduced patient-reported pain and burning sensation. The I-PRF group also demonstrated significant improvement compared to the control group, although less pronounced than HA, indicating its positive effect on palatal wound healing and patient comfort. When compared with the existing literature, Çankaya et al. reported no statistically significant difference in vascularization between the HA-treated group and the control, as assessed by laser Doppler flowmetry [3]. In contrast, Yildirim et al. found that topical HA application after FGG reduced postoperative pain and burning sensation and accelerated wound healing through increased epithelialization and color harmony [22]. Similarly, Alpan et al. observed that HA application for 14 days enhanced epithelialization and color matching compared to hypochlorous acid or flurbiprofen [1]. Regarding I-PRF, Kızıltoprak et al. reported that autologous fibrin glue stimulated palatal wound healing more effectively than I-PRF [13]. Our findings align with these studies in demonstrating the beneficial effects of HA and I-PRF on wound healing, while providing novel comparative data between the two treatment modalities.

The clinical procedure of palatal FGG retrieval is primarily challenging due to the need to obtain sufficient tissue while minimizing postoperative pain and reducing the risk of complications [29]. In this study, although there was no significant difference between groups in terms of VAS pain measurements during the first days postoperatively, patients in the I-PRF group required fewer analgesics on day five, suggesting a modest benefit in pain management. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the effectiveness of HA in controlling postoperative discomfort. Khalil et al. reported that HA significantly reduced postoperative pain after FGG in a randomized controlled trial without the use of painkillers [4]. Similarly, Ehab et al. found that topical application of 0.2% HA decreased palatal pain compared to the untreated control group [30]. Regarding I-PRF, Kızıltoprak et al. observed that pain VAS scores on the seventh day were significantly lower in patients treated with autologous fibrin glue compared to both the I-PRF and control groups [13]. Our results extend these observations by highlighting that I-PRF may reduce analgesic consumption, even if VAS scores are similar, indicating a potential clinical benefit in postoperative pain management.

Research in the field of periodontology suggests that 0.8% HA is more suitable for physicians to use in-office, while 0.2% HA is more suitable for patients to use at home [31]. In this study, we chose to apply 0.8% HA once to the palatal donor site immediately after FGG harvesting. This approach contrasts with Yıldırım et al., who applied a 0.2% HA gel and reported improved complete epithelialization (CE) at day 14 compared to a 0.8% HA gel; however, in their protocol, the periodontal dressing was removed on day 3, and HA was reapplied [22]. Our results demonstrate that a single in-office application of 0.8% HA is effective in promoting wound healing, supporting its practical use in clinical settings. After the FGG operation, wound healing of the donor site is crucial. Various agents such as stents, collagen gelatin scaffolds, absorbable gelatin sponge, oxidized cellulose, sterile gauze with external pressure, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), medicinal plant extract (MPE), platelet concentrates. Equine-derived collagen is recommended to protect the palate [29, 30, 32]. In this study, the absorbable gelatin sponge prolonged the efficacy of HA and I-PRF impregnated within it, while the acrylic stent protected against trauma. Ehab et al. reported that applying a topical dressing to a palatal wound can achieve higher local concentrations of healing-promoting, analgesic, and antiseptic agents while minimizing potential side effects or sensitization associated with systemic administration [30]. In this study, an acrylic stent (Sx plate) was applied for 3 days to protect the palate and maintain the applied materials at the relevant site for a more extended period. Additionally, a gelatin sponge was sutured to the donor site to ensure prolonged release of the applied substances and to control bleeding. Wessel et al. reported that patients with oxidized regenerated cellulose applied to the palate after FGG showed complete and rapid healing by day 21, whereas 40% of sites in the absorbable gelatin sponge group healed more slowly [33]. To avoid potential bias, the same treatment was applied to all groups, including the control group.

Patient-centered evaluations are essential for determining treatment success and patient comfort. Complications that reduce the patient’s quality of life can often occur after mucogingival surgeries, and the impact of these complications on quality of life varies depending on the type of wound healing associated with different periodontal surgical procedures, such as primary and secondary wound healing [34]. In this study, no statistically significant difference was observed between groups in the total scores of the OHRQoL questionnaire. However, all groups showed lower scores on day 14 compared to day 7, indicating an overall improvement in patient comfort over time. These findings align with literature. Şen et al. reported no significant difference between L-PRF and A-PRF groups during the first 7 days in their study comparing L-PRF and A-PRF after FGG. However, the PRF groups showed improvements in quality of life at 14 days, 1 month, and 6 months postoperatively [35]. Similarly, Tonetti et al. observed changes in OHIP-14 scores following plastic periodontal surgery, with an early increase followed by a return to baseline within the first two postoperative weeks [36]. Although consistent with previous studies, our results did not demonstrate the superiority of any treatment modality over others in terms of patient-reported comfort and quality of life.

In this study, CE, as indicated by the absence of H₂O₂ bubbling, was achieved by day 21 in the HA and I-PRF groups, whereas it was delayed until day 28 in the control group. Yıldırım et al. reported that complete epithelization was achieved by day 21 in the hyaluronic acid groups, whereas it was delayed until day 42 in the control group [22]. Lektemur Alpan et al. revealed that CE was achieved on day 21 in the HA group, whereas it was delayed until day 28 in the other groups [1]. Oliveira et al. demonstrated that CE showed no significant differences among the control, placebo, and test group treated with green tea extract and HA, indicating that the investigated protocol did not provide additional clinical healing benefits [15]. Collectively, the available evidence, including this study, suggests that HA generally accelerates CE compared to controls. However, some studies, such as that of Oliveira et al., reported no significant differences between groups.

The present study had some limitations. OHRQoL and VAS provide subjective evaluations of the effects of oral health on quality of life in both positive and negative domains, such as burning, pain, and chewing sensations, rather than an objective parameter. Although statistically significant differences in VAS scores were observed between the groups, these differences may have limited clinical relevance. Moreover, the use of a locally applied therapeutic agent by participants in the study groups might have contributed to lower VAS scores compared to the control group. Additionally, our study found no significant differences between the groups in responses to the OHRQoL survey. Another limitation of this study is the absence of histological examination, as obtaining a tissue sample from the donor site for this purpose could have negatively impacted the healing process. The interpretation and comparison of results may be influenced by factors such as patient compliance and the reliance on subjective professional evaluations of color match, rather than utilizing objective methods like digital color assessment. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. The use of acetate stents to protect the palatal donor site, which inherently reduces postoperative discomfort, may also have influenced patient-reported outcomes. Finally, the complete standardization of graft thickness was not achieved, which could affect the healing process and measured outcomes.

Conclusion

This randomized clinical trial demonstrated that both HA and I-PRF, when used adjunctively with a sterile gelatin sponge, effectively improved palatal donor site healing and reduced postoperative discomfort compared to the use of the sponge alone. However, HA consistently provided superior clinical outcomes compared to I-PRF, particularly in terms of wound healing parameters, epithelialization, and reduction of postoperative morbidity. While I-PRF contributed to a less painful postoperative period at specific time points, the overall findings favor HA as the more effective adjunctive material. These results align with previous studies that support the beneficial effects of HA in promoting wound healing and reducing patient morbidity, while also providing novel evidence on the comparative performance of I-PRF. Taken together, the findings highlight the clinical relevance of HA as a more advantageous option in palatal wound management, reinforcing its value within the broader context of regenerative periodontal and oral surgical interventions.
