TRIM59 alleviates neuronal ferroptosis and promotes functional recovery after spinal cord injury by mediating ubiquitination and degradation of ANXA2
Authors: Tao Hu, Wei Wang, Xuan Zhao, Peng Cui, Haojie Zhang, Zuoran Fan, Dongfan Wang, Xinli Hu, Haixia Huang, Xiaolong Chen, Yuluo Rong, Shibao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe central nervous system disorder for which effective therapeutic interventions remain limited. Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis is a key contributor to secondary neuronal damage following SCI, yet its upstream regulators—particularly those involving post-translational modifications such as ubiquitination—remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to determine whether the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM59 modulates neuronal ferroptosis and functional recovery after SCI, and to elucidate its molecular substrate and underlying mechanism of action.MethodsTRIM59 expression was modulated using lentiviral vectors in cultured neurons and adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) in mice. Functional recovery was assessed through the BMS, inclined plane test, footprint analysis, and motor evoked potentials. Ferroptosis was evaluated via biochemical assays, BODIPY C11 staining, ROS detection, TEM, and ferroptosis markers. Molecular interactions were analyzed by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), ubiquitination assays, and cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiment.ResultsTRIM59 expression was significantly downregulated during Erastin-induced ferroptosis in neurons and in spinal cord tissue during the acute phase (days 1–3) after SCI. Downregulation of TRIM59 exacerbated the hallmark features of ferroptosis and impaired motor recovery, whereas TRIM59 overexpression attenuated ferroptosis and promoted neurological restoration. Mechanistically, TRIM59 directly bound to ANXA2 and mediated its K48-linked polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. The neuroprotective effect of TRIM59 was abolished by a catalytically inactive C30A mutant or by ANXA2 overexpression, whereas ANXA2 knockdown rescued the ferroptosis and functional deficits induced by TRIM59 deficiency.ConclusionThis study identifies a novel TRIM59–ANXA2 regulatory axis that critically governs neuronal ferroptosis and functional recovery after SCI. TRIM59 functions as an endogenous suppressor of ferroptosis by targeting ANXA2 for K48-linked ubiquitin–proteasome–mediated degradation. These findings not only elucidate a key post-translational mechanism in SCI pathophysiology but also position TRIM59 as a promising therapeutic target for neuroprotection and functional restoration following SCI.The translational potential of this articleTargeting the TRIM59–ANXA2 pathway offers a promising therapeutic strategy to inhibit ferroptosis and promote neuroprotection and functional recovery after SCI. Enhancing TRIM59 activity or disrupting ANXA2 stability could pave the way for novel treatments in clinical neurotrauma.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) represents a catastrophic neurological condition that frequently leads to profound sensory and motor deficits [1]. Annually, at least 250,000 new SCI cases are reported globally [2]. While survival rates for patients with SCI have steadily improved in recent decades [3], the health burdens and economic costs associated with SCI continue to rise, presenting significant challenges in clinical practice [4]. In the absence of timely and adequate intervention, SCI may lead to persistent or irreversible dysfunction of the spinal cord [5]. Therefore, investigating the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying SCI, as well as identifying effective and clinically viable therapeutic strategies, is of paramount importance.

Ferroptosis represents a distinctive mode of regulated cell death that is mechanistically distinct from apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy [6,7]. This process is distinguished by iron-dependent intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and excessive lipid peroxidation, leading to fatal oxidative damage of cellular membranes and organelles [7,8]. Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis constitutes a predominant cell death mechanism across various disease models and contributes markedly to the pathogenesis of central nervous system (CNS) disorders, especially in traumatic SCI [9,10]. However, the molecular mechanisms governing ferroptosis in neurons, especially within the context of SCI, remain incompletely understood.

Post-translational modifications, including ubiquitination, protein degradation regulation, and membrane trafficking, play critical roles in cellular processes [11]. While the involvement of ferroptosis in secondary SCI pathogenesis is well-established, the upstream regulatory mechanisms, particularly those driven by post-translational modifications, remain poorly defined. The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) has been recognized as a pivotal regulator of cellular homeostasis, serving a critical function in modulating the stability of proteins that participate in cell death and survival processes [12,13]. Our team has previously conducted research in the field of SCI, focusing on ubiquitination and ferroptosis. We have demonstrated that USP11 regulates autophagy-dependent ferroptosis following spinal cord ischemia-reperfusion injury through deubiquitinating Beclin 1 [5]. Additionally, we found that Rg1-EVs play a critical role in neuronal protection and functional recovery after SCI by promoting MYCBP2-mediated ubiquitination of S100A9 [14]. Within the UPS, E3 ubiquitin ligases serve as molecular switches, conferring substrate specificity and regulating pathological processes. The tripartite motif (TRIM) family of E3 ubiquitin ligases has been implicated in diverse cellular functions, including innate immunity, autophagy, and carcinogenesis [15]. TRIM59, one component of this protein family, has gained research focus due to its role in inflammatory responses and cancer advancement [[16], [17], [18]]. However, its expression, function, and regulatory targets in the CNS, particularly in the context of traumatic injury and ferroptosis, remain unknown, highlighting a significant gap in understanding the ubiquitin-dependent regulation of neuronal death following SCI.

Identifying key driver proteins that directly mediate ferroptosis is essential for the development of targeted therapies. Annexin A2 (ANXA2), functioning as a protein that binds to calcium and phospholipids, serves as a crucial mediator in membrane-related activities, reactions to oxidative stress, and regulation of iron balance—mechanisms that exhibit strong connections to ferroptotic processes [19]. Increasing evidence suggests that ANXA2 can promote lipid peroxidation and sensitize cells to ferroptosis stimuli [20], positioning it as a potential positive regulator of ferroptosis. However, the regulatory mechanisms of ANXA2, particularly its ubiquitination in neurons, remain unclear. This study hypothesizes that a specific E3 ubiquitin ligase targets ANXA2 for degradation, serving as a critical brake on the ferroptosis cascade in neurons.

This study demonstrates that TRIM59 expression is markedly diminished in both Erastin-induced neuronal ferroptosis in vitro and in spinal cord tissue following SCI in vivo. Genetic knockdown of TRIM59 exacerbates ferroptosis and hinders functional recovery after SCI, whereas overexpression of TRIM59 provides neuroprotection by suppressing neuronal ferroptosis and enhancing neurological recovery. Mechanistically, ANXA2 serves as a novel substrate of TRIM59-mediated ubiquitination, with TRIM59 promoting K48-linked polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of ANXA2 (Graphical abstract). These findings unveil a previously unrecognized TRIM59–ANXA2 axis that critically regulates neuronal ferroptosis and functional recovery after SCI, offering fresh perspectives on how ubiquitination influences cell death mechanisms while suggesting promising therapeutic possibilities for neural protection.

Material and methods

Primary neuron and cell line culture

Primary neuronal cultures were prepared followed established protocols [5]. Briefly, Primary cortical neurons were isolated from embryonic day 16–18 (E16–E18) C57BL/6 mouse embryos, followed by the extraction of cerebral cortices subsequent to meningeal vasculature removal. Neural tissue underwent enzymatic processing with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution for dissociation. The resulting mixture was subjected to centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C) to generate a cellular suspension. Following cell quantification, the neurons were placed in poly-L-lysine-treated culture plates containing Neurobasal medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) enhanced with B27 (2%, Gibco Laboratories), glutamine (2 mM, Gibco), and penicillin-streptomycin (1%, Beyotime, China). Culture medium replacement occurred every 48 h at 50% volume. The HEK 293 T cell line, acquired from Procell Life Science and Technology Co. (CL-0005, Wuhan, China), was maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco, Waltham, MA) fortified with fetal bovine serum (10% FBS; SERANA, Brandenburg, Germany).

Spinal cord injury model

C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 weeks old) underwent SCI model establishment following previously documented protocols [14]. The investigation received approval from Xuanwu Hospital's Ethics Committee at Capital Medical University. The mice received initial isoflurane anesthesia followed by pentobarbital sodium administration intraperitoneally. The animals were positioned prone and secured on the operating platform for T10 vertebral identification. Following standard preparation, the surgical site underwent 75% ethanol sterilization and sterile drape placement. A longitudinal skin cut revealed the T10 vertebra after paraspinal muscle dissection. The surgical team executed a T10 laminectomy, removing vertebral components to access the spinal dural sac. The injury model utilized a weight-drop technique, delivering impact from a 5-g rod released 6 cm above the exposed dura. Antibiotic prophylaxis started preoperatively and continued for five postoperative days. Post-surgery bladder care involved twice-daily manual expression until spontaneous voiding function returned. The experimental protocol adhered strictly to institutional animal welfare guidelines.

Functional behavior analysis

Mice were maintained under a 12-h light-dark cycle with unlimited access to food and water. Before behavioral testing, all animals were permitted to acclimate to the experimental room or testing apparatus for 1 h.

Starting from the first day following SCI, neurological function was evaluated at predetermined time points utilizing the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) for locomotion.

The inclined plane test was conducted using a testing apparatus as described previously. The maximum angle at which a mouse could sustain its posture for at least 5 s was documented.

Footprint analysis was executed according to established methods. Forepaws and hindpaws were coated with blue and red dyes, respectively, and the mice were prompted to walk in a straight line across a sheet of white paper. The resulting footprints were examined to evaluate locomotor coordination recovery.

On day 28 post-injury, motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were evaluated using electromyography (EMG). A stimulating electrode was positioned on the exposed spinal cord rostral to the injury site, while recording electrodes were inserted into the gastrocnemius muscles. A reference electrode was placed in the tendon of the distal hindlimb muscle, and a ground electrode was positioned subcutaneously. Signal latency and amplitude were documented and analyzed to assess the functional integrity of the motor pathways.

Calcein-AM/PI staining

Neuronal cells were seeded into 24-well plates at an appropriate density. After experimental treatments, 250 μL of Calcein-AM/PI staining working solution (Beyotime, China) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Upon completion of incubation, samples were analyzed using a fluorescence microscope. Live cells exhibited green fluorescence (Calcein-AM), and dead cells displayed red fluorescence (PI). Cell viability and cytotoxicity were quantitatively assessed using ImageJ software.

CCK-8

Cells underwent placement in 96-well plates with 1 × 104 cells allocated per well. Following the specified treatments, fresh medium replaced the existing culture medium. The addition of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, China) occurred per the supplier's protocols, succeeded by a 3-h incubation period at 37 °C. A microplate reader measured the absorbance at 450 nm for individual wells to assess cell viability. Optical density values served as indicators of metabolic activity and relative cell viability.

Determination of GSH, MDA, and Fe2+ content

The concentrations of malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and iron ions (Fe2+) were measured using MDA (F01963, Westang, China), GSH (G02170, Westang, China), and iron (G01670, Westang, China) assay kits, respectively, following the manufacturer's protocols. For analysis of MDA, GSH, and Fe2+ levels in mouse spinal cord tissues, tissue samples from each experimental group were homogenized and processed accordingly. Homogenates were subjected to quantitative analysis using the respective assay kits, with absorbance measured via a microplate reader. MDA, GSH, and Fe2+ concentrations were determined by comparing the absorbance values to standard curves.

Measurement of unstable intracellular iron

Intracellular labile iron pool (Fe2+) levels were detected using the fluorescent probe SiRhoNox-1 (FerroFarRed, Goryo Chemical Japan), as previously described [21]. Cells were seeded onto confocal culture dishes and stained with SiRhoNox-1 (5 μM) for 1 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, imaging buffer replaced the culture medium. A confocal laser scanning microscope captured the fluorescence emissions, while ImageJ software facilitated the analysis of specific fluorescence signal intensities.

ROS detection

The measurement of cellular ROS concentration utilized a ROS Detection Kit (Beyotime, China). Following treatment protocols, DCFH-DA probe application proceeded in accordance with kit specifications, with samples maintained at 37 °C under dark conditions for 20 min. Subsequently, excess probe elimination occurred through thorough washing procedures. Flow cytometric analysis quantified the fluorescence signals representing ROS generation, and FlowJo software facilitated data analysis.

JC-1 measurement

Cells underwent seeding in 6-well plates, received one PBS rinse, followed by the addition of 1 mL culture medium per well. A volume of 1 mL JC-1 staining working solution (Beyotime, China) was introduced to individual wells and mixed with gentle agitation. The preparation underwent incubation at 37 °C within a humidified CO2 environment for 20 min. Following the incubation period, the staining solution was extracted, and wells received two rinses using 1 × JC-1 staining buffer. Fresh culture medium (2 mL) was introduced to each well. Red fluorescence emerges from JC-1 aggregates, suggesting elevated mitochondrial membrane potential, whereas green fluorescence indicates monomeric JC-1, characteristic of reduced membrane potential conditions. Fluorescence microscopy enabled quantitative analysis of the red-to-green fluorescence ratio, serving as a measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential.

Mitochondrial superoxide assay

Mitochondrial superoxide generation was detected utilizing MitoSOX Red reagent (Beyotime, China) serving as a fluorescent marker. The cultured cells underwent placement in 6-well plates followed by exposure to 5 mM MitoSOX solution under dark conditions at 37 °C for a 30-min duration. Following the exposure period, the cells received three rinses with pre-warmed PBS to eliminate residual probe. The resulting fluorescence underwent observation via fluorescence microscopy, where the intensity of red fluorescence indicated mitochondrial superoxide concentrations. Images were captured under identical exposure settings to allow comparative analysis of oxidative stress across experimental groups.

Lipid peroxidation assay

Measurement of lipid peroxidation, which indicates ferroptotic activity, was performed utilizing BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Beyotime, China) following standard protocols. The experimental procedure involved exposing cells to 2 μM BODIPY 581/591 C11, maintaining them at 37 °C for a 30-min duration. Subsequently, excess probe was eliminated through dual washing steps with cold sterile PBS. Microscopic examination of fluorescence signals occurred immediately afterward. The transformation from red fluorescence (indicating intact lipids) to green emission (representing oxidized state) in BODIPY 581/591 C11 enabled quantification of lipid peroxidation through analysis of green-to-red fluorescence intensity ratios.

DHE staining

Oxidative stress levels in the spinal cord were assessed through DHE staining. Briefly, freshly prepared frozen spinal cord sections were incubated in a humidified chamber with 2 μmol/L dihydroethidium (DHE; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the segments received washing treatment and were promptly examined via fluorescence microscopy. When superoxide interacts with DHE, it generates ethidium, which binds to DNA sequences and produces red fluorescent signals. The fluorescence intensity served as an indicator of ROS production.

TUNEL staining

Regarding TUNEL staining analysis, spinal cord tissue segments were obtained from mice at day 14 post-SCI. The tissue segments underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min, followed by thorough PBS cleaning, and membrane penetration using 0.3% Triton X-100 solution for 15 min. Following additional PBS cleansing, the segments were processed with TUNEL assay kit (Beyotime, China) per the supplier's protocols. A fluorescence microscope was utilized to acquire fluorescent images, while ImageJ software facilitated the unbiased counting of TUNEL-positive cellular structures across several representative visual fields per segment.

Mitochondrial morphology observed by TEM

The specimens underwent fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution overnight while maintained at 4 °C, followed by secondary fixation utilizing 1% osmium tetroxide for 60 min at ambient temperature. The materials underwent sequential dehydration via increasing ethanol concentrations, then received embedding within epoxy resin medium. An ultramicrotome was employed to generate ultrathin sections (85 nm thickness). For enhanced contrast, the prepared sections received staining with 2% lead citrate and uranyl acetate solutions. Ultrastructural examination at high magnification was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for image acquisition.

Immunofluorescence staining

Spinal cord specimens were obtained at distinct intervals following SCI and sectioned into 8-μm-thick cryostat slices. Neural components or tissue segments underwent fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by membrane permeabilization utilizing 0.3% Triton X-100, and blocking with 10% goat serum at ambient temperature for 60 min to reduce background staining. The samples were exposed to primary antibodies during overnight incubation at 4 °C (Table S1). Subsequently, the sections received secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 conjugates; 1:200; Shanghai Fushen Biotechnology) for 60 min at ambient temperature, then underwent nuclear staining with DAPI (Beyotime, China). A fluorescence microscope was utilized to capture images, and ImageJ software facilitated the quantitative assessment.

Western blotting analysis

Cell samples underwent protein extraction procedures on ice. Initially, cultured cells received ice-cold PBS washing before extraction with RIPA buffer. For tissue specimens, extraction involved adding lysis buffer (1 mL per 100 mg tissue) and mechanical homogenization. The extraction mixture remained on ice for 30 min to achieve complete cellular disruption. Subsequently, the lysates were placed in pre-cooled microcentrifuge tubes and subjected to centrifugation (12,000×g, 15 min, 4 °C). After centrifugation, the protein-containing supernatant was separated, while discarding the precipitate. Protein quantification utilized the BCA assay (Beyotime, China). Identical protein quantities (25 μg per sample) underwent separation via 10% SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad). The separated proteins were electroblotted onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (pore size: 0.2 μm; Millipore). The membranes underwent blocking in 5% non-fat milk dissolved in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 h at ambient temperature, followed by primary antibody exposure (Table S2) at 4 °C overnight. Post-washing, the membranes received horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Servicebio, 1:30,00) for 1 h at room temperature. Final washing preceded enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent application. Protein band visualization occurred through an imaging analysis system, with band intensities quantified via ImageJ software.

Reverse-transcription quantitative PCR

RNA isolation was conducted, followed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis utilizing SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Qiagen, USA) per the supplier's protocols. The expression values of target genes underwent normalization against actin, which served as the internal control gene, and the relative transcript levels were determined through the 2−ΔΔCt calculation method. The specific primer sequences employed for gene analysis can be found in the Supplementary Information (Table S3).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells underwent washing with chilled PBS followed by incubation in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000×g at 4 °C for 25 min, supernatants were extracted. A segment of the supernatant was set aside for conventional Western blot examination. For immunoprecipitation, the remaining extract was processed: magnetic beads containing protein A + G were combined with the specific antibody and mixed at ambient temperature for 15–60 min using a Flip mixer to ensure bead-antibody attachment. This preparation was introduced to the cellular extract and maintained at 4 °C overnight with continuous gentle rotation. Subsequently, magnetic separation isolated the beads, and the precipitated complexes underwent triple washing with 1× TBS to eliminate non-specific protein binding. The final protein complexes were extracted using SDS-PAGE loading buffer, heated to 95 °C for 5–10 min, and analyzed via Western blot for subsequent detection.

Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

Neuronal protein extracts underwent immunoprecipitation utilizing designated antibodies combined with protein A + G magnetic beads, following established protocols. The precipitated protein specimens were resolved through SDS-PAGE separation and subsequently stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to detect specific protein bands for mass spectrometry examination. Regions of interest from the gel were excised with a clean scalpel or rubber spatula, and the gel pieces were sent to OE Biotech Co., Ltd. For in-gel digestion and subsequent mass spectrometry analysis.

Plasmid and Lentivirus infection

Flag-labeled TRIM59, its C30A mutant form, and Myc-labeled ANXA2 were generated through the incorporation of their corresponding N-terminal Flag or Myc epitope sequences, supplied by Genebay Biotech. The plasmids containing HA-labeled ubiquitin K63 and K48 were obtained from Genebay Biotech. Lentiviral constructs for TRIM59 and ANXA2 suppression and expression (Lv-shTRIM59, Lv-TRIM59, Lv-shANXA2, and Lv-ANXA2) were engineered by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). A control lentiviral construct was created as a reference. Cell transfection was conducted utilizing Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) per the supplier's protocols.

Adeno-associated virus infection

To modulate the exogenous expression of TRIM59 and ANXA2 in vivo, AAV9 vectors harboring TRIM59 shRNA (AAV9-hSyn-shTRIM59), ANXA2 shRNA (AAV9-hSyn-shANXA2), TRIM59 overexpression (AAV9-hSyn-TRIM59), and their respective control vector (AAV9-hSyn-Scrambled) were constructed by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). After SCI, the mice underwent stereotaxic positioning, and viral particles (1 × 109 total) were administered into the spinal cord parenchyma at 0.25 μL/min.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses and graphical presentations were executed utilizing GraphPad Prism (version 10). Data are denoted as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For statistical evaluation between two groups, Student's t-test was applied, while one-way or two-way analysis of variance was used for comparisons among more than two groups. Following ANOVA application, Tukey's test was implemented for post hoc multiple comparisons to identify specific differences among groups. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05.

Results

TRIM59 is downregulated during ferroptosis in neuronal cells

Exposure of neuronal cells to the ferroptosis activator Erastin led to a marked decrease in cellular survival (Fig. S1A). Dead/Live cell staining showed a dose-dependent increase in neuronal cell death with escalating Erastin concentrations (Fig. S1B and C). Hallmark events of ferroptosis, such as Fe2+ accumulation, MDA elevation, GSH depletion, and lipid peroxidation, were observed following Erastin treatment. Specifically, levels of Fe2+, MDA, and lipid peroxidation increased, while GSH levels decreased (Fig. S1D–H). Additionally, Erastin administration caused a substantial rise in cellular ROS production (Fig. S1I and J). Protein analysis through Western blotting indicated that Erastin reduced GPX4 and FTH1 expression, while simultaneously enhancing ACSL4 and PTGS2 protein levels (Fig. S1K–O).

The molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis in neuronal cells were investigated through microarray screening, revealing notable TRIM59 suppression after ferroptosis activation (data not shown). Subsequent qPCR validation demonstrated significant reduction in TRIM59 transcription levels (Fig. S2A). Consistent with these findings, Western blot assessment indicated substantial decline in TRIM59 protein expression following Erastin administration (Fig. S2B and C). The localization of TRIM59 within neuronal cells was established through immunofluorescence visualization (Fig. S2D). The data indicates that TRIM59 reduction serves as a critical component in Erastin-mediated ferroptotic response within neuronal cells.

Ferroptosis occurs following SCI, accompanied by a significant downregulation of TRIM59 expression in vivo

Subsequently, the research examined the occurrence of ferroptosis following SCI. Analysis through Western blot demonstrated that GPX4 and FTH1 expression exhibited substantial reduction post-SCI, showing maximum decrease between days 1 and 3 after trauma. The expression patterns showed partial restoration from days 5 to 7, suggesting ferroptosis primarily manifests in the initial stage of SCI. Conversely, ACSL4 and PTGS2 expressions showed elevation from day 1 to 7 post-trauma (Fig. 1A–E). Additionally, measurements of GSH, MDA, and Fe2+ were conducted. The levels of MDA and Fe2+ reached their maximum on day 1 post-trauma, followed by progressive reduction, yet remained elevated compared to sham group values. GSH concentrations dropped to their minimum on day 1 post-SCI and gradually increased afterward, while staying below sham group levels (Fig. 1F–H). Examination through DHE staining indicated heightened fluorescence intensity within damaged spinal cord specimens (Fig. 1I–J), suggesting increased oxidative stress. In vivo, TRIM59 exhibits essential functions in modulating ferroptosis. Examination of TRIM59 expression at both transcriptional and translational levels following SCI demonstrated minimal expression during days 1–3 post-trauma, followed by progressive elevation, yet remained diminished compared to sham controls (Fig. 1M–O). Additionally, immunofluorescence analysis validated the substantial decrease in TRIM59 levels within the damaged spinal cord (Fig. 1P). The findings suggest that spinal cord trauma triggers neuronal ferroptosis while suppressing TRIM59 expression, pointing to TRIM59's potential involvement in mediating neuronal ferroptotic responses after SCI.

TRIM59 knockdown decreased cell viability, while its overexpression partially rescued this inhibitory effect (Fig. 2D–F). Furthermore, Lv-shTRIM59 exacerbated ferroptosis, whereas TRIM59 overexpression reversed these effects (Fig. 2G–M; Fig. S3F and G). Given the established link between ferroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction, mitochondrial functional changes induced by Erastin-triggered ferroptosis were further investigated in the presence of Lv-shTRIM59 or Lv-TRIM59. Lv-TRIM59 markedly diminished mitochondrial superoxide levels in neuronal cells, whereas Lv-shTRIM59 markedly increased them (Fig. 2O and P). Additionally, JC-1 staining demonstrated that TRIM59 overexpression markedly elevated mitochondrial membrane potential, while TRIM59 knockdown caused a pronounced decrease in membrane potential (Fig. 2Q and R). Erastin treatment led to the reduction or loss of mitochondrial cristae, as well as rupture and wrinkling of the outer mitochondrial membrane. TRIM59 overexpression partially reversed these morphological abnormalities, while TRIM59 knockdown exacerbated mitochondrial damage, with fewer cristae and more pronounced mitochondrial swelling (Fig. 2S). These results collectively suggest that TRIM59 overexpression restores mitochondrial function and suppresses oxidative stress-induced ferroptosis in vitro.

Next, to verify that TRIM59 exerts its protective effects by suppressing ferroptosis, we treated neuronal cells with Ferrostatin-1, a specific ferroptosis inhibitor. Ferrostatin-1 effectively attenuated ferroptosis events induced by Lv-shTRIM59 (Fig. S4A–C; Fig. S4G–O). In Dead/Live cell staining and CCK-8 viability assays, Ferrostatin-1 significantly reduced cell death (Fig. S4D–F). Regarding mitochondrial function, Ferrostatin-1 markedly decreased mitochondrial superoxide levels (Fig. S4P and Q), enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. S4R and S), and mitigated the loss of mitochondrial cristae and structural membrane damage (Fig. S4T). These results further substantiate that TRIM59 exerts its biological function primarily by inhibiting ferroptosis.

Overexpression of TRIM59 decreases ferroptosis and promotes functional recovery after SCI

To examine TRIM59's function in neurons after SCI, AAVs were employed for specific TRIM59 enhancement or reduction. Validation of transduction efficacy occurred through Western blotting and qPCR, establishing effective TRIM59 modulation (Fig. S5A–F). In addition, immunofluorescence identified the specific expression of AAV-TRIM59 in neuronal cells in the spinal cord (Fig. S5G). HE staining analysis of heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissue sections showed that no obvious abnormal signs were found in the SCI group compared with the AAV-TRIM59 group, proving that AAV-TRIM59 has good biological safety (Fig. S5H). Protein analysis indicated that AAV-TRIM59-administered mice displayed notably elevated GPX4 and FTH1, alongside decreased ACSL4 and PTGS2 expression (Fig. 3A and B; Fig. S6A–E). Assessment of Fe2+, MDA, and GSH concentrations followed. Administration of AAV-shTRIM59 led to reduced GSH alongside elevated Fe2+ and MDA, while AAV-TRIM59 delivery produced opposite effects (Fig. 3C–E). These observations suggest TRIM59's inhibitory effect on ferroptosis post-SCI. TRIM59 promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of ANXA2

The effect of TRIM59 silencing on ANXA2 expression levels in neuronal cells was investigated. Compared to Lv-shnc, TRIM59 knockdown increased ANXA2 protein levels, but had no impact on its mRNA expression (Fig. S8A–C). This suggests that TRIM59 regulates ANXA2 not at the transcriptional level, but rather through post-translational modulation of ANXA2 protein stability. Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 further elevated ANXA2 protein levels, which were already increased due to TRIM59 knockdown (Fig. 5A), supporting the role of TRIM59 in the proteasomal degradation of ANXA2. To determine whether the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of TRIM59 is necessary for ANXA2 regulation, the effects of wild-type (WT) TRIM59 and a catalytically inactive C30A mutant were examined. Overexpression of WT TRIM59 markedly diminished ANXA2 protein levels, while the C30A mutant failed to do so (Fig. 5B), indicating that TRIM59's ubiquitin ligase activity is essential for ANXA2 degradation. To assess the impact on protein stability, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays were performed. Overexpression of TRIM59 accelerated ANXA2 degradation, whereas TRIM59 knockdown markedly prolonged its half-life (Fig. 5C–F), confirming that TRIM59 promotes the proteasome-dependent destabilization of ANXA2. TRIM59 silencing markedly diminished ANXA2 ubiquitination levels while increasing its steady-state protein abundance relative to Lv-shnc (Fig. 5G and H). Discussion

SCI triggers a cascade of secondary pathological events that markedly exacerbate tissue damage and hinder functional recovery [22]. Among these events, regulated cell death pathways—particularly ferroptosis—have emerged as critical contributors to neuronal loss [23,24]. Ferroptosis, distinguished by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, has been associated with various neurodegenerative and traumatic conditions [[25], [26], [27]]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms governing ferroptosis in the context of SCI remain incompletely understood.

Our study establishes ferroptosis as a pivotal pathogenic event during the early phase of SCI, confirming and extending recent findings in the field [26,28,29]. A key unresolved question has been the identification of the upstream molecular regulators that control this process. In this study, TRIM59, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, was detected as a critical suppressor of neuronal ferroptosis and a key promoter of neurological recovery following SCI. The temporal correlation between the downregulation of TRIM59 and the peak of ferroptosis activity in vivo provides a strong indication of its functional involvement, which was rigorously validated through gain- and loss-of-function experiments in both cultured neurons and animal models. Our findings reveal a novel TRIM59–ANXA2 regulatory axis that modulates neuronal ferroptosis via ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, offering new mechanistic insights into SCI and identifying a potential therapeutic target for its treatment.

Growing evidence highlights TRIM59's critical role in various biological processes, including tumorigenesis [30], apoptosis [31], ferroptosis [32], and DNA damage repair [33]. Recently, Chen et al. [34] documented that TRIM59-mediated ferroptosis facilitates neuroblastoma development and chemosensitivity via p53 ubiquitination and degradation. Therefore, TRIM59 may control iron metabolism through post-translational modifications of essential proteins participating in the iron metabolic pathway. While TRIM59 has been studied extensively in oncology, its role in the CNS, particularly in neurotrauma, represents a novel and significant finding. The context-dependent function of E3 ligases is well-recognized; a protein that acts as an oncogene in one tissue can serve a protective role in another. Our discovery of TRIM59's neuroprotective role challenges the notion that it is solely associated with cancer biology and emphasizes the importance of investigating protein functions in disease- and tissue-specific contexts. The dramatic downregulation of TRIM59 following injury may itself be maladaptive, exacerbating neuronal loss, making the restoration of TRIM59 function a promising therapeutic strategy. Microarray analysis revealed that TRIM59 expression is downregulated during ferroptosis in neuronal cells. To validate this, this study examined TRIM59 expression in vivo and found that both mRNA and protein levels of TRIM59 were markedly reduced after SCI. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that TRIM59 is predominantly expressed in neuronal cells, suggesting its potential involvement in neuronal injury. Accordingly, overexpression of TRIM59 markedly suppressed ferroptosis and promoted functional recovery, while TRIM59 knockdown produced the opposite effects. Furthermore, our results demonstrate a close association between SCI and ferroptosis. SCI is a complex pathological process involving multiple modes of cell death, but the coordination and interplay among these pathways require further investigation.

The most significant mechanistic insight from our work is the identification of ANXA2 as a bona fide substrate of TRIM59-mediated ubiquitination and degradation. This discovery bridges the gap between the E3 ligase activity of TRIM59 and the execution of ferroptosis. ANXA2 has recently emerged as a positive regulator of ferroptosis in other contexts, and our data are fully consistent with this role in neurons. Luo et al. identified ANXA2 as a central hub in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and highlighted its potential to promote ferroptosis in HepG2 cells [35]. Furthermore, Yu et al. demonstrated that ANXA2, through its efficient miRNA loading capacity, enables engineered extracellular vesicles (iEV-150) to carry high levels of miR-150-3p, which can be delivered to tumor cells to trigger a robust ferroptosis program [36]. These findings highlight ANXA2's emerging role as a key modulator of ferroptosis across diverse disease contexts. Consistent with prior studies, TRIM59 exerts protective effects against neuronal ferroptosis, at least partially through the regulation of ANXA2. Our Co-IP results confirm a physical interaction between TRIM59 and ANXA2 in neuronal cells. To further investigate this regulatory relationship, ANXA2 mRNA and protein levels were examined following TRIM59 knockdown. Interestingly, downregulation of TRIM59 markedly elevated ANXA2 protein levels without affecting its mRNA expression, suggesting that post-translational mechanisms are crucial in modulating ANXA2 stability. ANXA2 has been widely reported to be regulated by the UPS in various disease models [37,38]. Notably, a previous study revealed that TRIM65 promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of ANXA2, enhancing the invasiveness of bladder urothelial carcinoma cells [39]. In the present study, TRIM59 stably interacts with ANXA2 and mediates its K48-linked polyubiquitination, leading to proteasomal degradation and subsequent reduction of ANXA2 levels in both neuronal cells and injured spinal cord tissue. The increased binding between TRIM59 and ANXA2 under ferroptosis conditions suggests a dynamic and potentially feedback-driven relationship—possibly, the cell attempts to upregulate TRIM59-mediated degradation of ANXA2 to counteract stress, but this compensatory mechanism is overwhelmed by injury-induced TRIM59 downregulation. Notably, depletion of ANXA2 both in vitro and in vivo effectively reversed the exacerbated ferroptosis induced by TRIM59 deficiency, indicating that ANXA2 stability is vital for TRIM59-mediated modulation of neuronal ferroptosis. Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that TRIM59 modulates neuronal ferroptosis by directly controlling the stability of ANXA2 through ubiquitin-dependent proteolysis.

Despite these advances, several limitations in our study suggest important directions for future research. First, the upstream signals that cause the downregulation of TRIM59 expression following SCI remain unclear. Identifying whether transcriptional repression, mRNA instability, or protein degradation pathways are involved will provide a more comprehensive understanding of this regulatory axis. Second, while this study focused on neuronal ferroptosis, the potential role of the TRIM59-ANXA2 pathway in other CNS cell types, such as astrocytes and microglia, which also contribute to SCI pathology and undergo ferroptosis, requires further investigation. Third, the development of small-molecule agonists that can enhance TRIM59's E3 ligase activity or disrupt the TRIM59-ANXA2 interaction would be a critical step toward translating these findings into therapeutic applications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our work identified a previously unrecognized TRIM59-ANXA2 molecular axis that is both necessary and sufficient for regulating neuronal ferroptosis after SCI. This study presents compelling evidence that TRIM59 acts as a crucial neuroprotective E3 ubiquitin ligase, targeting pro-ferroptosis ANXA2 for proteasomal degradation via K48-linked polyubiquitination. This study not only advances our mechanistic understanding of ubiquitin-dependent regulation of cell death in the CNS but also positions TRIM59 as a promising therapeutic target and ANXA2 as a key biomarker for mitigating secondary damage and promoting recovery after SCI. Modulating this axis could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for SCI and other neurological disorders where ferroptosis is implicated.
