Change in Nutritional Status of Children in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit over First 14 Days: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Morik Loyi, Devendra Mishra, Urmila Jhamb, Romit Saxena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsWe studied changes in nutritional status from baseline, as measured by serial skinfold thickness measurements and their association with patient factors, disease factors, and biochemical factors, as well as time to the start of enteral feeds.Patients and methodsWe enrolled 50 consecutive children aged 2–5 years from the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) of a public sector hospital in India between 14 February, 2024 and 31 August, 2024. Baseline demographic and clinical data were recorded. Serial anthropometric parameters were measured for all children at the time of admission (+4 hours), day 3, day 7, and day 14 or at hospital discharge, if earlier. We used the World Health Organization (WHO) growth charts to interpret the anthropometric data.ResultsOf the 50 children enrolled (mean age 3.17 ± 1.22 years; 46% females), 58% were underweight, 52% had wasting, and 52% had short stature at admission. Only 10% children (n = 5) had triceps skinfold thickness for age <−2 standard deviation (SD) at baseline. 32 (64%) of the children demonstrated a reduction in triceps skinfold thickness, 72% (n = 36) experienced weight loss, and 58% (n = 29) showed a decrease in mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC). However, we did not find a statistically significant association of change in triceps skinfold thickness with age, severity of illness, outcome, duration of stay, and early start of enteral feeds.ConclusionThis study shows the feasibility of skinfold calipers as a serial monitoring tool in the PICU. Nutritional status worsens during the initial 2 weeks of PICU stay, despite early enteral feeding policies.Keywords: Critical illness, Enteral feeding, Malnutrition, Outcome, Triceps skinfold thickness, Wasting

Introduction

Clinicians and parents who look after critically sick children often notice that children tend to lose weight during an illness, especially critically ill intubated and ventilated children. Unplanned weight loss may result in an increase in complications as bed sore development.1,2 This anthropometric decline is due to prolonged metabolic stress response, which results in muscle breakdown. Protein and energy deficits accumulate and manifest as loss of weight and lean body mass over the course of prolonged PICU stay.3–5 However, few tools are available for serial anthropometric monitoring in bed-bound children in resource-limited settings. This paucity is compounded by the peculiarity of physical, lean mass, and anthropometric attributes of children with Southeast Asian ancestry, which necessitates local data generation.6 Weight as a screening anthropometric tool is difficult in unconscious children. Given the ease of skinfold thickness use and its application in various programs across the world, we undertook a pragmatic trial to ascertain if serial skinfold thickness measurement can be a feasible tool for assessing change in anthropometric assessment during PICU stay.7–9

Patients and Methods

This descriptive longitudinal study was initiated after obtaining institutional ethics committee clearance. We enrolled consecutive children aged between 2 and 5 years who were transferred to the PICU at our hospital between 14 February, 2024 and 31 August, 2024. The inclusion criterion was admission to the PICU for an acute illness (onset <14 days), with an anticipated stay of at least 7 days. Children who died, those who were transferred out of PICU to ward, or those who were discharged within 72 hours of transfer in, were excluded. We also excluded children transferred for surgical procedures or post-surgical care; those diagnosed with chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, or diseases associated with malabsorption (cystic fibrosis, celiac disease, etc.); and those with an intestinal disease, intestinal trauma, or intestinal surgery (colostomy, gastrostomy, tracheoesophageal fistula, etc.) or such a surgery during their stay. Additionally, we excluded children in whom skinfold thickness measurement was not possible (e.g., burn injury).

The primary outcome variable was the change in the nutritional status at discharge or day 14 of stay, whichever is earlier, as compared to that at admission assessed by one or more anthropometric parameters viz., triceps skinfold thickness, weight, length, and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC). Secondary outcome variables included the association of patient factors (age and sex), disease factors (primary disease, reason for PICU admission), and biochemical factors (serum albumin, hemoglobin, kidney function tests, and liver function tests) with the change in nutritional status, and the association of change in nutritional status with the day of starting enteral feeds (full or partial).

Hulst et al. noted a change in nutritional status in 51% of children with a PICU stay of at least 7 days.5 To identify a similar proportion of PICU patients, we calculated the sample size with the StatCalc app of the EpiInfo program, for a 10% absolute precision, 80% power, and 95% confidence. The sample size calculated was 96. With an estimated 5–8 children in this age-group admitted every month in the PICU, we planned to enroll 50 children in the study due to the constraints of time available.

All eligible children were enrolled in the study within 18 hours of transfer to the PICU, after taking informed written consent from one of the parents. Basic demographic details and diagnosis were recorded in a pre-tested structured form. Baseline data of anthropometric parameters like weight, height/length, triceps skinfold thickness, MUAC, and weight for height/length were recorded at enrollment. All the measurements were done by a single trained investigator.

For measuring triceps skinfold thickness, the mid-point of the line connecting the highest edge of the posterior border of the acromion process, located at the top of the shoulder, to the tip of the olecranon process in the elbow, along the back portion of the arm, was carefully identified and marked using a pen. Following this, a small fold of skin and underlying subcutaneous fat tissue was gently pinched using the thumb and index finger, positioned approximately 2 cm above the marked midpoint to ensure accurate placement, with the skinfold held parallel to the arm's long axis. Once the skinfold was properly grasped, the jaws of a skinfold body caliper (Cescorf Ltd, Brazil) were precisely aligned over the marked point, perpendicular to the length of the fold, to obtain a reliable measurement of the triceps skinfold thickness, which was recorded to the nearest mm for accuracy. This measurement was repeated for three consecutive readings in the same sitting, and an average of these readings was calculated and documented for consistent results. Triceps skinfold thickness for age was recorded according to the World Health Organization (WHO) growth charts.7

Mid-upper arm circumference was measured at the midpoint of the line joining the angle of the acromion process and the lateral epicondyle. A non-shrinkable, non-stretchable, flexible tape was placed flat around the arm while ensuring that the underlying tissue was not compressed. The two ends of the overlapping tape were pulled together so that the zero end was held below the measurement value. The measurement was taken on the lateral aspect of the arm and was recorded in mm. These were then interpreted according to the WHO growth charts.10

Weight and length/height were measured using standard methodology, if feasible, and interpreted according to WHO growth charts.11,12 All the anthropometric parameters were repeated on day 3, day 7, and day 14 of stay (or at discharge, if earlier). In cases of mortality before the completion of 14 days of stay, the last recorded anthropometric parameter was used for analysis.

Along with this, the time and date of starting partial enteral feeds (<80% of total energy requirement) and full enteral feeds (>80% of total energy requirement) were also noted.13 Information about biochemical parameters like kidney function tests (blood urea, serum creatinine, serum electrolytes), liver function tests (serum bilirubin, serum glutamic oxaloacetate transaminase, serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase, total protein, serum albumin), and complete blood count were also recorded. All participants were followed daily till PICU stay.

In our PICU, we aim to start nasogastric feeds for critically ill patients as soon as possible, as per the clinical condition of the child. The feeding is predominantly two-hourly intermittent feeds; continuous feeding is only used for selected indications. Thereafter, there is nurse-led serial monitoring for feeding intolerance in terms of gastric residual volume >50% of the volume administered in the previous 4 hours, an increase in abdominal girth, presence of bile and blood in pre-feed aspiration. Daily monitoring of feeds in terms of volume of feeds (in mL/kg as per the Holiday–Segar formula), calories (kcal/kg as per the Holiday–Segar formula), and proteins, as per tolerance of feeds. Children are started on predominantly milk-based feeds, supplemented by multivitamins as needed. After stabilization of the patient, feeds are usually diversified by adding other sources of proteins, such as lentils, soup, etc., calculating carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids, in keeping with the protein-sparing effect. The feeds are titrated to 80–100% of dietary requirement in terms of fluids; further adjustment is usually done by sequential daily fluid overload status and daily fluid output percentage. Daily calories and proteins are titrated to 80–100 kcal/kg and 2–3 gm/kg proteins, respectively. These details are monitored serially in daily monitoring charts and titrated on a daily basis. This same policy was followed during the course of this study.

Data entry was performed using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet by a single researcher, with manual rechecking of 20% entries by another researcher. Interpretation of anthropometric measurements was done using the Anthropometric Calculator app based on the WHO growth chart. Data analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel for descriptive data and SPSS 26.0 for comparisons. Qualitative data were summarized as frequency (%) and quantitative variables as mean [standard deviation (SD)] or median (interquartile range: IQR). The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test. The difference between admission anthropometric measurements and day 14 measurements was compared with patient, biochemical, and disease-related factors using a paired t-test or a Wilcoxon signed rank test. For all tests, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 50 children [mean age 3.17 (1.22) years; 46% females] were enrolled (Fig. 1). The majority of the children enrolled in the study (n = 29, 58%) were found to be underweight (<−2 SD weight for age). Short stature (length for age < 2 SD) and wasting (weight for height/length <−2 SD) were found in 52% each in the study population. Mid-upper arm circumference <−2 SD was also seen in 52% (n = 26) of the enrolled children, and 10% (n = 5) of children had triceps skinfold thickness for age <−2 SD. A statistically significant association was not found between the patient's sex (p = 0.460) or the patient's age (p = 0.419) with the change in triceps skinfold thickness. This relationship was also not statistically significant (p = 0.397, p = 0.172) for those who were evaluated at 14 days.

No statistically significant association was seen between change in triceps skinfold thickness and primary organ system involved (p = 0.181 and p = 0.148, respectively), pediatric index of mortality 2 scoring system (PIM2) severity score (p = 0.571 and p = 0.465, respectively) and reason of ICU admission (p = 0.587 and p = 0.964, respectively), for the whole group and children evaluated at day 14.

We did not find a statistically significant relationship between change in triceps skinfold thickness during stay and either anemia (hemoglobin <11 gm/dL) (p = 0.830) or low serum albumin (<2.5 gm/dL) (p = 0.136) at presentation. Similarly, no statistically significant association was found between changes in triceps skinfold thickness and anemia (p = 0.363), hypoalbuminemia (p = 0.402) measured at day 14 of stay.

There was no statistically significant association seen between time of start of enteral feeds (early <48 hours and late >48 hours) and change in triceps skinfold thickness (p = 0.496) for the full group or at day 14 of stay (p = 0.264). No statistically significant association was observed between outcome (death vs discharge/transfer) and change in triceps skinfold thickness (p = 0.725).

Additional analyses for the association of change in triceps skinfold thickness with various biochemical and hematological parameters did not reveal any significant association.

Discussion

In this longitudinal descriptive study involving 50 consecutive children aged 2–5 years in the PICU, a reduction in triceps skinfold thickness was seen in 64% children, 72% experienced weight loss, and 58% showed a decrease in MUAC within the first 14 days of stay. None of the patient- or disease-related factors studied were found to have a statistically significant association with change in nutritional status.

Skinfold calipers are a relatively accurate, reproducible, mobile, inexpensive, safe, and feasible strategy in the PICU for monitoring serial nutritional status in PICU, with good inter-rater and intra-rater reliability.14–20 We have found it to be a very feasible tool for monitoring of serial anthropometric status in our PICU, and it is being used for serial nutrition assessment in the PICU. We found that the skill of using them has a steep learning curve, and we have been imparting training to further PICU doctors, based on nano-learning-based tools developed by the investigators. In our study, we could not do skinfold thickness at other sites, such as subscapular and supra-iliac skinfold thickness, due to a lack of uniform access in intubated patients. Though there are new ultrasound-guided tools for assessment of quadriceps thickness and rectus femoris cross-sectional area, skinfold thickness measurement can be serially done across low-resource settings.3

Anthropometric change over the course of stay: In the acute illness, there exists a catabolic adaptive response, whereby endogenous nutrients are diverted away from the muscle to provide energy and free amino acids. This is hypothesized to result from induction of atrogenes by FOXO transcription factor and ubiquitin–proteasome complex (UPS)-mediated proteolysis, resulting in autophagy, compounded by muscle protein loss due to bioenergetic failure.4,21–25 Immobilization, disturbed metabolism, and inflammation compound muscle loss in critical illness.25 In spite of a standardized protocol of PICU nutrition, we observed that there was still a drop in anthropometric parameters, though the drop in the parameters was not significant.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study had several limitations, primarily due to the small sample size, which was due to the limitations of time. We used a narrow age range of 2–5 years, due to this group being a critical period for a child's growth and development, with nutritional deficiency during this age being associated with wasting and stunting.26 Many participants were discharged or died prior to the first planned assessment at day 14, thereby leading to a heterogeneous time of evaluation of the outcome data. Moreover, newer calorimetric methods to document nutrient intake during PICU stay and muscle mass assessment to document sarcopenia are now available.27 We, however, did not have access to the same, and therefore used a more widely available and easily performed anthropometric assessment method. However, despite these limitations, our study is one of the first prospective study which has documented a change in nutritional status in the Indian setting, which has used standardized anthropometric measures (including triceps skinfold thickness) and documented the nutritional status both at baseline and on follow-up.

Conclusion

The results of this small single-center prospective study demonstrated a significant fall in nutritional status in the majority of children in the PICU after 2 weeks of stay. Future studies may try to document the rate of fall of change over time and response to various PICU nutritional care bundles in mitigating this change. Researchers may also try to delineate additional baseline biochemical or disease markers that may identify children at risk of worsening nutritional status in the PICU. International guidelines for nutritional care of children in PICU have been published recently, and the need for Indian guidelines is also underscored.

Clinical Significance

Monitoring nutrition status is very important in critically ill children, but it is often ignored due to a lack of adequate methods of serial monitoring. This study highlights the feasibility of using skinfold calipers in serial monitoring of nutritional status in the PICU setting. It also highlights the factors impacting the change in triceps skinfold thickness over the first 2 weeks of PICU stay. Given the steep learning curve and reproducibility of skinfold thickness measurement, it can be used widely for serial nutritional monitoring in children.
