Incidence, Complications, and Quality of Life in Adults ≥ 60 Years Old With Laboratory‐Confirmed RSV Infections Attended in Primary Care and in Hospitals: A Study Over Three Seasons in Valladolid, Spain

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection in adults ≥ 60 years significantly affects health, particularly in those with comorbidities. Despite evidence on disease burden, data on quality of life (QoL) and complications after infection remain scarce. This study describes the incidence and prevalence of laboratory‐confirmed RSV infections over three consecutive seasons, as well as related complications and QoL in primary care and hospitalized patients.MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational study during the 2022–2023, 2023–2024, and 2024–2025 seasons in three primary care centers and two hospitals in Valladolid, Spain. Patients ≥ 60 years with acute respiratory infection (ARI) and laboratory‐confirmed RSV were included. Follow‐up was performed at 90 days to assess complications and at 30 days to evaluate QoL.ResultsOf 973 primary care patients, 477 were tested and 113 (23.9%) were RSV‐positive. In hospitals, 956 cases were screened and 172 included. RSV incidence in primary care was 8.4, 34.9, and 29.2/1000 inhabitants for the three respective seasons. The most frequent complications were new ARI (23.0%) in primary care and viral/bacterial co‐infection (31.4%) in hospitalized patients. Hospitalization occurred in 7.1% of primary care cases, 15.1% of inpatients were readmitted, 4.7% admitted to ICU, and 3.5% died. Worsening of prior conditions occurred in 81.6% with COPD, 67.7% with asthma, and 31.1% with cardiac disease. QoL was impaired in > 50% during the acute phase, improving by 30 days but persisting in > 30%.ConclusionsRSV infection in adults ≥ 60 years carries a considerable burden, with frequent co‐infections, disease decompensation, and sustained QoL impairment despite partial recovery.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection in people ≥ 60 years old is a clinical entity for which there is a need for further scientific evidence to support the implementation of preventive strategies, clinical decisions, and public health measures. RSV infections disproportionately affect the elderly and those with preexisting comorbidities, as well as very young children. Many efforts have been dedicated to studying the burden of disease, as well as the characteristics of RSV infection in infants and toddlers. However, 75% of RSV deaths occur in people over 65 years old across many Western countries [1, 2].

The estimated burden of disease caused by RSV infection in ≥ 60‐year‐olds varies depending on the study, country, and clinical setting, for reasons including differences in methodology and limitations in RSV testing. Based on recent publications, RSV is the fifth most common cause of community‐acquired pneumonia in adults [3]. In addition, RSV is responsible for approximately 4.6%–7.8% of all respiratory illnesses in the elderly every year, with incidences in primary care of 6.7 cases/1000 people and over 23.2 cases/1000 people in hospital settings [4, 5, 6, 7]. There is evidence that RSV causes around 759,000 hospitalizations per year in the elderly worldwide, with a hospitalization rate of around 21–402 cases/100,000 inhabitants, depending on the country [8, 9, 10, 11].

RSV infection is especially severe in the most vulnerable people. Some studies highlight that the risk of death from RSV in patients with chronic respiratory illness, immunodeficiency, and cardiovascular disease is roughly 3%–5% [12]. Others reveal mortality is up to 15‐fold higher in those 65 years or older and those aged 50–64 years, compared with those aged 18–59 years, and particularly high in those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and heart failure (HF) [13]. People living in long‐term care facilities are also at an increased risk, with estimates suggesting that RSV is responsible for 17 out of every 1000 deaths [14].

In contrast, there is limited evidence regarding RSV complications impacting ≥ 60‐year‐old people. Some authors have pointed out that on some occasions, the RSV infection in the elderly requires hospitalization and, in some cases, critical care and the use of mechanical ventilation [15], triggering cardiovascular decompensations like HF [16], COPD exacerbations [17], and, in some cases, death [18].

RSV can also impact the quality of life (QoL) of people who experience an infection. However, little is known about the magnitude of this impact and about how patients ≥ 60 years old recover after an infection. Some studies have shown that RSV can cause an appreciable impact on daily activities, social activities, and relationships, with one study showing physical function impairment in more than 80% of patients of this age group [19]. Also, more than half of the patients in this study reported that 1 month after the infection, they were still impacted by the disease.

Although published evidence recognizes RSV disease as a cause of complications and QoL loss in this age group, more data are needed to determine the full extent of its impact. This study aims to describe patients' characteristics, incidence, prevalence, subsequent complications, and QoL in people ≥ 60 years old with laboratory‐confirmed RSV‐ARI in primary care or in hospitals during three consecutive seasons in the province of Valladolid, Spain.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This prospective observational study was conducted to assess the burden of RSV in patients aged 60 years or older with laboratory‐confirmed RSV infection in both primary care and hospital settings. The study was conducted in two hospitals and three primary care settings of the province of Valladolid, Spain. The two participating hospitals were “Hospital Clínico Universitario de Valladolid” and “Hospital Universitario Río Hortega.” The total population aged ≥ 60 years covered by both hospitals is about 160,000 inhabitants. For the study of primary care patients, three primary care centers and their emergency rooms of the same city were involved (Cigales, Circular, and Pilarica). These primary care centers covered 3587 patients ≥ 60 years old in 2022, 3544 patients in 2023, and 3477 patients in 2024.

The study period comprised three consecutive seasons, 2022–2023, 2023–2024, and 2024–2025. The recruitment period started in Week 49/2022 and finalized in Week 39/2025, but the last patient was recruited in Week 8/2025. The primary objective of this study was to estimate the incidence of RSV infections in primary care and prevalence in both clinical settings, and the secondary objectives were to describe both primary care and hospitalized populations with laboratory‐confirmed RSV infection in terms of comorbidities and symptoms, to describe the complications suffered by both populations during the 90‐day follow‐up, and to describe the QoL during a 30‐day follow‐up.

Study Population

Patients aged ≥ 60 years old, with an acute respiratory infection (ARI) fulfilling the WHO definition [20] (sudden onset of at least one of the following symptoms: cough, sore throat, dyspnea, and runny nose and a clinical judgment of infection), with an onset date of the first symptom/sign less than/equal to 7 days before the medical visit, and with laboratory‐confirmed diagnosis of RSV in nasopharyngeal samples were eligible for inclusion. Patients terminally ill, immobilized, or with mental disorders were excluded from the study. These kinds of patients were excluded from the study to ensure the completion of QoL parameters, avoiding missing information or presenting data that could be biased by misunderstanding or by lack of responses.

Patients attending primary care settings were recruited upon diagnosis of an ARI. The study was explained to the patients, and those who consented provided nasopharyngeal swabs, which were tested using a multiplex RT‐PCR, and those who tested positive for RSV were included in the study. For hospitalized patients, we performed a daily search of hospital admission records for laboratory‐confirmed RSV cases with testing conducted as per routine clinical practice, and eligible patients were contacted by telephone to request their participation and obtain informed consent (Figure 1).

Methodology and Data Collection

Data Sources

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from patients' medical records and recorded into a RedCap database for analysis. Microbiological data for inpatient selection were collected daily via the MICROB and MODULAB systems in the participating hospitals. Data were collected at recruitment and during the follow‐up until 90 days after recruitment.

Molecular Diagnostic of RSV Infections

In the case of primary care patients, they were tested using Filmarray Respiratory Panel (Biomerieux). In the case of hospitalized patients, different multiplex‐type molecular panels were used during study period: Luminex NxTAG Respiratory Pathogens (Luminex, USA), Biomerieux FilmArray RP (Biomerieux, France), GenXpert Influenza, RSV rapid diagnostic (Cepheid, USA), and Seegene AllPlex Respiratory Panel (Seegene, South Korea). These panels identify the following respiratory pathogens: RSV A and B; influenza A, B, H1, and H3; human CoV‐OC43, NL63, HKU1, and 229E; parainfluenza 1, 2, 3, and 4; human metapneumovirus; human bocavirus; adenovirus; rhinovirus/enterovirus; Chlamydophila pneumoniae ; Mycoplasma pneumoniae; Legionella pneumophila ; Bordetella pertussis ; and Bordetella parapertussis .

Study Variables

Data were collected for both primary care and hospitalized patients in terms of demographic variables (age, sex, date, clinical setting, and smoking status), clinical variables (comorbidities, high‐risk conditions, such as cardiovascular diseases, respiratory diseases, immunocompromised conditions, oncological disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease, and other risk conditions) and presenting signs and symptoms. Symptoms were obtained by direct interview at the moment of recruitment in both primary care and hospitalized patients. Complications were analyzed during the 90‐day follow‐up: co‐infections, new ARIs, hospitalization (for patients from primary care only) and rehospitalizations, ICU admission, death, asthma or COPD exacerbation or decompensation (only in those patients previously suffering asthma/COPD), and decompensation of heart condition (only in those patients previously suffering cardiac conditions). For the study of co‐infections, in the case of primary care patients, concomitant viral or bacterial infections together with RSV were obtained from the same test used for screening (Filmarray Respiratory Panel and Biomerieux). In the case of hospitalized patients, information about viral and bacterial co‐infection was gathered from microbiology unit records.

QoL Analysis

The study of the evolution of QoL after infection was performed using the EuoQoL EQ‐5D‐3L tool [21]. This methodology first focused on the analysis of the impact of the disease on the five different parameters analyzed (mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression). Secondly, the utility index (UI) was calculated, and after that, the visual analogue scale (VAS) was provided by the patient. A telephonic interview was performed on the day of the diagnosis (recruitment), on Day 15, and Day 30.

Statistical Analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed focusing on the evaluation of qualitative and ordinal variables, shown as frequencies and percentages. This was intended for the calculation of demographic and clinical variables, signs and symptoms, as well as the description of complications. The incidence of RSV laboratory‐confirmed infections in primary care (cases/1000 inhabitants) was calculated on the basis of the next formula: Incidence=TotalARIpositive forRSV/TotalARIenrolled/TotalARIeligibleNumber of persons coveredbythe three clinical sites×1000 where “Total ARI positive for RSV” is the total number of patients who tested positive for RSV, “Total ARI enrolled” is the total number of patients who consented to participate in the study and agreed to be tested by the RT‐PCR, “Total ARI eligible” is the total number of eligible patients because they suffered an ARI, and “Number of persons covered by the three clinical sites” is the number of patients covered by the three primary care settings involved in the study. Incidence was calculated on a monthly basis and by season. The incidence of other respiratory viruses (ORVs) laboratory‐confirmed infections in primary care (cases/1000 inhabitants) was calculated following the same formula, but changing “Total ARI positive for RSV” by “Total ARI positive for ORVs.” This last result was the total number of ORVs diagnosed in primary care. The 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was also calculated for the incidence in primary care.

For the calculation of the impact of RSV infection on QoL following the EQ‐5D‐3L parameters, three categories were used for analysis: affected, not affected, and partially affected, which were analyzed at recruitment, 15th day, and 30th day. The calculation of the impact on these parameters was shown as frequencies and percentages. VAS and UI scores were calculated using published methodology [22]. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0, R software, and GraphPad 5.01.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Valladolid East Ethics Committee under the code PI‐22‐2867. The patients were requested to sign an informed consent form before any sampling. This study adheres to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki regarding the study of human subjects.

Results

Population Characteristics

In primary care settings, during the three seasons analyzed, a total of 973 patients with an ARI were screened (346 in 2022–2023, 372 in 2023–2024, and 255 in 2024–2025). Of those, 477 accepted to participate in the study and agreed to be tested by RT‐PCR. Among these participants, 113 (23.9%) had a laboratory‐confirmed RSV infection, 245 (51.6%) had ORVs, and 116 (24.5%) were negative for any respiratory virus. RSV represented 31.6% (113 out of 358) of the total viral detections in the patients who tested positive for any respiratory virus.

In hospital settings, a total of 956 patients with an RSV laboratory‐confirmed infection were screened (179 in 2022–2023, 486 in 2023–2024, and 291 in 2024–2025). Of those, 270 met the inclusion criteria, and finally, 172 (17.99%) consented to participate in the study and were enrolled.

When considering completion of QoL questionnaires, among primary care patients, 107 (94.7%) of those who consented finalized the 30‐day follow‐up, while there were 160 (93.0%) in the case of hospitalized patients.

The mean age of RSV‐positive patients in primary care and hospital settings was 75.2 (95% CI, 73.5–76.9) and 79.5 years (95% CI, 78.2–80.8), respectively (Table 1). The percentage of females was 55.7% in primary care and 55.8% in hospitalized patients. The most frequent comorbidities in both primary care and hospitalized patients were chronic cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, as well as diabetes (Table 1). Epidemiological Features of RSV Infection in Both Primary Care and Hospitalized Patients

The incidence of RSV infection in patients recruited in primary care was 8.4 cases/1.000 inhabitants (95% CI, 5.4–11.4) in season 2022–2023, 34.9 cases/1.000 inhabitants (95% CI, 28.9–40.9) in 2023–2024, and 29.2 cases/1.000 inhabitants (95% CI, 23.6–34.8) in 2024–2025 (Figure 2). The incidence of ORVs in primary care was 59.4 cases/1.000 inhabitants (95% CI, 51.7–67.1) in season 2022–2023, 50.3 cases/1.000 inhabitants (95% CI, 43.1–57.5) in 2023–2024, and 32.2 cases/1.000 inhabitants (95% CI, 26.3–38.1) in 2024–2025. RSV detections peaked in February in the 2022–2023 season (2.9 cases/1000 inhabitants), in January in 2023–24 (12.6 cases/1000 inhabitants), and in December of 2024–25 (12.7 cases/1000 inhabitants).

Complications After RSV Laboratory‐Confirmed Infection in Both Primary Care and Hospitalized Patients

After recruitment, we performed a 90‐day follow‐up after enrollment in both primary care and hospital patients, focusing on the complications after the RSV infection. Generally, the most frequent complication in primary care patients was the occurrence of a new ARI (23.0%), while in hospitalized patients it was the detection of co‐infections with other viruses/bacteria (31.4%) (Table 3 and Figure 4). A total of 26 hospitalized patients (15.1%) were readmitted to the hospital, while 8 primary care patients (7.1%) required hospitalization after their medical encounter. None of these primary care patients required ICU admission during their hospitalization, but a total of 8 (4.7%) hospitalized patients were admitted to this unit. A total of 6 (3.5%) hospitalized patients died during the 90‐day follow‐up, and 3 of them died during the first 30 days.

We also evaluated the number of patients who suffered a decompensation of their underlying diseases. A total of 31 out of 38 (81.6%) hospitalized patients suffering COPD experienced a decompensation, 21 out of 31 (67.7%) suffered an asthma decompensation, and 47 out of 151 (31.1%) suffered a decompensation of their cardiac condition. In the case of primary care patients, 4 out of 10 (40.0%) suffered a COPD decompensation, 6 out of 13 (46.2%) suffered an asthma decompensation, and 9 out of 86 (10.5%) suffered a heart decompensation. In the case of hospitalized patients, the most common cardiac decompensations observed were tachycardia (23.5%) and arrhythmias (22.9%). Patients with a history of atrial fibrillation had a higher frequency of arrhythmic events (46%), while those with HF exhibited arrhythmias (35.6%) and decompensation of their HF (31.1%). The proportion of cardiovascular diagnoses increased from 21.1% on admission to 30.7% on discharge, including five atrioventricular and bundle branch blocks requiring pacemakers in at least two cases, four acute myocardial infarctions myocardium, two cardiorespiratory arrests, and atrial fibrillation and new‐onset HF.

Then, an age‐based analysis was performed, splitting the population into two age groups: 60–79 and ≥ 80 years old. In hospitalized patients, co‐infections, new ARIs, ICU admissions, and death were more frequent in the 60–79 years age group (35.9%, 32.6%, 7.6%, and 4.4% respectively) compared with the ≥ 80 years old age group (26.3%, 16.3%, 1.3%, and 2.5%, respectively). However, readmission was most frequent in the ≥ 80 years old age group (15.0% compared with 13.0% in 60–79 years). Worsening of their previous conditions was more frequent in the 60–79 years old age group compared with the ≥ 80 years old age group, except for the decompensation of heart conditions. Similarly, in primary care patients, co‐infections and new ARIs were more frequent in the 60–79 years old age group (12.5% and 2.4%, respectively), while hospitalization was more frequent in ≥ 80 years old age group (12.2%).

Impact of the RSV Infection on the QoL

The analysis of the five parameters of the EQ‐5D‐3L showed that, at recruitment, more than 50% of primary care patients had total or partial impairment in mobility, usual activities, and pain/discomfort, with pain/discomfort being particularly high, showing values above 90% (Table 4 and Figure 5). In the case of hospitalized patients, at recruitment, all parameters were affected or partially affected, exceeding 75% in all cases.

Discussion

The results of our study showed that RSV is involved in approximately 24% of ARIs managed in primary care during the winter season in people aged ≥ 60 years. These results are consistent with a recent review reporting that medically attended RSV infections represent 17%–28% of all detected viruses [23] and higher than earlier studies conducted in the United States and Europe [4, 7, 24, 25, 26]. Most of these studies were conducted before the COVID‐19 pandemic, when diagnostic methods were less frequent, so the current data may better reflect the actual burden of RSV in older adults.

The incidence reported in primary care by our study (8.4–34.9 cases/1000 population) was similar to that reported in other studies (5.0–48.6 cases/1000 population) [27, 28, 29, 30]. However, data from our first season may have been underestimated because the recruitment started late and also because the circulation of COVID‐19 during the latest stage of the pandemic could have somewhat impacted the RSV circulation [31, 32, 33].

In the present study, most patients had a preexisting comorbidity, especially cardiovascular diseases, metabolic diseases, and chronic respiratory diseases, such as COPD and asthma. According to recent data, the presence of two or more comorbidities at the same time increases 7‐fold the risk of hospitalization during an RSV infection [34, 35]. For instance, COPD increases the risk of hospitalization 3.5–13.4‐fold, diabetes 2.44–6.44‐fold, and HF 4.0–7.66‐fold [13, 34, 36, 37]. Our results also confirm that age is itself a risk factor, as older patients were more frequently attended to at the hospital settings.

The most frequent complications in both primary care and hospitalized patients were new ARIs and viral/bacterial co‐infections. Co‐infections are especially relevant because they are a clear risk factor for patient worsening, increasing hospitalization rates by 4‐fold, by 2‐fold the risk of severe disease, and by 2.7‐fold the risk of death [37, 38]. In our study, co‐infection rates were lower in primary care patients compared with a study conducted in 2017 (19.7%) [39] but higher in hospitalized patients versus that reported in other recent reports (9.3%–12.1%) [38, 40]. Readmission was also frequent (15.1% within 90 days), within the range reported in other studies (6.5%–17%) [41, 42]. Nearly 5% of hospitalized patients required ICU admission and 3.5% died. Those values were similar to others reported in recent studies (ICU, 4%; in‐hospital death, 6%) [43] but lower compared with others showing ICU admission rates over 10%–20% and in‐hospital death rates over 5%–10% [44, 45].

Decompensation of previous cardiac or respiratory pathologies was especially relevant. COPD decompensation occurred in more than 80% of hospitalized patients and in 40% of primary care patients with COPD and asthma exacerbation in more than 67% and 46% of hospitalized and primary care patients, respectively. These data revealed that RSV infection is clearly associated with clinical worsening in patients with chronic respiratory diseases. However, cardiac diseases were also impacted. Decompensation of cardiac conditions was also frequent (30% of hospitalized patients), in line with other previous studies (14%–22%), where acute HF was the most frequent complication [16, 46].

The impact of RSV on QoL of patients was relevant in both settings, but especially severe in hospitalized patients. More than 50% of hospitalized patients showed a marked impairment in different parameters, which was consistent with previous studies [7, 19, 47]. QoL improved substantially during the 30‐day follow‐up, but the impact of the disease persisted beyond 1 month after diagnosis. Previous studies have shown that QoL impairment is significant during the first 15 days after diagnosis [48] and may extend up to 6 months, requiring postdischarge care in up to 14% of subjects [49].

The main limitation of the study was the highly restrictive inclusion criteria, which have excluded patients with dementia or terminal illnesses who may represent the highest impact groups of the disease caused by RSV. As a result, our findings reflect a specific population, probably the people at the lower risk among older adults, which may explain the lower mortality rates encountered compared with other studies performed in elderly populations. In addition, we did not assess QoL before infection, so we cannot determine whether recovery was complete or whether QoL was permanently diminished. On the other hand, recruitment during the first season (2022–2023) started later due to project issues, introducing a potential bias in the calculation of incidence in that season. Finally, both non‐RSV and negative cases were not explored in hospitalized patients, so the representativeness of RSV infections among all ARIs in hospital settings was not addressed.

Conclusions

RSV poses a considerable clinical burden in ≥ 60‐year‐old patients, both in primary care and in hospital settings. Infection frequently occurs in patients with different chronic pathologies, with cardiovascular disease, COPD, and asthma particularly common. New ARIs and viral/bacterial co‐infections are frequent, as are readmissions and the exacerbation of underlying chronic conditions. Furthermore, RSV infection has a significant impact on patients' QoL, not only during the acute phase of the illness but also persisting throughout recovery. These findings demonstrate that the impact of RSV on older adults is underestimated and support the prioritization of preventive strategies in this population.
