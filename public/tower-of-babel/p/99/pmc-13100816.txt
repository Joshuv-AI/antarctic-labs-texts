Template Switching as a Driver of Promoter Evolution in Yeast: Case Study of the GRE2 Gene
Authors: Michal Rozenfeld, Maayan Bachar, Raz Cohen, Evgeniya Marcos-Hadad, Shira Milo, Shay Covo, Einat Hazkani-Covo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInverted repeats (IRs) are sequences with internal symmetry that can form non-canonical DNA structures. DNA polymerase template switching between imperfect IR arms can homogenize the arms and expand their size. IRs play an important role in the binding of transcription factors (TFs), particularly those that function as dimers. We used comparative genomics to identify recent IR-expansions in the vicinity of promoter regions in the lineage of Saccharomyces cerevisiae. From the thousands of events identified, we further focused on ones with significant expansion, outside of simple repeats and within confirmed binding sites of TFs. These events are located at 107 loci next to 130 genes and could be observed across the phylogenetic tree, with some events that occurred independently several times. We further focused on 2 genes that showed the longest IR expansion and created IR-expanded mutants in the BY4741 lab strain. While the expansion did not change much at the ERT1 gene, the gre2 mutant with IR expansion was very different from BY4741. In the GRE2 IR-expanded mutant, GRE2 expression was higher than in wild-type; the mutant grew more slowly but was relatively more resistant to glycolaldehyde, a substrate of Gre2p. Taken together, we show here that IR-expansion at promoters is frequent and can shape the evolution of transcription regulation.

We discovered that inverted repeats (IRs) often become perfect or expand near genes and suggest they can impact how genes are expressed. In one case, these changes altered GRE2 gene expression, slowed growth, and increased resistance to glycolaldehyde. This suggests that the template switching mechanism, which is responsible for the homogenization and expansion of IRs, can reshape traits and play a role in the evolution of gene expression.

Introduction

Transcription regulation is a complex process that involves the interplay between cis-regulatory elements in promoters and enhancers and the binding of transcription factors. One aspect of this regulation that has garnered significant attention is the role of inverted repeats (IRs) and palindromes in promoters. IRs are sequences containing 2 copies of a DNA sequence arranged in a reverse-complement orientation, which can either be perfect or imperfect (Fig. 1a). By binding as dimers to perfect IRs, transcription factor complexes effectively double their recognition sequence, thereby increasing both affinity and specificity (Wilce et al. 2012). The classic example is the lac repressor, which binds a palindromic operator with 24-bp arms that are essential for operon regulation (Gilbert and Maxam 1973; Dong et al. 1999). Since then, numerous studies have highlighted the importance of IRs in transcription factor binding. For instance, Sox9 binds a 6-bp IR arm motif in extracellular matrix genes such as ACAN, COL11A2, COL9A1, and MART3, and it has been suggested that it may function as a homodimer (Oh et al. 2010, 2014). Genome-wide analyses reinforce this view: palindromic transcription factor motifs are among the most frequent promoter elements in the human genome (FitzGerald et al. 2004). These include binding sites for the USF family of dimeric bHLH-ZIP proteins (Bendall and Molloy 1994), the CRE element bound by CREB homodimers or Fos–Jun heterodimers (Vinson et al. 2002), and Nrf-1, which regulates nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes (Scarpulla 2002).

IR dynamics can alter transcription factor binding and gene expression. In bursal lymphoma, deregulation of MYC was linked to tumor-specific new palindromes and MYC binding sites (Neiman et al. 2006, 2008; Kuzyk and Mai 2014). Similarly, p53 binding to the FLT1 promoter is stronger when both IR arms are present, while single-arm binding reduces transcriptional activity (Menendez et al. 2010). In yeast, Cbf1 binds palindromic sequences as short as 6 bp in the MET promoter and centromere DNA element I. In vitro, Correction of imperfect IRs to perfect ones, as well as IR and palindrome expansion, can alter transcription factor affinity and gene expression (Wilmen et al. 1994).

A key mechanism influencing IR dynamics is DNA polymerase template switching, which can correct or expand imperfect IRs into perfect ones (Lovett 2004, 2017). First proposed by Ripley (1982) in bacteriophages, template switching involves 2 jumps of DNA polymerase between templates. One IR arm serves as a template for the synthesis of the other arm (Fig. 1b), either within the same molecule (intramolecular) or between molecules (intermolecular) (Bzymek and Lovett 2001). Template switching has been shown to have several evolutionary impacts. It contributes to protein evolution by introducing clustered nonsynonymous substitutions (Abraham and Hazkani-Covo 2021). Additionally, template switching has a role in the evolution of RNA (Mönttinen and Löytynoja 2022; Mönttinen et al. 2023).

We investigated potential IR-mediated template switching events near promoters of Saccharomyces cerevisiae genes, hypothesizing that such events could influence gene expression during evolution. Harbison et al. (2004) mapped yeast transcriptional regulation and showed that most transcription factor binding sites (74%) are located 100 to 500 bp upstream of the transcription start site (TSS), with fewer sites closer than 100 bp or beyond 500 bp. To account for this, we analyzed 1,000 bp upstream of protein-coding genes to identify IR expansions and arm homogenization between each strain and the reference lab strain. We focused on IR changes overlapping known transcription factor binding sites or regions showing differential expression in the lab strain, using pairwise comparisons with S288C. From this analysis, we identified IR expansions across strains and experimentally tested 2 cases: the methylglyoxal reductase GRE2 and the ethanol-regulated transcription factor ERT1. We found that IR expansion significantly affects GRE2 expression.

Results

We examined whether template switching shapes promoter evolution by converting imperfect inverted repeats (IRs) into perfect ones or expanding existing IRs. We searched up to 1,000 bp upstream of S. cerevisiae genes, comparing the well-studied reference strain S288C with other isolates from SGD (Cherry et al. 2012) and the 1002 Yeast Genomes Project (Peter et al. 2018).

From this screen, we identified thousands of events, applying filtering criteria (arm length ≥10 bp; spacer ≤70 bp; ≥3 changes compared to the reference) allowed us to focus on 1,816 candidate template-switching events. After removing simple and tandem repeats, 917 IRs remained across 338 loci. About one-third (199 events, 107 loci, Tables S1 to S3) overlapped known transcriptional regulatory elements, with IR arms ranging from 10 to 48 bp. This is statistically significant compared to 100 simulations of locations upstream of identified genes (empirical P < 0.01, see Materials and Methods). Notably, 21 events occurred between divergent gene pairs.

Across strains, most perfect IRs overlapping regulatory elements are shared by 2 or more isolates (Fig. 1c and d, Fig. S1). We identified 38 strain-specific IRs and 69 that are present in multiple strains; overall, 871 of 1,051 strains carried 1 to 7 perfect IRs. Their distribution was non-random across the phylogeny, with greater divergence from S288C correlating with more perfect IRs (Spearman ρ = 0.56, P < 0.001). Using GLOOME (Cohen et al. 2010), we inferred 52 single-gain events and 55 cases of multiple gains, 17 of which had high confidence (expectation ≥ 0.9). Nine strains showed strong evidence for single-gain events at numerous loci (Table S4).

While the analysis focused on events up to 1,000 bp upstream of the gene's coding sequence (CDS), events within the 107 loci tended to be closer to the translation start site (393 ± 270 bp), and the transcription start site (224 ± 218 bp) than the maximum permitted distance (Fig. 2). Most events were mapped within 200 bp of the TSS.

In other cases of this class, the same type of event is shared among strains. For example, an IR with a 20-bp arm between the divergent genes YKL007W and YKL008C occurs in 4 strains (Fig. 3b). Eight transcription factors, including Cbf1 (left arm) and Abf1 (right arm), bind this site, which also lies within a ChIP-validated region. Two African beer strains (N34:2-4(a), CER; N26-1-10:7-5(a), CES) share an identical IR differing only in the left arm, while 2 palm wine strains (FTPW32, CQA; FTPW35, CQB) show a distinct variant with modifications in both arms and a 5-bp spacer inversion (Fig. S3). In our dataset, 12 out of 107 events (11%) showed IR variation at the same locus. While most of this variation is due to overlapping IR sequences, 6 loci (5.6%) include IR variants at identical coordinates, exemplified by the event in Fig. 3b.

The second class includes cases where both arms are modified compared to the reference strain, often through the expansion of a shorter IR present in S288C. In the YBR239C/ERT1 gene, a short IR with an arm of 7 bp found in the reference strain has expanded to a perfect IR with an arm of 25 bp in 32 other strains, primarily from the Sake group (Figs. 1d and 4a, Fig S1). This expansion creates a new Dal80 binding site but mutates other sites for Ste12, Dig1, Aft2, and Rap1. The mechanism involves interstrand template switching and spacer inversion (Fig. 4b).

Similarly, the YOL151W/GRE2 gene, which has a short 9 bp IR arm in the reference strain, has expanded to a 24 bp arm length in 21 strains from the Asian fermentation group (Figs. 1d and 5a, Fig. S1). This expansion keeps the original Cin5 binding sites intact as well as the distance from TSS. A smaller 16 bp IR variant also exists in a nearby position in 2 strains from African palm wine. This expansion also occurs via interstrand template switching (Fig. 5b). These events suggest that template switching can remodel both arms simultaneously, expanding existing IRs and potentially amplifying their regulatory impact.

GO annotation (Ashburner et al. 2000; Aleksander et al. 2023) of genes within the 107 IR loci indicates that the gene set spans a broad range of biological processes, with approximately 31% of genes associated with metabolic functions. Using the S. cerevisiae genome as the reference, GO enrichment analysis (Thomas et al. 2022) reveals a significant over-representation of genes involved in central metabolic processes, particularly organic and carboxylic acid metabolism, indicating that genes associated with core metabolism are more frequent in the dataset than expected by chance. Both ERT1 and GRE2 are annotated within the enriched small-molecule metabolic process category (Table S5).

Experimental Analysis of GRE2 and ERT1

We next selected 2 genes, ERT1/YBR239C (Fig. 4) and GRE2/YOL151W (Fig. 5), to test the effect of long IRs on gene expression in a laboratory setting. These genes were chosen because (i) they show a big difference in IR length between the laboratory reference strain and the strain carrying the long IR, and (ii) their functions allow straightforward screening of the 2 variants under laboratory conditions. Experiments were conducted in strain BY4741, the wild-type reference used in the yeast deletion collection. Genetic variation between BY4741 and its progenitor S288C is minimal (Winston et al. 1995).

ERT1

The ERT1 gene (Ethanol Regulated Transcription factor, YBR239C) is involved in gluconeogenesis and the utilization of fermentable carbon sources. Deletion of ERT1 has been reported to increase competitive fitness, replicative lifespan, and transposable element mobility compared to wild-type. Because promoter changes in this gene were observed in strains isolated from sake fermentation, we hypothesized that these changes might have functional significance.

We first grew the BY4741 lab strain and its derivative carrying the long IR under various carbon sources and growth conditions (breathable tubes and 96-well plates), but no differences were observed. We then cultured the strains in 20 ml of complete medium containing 2% ethyl-acetate as the sole carbon source in 100 ml breathable flasks. The median growth curve (Fig. S4) suggested the largest change occurred at 48 h, but this was not statistically significant. Because RNA sequencing can detect subtle differences not apparent in growth phenotypes, we compared the expression patterns of the wild-type and IR-expanded mutant grown in 2% ethyl-acetate. RNA quality from 48-h cultures was insufficient, but in this experiment, differences in the growth rate could be observed after 24 h (Fig. S4). RNA-seq performed on 24-h samples revealed no significant differences in gene expression between wild-type and ert1 mutant cells (all adjusted P-values > 0.05).

GRE2

Next, we tested the effect of IR expansion at the GRE2 locus. Gre2 is a stress-related protein: a null mutant is sensitive to glycolaldehyde, whereas overexpression enhances tolerance to this compound. This is an appealing example since the strains involved are fermentation strains, and glycolaldehyde is a byproduct of fermentation. We measured the growth of BY4741 and the GRE2 IR-expanded mutant in complete medium with 2% glucose, with or without various concentrations of glycolaldehyde. In the absence of glycolaldehyde, the IR-expanded mutant grew more slowly than BY4741 (Fig. 6a), with a generation time of 110 min compared to 66 min for BY4741 (Fig. 6b). Final O.D. after 70 h was also reduced in the mutant (0.60 vs. 0.863 for BY4741, Fig. 6a).

Exposure to glycolaldehyde slowed growth for both strains. For BY4741, generation time increased with glycolaldehyde concentration: 96 min at 5 mM, 117 min at 10 mM, and 133 min at 15 mM. In contrast, the IR-expanded mutant showed a weaker response: 131 min at 5 mM, 146 min at 10 mM, and 146 min at 15 mM (Fig. 6b). A similar trend was observed in final O.D. values: BY4741 decreased with increasing glycolaldehyde (0.77 at 5 mM, 0.74 at 10 mM, 0.69 at 15 mM), whereas the IR strain remained relatively stable (0.59, 0.65, and 0.59, respectively). These results suggest that the IR expansion in GRE2 provides partial protection against glycolaldehyde exposure, at the cost of reduced growth under standard conditions.

To investigate the molecular effects of the IR expansion at the GRE2 locus, we performed RNA sequencing of BY4741 and the IR-expanded mutant. Under standard growth conditions without glycolaldehyde, GRE2 expression in the IR strain was nearly 4-fold higher than in BY4741 (log2 fold change = 1.92, adjusted P = 0.0002) (Fig. 7).

STRING analysis (Szklarczyk et al. 2023) of the full transcriptome revealed that genes overexpressed in the IR-expanded mutant formed a tight cluster related to carbon metabolism (Fig. 7a, Table S6), including components of the citrate and glyoxylate cycles (CIT3, ICL2, POX1, CYB2, SYM1, AGX1) and genes involved in metabolism of fatty acids and aldehydes (POX1, ALD2). Notably, the stress-induced transcriptional repressor XBP1 was upregulated in the IR-expanded strain. A few of the induced genes are normally repressed by glucose despite its presence in the medium (CYB2, ICL2)—likely due to incomplete consumption associated with slow growth. STRING analysis of underexpressed genes revealed clusters of ribosomal proteins (CBF5, MAK21, RRP14, IFH1, TMA23, DRS1, NOP4, PXR1, NOP56, KRI1, BUD22, NOP58, FUN12) and RNA processing factors (TRM82, RRP42) (Fig. 7b, Table S6). These underexpressed genes are hallmarks of growth arrest, consistent with the upregulation of XBP1 and the extended generation time observed in the IR-expanded mutant.

Next, we tested the hypothesis that the mutation at the GRE2 promoter primes the response to glycolaldehyde by preactivating similar transcriptional networks. To this end, we analyzed the transcriptomic changes following 10 mM glycolaldehyde exposure in BY4741 strain. We hypothesized that the effect of glycolaldehyde exposure on BY4741 would be similar to that of IR expansion. The results showed that the expression level of GRE2 gene did not change significantly (log2fold change is 0.84, adjusted P: 0.40). Next, the effect on the total transcriptome was analyzed. STRING analysis revealed that glycolaldehyde exposure caused over expression of genes which take part in ribosome structure and function (Fig. S5 and Table S7) (SSB1, RPS5, YEF3, RPL5, STM1, NOP58), fatty acids metabolism (FAS2, POX1, ACC1), fermentation (PDC1, CDC19, FBA1) and methionine synthesis and catabolism (MET2, ARO10). STRING analysis of under-expressed genes revealed mitochondrial (MRP7, MRPS9, COX18, COX10, MRPL44, MTG1, NGR1) and ribosomal genes (NOG1, RPF2, NOC4, NRP1, PRP42) (Fig. S5 and Table S7).

Because some modules altered by IR expansion resemble those affected by glycolaldehyde, we tested for overlap in differentially expressed genes. The overlap was minimal: only 9 of 142 genes induced by IR expansion were also induced by 10 mM glycolaldehyde in wild-type (Fig. 8). In conclusion, there is no evidence to support the hypothesis that glycolaldehyde activates the GRE2 transcription program the same way the expanded IR mutation does.

We next asked whether the GRE2 IR-expanded mutant shows a distinct transcriptional response to glycolaldehyde. Therefore, we compared the transcriptome of this strain with 0 and 10 mM glycolaldehyde (Table S8). Comparing 0 and 10 mM conditions revealed only 18 differentially expressed genes (Padj < 0.05, |log2FC| > 1; Table S8). By contrast, glycolaldehyde exposure in wild-type altered the expression of ∼200 genes, and the IR expansion alone altered >250. These findings suggest that the gre2 mutant is already adapted to glycolaldehyde at the transcriptional level. Yet, there is an alternative explanation for these results. It is possible that the effect of GRE2 overexpression on the transcriptome under optimal growth conditions is irrelevant to glycolaldehyde exposure, and the fact that gre2 mutant is not inhibited by glycolaldehyde like the wild-type is due to its activity as a reductase that directly detoxifies aldehydes (Lam et al. 2021).

Discussion

In our phylogenetic analysis of Saccharomyces cerevisiae, we identified multiple transitions to perfect IRs near promoters. The same locus often exhibited different template-switching outcomes or slightly shifted coordinates across strains (Table S1). Notably, we observed 55 loci with independent multiple gains of perfect IRs, 17 with very high confidence. This pattern suggests that certain promoters may act as hotspots for template switching. In addition, 9 of 1,051 strains showed transitions to perfect IRs at multiple promoters with high GLOOME gain scores, indicating that IR expansion may play a broader role in shaping the transcriptome of S. cerevisiae. This analysis compares all strains to the reference strain S228C, for which extensive information on transcriptional regulatory elements is available. However, a limitation is that S288C has been characterized as a phenotypic extreme and is highly atypical of the species (Borneman et al. 2011; Warringer et al. 2011).

We classified the events into 2 classes. In the first, one IR arm is conserved while the other differs from the reference, converting an imperfect IR into a perfect one. In the second, both arms are modified, often through the expansion of a shorter IR present in the reference strain. This latter class includes the GRE2 and ERT1 loci, which we investigated further experimentally. While the expansion of the GRE2 locus increased the expression of the downstream gene, this was not observed in the ERT1 locus. Considering the high frequency of template switching events, we suggest that Saccharomyces cerevisiae is tolerant of incorporating IRs, and this may not always result in major changes in transcriptional outcomes.

While some transcription factor binding sites are maintained under selective pressure, they also undergo frequent turnover, often shaped by the combinatorial nature of their interactions (Moses et al. 2003; Morata et al. 2018). We identified 3 possible outcomes when one IR arm serves as a template for the other, producing a perfect IR. First, if the conserved arm contains a transcription factor binding site, homogenization of IR can create 2 identical sites (e.g. YLR055C, Fig. 3a) that can enable homodimer binding. Second, if the transcription factor binding arm is overwritten by the non-binding arm, the site is lost. Third, when each arm carries different binding sites (e.g. ERT1, Fig. 4; YKL007W, Fig. 3b), homogenization of IR can have a complicated effect on the transcription of the downstream gene. These latter scenarios are especially relevant given the central role of dimerization in transcription factor function over evolutionary timescales.

Many cis- and trans-factors influence gene regulation (Hill et al. 2021), and cis-regulatory variation in the core promoter is a key driver of heritable expression differences. Our results suggest that template switching represents an additional mutational mechanism that can both generate new regulatory opportunities and erode existing ones, thereby introducing evolutionary plasticity into promoters.

The IR arm expansion from 9 to 24 base pairs in the GRE2 promoter led to a four-fold increase in gene expression (Figs. 5 and 7). At this point, we do not know how the IR expansion at the GRE2 locus increases gene expression. The transcription factor Cin5 is predicted to bind at this locus. Although the IR expansion does not alter the Cin5 binding motifs, it increases the spacing between them by 6 nucleotides and is expected to change the local stem–loop structure. At present, it is unclear whether altered gene expression results from changes in Cin5 binding affinity or cooperativity, or instead from modifications to the chromatin environment. Even without changing Cin5 affinity, chromatin changes may allow more effective recruitment of RNA Pol II. Cin5 is a classic stress-induced transcription factor regulated by Msn2 and Yap1 as part of the yeast environmental stress response. The promoter change may activate GRE2 expression in a manner less dependent on global stress response.

RNA-seq analysis of the GRE2 IR-expanded mutant revealed overexpression of XBP1 (Fig. 7). This, by itself, can explain the slow growth phenotype since Xbp1p is a repressor of genes associated with growth, including ribosome biogenesis. Indeed, ribosome biogenesis genes are downregulated in the IR-expanded mutant (Fig. 7). Additionally, several genes normally induced under glucose limitation (HXT5, CYB2, ICL2; Guiard 1985; Luttik et al. 2000; Diderich et al. 2001; Verwaal et al. 2002) were upregulated, suggesting that GRE2 overexpression activates stress pathways and use of alternative sugar sources even in glucose-rich conditions.

The IR-expansion in the promoter region of GRE2 occurred in an Asian fermentation clade. According to the literature, this expansion and the following increase in the expression of GRE2 are relevant to ecology and physiology. First, it was previously shown that GRE2 is overexpressed in a Sake fermentation yeast strain during the Sake brewing process (Wu et al. 2006). The expression of GRE2 is increased during the process and then decreased at later stages, a pattern that was shared with oxidative stress genes. Second, during Sake brewing, there is an increase in osmolarity that leads to osmotic stress. The response to this stress is through the HOG1 pathway, that GRE2 was previously described to be part of (Hohmann 2002; Wu et al. 2006). Third, there is evidence linking increased expression of GRE2 to the production of 2-Phenylethanol (Holyavkin et al. 2023), a compound that provides aromatic taste to Sake (Tatsukami et al. 2018).

The IR expansion at the GRE2 promoter and its effect on GRE2 expression should be discussed in a broader biotechnological context. While glycolaldehyde may be an important factor in Asian brewing, it has been heavily studied in other industrial fermentation environments. The expression pattern we observe in the mutant may confer an advantage under industrial fermentation and especially glycolaldehyde exposure, consistent with previous reports that GRE2 overexpression improves growth under such conditions in multiple yeast species (Jayakody et al. 2013, 2018; Wang et al. 2016). However, enhancing the expression of GRE2 improved ethanol production when engineered yeast fermented lignocellulosic feedstocks, a very different substrate from the one used here. During lignocellulose fermentation, yeast cells are already under stress due to exposure to toxins such as furfural and HMF (Heer et al. 2009; Lam et al. 2021). Under these conditions, GRE2 overexpression is beneficial not because it induces stress responses, but because its reductase activity reduces toxic compounds, thereby alleviating stress. Similarly, our results suggest that Gre2 activity may reduce the toxicity of glycolaldehyde. Glycolaldehyde exposure in BY4741 did not induce GRE2 expression at the mRNA level (Fig. S5, Table S6). At the transcriptome level, we observed no significant overlap between glycolaldehyde response and the GRE2 IR-expended mutation (Fig. 8). Thus, we suggest that the modest glycolaldehyde growth phenotype in the GRE2 IR-expanded mutant is either due to preactivation of a general stress response program but not a specific glycolaldehyde response or due to direct detoxification of glycolaldehyde.

A better stress response is desirable for industrial yeasts. However, it seems that the expansion of the IR at the promoter of GRE2 has an adverse effect on growth. It is important to emphasize that our experimental design targeted a single gene, whereas natural isolates used in industrial processes carry numerous mutations that may interact synergistically with the GRE2 IR expansion to enhance growth. Evolutionary analysis of yeast species that are tolerant to reactive oxygen species reveals frequent expansion of GRE2 gene family (Aranguiz et al. 2025), suggesting that there are multiple evolutionary solutions to increase GRE2 activity under continuous stress exposure.

While many types of mutations are known to alter promoter activity, our study shows that template switching offers a mechanistic pathway for promoter evolution. Template switching is unique in several aspects. First, these events are sequence-specific in the sense that they are concentrated within short imperfect or perfect IR loci. Perfect IRs are statistically enriched in genomes, suggesting a role for template switching (Strawbridge et al. 2010; Lavi et al. 2018). Moreover, IR-based template switching is not evenly distributed across genomes. Walker et al. (2021) showed that in the human genome, these events are enriched near transcription factor binding sites, super-enhancers, and introns.

Additionally, template switching is affected by the genetic background of the cell. We previously demonstrated that yeast cells defective in Rad27 (Omer et al. 2017), a protein that participates in Okazaki fragment maturation, show an increased template switching rate during replication. Sugawara et al. (2024) showed that yeast cells defective in mismatch repair exhibit an increased template switching rate during double-strand break repair. Neither Rad27 nor mismatch repair genes are essential in fungal and human cells, and they are commonly defective in mutator lineages and tumors. Hence, these may affect the template switching rate and, consequently, transcription site plasticity.

Conclusion

The frequent template-switching events that form or expand perfect IRs at Saccharomyces cerevisiae promoters suggest that this is a mechanism of evolution to rewire gene expression networks.

Materials and Methods

Data

We screened all verified protein-coding genes on the 16 nuclear chromosomes using the SGD annotation file saccharomyces_cerevisiae.20220720.gff. The verified set includes 5,220 genes, excluding dubious genes, pseudogenes, and transposable elements. We further excluded 332 genes of unknown function, resulting in a total of 4,888 genes analyzed. Strains were collected from SGD (Cherry et al. 2012) and the 1011 Genomes Project (Peter et al. 2018). For each of the 4,888 genes, we extracted 1,000 bp upstream of the ATG, generating 1,003 bp sequences. After removing duplicates, the final dataset included 1,051 strains.

For the SGD dataset, we downloaded orf_genomic_1000.fasta.gz, which contains 1,000 bp upstream of the ATG for all “Verified,” “Uncharacterized,” and transposable element ORFs in S288C. From this, we retained only the 4,888 selected genes and successfully retrieved upstream sequences for 4,874. For other strains, upstream regions were identified using S288C gene names in their GenBank files. For the 1011 Genomes dataset, we downloaded CDS and full genome sequences and located each gene's ATG by BLASTN against the 4,888 gene set. We then extracted 1,000 bp upstream of the ATG, retaining only genes with fully identified start sites. In total, 4,268 genes were identified.

IR Detection

For each orthologous group, we searched for perfect IRs with arm lengths ≥10 bp and spacers ≤70 bp across all strains using the EMBOSS palindrome package (Rice et al. 2000). We define an IR locus as the IR including its spacer and arms. To focus on IRs likely generated by template switching, ortholog sets were aligned with MAFFT (Katoh et al. 2009), and we excluded any locus where the perfect IR arm differed by fewer than 2 sites compared to a non-IR strain. This removed identical IRs or those with only a single change relative to other IRs at the locus. We next identified perfect IRs for which the corresponding region in S288C or BY4741 was imperfect, requiring at least 3 differences between the 2 arms (in one or both arms). This set is referred to as the variant IRs. IRs overlapping tandem repeats were excluded using non-B-gfa (Cer et al. 2013), and a permissive simple repeat filter was applied to remove repetitive regions.

Overlapping IR Variants with Known Regulatory Elements

To narrow down our results, we cross the IR variant set with 5 tables from SGD and UCSC genome browser that include genomic locations potentially associated with gene expression regulation. These tables include both ChIp-chip experiments and predicted transcription factor binding sites. All tables are based on S288C coordinates, so positions of the perfect IR are translated to S288C ones before being tested for overlap.

The following tables were used in the analysis: (A) The transRegCode table (A1) includes transcription factor binding sites from ChIp-chip experiments. This table is provided by the UCSC genome browser and presents putative regulatory elements supported by cross-species evidence (Harbison et al. 2004). This analysis revealed 11,000 high-confidence interactions between regulators and promoter regions. Additionally, the associated transRegCodeProbe table (A2) shows the location of the probes spotted on a slide in the ChIP-chip experiments of the transRegCode table (Harbison et al. 2004). We use probes as well, since they might include more information. (B) The ORegAnno table displays literature-curated regulatory regions, transcription factor binding sites, and regulatory polymorphisms from Open Regulatory Annotation (Lesurf et al. 2016). (C) The esRegGeneToMotif table displays predicted transcription factor binding sites derived from sequence similarities and microarray expression datasets (Segal et al. 2003). (D) SGD table includes genome-wide location binding analysis of the transcription elongation factor TFIIS and RNA polymerase II and III (Ghavi-Helm et al. 2008). We only report events with a log2 binding ratio above 1 or below −1.

We tested IR variant events where at least one of the table entrances above is included within an arm (IR arm includes the event). For events that are positive in one table, we also reported a larger table entrance that includes an IR arm (Event that includes an IR arm). For IR with a spacer up to 10 bp, we also reported when a part of the locus (but not precisely the arm) includes an event from one of the tables (Event overlaps a locus).

To test possible enrichment of IR overlap with transcriptional regulatory elements, we simulated 100 sets of IR locations upstream of genes and with the same IR arm and spacer lengths as in the dataset of 338 loci. We repeated the 100 simulations twice: once allowing the IRs to be located on distances of 1,000 bp and once allowing IRs to be located 300 bp upstream of the CDS. We then tested for overlap with transcriptional regulatory elements and placed the observed value on the empirical distribution of the simulations to obtain an empirical P-value.

Evolution Dynamics of IR Variants

Genes with IR variants were realigned for all strains to identify identical or similar IR variant loci in the 2 datasets. S288C was used as an anchor to determine the location on the alignment.

Phylogeny

Multiple sequence alignment of 4,878 CDS of the genes used in this study from 1,051 strains was performed using MAFFT V3.70578 (Katoh et al. 2009) with default parameters. MSAs were concatenated, and the best maximum likelihood (ML) tree was reconstructed by IQTREE2 (Minh et al. 2020) using GTR + G matrix with fast 1,000 bootstraps. The consensus tree was then used in this study.

We used the Gain Loss Mapping Engine (GLOOME) server to analyze gain and loss events based on the phyletic patterns of perfect and imperfect IRs and the reconstructed phylogenetic tree (Cohen et al. 2010; Cohen and Pupko 2011). To perform this analysis, each IR locus was first encoded as perfect (“1”), imperfect (“0”), or unknown (“?”) for all strains. The analysis was conducted using default settings for both the likelihood-based and stochastic mapping methods, enabling estimation of the expected number of gain events in each IR locus across the user tree. We define the cutoff of 0.9 as a high expectation for a gain event. iTOL was used for visualization (Letunic and Bork 2021).

Modification of Yeast BY4741 Genome Using CRISPR Cas9

We planned gRNA sequence to guide Cas9 to cleave near the IR sites using Benchling. The Guide RNA sequences were ligated to the bRA66 plasmid, in which both gRNA and Cas9 are encoded. bRa66 plasmid was first cut by Bpl1 enzyme (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts). Reaction consisted of 1 µg bRA66 plasmid, 1 µl of 50× SAM, 5 µl of 10× Buffer Tango, 10 Units of Bpl1 enzyme, and 34 µl of double-distilled water. Digestion was done overnight at 37 °C. Next, oligonucleotides were ligated to the cut plasmid (sequence is shown in Table S9 as gRNA). The synthesized gRNA encoding oligonucleotides were diluted to 100 µM. 5 µl from each strand pair was mixed and vortexed briefly. The strands were annealed using SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler PCR machine with the following protocol: 95 °C for 5 min, gradual decrease to 85 °C (2 °C per second), and gradual decrease to 25 °C (0.1 °C per second). Annealed gRNA strands were kept on ice. Annealed gRNA oligonucleotides were ligated with bRA66 plasmid using T4 ligase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts) with a 3:1 molar ratio of insert over vector. 2 µl of T4 Buffer, 400 Units of T4 Ligase, and 10.3 µl of double-distilled water. Ligation was done at 16 °C overnight. The ligated products were then transformed into NEB10-beta Electrocompetent cells using Biorad's MicroPulser Electroporator, and transformed cells were spread on LB + 100 µg/ml Ampicillin plates. Positive clones that contained the insert were verified by sequencing. Next, we transformed BY4741 yeast with the bRA66 plasmid containing the gRNA sequence and the Donor DNA sequences. The donor DNA molecules consisted of the expanded IR with 50 base pairs from each side that are homologous to BY4741 wild-type (Table S9). Donor DNA strands were diluted to 100 mM each. We then took 11 µl from each tube and mixed each pair (Forward and Reverse strands) using a brief vortex to make a 50 mM solution. Strands were then annealed at 94 °C for 2 min and kept on ice.

Transformation was done using the modified LiAc protocol as described in Storici and Resnick (2006). 1 µg of gRNA plasmid and 20 µl of 50 µM donor DNA were added to 50 µl of the yeast cell solution. Cells were spread on YP-GAL + 400 µg/ml hygromycin plates.

Hygromycin-resistant colonies were isolated and sequenced around the CRISPR Cas9 cut site to verify the genetic modification. The colonies were then streaked on YPD plates for several rounds, and hygromycin-sensitive clones were selected (loss of bRA66 plasmid).

The GRE2 IR-expanded mutant was created in a different way using the Delitto Perfetto method (Storici and Resnick 2006). To this end, using pCORE URA3, KanMax4 plasmid (Storici and Resnick 2006) was used in a PCR reaction as a template. GRE2 core cassette primers 1 and 2 (Table S9) were used to amplify the desired product. These primers anneal pCORE and contain homology to the GRE2 site of modification. After PCR and product clean-up (as above), the PCR fragment was used to transform BY4741 as described above. The yeast suspension was spread on—URA complete plates. Several transformants were picked, and replicas were plated on YPD plates containing G418. URA autotroph, G418-resistant colonies were confirmed to have the CORE cassette inserted at the desired locus using GRE2 verification primers (Table S9). Next, these colonies were transformed with GRE2 donor oligonucleotides that contain the desired change (IR-expansion) at the GRE2 locus and flanking sequence homologous to the BY4741 genome. Colonies were selected on 5-FOA plates and then confirmed to be URA auxotrophs and G418 sensitive. Confirmed colonies were verified to include the desired modification by sequencing the PCR product using GRE2 verification oligonucleotides as primers.

PCR

To verify the IR-expanded mutants, the loci were amplified using PCR. PCR primers were planned by the Benchling program and ordered from IDT DNA (Coralville, Iowa). For the PCR reaction, we used 100 ng of extracted DNA, 0.5 µl of each 10 µM primer (Table S9), Taq 2X (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and double-distilled water to a 25 µl total volume of reaction.

The amplification was done in a SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler PCR machine using the following program: First, there was an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min. Next, there were 30 cycles of the following: subsequent denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing for 30 s (each primer had a different annealing temperature, see Table S9), and extension was done at 74 °C, with 1 kb per minute. Final extension was 7 min at 74 °C.

5 µl of the PCR samples were loaded into 1% agarose gel and run using electrophoresis, to check the existence of the bands. When we got a desired band, the remaining PCR samples were purified using the GeneJet PCR purification kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). PCR products were sequenced.

Growth Assays

Wild-type and ERT1 IR-expanded mutant were grown in complete media with different carbon sources: glucose, galactose, glycerol, and ethyl-acetate (see above).

Wild-type and GRE2 IR-expanded mutant were grown in compete + glucose 2% media with different concentrations of glycolaldehyde.

Growth in Media—96-Well Plate Reader

Yeast colonies were isolated from YPD plates and grown in with shaking at 30 °C for 2 d. The culture was diluted to an O.D of 0.01(600 nm) in selected growing medium. 100 µl cultures were then grown using a Tecan Spark plate reader for about 48 h at 30 °C. Growth protocol: 250 cycles, with each cycle containing 30 s of waiting, 6 min of shaking, and additional waiting time. O.D reading was done during waiting time, every 22 min. Shaking was done at an orbital amplitude of 1.5 mm.

Growth in Media-Erlenmeyer Breathing Flask

Colonies from BY4741 and ERT1 IR-expanded mutant were isolated from YPD plates and diluted in 2 ml of liquid YPD media each. Solutions were shaken at 30 °C for 2 d. At the end of the inoculation, the samples were diluted to 600 nm O.D of 0.01 in a solution of 20 ml of complete media with 2% of ethylacetate and grown for 5 d at 30 °C at 200 rounds per minute. The OD at 600 nm of the samples was measured every 24 h.

Calculation of Generation Time

The generation time of plate reader samples was calculated using the Growthcurver R package (Sprouffske and Wagner 2016). Using this package, we were able to get graphs that were normalized to the blank colonies, and we got generation time and growth rate results.

RNA Extraction from Yeast

Cells were collected for RNA extraction after 24 h of inoculation. RNA extraction was done using the Epicentre MaterPure RNA purification (Illumina Company). The sample's RNA concentration was measured using Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen), and the quality of the samples was checked using the Agilent Technologies 2200 tapestation. Samples that contained high amounts of DNA were treated with DNase following TRIzol RNA purification, as advised by the Invitrogen user guide for DNase. The samples were incubated in TRIzol reagent (SIGMA, Rehovot, Israel) and Chloroform (Sigma Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany). Using centrifugation, the mixture is separated into a lower phenol–chloroform fraction and a colorless upper aqueous phase containing the RNA. The RNA was isolated and precipitated in isopropanol (SIGMA Life Science, Rehovot, Israel). Samples were then incubated and centrifuged. Next, the RNA was washed with Ethanol (CARLO ERBA Reagents GmbH, Emmendingen, Germany) and solubilized in RNAse-free water.

RNA Sequencing and Analysis

RNA was sent for single-end sequencing at the genomic application lab of the Faculty of Medicine of the Hebrew University. Analysis was done using the following pipeline: reads were trimmed using fastp v1.01 (Chen 2023) and aligned to the reference genome (GCF_000146045.2) using STAR v2.7.11b (Dobin et al. 2013). Read quality was assessed using FASTQC v0.11.8, before and after trimming. Duplicates were removed using GATK4 v4.5 (McKenna et al. 2010). Reads were counted using HTseq-count v2.05 with intersection-nonempty mode. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2 v1.44.0 (Love et al. 2014) using control as the reference level. For further analysis, only genes with a change in adjusted P value ≤ 0.05, where their log2fold change was greater than or equal to 1 (“Over-expressed genes”) and smaller than or equal to minus 1 (“Under-expressed genes”). To classify DEGs to cellular pathways and networks, STRING analysis (Szklarczyk et al. 2023) was performed (0.4 confidence score; full STRING network). The most connected clusters are then visualized using Cytoscape with node color representing the log2 fold change (Doncheva et al. 2023). A Venn diagram was plotted using VennDiagram R package (Gao et al. 2021).
