CoReGRN: a context-aware post-processing framework for gene regulatory network inference revealing regulatory signatures in HIV-Leishmaniasis
Authors: Emma Paul M., Jereesh A. S., G. Santhosh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate inference of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) from single-cell gene expression data is challenging due to noise, data sparsity, and variability in gene-gene associations across cells. We propose CoReGRN (Contextual Refinement of Gene Regulatory Networks), a nonparametric, context-aware post-processing framework that refines inferred GRNs by reweighting candidate regulatory interactions using Mutual Information based association strength and local network context. The method uses empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) based scores to assess how unusual each interaction is relative to the connectivity patterns of the genes it connects. Then combines this contextual information with the original edge confidence scores. We evaluate the CoReGRN framework on gold-standard datasets from the BEELINE benchmark suite across multiple state-of-the-art GRN inference algorithms. The results show consistent performance improvements, with average absolute gains of 0.097 in AUROC, 0.130 in AUPR, 0.133 in MCC, and 0.095 in F1-Score. We further apply the framework to a single-cell HIV-Leishmaniasis dataset, where the refined networks support the analysis of disease-specific regulatory hubs and interactions. Comparison with existing biological knowledge identifies both known and potentially novel regulatory relationships across HIV infection, HIV-Leishmaniasis, and HIV-Visceral Leishmaniasis conditions. The analysis includes hub gene identification, interaction network analysis, and literature based validation using GeneMANIA.

Introduction

Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) provide a structured representation of transcriptional control, enabling insight into how genes coordinate cellular function. With the advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), it has become possible to explore these networks at higher resolution. However, inferring accurate GRNs from single-cell data remains a major computational challenge due to technical noise, sparsity, and cell-type heterogeneity, which collectively degrade the performance of existing inference algorithms [1, 2].

A wide range of GRN inference algorithms have been proposed, including tree-based approaches, Mutual Information (MI) based methods and network propagation models. Although these inference algorithms have advanced the field considerably, the raw predicted networks often contain spurious or indirect interactions, making post-processing a critical step for improving biological relevance and interpretability.

Some widely used post-processing strategies include edge refinement, reweighting, and insertion of prior knowledge to improve network accuracy and robustness [3, 4]. This work introduces a post-processing module designed to improve the predicted edges from existing GRN inference algorithms using reweighting of predicted scores.

The CoReGRN module can be combined with existing algorithms, serving as an enhancement module rather than a replacement or stand alone framework. We evaluate this approach using gold-standard datasets from the BEELINE [2] benchmark suite across multiple algorithms. The results showed consistent improvements in Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), Area Under the Precision–Recall curve (AUPR), Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and F1-Score.

One of the latest technique AGRN combined with the CoReGRN is further applied to an HIV-Leishmaniasis coinfection single-cell dataset. Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and leishmaniasis represent two major infectious diseases that disproportionately affect vulnerable populations, particularly in low- and middle-income regions where their geographic distributions overlap [5]. HIV infects and depletes CD4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{+}$$\end{document} T cells and other immune cells, leading to progressive immunosuppression, while Leishmania parasites such as L. donovani and other species cause a spectrum of clinical disease from cutaneous lesions to lethal Visceral Leishmaniasis (VL) by infecting macrophages and evading host immunity [6, 7]. Co-infection with HIV and Leishmania profoundly exacerbates disease pathogenesis; HIV-mediated immunosuppression increases the susceptibility to active VL by orders of magnitude and facilitates parasite persistence, while Leishmania infection can accelerate HIV disease progression and activate latent viral reservoirs, compounding immune dysfunction [5, 8]. This synergistic interaction leads to higher treatment failure, relapse rates, and mortality compared with either infection alone and poses a significant barrier to control programs for both diseases [5].

Given the complex immune interactions and increasing occurrence of HIV-Leishmania co-infection, there is a need to better understand the cellular and molecular patterns that characterize the disease progression. The single-cell transcriptomic dataset used in this study and taken from the CZ CELLxGENE platform enables detailed analysis of immune cell states and gene expression dynamics in HIV and Leishmania co-infection [9].

The main contributions of this work are as follows. First, we introduce CoReGRN, a nonparametric contextual reweighting framework for refining pre-existing gene regulatory networks by combining MI-based association strength with node-specific empirical context. Second, we evaluate the method on benchmark BEELINE datasets and show that it improves GRN reconstruction performance over the baseline networks across multiple evaluation metrics, with statistically significant gains. Third, we illustrate its practical use in a biologically relevant HIV-Leishmaniasis case study, in which genes were first shortlisted and an initial GRN was inferred using AGRN [10]. CoReGRN was then applied to refine this network. The resulting network for the four conditions (HIV infection, Leishmaniasis, HIV-VL and Normal) was then examined for its biological plausibility in the context of HIV-Leishmaniasis coinfection.

Related works

Gene regulatory network (GRN) inference uses gene expression data to identify regulatory relationships between genes (causal or correlative) to better understand cellular processes and disease progression. The development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has made it possible to reconstruct GRNs at the level of individual cells, revealing cellular heterogeneity that is not visible in bulk expression data [2]. As a result, many algorithms have been proposed for GRN inference from single-cell data, each based on different modeling assumptions and computational strategies. Boolean and logic-based approaches, such as SCNS (Single Cell Network Synthesis) [11], construct executable regulatory networks from discretized single-cell expression states and can be used to simulate state transitions. Other methods, including dynamic correlation based approaches like LEAP (Lag-based Expression Association for Pseudotime-series) [12], use pseudotemporal ordering to identify time-lagged gene relationships and infer potential regulatory directionality. Probabilistic models such as GRNVBEM [13] employ variational Bayesian expectation-maximization to estimate autoregressive network structures with uncertainty quantification, while SINGE (Single-cell Inference of Networks using Granger Ensembles) [14] extends Granger causality analysis to irregularly sampled pseudotime data through kernel-based smoothing and ensemble aggregation.

Machine-learning and information-theoretic approaches have further diversified GRN inference strategies. GreyNet [15] combines grey relational analysis with dynamic windowing and multivariate regression to infer directed and weighted networks. SCRIBE [16] leverages Restricted Directed Information (RDI) and RNA velocity to quantify information flow between genes, improving causal inference through integration of transcriptional dynamics. AGRN [10] employs an ensemble machine learning framework that integrates random forest, extra tree, and support vector regressors, using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to compute gene importance scores. By combining the strengths of tree-based and kernel-based models through optimized weighting, AGRN achieves more accurate GRN inference across benchmark datasets, outperforming many existing tree-based methods such as GENIE3 [17] and GRNBoost2 [18] in evaluation metrics [10].

Classical information theoretic methods have also played a foundational role in GRN inference from transcriptomic data. Relevance networks and the Context Likelihood of Relatedness (CLR) framework [4] introduced the principle of evaluating gene-gene associations relative to gene specific background distributions, thereby reducing indirect and promiscuous correlations. Similarly, ARACNE [19] applied the data processing inequality to prune indirect interactions and recover sparse regulatory structures. While these methods were originally developed for bulk expression data and require exhaustive pairwise computations, their core ideas, context-aware scoring and suppression of indirect effects continue to influence modern GRN inference pipelines. However, their direct application to single-cell data remains challenging due to sparsity, noise, and scalability constraints, motivating adaptations and modular refinements rather than full network reconstruction.

While considerable attention has been devoted to data preprocessing in GRN inference, the role of post-processing predicted networks has received comparatively less systematic analysis. GRN inference methods often produce dense, noisy edge lists whose raw confidence scores are not directly comparable across genes or methods, complicating interpretation and downstream analysis. Post-processing strategies such as score normalization, contextual filtering, and network refinement can help mitigate method-specific biases, enhance signal-to-noise ratios, and improve the biological coherence of inferred networks. However, unlike preprocessing, post-processing remains largely ad hoc and method dependent, with few standardized approaches for refining inferred GRN in a statistically principled manner.

Here, we introduce a CLR (Context Likelihood of Relatedness) inspired, nonparametric contextual reweighting technique that refines pre-existing gene networks by integrating MI based association strength with node-specific empirical context. Our approach replaces parametric z-score normalization with empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs), enabling robust contextual scoring without distributional assumptions, and blends this context aware score with the original edge confidence. This design allows the method to act as a general-purpose refinement layer applicable to a wide range of inferred networks.

Data and methods

BEELINE benchmark datasets

To evaluate the effects of post-processing on GRN inference, we used four gold-standard datasets from the BEELINE benchmark suite GSD, HSC, VSC, and mCAD; each with ten simulations [2]. Each dataset provides simulated single-cell gene expression matrices with corresponding curated ground-truth networks, enabling objective performance assessment. The GSD network contains 19 genes and 86 directed edges, the HSC network includes 11 genes and 30 edges, the VSC dataset includes 8 genes and 15 interactions and the mCAD dataset has 5 genes and 14 interactions. Each dataset has a total cell count of 2000. The datasets were used as reference systems to quantify algorithmic improvements introduced by the CoReGRN.

HIV-Leishmaniasis RNA-seq dataset

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data used in this study were obtained from a publicly available CZ CELLxGENE collection, generated as part of a recently published study on HIV and Leishmaniasis co-infection [9]. The dataset comprises cross-sectional samples spanning multiple disease conditions and includes a total of 17,308 cells. For the purposes of this work, four disease categories were considered: normal (healthy controls, 5,582 cells), HIV infectious disease (1,751 cells), HIV infectious disease with Leishmaniasis (2,354 cells), and HIV infectious disease with VL (7,621 cells). Given the cross-sectional nature of the dataset, all analyses were performed at a single time point per individual without longitudinal tracking. All four datasets had a total of 35,477 genes. To focus on biologically relevant gene regulatory mechanisms associated with infection and co-infection states, disease-associated genes were curated from DisGeNET [20, 21], prioritizing high-confidence genes linked to HIV infection, Leishmaniasis, and HIV-VL. This resulted in three disease-specific gene sets comprising 32 genes for HIV infection, 21 genes for Leishmaniasis, and 18 genes for HIV-VL.

For each disease condition (HIV infection, HIV-Leishmaniasis, and HIV-VL), the disease-associated gene list was used to subset the scRNA-seq expression matrix. For comparison, a corresponding healthy control subset was created for each condition using the same disease specific gene list, so that the disease and control data contained the same set of genes. This approach allowed direct condition-specific comparisons while reducing potential confounding due to differences in gene selection. In addition, because the CoReGRN relies on pairwise association measures and context-aware normalization, restricting the gene set substantially reduces the combinatorial search space and enables efficient computation without altering the underlying scoring framework.

The filtered expression matrices were then used for GRN inference. For each disease-control pair, the selected gene sets were analyzed using the AGRN inference framework, followed by post-processing module proposed in this study to refine the inferred regulatory interactions. AGRN was chosen for the case study because it is a recent ensemble based GRN inference method that combines multiple machine learning models and was reported to perform strongly on benchmark datasets. We therefore used it as a representative high performing upstream inference method. This workflow ensured that GRN inference focused on disease-relevant genes while allowing consistent comparisons across disease conditions and their matched controls. Empirical context normalization via ECDF

To incorporate network context, we compute an empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) based score for each edge endpoint. Specifically, for a given edge (i, j), the normalized MI value is ranked among all MI values of edges incident to gene i, yielding a percentile score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i$$\end{document}. These percentile scores quantify how exceptional the association between i and j is relative to each gene’s local neighborhood in the network. Blending with original network confidence

To incorporate prior network knowledge, the original edge weights are normalized:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{w}_k = \frac{w_k - \min _{\ell } w_{\ell }}{\max _{\ell } w_{\ell } - \min _{\ell } w_{\ell } + \varepsilon } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The final refined edge weight is obtained via linear blending:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_k = \alpha \tilde{S}_k + (1 - \alpha )\tilde{w}_k, \qquad \alpha \in [0,1] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

When α = 1, the method relies solely on expression-derived context; smaller values of α regularize the result toward the prior network. This blending strategy allows the method to function as a tunable refinement layer, balancing new evidence derived from expression-based associations with prior or model-specific information encoded in the input network.

Evaluation

To ensure a fair and method-agnostic comparison across gene regulatory network inference algorithms with heterogeneous scoring scales, we adopted a rank-based evaluation strategy. For each inferred network, the AUROC and the AUPR were computed using continuous edge scores over the full universe of possible gene pairs, with missing edges assigned a score of zero.

For threshold-dependent metrics, including MCC, and F1-Score, binary predictions were derived by selecting the top 10% of edges among those explicitly reported by each inference method, ranked by their confidence scores. All remaining edges were treated as negatives. This approach avoids the use of absolute score thresholds, which are difficult to compare across methods because scoring ranges and distributions differ. Instead, it relies on the relative ranking of edges produced by each algorithm.

Classification performance was evaluated using a fixed fraction of the highest-confidence predictions. This provides a consistent basis for comparison across different GRN inference methods and focuses the analysis on the most biologically plausible regulatory interactions identified by each model.

To check whether the performance gains were statistically significant, the results before and after post-processing were compared for each evaluation metric across matched dataset-method pairs. A paired t-test was used to test for changes in the mean values, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used as a non-parametric robustness check.

Workflow

All analyses involving the HIV-Leishmaniasis dataset were conducted using Python on a high-performance Linux computing node equipped with 2x Intel Xeon Broadwell E5-2690 CPUs (14 physical cores each, 2.6 GHz) and 250 GB RAM. This level of computational power was mainly required for testing the various parameters on GRN inference on the HIV-Leishmaniasis dataset using AGRN. The CoReGRN post-processing component was relatively lightweight. The computational cost of CoReGRN was evaluated in a Google Colab environment with 12 GB RAM and 255 GB available disk space on the GSD-2000 dataset, which contained 342 candidate edges across 19 genes and 2000 cells. The post-processing step took 6.60 s, while the full pipeline, including data loading and preprocessing, took 11.80 s. Runtime increased approximately linearly with the number of candidate edges \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(T \propto E^{1.03})$$\end{document}, and most of the computational cost came from the mutual information calculation. By comparison, the contextual reweighting, blending, and filtering steps added very little additional overhead. For each GRN inference algorithm, we first generate GRNs for both the GSD, HSC, VSC and mCAD datasets. Each dataset contains 10 simulated gene expression samples. The post-processing module is subsequently applied and the resulting network is evaluated on AUPR, AUROC, F1-Score, MCC. This optimal pipeline is then applied to the HIV-Leishmaniasis dataset for each of the three disease conditions and corresponding normal states. The real dataset analysis begins by cleaning the single-cell dataset and extracting both normal and diseased samples across 3 diseased conditions. For algorithms that require pseudotime series, slingshot [24] was used to compute the corresponding pseudotime series data. After obtaining the GRNs, a comparison was performed were the diseased network is compared against normal. Finally, the top 10% of inferred regulatory edges are compared between normal and disease conditions to identify hub genes, disrupted regulatory relationships, overlap with existing literature, and novel edges.

Results and discussion

CoReGRN method is conceptually related to the GRN inference mechanism used in the Context Likelihood of Relatedness (CLR) framework and the ARACNe algorithm, which are both widely used for inferring GRNs from expression data, yet it differs from each in important respects. Like CLR, our approach evaluates pairwise associations in the context of gene-specific interaction distributions, thereby reducing the influence of globally correlated or hub nodes. However, whereas classical CLR computes MI for all possible gene pairs and derives parametric z-scores from full background distributions, our method applies a nonparametric, ECDF-based contextual normalization restricted to the edges present in a candidate network. This design enables robust context-aware refinement without assuming a specific distributional form and allows the method to scale as a post-processing step on networks produced by diverse inference pipelines. The proposed method assumes pairwise statistical dependencies and is therefore sensitive to the dimensionality of the gene set under consideration. By limiting the analysis to disease relevant genes, we focus on interactions within a biologically meaningful subnetwork and reduce the computational cost of evaluating all possible gene pairs. While this approach may miss regulatory interactions involving genes outside the selected set, it is consistent with the goal of this study, which is to characterize disease specific regulatory structure rather than reconstruct a genome wide network.

Several methods demonstrate consistently strong improvements across AUROC, AUPR, MCC, and F1-Score. In particular, GRNVBEM, GreyNet, GRISLI, SCRIBE, and SINCERITIES show large and well-balanced gains across all reported metrics. For example, GRNVBEM exhibits substantial increases in AUROC (+0.12), AUPR (+0.17), MCC (+0.12), and F1-Score (+0.09), indicating that contextual reweighting is particularly effective for probabilistic and ensemble-based inference methods. Similar performance gains are observed for GreyNet and SCRIBE, both of which benefit from improved edge ranking and downstream classification quality.

Notably, PPCOR stands out with an exceptionally large improvement in AUROC (+0.45) and AUPR (+0.29), suggesting that the proposed post-processing strategy markedly enhances the ranking quality of correlation-based methods. However, the corresponding gains in MCC (+0.12) and F1-Score (+0.09) are comparatively modest, indicating that improved ranking does not always translate directly into optimal classification performance under a fixed operating point.

In contrast, some methods show smaller or more variable improvements. For instance, AGRN, GENIE3, and Normi exhibit only modest gains in AUROC and AUPR, along with very small or near-zero changes in MCC and F1-Score (including slight negative changes for GENIE3). These results suggest that CoReGRN is most useful when the original inferred network contains substantial noise, weak edge separation. Since GRN inference methods differ in their modeling assumptions and in the sparsity and calibration of their score distributions, the benefit of post-processing is not uniform. CoReGRN appears to function as a method-dependent denoising and edge-refinement step that is most beneficial when recoverable false positives remain in the original network. These findings suggest that future work should link post-processing more closely to the behavior of individual inference methods in order to develop more robust and method-aware denoising strategies for GRN reconstruction.

To evaluate whether the gains after CoReGRN post-processing were statistically robust, paired significance tests were conducted across method–dataset comparisons. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed significant improvements across all evaluated metrics, including AUPR (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 4.73 \times 10^{-10}$$\end{document}), AUROC (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 3.44 \times 10^{-9}$$\end{document}), F1-Score (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 5.93 \times 10^{-8}$$\end{document}), and MCC (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 6.12 \times 10^{-8}$$\end{document}). Mean values increased from 0.6063 to 0.7367 for AUPR, from 0.7900 to 0.8868 for AUROC, from 0.2400 to 0.3354 for F1-Score, and from 0.1884 to 0.3219 for MCC.

Overall, the results indicate that the proposed context-aware post-processing framework can produce clear absolute performance improvements across both ranking-based and threshold-based evaluation metrics. While the degree of improvement varies across algorithms, the consistent gains observed for a broad range of inference methods highlight the general applicability of the approach. The observed variability further suggests that the effectiveness of post-processing is influenced by the underlying score distributions and modeling assumptions of each method, motivating future work on adaptive or method-specific refinement strategies.

Alpha sensitivity analysis

To evaluate robustness to the score-fusion parameter, we carried out a sensitivity analysis for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in [0.1, 0.9]$$\end{document} using the four BEELINE datasets: GSD, HSC, VSC, and mCAD. Overall, CoReGRN performed consistently across this range, with a general tendency toward better results at higher α values. Averaged across datasets, AUPR increased from 0.6525 at α = 0.1 to 0.7317 at α = 0.9, while AUROC rose from 0.8386 to 0.8842 over the same range. This trend is shown in the Supplementary Figures 1 and 2 in Supplementary File. Performance gains began to level off when α was between 0.7 and 0.9.

At the dataset level, HSC and VSC showed consistent improvement as more weight was assigned to the post-processing score, suggesting that expression-based recalibration provided useful information beyond the original edge ranking. In GSD, the improvement was smaller and varied by metric, whereas in mCAD the effect was minimal, which is consistent with its already strong baseline performance. Overall, the sensitivity analysis suggests that setting α in the range of 0.7–0.9 provides the best, or close to the best, average performance across datasets. Most low to moderate performing methods benefited from larger α values, with performance typically peaking around 0.8–0.9. In contrast, stronger or more stable methods, such as GENIE3, PIDC, AGRN, and Normi, were much less sensitive to α and in some cases showed slight declines at higher values. The results of the α sensitivity analysis are provided in the Supplementary Analysis file, including Supplementary Figures 1 and 2 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Sensitivity analysis of edge thresholds

Performance was evaluated not only using the threshold-independent metrics AUPR and AUROC, but also using the threshold-dependent metrics MCC and F1-Score. Threshold-based evaluation was performed on the top 5%, 10%, 20%, 30%, and 50% of ranked edges. In general, both MCC and F1-Score improved as the threshold was relaxed and a larger fraction of edges was retained, largely because recall increased more rapidly than precision declined. Nevertheless, stricter thresholds may remain preferable for biological interpretation when the objective is to obtain a smaller, high-confidence subnetwork. The results of the edge threshold sensitivity analysis are provided in the Supplementary Analysis file, in Supplementary Figures 3 to 6.

Comparison with CLR and ARACNE

To assess the effectiveness of the proposed post-processing strategy, we compared its average improvement over baseline methods against two similar approaches, CLR and ARACNE used as post processing strategy, across all datasets and methods. The proposed approach consistently achieved the largest gains on every evaluation metric as shown in Supplementary Table 3 in the Supplementary Files. Specifically, the proposed method yielded average improvements of 0.0968 in AUROC, 0.1303 in AUPR, 0.1335 in MCC, 0.2027 in Precision, 0.0625 in Recall, and 0.0954 in F1-Score. In comparison, CLR achieved improvements of 0.0487, 0.0744, 0.0883, 0.1347, 0.0410, and 0.0627, respectively, while ARACNE produced substantially smaller gains of 0.0059, 0.0122, 0.0160, 0.0240, 0.0077, and 0.0116. On average, the proposed post-processing method showed larger improvements than CLR and ARACNE across all considered metrics, suggesting that it is a promising and effective refinement strategy for enhancing inferred regulatory networks.

HIV-Leishmaniasis dataset

This study presents a network-centric view of host regulatory responses across HIV infection, HIV-Leishmaniasis, HIV-VL, and healthy conditions by integrating disease relevant gene sets with GRN inference. For each condition, a GRN was inferred independently using the corresponding expression data and a disease relevant shortlisted gene set. The disease-relevant shortlisted gene sets for the three conditions HIV infection, HIV-Leishmaniasis, and HIV-VL are discussed below, together with their shared and condition-specific genes. This is followed by a network comparison at three levels: hub gene analysis, interaction analysis, and biological support for inferred interactions. Hub gene analysis examines the genes with the highest connectivity across the four conditions and compares their patterns of interaction. Interaction analysis focuses on the overlap among the top 10% highest-ranked inferred interactions. Finally, biological support for inferred interactions assesses whether the overlapping edges are supported by the existing literature. All networks in this work are plotted using the Cytoscape tool [25, 26].

A core set of immune and inflammatory genes CRP, IL-10, IFNG, and MBL2 were found to be shared across all three disease conditions and consequently incorporated into all GRNs. These genes are well-established mediators of inflammation, immune regulation, and host defense in chronic infectious diseases [27–30].

A pronounced molecular overlap was observed between the HIV-Leishmaniasis and HIV-VL gene sets, consistent with their shared parasitic etiology. Common genes included key pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL1B, IL-6, TNF), chemokines involved in leukocyte recruitment (CXCL8), macrophage activation and survival factors (CSF2, LTA), and antigen presentation genes (HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1), all of which play central roles in anti-leishmanial immune responses [31, 32].

Despite this overlap, HIV-VL data contained a small number of genes not present in the broader HIV-Leishmaniasis set, including IL10RA, IL2RB, and IL-32. These genes have been implicated in cytokine signaling modulation, T-cell responsiveness, and inflammatory regulation during chronic infection, indicating condition-specific regulatory components incorporated into the HIV-VL GRN [31, 33].

In contrast, the HIV data based gene set exhibited a broader immune regulatory repertoire. Unique genes in this condition were predominantly associated with T-cell activation and co-stimulation (CD28, CD40LG, IL-2, IL2RA), cytotoxic lymphocyte function (PRF1, FAS, KIR3DL1), and chemokine-mediated immune cell trafficking (CCL3, CCL4L2, CCL5), reflecting hallmark mechanisms of HIV-associated immune activation and cytotoxic responses [34–36].

The overlap between HIV data and HIV-Leishmaniasis data, including CCR5, IL-10, IFNG, MBL2, CCL2, and CRP, highlights pathways related to immune activation, cytokine regulation, and cellular trafficking [37, 38].

Hub gene analysis

Hub genes were defined as nodes contributing at least 20% of the total regulatory edges in each inferred GRN. Comparative analysis of hub distributions revealed marked condition-specific differences in network centrality patterns.

In contrast, significant hub overlap was observed between the HIV-Leishmaniasis network and the normal condition, including HLA-DQA1, HLA-DRB1, SLC11A1, and HMOX1. The recurrence of MHC class II genes (HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DRB1) is consistent with the central role of antigen presentation in anti-leishmanial immune responses [32, 39]. The presence of SLC11A1, a macrophage associated resistance gene involved in intracellular pathogen control, further supports macrophage centered regulatory architecture in Leishmaniasis associated networks [40]. Similarly, HO-1 (encoded by HMOX1) is a stress-inducible enzyme that modulates inflammation and immune responses, including anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects, across a variety of pathological conditions [41].

The HIV-VL network shared HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DRB1 as hubs with the HIV-Leishmaniasis network, highlighting the sustained importance of antigen presentation across Leishmaniasis associated disease states. Additionally, IL-32 and HLA-DRB1 emerged as shared hubs between HIV-VL and the normal network. IL-32 has been reported to play a role in amplifying inflammatory cytokine responses and shaping immune activation during chronic infection, including Leishmaniasis and HIV [33, 42].

Condition-specific hub analysis revealed that HIV-VL uniquely exhibited IL10RA and HLA-DQA1 as dominant hubs, suggesting increased regulatory emphasis on immunomodulatory signaling and antigen presentation in the visceral disease context. IL-10 signaling has been widely associated with disease persistence and immune suppression in VL [31, 43]. In contrast, the HIV-only and HIV-Leishmaniasis networks did not display unique hub genes under the applied threshold.

The normal network uniquely identified FCGR3A as a hub, its established role in Fc receptor-mediated immune surveillance and basal myeloid cell function in healthy immune systems [44].

Collectively, these results indicate that GRN hub architecture is highly context-dependent, with Leishmaniasis, particularly visceral disease, driving a shift toward antigen presentation, macrophage-associated resistance, and cytokine modulation as central regulatory processes. No common hub genes were observed between the HIV-only network and either of the co-infection networks, which could be attributed to the shortlisted gene set used.

Interaction analysis

To further investigate regulatory similarity beyond hub nodes, we compared high-confidence regulatory edges across disease conditions using the top 10% of inferred interactions. Edge-level comparisons showed clear differences in network overlap across disease conditions.

A relatively large number of top-ranked edges were shared between the HIV and normal condition-specific networks (98 edges), indicating substantial overlap in inferred interaction structure within the analyzed gene space. Moderate overlap was also observed among Leishmaniasis-related conditions, with 38 shared edges between HIV-Leishmaniasis and normal and 30 shared edges between HIV-VL and normal, suggesting conserved regulatory patterns linked to parasitic infection. In contrast, only 7 edges were shared between HIV-VL and Leishmaniasis, indicating limited overlap which could be due to the specific disease subset choosen for analysis.

Unique edge analysis further demonstrated condition specific regulatory signatures, with each disease state retaining a distinct subset of high-confidence interactions. The number of unique edges ranged from 10 to 49. In the HIV GRN, the topology is dominated by dense interconnections among chemokine signaling and cytotoxic effector nodes, including CCL5, PRF1, HLA–B, and FCGR3A, forming a compact immune response subgraph consistent with CD8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{+}$$\end{document} T-cell and NK cell-mediated viral control mechanisms [36, 45–47]. In the HIV-Leishmaniasis network, a highly interconnected module is centered on antigen presentation and macrophage associated genes (HLA-DQA1, HLA-DRB1, SLC11A1, HMOX1), reflecting coordinated innate immune and inflammatory processes required for effective control of intracellular Leishmania infection [40, 48]. In the HIV-VL GRN, overall network centralization increases markedly, with IL-32 emerging as a dominant hub node linking cytokine signaling, immune regulation, and macrophage activation pathways, consistent with amplified inflammatory coupling in severe disease states [49]. The seven shared edges between HIV-VL and Leishmaniasis included regulatory interactions involving HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, and IL10RA. The complete list of shared and unique edges is provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Despite its flexibility and robustness, the CoReGRN has several limitations. First, Since the upstream GRN inference methods in this setting assign weighted scores across the full candidate edge space, CoReGRN post-processes the entire ranked set of candidate interactions rather than a small preselected subset. Its ability to recover true edges, however, is limited when the original scores are zero or close to zero, and it cannot identify interactions involving genes outside the analyzed gene set. Therefore, improvements produced by CoReGRN should be interpreted as improvements in edge prioritization within the inferred candidate space. Second, contextual normalization is computed only over edges incident to each gene within the candidate network rather than over all possible gene pairs, which may bias percentile estimates for genes with very few incident edges or highly uneven degree distributions. Third, the approach remains fundamentally pairwise and does not explicitly model higher-order or combinatorial regulatory logic, potentially limiting its ability to capture interactions that depend on coordinated regulation by multiple factors or conditional activation. In addition, because the method relies on MI estimated from gene expression data, its effectiveness depends on the availability of sufficiently diverse and informative samples; regulatory mechanisms governed primarily by post-transcriptional or post-translational processes may therefore not be detected. MI was estimated using a nonparametric k-nearest neighbor approach, in which MI is derived from entropy estimates based on distances between neighboring observations. No explicit imputation or zero-inflation-specific correction was included in the current implementation. Imputation was avoided to limit the risk of introducing artificial correlations, although this means that the MI estimates may still be affected by sparsity. Future work could explore whether sparsity-aware preprocessing, dropout-aware estimators, or zero-inflation-aware dependence measures improve the robustness of CoReGRN. Another limitation of this study is that, As the analyzed genes were already preselected for disease relevance, standard enrichment analysis would provide only partly circular support. The biological interpretation therefore focused not on re-establishing disease association, but on whether the refined network captured plausible interaction patterns, hub genes, and subnetworks consistent with known HIV-Leishmaniasis biology. Comparison with GeneMANIA served only as supportive evidence for biological plausibility, not as a substitute for functional validation. Further evaluation of CoReGRN on a broader range of simulation settings and independent biological datasets, particularly those differing in noise, sparsity, and regulatory complexity, would help clarify the conditions in which the method performs best. Finally this study did not cover a standardized runtime and memory benchmark comparison across all 17 upstream GRN inference methods. Since these methods were executed in different software and computational environments, direct efficiency comparisons were not feasible. A controlled benchmark of both inference accuracy and computational cost would therefore be an important direction for future work.

Conclusion

We present a CLR inspired, nonparametric framework for network refinement that combines MI based association strength with node specific contextual normalization. Instead of using parametric background models, the method relies on ECDF-based scoring and operates directly on existing networks, making it a flexible and robust post-processing approach. The CoReGRN method is well suited as a post-processing step in modern network inference pipelines, where candidate interactions have already been identified but require further contextual filtering. By combining context-aware scores with the original edge confidence values, a refined network is created which shows consistent improvements in the evaluation metrics applied. Overall, this approach extends the core principles of CLR to a refinement technique of gene networks derived from heterogeneous inference methods.
