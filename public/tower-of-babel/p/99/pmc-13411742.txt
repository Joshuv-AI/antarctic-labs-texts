Prevalence, associated factors, and outcomes of workplace violence against doctors in China: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kaiqin Li, Elaina Rose Johar, Jiali Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorkplace violence is a major occupational and public health concern in healthcare settings. Although doctors are a high-risk group, existing research has primarily focused on healthcare workers in general or on nurses, with less attention to doctors. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence on the prevalence, types, associated factors, outcomes, and perceived impacts of workplace violence against doctors in China.MethodsThis review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure were systematically searched from database inception to 31 December 2025. Eligible studies were Chinese- or English-language cross-sectional or mixed-methods studies with extractable cross-sectional data that reported the 12-month prevalence of overall workplace violence among doctors in China. Data were synthesized using random-effects meta-analysis in R, and heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I2 statistics.ResultsNineteen studies involving 114,270 doctors were included. The pooled 12-month prevalence of workplace violence against doctors in China was 60.85% (95% CI: 49.16%–71.42%; range: 14.26%–90.40%). Verbal violence was the most common type, with a pooled prevalence of 49.56% (95% CI: 29.69%–69.57%), followed by threats (28.08%; 95% CI: 15.90%–44.64%), physical violence (14.36%; 95% CI: 7.77%–25.02%), sexual harassment (11.81%; 95% CI: 5.75%–22.73%), and sexual assault (4.66%; 95% CI: 2.19%–9.62%). Patients and their family members or relatives were the main perpetrators. Workplace violence was associated with demographic factors, professional characteristics, workload-related factors, and clinical setting and organizational factors, including male sex, higher educational attainment, professional title or level, longer working hours, and high-risk clinical settings. Subgroup analyses showed a significantly lower pooled prevalence during the COVID-19 pandemic than before the pandemic. Workplace violence was also associated with adverse psychological, occupational, and organizational outcomes; perceived impacts included emotional distress, reduced work efficiency, decreased patient trust, and thoughts of leaving the profession.ConclusionsWorkplace violence against doctors in China remains highly prevalent, with verbal violence as the dominant form. The findings suggest that workplace violence may be more likely in high-demand clinical contexts characterized by heavy workloads, frequent doctor–patient interactions, and unmet patient expectations. Structural, organizational, and policy measures are needed to reduce service pressure, strengthen prevention and reporting systems, and protect doctors’ well-being and retention.PROSPERO registration number CRD420251088438.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27898-w.Keywords: Workplace violence, Doctors, China, Meta-analysis

Background

The health workforce is a core resource for the effective functioning of the health system, and its size and quality are directly associated with the accessibility of healthcare services and national development [1, 2]. In 2023, the global health workforce was estimated at approximately 70 million, of whom about 52 million were doctors, nurses, midwives, dentists, and pharmacists [3]. Doctors are one of the five key occupations in the global health workforce [4]. In China, “doctor” refers to medical professionals who have obtained legal qualifications and are registered to practise in medical and healthcare institutions, including practising doctors and assistant practising doctors [5]. They play a central role in clinical decision-making, diagnosis, and treatment, and are critical to the coordination of patient care and the quality and safety of health services [6].

However, shortages of doctors have become a global challenge and have increasingly constrained the sustainable development of the health system [7, 8]. Despite their large numbers globally, there is a persistent and increasing shortage of doctors worldwide, driven by population growth and aging [2, 4]. Using harmonised data from labour force surveys, censuses, and the WHO National Health Workforce Accounts across 196 countries and territories from 1990 to 2019, Haakenstad et al. estimated that, as of 2019, an additional 6.4 million doctors would be required globally to meet minimum workforce density thresholds for achieving effective service coverage [9]. Therefore, Target 3.c of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) identifies strengthening health workforce retention as a key priority, particularly in developing countries [10].

Workplace violence (WPV) is one of the most complex and dangerous occupational hazards in the healthcare environment [11]. The International Labour Organization (ILO), International Council of Nurses (ICN), World Health Organization (WHO), and Public Services International (PSI) define WPV as incidents in which staff are abused, threatened, or assaulted in circumstances related to their work, including commuting to and from work, involving an explicit or implicit challenge to their safety, well-being, or health [12]. Health workers are known to be particularly at risk of WPV, with almost one quarter of all violent incidents at work concentrated in this sector [6]. Globally, between 8% and 38% of healthcare workers experience physical violence during their careers, while many more are subjected to threats or verbal aggression [13]. For doctors, WPV represents one of the most complex and dangerous occupational hazards, with serious implications for their safety and professional sustainability [14, 15].

WPV against doctors in China remains a pressing public health concern [16]. A nationwide large-scale study reported that 51.5% of doctors had experienced physical violence and 90.0% had experienced non-physical violence [17]. In some cases, WPV against doctors has escalated into severe medical violence incidents and become a significant social issue [18]. News reports from 2010 to 2020 revealed that nurses were more likely to experience non-lethal physical violence, whereas doctors faced a higher risk of lethal attacks [19]. The 2013–2021 litigation records revealed that the majority of victims in medical violence incidents were doctors (59.9%), followed by nurses (26.1%) [20].

WPV is increasingly recognized as a public health concern [21] and poses severe consequences not only for doctors but also for patients and hospitals. It harms doctors’ physical and mental health [22–24], weakens the doctor–patient relationship [25], and may contribute to higher hospital operating costs [26, 27]. Importantly, WPV may also be associated with retention-related outcomes among Chinese doctors, including burnout, lower job satisfaction, and turnover intention [28, 29]. Therefore, addressing WPV is relevant not only to occupational safety but also to the sustainability of the medical workforce and is consistent with SDG targets 3.c and 8.8, which emphasize health workforce retention and safe and secure working environments [10].

Despite growing recognition of this issue, the existing literature on WPV has focused mainly on healthcare workers in general or on nurses, with relatively little attention paid to doctors specifically [30]. This gap is important because doctors may constitute a specific high-risk occupational group while also occupying key clinical roles in diagnosis, treatment, and patient care. Greater attention to specific vulnerable groups may help policymakers develop strategies to support and incentivize healthcare workers to remain in their jobs [31]. In this context, evidence on WPV against doctors in China remains insufficiently synthesized, particularly regarding its prevalence, types, associated factors, outcomes, and perceived impacts [32]. To address these gaps, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to synthesize evidence on the prevalence, types, associated factors, outcomes, and perceived impacts of WPV against doctors in China.

Methods

Aim of the review

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize evidence from both Chinese- and English-language literature to estimate the prevalence and types of WPV against doctors in China and to summarize associated factors, outcomes, and perceived impacts. Potential strategies for preventing and managing WPV in clinical settings were also identified.

Design

This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [33]. A comprehensive and structured approach was used to identify, select, appraise, and synthesize studies on WPV against doctors in China.

Research question

This review was guided by the following question: What are the pooled 12-month prevalence and types of WPV against doctors in China, and what associated factors, outcomes, and perceived impacts are reported? The research question was aligned with the review’s objectives, title, and eligibility criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) the study population comprised doctors only, including physicians and relevant specialties (e.g., surgeons, internists, anesthesiologists, pediatricians, psychiatrists, and dentists); (2) the study reported the overall prevalence of WPV, rather than subtype-specific prevalence only; (3) the recall period was the previous 12 months, or was described as “past year” or “last year”; (4) the study used a cross-sectional design, or was a mixed-methods study from which relevant cross-sectional quantitative data could be extracted; and (5) the publication was in Chinese or English.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) they included mixed healthcare workers or any non-doctor groups (e.g., nurses, pharmacists, technicians, medical students, or other healthcare workers), even if doctor-related results were presented; (2) they reported only subtype-specific WPV prevalence and did not provide an overall WPV prevalence estimate; (3) they used a recall period other than the previous 12 months (e.g., lifetime, past week/month, or during employment) or did not specify the recall period; (4) they employed designs from which relevant cross-sectional quantitative prevalence data could not be extracted, such as cohort studies, case–control studies, purely qualitative studies, or intervention trials; or (5) they were published in languages other than Chinese or English.

Search strategy

To address the research question, a systematic search was conducted in Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and CNKI. The search strategy combined terms related to WPV, doctors, and China, using Boolean operators. Equivalent Chinese search terms were used for the CNKI search. The full search strategy for each database is provided in Additional file 1. Searches were conducted in four rounds. The initial search was performed in July 2025, followed by update searches in November 2025, February 2026, and April 2026. The final search covered records from database inception to 31 December 2025. Additional relevant studies were identified by manually screening the reference lists of all included articles. Full-text articles were retrieved for potentially eligible records that were accessible and not duplicates, and were assessed in detail. This review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251088438).

Search outcomes

The database search identified 623 records (PubMed: 100; Scopus: 258; Web of Science: 164; CNKI: 101). All records were exported into Excel and managed in Zotero. After removing 252 duplicate records, 371 records remained for title screening. Based on title screening, 194 records were excluded. The remaining 177 records proceeded to abstract screening, of which 89 were excluded. Consequently, 88 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 69 full-text articles were excluded for various reasons, including ineligible study design, full text not available, outcome not reported, recall period not 12 months, wrong population, or duplicate data source. Ultimately, 19 studies were included in the review. Two authors independently screened titles/abstracts and assessed full texts. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. The study selection process is presented in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1), and detailed screening decisions and reasons for exclusion at each stage are provided in Additional file 2.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized form developed in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology [34]. Extracted information included the first author and publication year, study design, setting, participant characteristics, effective response rate, and WPV measurement instrument. Key findings were also extracted, including the 12-month prevalence of WPV overall and by subtype, perpetrators, perceived causes, risk factors, statistically associated outcomes, descriptively reported perceived impacts, and recommendations. Two reviewers independently extracted all data, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Data synthesis and reporting

Extracted data were organized in Excel and summarized using descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, and ranges). Findings were presented as narrative summaries and in tables and figures. Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies, which comprises nine criteria [34]. These criteria assess the appropriateness of the sample frame and participant recruitment, sample size adequacy, detailed description of the study setting and participants, coverage of the identified sample, valid methods for WPV identification, standard and reliable measurement of WPV for all participants, appropriate statistical analysis, and the adequacy of the response rate. Two reviewers independently appraised each study, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion. Each criterion was rated as “Yes”, “No”, “Unclear”, or “Not applicable”. Studies with total scores of 7–9 were classified as having a low risk of bias, those with scores of 4–6 as having a moderate risk of bias, and those with scores of 0–3 as having a high risk of bias. The quality appraisal was used to describe the methodological robustness of the included studies rather than as an exclusion criterion. Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1). A random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled prevalence of WPV against doctors in China with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Forest plots were used to display study-specific prevalence estimates. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I2 statistic; values > 50% were considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity. Publication bias was explored using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially omitting each study to assess the robustness of the pooled estimate.

Results

Characteristics of the reviewed studies

A total of 19 original research articles published between 2014 and 2025 were included in this review. The reported data collection years ranged from 2010 to 2022, indicating a sustained research focus on this topic over more than a decade. Geographically, 12 studies were conducted in specific provinces or municipalities, while the remaining seven studies investigated nationwide samples. Altogether, the included studies contributed a pooled sample of 114,270 participants, comprising 58,810 males (51.46%) and 55,305 females (48.40%). This gender distribution is broadly consistent with the male-to-female pattern reported in China’s Seventh National Population Census (2020). A small discrepancy between the total sample size and the sum of gender-specific counts was observed in two studies due to missing gender information for 147 participants in Gong et al. (2014) [35] and 8 participants in Sun et al. (2017) [16]. The missing data were limited to sex information and should not affect the pooled prevalence of WPV, because prevalence was calculated as the proportion of participants reporting WPV among the total sample. Meta-analytic findings

Overall prevalence of workplace violence

In this study, the pooled prevalence of WPV against doctors in China was 60.85% (95% CI: 49.16%–71.42%), ranging from 14.26% to 90.40%. There was substantial heterogeneity across the included studies (I2 = 99.8%; p < 0.001). A random-effects model was therefore used to estimate the pooled 12-month prevalence of WPV against doctors, as shown in Fig. 2.

Prevalence by type of violence

Across the included studies, different instruments were used to measure WPV. Eight studies [17, 36, 41, 45, 47–50] used the Chinese version of the Workplace Violence Scale (WVS), developed by Wang et al. (2006) [37]; five studies [28, 40, 42, 43, 51] used a questionnaire developed by Chen (2011) [38]; two studies [16, 46] used a questionnaire used by Zhang et al. (2016) [39]; and four studies used a single-item question to assess WPV [14, 29, 35, 44]. Because WPV subtype definitions and labels varied across measurement approaches, the meta-analysis of prevalence by violence type was restricted to studies using the Chinese version of the WVS to improve comparability across studies. The Chinese version of the WVS classifies WPV into five subtypes: verbal abuse, threats, physical assault, verbal sexual harassment, and physical sexual assault.

Of the eight studies using the Chinese version of the WVS, one did not report subtype-specific prevalence [36]. Another study reported verbal sexual harassment and physical sexual assault as a combined category [47]. Consequently, seven studies were included in the pooled analyses of verbal abuse, threats, and physical assault [17, 41, 45, 47–50], and six studies were included in the pooled analyses of verbal sexual harassment and physical sexual assault [17, 41, 45, 48–50]. Across these studies, verbal violence had the highest pooled prevalence, at 49.56% (95% CI 29.69%–69.57%; I2 = 99.8%). This was followed by threats, with a pooled prevalence of 28.08% (95% CI 15.90%–44.64%; I2 = 99.7%), and physical violence, with a pooled prevalence of 14.36% (95% CI 7.77%–25.02%; I2 = 99.4%). Lower pooled prevalence estimates were observed for sexual harassment, at 11.81% (95% CI 5.75%–22.73%; I2 = 99.5%), and sexual assault, at 4.66% (95% CI 2.19%–9.62%; I2 = 99.8%). Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses of WPV prevalence against doctors in China were performed by survey period, study population, hospital level, hospital type, measurement instrument, and language (Table 5). Robustness of the pooled estimate

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using leave-one-out analyses and influence diagnostics. The pooled prevalence remained broadly stable after omitting each study in turn, ranging from 58.42% to 63.78% (overall pooled prevalence: 60.85%). Influence diagnostics based on a random-effects model on the logit-transformed scale suggested that most studies had a limited impact on the pooled estimate. Feng et al. (2022) [45] showed the greatest Cook’s distance (Cook’s distance = 0.265; DFFITS = − 0.538; studentized residual = − 2.277), and Yan et al. (2023) [17] showed the largest absolute DFFITS and studentized residual (Cook’s distance = 0.174; DFFITS = 0.566; studentized residual = 2.385). Hat values were similar across studies (0.052–0.053), indicating no single study exerted disproportionate leverage. Omitting Yan et al. (2023) [17] produced the largest reduction in between-study variance (τ2 from 1.1087 to 0.6021), but heterogeneity remained extremely high across all analyses (I2 = 99.7%–99.9%).

Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. 3) and by quantitative tests. Egger’s regression test did not indicate funnel plot asymmetry (t = 1.59, df = 17, p = 0.1294), and Begg’s rank correlation test also suggested no evidence of small-study effects (z = − 1.36, p = 0.1724). As a sensitivity analysis, the trim-and-fill method imputed eight potentially missing studies; after adjustment, the pooled prevalence decreased to 46.37% (95% CI: 35.13%–57.99%). Given the extremely high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.8% in the main analysis and 99.9% after trim-and-fill adjustment), funnel plot–based methods and trim-and-fill results should be interpreted with caution. Detailed leave-one-out results, influence diagnostics, and trim-and-fill analyses are provided in Additional file 5.

Narrative synthesis

Perpetrators of workplace violence

Information on perpetrators of WPV against doctors was reported in five studies [17, 28, 42, 45, 50]. Across these studies, WPV against doctors was most commonly perpetrated by patients and patients’ family members or relatives. The proportion of patients as perpetrators ranged from 12.49% to 81.63% [17, 28, 42, 45, 50], while the proportion of patients’ family members or relatives ranged from 40.38% to 70.35% [17, 28, 42, 45, 50]. One study also reported patients’ friends as perpetrators (6.81%) [42]. Two studies reported the sex of perpetrators, showing that most perpetrators were male, with proportions of 77.78% [42] and 79.49% [45], respectively. Two studies reported the age distribution of perpetrators. In one study, perpetrators aged 30–50 years accounted for 60.58% [42]; in another study, perpetrators aged ≥ 50 years accounted for 61.38% [45].

Perceived causes of workplace violence

Perceived causes of WPV against doctors were reported in five studies [17, 42, 43, 45, 50]. The most frequently reported causes were related to unmet patient needs or expectations, including rejected unreasonable demands and unmet expectations, with reported proportions ranging from 44.83% to 81.63% [17, 42, 45, 50]. Long or extended waiting time was also commonly reported, with proportions ranging from 19.83% to 47.96% [17, 42, 43, 45, 50]. Dissatisfaction with treatment plans, curative effects, or treatment outcomes ranged from 25.70% to 46.78% [17, 42, 43, 45]. Cost-related concerns ranged from 9.78% to 35.58% [17, 42, 43]. Other perceived contributors included miscommunication or unclear/complicated treatment processes (7.82%–25.32%) [43, 50], patient intoxication or drug/alcohol use (3.35%–43.60%) [17, 43], hospital system or equipment problems (28.98%), and patients’ psychological problems (26.33%) [50].

Factors associated with workplace violence

Risk factors associated with WPV against doctors were reported in 12 studies [14, 17, 28, 36, 41–45, 48, 50, 51]. Overall, the evidence suggested that WPV risk reflects a combination of demographic factors, professional characteristics, workload-related factors, and clinical setting and organizational factors, with some associations varying across settings and study populations.

First, demographic factors associated with WPV against doctors were reported in ten studies [14, 17, 28, 36, 41, 44, 45, 48, 50, 51]. Male doctors [14, 36, 41, 44, 45, 51], those with higher education (e.g., bachelor’s degree or above) [14, 17, 50], those aged 31 years or older [28, 51], and married doctors [14, 28] were more likely to report WPV. By contrast, one study reported higher WPV risk among younger and single doctors [48].

Second, professional characteristics were reported in seven studies [14, 17, 28, 36, 42, 44, 48]. In some studies, WPV was more likely to be reported by junior doctors [28, 36] and was also associated with lower income [17, 42]. Conversely, other studies linked WPV to higher professional levels [14, 17, 48] and to longer work experience or job tenure [28, 44, 48]. Other reported professional factors included long-term contract or formal employment [28, 48], clinician role [14], and holding an administrative position [44].

Third, workload-related factors were reported in five studies [28, 43, 45, 48, 50]. Doctors with higher patient consultation volumes and those who worked overtime more frequently were more likely to be exposed to WPV [45]. Longer working hours were also associated with WPV, including longer daily working hours (≥ 12 h/day) [28] and longer weekly working hours [43, 48]. By contrast, doctors without night shifts had lower odds of experiencing WPV [50].

Fourth, clinical setting and organizational factors were reported in eight studies [14, 17, 28, 42–44, 48, 51]. Higher WPV risk was reported in specific clinical departments, including emergency medicine, pediatrics, surgery, orthopedics, obstetrics and gynecology, internal medicine, and mental health [14, 28, 43, 51]. Institutional and regional factors were also associated with WPV, including working in public hospitals, tertiary or higher-level hospitals, provincial capitals, medium-developed regions, urban practice areas, and community health service centers [14, 17, 44, 48]. Organizational and environmental factors included lack of WPV-related training or preventive measures, lack of encouragement for reporting WPV, ineffective handling of WPV, absence of fixed seating in waiting areas, and poor lighting during night work [17, 42]. By contrast, better work and practice environments, better doctor–patient relationships, and occasional home visits were associated with lower WPV risk among general practitioners [45].

Outcomes and perceived impacts of workplace violence

Statistically associated outcomes of WPV against doctors were reported in ten studies [16, 28, 29, 35, 36, 40, 46–49], while perceived impacts after experiencing WPV were descriptively reported in two studies [42, 50]. Overall, these outcomes and perceived impacts fell into two broad categories: psychological and well-being outcomes/impacts, and occupational and organizational outcomes/impacts.

First, psychological and well-being outcomes were reported in six studies [16, 35, 40, 42, 47, 50]. WPV was positively associated with anxiety [35], depression [35, 40], psychological stress [16], fear of future violence [47], and ego depletion [47]. It was negatively associated with sleep quality [16], subjective health [16], and life satisfaction [40]. In addition, two studies descriptively reported doctors’ perceived psychological impacts after experiencing WPV, including anger (52.70%), feeling wronged/grievance (50.69%), and anxiety/depression (25.89%) in one study [42], and anxiety (32.72%), insomnia (21.66%), and depression (14.29%) in another study [50].

Second, occupational and organizational outcomes were reported in nine studies [28, 29, 36, 42, 46–50]. WPV was positively associated with job stress [29, 36], job burnout [28, 47, 49], emotional exhaustion [29, 46], and turnover intention [28, 29, 46, 48]. It was negatively associated with job satisfaction [28, 29, 36, 46], job initiative [36], general self-efficacy [36], social support [28], and organizational support [29]. In addition, two studies descriptively reported occupational and organizational impacts after experiencing WPV, including decreased work enthusiasm (52.85%), considering leaving the profession (40.22%), and prioritizing self-protection of rights (33.90%) in one study [42], and reduced work efficiency (56.22%) and decreased patient trust (46.54%) in another study [50].

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence on WPV against doctors in China, drawing on studies identified from database inception to 31 December 2025. Across 19 studies including 114,270 doctors, the pooled 12-month prevalence of WPV was 60.85% (95% CI: 49.16%–71.42%). Verbal violence was the most frequently reported form of WPV, with a pooled prevalence of 49.56% (95% CI: 29.69%–69.57%), followed by threats (28.08%; 95% CI: 15.90%–44.64%) and physical violence (14.36%; 95% CI: 7.77%–25.02%). Sexual harassment (11.81%; 95% CI: 5.75%–22.73%) and sexual assault (4.66%; 95% CI: 2.19%–9.62%) were reported less often. Patients and their family members or relatives were identified as the primary perpetrators [17, 28, 42, 45, 50]. Where reported, perpetrators were predominantly male [42, 45], and middle-aged or older groups accounted for a substantial proportion [42, 45].

Put differently, nearly three in every five doctors in China were estimated to have experienced WPV in the past year. This pooled estimate was higher than the prevalence estimates reported among doctors in Bangladesh (55.5%) [52], Saudi Arabia (56.3%) [53], Italy (38.2%) [54], and Belgium (37.0%) [55]. Population growth and aging have continued to increase demand for healthcare services, placing additional pressure on health systems and exacerbating persistent doctor shortages [2, 4]. This pressure may increase the risk of WPV, particularly when patient needs or expectations are not met [17, 42, 45, 50], unreasonable demands are rejected [50], waiting times are long [17, 42, 43, 45, 50], medical costs are perceived as high [17, 42, 43], or dissatisfaction with treatment outcomes [17, 42, 43], healthcare services [17, 45], or communication arises [43, 50].

The associated factors provide further insight into which doctors may be more vulnerable to WPV. Overall, the findings suggest that WPV risk is shaped by a combination of demographic factors, professional characteristics, workload-related factors, and clinical setting and organizational factors. Because some findings differed across studies, these factors should be interpreted as context-dependent rather than as a single uniform risk profile.

Among demographic factors, male sex was the most frequently reported risk factor in the included studies [14, 36, 41, 44, 45, 51]. This pattern may partly reflect men’s greater representation in high-risk departments, such as psychiatry and emergency medicine [36], and possible sex-related differences in communication styles and personal risk-reduction strategies in clinical practice [36, 41]. In contrast to the pattern observed in this review, however, studies from Belgium [55], Turkey [56], Saudi Arabia [53], Italy [54], and Bangladesh [52] have reported a higher risk of WPV among female doctors.

Higher educational attainment was also associated with WPV against doctors [14, 17, 50]. This finding is consistent with findings reported among healthcare workers [57] and nurses [58–60]. One possible explanation is that doctors with higher educational attainment may be more likely to hold more complex clinical responsibilities, work in higher-level hospitals, or manage patients with more severe or complicated conditions. These roles may increase their exposure to patient dissatisfaction, unmet expectations, and system-level pressures, thereby increasing the likelihood of WPV [61].

Professional characteristics appeared to suggest two different pathways of risk. One pathway was related to early-career or lower-resource vulnerability. This was reflected in findings for junior doctors [28, 36], which may partly reflect less clinical experience [36], and for doctors with lower income [17, 42]. These doctors may be more vulnerable because they often have less authority, less experience in managing difficult interactions, and fewer resources to cope with workplace conflict [61]. The other pathway was associated with a higher professional level, as reflected in findings for doctors with higher professional levels [14, 17, 48] and longer work experience [28, 44, 48]. One possible explanation is that doctors with higher professional levels often undertake more complex clinical responsibilities and may therefore face heavier workloads, greater patient expectations, and more frequent exposure to difficult doctor–patient interactions [41, 62].

Workload-related factors showed a relatively consistent pattern. Higher patient consultation volumes and overtime work were associated with a greater WPV risk [45]. Longer working hours were also associated with WPV, including longer daily working hours (≥ 12 h/day) [28] and longer weekly working hours [43, 48]. Conversely, one study found that physicians without night shifts had lower odds of experiencing WPV [50]. These factors may increase doctors’ exposure to patients and their families. They may also contribute to fatigue, time pressure, and fewer opportunities for effective communication. In such high-intensity clinical environments, tense and frequent interactions may be more likely to escalate into violent incidents [63].

The subgroup analyses also provide insight into when WPV may have been more prominent. The pooled prevalence was significantly lower in studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic [14, 44–50] than in those conducted before the pandemic [16, 17, 28, 29, 35, 36, 40–43, 51]. One possible explanation is that the pandemic changed healthcare utilization and service delivery, with reduced face-to-face contact in some settings and increased public recognition of healthcare workers during this period [64]. However, studies from several countries have reported a general rise in violent incidents during the pandemic, which has been attributed to longer waiting times, heightened anxiety, pandemic-related psychological distress, and constraints on the treatment of severely ill patients due to COVID-19 restrictions [54, 65].

The consequences of WPV for doctors are multifaceted. First, WPV may cause direct physical harm. Physical violence may result in bodily injury and, in extreme cases, death [66]. Second, WPV was linked to psychological and well-being outcomes. Statistically associated outcomes included anxiety [35], depression [35, 40], psychological stress [16], fear of future violence [47], ego depletion [47], poorer sleep quality [16], reduced subjective health [16], and lower life satisfaction [40]. These findings indicate that WPV is not only an occupational safety issue but also a potential threat to doctors’ psychological resilience and overall well-being. Third, WPV was also linked to occupational and organizational outcomes. These included job stress [29, 36], burnout [28, 47, 49], emotional exhaustion [29, 46], turnover intention [28, 29, 46, 48], lower job satisfaction [28, 29, 36, 46], reduced job initiative [36], lower general self-efficacy [36], and lower social or organizational support [28, 29]. These outcomes suggest that WPV may undermine doctors’ engagement, retention, and capacity to provide sustained care. Separately, two studies descriptively reported perceived impacts after WPV, including emotional distress, reduced work efficiency, decreased patient trust, and thoughts of leaving the profession [42, 50]. Together, these findings suggest that WPV can affect not only individual doctors but also doctor–patient relationships and the quality and continuity of care [25].

The findings of this review suggest that prevention strategies should move beyond general recommendations and focus on concrete interventions aligned with the identified risk patterns and recommendations from the included studies. Because WPV was frequently linked to unmet expectations, long waiting times, high workload, and high-demand clinical settings, hospitals should implement structural measures to improve triage, optimize staffing and patient flow, shorten waiting times, and reduce excessive workload [17, 43, 48]. Targeted organizational protection is also needed in departments repeatedly identified as higher-risk settings, including emergency medicine, pediatrics, surgery, orthopedics, obstetrics and gynecology, internal medicine, and mental health [14, 28, 43, 51]. Hospitals should prioritize WPV prevention training, doctor–patient communication training, de-escalation skills, improved alarm and surveillance systems, rapid-response mechanisms such as “code green” teams, and clear reporting pathways for violent incidents [14, 17, 28, 36, 41, 42, 51]. In addition, hospitals should provide timely psychological and organizational support for doctors after WPV incidents, while broader policy actions should strengthen legal protections, formal WPV management programs, and public education to improve understanding of medical care and reduce unrealistic expectations in doctor–patient interactions [14, 36, 42, 45, 50].

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to specifically synthesize evidence on WPV against doctors in China. By restricting inclusion to doctor-only samples and a 12-month recall period, the review improved the comparability of prevalence estimates across studies. In addition, this review not only pooled the overall prevalence and prevalence by violence type but also synthesized evidence on perpetrators, perceived causes, associated factors, outcomes, and perceived impacts, providing a more comprehensive understanding of WPV against doctors in China.

This review also has several limitations. First, because the analyses were based on cross-sectional data, associations between WPV and associated factors, outcomes, or perceived impacts cannot be interpreted as causal relationships. Second, although 19 studies were included, heterogeneity was extremely high in the pooled analyses, limiting the precision and generalizability of the summary estimates. Third, the included studies used different WPV measurement instruments and WPV subtype definitions. Although the subtype-specific meta-analysis was restricted to studies using the Chinese version of the WVS to improve comparability, this also reduced the number of studies included in those analyses. Fourth, subgroup analyses showed numerically higher pooled prevalence among emergency doctors, in secondary and tertiary hospitals, and in public hospitals, but most subgroup differences were not statistically significant, except for the survey period. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of studies in some subgroups, high heterogeneity, and incomplete reporting of study characteristics.

Conclusion

WPV against doctors remains a serious occupational and public health concern. This review found a high pooled prevalence of WPV among doctors in China, with verbal violence as the dominant form and patients and their family members or relatives as the main perpetrators. The findings suggest that WPV may be more likely in high-demand clinical contexts, including settings with heavy workloads, frequent doctor–patient interactions, and unmet patient expectations, and may be associated with substantial psychological, occupational, and organizational outcomes and perceived impacts. These findings underscore the importance of protecting doctors’ well-being and retention, and of promoting safer and more secure working environments, consistent with l SDG targets 3.c and 8.8. Future research should focus more explicitly on doctors as a distinct high-risk occupational group, particularly in public hospitals, tertiary hospitals, and high-risk departments such as emergency medicine, pediatrics, surgery, and internal medicine.
