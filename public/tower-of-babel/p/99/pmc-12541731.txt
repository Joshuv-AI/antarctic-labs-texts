Knowledge of hypoglycemia and awareness of diabetes complications among diabetic patients
Authors: Eman A. Alshafei, Salhah S. Alsulami, Iman M. Wahby, Abdulrahman T. Abudawood, Taif T. Algaidy, Asma A. Alyoubi, Taif Y. Alyoubi, Arwa A. Alnoumani, Halah F. Alqurashi, Amal H. Alawdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:To assess awareness of diabetes complications and knowledge of hypoglycemia.Methods:This cross-sectional study was conducted in a tertiary hospital between December 2022 and November 2023. The participants were 362 adult patients with diabetes selected using systematic random sampling. Data were collected through structured interviews using a validated questionnaire that included sections on socio-demographic characteristics, diabetes history, and knowledge of hypoglycemia and awareness about diabetes complications. Descriptive statistics and one-way ANOVA were used for data analysis.Results:The majority of participants were female (71.3%), and 227 (62.7%) had been diabetic for more than 10 years. Approximately 37% are taking a combination of antidiabetic medications. Hypoglycemia reported in 218 (60.2%) and hypoglycemic coma reported in 37 (17.1%). Overall, 81.1% had good knowledge of hypoglycemia, and 91.4% had good awareness of diabetes complications. Better knowledge was associated with higher education, insulin treatment and other combination therapies, and previous experience with hypoglycemia. Patients with type 1 diabetes or a long duration of diabetes were more aware of diabetes complications.Conclusions:Patients with diabetes had a high degree of knowledge and awareness, but hypoglycemia was still a prevalent issue. Neuroglycopenic symptoms and long-term consequences are insufficiently recognized. Teaching and coaching can help prevent hypoglycemia.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a multifactorial disorder influenced by inherited genes, ethnic background, and parental behaviors that can alter the child’s risk.1 Diabetes mellitus causes elevated blood glucose due to impaired insulin secretion or function.2 Diabetes can damage multiple organs and cause symptoms such as thirst, frequent urination, vision problems, and weight loss. Severe cases may lead to ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar states, possibly causing coma.3 The primary forms of diabetes mellitus include diabetes type 1, diabetes type 2, and gestational diabetes. As stated by the American Diabetes Association, diabetes increases the risk of serious complications such as stroke, nerve and eye disorders, skin and kidney issues, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension. Early diagnosis and treatment can delay or prevent these complications.2

In 2021, approximately 11.6% of the total population in the United States was diagnosed with diabetes, representing a total exceeding 38 million individuals. Among adults, 14.7% were affected, with 8.7 million unaware. American Indian and Alaska native adults had the highest prevalence rate (13.6%), with the rates were 12.1% among Black individuals, 11.7% among Hispanics, 9.1% in Asians, and 6.9% in Caucasians populations.4

The burden of diabetes is escalating rapidly in Saudi Arabia, posing a rising public health issue associated with increased likelihood of related complications and early mortality. Recent data from the International Diabetes Federation show that 17.7% of the adult population was affected by diabetes in 2021, amounting to over 4.27 million individuals.5 Saudi Arabia is ranked among the top 10 globally for with the highest rates of diabetes prevalence worldwide and is estimated to fall within the top 5 leading nations in type 2 diabetes cases by 2030.6 These statistics highlight the urgent need for early interventions and comprehensive strategies to mitigate complications such as diabetic coma.

A diabetic coma is a reversible but serious emergency caused by severe glucose imbalance in people with diabetes.7 It includes 3 main types: severe hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) seen in type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) typically associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).7 Raising awareness about these complications may help reduce their occurrence and improve outcomes for diabetic patients.3 Severe hypoglycemia is a critical complication of DM that results from a marked decline in blood glucose levels, often below 40 mg/dL.8 It can lead to a range of neuroglycopenic symptoms such as dizziness, weakness, confusion, and in extreme cases, loss of consciousness or hypoglycemic coma. Iatrogenic hypoglycemia is frequently seen in patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes receiving intensive glucose-lowering therapies, particularly insulin or sulfonylureas.9

Clinically, hypoglycemia may present with 2 major symptom patterns: neuroglycopenic symptoms, which result from glucose deprivation in the central nervous system (CNS) and may include confusion, behavioral changes, exhaustion, seizures, unconsciousness, and even death if untreated; and neurogenic symptoms, which can be either adrenergic, such as tremors, palpitations, and anxiety, or cholinergic, such as hunger, sweating, and paraesthesia.10 Despite the presence of these warning signs, limited awareness among patients and healthcare providers regarding hypoglycemia’s clinical features and risk factors contributes to its high prevalence and serious, yet preventable, consequences.11,12 A study conducted in India reported that 22% of participants had low awareness of hypoglycemia, while 78% demonstrated high awareness. Furthermore, 54% showed insufficient knowledge regarding its management, and 18% lacked adequate understanding of prevention strategies.12 A cross-sectional study conducted by Alanazi et al2 reported a high level of public awareness about diabetes complications in Saudi Arabia, with 91.1% of respondents answering more than 75% of the awareness questions correctly.2 However, a study by Altowayan et al11 found that patients with T2DM in Al-Qassim had poor knowledge about hypoglycemia as a complication of their condition. These findings highlight the gap in patient-level awareness. Therefore, this research was conducted to determine the extent of knowledge regarding hypoglycemic coma and explore the factors associated with it among individuals with diabetes attending the outpatient clinic at King Abdulaziz University Hospital in Jeddah.

Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted at the outpatient clinics of King Abdulaziz University Hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, between December 2022 and November 2023. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (Study No.535-22) dated 11/12/2022. Ethical approval was based on the Declaration of Helsinki, and written consent was obtained from each participant.

The study comprised all adult diabetic patients (18 years and older). Children, women with gestational diabetes, and patients who refused to provide consent were excluded.

The sample size was predetermined with Raosoft at a confidence level of 95% and a 5% error margin, relative to the total number of diabetic patients who visited the internal medicine clinics during the data collection period, the estimated sample size was calculated to be 362.13

Data were gathered through individual interviews was carried out by qualified research assistants using a standardized questionnaire modified during the current research.2,8,9 The questionnaire was first created in English, then translated into Arabic, followed by a reverse translation back into English by 2 certified translators to ensure accuracy and consistency. The final Arabic version was tested in the same setting with 30 patients with diabetes who were not involved in the main trial. Based on the input, modest changes were made to the wording of some questions to increase clarity and comprehension.

The questionnaire was divided into 4 sections: 1) Sociodemographic data: included the participant’s age, gender, marital status, employment, education level, and income. 2) Clinical features: this section collected information about each participant’s type of diabetes, duration of diagnosis, current medications, and history of co-existing illnesses, such as hypertension and high cholesterol. 3) Knowledge of hypoglycemia: this section used a previously validated questionnaire to measure each participant’s awareness of hypoglycemia symptoms (both neurogenic and neuroglycopenic), probable triggers, and therapy.14 Participants could submit various answers and were instructed to respond with “yes” or “no.” Some questions provided erroneous options to challenge the respondents. The knowledge assessment form deemed participants to have good knowledge of hypoglycemia if they accurately identified at least 3 symptoms, one precipitating factor, and one remedial measure. 4) Awareness of diabetes complications: this section evaluated each participant’s awareness of diabetes complications using a previously validated questionnaire.2 The questionnaire consisted of 7 questions and could have a maximum score of 14 points. Each correct response received 2 points, whereas erroneous answers received zero points. A score greater than 10 points indicated a high level of awareness.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from this research were analyzed using IBM SPSS (version 25, IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, United States). A basic descriptive statistical method was used to determine the attributes of variables in the form of counts and percentages for category and nominal variables. A one-way ANOVA with a least significant difference (LSD) was employed. The tests were conducted on the assumption of a normal distribution. A significance level of less than 0.05 was used as a criterion for rejecting the null hypothesis.

No statistically significant difference was observed in the percentage values of participants with good knowledge about hypoglycemia when comparing genders, age groups, income levels, types of diabetes, or years since diagnosis. However, a statistically significant difference (p=0.045) was observed in knowledge among participants with different education levels, as knowledge was slightly better among those with higher education than among those with lower education (illiterate or elementary/middle school). Participants taking insulin or a combination of medications displayed a statistically significant association with better knowledge (p=0.04) compared to those solely on metformin. Individuals who had personally experienced a hypoglycemic attack demonstrated a strong statistical correlation with higher knowledge levels (p<0.0001) (Table 4).

Several factors showed statistically significant associations with awareness of diabetic complications. Income level showed a significant association (p<0.0001), as individuals with a lower monthly income (p<5000 SAR) had a higher level of awareness (95.2%) compared to those with higher incomes (75.2% and 90.4% for the 2 higher income brackets). Patients with T1DM displayed a greater awareness of complications (97.6%, p=0.004) compared to patients with T2DM (86.3%). The duration since diagnosis also played a role (p=0.049), as awareness was highest in recently diagnosed patients (100%) and those diagnosed over 10 years (89.4%) compared to the 6-10 year group (81.6%). Participants taking metformin showed a statistically significant association with higher awareness (97.1%, p=0.009). However, the level of awareness was lower in users of insulin (88.9%) or a combination of medications (83.2%) than in users of metformin alone. Individuals who had experienced a hypoglycemic attack demonstrated a statistically significant association with greater awareness (95.8%, p=0.001) (Table 5).

Discussion

Hypoglycemia is a life-threatening event, and one of the commonly used medications has hypoglycemia as a potential side effect.15 The study aimed to assess participants’ knowledge level regarding hypoglycemia and the awareness of diabetes-related complications among a sample of individuals diagnosed with DM. In addition, the correlations between knowledge of hypoglycemia, awareness of DM complications, and participant factors were evaluated. Among the 362 patients with diabetes being followed in a tertiary hospital, 218 (60.2%) had experienced hypoglycemic attacks. The global average, by comparison, indicates a prevalence of hypoglycemia between 0.074% and 73.0%.16

The study sample selected from this segment of the Saudi Arabian population showed a better knowledge of hypoglycemia than the population used in previous studies conducted in India12,14 as well as another Saudi population enrolled in a recent study from Jeddah.17 This difference can be linked to the easy accessibility to educational programs as part of the regular healthcare follow-up at the study institution and to the more than 60% of the study group who had been diagnosed with diabetes for more than 10 years. Another explanation could be methodological discrepancies between the studies.

The manifestations of hypoglycemia are commonly divided into 2 major categories: adrenergic symptoms and neuroglycopenic symptoms. Adrenergic symptoms, such as dizziness and drowsiness, were the most common symptoms identified by the participants, followed by sweating and shaking. Conversely, neuroglycopenic symptoms, such as weakness, blurred vision, and headache, were identified less frequently by the participants. These findings are in alignment with a previous Sudanese study, which reported hand tremor and sweating as the 2 most common symptoms.18 Sweating may indicate the onset of hypoglycemia, particularly in individuals using insulin.19 These responses to hypoglycemia are related primarily to the activation of counter-regulatory hormones, including cortisol, glucagon, and catecholamines, which produce the autonomic symptoms.20 Failure of the patient to recognize these symptoms and the need to take preventive measures is interpreted as hypoglycemia unawareness.20

In the present study, timely meals were known as preventive measures among the majority (83.4%) of the participants, whereas taking medications as prescribed by doctors was reported less frequently. Nevertheless, even though nearly two-thirds of our sample had been diagnosed with diabetes for more than 10 years, and 60% had experienced a hypoglycemia episode in the past year, fewer than half of the participants recognized Home-based blood sugar monitoring (SMBG) as a way to prevent hypoglycemia. The available literature supports the use of SMBG and recently continuous glucose monitoring as beneficial for improving glucose control and preventing hypoglycemia in patients being treated with insulin for T1DM and T2DM.21 Over 50% of participants in the present study ate chocolate or drank sugary drinks to manage acute episodes of hypoglycemia. Approximately 60% of the participants recognized coma as one of the sequelae of untreated hypoglycemia.

Consistent with the findings of Shriraam et al,14 illiteracy was associated with a poor knowledge score, thereby highlighting the significance of offering simplified educational materials for patients with low educational attainment. Patients who had already experienced hypoglycemia were also more knowledgeable than those who had never had hypoglycemia, in agreement with previous findings in the Alzahrani study.17 Furthermore, individuals who received combination antidiabetic medications had a higher knowledge score.17 Regarding awareness about diabetes complications, patients with T1DM were more aware than patients with T2DM regarding diabetes complications.

The strength of the present study is that it discusses diabetes, a significant and widely prevalent condition in Saudi Arabia,22 in the context of hypoglycemia, its most serious and life-threatening consequence. This study specifically examined the knowledge of hypoglycemia and awareness of diabetes complications among diabetic patients. On the other hand, this study has certain limitations. Due to its cross-sectional approach, the study cannot determine cause-and-effect associations, and since data were collected from patients at a single center, the findings may not be generalizable to the wider population.

In conclusion, this study emphasized the importance of patient-centered education followed by periodic reinforcement to improve awareness of diabetic complications and understanding of acute hypoglycemia. This instruction should be delivered by a multidisciplinary team that includes the physicians, nurses, dietitians, and certified educators who help to manage a busy medical clinic. Although the majority of patients in this study had a long history of diabetes, had firm knowledge about hypoglycemia, and were aware of diabetes complications, hypoglycemia remained a prevalent issue. Identifying neuroglycopenic signs and recognizing the possible consequences of hypoglycemia were underappreciated. As a result, patient-centered educational programs are critical for vulnerable groups, as are periodic reinforcement and closed-loop discussions to assure understanding, particularly among patients with low educational attainment.
