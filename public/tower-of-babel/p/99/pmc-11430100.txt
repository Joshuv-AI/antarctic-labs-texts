Prevalence of schistosomiasis and its association with anemia in Ethiopia, 2024: a systematic review and meta‑analysis
Authors: Deresse Abebe Gebrehana, Gebretsadik Endeshaw Molla, Woretaw Endalew, Destaw Fetene Teshome, Fantahun Ayenew Mekonnen, Dessie Abebaw Angaw
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchistosomiasis is a neglected tropical disease that affects over 250 million people, predominantly in impoverished communities, including those in Ethiopia. However, there is currently no available national data regarding its prevalence in Ethiopia or its potential association with anemia.MethodsThe Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-2020) guideline was followed in our study. A comprehensive search strategy was conducted using international databases (PUB Med, Embase, Scopus, and Science Direct) and websites (Google Scholar and Google) from their date of inception to April 22, 2024. The quality of the retrieved studies was assessed using an adapted version of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Publication bias and statistical heterogeneity were statistically assessed. A random effects model was applied, and all analysis was performed with STATA 17 statistical software.ResultThe prevalence of schistosomiasis was assessed in twelve studies with 5747 participants. S. mansoni was studied in 11 research papers, while S. hematobium was assessed in one study. Using a random effect model due to high heterogeneity (I2 = 98.46%; P < 0.001), the overall pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis in Ethiopia was 22% (95% CI: 14.3, 29.6). The prevalence of schistosomiasis was about two and a half times higher in rural settings (23%) (95% CI: 15, 31) than in urban areas (10%) (95% CI: 3, 17). The burden of schistosomiasis was 23% between 2020 and 2024, compared to 16% from 2015–2019, with a higher prevalence among pediatric age groups (22%) versus 14% in mixed pediatric and adult age groups. The magnitude of schistosomiasis for good and very good-quality papers was 22% and 17%, respectively. In all the subgroup analyses, there was considerable statistically significant heterogeneity. No study was found to evaluate the association of schistosomiasis with anemia.ConclusionThe study reveals Ethiopia's higher schistosomiasis prevalence. Rural areas have a high burden of schistosomiasis. No eligible study was found to evaluate the association of schistosomiasis with anemia. Therefore, prevention and control measures should focus on rural settings.Trial registrationThe registration number at PROSPERO is CRD42024538522.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-09926-5.

Introduction

Schistosomiasis, one of the neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), affects more than 250 million people, with an estimated 779 million at risk. It is known to be transmitted in 78 endemic countries, 51 of which have moderate to high transmission rates, particularly in poor communities lacking access to safe drinking water and adequate sanitation [1, 2]. Human schistosomiasis, also known as bilharzia, is a parasitic disease caused by infection with trematode worms of the genus Schistosoma.

The two main types of the disease, intestinal and urogenital schistosomiasis, are caused by six different species of Schistosoma. Intestinal schistosomiasis is caused by Schistosoma mansoni, Schistosoma japonicum, Schistosoma mekongi, Schistosoma guineensis, and Schistosoma intercalatum, while urogenital schistosomiasis is caused by Schistosoma haematobium [1]. Intestinal schistosomiasis can lead to intestinal inflammation, periportal fibrosis, portal hypertension, and esophageal varices. In contrast, urogenital schistosomiasis can cause bladder carcinoma and contribute to infertility and cervical cancer [3].

The schistosome life cycle involves two hosts: freshwater snails and mammals (such as humans, mice, and dogs). Asexual reproduction, which includes growth into cercariae, occurs in freshwater snails, while sexual reproduction, involving the production of eggs, takes place in mammals [4]. Several diagnostic techniques are available for schistosomiasis, including the Kato-Katz method, miracidium hatching test (MHT), formol-ether concentration technique (FECT), circulating cathodic antigen (CCA) test, point-of-care test (POCT), and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based techniques [4].

About 5.01 million people in Ethiopia suffer from schistosomiasis, and another 37.5 million are at risk for infection [2]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis done among pregnant women globally, schistosomiasis was found to be significantly associated with anemia [5].

Although numerous primary studies on schistosomiasis have been conducted in various parts of Ethiopia since the implementation of mass drug administration (MDA) in November 2015 [6–17], the prevalence of schistosomiasis has been inconsistent. The meta-analyses conducted in Ethiopia were limited to either a single Schistosoma species or the pediatric age group [18, 19]. Consequently, recent evidence on the national prevalence of schistosomiasis and its association with anemia across all age groups and different Schistosoma species is lacking.

It will be used to evaluate the 2020 Schistosomiasis Morbidity Control Plan [20] by comparing current and past prevalence rates. Additionally, this review will assess the progress of the 2025 Schistosomiasis Elimination Plan [21], enabling timely adjustments, efficient resource allocation, and evidence-based decision-making. The current systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis and its association with anemia among the population of Ethiopia.

Selection process

Two investigators (DAG and GEM) independently searched for and identified eligible studies. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher (WE) was consulted to resolve the issue. After removing duplicates, the screening was conducted based on titles and abstracts. Subsequently, reports retrieved for further assessment were evaluated for eligibility. Data collection process and data items

The data extraction form was initially prepared by DAG and then shared with GEM and WE for further modification and discussion. Using the final version of the form, data from two articles were collaboratively extracted by DAG, GEM, and WE as a pilot process. For the remaining ten articles, data extraction was conducted independently by DAG and GEM using the pre-formed data extraction form. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with the third reviewer, WE. The extracted data, including the author’s name, year of publication, year of data collection, study design type, number of participants with schistosomiasis, sample size, diagnostic methods, sex, region, and schistosoma species, were recorded in an Excel sheet (Supplementary file 1).

Study risk of bias assessment

The adaptive version of the New Castle Ottawa Scale [22] for cross-sectional studies was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies. The criteria included three domains: the selection of participants, the comparability of study groups, and the ascertainment of outcomes of interest in each study. Each domain had a maximum score of 5, 2, and 3 stars, respectively, making a total possible score of 10 stars. Based on this classification, studies with composite scores of 9–10, 7–8, 5–6, and 0–4 were considered to have very good, good, satisfactory, and unsatisfactory quality, respectively (Table 2).

Synthesis methods

Studies retrieved for assessment were evaluated for eligibility by reading the full text. Variables requiring reclassification were recoded. The results of individual studies were presented using tables and forest plots. Data analysis was conducted using STATA 17 statistical software. The pooled prevalence estimate of schistosomiasis, with a 95% confidence interval, was calculated using a random-effects meta-analysis due to significant heterogeneity among the included studies. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using a forest plot, Cochran’s Q test, and the I2 statistic. The percentage of true heterogeneity rather than sampling error or chance differences among the included studies was given by I2. Cochran's Q test was used to validate the existence of heterogeneity, and statistically significant heterogeneity was illustrated by a p-value of less than 0.05. Additionally, the I2 test was employed to quantify the degree of statistical heterogeneity between studies; values indicating low, medium, and high heterogeneity, respectively, were 25, 50, and 75% [23]. A tau-square test was also used.

To explore the potential causes of heterogeneity among the included studies, subgroup analyses were conducted based on study setting (rural vs. urban), year of data collection (2015–2019 vs. 2020–2024), age groups (children vs. mixed age groups), and methodological quality of the included studies (very good quality vs. good quality). To assess the robustness of the synthesized results, a sensitivity analysis was performed.

Reporting bias assessment (Publication bias)

After the asymmetric funnel plot was found, publication bias was assessed using the Egger weighted regression test (P < 0.05) [24], and this bias was handled by trim and fill analysis [25].

Certainty assessment

The quality of evidence and strength of recommendations were assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system by two reviewers, DAG and GEM. The five determinants for downgrading—individual study limitations, inconsistency of results, indirectness of evidence, imprecision, and publication bias—along with the three factors for upgrading—dose–response gradient, large magnitude of effect, and confounding—were applied [26]. Inter-rater reliability was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa.

Result

Study selection

A total of 724 records were found in databases and websites. After 211 duplicate articles were removed, 513 articles were left and screened by title and abstract. 495 were excluded (356 by title and 139 by abstract). 18 articles were sought for retrieval, and 6 studies were excluded with justification. Finally, 12 studies that passed the eligibility criteria were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

From the 12 studies that were included in the systematic reviews and meta-analysis, a total of 5747 (2808 male and 2939 female) study participants were enrolled [6–17]. A cross-sectional research design was applied to all the selected papers, and they were published between 2018 [7] and 2023 [12, 14, 15]. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, papers with sample sizes ranging from 280 [7] to 1683 [14] were utilized. The study participants were in the pediatrics age group in five studies [10, 11, 14–16], whereas mixed age groups (pediatrics and adult) were used in seven studies [6–9, 12, 13, 17].

Regions

All 12 studies were done in Ethiopia, with five studies conducted in the Amahara Region [6, 10, 11, 16, 17]. Of the remaining seven studies, two were performed in the regions of Tigray [7, 13], two in Oromia [8, 15], two in Benishangul Gumuz [9, 12], and one study was conducted in the Sidama Region [14].

Species of schistosomiasis

Out of the 12 studies, S. mansoni was investigated in 11 of them [6–11, 13–17], while S. hematobium was examined in just one study [12]. No mixed infection was investigated by any of the included studies.

Diagnostic methods

Five and two of the included studies were diagnosed by the Kato-katz technique [8, 9, 14–16] and the wet mount and FEC combination [10, 17] respectively. The Kato-Katz and FEC combination [6, 13] was applied in two more studies, while the remaining three investigations were diagnosed via FEC [11], urine filtration [12], wet mount, Kato-Katz, and FEC combination [7] (Table 1). The I2 statistic was 98.46%, indicating substantial heterogeneity across the included studies other than chance. Reporting publication bias

In order to objectively determine the presence or absence of publication bias, Egger’s regression test was performed (Fig. 9), and the estimated bias coefficient (intercept) was 12.47 with a standard error of 4.43, giving a p-value of 0.018 (Table 4). As a result, there was publication bias as per the strong evidence (large bias coefficient and standard error of proportion) provided by Egger’s test. To handle this publication bias, a trim and fill analysis was conducted, and two imaginary studies were imputed on the right side of the funnel plot (Fig. 10). Discussion

The objective of this meta-analysis was to assess the pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis and its association with anemia in the Ethiopian population, using eligible primary studies conducted in the country. All these studies reported infections of Schistosoma mansoni and Schistosoma haematobium, with no mentions of Schistosoma japonicum, Schistosoma mekongi, Schistosoma intercalatum, or mixed infections. Approximately 91% of the studies documented Schistosoma mansoni infections, while 9% reported Schistosoma haematobium infections.

Ethiopia’s approach to controlling schistosomiasis in 2020 was part of a broader strategy to combat neglected tropical diseases (NTDs). The plan aimed to control the morbidity of schistosomiasis by 2020 [20] and break its transmission by 2025 [21]. Key components of the 2020 Control Plan include Mass Drug Administration (MDA), Health Education and Behavior Change, Improved Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH), Snail Control, Surveillance and Monitoring [20]. The 2020 control plan led to significant progress in reducing the prevalence of schistosomiasis in Ethiopia. However, several challenges remain unresolved, including logistical issues in drug distribution, limited access to clean water, and insufficient sustained community engagement [21].

The goals of the 2025 schistosomiasis elimination plan are to reduce the prevalence of schistosomiasis and eliminate it as a public health problem. The plan aims to reduce the prevalence of schistosomiasis to less than 2% using the Kato-Katz diagnostic method or maintain a prevalence of heavy infections below 1% in all implementation units [21]. Additionally, it seeks to achieve the elimination of schistosomiasis as a public health problem by 2025, meaning the disease will no longer pose a significant health threat to the population [21]. Similar to the challenges faced by the 2020 schistosomiasis control plan, the 2025 elimination plan also encounters issues such as logistical problems in drug distribution, limited access to clean water, and the need for sustained community engagement [21].

The current systematic review and meta-analysis revealed a pooled prevalence estimate of schistosomiasis among the Ethiopian population at 22% (95% CI: 14.3, 29.6). This finding is consistent with an SRMA in Brazil, which found a prevalence of 18.27% [27], and a national survey in Uganda, which reported a prevalence of 25.6% [28].

This finding was higher than the pooled prevalence from a SRMA conducted in the Philippines, which reported a prevalence of 7.2% (95% CI: 2.2, 13.3) [29]. The Philippines has made substantial progress in controlling schistosomiasis through concerted public health efforts, including mass drug administration, snail control programs, and improved water and sanitation infrastructure [30]. The lower prevalence in the Philippines can also be attributed to the effective implementation of these control measures and the country’s commitment to reducing the disease burden [31].

Furthermore, the results of this SRMA were higher than those from national surveys conducted in Kenya (2.1% [95% CI: 1.2, 3.5]) [32] and Sierra Leone (11.4% [95% CI: 10.4, 12.5]) [33]. The relatively lower burden of schistosomiasis in Kenya can be attributed to the implementation of several effective control measures, including mass drug administration, snail control programs, and improved water and sanitation infrastructure [34, 35]. Similarly, the comparatively reduced national prevalence of schistosomiasis in Sierra Leone is largely due to the implementation of mass drug administration (MDA) programs, improved water and sanitation infrastructure, and targeted health education campaigns [36, 37].

On the other hand, the figure in this SRMA is lower than the result from an SRMA conducted in Zambia, which reported a prevalence of 34.9% (95% CI: 20.7–50.6) [38]. This difference could be attributed to Zambia’s environmental and socio-economic conditions, such as the presence of large water bodies and irrigation schemes, which create favorable conditions for the transmission of schistosomiasis [39]. Furthermore, this figure is also lower than the national survey conducted in Mozambique, which reported a prevalence of 48.0% (95% CI: 47.7, 48.3) [40]. Mozambique’s extensive network of rivers and lakes provides ideal breeding grounds for freshwater snails, which are vectors for schistosomiasis [41]. Additionally, challenges in the widespread implementation of schistosomiasis control measures, such as mass drug administration (MDA) campaigns, health education, and improved sanitation, partly due to logistical and infrastructural issues, might also contribute to the higher prevalence of schistosomiasis in Mozambique [42]. Furthermore, Mozambique’s higher poverty rates compared to Ethiopia might significantly impact the greater prevalence of schistosomiasis in Mozambique [43].

The pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis was higher in rural settings. This finding was supported by reviews from the Democratic Republic of the Congo and a prior meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia [18, 44]. Rural populations often engage in activities that increase their contact with infested water bodies. For example, irrigation practices, swimming, and fishing are common in rural areas, leading to higher exposure to schistosome larvae [2]. Additionally, rural areas typically have less access to healthcare services, which delays diagnosis and treatment of schistosomiasis [45]. Furthermore, the absence of safe water sources in rural areas forces communities to rely on natural water bodies, which are often contaminated with schistosome larvae [46].

Our study demonstrated that the prevalence of schistosomiasis was higher in the pediatric age group compared to mixed age groups (children and adults). This is supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia [19], Nigeria [47], and the Philippines [29] and a national survey performed in Uganda [28]. This difference can be attributed to several behavioral, and biological factors. Children, particularly those of school age, are more likely to engage in activities that expose them to infested water bodies. Activities such as swimming, playing, and fishing in contaminated water increase their risk of infection [1]. Furthermore, children may have a less developed immune response to the parasite, making them more susceptible to infection and reinfection [48].In addition to this, the chronic nature of the disease means that children, once infected, can carry the parasite for years, contributing to higher prevalence rates in this age group [48].

In this meta-analysis, a subgroup of good-quality studies had a higher prevalence of schistosomiasis compared to the pooled prevalence in very good-quality studies. This variation might be associated with a less robust random sampling techniques used in good-quality studies relative to very-good-quality studies [49]. Hence, more cases might be selected in good quality studies, resulting in a high prevalence of schistosomiasis.

Using November 2015 as an implementation time for mass drug administration for schistosomiasis in Ethiopia [50] and 2020 as a time for schistosomiasis morbidity control [20], subgroup analysis using the year of data collection was done in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Studies with a data collection period from 2020 to 2024 had a two-and-a-half times higher prevalence of schistosomiasis (23%) compared to studies with a data collection period from 2015 to 2019 with a pooled schistosomiasis prevalence of 16%. The potential reasons could be the interruption of MDA due to the COVID-19 pandemic [51] and the persistent civil war induced human and economic crisis in Ethiopia [52]. Other explanations could be the lack of control for freshwater snails, the intermediate hosts for Schistosoma species due to recent water resource development for irrigation in the country [53], climate change or the increment in temperature [54, 55], and the inability of praziquantel for the prevention of possible reinfection [56].

Even though no eligible study in this systematic review and meta-analysis was found to assess the association between schistosomiasis and anemia, in a systematic review and meta-analysis of schistosomiasis conducted globally among pregnant women, schistosomiasis was found to be associated with anemia [5]. Furthermore, in a systematic review and meta-analysis of the global anemia burden from 1990 to 2010, schistosomiasis was one of the main conditions that increased the prevalence of anemia [57].

Limitations of the study

In spite of the fact that our study contained crucial information, it is not without limitations. First, the substantial heterogeneity was not handled despite conducting sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis by study setting, year of data collection, age group of study participants, and methodological quality of the included articles. Second, different diagnostic methods with varying ranges of sensitivity and specificity were applied in the eligible primary studies, which in turn might affect the pooled prevalence estimate. Third, despite using a comprehensive search strategy, appropriate screening procedures, and eligibility assessments, we were unable to find articles that assessed the association of schistosomiasis with anemia in the absence or control of other established causes of anemia like hookworm infection, malaria, and cancer. Fourth, the prevalence of schistosomiasis might be underestimated due to the chance of stool-negative schistosomiasis or the use of a number of exclusion criteria.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis illustrated that the prevalence of schistosomiasis in Ethiopia was higher, indicating the national control program to attain transmission break by 2025 difficult to be achieved. It also showed the lack of primary research on the association of schistosomiasis with anemia, provided that established causes of anemia were controlled.

Recommendation

For practice or intervention: The prevalence of schistosomiasis could potentially be reduced with the comprehensive treatment of at-risk population groups, safe water access, better sanitation, hygiene education, and snail control. Physicians should have a high index of suspicion for anemia while treating schistosomiasis.

For policy, the development of predictive models that may be used to illustrate snail suitability regions in order to guide control efforts is vital, as is the strengthening of community engagement methods through health education.

For future research: By controlling hookworm and other causes of anemia, future researchers of the primary studies should employ stratification analysis to distinctly identify the relationship between schistosomiasis and anemia. For an effective control approach, a meta-analysis of the variables that contribute to the endemicity of schistosomiasis would also be crucial.
