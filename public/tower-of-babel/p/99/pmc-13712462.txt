Systematic Review and Meta-regression of Longitudinal Outcomes of Pulmonary Valve Replacement in Tetralogy of Fallot
Authors: Louis-Olivier Roy, Laurence Watelle, Pier-Ann Champagne, Samuel Blais, Frédéric Dallaire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite excellent survival after tetralogy of Fallot repair, pulmonary regurgitation (PR) is common. Pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) is frequently performed, but its long-term efficacy remains uncertain. We conducted a meta-regression to assess the impact of PVR on key cardiac outcomes over time.MethodsWe included studies reporting PR, right ventricular (RV) and left ventricular volumes, QRS duration, RV ejection fraction, peak VO2, or New York Heart Association class before and after PVR in patients with tetralogy of Fallot. A multilevel random-effects meta-regression was performed using time since PVR as a covariate. Cubic splines were used to explore temporal trends and meta-analysis to quantify the impact of PVR over time.ResultsSixty-nine studies were included. Compared with pre-PVR values, PVR yielded substantial absolute reductions in PR fraction and RV end-systolic and end-diastolic volume. Left ventricular end-diastolic volume increased slightly. New York Heart Association class also improved. The median follow-up was 14.4 months (interquartile range: 10.3-31.1 months) with only 1 study extending beyond 10 years. Estimated annual changes after PVR were minimal for all parameters and negligible for key parameters such as PR fraction (0.36%, 95% confidence interval [CI]: –0.05% to 0.77%), RV end-systolic volume (–0.58 mL/m2, 95% CI: –1.62 mL/m2 to 0.45 mL/m2), and RV end-diastolic volume (–0.49 mL/m2, 95% CI: –0.72 mL/m2 to 1.72 mL/m2).ConclusionsThe long-term impact of PVR remains uncertain. Although short-term improvements are clear, insufficient long-term data preclude firm conclusions on sustained benefits.Keywords: pulmonary valve replacement, tetralogy of Fallot, pulmonary regurgitation, meta-analysis, meta-regression, systematic review

The initial repair of tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) consists of ventricular septal defect closure and relief of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction.1 When the obstruction is too severe or if the pulmonary annulus is too small, a transannular patch is needed, causing pulmonary regurgitation (PR) and eventually progressive dilation of the right ventricle (RV).2 This can lead to loss of right ventricular contractile capacity, diminution of functional capacity, and an increase in the risk of life-threatening arrythmias.3,4

As a result, pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) often becomes necessary. Although it effectively reduces PR and helps decrease right ventricular volume in the short term,5,6 long-term evolution remains poorly understood. Several meta-analyses,7, 8, 9 including a recent one involving 7544 patients, have examined the long-term outcomes of PVR in individuals with repaired TOF, showing that the pooled mortality at 30 days, 5 years, and 10 years after PVR was 0.87%, 2.7%, and 6.2%, respectively.9 When comparing pre- and post-PVR values, QRS duration and exercise test performance remained similar,9 whereas right ventricular volumes were significantly reduced.8,9

However, the timing of the reassessment of cardiac parameters after PVR is crucial, as these parameters may evolve with time. Some evidence suggests that initial improvement after PVR may not last more than a few years, with right ventricular volume and PR potentially returning to baseline levels within 7-10 years.10 Yet most previous meta-analyses did not consider this temporal effect, and only the overall pre- to postoperative effect has been assessed. Understanding the long-term physiological changes after PVR is critical for selecting PVR candidates.

In this study, we performed a meta-regression to pool results of studies assessing the pre-and post-PVR change of cardiac parameters, and to better characterize the change of cardiac parameters with time after PVR in patients with repaired TOF. Methods

Study design and objectives

This systematic review follows the guidelines of the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) statement for systematic reviews. We aimed to evaluate the effect of PVR on PR, ventricular volumes, right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF), QRS duration, VO2 max, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) status using time after PVR as a meta-regression variable. This study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025642113).

Eligibility criteria

We included studies in which the enrolled population consisted of human patients of any age with previously repaired TOF or Fallot physiology, that is, those with an anatomical or functional obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract resulting in right-to-left shunting who underwent a first PVR (all types of PVR techniques and valve prostheses were eligible). Eligible studies needed to be an original experimental and nonexperimental study written in French or English that reported at least 1 targeted outcome in the form of a measure of central tendency (mean, median) and a measure of variability (interquartile range [IQR] or standard deviation [SD]), that reported at least 1 pre- and 1 post-PVR measure, and that explicitly reported time of outcome assessment. Published abstracts were considered when the full article could not be retrieved.

Exclusion criteria included case studies, case series with <10 patients, studies involving a mixed congenital heart disease population (except if there was an extractable subanalysis for patients with TOF), and studies in which patients underwent other major surgeries simultaneously, defined as surgeries that necessitated cardiopulmonary bypass or that introduced a major change in physiology. Pulmonary artery patch, enlargement of RV outflow tract, valve repair, septal defect closure, and maze surgery were not considered major surgeries. When multiple studies reported data from the same cohort at the same time point, we retained the study with the largest sample size for each measurement to avoid data redundancy.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed with a professional university librarian and combined terms encompassing both the concepts of TOF and PVR (full search strategy available in the Supplemental Methods section). The following databases were searched on August 22, 2024: PubMed, MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, and Cochrane database. ClinicalTrials.gov was also searched for ongoing clinical trials, as well as the reference list of the relevant articles. No filters were used. The complete list of keywords and MeSH search strategy is available in the Supplemental Methods section.

Outcome of interest

The outcomes of interest for extraction were selected based on a literature review and the clinical expertise of a paediatric cardiologist (FD). We selected outcomes for which PVR could plausibly have a significant physiological impact. An extensive list of all outcomes reported in articles relative to PVR was made, and most relevant clinical outcomes were retained after discussions. The selected outcomes to be analysed were PR, left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes (LVEDV, LVESV, indexed and not indexed), RV end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes (RVEDV and RVESV, indexed and not indexed), RV dysfunction, QRS duration, RVEF, peak VO2, NYHA status, quality of life scores, and RV/LV volume ratio.

PR was reported either as a category (absent, mild, moderate, and severe) or as a percentage. RV dysfunction was reported as a category (mild, moderate, or severe). We used the category labels as they were reported in the included studies. VO2 max was either reported as an absolute peak value (peak VO2) or expressed as a percentage of the predicted maximal VO2 for the individual. Because fewer than 10 data points were reported, PR categories, not indexed ventricular volumes, RV dysfunction category, RV/LV ratio, percentage of peak VO2, and health-related quality of life scores from any questionnaires were excluded from the analysis.

Data extraction

A first screen based on title and abstract followed by full text review was conducted in Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia)11 by 2 independent reviewers (LW and L-OR). Any disagreements were resolved by discussions. A third independent reviewer (FD) was consulted if disagreements persisted. Data extraction and entry were made using a REDCap form to ensure standardized data collection and minimize errors. Data extraction was conducted by 4 reviewers. A first reviewer extracted the relevant data, whereas a second reviewer would validate that the data were entered properly by the first reviewer. All entries were validated by a different reviewer to ensure exactitude of the data. Any conflict between reviewers was resolved by discussions. When a study reported long-term data but no time after PVR, the corresponding author of the study was contacted by email to obtain missing data.

The following demographic and clinical covariates were collected when available: sex at birth distribution, age at repair, primary repair type, age at PVR, and proportions of patients with a transannular patch as their repair surgery.

Quality appraisal

We could not find any validated tool for observational pre-post studies without a control group. We used the National Institutes of Health “Quality Assessment Tool for Before-After (Pre-Post) Studies With No Control Group”12 to assess for methodology in our review. Although this tool has not been formally validated, it enables quality assessment of studies examining the effect of an intervention on a single group, which was the design of most studies included. This tool comprises 12 questions. However, 2 were not applicable to our study's methodology because our analysis focused on raw parameter values rather than results of inferential statistics. We thus excluded question 8 "Were the people assessing the outcomes blinded to the participants' exposures/interventions?" and question 12 "If the intervention was conducted at a group level (e.g., a whole hospital, a community, etc.), did the statistical analysis take into account the use of individual-level data to determine effects at the group level?" The remaining 10 questions were assessed by 2 independent reviewers in parallel. Each reviewer assigned an overall quality rating of good, fair, or poor based on their global appraisal of internal validity. This rating was not directly computed from the sum of individual item scores. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Finally, both reviewers met to discuss any discrepancies and reach a consensus on the final score.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data management and statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC) and R (version 4.5.1). All analysis followed the Cochrane review methodology.13

Descriptive statistics were used to report study characteristics in the form of mean, median, and frequency when appropriate. Pooled descriptive statistics were weighted on the number of participants per study. Outcomes reported by fewer than 10 studies were not considered for meta-analysis or regression, as pooled estimates based on small numbers may be potentially misleading.

Meta-analyses were first conducted to assess the impact of PVR on selected cardiac parameters. The metafor package in R (version 4.8-0)14 was used applying random effects models with restricted maximum likelihood estimation to account for potential clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. A multilevel random effect was included to account for the nonindependence of multiple effect sizes derived from the same study, such as distinct patient groups within a single study or repeated outcomes reported in the same cohort.

Meta-regression models were conducted using the metafor package, with time since PVR as a continuous moderator. This allowed us to quantify the variation of cardiac outcomes with time since PVR. These models provided an intercept (representing the baseline estimated effect of PVR compared with pre-PVR values) and a slope coefficient for time since PVR, each reported with their standard error (SE) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

To explore potential nonlinear temporal trends that could not be captured by a linear meta-regression, we conducted complementary analyses using weighted linear models with restricted cubic splines via the lm() function in R.15 These models used the same inverse-variance weights as those from the meta-analyses and meta-regressions. The spline analyses were intended as an exploratory extension, designed to refine the visualization and interpretation of time-dependent effects.

Heterogeneity for the meta-analysis and the meta-regression was assessed through the between-study variance component and the I2 statistic, calculated as the proportion of total variance attributable to between-study variability. Pooled effect sizes were computed using the relative weight of each study derived from the inverse of their variance. Most studies reported data in the form of mean and SD, whereas some studies reported median with IQR or full range. We retained these values and estimated mean and SD in these cases when necessary. The process is described in Supplemental Methods.

To explore sources of heterogeneity and variability in our models, we conducted stratified analyses according to several factors: studies limited to paediatric populations, studies with predominantly an adult population (not limited to a paediatric population), studies in which primary repair was done with a transannular patch in more than 70% of patients, more recent studies (2015 and later), earlier studies (before 2015), the top 75% of studies based on quality score from our quality assessment tool, and analyses excluding studies deemed to have poor methodological quality. The heterogeneity and limited reporting of PVR approaches (surgical vs percutaneous), valve types, and TOF repair types prevented their inclusion in sensitivity analyses. To explore potential publication bias and small-study effects, funnel plots were constructed for each outcome parameter using the results from the meta-analysis. Funnel plots display effect size (mean difference) against study precision (SE). In the absence of bias or heterogeneity, data points should form a symmetrical funnel. Visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry was used as a descriptive tool to identify potential biases across studies.

Effect of PVR on pulmonary regurgitation

Data on PR fraction (%) were available from 26 studies and across 30 time points. Most studies reported outcomes within the first 4 years after PVR. Only 3 studies extended follow-ups beyond this period, with data at approximately 6, 7, and 11 years, which limited the ability to evaluate the long-term impact of PVR on PR. The pooled mean difference estimated by meta-analysis showed a marked reduction in PR after PVR (–38.7%; 95% CI: –40.6 to –36.7). Heterogeneity was considerable (I2 = 77.1%; Table 3).

When time was incorporated into the model, the baseline effect (intercept) of PVR was –39.4% (95% CI: –41.5 to –37.4; SE 1.0). The annual change indicated a negligible increase of PR after PVR (β = 0.4% per year; 95% CI: 0.0-0.8; SE 0.2). Adding time to the model did not reduce heterogeneity, which remained considerable (I2 = 75.9%; Fig. 2).

The inspection of cubic spline models, together with the meta-regression results (Fig. 2), confirmed a strong initial reduction in PR after PVR, followed by a slight increase over the first 2.5 years after PVR, after which PR appeared to stabilize.

Sensitivity analyses showed that in studies published before 2015, the progression of PR was moderately greater (β = 2.3% per year; 95% CI: 0.7-3.8; SE 0.7; Supplemental Fig. S10).

Effect of PVR on right ventricle volumes

Data were available for RVESV in 43 studies across 57 time points and 50 studies for RVEDV across 63 time points. Most studies reported outcomes within the first 4 years after PVR, with only 3 extending follow-ups to approximately 5-7 years, one to 8 years for RVEDV, and another to nearly 11 years, which limited our ability to assess the long-term impact of PVR. The pooled mean difference estimated by meta-analysis showed a marked reduction in RVESV (–33.7 mL/m2; 95% CI: –37.4 to –30.1) and RVEDV (–60.1 mL/m2; 95% CI: –64.9 to –55.4) after PVR. Heterogeneity was considerable for both RVESV and RVEDV, respectively (I2 = 77.5% and 81.9%; Table 3).

When time was incorporated into the model, the baseline effect (intercept) of PVR was –32.6 mL/m2 for RVESV (95% CI: –36.8 to –28.5; SE 2.1) and –61.1 mL/m2 for RVEDV (95% CI: –66.4 to –55.8; SE 2.6). The annual change was likely clinically negligible for both parameters and associated with wide CIs (RVESV: β = –0.6 mL/m2 per year; 95% CI: –1.6 to 0.4; SE 0.5; RVEDV: β = 0.5 mL/m2 per year; 95% CI: –0.7 to 1.7; SE 0.6). Adding time to the model almost had no impact on heterogeneity, which remained considerable (RVESV: I2 = 77.4%; RVEDV: I2 = 81.5%; Fig. 3).

The inspection of cubic spline models, together with the meta-regression results, highlighted the heterogeneity of the reported effect sizes (Fig. 3). We found a marked initial reduction in RVESV and RVEDV after PVR, followed by a slight tendency toward redilatation over time, as illustrated by the spline models.

Sensitivity analyses for RVESV and RVEDV did not reveal any additional trends because the CIs on the slopes calculated were too wide to draw any meaningful conclusions (Supplemental Figs. S11 and S12).

Effect of PVR on left ventricle volume

Data were available for LVESV in 23 studies across 27 time points and 25 studies for LVEDV across 29 time points. For both LVESV and LVEDV, most studies reported outcomes within 3 years of PVR and only 1 study provided data beyond 10 years, thus limiting the assessment of the longer-term impact. The pooled mean difference estimated by meta-analysis showed a small increase in LVESV (1.4 mL/m2; 95% CI: 0.0-2.8) and LVEDV (5.5 mL/m2; 95% CI: 3.6-7.4) after PVR. Heterogeneity was moderate for both LVESV and LVEDV, respectively (I2 = 51.4% and 40.3%; Table 3).

When time was incorporated into the model, the baseline effect (intercept) of PVR was 0.5 mL/m2 for LVESV (95% CI: –1.2 to 2.2; SE 0.8) and 4.6 mL/m2 for LVEDV (95% CI: 2.3-7.0; SE 1.1). The annual change was small and the 95% CI included the null effect for both parameters (LVESV: β = 0.4 mL/m2 per year, 95% CI: –0.1 to 0.9, SE 0.3; LVEDV: β = 0.4 mL/m2 per year, 95% CI: –0.3 to 1.1, SE 0.3). Adding time to the model had a minimal impact on heterogeneity for LVESV (I2 = 48.8%) and LVEDV (I2 = 39.3%; Fig. 3).

The inspection of cubic spline models (Fig. 3) did not reveal any particular trend on LVESV with time but allowed the visualization of a mixed effect of PVR, with some studies reporting a volume reduction while others did not. For LVEDV, the largest effect sizes occurred in the first 3 years after PVR but high variability between studies. The spline illustrated the increase in LVEDV during the first 3 years after PVR, followed by a stagnation in volume increase, but the CIs were wide.

Sensitivity analyses for LVESV and LVEDV did not reveal additional trends (Supplemental Figs. S13 and S14).

Effect of PVR on RVEF

Data on RVEF were available from 43 studies across 52 time points. Most studies reported RVEF outcomes within the first 2 years after PVR, restricting the ability to assess the long-term impact, as only 5 studies provided data beyond 5 years of follow-up. The pooled mean difference from meta-analysis was 0.5 percentage points (95% CI: –0.7 to 1.7), indicating a negligible effect of PVR on RVEF. Heterogeneity across studies was substantial (I2 = 74.2%; Table 3).

When accounting for time in meta-regression, the baseline effect (intercept) was –0.4 percentage points (95% CI: –1.8 to 0.9; SE 0.7). There was a small annual increase of 0.4 percentage points per year of follow-up after PVR (95% CI: 0.1-0.8; SE 0.2). Incorporating time did not explain the heterogeneity, which remained substantial (I2 = 73.8%; Fig. 2).

Visual inspection of spline curves revealed heterogeneous effect sizes for RVEF, with some studies reporting improvements after PVR and others not (Fig. 3). Overall, there appeared to be a trend toward improvement, although the wide 95% CIs limit the certainty of this observation.

Sensibility analyses restricted to studies published before 2015 suggested a negative effect of PVR on RVEF, although the effect size was small and imprecise (β = –0.2% per year; 95% CI: –1.6 to 1.1; SE 0.6; Supplemental Fig. S15).

Effect of PVR on QRS duration

Data were available from 29 studies across 31 time points. Most studies reported outcomes within the first 3 years after PVR, limiting the ability to evaluate the longer-term impact of PVR on QRS duration. The pooled mean difference estimated by meta-analysis was nonsignificant, although trended in a diminution in QRS duration (–1.6 milliseconds; 95% CI: –4.1 to 0.8). Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 55.6%; Table 3).

When time was incorporated into the model, the baseline effect (intercept) of PVR was –1.3 milliseconds (95% CI: –4.9 to 2.2; SE 1.7). There was a small decrease of QRS duration with time, but the effect size was small, and CIs were wide (β = –0.2 milliseconds per year; 95% CI: –1.3 to 1.0; SE 0.6). Adding time to the model did not explain heterogeneity, which remained moderate (I2 = 57.2%; Fig. 2).

The inspection of cubic spline models illustrated a small initial decrease in QRS duration immediately after PVR, followed by relative stability over time (Fig. 2). However, the wide 95% CIs preclude drawing firm conclusions. At the level of individual study results, several studies reported a beneficial reduction in QRS duration after PVR, whereas few others showed an increase, underscoring the inconsistency of findings. Subgroup sensitivity analyses showed no particular patterns (Supplemental Fig. S16).

Effect of PVR on peak VO2

Data were available for peak VO2 in 11 studies across 11 time points. The pooled mean difference estimated by meta-analysis indicated no significant effect of PVR on peak VO2 (0.4 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: –0.5 to 1.3) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 21.7%, Table 3).

When time was incorporated to the model, the baseline effect (intercept) of PVR was 0.9 mL/kg/min (95% CI: –0.7 to 2.4; SE 0.7). The annual change of peak VO2 was small and likely negligible (β = –0.2 mL/kg/min per year; 95% CI: –0.7 to 0.3; SE 0.2). Adding time to the model could not explain a portion of the heterogeneity between the studies (I2 = 24.1%; Fig. 2).

The inspection of cubic spline models (Fig. 2) highlighted discrepancies across studies, with nearly half (5 of 12) not reporting any change in peak VO2 after PVR. Visual inspection of the spline curves suggested an early gain in peak VO2 immediately after PVR, followed by a subsequent decline, but with wide 95% CIs.

Because of the limited number of available studies, subgroup analyses could not be performed for this parameter.

Effect of PVR on NYHA status

Data were available from 19 studies across 21 time points. Most of the studies included were reporting outcomes in the first 2 years after PVR. The pooled mean difference estimated by meta-analysis indicated a beneficial effect of PVR on NYHA status, corresponding to an improvement slightly under one functional class (–0.8; 95% CI: –1.0 to –0.6), with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 89.0%; Table 3).

When time was incorporated to the model, the baseline effect (intercept) of PVR was –0.7 functional class (95% CI: –0.9 to –0.6; SE 0.1). The annual change was null (β = –0.02 functional class per year; 95% CI: –0.04 to 0.00; SE 0.01). Adding time to the model reduced slightly heterogeneity but remained considerable (I2 = 85.2%; Fig. 2).

The inspection of cubic spline models (Fig. 2) highlighted that the initial improvement remained constant in time, with a slight trend toward further improvement, but with wide 95% CI.

Subgroup analyses did not identify additional patterns in the effect of PVR on NYHA status (Supplemental Fig. S17).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to decipher from the current literature whether the short-term haemodynamical benefits observed after PVR are lost on the long term, as observed in the study suggested by Hallbergson et al.10 To our knowledge, this is the first meta-regression on the effect of time after PVR on physiologic outcomes. We found several studies reporting on short-term outcomes after PVR, but the scarcity of long-term outcomes mostly prevented us from drawing a firm conclusion.

We could not find a clinically significant effect of time on any of the parameters investigated. However, spline models reveal a nonlinear relationship between time and ventricular volumes. PVR was associated with a rapid reduction in right ventricular volumes immediately after surgery. This reduction appeared to peak between 2 and 2.5 years, after which the ventricle gradually began to regain volume for both parameters. This trend was driven by the few studies with data beyond 5 years. Within the available follow-up period, spline analysis did not indicate a complete return to baseline volumes, which differs from the longitudinal retrospective cohort with follow-up extending up to 10 years, as reported by Hallbergson et al.10 However, our results should be interpreted with caution because of the scarcity of long-term data and the wide CIs.

Visual inspection of spline curves showed an inverse pattern for LVEDV, which tended to increase until 2 years after PVR before stabilizing or slowly decreasing thereafter. LVESV appeared to follow a more constant pattern with a light increase with time. However, there were fewer data for these outcomes especially after 36 months, rendering interpretation of splines difficult. The wide CIs further limit the ability to reliably predict long-term ventricular remodelling.

Spline inspection and meta-regression did not reveal any clear patterns with time for QRS duration, peak VO2, or NYHA class after PVR, which was in accordance with existing literature.9

Our results do support that PVR in patients with TOF had a short-term effect on reducing right ventricular volumes and PR fraction. These findings are consistent and very similar to the results of the meta-analysis from Van den Eynde et al.9 We found a small nonstatistically significant effect on LVESV and observed a slight increase in LVEDV. This also aligns with the observation from Van den Eynde et al.9 Reduction of the PR fraction also confirmed the findings from the same previous analysis. Finally, we also confirmed that PVR did not seem to have a significant effect on either RVEF, QRS duration, or peak VO2, as previously reported.7, 8, 9

This study offers additional interesting insight into debate about ideal PVR timing in patients with TOF. Although it has been suggested that waiting too long before surgical intervention could compromise right ventricular recovery,68 redo rates around 4%-5% have been observed after 5 years in this population.8,9 Moreover, a reintervention rate of up to 40% was observed in patients with congenital heart disease 10 years after PVR in one study,83 whereas 20%-60% of patients with TOF had structural valve deterioration or reintervention 10 years after PVR in another study.84 The question of how long the beneficial effects of PVR on cardiac function truly persist remains mostly unanswered. With this analysis, we emphasized that these effects remain largely unknown beyond 5 years. Although numerous studies have reported short- and midterm outcomes, data on outcomes beyond 10 years, apart from mortality or reintervention, remain scarce. More long-term studies are needed to better understand the complex haemodynamics of repaired TOF after PVR.

Several subanalyses were conducted to explore variability between studies, but most yielded results consistent with the main analysis. These analyses were based on easily identifiable study-level variables (eg, year of publication and methodological quality), which do not necessarily capture the clinical profiles of the patients. Ideally, we would have performed subanalyses using clinical data such as the type of TOF, the surgical vs transcatheter approach, the type of pulmonary valve used, or surgeries performed concurrently with or before PVR. These analyses would have been highly relevant to identify which patient subgroups derive the most benefit from PVR. However, the included studies were highly heterogeneous in reporting these variables, and only a few studies reported exclusively surgical or exclusively transcatheter PVR and other key clinical characteristics named above, making meaningful subanalyses impossible. Such analyses could have helped explain the substantial heterogeneity observed for many parameters and better clarify which patient subgroups derive the most benefit from PVR.

Our appreciation of the “success” of PVR can be multifactorial. Although cardiac clinical parameters such as ventricular volumes, PR fraction, and functional status are well reported in the literature, their change over time has been far less thoroughly examined, highlighting the added value of our time-based meta-regression. However, interpreting the overall benefit of PVR also requires integrating other clinical endpoints, including reinterventions, mortality, heart failure, and patient-reported quality of life. Previous meta-analyses, including those by Ferraz-Cavalcanti et al.8 and Van den Eynde et al.,9 have evaluated mortality and reintervention rates, but compiled evidence on other clinically meaningful outcomes remains limited. To this day, we do not know with certainty whether PVR improves long-term survival, meaningfully reduces life-threatening complications, and improves global and long-term quality of life.

This review has several strengths. We report data from approximately 2768 patients across multiple countries, providing a large-scale and diverse sample. Given that most of the included studies were observational in nature, the findings can reasonably be considered reflective of real-world clinical practice. We conducted pertinent sensitivity analyses, which allows us to affirm that our results did not significantly vary depending on many factors such as period of publication or type of primary repair. Finally, we were the first meta-analysis to look at the effect of time on several physiological variable following PVR in patients with TOF.

This study also has limitations. Because the decision to perform PVR is strongly influenced by factors such as disease severity, age, comorbidities, and center-specific practices, observational studies are particularly prone to confounding by indication, which limits causal interpretation of the observed outcomes. Timing of outcome was reported in the form of means or medians, which leads to loss of information about individual patients. Finally, heterogeneity was considerable in most of the analysis, therefore complicating the interpretation of the results. Although some pooled estimates displayed narrow CIs due to the large number of contributing studies, the substantial heterogeneity primarily reflects genuine variability between studies.

In conclusion, PVR exerts a clear and immediate positive effect on key parameters such as PR, RVESV, RVEDV, LVEDV, and NYHA class. These improvements are observed shortly after the procedure. However, long-term data were scarce, and we could not identify significant or robust changes in these parameters over time. The effect of PVR on LVESV, RVEF, peak VO2, and QRS duration was limited, in terms of both overall impact and changes over time. Overall, although PVR confers short-term benefits on some parameters, the available literature remains insufficient to characterize the long-term evolution of cardiac parameters and to inform the optimal timing of a subsequent PVR.
