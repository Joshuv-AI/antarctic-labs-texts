Diagnostic plasma protein signatures in sporadic and BRCA1/2 breast cancer patients via targeted proteomics
Authors: Kristrun Y Holm, Yassene Mohammed, Valdis Gunnarsdottir Thormar, Finnur F Eiríksson, Christoph H Borchers, Stefan Sigurdsson, Jon G Jonasson, Margret Thorsteinsdottir, Sigridur K Bodvarsdottir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly breast cancer diagnosis is critical, particularly for individuals at hereditary risk. The aim of this study was to identify blood-derived protein biomarkers for breast cancer diagnosis to improve current screening strategies.MethodsAbsolute quantification of 98 plasma proteins was performed via a UPLC-MRM-MS assay on plasma samples from 135 breast cancer patients and paired controls, one-third of which were of hereditary BRCA1/2 origin, and from women diagnosed within 5 years from blood collection. Plasma protein concentration profiles were compared between patients and paired controls across breast cancer subgroups.ResultsProteomic analysis revealed four plasma proteins with elevated abundance and four other proteins with reduced abundance across different breast cancer subgroups. Haptoglobin (HP) was elevated in sporadic cases (n = 88) and cases with grade 3 tumors (n = 44), adipocyte plasma membrane-associated protein was elevated in cases with large tumors (n = 60) and grade 3 tumors (n = 44), lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) was elevated in node-positive (n = 58) and HER2 (n = 9) cases, and secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich was elevated in luminal B (n = 45) cases. Transthyretin was reduced in luminal B patients, and apolipoprotein A4 was reduced in HER2 patients. Immunoglobulin heavy constant mu (IGHM) and CD5 antigen-like (CD5L) were lower in BRCA1/2 (n = 47) and triple-negative (n = 17) patients than in paired controls. IGHM and CD5L were also lower in BRCA1/2 patients than in paired sporadic patients. Additionally, IGHM in plasma was lower among BRCA2 mutation carriers (n = 7) diagnosed with breast cancer within 5 years of blood collection than among paired controls and CD5L was also lower than among paired controls and sporadic patients diagnosed within 5 years of blood collection. The plasma proteins associated with breast cancer specific survival were LBP, HP, and IGHM.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that plasma protein concentrations may have diagnostic and prognostic value for breast cancer screening and could provide valuable insights for biomarker discovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02348-y.Keywords: Breast cancer, Plasma, BRCA1/2, Targeted proteomics, Mass spectrometry, Case-control study, Prognosis, Diagnosis

Background

Breast cancer is the most prevalent cancer among women worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related death in women [1]. Effective screening and early diagnosis are critical, enabling early intervention that leads to a better prognosis. X-ray mammography screening is widely used for breast cancer diagnosis, but early-stage tumors are still difficult to detect in women with dense breast tissue, such as young and premenopausal women [2]. This limitation is even more pronounced among individuals at increased risk of developing breast cancer at a young age, including carriers of high-risk germline mutations in BRCA1 or BRCA2.

Several prognosis-related protein biomarkers for breast cancer, including the estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and Ki-67 proliferation markers, are used in clinical practice. These biomarkers are well established as tumor-tissue biomarkers and, together with clinicopathological variables such as nodal status, tumor size and histological grade, are used to determine the breast cancer subtype and prognosis and to assess treatment options [3, 4]. On the basis of ER, PR, HER2 and Ki-67 expression levels, breast tumors are classified into the molecular subtypes luminal A, luminal B, HER2-type tumors, and triple-negative/basal-like subtypes [4]. While these biomarkers are valuable for breast cancer subtyping, they can be accessed only after diagnosis and collection of biopsies. Blood-derived biomarkers may offer a minimally invasive approach for early breast cancer diagnosis. Moreover, exploring blood-derived proteomic concentrations could provide valuable insights into the molecular diversity and heterogeneity among breast cancer subgroups. Plasma and serum are easily accessible biofluids that are well suited for health monitoring and the detection of disease-related biomarkers to assess health status [5].

Over the past few decades, bottom-up discovery proteomics via mass spectrometry (MS) methods has been used to determine disease-related protein expression [6]. Targeted methods such as ultra-performance liquid chromatography multiple reaction monitoring MS (UPLC-MRM-MS) have proven to be well suited for biomarker discovery, as they provide precise and reliable measurements that can detect subtle biological changes [7]. Using UPLC-MRM-MS analysis, the absolute concentration of endogenous peptides can be determined via calibration curves generated from the peak area ratio of synthetic natural-abundance (NAT) peptides to stable isotope–labelled internal standards (SISs) [8]. This method can be used for protein quantification in various biological fluids, including human plasma, serum, urine and tissue biopsies [8–11].

The aim of this case‒control study was to identify blood-derived protein markers for breast cancer diagnosis to improve current screening strategies. By using UPLC-MRM-MS targeted proteomics, eight breast cancer-associated plasma proteins were identified. Three of these proteins also demonstrated prognostic value for breast cancer-specific survival.

Methods

The study cohort

Plasma samples from a retrospective cohort of breast cancer patients and paired controls (based on calendar year and age at sample collection) were analysed via UPLC-MRM-MS targeted proteomics. Blood samples were collected either just before surgery (n = 135) or within 5 years of breast cancer diagnosis (n = 14) between 1988 and 2004 (median year 1999). Samples were collected at Landspítali University Hospital, Reykjavik, Iceland, or as a part of a national blood sample acquisition project from individuals diagnosed with breast cancer as well as from their relatives and controls [12]. Breast cancer cases and controls were screened for two germline BRCA1/2 mutations: the Icelandic BRCA2 999del5 (officially c.771_775del) founder mutation, which is responsible for more than 70% of the BRCA1/2 breast cancer predisposition in the population, and another less common BRCA1 c.5074G > A mutation [13–15]. BRCA1 inactivation by promoter methylation was analyzed in the tumor DNA [16]. Cases that were found to be negative for screened germline mutations or somatic BRCA1 inactivation were defined as “sporadic” cases. Each BRCA1/2 case sample was paired with a sporadic case sample on the basis of the time of plasma sample collection, age at breast cancer diagnosis, and subtype. This Icelandic study cohort is well defined with known breast cancer subtypes and pathological variables. The classification of subtypes was based on immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of biomarkers on tissue microarrays for ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67, which revealed HER2 positivity with an IHC score of 3 and Ki-67 positivity with ≥ 15% stained nuclei. Data on clinicopathological parameters, including tumor size, nodal status, and Nottingham tumor grade, were obtained from the Department of Pathology, Landspítali University Hospital, Reykjavik, Iceland [17–19]. Plasma samples were obtained from the Icelandic Cancer Society biobank and stored at − 80 °C prior to analyses. All blood samples were pre-processed using a standardized plasma preparation protocol, including controlled centrifugation time and freeze-thaw cycles were avoided.

Sample preparation and UPLC-MRM-MS targeted proteomics

Automated bottom-up proteomics sample preparation, including overnight digestion of human plasma samples as well as preparation of calibration standards and quality control (QC) samples, was carried out as previously described [20]. All the plasma samples (10 µg total protein on the column) were analyzed via a Waters ACQUITY UPLC coupled with a Xevo TQ-XS mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA) in positive ionization mode, as previously described with minor modifications [20, 21]. Six peptides previously associated with breast cancer were custom synthesized by MRM Proteomics, Inc., and added to the PeptiQuant™ 125-protein human plasma kit (MRM Proteomics, Inc., Montreal, QC, Canada). These peptide surrogates were for osteopontin (SPP1, P10451), matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9, P14780), 72 kDa type IV collagenase (MMP2, P08253), coagulation factor XIII A chain (F13A1, P00488), coagulation factor XIII B chain (F13B, P05160), and heat shock protein beta-1 (HSPB1, P04792) [22–25]. The UPLC-MRM-MS analyses were performed in eight separate batches, whereas each batch included calibration standards, QC samples, blanks, and, on average, 42 human plasma samples comprising case‒control pairs.

Data processing

Peak-area data were generated via Skyline Quantitative Analysis software (version 22.2.0.351, University of Washington) [26]. For each peptide, the NAT/SIS peak area ratio of the calibration standards was calculated to generate a calibration curve via 1/x2-weighted linear regression. This calibration curve was used to determine the protein concentrations based on the concentrations of their peptide surrogates in all the samples, expressed as femtomoles per microliter (fmol/µL). The acceptance criteria for the calibration standards and QCs were as previously described [21, 27]. Proteins were selected for further analysis if their concentrations were above the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) in 50% or more of the samples. Missing values (i.e., if a protein was below the LLOQ in the sample) were imputed using the lowest concentration measured for the respective protein in the dataset. To account for variability in total protein concentration across samples, protein concentrations were normalized by dividing the individual protein concentration by the total protein content measured within the corresponding sample. For the assessment of intra- and inter-batch precision and accuracy, three replicates of QC samples at three different concentrations and calibration standards at 10 concentrations were generated for each batch (n = 8). Batch effects were addressed and corrected via the empirical Bayes-based ComBat method (Bioconductor sva package, R) [28].

Statistical analysis

Statistical data analyses were conducted via R (version 4.2.2), and multivariate data analysis was performed via SIMCA Pro 18 software (Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics, Umeå, Sweden). Associations between categorical clinicopathological variables were examined via either Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for multivariate visualization. Comparative analysis of the protein concentrations was performed on the basis of a statistical test and fold change. The nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was selected for p- value calculation due to non-normal data distribution. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to test the normality of the data. Proteins meeting the criteria of nominal (unadjusted) p < 0.05 and log2-fold change (log2FC) ≤ − 0.25 or ≥ 0.25 were considered differentially abundant and included in further analyses. Volcano plots were generated to visualize differential protein abundance in relation to clinicopathological characteristics between cases and paired controls. Survival curves (to compare survival between the lower quartile (0–25%) and the upper quartile (75–100%) of the normalized concentrations per protein across samples) were generated via the Kaplan‒Meier method. Survival curves were compared via the log-rank test, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Patients diagnosed with breast cancer were followed from diagnosis until death or the 10-year follow-up. The outcome was breast cancer-specific survival. Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusted for age, was applied to proteins that satisfied the proportional hazards assumption.

Results

Identification of plasma proteins for breast cancer diagnosis

The overall study design is presented in Fig. 1. Sample preparation was fully automated via a liquid handling robot. The plasma samples were proteolytically cleaved with trypsin, SIS was added, and the samples were concentrated via solid-phase extraction (SPE). UPLC-MRM-MS analysis was conducted on plasma samples from patients with breast cancer and paired controls. The breast cancer study cohort is well defined with respect to breast cancer biomarker-based subtypes, clinicopathological variables and BRCA1/2 germline mutations. Using UPLC-MRM-MS analysis, the absolute quantification of multiple peptides was performed simultaneously via calibration curves generated with the NAT and SIS peptides, from which the endogenous protein concentrations were determined via the NAT/SIS peak area ratio. The endogenous protein concentration values were normalized, and minimal batch effects were corrected. The clinical data were used to group samples for statistical analysis of protein concentrations via volcano plots and box plots. Differentially abundant proteins between cases and controls were further analysed in relation to breast cancer-specific survival probability.

Characteristics of the study population

Among the 135 samples from breast cancer patients, 45 were from BRAC2 999del5 carriers, one was from a BRCA1 c.5074G > A carrier, and one was from a patient with BRCA1 inactivation by promoter methylation in the tumor tissue, for a total of 47 BRCA1/2-related cases (35%) and 88 cases (65%) of sporadic origin (Table 1). Four healthy controls were from BRAC2 999del5 carriers, and those were paired with BRCA2 999del5 breast cancer patients. The tumors from sporadic and BRCA1/2 patients did not differ in tumor size, nodal status, or hormone receptor status (ER or PR). However, the BRCA1/2 patients had significantly lower HER2 expression (p = 0.010), higher Ki67 expression (p = 0.003), and had significantly different phenotypic classifications according to breast cancer subtype (p < 0.001).

Quantifiable plasma proteins and batch adjustment

The results demonstrate the strong analytical performance of the assay, with precise quantification across all calibration standards (Figure S1A) and the QC sample levels (Figure S1B) in all batches, with relative standard deviations of < 15% for all 131 proteins analysed. A total of 98 proteins were quantifiable with concentrations above their LLOQ in more than 50% of the samples and were included for further analysis (Table S1). Among the samples, the total concentration of quantified proteins varied across samples, ranging from 140,000 to 530,000 fmol/µL, with concentrations between 300,000 and 400,000 fmol/µL in most samples (Figure S2A). To account for this variability, protein concentrations were normalized to the total concentration of quantifiable proteins within each sample. Following normalization, a batch difference became apparent (Figure S2B), which was subsequently corrected. After batch adjustment, batch-related differences were negligible, resulting in improved comparability across samples (Figure S2C).

Plasma proteins associated with breast cancer clinical and pathological variables

For the entire study cohort, no significant difference in plasma protein concentrations was observed between the breast cancer patients (n = 135) and the paired controls. However, among patients with positive nodal (N+) status (n = 58), lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) was more abundant (p = 0.022, log2FC = 0.342; Fig. 2A) than paired controls. In the subgroup of N+ sporadic cases (n = 38), the concentrations of four plasma proteins, including apolipoprotein L1 (APOL1; p = 0.003, log2FC = 0.277), complement C9 (C9; p = 0.019, log2FC = 0.284), haptoglobin (HP; p = 0.025, log2FC = 0.356), and CD5 antigen-like (CD5L; p = 0.030, log2FC = 0.329), were relatively high, whereas adiponectin (ADIPOQ; p = 0.017, log2FC = -0.389) exhibited relatively low abundance (Figure S3A). Compared with paired controls, breast cancer patients with a tumor size > 20 mm (T2-T3, n = 60) presented a greater abundance of adipocyte plasma membrane-associated protein (APMAP; p = 0.007, log2FC = 0.299; Fig. 2B). Among sporadic cases with > 20 mm (T2-T3) tumors (n = 36), APMAP was also found to have greater abundance (p = 0.004, log2FC = 0.404), along with beta-ala-his dipeptidase (CNDP1; p = 0.006, log2FC = 0.292) and LBP (p = 0.049, log2FC = 0.384) (Figure S3B), than paired controls. On the other hand, BRCA1/2 cases with > 20 mm (T2-T3) tumors (n = 24) presented a lower abundance of serum paraoxonase/arylesterase 1 (PON1; p = 0.001, log2FC = -0.250) than paired controls (Figure S3C). Among the breast cancer patients with Nottingham grade 3 tumors (n = 44), the two plasma proteins APMAP (p = 0.024, log2FC = 0.309) and HP (p = 0.029, log2FC = 0.455) were more abundant than paired controls (Fig. 2C). Among luminal B cancer cases (n = 45), secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich (SPARC) was more abundant (p = 0.013, log2FC = 1.111), whereas transthyretin (TTR) was less abundant (p = 0.028, log2FC = -0.294) than paired controls were (Fig. 2D). The apparent upregulation of SPRAC was dependent on only one case with a very high abundance. Moreover, among sporadic luminal B cases (n = 21), SPARC was more abundant (p = 0.026, log2FC = 2.34), whereas fibulin-1 (FBLN1; p = 0.010, log2FC = -0.274), apolipoprotein C1 (APOC1; p = 0.022, log2FC = -0.439), and ADIPOQ (p = 0.029, log2FC = -0.389) were less abundant than paired controls (Figure S3F). Plasma proteins associated with sporadic and BRCA1/2 breast cancer patients

Plasma protein concentrations were evaluated in sporadic and BRCA1/2 breast cancer cases and compared with paired controls. Among the sporadic cases (n = 88), HP was the only plasma protein found to have elevated abundance (p = 0.016, log2FC = 0.274; Fig. 3A). However, among the two proteins identified in BRCA1/2 patients (n = 47), IGHM (p = 0.038, log2FC = -0.557) and CD5L (p = 0.012, log2FC = -0.381) presented lower abundances than paired controls (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, when the plasma protein concentrations of BRCA1/2 patients (n = 47) were compared with those of paired sporadic patients, the plasma protein abundances of IGHM (p = 0.003, log2FC = -0.672) and CD5L (p = 0.004, log2FC = -0.403) were again lower than those of the BRCA1/2 patients (Fig. 3C). Moreover, both IGHM and CD5L had lower abundances in plasma from BRCA2 999del5 mutation carriers, which had been collected within 5 years prior to breast cancer diagnosis (n = 7), compared with paired controls and sporadic patients, whose samples had also been collected within 5 years of diagnosis. Compared with paired control (p = 0.048) and sporadic cases (p = 0.026) (Fig. 4A), IGHM exhibited a lower abundance among BRCA2 cases, whereas CD5L exhibited a lower abundance among BRCA2 cases only when compared with paired sporadic cases (p = 0.025; Fig. 4B). The IGHM and CD5L concentrations were highly correlated with each other in the study cohort (correlation coefficient = 0.97; Fig. 4C).

Prognostic related plasma proteins

The prognostic relevance of the plasma proteins that were found to be associated with breast cancer subgroups in the present study was analysed for 10-year breast cancer-specific survival. High LBP plasma concentrations, which were associated with elevated abundance among breast cancer patients with N+ status and of the HER2 subtype, were found to be associated with shorter breast cancer-specific survival in the entire study cohort (0–25% vs. 75–100%, HR = 1.48, log-rank p = 0.012) and within the subgroup of N+ breast cancer patients (0–25% vs. 75–100%, HR = 1.83, log-rank p = 0.033) (Fig. 5A) but not among patients with the HER2 subtype (data not shown). High plasma concentrations of HP, which were associated with sporadic and grade 3 cases, were also found to be associated with reduced breast cancer-specific survival within the entire study cohort (0–25% vs. 75–100%, HR = 1.58, log-rank p = 0.012) but not with sporadic breast cancer cases (Fig. 5B) or the grade 3 subgroup (data not shown). Low IGHM concentrations, which were associated with TNBC and BRCA1/2 cancer cases, were found to be associated with breast cancer-specific survival among BRCA1/2 patients (0–25% vs. 75–100%, HR = 0.50, log-rank p = 0.031) but not for the entire breast cancer study cohort (Fig. 5C). Among the analyzed subgroups, no other differentially abundant plasma proteins, including APMAP, SPARC, TTR, APOA4, and CD5L, were associated with shorter breast cancer-specific survival.

Discussion

In this study, we used targeted proteomics for the absolute quantification of plasma proteins from breast cancer patients compared with paired healthy women. Our proteomic analysis revealed differential abundances of eight plasma proteins, with four proteins, LBP, APMAP, HP and SPARC, being elevated, and another four, TTR, APOA4, IGHM and CD5L, being reduced. These alterations were associated with key pathological features such as positive nodal status, large tumor size, high tumor grade, breast cancer subtypes, and BRCA1/2 mutation status. The differential abundance criteria, which are based on the Wilcoxon rank test and the log2FC criteria, were applied to identify proteins that are readily measurable, reproducible, and capable of discriminating groups, with potential as diagnostic and prognostic markers. Three of the eight plasma proteins were also found to be associated with 10-year breast cancer-specific survival. While these findings indicate a clinical relevance of these proteins, this study represents a discovery phase, and their diagnostic performance in breast cancer would need to be validated in future studies with an independent cohort.

Compared with controls, the pro-inflammatory protein LBP in the plasma of breast cancer patients with N+ status and the HER2 subtype was elevated. LBP levels have previously been reported to be increased in the plasma of colorectal cancer patients [29]. High plasma concentrations of LBP were shown to be associated with shorter breast cancer-specific survival for the entire study cohort and for the subgroup of N+ breast cancer patients in the present study. A recent study identified LBP as a predictor of the response of TNBC to immunotherapy, although the role of LBP in immunotherapy needs to be further explored [30]. LBP plays a key role in the immune response by binding to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to either detoxify it or present it to monocytes to induce an inflammatory response [31]. Previous studies have associated LPS with the promotion of adhesion of breast cancer cells to the vascular endothelium through the activation of monocytes and endothelial cells, a key step in hematogenous metastasis, suggesting that LPS could be an important risk factor for cancer metastasis [32].

High concentrations of APMAP were found to be associated with breast cancer patients with > 20 mm (T2-T3) tumors and grade 3 tumors in the present study. APMAP is characterized as a transmembrane protein that is an endoplasmic reticulum-localized antioxidant that promotes lipid and lipoprotein homeostasis in the endoplasmic reticulum network. Recently, high plasma APMAP levels were found to be associated with phyllodes tumors in the breast, which is a rare tumor type that primarily affects women at a young age [33]. APMAP was recently identified as having elevated expression related to epigenetic regulation in non-basal TNBC, which belongs to a subgroup of luminal androgen receptor-positive tumors [34].

HP is a plasma glycoprotein that binds free haemoglobin, prevents the loss of iron and inhibits oxidative activity [35]. In our study, we found that elevated plasma levels of HP were associated with sporadic breast cancer cases, cases with positive nodal status and cases with grade 3 tumors. A recent study revealed increased HP expression in breast tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues and in the plasma of breast cancer patients compared with controls [36]. The same study revealed higher protein levels of HP in the serum of breast cancer patients than in that of controls and that the post-operation HP levels in the serum were lower than the pre-operation levels. Circulating HP was also shown to activate metabolic reprogramming that forces cells to generate energy through glycolysis rather than the Krebs cycle, which is well known as the Warburg effect. These findings could indicate that HP plays an oncogenic role through regulating glycolysis and maintaining epithelial to mesenchymal transition and chemoresistance in breast cancer. Our results revealed worse 10-year breast cancer-specific survival for patients with high plasma levels of HP in the entire study cohort and in the subgroup of sporadic cases. Elevated levels of HP in serum have been reported across various types of cancer, including breast cancer, and are recognized as indicators of poor cancer prognosis and clinical outcome [37–42]. One of these studies demonstrated that higher levels of HP in the serum of breast cancer patients were associated with a higher tumor grade, as we also reported in the present study, and a reduced survival probability in TNBC patients [41]. High levels of HP have been associated with metastasis in TNBC patients [43] and in xenograft mouse models [44]. Moreover, the overexpression of HP and alterations in its glycosylation status may highlight the important role of HP in breast tumorigenesis [45, 46].

High plasma levels of the SPARC protein were found to be associated with luminal B breast cancer in our study, with a single patient expressing a high level of the SPARC protein. SPARC is a well-established stromal marker that is strongly associated with cancer-associated fibroblasts and has variable expression levels in breast cancer tissues [47]. High SPARC expression has been shown to be associated with a poor prognosis in patients with TNBC, suggesting a novel approach as an immunotherapeutic marker [48].

One of the plasma proteins with lower concentrations in luminal B breast cancer patients than in paired controls is the extracellular protein TTR, also known as prealbumin. Other studies have reported lower concentrations of plasma TTR in breast cancer patients than in controls [49, 50]. Lowered TTR expression levels have been associated with tamoxifen resistance [51]. This is, however, contradictory to the results of other studies where plasma TTR was found at significantly higher levels among breast cancer patients than among healthy controls and in patients with rare phyllodes breast tumors [33, 52].

Another plasma protein, the antioxidant protein APOA4, exhibited a lower abundance among breast cancer patients with the HER2 subtype than in paired controls in the current study. In a recent study, APOA4 was found to be lower in breast cancer patients than in controls [49]. Furthermore, APOA4 was reduced in BRCA1 mutation carriers who developed breast cancer compared with both non-mutant healthy controls and cancer-free BRCA1 mutation carriers [53]. The lower plasma APOA4 levels among HER2- and BRCA1-mutated breast cancer patients are in agreement with the lack of ER, as estrogen has been shown to induce transcription of the APOA4 gene [54].

The reduced abundance of the two plasma proteins IGHM and CD5L among the TNBC patients and BRCA1/2 patients was of high interest, while most patients within the TNBC subgroup were related to BRCA1/2 (11 of 17). Both of these proteins were already present in reduced abundance within the plasma of BRCA2 999del5 carriers up to 5 years before breast cancer diagnosis compared with paired sporadic breast cancer cases, which were also undiagnosed at the time of blood collection. Worse 10-year breast cancer-specific survival was found to be associated with low IGHM plasma levels among BRCA1/2 breast cancer patients but not among the entire breast cancer cohort in the present study. The IGHM gene encodes the C chain region of the mu heavy chain, which (along with two light chains) defines the human immunoglobulin M (IgM) isotype expressed by B cells. Natural IgM can bind and induce apoptosis in cancerous cells, indicating that IgM immunosurveillance forms an early defense mechanism against tumor development [55]. One study revealed lower IGHM plasma levels in breast cancer patients than in controls, whereas IGHM levels were increased in patients with more advanced stage III-IV tumors, indicating lower IGHM levels in relation to early breast cancer [49]. Interestingly, the IGHM protein on plasma exosomes was detected only in healthy females but not in patients with breast cancer in a recent study [56]. Low IGHM expression in breast tumors has been associated with low immune-cell responses and is correlated with unfavorable survival [57–60].

The CD5L glycoprotein is a key player in acute and chronic inflammatory processes. It is synthesized and secreted primarily by macrophages and exhibits anti-apoptotic activity [61]. All the circulating IgM immune complexes consist of five IgM protomers coupled together by a joining (J) chain, in addition to the protein CD5L, which forms an asymmetrical pentamer [62]. There are few reports about CD5L in relation to breast cancer. The involvement of CD5L has been suggested in lung adenocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma [63, 64]. CD5L appears to have opposing effects on these two tumor types: driving lung cancer and inhibiting liver cancer. Low CD5L expression in hepatocellular carcinoma has been associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis. The same study reported that serum CD5L levels were significantly lower in individuals with hepatocellular carcinoma than in healthy controls [63]. However, high CD5L expression in the tumor-associated macrophages of lung adenocarcinoma was correlated with poor prognosis and increased tumorigenesis [64].

Novel plasma biomarkers are highly important for carriers of BRCA1/2 germline mutations, which are at high risk for breast cancer development at an early age and generally have a poor prognosis. Many healthy carriers of BRCA1/2 mutations undergo risk-reducing bilateral mastectomy (RRBM) to prevent the onset of breast cancer [65]. Thus, novel candidate biomarkers, such as IGHM and CD5L, which are highly correlated with each other, can be used for early breast cancer diagnosis to predict the onset of aggressive hereditary breast cancer. This could aid in clinical decision-making regarding RRBM and could be used for BRCA1/2-related homologous recombination deficiency, which has been shown to affect up to 22% of sporadic breast tumors [66].

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the potential of targeted proteomics via UPLC-MRM-MS to discover blood-derived diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in human plasma for the treatment of breast cancer. We identified eight plasma proteins that are differentially altered across key pathological features, breast cancer subtypes, and BRCA1/2 mutation statuses, with three proteins associated with breast cancer-specific survival. These findings highlight the importance of considering the clinical and genetic heterogeneity of breast cancer in the search for blood-derived biomarkers and suggest that our results could provide valuable insights for biomarker discovery.
