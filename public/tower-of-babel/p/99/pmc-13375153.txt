Women's Perspectives Regarding Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) After Their Diagnosis: A Qualitative Study in Pakistan
Authors: Misbah Jehangir, Shenqiang Qu, Ijaz ul Haq, Mashael Huwaikem, Sohail Akhtar, Shahbaz Ahmad Zakki, Gausal Azam Khan, Xiao‐shuang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome affects approximately 8%–13% of women during their reproductive age, and receiving a diagnosis can deeply affect their emotional well‐being and personal choices. This study explores how women's experiences after their diagnosis and examines the influence of diagnosis on their psychological well‐being and lifestyle decisions.DesignThis Qualitative descriptive study engaged 40 pre‐menopausal women between the ages of 18‐45, recruited from the District Headquarter Hospital, Haripur, Pakistan. Semi‐structured interviews were conducted both in‐person and telephonically, for flexibility and depth in data collection. Thematic Analysis was carried out using NVivo Software to identify the patterns and key insights.ResultsThe majority of participants had irregular menstrual cycles (73%), hirsutism (80%), and weight gain (78%). The challenges faced by women with PCOS were categorized into four key themes: (1) Preliminary Assessment, (2) Psychosocial Impact of Being Diagnosed with PCOS, (3) Health Concerns, and (4) Impact on Life Decisions. This study showed that women with PCOS encounter multiple difficulties; however, they believed that parental and spousal support could help them cope with the syndrome.ConclusionsThis study highlights the importance of timely diagnosis, emotional support, and receiving proper education about PCOS diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrinological condition in women, affecting about 8%–13% of women in reproductive age [1]. It is considered a complex condition with a variety of symptoms that could be harder to manage with age [2]. These symptoms often include irregular periods, excess hair growth, and the presence of multiple cysts in the ovaries [3]. Women with PCOS who are overweight and who have insulin resistance are mostly facing health issues like hypertension, abnormal cholesterol levels, and other signs of metabolic syndrome, than women who don't have PCOS [4]. These metabolic issues of the syndrome may increase the poor cardiovascular risk profiles, such as dyslipidemia and hypertension [5].

The psychosocial effects of PCOS include body image concerns, fertility‐related stress, and social stigma, further compounding the emotional burden of the condition [6]. emotional distress experienced by affected individuals. Women often feel self‐conscious about their appearance, which can negatively affect their interpersonal relationships and social interactions [7]. Weight‐related stigma and societal pressure about fertility and body appearance negatively affect women's social and psychosocial well‐being [8]. For the management of PCOS, women's psychosocial challenges should be addressed [9].

They face many challenges in their daily life due to PCOS. Hormonal imbalance and societal pressure directly impact women's mental health in the face of anxiety and depression [10]. Both the physical and emotional symptoms of PCOS can interfere with the women's ability to participate in education and the workforce, sometimes limiting their career opportunities and choices in life [11]. Delayed diagnosis of PCOS is due to limited awareness and inadequate training of healthcare providers in Pakistan [12]. In a culture where fertility is deeply valued, the possibility of anovulatory infertility can cause stress in marriages and bring social pressure that influences women's decisions around marriage and family planning [13]. Researchers have suggested that many women experience frustration due to inconsistent medical advice, inadequate healthcare support, and misinformation about the condition [14]. Studies show that many women feel frustrated due to improper medical advice, inadequate healthcare support, and a lack of information about their condition [4]. Doctors are not seriously concerned about the patient's mental health, which increases their emotional illness [15]. Women with PCOS frequently struggle with psychological issues such as anxiety and depression, which are due to societal pressure and a lack of support [10]. The stigma and cultural experiences around reproductive health could stop women from getting medical advice. The societal experiences, especially around being fertile and marriageable, put extra stress on women diagnosed with PCOS, affecting their emotional well‐being and social status [16].

PCOS diagnosis in adolescents is particularly complex because many adolescents have irregular menstrual cycles and polycystic ovarian morphology without hyperandrogenism [17]. In addition, adolescents with norm cyclic nonhyperandrogenism present a diagnostic challenge, as chemical or biochemical androgen excess may exist despite an apparently regular cycle. These overlapping pathological and physiological features increase the risks of misdiagnosis, overdiagnosis, and unnecessary psychosocial labeling [18]. Although treatment strategies remain debated, current approaches often emphasize lifestyle modification and, in some cases, the use of combined oral contraceptives and insulin‐sensitizing agents, which may provide both reproductive and metabolic benefits [19].

Previously, women with PCOS faced social challenges such as cultural barriers, difficulties in obtaining a diagnosis, and negative reactions from family members, which often led to confusion [20]. Other studies, such as one conducted in Iran, examined the impact of PCOS on quality of life [21], while a study from Oman highlighted women's experiences in coping with PCOS‐related infertility [22]. Many local studies have focused on knowledge, attitudes, practices, and quality of life among young females with PCOS [12, 23], but there is limited research on how women adapt over time after receiving a diagnosis, particularly in semi‐urban areas. In addition, methodological transparency is under‐reported in many South Asian qualitative studies. The current study was aimed to explore women's experiences after their diagnosis in semi‐urban Pakistani contexts. This research will help in understanding the impact of PCOS on women's psychosocial well‐being and lifestyle decisions.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional, qualitative, observational study, situated within a constructive paradigm, employed thematic analysis, incorporating semi‐structured face‐to‐face and telephonic interviews, to explore women lived experiences and perspectives following their diagnosis between August and November 2024. This approach was chosen to allow an in‐depth understanding of subjective experiences within their social contexts. This study was defined and reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SQRQ) guidelines to enhance methodological transparency and rigor (Supporting Information: File S1).

Study Sample

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling. This sampling technique was chosen to ensure inclusion of women with diverse backgrounds and experiences related to the study objectives. A total of 40 women with self‐reported PCOS diagnoses were recruited from the outpatient department (OPD) of DHQ Hospital, Haripur, Pakistan. Verbal informed consent was obtained from each participant as the study involved minimal risk, and the ethics committee approved verbal consent as a culturally appropriate and practical method for ensuring participant autonomy. Saturation was achieved when no new codes and insights emerged from additional interviews. After 28 interviews, recurring themes were consistent, suggesting thematic sufficiency. We continue our interviews till 40 in the hope of new ideas.

Inclusion Criteria

Women who had a formal diagnosis of PCOS, aged between 18 and 45 years old, and were willing to participate in in‐depth interviews were included.

Exclusion Criteria

Women with co‐morbidities and those under 18 or over 45 years old were excluded from the study, as well as women who were not willing to provide informed consent.

Data Collection

The semi‐structured interview guide was developed with the help of previous literature and supporting data [31]. Interviews were conducted by a female researcher with a background in Public Health who was trained in conducting qualitative interviews. Participants feel comfortable with her and openly discuss their concerns. Interviews lasted for 20–60 min and were transcribed verbatim. Interviews were conducted in local languages like Urdu and Hindko and then translated into English by preserving meaning and accuracy. During translation, it was ensured that it didn't change the meaning and accuracy. All interviews were audio recorded with the participant's consent, and the researcher made reflexive notes during and immediately after each session. Data collection and preliminary analysis occurred concurrently, allowing insights to inform subsequent interviews. Interviews were conducted in a safe and confidential place where no family member or healthcare provider was present, to protect against any social harm. Participants were voluntary, and if they felt uncomfortable, they had a chance to skip any question or withdraw from the interview at any time.

Interview Guide

The interview guide was divided into six sections. Section A focused on demographic details to capture the details of the participants. Section B concerns the PCOS medical history. Section C addresses the experiences after diagnosis. Section D focused on the diagnostic effect that impacts their psychosocial well‐being and daily life decisions. Section E is for understanding the overall thoughts about their diagnosis. Section F examines family and social support. The guide was reviewed for its relevance and appropriateness.

Pilot Study: The guide was first piloted with five women with PCOS to refine the research questions. Throughout the data collection process, the interviewer continuously transcribed the participants' entire dataset, formed a preliminary list of codes, reviewed all transcripts, and developed salient themes. Data of the pilot study was not included in the final analysis.

Data Analysis

Data was transcribed multiple times to check familiarity, developing initial codes, identifying meaningful patterns, and phrases in participants' responses. These codes were compared, grouped, and refined into broader themes. NVivo 15 software (Lumivero) was used to analyze the data systematically. The themes were checked repeatedly to ensure they accurately represented participants' voices. Reflexive journaling was used to minimize biases, and multiple researchers cross‐checked codes. Preconceptions were bracketed, and discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. Regular discussion within the team provides additional insights and minimizes subjective biases. All data were anonymized and stored in a separate folder with a password that is only accessible to the research team.

Thematic Analysis using Braun and Clarke's six‐step approach was used to analyze the data. Initial coding was developed based on research objectives and questions, and additional codes were derived from the participants' own words as the interviews were examined line by line. The codes were grouped into general groups that formed the final themes.

Results

Sample Characteristics

Most (60%) of the women with PCOS were married, and 45% had primary or secondary education. The majority (68%) of the respondents were between 18 and 30 years old at the time of diagnosis. Only 24 women who were married had to ask about their children. The most common self‐reported symptoms that prompted seeking medical attention during the interview were irregular menstrual cycles (73%), hirsutism (80%), and weight gain (78%). Respondents reported having 2–3 symptoms of PCOS at diagnosis, but this number increased to 4–5 over time (Table 2).

Psychosocial Impact of Being Diagnosed With PCOS

Psychosocial well‐being has a substantial positive and negative impact on health outcomes in women with PCOS. Parents' and husbands' support was described as a source of strength, reduced anxiety, and a sense of stability. In contrast, some participants reported withholding their condition from family, which contributed to heightened stress, social withdrawal, and feelings of isolation.

Positive Impact

Women reported that a stronger psychosocial support system helps them in coping with the disease. They also declared that when they have backend support, they adhere to the treatment, not losing hope, and interact towards a better quality of life. One respondent described this as a strength.

“Ahh…it makes my relationship strong. Whenever my husband sees me, he feels my pain.” (35 years, diagnosed age: 30 years)

For some women, mutual care and empathy increased marital closeness. Husbands' attunement to their pain strengthened intimacy and facilitated practical support and shared coping. They believed that the protective and supportive response of husbands might act as a buffer to the psychological burden of the condition and support adjustment.

Patients reported that their parents provided reassurance, encouragement, and empathy, which helped decrease feelings of isolation and distress related to PCOS symptoms (weight gain, acne, and infertility concerns). As one of the patients stated:

“Ummm… My parents support me. I received treatment with their support. I am here for a regular checkup. Ahh… I made it possible to manage my symptoms of acne, hirsutism, and irregular menstrual cycle, and now I'm noticing improvement in my health.” (23 years, diagnosed age; 18 years)

It reflects that parents' support facilitates access to healthcare and treatment adherence. Women seeking regular checkups and managing their physical symptoms on their own was attributed to their counseling and support. These reported improvements illustrate that family support enhanced both care‐seeking behavior and perceived well‐being.

Negative Impact

During their PCOS journey, many women reported a lack of emotional support from their husbands and in‐laws. That minimal and frequent support leads them to psychosocial illness, reduction in treatment adherence, and worsening overall quality of life, as shown in Figure 1. Some women keep their condition secret from their husbands and in‐laws due to their anger, fear of divorce, and societal unacceptance. They experience intense pressure and blame them for delays in conceiving without understanding their medical condition. As one woman reported:

“My husband blamed it on your fault, you don't want to conceive, he shouts at me and doesn't understand it's a medical issue that triggers my psychological illness.” (28 years; diagnosis age: 23 years)

Husbands' lack of understanding and judgmental attitudes intensified PCOS‐related anovulatory infertility, leading to psychological distress. Some husbands perceived it as intentional rather than a medical condition, which led to emotional suffering and reinforced feelings of guilt, isolation, and marital strain.

Many PCOS women struggle with their self‐confidence and self‐esteem due to visible physical symptoms such as acne, hirsutism, and weight gain. Societal beauty standards and social comparisons further contribute to feelings of dissatisfaction and depression.

“After getting married, I started noticing PCOS symptoms. My husband didn't even want to see my face. So, I isolated myself and fought alone with a lot of societal trauma and anxiety.” (25 years, diagnosed at age 23).

The onset of PCOS symptoms after marriage was compounded by the husband's rejection, leading to profound emotional and social isolation. The absence of partner support, combined with societal pressures, intensified the participants' experience of trauma and anxiety, illustrating how marital dynamics shaped the psychosocial burden of the condition.

Contrasting Roles of Husband and Parent in Psychosocial Adjustments

Parents and husbands have different roles in supporting women. A supportive husband encourages them to attend medical visits and accept infertility as a medical condition, which may reduce psychological distress. In contrast, some unsupportive husbands blame their wives for intentionally not conceiving and for having poor physical health. In this way, it also leads to isolation, marital strain, and lower self‐esteem. At the time of diagnosis, parents were considered a source of early support, financial help, and protection from community gossip. However, parental pressure regarding marriage prospects and limited understanding of PCOS reinforced stigma and left women constrained.

These contrasts highlight how husband support influenced long‐term adjustment within marriage, while parental support played a stronger role in immediate coping and social protection.

Fear of Infertility

Many of the PCOS women experience psychological distress that leads to chronic stress, anxiety, and depression, which worsen their PCOS symptoms. Many women experience low self‐esteem and feelings of inadequacy, believing they are unable to fulfill societal expectations of motherhood (Figure 2). Unmarried girls are also afraid of being infertile after marriage, and many married women fear being infertile due to past miscarriages (Figure 2). These conditions make women physically and mentally weak.

Impact on life decisions

PCOS has a negative impact on women's life decisions; it may impact their health, profession, education, and relationships. Many women have fertility issues, which have a negative impact on their family planning decisions and expectations towards motherhood. PCOS symptoms affect their social interactions, relationships, and confidence as the emotional toll of PCOS leads to anxiety, depression, and low self‐esteem. Major life decisions that affect their life decisions are family planning, academic setbacks, loss of confidence, avoiding social interactions, and dietary changes (Figure 3).

Impact of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) on life decisions of women.

Family Planning

PCOS is a major obstacle to family planning. They feel like incomplete women because of their uncertain menstrual cycles and the difficulty of getting pregnant. Many women used the word “combat” in relation to infertility.

“I am unable to make my family because of PCOS. I do not dare to combat infertility; it disrupts my family planning” (24 years of age, diagnosed at age 17 years).

This shows how anovulatory infertility linked to PCOS was a profound disruption for women. The inability to conceive was described not only as a biological limitation but also as an inability to fulfill the expected roles of motherhood and sustain family life.

Women experience many miscarriages, which also affects their family planning.

“Ahem…. PCOS makes my life extremely difficult. I got pregnant 2 times, but after 4 to 5 months of pregnancy, I had miscarriages. I thought that due to preeclampsia, I lost my baby.” (29 years of age, diagnosed at age 24 years)

The participants linked her repeated miscarriages with preeclampsia, describing how reproductive challenges intensified her sense of loss and difficulty in achieving a sustainable pregnancy. Her statements reflect the grief over losing children and the perception that PCOS creates hurdles to healthy conception.

Education

PCOS affects women's education, including their physical, emotional, and mental health. One of the participants describes her situation like this:

“My periods are unpredictable and get horrible most of the time, which makes me stop going to college.” (19 years old, diagnosed age: 18)

This illustrates how menstrual irregularities and dysmenorrhea can disrupt education, reflecting the broader impact of PCOS on academic participation and social engagement.

Women stopped their education because of irregular, heavy menstrual cycles, which are difficult for a woman to deal with.

“Yes, it has an impact on my education. I was so unwell that I couldn't sit or stand much, so I simply completed my fourth‐year papers and discontinued further studies because I had to endure so much pain.” (22 years of age, diagnosed age: 20)

Participants illustrated that PCOS symptoms disrupt their daily functioning and decision‐making. The intensity of PCOS symptoms makes it difficult to continue education, showing how the severity forces women to adjust their priorities and narrow their future aspirations.

Emotional and Mental Well‐Being

Women reported that they think PCOS not only affects them physically, but it also has a deep connection with their psychological illness. They noticed that their hormones get imbalanced anytime, which is totally frustrating and anxiety‐provoking.

“I avoid my college due to persistent menstrual irregularities, weight gain, and acne, so I avoid going to college and social gatherings. Umm…I overthink it and am emotionally drained day by day.” (18 years old, diagnosed age; 16 years old).

Visible and symptomatic aspects of PCOS, such as menstrual irregularity, weight gain, and acne, contributed to feelings of insecurity and social withdrawal. This reflects the cumulative emotional toll, where overthinking and persistent distress led to both reduced social participation and declining psychological well‐being.

A lot of Women stated that they struggled with their bad body image, and they considered that they had lost their self‐confidence. As one of the women said:

“It had a profound impact on my self‐confidence. I could not work or enjoy myself with my husband and my friends. I felt that due to my excessive weight gain, I lost my spark.” (24 years old, diagnosed age; 20 years old)

Weight gain associated with PCOS undermined women's self‐confidence and sense of identity. Loss of enjoyment in marital and social relationships adds to a broader psychosocial burden, where bodily changes diminish self‐worth and create feelings of lost vitality.

Dietary Changes

Females were not as aware of PCOS, and were confused, stressed, and had no guidance regarding the dietary changes they needed to combat PCOS. As one of the women said,

“After my PCOS diagnosis, the doctor didn't guide me as such regarding my diet plan. So, I was very confused and anxious about it. Ahh… I heard that just by maintaining healthy habits, we would cope with PCOS.” (30 years old, diagnosed age: 28 years)

It reflects the uncertainty women faced when medical guidance on lifestyle management was insufficient. The absence of clear dietary advice created confusion and anxiety, but their reliance on general information, such as “a healthy diet,” effectively helped them manage their condition.

Dietary changes have had a great positive and negative impact on their psychological, social, and overall health. Some women stated that they avoid those foods that are in their daily consumption.

“Umm…It just had a significant impact on my food. I had to quit those foods that are my everyday consumption. But I feel a little improvement in my condition.” (22 years old, diagnosed age: 22).

It shows that dietary adjustments required for PCOS management disrupted the participants' usual eating habits. While giving up routine foods was experienced as a challenge, health improvements encouraged a sense of benefit and motivation to continue lifestyle changes.

Social Life: Women with PCOS experience their social lives through their irregular menstrual cycle, acne, excessive weight gain, and androgenic alopecia. These symptoms lead to feelings of embarrassment, dissatisfaction, feelings of judgment, and social anxiety. As one of the women claimed:

“2 days ago, when I went to my children's school, I had no idea about my period date at that time. Suddenly, I found a red mark on my shawl in front of everyone, and that was embarrassing for me.” (34 years old, diagnosed age; 25 years old)

This captures how unpredictable the menstrual cycle can be, creating moments of public embarrassment and distress. The incident highlights how PCOS symptoms interfere with everyday social roles and reinforce feelings of vulnerability.

Some women stated that before their PCOS diagnosis, they used to be very outgoing, but following the diagnosis, they began avoiding family gatherings due to experiences of anovulatory infertility. They reported feeling insecure and jealous when they saw anyone who had conceived.

“Ummm…. Recently, I went to a family gathering. I noticed everyone adores my younger sister, who has an infant baby. Some women questioned me about my child. I felt embarrassed and heartbroken at that moment. After that day, I decided to avoid gatherings.”

Social interactions around fertility heightened the women's sense of discomfort and exclusion. Questions about conceiving, combined with visible comparisons to fertile relatives, intensified feelings of embarrassment and social scrutiny. Such experiences contributed to deliberate withdrawal from gatherings and highlighted how stigma around infertility affects women's social participation.

Discussion

PCOS has a greater impact on women's physical health, psycho‐social health, and daily lifestyle decisions. This is because of the major factors like symptom severity, late diagnosis, and health challenges. Diagnosing PCOS in adolescents is a critical challenge because it overlaps with normal pubertal development. A study highlights that many teens exhibit PCOM with normal ovarian function; there is a high risk of overdiagnosis when ultrasound findings are used without hyperandrogenism or persistent symptoms, such as metabolic abnormalities [32]. A previous study found that combining low‐dose metformin with an oral contraceptive can produce sustainable metabolic improvements in adolescents with PCOS, including reductions in BMI, improvements in insulin resistance, and better androgen levels [33]. Diagnosis in adolescents not only requires but also early metabolic and lifestyle guidance to manage risk and reduce psychosocial burden [34].

Women's initial reactions to their PCOS diagnosis are often marked by fear, confusion, and frustration because of their symptom severity and the length of their symptoms. Women's initial reactions include shock and anxiety, especially when it comes to the symptoms of PCOS [35]. According to [36]Women had internalized stress and confusion, which was exacerbated by a lack of opportunities to express their emotions due to social and cultural expectations. Newly diagnosed women struggled with identifying and understanding symptoms, while women with a longer illness duration were more adaptive and had a more coherent illness representation. This pattern reflects the Common‐Sense Model of Illness Representation, which explains a patient's understanding of their illness based on symptoms and perceived consequences [37]. They struggle with identity and the cause of PCOS. Although a hormonal imbalance causes it, a lack of clear information confuses them. They perceived it as a long‐term condition because of its association with anovulatory infertility and social stigma, expressing uncertainty about its manageability.

Women with PCOS feel fearful due to their symptoms, compounded by limited knowledge and awareness, which triggers low self‐confidence and a feeling of being “incomplete.” Multiple studies have reported that women with PCOS have lower self‐esteem and a heightened perception of symptom severity compared to women without a diagnosis [38]. It is also a major cause of anovulatory infertility and is associated with long‐term metabolic and oncological risks, such as type II diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and endometrial cancer [39]. Early diagnosis may minimize fertility issues and allow for timely interventions. It also increases the chance of successful pregnancies [6]. Married women are more concerned about conceiving or fertility, while unmarried women focus on body appearance and marriage prospects. The Health Belief Model is relevant in this context, illustrating women's beliefs about the outcomes of their health condition [40]. Most women with PCOS perceived it as a critical condition because of symptom severity, particularly physical symptoms such as anovulatory infertility, excessive weight gain, and oligomenorrhea, which also make them vulnerable. Some women believe that healthy lifestyle modifications can help them cope with the disease. Many face barriers such as limited medical guidance, high cultural expectations, and a lack of family support.

PCOS has a greater impact on women's diet, education, social relationships, and family planning. PCOS alters conditions affect their daily activities and priorities. As a result of these issues, it leads to low confidence, shame, embarrassment, and to avoid them, they may isolate themselves. It directly impacts their education and social life. PCOS has negatively affected women's academic performance due to its clinical manifestations [41]. Furthermore, according to [38] PCOS has a significant impact on women's overall quality of life, specifically in those societies where discussing reproductive health is considered taboo. Women with PCOS spend their lives with infertility. Unmarried women are anxious about their lives and ask themselves about when to get married and, if they get married, whether they will ever conceive. As a study reported, the lack of ovulation or infrequent ovulation leads to irregular or absent menstruation, and in this case, natural conception is extremely unlikely in the absence of ovulation [42]. These findings relate to the Biopsychosocial Model, which interrelates the physical symptoms, psychological, and social factors to shape health outcomes [43]. PCOS is a multi‐dimensional condition that requires holistic management and addresses not only medical treatment but also emotional support and social understanding.

Social gatherings and personal relationships are affected due to PCOS symptoms like Infertility, weight fluctuations, and other physical symptoms. Women who have PCOS often report frequent issues with their general well‐being, sexual satisfaction, and social functioning [44]. Social demands and unattainable beauty standards, combined with the ongoing physical symptoms, lead them to severe trauma. They avoid social gatherings and face relatives due to their physical appearance. Social stigma is most common in PCOS patients [45]. Women reported that their relatives questioned them about their anovulatory infertility, and they felt embarrassed. PCOS symptoms can increase mental health issues and negatively impact women's societal relationships aspects leading to the negative impact of psycho‐social factors on women's lives. According to Stigma Theory, these perspectives highlight that labeling, stereotypes, and loss of status reinforce marginalization within families and communities [46].

Many studies have examined psychosocial health and care experiences [47], but this study offers a context‐specific perspective from Pakistan, highlighting women's decision‐making and social consequences. It contributes new evidence by emphasizing the marital and family planning pressures associated with PCOS in a semi‐urban Pakistani setting, an area overlooked in prior research, which has mainly focused on psychological burden or infertility in metropolitan contexts. Our findings capture how cultural expectations around marriage and childbearing intersect with women's lived experiences of PCOS.

Certain limitations were acknowledged in this study. Due to cultural sensitivity, most of the women feel ashamed, fear of judgment, and are embarrassed by sharing their true feelings or experiences. This could decrease the chance of sharing accurate information during data collection. This study explores self‐reported data by personal perceptions that lead to under‐reporting and over‐reporting for some experiences. Future longitudinal studies will incorporate a more comprehensive understanding of how women's perceptions will change with time. Despite these limitations, this study highlights the perception of women's health challenges they face during PCOS, the impact of psycho‐social well‐being, and daily life decisions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that a PCOS diagnosis profoundly shapes women's lives by affecting their health management practices, their psychosocial well‐being, and daily life decisions. Strong parental and husband support helps in coping with the syndrome, while a lack of support exacerbates emotional distress and social isolation. Women faced significant health challenges, fear about infertility, and uncertainty regarding life decisions, highlighting the need for integrated approaches that address both medical and psychological dimensions of PCOS. By situating these findings in Pakistan's marital and cultural context, this study extends existing research and underscores the importance of culturally sensitive, comprehensive care.
