Association between MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathway‐related gene polymorphisms and migraine
Authors: Mingxue Wang, Yujia Gu, Shuhan Meng, Lixin Kang, Jing Yang, Degang Sun, Yuxing Liu, Ze Wan, Yi Shan, Dongjie Xue, Chang Su, Shufen Li, RanYan, Yu Liu, Yonghui Pan, Yashuang Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe causes of migraine remain unclear. Evidence suggests that the MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways play a role in migraine pathogenesis. However, studies on genetic polymorphisms in the two pathways associated with migraine are still limited.MethodsThis study included 226 migraineurs and 452 age‐ and sex‐matched nonmigraine control individuals. Genotyping of 31 Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) in 21 genes was performed. The relationship between migraine and gene polymorphisms was analyzed by using logistic regression. SNP–SNP interactions were examined by a generalized multifactor dimension reduction (GMDR) approach. The possible role of SNPs was evaluated with gene expression data from the GTEx database.ResultsThe RASGRP2‐rs2230414 GT genotype was associated with decreased migraine risk compared with the wild‐type GG genotype [ORadj (95% CI): 0.674(0.458–0.989)]. PIK3R1‐rs3730089 was associated with migraine in the recessive model [ORadj (95% CI): 1.446(1.004–2.083)]. The CACNA1H‐rs61734410 CT genotype was associated with migraine risk [ORadj (95% CI): 1.561(1.068–2.281)]. One significant two‐way SNP–SNP interaction was found (PRKCA rs2228945‐BDNF rs6265) (p = 0.0107). Significant eQTL and sQTL signals were observed for the SNP rs2230414.ConclusionsThis is the first study to systematically reveal significant associations between MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathway‐related gene polymorphisms and migraine risk.Keywords: MAPK, migraine, PI3K/Akt, signaling pathway, single nucleotide polymorphism

INTRODUCTION

Migraine is the second highest cause of disability worldwide and the leading cause among women under 50, according to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study (Steiner et al., 2020). It has been suggested that migraine is a complicated and polygenetic disorder with a heritability of approximately 40%–70% (Sutherland & Griffiths, 2017). Traditionally, research on the molecular mechanisms underlying migraine has focused on monogenic studies. However, advances in DNA sequencing and genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have uncovered a broader array of genetic factors and pathways, offering new insights into migraine (Gormley et al., 2016; Rasmussen et al., 2020). Despite these advances, GWAS findings have shown limited replicability. The largest GWAS to date, involving 102,084 cases and 771,257 controls, identified 123 genomic loci associated with migraine, of which 86 were novel (Hautakangas et al., 2022). This suggests that relying solely on GWAS is insufficient for a comprehensive understanding of migraine genetics.

The MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways play a vital role in migraine pathogenesis (Lin et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Long et al., 2020). These pathways are involved in a variety of cellular processes, including neuronal signaling, inflammation, and cellular stress responses. The MAPK pathway is particularly implicated in peripheral and central sensitization, pain hypersensitivity, neuronal plasticity, and inflammatory responses associated with migraine (Cheng et al., 2018; Impey et al., 1999; Iwashita et al., 2013; Nakaya et al., 2016). In animal models, inhibition of the MAPK pathway has been shown to reduce inflammation and neuropathic pain (Ji et al., 2009). Despite these findings, the role of MAPK pathway gene polymorphisms in migraine remains underexplored.

Similarly, the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway regulates crucial cellular functions such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and glucose transport (Liu et al., 2017). Recent studies have highlighted the importance of the Akt and ERK1/2 pathways in migraine pathogenesis and neural signaling coordination (Guan et al., 2015; Matsuzaki & Darcha, 2015; Srivastava et al., 2014). Inhibition of the upstream modulator of the PI3K/Akt pathway may suppress glycogen synthase kinase (GSK)‐3β expression (Liu et al., 2017). Additionally, differentially regulated circRNAs might influence migraine development by modulating inflammation through the PI3K/Akt pathway (Lin et al., 2020). Activation of the microglial glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor (GLP‐1R) has been shown to inhibit central sensitization in chronic migraine via the PI3K/Akt pathway (Jing et al., 2021). Furthermore, functional enrichment analyses have implicated the focal adhesion‐PI3K‐Akt–mTOR and MAPK signaling pathways in migraine (Yang et al., 2018). However, most of these studies are in vitro or animal‐based, with limited validation in humans. Moreover, there is a scarcity of research on polymorphisms in PI3K/Akt pathway‐related genes in migraine patients.

The integration of findings from both pathways suggests a synergistic role in migraine development and progression. However, the genetic polymorphisms in MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathway‐related genes and their association with migraine risk and subtypes remain inadequately studied. This gap in knowledge highlights the need for comprehensive research to evaluate these associations systematically. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by selecting 21 genes related to the MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways, based on existing literature, and systematically investigating their polymorphisms' role in genetic susceptibility to migraine, including migraine with and without aura. By conducting a case control study in a Chinese population, we aim to provide new insights into the genetic factors contributing to migraine and potentially identify novel therapeutic targets.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

In this case control study, we enrolled 251 migraine patients and 600 nonmigraine control individuals. All participants were enrolled from four centers (the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University and three hospitals of Agricultural Reclamation, Heilongjiang Province: Hong Xinglong Administration Hospital, Bao Quanling Central Hospital, and Bao Quanling Farm Hospital) in northern China. Migraine patients were recruited from the neurology clinics of each hospital, and controls were from the medical examination centers of each hospital during the same period. Migraine patients were diagnosed by two professional neurologists in the Department of Neurology according to the ICHD‐3β criteria (Headache Classification Committee of the International Headache, 2013). The patients were matched at a 1:2 frequency based on sex and age (±2). We also performed sensitivity analysis on the nonmatching data, and detailed characteristics are shown in Table S6. All subjects signed informed consent forms. The Harbin Medical University Medical Science Ethics Committee approved this study.

Anxiety, depression, and sleep quality assessment

The Self‐Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) is a 20‐item questionnaire that was used to assess anxiety levels (Zung, 1971). The total score was calculated by multiplying the raw score by 1.25. SAS scores under 50 indicated no anxiety, and scores over 50 indicated anxiety. Every elevation of 10 in scores indicated increasing levels of anxiety (mild, moderate, and severe anxiety). The Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) was used to assess depression levels (Kroenke et al., 2001). PHQ‐9 scores are divided into four levels: 0–4, which indicates no depression; 5–9, which indicates mild depression; 10–14, which indicates moderate depression; and over 14, which indicates moderate or severe depression. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to assess sleep quality and is a 19‐item questionnaire composed of 7 parts (Buysse et al., 1989). Each part is weighted from zero to three scores; thus, the total score is less than 21. In this study, poor sleep quality was defined as PSQI scores ≥5.

Genomic DNA extraction

First, 2 mL of peripheral blood was collected from all subjects in tubes without anticoagulants and then stored at −80°C. This was followed by genomic DNA extraction (QIAamp® DNA Blood Mini Kit; Qiagen, Germany) as instructed. Later, DNA was measured using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA).

SNPs selection and genotyping

SNPs of MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathway‐related genes were selected in CHB and CHS (Han Chinese in Beijing and Southern) from the 1000 Genomes Project based on tag SNPs with a MAF >0.1 and R 2 > 0.8 using HaploView (version 4.2) software. Another 10 SNPs were selected from the literature, for a total of 31 SNPs in 21 genes (Table S1). We selected SNPs located in the untranslated region (3′‐UTR, 5′‐UTR, introns) and exonic regions of the gene and without LD (R 2 < 0.8) as candidate SNPs. 48‐plex SNPscan kit (Shanghai, China). We randomly selected 60 samples (two of which were contaminated and not included in the analysis) for repeat genotyping. A total of 16 SNPs had 100% concordance, and the other 15 SNPs were above 94.7% (Table S2).

Statistical and bioinformatic analyses

Analysis of the HWE (Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium) for every SNP in control individuals was performed using the SNPassoc package of R (version 4.0.3) (Gonzalez et al., 2007). Logistic regression was adopted in the association analysis of gene polymorphisms in two pathways and migraine. R 4.0.3 was used for statistical analyses. p < 0.05 was considered significant. Multitest calibration was performed using Bonferroni correction (0.05/31). Haploview 4.2 software was used for LD and haplotype analyses. SNP–SNP interaction effects were determined using GMDR instruments (generalized multifactor dimensionality reduction). Power analysis was performed using PASS 15 software.

We used the GTEx data to study the tissue‐specific expression of quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and splice quantitative trait loci (sQTL) to further analyze the effect of significant SNPs on gene expression. The GTEx online database is available at https://gtexportal.org/home/, and it contains 54 human tissues from nearly 1000 individuals (24).

RESULTS

Characteristics of participants

A total of 226 migraine patients and 452 nonmigraine control groups were included in the study. All basic characteristics of migraine patients and control individuals from matched or unmatched data are shown in Table 1 and Table S6. Migraine and nonmigraine subjects were 42.394 ± 13.489 and 42.754 ± 13.476 years of age, respectively. No significance difference in either age or sex was found between migraines and control individuals, but the educational level, occurrence of depression and prevalence of sleep disorders between the two groups were significantly different (p < 0.001). Out of 226 migraineurs, 46 (20.354%) had migraine with aura, and 180 (79.646%) had migraine without aura. The mean ages were 41.696 ± 12.456 and 42.572 ± 13.768 years in the MA and MO groups, respectively (Table 1).

Association between gene polymorphisms and migraine susceptibility

All 31 SNPs were consistent with HWE (p > 0.05) (Table S1). The genotype distribution of rs2230414, rs61734410, rs3732033, and rs2049046 was significantly different between the migraine and control groups (Table 2). For RASGRP2‐rs2230414 (G>T), the GT genotype was associated with decreased susceptibility to migraine compared to the wild‐type GG genotype [OR (95% CI): 0.674 (0.458–0.989), p = 0.044]. rs2230414 was associated with migraine risk in recessive and additive models [ORadj (95% CI): 0.624 (0.436–0.889), p = 0.009; 1.603 (1.125–2.295), p = 0.009, respectively].

PIK3R1‐rs3730089 (G>A) was associated with increased susceptibility to migraine in the recessive model [ORadj (95% CI): 1.446 (1.004–2.083), p = 0.047], and associated with decreased migraine susceptibility in the additive model [ORadj (95% CI): 0.691 (0.480–0.996), p = 0.047]. The CACNA1H‐rs61734410 genotype CT frequency was higher than the TT frequency in migraine patients compared to nonmigraine subjects [ORadj (95% CI): 1.561 (1.068–2.281), p = 0.021]. rs61734410 was also related to migraine in both the dominant and recessive models [ORadj (95% CI): 1.582 (1.093–2.290), p = 0.015; 1.531 (1.050–2.233), p = 0.027, respectively].

In addition, PRKCA‐rs2286674 (A>G) was associated with increased susceptibility to migraine in the dominant and recessive models [OR (95% CI): 1.648 (1.022–2.741), p = 0.046; 1.439 (1.045–1.987), p = 0.026]. In the additive model, PRKCA‐rs2286674 was associated with decreased susceptibility to migraine [OR (95% CI): 0.695 (0.503–0.957), p = 0.026]. Compared with control individuals, the frequency of the G6PC2‐rs3732033 TC was higher than that of TT in migraine patients [OR (95% CI): 1.552 (1.028–2.330), p = 0.035]. rs3732033 was also associated with migraine in the genetic models [the recessive model was CC vs. TC + TT: OR (95% CI): 1.563 (1.036–2.347), p = 0.032; the additive model was CC + TT vs. TC: OR (95% CI): 0.640 (0.426–0.965), p = 0.032]. However, the adjusted ORs of the two SNPs (rs2286674 and rs3732033) were not significant.

Moreover, the AA genotype of BDNF‐rs2049046 (T>A) was higher in migraine patients than in nonmigraine patients when taking TT as a reference [ORadj (95% CI): 1.653 (1.003–2.728), p = 0.049]. rs2049046 A was associated with a higher risk of migraine [OR (95% CI): 1.301 (1.037–1.631), p = 0.023]. However, none of these meaningful results passed the Bonferroni correction with p < 0.0016 (0.05/31), and all associations were no longer statistically significant. Nevertheless, these meaningful results cannot be denied. The results of the other 25 SNPs are summarized in Table S3. Five SNPs (rs2230414, rs61734410, rs2049046, rs2286674, rs3732033) were associated with migraine in the sensitivity analysis (Table S7).

Haplotype analysis

Two haplotype blocks were detected in SYK and G6PC2, and relatively strong LDs were observed in SYK rs2290887‐rs2306042 (D′ = 1.000, r 2 = 0.07) and G6PC2 rs492594‐rs3732033 (D′ = 1.000, r 2 = 0.07) (Figure S1). However, we did not find any haplotypes statistically associated with migraine (Table S5) or in the unmatched data analysis (Table S9).

Gene–gene interaction and risk alleles

GMDR analysis results are presented in Table 3. One significant two‐way interaction model (PRKCA rs2228945 × BDNF rs6265) contributed to the best model, which showed a CVC of 10/10 and a testing accuracy of 0.5877 (p = 0.0107) (Figure S2). In the sensitivity analysis of the matched data, we found that a significant five‐locus model (rs1480153 × rs2279574 × rs2286674 × rs492594 × rs7342847) contributed the highest CVC of 9/10 and p of 0.0107 (Table S9; Figure S3). In addition, we investigated the relationship between the risk alleles of PPP2R2B‐rs1480153 G, DUSP6‐rs2279574 A, PRKCA‐rs2286674 A, G6PC2‐rs492594 G, and PRKCA‐rs7342847 T and susceptibility to migraine. Figure S4a shows the percentage of migraine patients and nonmigraine control subjects in every group of risk SNP alleles. Compared with subjects with zero to four risk alleles, migraine patients with nine to 10 risk alleles had the highest migraine risk. After covariate adjustment, the OR (95% CI) for migraine was 1.604 (95% CI = 1.053–2.471, p = 0.029) (Figure S4b).

Subgroup analysis

In addition, we found that migraine patients with or without aura had different significant SNPs, as shown in Table 4. The frequency of the PPP2R2B‐rs1480153 AG genotype was lower in migraine patients than the wild‐type genotype GG in MA compared to control individuals (ORadj = 0.425, 95% CI = 0.188–0.943). The frequency of the EGFR‐rs2227983 GG genotype was significantly lower in MA than in nonmigraine control individuals when using the AA genotype as a reference (ORadj = 0.321, 95% CI = 0.100–0.891). The frequency of the rs2227983 A allele was higher in the migraine group [OR (95% CI): 1.746 (1.116–2.730)]. CAMK1D‐rs2895526 and NGF‐rs7523831 were associated with migraine with aura risk in a dominant model [ORadj (95% CI): 4.505 (1.366–22.161) and 3.626 (1.248–14.015), respectively]. The frequency of the PRKCA‐rs7342847 CC genotype was significantly lower in MA than in nonmigraine control individuals when taking the TT genotype as a reference (ORadj = 0.216, 95% CI = 0.046–0.715). The rs7342847 T allele was associated with increased MA risk [OR (95% CI): 1.762 (1.109–2.799)]. However, four other SNPs (SYK‐rs2290887, PIK3R1‐rs3730089, G6PC2‐rs3732033 and CACNA1H‐rs61734410) were significantly associated with migraine without aura rather than migraine with aura.

In the sensitivity analysis, we obtained comparable findings, as shown in Table S8. We found that four SNPs (PPP2R2B‐rs1480153, BDNF‐rs2049046, EGFR‐rs2227983, CAMK1D‐rs2895526) were significantly associated with migraine with aura, while three other SNPs (G6PC2‐rs3732033, PRKCA‐rs2286674, CACNA1H‐rs61734410) were associated with migraine without aura. Significant eQTL signals were identified for the rs2230414, rs61734410, rs1342847, and rs1480153 SNPs (Tables S11–S14). The threshold of p values was 0.001 (0.05/49 or 0.05/44). Multiple significant signals (31/49) were identified for the rs61734410 SNP and gene expression levels of PRKCA (Figure 1b,c; Table S13). Four significant signals for rs61734410 were found. As shown in Figure 1a,d, the rs2230414 T allele was correlated with increased RASGRP2 expression levels in neuro‐tibial (p = 8.60 × 10−6), and the rs1480153 G allele increased PPP2R2B gene expression in the brain‐spinal cord (p = 1.80 × 10−6). Twelve sQTL signals for rs2230414 on RASGRP2 were identified, and the rs2230414 T allele was correlated with reduced RASGRP2 levels of variable splicing in human tissues such as Nerve—Tibial (Table 5).

Power analysis

We calculated the power of the study for 31 SNPs using odds ratios (ORs) of 2, 3, and 4 to assess whether the sample size of this study was sufficient to be biologically meaningful. Except for the rs1006737 and rs35737760 SNPs, the power analysis indicated that our sample size achieved sufficient power (>0.874) to conclude with OR 2.0 or higher (Table S15).

DISCUSSION

This is the first study to systematically assess the relationship between polymorphisms in MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathway‐related genes and the risk of migraine susceptibility. We investigated 31 SNPs of 21 genes related to the MAPK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways and attempted to reveal a possible association between the polymorphisms of genes related to the two pathways and migraine patients in China. Our findings identified novel associations of RASGRP2‐rs2230414, PIK3R1‐rs3730089, CACNA1H‐rs61734410, PRKCA‐rs2286674, and G6PC2‐rs3732033 with migraine susceptibility. These pathways are crucial for neuronal signaling, inflammation, and cellular stress responses, which are central to the pathophysiology of migraine. The MAPK pathway contributes to central sensitization, neuronal plasticity, and inflammatory responses in migraine (Cheng et al., 2018; Impey et al., 1999; Iwashita et al., 2013), while the PI3K/Akt pathway regulates neural signal transmission and promotes inflammation, driving migraine development (Guan et al., 2015; Lin et al., 2020; Matsuzaki & Darcha, 2015; Srivastava et al., 2014). Dysregulation in these pathways may lead to increased neuronal excitability and abnormal pain processing, contributing to migraine onset. By highlighting these genetic variations, our findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms that may underlie migraine susceptibility, thus informing potential new targets for therapeutic intervention (Table 7).

RASGRP2 is located on chromosome 11 and contains 22 exons. It activates Rap1 and R/Ras and suppresses apoptosis through the activation of the R/Ras/PI3K/Akt pathway (Takino et al., 2019). RASGRP2 is not only a protective factor for the maintenance of vascular health in the vascular endothelium but also an important signal transduction junction for αIIbβ3 integrin initiation in platelets (Canault & Alessi, 2020; Takino et al., 2021). Furthermore, bioinformatics evidence also indicates that the rs2230414 T allele is correlated with higher RASGRP2 gene expression levels and lower levels of selective splicing of the RASGRP2 gene. However, the expression level of rs2230414 and the pathogenesis of migraine are not clear. The PIK3R1 gene encodes the p85α subunit, which is located on chromosome 5. One previous study found that rs3730089 was associated with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and schizophrenia (SCZ) risk (Huang et al., 2020; Karadoğan et al., 2018). Although there is no causal relationship between T2DM and migraine in the latest study, the two share 33 genes (Islam et al., 2022). Additional studies should be conducted on the RASGRP2 and PIK3R1 genes to unravel the underlying pathological mechanisms.

This study found that the CACNA1H‐rs61734410 polymorphism was associated with migraine susceptibility. Previous research has shown that the CACNA1E‐rs35737760 polymorphism is also associated with migraine (Ambrosini et al., 2017). Both CACNA1H and CACNA1E are important genes within the calcium channel family. Studies have found that somatic mutations in CACNA1H can genetically influence the development of aldosterone‐producing adenomas (Ambrosini et al., 2017). Given the crucial role of calcium channels in migraine, especially in familial hemiplegic migraine, we hypothesize that CACNA1H gene polymorphisms may reduce migraine risk by affecting calcium channel function (Kowalska et al., 2021). However, further research is needed to fully understand this mechanism.

PRKCA may be associated with the protective effect of EGCG against lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐induced inflammation through the inhibition of pro‐inflammatory cytokine release by the MAPK signal pathways (Wang et al., 2021). G6PC2 is mainly expressed in pancreatic islet β‐cells. It is able to generate an ineffective substrate cycle to counteract the action of glucokinase (Hutton & O'Brien, 2009). Recently, an animal study showed that islet‐specific G6PC2 activity affects fasting glucose by modulating GSIS (glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion) sensitivity to glucose (Bosma, Rahim, Singh, et al., 2020). In addition, it has been reported that partial inhibition of G6PC2 is important in avoiding the risk of hypoglycemia (Bosma, Rahim, Oeser, et al., 2020). The relationship between blood glucose and migraine has been long established, with studies finding increased glucose uptake in the posterior white matter areas of the brain and cerebellum in migraine patients (Kassab et al., 2009). Moreover, glucose levels affect the frequency of cortical spreading depression, which consequently influences migraine development (Hoffmann et al., 2013). Therefore, PRKCA and G6PC2 gene polymorphisms are very likely to contribute to migraine by affecting inflammatory factor release or blood glucose levels.

In addition, our results indicated a statistically significant involvement of BDNF‐rs2049046 in migraine, which has been demonstrated previously (Cai et al., 2017; Sutherland et al., 2014). As pain signals, calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP) and BDNF are elevated during migraine (Fischer et al., 2012). Moreover, CGRP stimulates BDNF release in neurons, which induces neuroplasticity (Buldyrev et al., 2006). The most studied polymorphisms in the BDNF gene are Val66Met (rs6265) and rs2049046. However, we did not find any association between rs6265 and migraine.

Migraines with or without aura (MA or MO) are the two major types of migraine. However, twin and genetic studies have shown that the two forms of migraine have different genetic susceptibility profiles (Pisanu et al., 2017). Our study also supports this finding. We showed that PPP2R2B‐rs1480153, PRKCA‐rs7342847, EGFR‐rs2227983, CAMK1D‐rs2895526, and NGF‐rs7523831 were associated with MA, of which the latter two SNPs (CAMK1D‐rs2895526 and NGF‐rs7523831) were previously demonstrated to be related to migraine (Li et al., 2017; Moreno‐Mayordomo et al., 2019). RASGRP2‐rs2230414 was associated with migraines both with and without aura. We first identified that SYK‐rs2290887, PIK3R1‐rs3730089, G6PC2‐rs3732033, and CACNA1H‐rs61734410 were associated with susceptibility to MO rather than MA. Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis we conducted yielded nearly consistent conclusions.

Moreover, the SNP rs1480153 G allele was associated with decreased susceptibility to MA and an increased PPP2R2B expression level. The PPP2R2B gene encodes B55β. Hypermethylation of specific cytosines of the PPP2R2B regulatory element leads to impaired upregulation of B55β (Madera‐Salcedo et al., 2019). Polymorphisms in PPP2R2B are also involved in neuropsychiatric disorders such as Alzheimer's disease and idiopathic tremor (Chen et al., 2009).

In our study, the frequency of the GG genotype of EGFR‐rs2227983 was related to a lower risk of MA. As the driver of tumorigenesis, EGFR is considered to be a biomarker of tumor drug resistance with a physiological function to regulate epithelial tissue development and homeostasis (Sigismund et al., 2018). Two studies found that the rs2227983 polymorphism was associated with several kinds of prognostic malignant cancers, such as metachronous colorectal liver metastases and breast cancer (Bekampytė et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2018). The biological function of rs2227983 requires further investigation.

Migraine is a polygenic, multifactorial disease with a combination of genetic and environmental factors, and gene–gene interactions are becoming increasingly important. GMDR analysis showed a significant two‐way interactive model (PRKCA rs2228945 × BNDF rs6265) contributing to the best model. We found a five‐locus interaction model between rs1480153, rs2279574, rs2286674, rs492594, and rs7342847 in nonmatching data. Furthermore, we found that migraine risk increased with an increasing number of risk SNPs, indicating an additive effect of genetic polymorphisms on migraine risks. In addition, we explored the potential expression of the rs2230414, rs61734410, rs342847, and rs1480153 SNPs using eQTL and sQTL data from GTEx. Nevertheless, these data should be interpreted with caution because GTEx data are from healthy individuals. In addition, GRS analysis showed that the GRS scores were associated with a high risk of migraine susceptibility. The discovery of these gene polymorphisms enriches migraine genetic studies and may provide a reference for the personalized treatment of migraine patients.

However, all previously obtained significant SNPs failed to reach statistical significance after strict correction criteria (Bonferroni correction). Nevertheless, it cannot be denied that our findings have positive implications for the exploration of migraine. Such results are inseparably related to the insufficient sample size. This is also the major limitation of our study. In particular, the sample of the subgroup analysis was limited, and the findings need to be further validated by a large sample study. Second, the genetic polymorphisms have significant ethnic differences, and we have only studied them in Asian populations. Due to the unique lifestyle habits of people in northern China, these genetic polymorphisms may be associated with migraine exclusively within this population and have not yet been systematically investigated in other ethnic groups.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we identified one known SNP and found five novel SNPs associated with migraine risk in the Chinese population. Additionally, we replicated four SNPs and found two novel SNPs associated with susceptibility to migraine with aura. However, only one SNP was associated with both MA and MO, and the other four newly identified SNPs were associated with migraine without aura. Further functional and genetic association studies with larger multicenter and multiethnic samples are needed to clarify the role of these SNPs in migraine.

Further research into the role of the MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways in the pathogenesis of migraine could provide direction for the development of new targeted therapies, offering hope for patients unresponsive to traditional treatments. Additionally, genetic screening to identify high‐risk individuals could facilitate preventive interventions, such as lifestyle modifications and prophylactic medications, thereby reducing the frequency and severity of migraine attacks and improving patients’ quality of life.
