Upcoming Treatments in Celiac Disease: From Luminal Enzymes to Oral Immune Tolerance
Authors: Juha Taavela, Luca Elli, Gerd Bouma, Jason A. Tye‐Din, Detlef Schuppan, Knut E. A. Lundin, Michael Schumann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCeliac disease (CeD) is a chronic autoimmune enteropathy triggered by dietary gluten in genetically predisposed individuals. Currently, a gluten‐free diet (GFD) is the only available treatment, being effective in improving mucosal health and symptoms. However, a considerable percentage of celiacs continue to exhibit residual mucosal damage and symptoms. In addition, dietary lapses, and social and nutritional problems pose difficulties with the GFD. Hence, there is an unmet need for new treatments in CeD, driving the development of pharmacologic interventions, with several new drugs in various stages of clinical development. These drugs target distinct steps in the immune pathology of CeD and are intended for patients who continue to experience symptoms despite trying to follow a GFD. Also, the treatment should heal the defective small intestinal mucosa, which is believed to be linked to the risk of long‐term complications. This review summarizes emerging CeD therapies, covering trial progress, mechanisms, and potential clinical applications.

Introduction

Celiac disease (CeD) affects approximately 1%–2% of the global population, though prevalence varies by region and genetic background [1]. It results from an immune response to dietary gluten, a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye, in individuals carrying HLA‐DQ2 or HLA‐DQ8 alleles [2]. The gluten‐free diet (GFD) remains the cornerstone of treatment and is usually effective at alleviating symptoms, promoting mucosal recovery and reducing the risk of developing long‐term complications [3, 4]. However, the GFD can be insufficient, as symptoms can persist in up to 40% of patients, defined as non‐responsive, and follow‐up endoscopy frequently reveal ongoing mucosal damage [5, 6, 7]. Furthermore, strict adherence to a GFD is challenging because gluten is ubiquitously used by the food industry, and even trace contaminations, as low as 50 mg of gluten, or one five‐hundredth of the average daily intake, can be sufficient to sustain an inflammatory immune response in the intestinal mucosa [8]. Additional problems of a GFD include potential nutritional imbalances, specifically lack of dietary fiber and various essential micronutrients [9, 10]. Accidental gluten exposure from dietary contaminations is an everyday problem faced by CeD patients, often leading to debilitating symptoms akin to acute food poisoning [11]. Social problems, such as difficulties eating out and anxiety around shared meals, also add to the daily burden [12]. These combined burdens underscore the unmet need among clinicians and patients for adjunctive or alternative treatment options in CeD.

Academic research has deepened our understanding of CeD pathogenesis, enabling the development of disease‐specific therapies. We have categorized the current interventional approaches to target the immune response in CeD in four mechanistic pathways (Figure 1): luminal or epithelial processes, including degradation or sequestration of gluten prior to immune recognition and modulation of intestinal permeability; intervening at the level of gliadin presentation, that is targeting transglutaminase‐2 (TG2) activity or disturbing the interaction of the MHC‐II complexing with gliadin; re‐introduction of oral immune‐tolerance to gliadin; and stopping pro‐inflammatory signaling [13, 14, 15, 16]. Several agents within these categories have reached Phase 2 clinical trials, with some demonstrating promising histologic and symptom‐based efficacy in gluten challenge studies (Table 1). However, subsequent studies should also demonstrate efficacy without a gluten challenge, in accordance with regulatory guidance from the FDA [49]. Guidance from regulatory authorities such as the FDA and EMA is crucial, as compliance with them can be decisive for the final market approval of a drug. The FDA's draft guidance for adjunctive treatments to a gluten‐free diet in CeD was received critically, yet it set new standards regarding key elements of study design. As such, the FDA mandates a 52‐week trial duration for chronically administered compounds and stipulates the use of well‐defined patient‐reported outcomes and histological endpoints as primary study objectives [49].

Immune pathology of celiac disease (CeD) and mechanisms of drug development targeting these pathways. Gluten is present in the small‐bowel lumen, where glutenases and gluten sequestrants can prevent mucosal gluten uptake. Gliadin peptides access the lamina propria either actively or passively via intestinal epithelial transport or ‘leaky’ tight junctions, which can be attenuated by drugs. Gliadin peptides are deamidated by transglutaminase 2 (TG2), either within the gut lumen or in the lamina propria. Deamidation introduces negative charges to the immunogenic epitope sequences of gliadin fragments and thereby increases their affinity to the MHC‐II complex (human leukocyte antigens (HLA)‐DQ2 or HLA‐DQ8) expressed mainly on macrophages, dendritic and B cells, which are the effector sites for HLA‐DQ blockers. Gliadin is presented to gliadin‐specific CD4+ T cells as the central hub for the gliadin‐triggered immune response. Activated T helper‐cells secrete cytokines such as interferon‐γ and IL‐21 and interact with B cells eliciting antibody secretion. Drugs like anti‐Ox40 L mAb restrict the activity of gliadin‐activated T‐cells. Tolerance to gluten can be re‐introduced by presenting immunodominant gliadin peptides in secondary lymphoid organs such as the liver and spleen, thereby eliciting a gliadin‐specific tolerogenic T cell response that can home to the affected intestine. Lastly, neutralization of cytokines, especially IL‐15, secreted by lamina propria myeloid and intestinal epithelial cells, can prevent epithelial damage elicited by intraepithelial CD8+ T‐cells. Herein, we undertake a narrative review of the landscape of CeD drug development to date, including both active and discontinued treatment strategies, while highlighting the challenges and limitations of celiac drug research, particularly those related to trial design and the selection of patient populations most likely to need and benefit from these therapies.

Luminal and Epithelial Protection

Luminal CeD therapies aim to neutralize or degrade gluten before it triggers an immune response. These approaches include enzymes breaking gluten into smaller, non‐immunogenic oligopeptides, antibody‐based agents binding gluten, and molecules decreasing intestinal barrier permeability.

Latiglutenase (IMGX003, previously ALV‐003) is an orally administered combination of two engineered gluten‐specific recombinant proteases: cysteine endoprotease B, isoform 2 from barley (Hordeum vulgare), and prolyl endopeptidase derived from the bacterium Sphingomonas capsulate [50]. In the first gluten challenge study, latiglutenase showed promising results, preserving the villous height–to–crypt depth ratio (VH:CrD) and reducing the number of intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) [13, 50]. However, in subsequent studies, latiglutenase did not demonstrate mucosal healing or symptom relief in non‐responsive celiacs and therefore did not meet regulatory guidance [21]. No clear effect was observed either in a later study with a higher dose of latiglutenase in well‐controlled CeD patients [22, 23].

Zamaglutenase (TAK‐062) is a computationally engineered endopeptidase that exhibits enhanced proteolytic activity and specificity toward proline–glutamine dipeptide motifs, which are enriched in immunogenic regions of gliadin peptides that resist enzymatic degradation in the bowel. In a phase I trial, it was well tolerated and capable of degrading up to 6 g gluten in vivo [51]. However, in the Phase 2 study TAK‐062 not only failed in reducing symptoms in CeD patients with minor simulated gluten exposure but also led to a significant reduction of the VH:CrD, implying an unprecedented negative effect on the mucosa [41, 52]. These results led to the discontinuation of the TAK‐062 program [28].

Other enzymes demonstrating efficacy in gluten degradation are Aspergillus niger prolyl endopeptidase (An‐PEP/GliadinX) [35, 53] and endoprotease 40 (E40) that effectively and rapidly digest gluten in ex vivo models. Of these, AN‐PEP is already available over the counter, without being developed as an accepted treatment for CeD, while E40 remains a candidate for future trials [36, 54].

Oral egg yolk‐derived antigliadin antibody (AGY010), developed to neutralize and sequester gluten while in the small bowel lumen, has demonstrated good safety and potential symptom improvement as published 8 years ago [27]. A similar mechanism of action is present for the BL‐7010‐copolymer‐poly (hydroxyethyl methacrylate‐co‐styrene sulfonate) of which no results have been published from a Phase 1/2 trial [55].

Another strategy to protect CeD patients from gluten has been to stabilize the intestinal barrier and to reduce transepithelial gluten passage to the lamina propria, with larazotide‐acetate as an example of such a drug. However, although it reduced symptoms, its Phase 3 trial was discontinued due to lack of efficacy [56, 57]. Remarkably, doubts on the published mechanism of this compound arose at a later point in time [58]. The sirtuin‐6 activator IMU‐856 is another drug targeting the intestinal epithelium. The postulated mechanism contributes to enterocyte regeneration by transcriptional regulation. Recently, IMU‐856 showed safety and a modest effect on small intestinal mucosa architecture in CeD patients challenged with gluten in a Phase 1 study [30, 31].

Transglutaminase 2 and HLA‐DQ Blockers

Tissue transglutaminase (TG2) is the CeD autoantigen, and autoantibodies to TG2 have a high predictive value for the presence of active CeD [59]. By its deamidating capacity, intestinal TG2 is crucial for mounting the T cell‐dependent immune response, as deamidation of glutamine residues within the gliadin epitopes significantly increases the affinity of these peptides to the MHC‐II binding groove, thereby enabling efficient gliadin presentation and consecutive activation and clonal expansion of gluten‐specific CD4+ T cells [60, 61]. TG2 inhibitors, developed early‐on, blocked in vitro gluten deamidation [62] and attenuated TG2 activation in an intestinal inflammation model in vivo [63]. Notably, mice with deletion of the TG2 gene do not display a pathogenic phenotype even in stressed conditions, suggesting compensation by other transglutaminases that are irrelevant for CeD [64, 65]. ZED1227 was developed as a potent and selective TG2 inhibitor, with more than 100‐fold higher specificity for TG2 over other transglutaminases such as TG1, TG3, TG6 or coagulation factor XIII, and absent toxicity in preclinical and Phase 1 clinical studies [66]. A phase 2a clinical study in 160 CeD patients in remission on a GFD challenged with 3 g of gluten daily for 6 weeks revealed that ZED1227 significantly attenuated or prevented gluten‐induced mucosal damage and inflammation, as well as mild gluten‐induced symptoms [14]. ZED1227 also prevented activation of the small intestinal proinflammatory transcriptome dominated by interferon‐γ signaling [9]. A recent phase 2b, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled clinical trial (CEC‐4), which included 397 CeD patients with persistent complaints and mild histological damage, evaluated ZED1227 administered at 3 × 10 mg, 3 × 25 mg, or 1 × 50 mg daily for 12 weeks [17]. Data analysis revealed that all patients (including the placebo‐treated patients) showed continuous improvement in symptom scores and histology, despite a 5‐week placebo run‐in phase before enrollment, and that only the 50‐mg once‐daily dose produced further histological improvement. This suggested that patients had changed their behavior after enrollment in the study and adhered more strictly to the gluten‐free diet (Hawthorne bias), which has also been observed in larazotide and latiglutenase trials. Regardless, some drug effects were evident in this well‐adherent population. Thus, a follow‐up trial has been initiated that will include a small inadvertent gluten exposure (SIGE, representing a total of 1.5 g gluten/week) and higher ZED1227 doses.

DONQ52 (Chugai) employs a different strategy: Blockade of the HLA‐gluten peptide complex by a bispecific antibody binding a complex of HLA‐DQ2.5 loaded with pathogenic gluten T cell epitopes. This prevents recognition by gluten‐specific CD4+ T cells. Ex vivo studies demonstrated potent inhibition of T cell activation following wheat exposure, with partial suppression after rye and barley exposure, indicating broad targeting across cereals [33]. A Phase 1 trial of DONQ52 in well‐controlled CeD patients was completed in early 2025 and a Phase 2 clinical trial in persistently active CeD will commence shortly.

Induction of Tolerance to Gluten

The critical role of CD4+ T cells recognizing gluten peptides presented by HLA‐DQ2 or HLA‐DQ8 was described in the early 1990s; gluten‐specific T cells are infrequent in healthy individuals, making the response highly disease‐specific [67, 68]. T cells found in the CeD intestinal mucosa are typically of a follicular T‐helper cell subtype, producing interferon‐γ and interleukin‐21 (IL‐21) [69]. The search for possible gluten‐specific regulatory T cells (Treg) has not been conclusive, which does not rule out the possibility that such cells can be induced [70, 71]. Thus, tolerance inducing immunotherapy is an attractive option as it is also supported by results from mouse models [72, 73]. Three different approaches will be explained as follows: Desensitization using peptides injected in the skin, re‐purposing of a putative immunomodulatory drug targeting active T cells and harnessing the ability of the liver to induce Tregs.

Nexvax2 (ImmusanT) was the first therapy designed specifically to induce antigen‐specific tolerance in HLA‐DQ2.5+ individuals with CeD [15, 74]. While early trials confirmed modulation of gluten‐specific T cell responses, Nexvax2 did not meet its primary endpoint of reducing symptoms during high‐dose gluten challenge in a Phase 2 study. Two to 4 hours after Nexvax2 injection, an oral gluten‐like symptomatic response with nausea and even vomiting was observed, consistent with the intended activation of gluten‐specific T cells [75]. This was paralleled by peaking serum levels of interleukin‐2 (IL‐2), an unexpected finding that subsequent studies confirmed to be a sensitive and specific biomarker of gluten‐induced T‐cell activation in CeD [75]. Many therapeutic trials have subsequently employed measurement of IL‐2 in serum or whole blood as a functional readout of gluten‐specific T cells [76, 77].

Teriflunomide, a drug known to interfere with the mitochondrial enzyme di‐hydroorotate dehydrogenase, is approved for the treatment of multiple sclerosis, presumably by interfering with the activation of high‐affinity, disease‐specific T cells [42]. However, no effect on gluten‐specific T cell activation was found, and the development was halted.

TAK‐101 (Takeda) is a gliadin‐encapsulating tolerogenic nanoparticle designed to induce immune deviation by delivering gliadin to tolerogenic antigen‐presenting cells in the spleen [78]. In a controlled gluten‐challenge study, TAK‐101 reduced gluten‐specific T cell activation and protected against small‐intestinal mucosal injury; further studies are awaited.

KAN‐101 (Anokion SA) uses peptides with a liver‐targeting glycosylation motif to deliver deamidated gliadin peptides to hepatic antigen‐presenting cells that have tolerogenic properties and can affect the intestine. Phase 1 data showed safety and reduction of gluten‐induced T cell activation, including attenuation of serum IL‐2 responses [76]. Unfortunately, this program was also recently terminated, in this case, for economic reasons.

VTP‐1000 (Barinthus Biotherapeutics) uses the company's self‐assembling nanoparticular tolerogenic immunotherapy platform to deliver 12 gluten peptides together with rapamycin. This design broadens T cell epitope coverage and integrates tolerogenic conditioning. A Phase 1 trial is underway.

TPM502 (Topas Therapeutics) is a polymer‐coated iron oxide nanoparticle conjugated to gluten antigens that primarily home to liver sinusoidal endothelium [79]. A phase 2a trial in CeD revealed a favorable safety profile with manageable gastrointestinal tolerability. Pharmacodynamic parameters indicated antigen‐specific tolerance induction with reduced whole‐blood IL‐2 and interferon‐γ secretion with symptoms being attenuated also after gluten challenge [18]. The treatment significantly reduced the response of circulating gluten‐reactive T cells to gluten challenge and increased gluten‐specific CD4+ regulatory T cells at the highest dose. Further studies are awaited.

Strategies Targeting Cytokine Signaling

Among the diverse therapeutic approaches under investigation for CeD, a growing group of novel anti‐cytokine agents have been developed. IL‐15 is a key cytokine in CeD, produced by intestinal epithelial and lamina propria macrophages and dendritic cells. This is illustrated by the finding that IL‐15 overexpression is required for gluten‐dependent mucosal damage and anti‐gliadin antibody production in a mouse model for CeD [80]. IL‐15 targets intraepithelial cytotoxic CD8+ T cells to arm these cells for eliciting epithelial damage [81]. Importantly, IELs in refractory CeD (RCD) type‐II express high levels of IL‐15 receptor, rendering them resistant to apoptosis [82]. The first trial targeting IL‐15 in patients with CeD used the antibody AMG714 and showed an improvement in clinical symptoms but did not prevent mucosal damage, although it did reduce gluten‐challenge–induced mucosal inflammation [16]. Assessment of AMG714 was continued (as PRV‐015) in a randomized Phase 2 trial in patients with non‐responsive CeD with non‐significant results, as recently posted in clinicaltrials.gov [24]. AMG714 was also studied in RCD type‐II patients, where it failed to reduce aberrant lymphocytes or improve duodenal architecture, but again ameliorated clinical symptoms [83]. Another IL‐15 antibody, CALY002, which is presumed to have superior neutralizing properties compared with AMG714 because it can neutralize both IL‐15 cis‐ and trans‐signaling, completed a phase 2b study in CeD; however, before the results were published, the antibody was sold to Novartis, and publication of the study remains pending [84]. A long‐acting version of IL‐15 blocking agent aiming to enhance the suppression of natural killer (NK) and cytotoxic CD8+ cells is also currently underway by Teva Pharmaceuticals in Phase 2 with results being awaited [25]. Dual‐acting therapies have also been developed in the form of FB102 and ANB033, which are CD122 antagonists that block the shared IL‐2/IL‐15 receptor β‐chain, modulating NK cells and CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets. A phase 1b trial with FB102 in well‐controlled CeD demonstrated statistically significant protection against gluten‐induced mucosal injury in a composite histological VH:CrD and IEL score [26], and a Phase 2 study is underway [32].

Amlitelimab (Sanofi) is a monoclonal antibody that blocks OX40 L, a key immune regulator of memory T cells that are relevant in CeD. It has demonstrated promising results in atopic dermatitis and is now being investigated in a phase 2b study of CeD patients who continue to experience symptoms despite following a GFD [20].

Tofacitinib, a small‐molecule JAK1/JAK3 inhibitor, well established for a variety of autoimmune diseases including ulcerative colitis, blocks several proinflammatory signals pertinent to CeD, including those elicited by IL‐15. This drug has not been extensively evaluated in CeD but has shown clinical and histological responses in an open‐label trial in patients with RCD type‐II, a condition frequently associated with somatic gain‐of‐function JAK/STAT mutations [37]. Similarly, ritlecitinib, a JAK3/TEC inhibitor, showed a decrease in celiac antibody levels in post hoc analysis in a study primarily researching the effect of the drug in vitiligo and alopecia areata [19].

Considerations on Design of Interventional Trials and Target Patient Populations for Emerging Therapies in Celiac Disease

Phase 2a trials are primarily proof‐of‐concept studies designed to evaluate drug efficacy and safety in gluten‐challenge settings, using variable gluten doses (2–10 g) and exposure durations (3–8 weeks) With higher doses, shorter durations have been used, which may increase symptom burden in patients but shorten trial duration without compromising efficacy readouts [19, 21]. A major endpoint in these studies has been mucosal architecture, typically quantified by the continuous VH:CrD, as mucosal damage is an established prognostic factor for long‐term complications such as increased mortality [85]. Participants in these trials have generally been asymptomatic CeD patients with healed duodenal mucosa, defined by a VH:CrD greater than 2.0 or lately more often 2.5 [13, 16, 41, 52, 78].

In phase 2b trials, most companies have aimed to assess drug efficacy in symptomatic CeD patients with ongoing mucosal damage, as this patient group was considered the primary target population for pharmacologic therapy. Following the FDA draft guidance, patient‐reported outcomes have been given greater relevance in these trials, although the correlation between symptoms and disease activity in CeD might be less robust than in other conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease [86]. Moreover, a significant Hawthorne effect has been observed; CeD patients enrolled in clinical trials tend to alter their behavior and demonstrate an improved GFD‐adherence experiencing fewer inadvertent gluten exposures compared to real‐world settings [22]. Therefore, many recent trials have adopted simulated intermittent gluten exposure (SIGE) protocols to better replicate real‐world conditions, as in the trials studying Amlitelimab, TAK‐062 and ZED1227 [41]. To date, there has been only one Phase 3 study in CeD, conducted with larazotide, and it was discontinued at interim analysis. Thus, a study design that accurately captures real‐life patient behavior and uses the most appropriate endpoints remains challenging [38, 56, 57].

The current therapeutic concept is to use these drugs as adjuncts to a GFD, primarily for patients who continue to experience chronic or recurrent symptoms [22]. An interesting alternative approach would be to study patients with acute intermittent symptoms, as those symptoms may be more strongly related to gluten consumption and may also affect a larger portion of the CeD community [11]. On the other hand, mucosal damage can also occur in asymptomatic individuals, who represent another subpopulation of patients that might benefit from adjunctive treatment [5]. Moreover, even among seemingly asymptomatic patients with improved mucosal histology, micronutrient malabsorption, ongoing social and psychological challenges, and the persistent risk of inadvertent gluten exposure can still impair daily life, raising the question of whether all CeD patients could be candidates for an adjunctive treatment [6, 9]. Naturally, factors such as drug costs and national reimbursement policies will influence early adoption if pharmacological therapies become available on the market in the future. In pediatric celiac disease, pharmacological therapies may have an important role to play; however, as is the case in other chronic inflammatory diseases, children are excluded from most drug trials, which will likely result in the approval of the first celiac drugs solely for adult patients. Moreover, patient‐specific factors such as differing drug metabolism will pose unique challenges for trial design and dosing [87]. Nevertheless, if a drug is approved in adults, efficacy data can be extrapolated to support pediatric approval, provided at minimum dedicated pharmacokinetic in children are conducted, with the need for additional safety data determined on a case‐by‐case basis [88]. A further underserved group is represented by RCD, particularly type II, which will require novel treatment strategies. However, its rarity limits commercial interest from the pharmaceutical industry, making the conduct of clinical trials challenging. Progress on drug development in RCD type II thus depends on multinational investigator‐initiated trials or small biotech companies supported by venture capital, as has been the case for tofacitinib and anti‐IL‐15 [82, 83].

Conclusion

The introduction of the GFD to the management of patients with CeD was a revolution; however, a need for adjunctive and potentially alternative treatments remains. Several compounds have shown significant promise in preventing gluten‐induced mucosal damage. Adjunctive treatment for CeD is highly anticipated by clinicians and patients, as it could improve overall health and quality of life, reduce dietary hypervigilance around inadvertent gluten exposure, and potentially reduce the risk of long‐term complications associated with persistent or recurrent mucosal injury. Together, these therapies, specifically those targeting modulation of the celiac immune response, are making progress. Coupled with validation of biomarkers of CeD severity and activity, and improved trial methodology, these developments will likely support the first clinically meaningful drugs for CeD to reach the market.
