Hardware in context

A medical imaging device functions by sending a signal into the body and detecting the amount of attenuation. These measurements allow for a cross-sectional image of the body to be reconstructed using algorithms. Screening modalities include CT, MRI, Ultrasound, Radiography, and PET. All these modalities can achieve at least 1 mm of spatial resolution but have several disadvantages such as a high cost, lack of real time information, ionizing radiation, and the inability to distinguish between hard and soft tissues [1,2]. Some novel imaging techniques utilise optics, electromagnetic waves, and vibrations but are still in development [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

Electrical Impedance Tomography (EIT) is a developing medical imaging technology. EIT injects current into the body and measures the resulting induced voltages. EIT is currently being developed for multiple different use cases. The most common use is for the monitoring of lungs. Several clinical devices are commercially produced for this purpose EIT is being currently researched for many other purposes such as epilepsy and stroke detection in the brain, cancer detection throughout the body, and monitoring of the heart/hemodynamic systems [7,8].

This approach has several advantages, such as a low hardware cost, real-time imaging and data, and no significant safety risks. However, the spatial resolution is relatively low and it lacks robustness in determining the true values of impedances. [9,10]. EIT relies on various hardware and software components to produce medically helpful images. The hardware injects current and read voltages from the boundary electrodes of the test subject. Software processes the measured voltages into a map of impedance changes. An estimate of the systems sensitivity is used to find this impedance map. The sensitivity is a matrix that relates changes in impedance to changes in electrode voltage. The sensitivity matrix is found by software packages such as PyEIT which model the domain using a Finite Element Model (FEM). The sensitivity matrix solves the inverse problem as it relates changes in impedance according to the boundary voltages measured at the electrodes. Several algorithms can solve the inverse problem. Software packages such as PyEIT include these algorithms. This work utilizes the GREIT algorithm as it is a standardized method of solving the inverse problem in saline tanks and chest EIT.

EIT hardware comprises four key areas: current stimulation; data acquisition; a commutation array; and a controller. The current injected has a small amplitude dependent on the frequency dictated by standard IEC60601 [12]. The frequency can range from DC to IR in the low MHZ. Voltages are read from electrodes using a differential signal chain. An Instrumentation amplifier and analogue filtering are often used to prepare the signal for demodulation [13,14].

Typical EIT devices operate in a serial topology where stimulation and sensing circuitry are switched between the electrodes using a commutation matrix. This approach decreases the maximum temporal resolution but improves consistency as hardware imperfections are consistent between measurement channels. Other devices operate with a parallel topology where each electrode has a dedicated stimulation and sensing circuit. This parallel approach increases flexibility and speed but also increases the cost of the device and introduces errors due to differences between channels [15].

Further, two methods of EIT device operation exist, absolute and differential. Absolute EIT uses an electrode model and attempts to determine the true impedance values of the tissue from one measurement set. This method suffers from several issues, making it not robust or practical for clinical use currently. Image production is highly sensitive to differences between real life and the modelled domain. These differences include electrode contact impedance and position. Additionally, noise and other artifacts introduced by instrumentation make absolute EIT difficult to achieve [7]. Differential EIT attempts to determine changes in impedance to mitigate these issues. It uses a comparison with a reference measurement removing common errors such as electrode impedance. While this method is more robust the applicability is reduced as it requires an impedance change [16].

Several open source and commercial devices exist for imaging using EIT. Commercial devices are robust but expensive and do not allow access to change the input and output data making them less useful for research. Open source devices have a variety of specifications, such as electrode count, frequency range, and Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR) [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. Open-source designs offer low-cost solutions that can be programmed with custom firmware. However, many are designed for traditional, single frequency EIT, and thus lack the flexibility to pursue other approaches without modification. This work describes an open source EIT device designed for improved signal integrity, the flexibility of the number of voltage acquisition channels, and a stimulation synthesis capable of standard, multifrequency, chirp, wavelet, and other arbitrary signals. This design flexibility will allow researchers to experiment with novel areas of EIT without the need for redesigning circuitry and/or firmware.

Hardware description

Overview

The device has been split into four separate boards allowing the construction of a PCB stack. These four boards include two analogue processors for stimulation and voltage acquisition, and a further two boards for a controller and dual rail power supply. Communication is required between the controller and the analogue boards. The device uses a communication protocol called Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI). SPI allows one controller to communicate with multiple devices by selecting the peripheral with a Chip Select (CS) pin.

Inline stacking PCB receptacles connected the board. Connections that are common with all boards include a dual power supply of 12 V, digital logic power of 3.3 V, and SPI connections, as well as the voltages from 16 electrodes. On each analogue board, the 16 electrodes are connected to the signal channel by two 16:1 multiplexers. A multiplexer acts as a digitally controlled switch between channels. The system can have extra acquisition and stimulation channels daisy-chained from the control board allowing up to two electrode belts with a maximum of 32 electrodes. Stimulation

The stimulation board aims to generate a current signal that can be digitally controlled. Several different functional groups act together to achieve suitable stimulation. The first step in the chain is to generate a clean, low power signal from a Digital to Analog Convertor (DAC). A DAC is a device that produces an analogue signal from digital commands. In this system, the control board communicates with the DAC by SPI allowing for the production of different signals.

An AD9102 was used as a DAC with a 50 MHZ sampling rate generates the initial signal. The AD9102 is a fully arbitrary waveform generator that outputs two differential current outputs, DI+ and DI-. These signals are combined and filtered into a single voltage signal VDAC with the circuit in Fig. 2. A differential signal is a signal that is transmitted using two wires with the second wire being the inverse of the signal on the first wire. A single signal is only sent using one wire.Fig. 2Differential to single sided convertor for a DAC output.

The DI+ and DI- signals are combined using a differential amplifier. A differential amplifier subtracts one signal from the other and multiplies it by a gain. The amplifier outputs a voltage signal according to Eq. (1) which has been simplified as R14=R13=R15=R16.(1)Vdiff=(R11DI+-R12DI-)

Filters are an essential part of both analogue boards. Filters reduce different frequencies in the signal and are useful for removing noise. A High Pass Filter (HPF) reduces frequencies below its cutoff frequency and a Low Pass Filter (LPF) reduces frequencies above its cutoff frequency. An LPF is also used to reduce aliasing effects. Aliasing occurs when unwanted higher frequency components from sampling in the DAC are present in the signal. Vdiff is filtered by a 2nd order LPF Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency at 2 MHz to get the VDAC signal. A Butterworth filter is a way of implementing an electrical filter using common components.

The next step in the chain is to convert this voltage signal into a current signal. EIT requires a current signal. Current signals are not altered by the loading conditions like a voltage signal. A Voltage Controlled Current Source (VCCS) with a high output impedance converts VDAC to a current signal. A VCCS is a circuit that outputs a signal with its current proportional to the input voltage. The circuit in Fig. 3 shows the VCCS used.Fig. 3Enhanced Current pump for use as a VCCS.

This analogue circuit injects a clean current signal into the test subject through two electrodes. This current generates an electrical field in the test subject. The internal impedances of the test subject determine the resulting voltage field. Boundary recordings of this electrical field provide the measurements needed to solve the inverse problem.

Acquisition

The acquisition board reads differential signals from two electrodes on a test subject. The signals are very small, 1-10mV, and interference from other devices can corrupt them. A signal chain with high amplification and suitable filtering processes these signals before measurement with an Analog to Digital Convertor (ADC). An ADC is a device that converts a voltage signal into the digital domain so a controller can read it. This ADC communicates with the controller board by an SPI connection which sends the measured voltage. The first part of the signal chain converts two differential signals to a single sided signal which can be seen in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Instrumentation amplifier and filtering of two signals.

Instrumentation amplifier and filtering of two signals.

The circuit in Fig. 4 contains several features. Two Transient Voltage Suppression (TVS) diodes protect the V+ and V- lines from large transients and surges. A TVS diode will only conduct when the voltage exceeds a specified value. This conduction helps remove any large voltages that might damage electrical components. An AD8421 instrument amplifier provides a large gain and combines the two signals. Instrument amplifiers are a type of differential amplifier with a large input impedance making them ideal for measuring small signals. The resistor R11 determines the gain of the AD8421. R11 is set to 200Ω to give the AD8421 a gain of 50 making the VAC signal follow Eq. (3).(3)VAC=50(V+-V-)

Before and after amplification, there is a 1st order High Pass Filter (HPF) with a cutoff frequency of 1 kHz that removes any low frequency components such as a DC offset. If an application requires low frequency components a jumper can bypass the HPFs.

To remove aliasing effects when sampling the VAC signal, it needs to be filtered by an LPF. A 2nd order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 2 MHz as seen in Fig. 5 was used.Fig. 5A Butterworth filter used in the acquisition signal chain.

A Butterworth filter used in the acquisition signal chain.

The ADC chosen was the ADS7057 which communicates with the controller by SPI. The analogue signal read by the ADS7057 needs to be between 0 and 3.3 V. The circuit in Fig. 6 adds a DC offset and protects the ADC from overvoltage.Fig. 6An ADC driver for the ADS7057.

An ADC driver for the ADS7057.

This circuit utilizes an inverting amplifier with a DC bias on the positive input. An inverting amplifier multiplies the signal by a negative gain resulting in the output being larger and inverted. A single sided voltage supply powers the amplifier. This power source prevents the output from being negative or greater than 3.3 V. The inverting amplifier has a gain of two, set by R12 and R5. The ADC samples the resulting signal, VADC.

This analogue board measures the voltage between two electrodes on the test subject. The voltages measured from the electric field generated by the current stimulation board are small and contain noise. The board amplifies and filters the signals before measurement by an ADC. The resulting measurements are Dirichlet boundary values used in the inverse problem during imaging.

Control

The control board communicates with the analogue boards and processes data before sending it to a host computer. An inline stacking connector provides SPI communication with the DAC and ADC on the analogue boards. Several FPC ribbon connectors control the multiplexing state on each board and the CS for each device. The ribbon cables enable multiple boards of the same design to be added to the stack with unique control via toggling the CS pin. The board has an STM32G474vet6 microcontroller. This controller was chosen as it provides a high-speed SPI communication interface and the computing power of a Cortex-M4 core.

Power

The device is relatively low powered and can be powered from a single sided 5 V source drawing a typical 300 mA from a DC power supply consuming 1.5 W. The analogue boards require dual power supply rails at ±12 V and the digital components require a 3.3 V power supply The ADP5070 switch mode power supply generates a dual ±15 V voltage. Switch mode power supplies use active components such as transistors to efficiently change the voltage of a power supply. These power supplies produce a large amount of noise due to the high switching frequencies required. Low dropout regulators reduce the ±15 V voltage to the required ±12 V supply. Regulators alter the power supply voltage using changes in resistance and are less noisy than switch mode power supplies when the change in voltage is small. A low dropout regulator connected to the 5 V line powers the digital components.

PCB design

The PCB design was carefully laid out to improve the signal quality. Key areas of improvement were to reduce the amount of crosstalk and common mode noise in the analogue boards. Crosstalk is an issue in circuit design where signals on two different traces couple together. Common mode noise is noise that occurs in components connected to the same signals. Reducing the loop area of traces helps to minimise crosstalk and common mode noise. Both analogue boards have an eight-layer stack to improve signal quality by introducing interplane capacitance and shielding for fast signals.

The stimulation and acquisition boards share a common general layout. Two 16:1 multiplexers are in the centre of the board on the front and back of the PCB. Electrodes connect to the multiplexers by an internal signal plane. This internal plane reduces the amount of crosstalk between the traces. The signal chain connects to the multiplexers. Components were strategically placed and connected with copper planes minimising the loop area. Stitching vias are scattered throughout the PCB which provides return signals to ground near all fast signals. These vias also improve the connectivity of copper ground planes. Firmware

The embedded firmware of the device controls the stimulation and acquisition of measurements. SPI connections program the DAC on the current stimulation board at power up. This setup determines the waveform generated and its frequency components as well as any modulation necessary. Following this setup, the device is ready to get frames of measurements.

To get a frame, the multiplexers on the current and voltage board are set to the first set of electrodes. The electrodes on the voltage board are set so that there is no overlap with the stimulation electrodes. The stimulation board injects current into the test subject when it receives a trigger. During this injection, the voltage board records a buffer of values. Once the buffer is full, the controller demodulates the values to find the magnitude. The system repeats this method for all valid electrode pairs according to the drive pattern. Once enough measurements are recorded, they are sent to a host computer to solve the inverse problem and display a reconstructed image.

Demodulation

The demodulation algorithm used in this device is the Goertzel Algorithm [27]. This algorithm determines the discrete Fourier coefficients of a signal for a specific frequency. The sampling normalised frequency ω determines the coefficient calculated:(4)ω=2πfDACfsamplewhere fDAC is the frequency of the current stimulation and fsample is the sampling frequency of the ADC.

An intermediate sequence is determined using an Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) filter defined:(5)zn=xn+2cosωzn-1-z[n-2]where z[n] is the resulting intermediate sequence, x[n] is the nth sample in the buffer of recorded data, and ω is the frequency computed from Eq. (4).

The final two outputs of this sequence can be used to determine the in phase and out of phase magnitudes at the given frequency as defined:(6)(cosωzN-1-zN-2)+j(sinωzN-1)=I+jQwhere z[N-1] and z[N-2] are the second and third to last values of the intermediate sequence computed in Equation X and ω is the frequency computed in Eq. (4).

These values can then be used to find the overall magnitude of the signal, as defined:(7)magnitude=I2+Q2where the magnitude is the size of the discrete spectrum at fDAC.

This method gives the same result as computing the Fourier transform and selecting the appropriate frequency. However, the Goertzel Algorithm is more efficient as it has a computational efficiency of order N instead of NLog2N as in the Fast Fourier Transform.

Impedance reconstruction

The device operates using time difference EIT. Time difference EIT finds the change in impedance between a reference frame and the current frame. The inverse problem of the standard EIT equation is solved with boundary measurements from the electrode sensing module. The standard EIT equation is a Laplacian Partial Differential Equation (PDE) which relates impedances to the voltages in the domain. The standard equation can be seen in Eq. (8) [[28], [29], [30]].(8)∇σ∇u=0where σ are the impedances in the domain and u are the voltages in the domain.

Two different types of boundary conditions are known during the measurement process. The current injected into the domain creates a Neumann boundary condition according to Eq. (9). The measured voltages are a Dirichlet boundary condition given by Eq. (10). These equations make up the standard form of the Complete Electrode Model (CEM) for current injection and voltage measurement [29].(9)∫∂Ωσ∂u∂ndS=Il(10)u+Zlσ∂u∂n=Vlwhere ∂Ω is the area of an electrode, n is a vector normal to the surface of the electrode, Il is the current injected at an electrode, Zl is the electrode impedance, and Vl is the voltage at an electrode.

The linearized inverse problem is solved by first computing the Jacobian matrix J which relates changes in impedance to changes in electrode voltage. This gives the matrix form of the problem in Eq. (11).(11)Jσ=ΔVlwhere Ji,j=∂Vi∂σi and ΔVl is a vector of the difference between the measured and reference boundary voltages.

To compute the Jacobian matrix the domain is discretized by using a Finite Element Model (FEM) as seen in Fig. 8. This model can be used to estimate the nodal voltages across the entire domain for a given current injection pattern and set of impedances. This can be used to find the change in electrode voltages for a small change in impedances as is necessary for the Jacobian [31].Fig. 8A 2D FEM model of a saline tank with boundary electrodes.

The device sends 208 magnitudes to a host computer every frame. Because there is often significantly more impedances to be computed than boundary measurements the problem is severely Ill-posed [32]. To overcome this some form of regularization is normally applied to include constraints to the inverse problem. The inverse problem is given by Eqs. (12), (13).(12)σ=argminσJσ-ΔV22+λ2R(σ)(13)Rσ=σQ2=σTQσ

The matrix Q is used to apply different forms of prior knowledge to the regularization. Some common examples include the L2, NOSER, Laplace, and Gaussian priors [33].

This work utilized PyEIT, an open-source reconstruction software to solve the forward and inverse problems [34]. This package enables programming in Python making the integration of USB communication and UI design easier, PyEIT has several different algorithms for solving the inverse problem including GREIT. GREIT was chosen for the test cases in this work as it is optimized for several desirable metrics such as position error and the number of artifacts in the reconstruction [16,35].

Build instructions

Four PCBs are manufactured and assembled using the Gerber files in the design repository. JLCPCB (Guangdong, China) produced the PCBs in Fig. 9. The boards can either be pre-assembled or self-assembled. If pre-assembling, the position and bill of material files can be generated from the KICAD projects in the repository. These files can be uploaded to JLCPCB, or similar, before PCB manufacture. If choosing to self-assemble, all components can be purchased through a supplier, such as DigiKey (Minnesota, USA). The boards can be assembled by applying solder paste to all the exposed pads, placing all components on the board, and heating it in a reflow oven. An example of an assembled board can be seen in Fig. 10.Fig. 9Four PCB’s produced by JLCPCB for use in an EIT device.Fig. 10One PCB in an EIT device that has been assembled.

Once all boards have been assembled the PCB stack can be put together as seen in Fig. 11. To create this stack, ribbon cables between the connectors on the controller board and the analogue boards should be joined together. Each board should then be gently pushed into the stacking connector of the board below it. The stack up should have the control board on the top, the power board, the current board, and finally the voltage board.Fig. 11An assembled PCB stack with ribbon cables connected.

After the stack has been assembled, the controller needs to be set to load from FLASH memory. This process can be done using an ST-LINK programmer with the STM32CubeProgrammer (Plan-les-Ouates, Switzerland). Connect the control board with the programmer and change the nSWBOOT0 bit to 0. Following this, the firmware needs to be uploaded to the board using the STM32CubeIDE (Plan-les-Ouates, Switzerland). Firmware can be found in the design files GitHub.

Operation instructions

To run the assembled device the PCB, connect the PCB to a 5 V power supply. Attach all electrodes from the device to the test subject. A USB connection is needed between the device and a host computer. For 16 electrodes, only one stack is needed. To run with up to 32 electrodes a second stack of the two analogue boards connected to the power/SPI header by a ribbon cable is needed. Once the device is setup, power the device and run the Python firmware. The device will send magnitude measurements by USB to the host computer. The measurements can be processed into a reconstructed impedance map using GREIT. The firmware on the host computer and the control board can be altered to achieve additional functionality such as sending alternate signals to single frequency EIT.

Validation and characterization

The acquisition board has two analogue high-pass (HPF) and low-pass (LPF) filters in the signal chain, an HPF at 1 kHz and an LPF at 2 MHz. These filters control the bandwidth of the signals received. Several metrics can be used to validate and characterise an EIT system [36]. These metrics can be split into two main categories. First, is the quality of the magnitude measurements. The second is the quality of the reconstruction.

Measurement quality

SNR: The Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR) is one of the most common metrics used in quantifying EIT devices. This compares the variability of measurements in a constant environment to the average reading. The SNR of a single measurement index is defined in Eq. (14):(14)SNRi=V¯iSD[V¯i]where V¯i is the average measurement on the ith channel for a set of measurements and SDV¯i is the standard deviation for the same channel and measurement set.

Accuracy: The accuracy of a set of measurements is given by the difference between the average reading of a channel and the simulated reading for the same measurement index. This metric is heavily linked to both the device and the similarity between the phantom used and the FEM used to generate the forward simulation results. This accuracy is defined in Eq. (15):(15)ACi=1-V¯i-V¯simi|)where V¯i is the average measurement on the ith channel for a set of measurements and V¯simi is the same average measurements from a FEM forward simulation.

Drift: Drift is a measure of how the results change over a large period in a constant environment. This value can be influenced by many factors, such as saline temperature and changes in electrode properties. Drift is computed for each of the channels based on Eq. (16):(16)Drifti=V¯it=0-V¯it=NV¯it=0

where V¯it=0 is the average measurement on the ith channel at an initial time and V¯it=N is the same average measurement on the same channel later, at time N. This definition shows the change in measurement normalised to the initial readings.

Reconstruction quality

Amplitude Response: This is a measure of the impact a target has on the sum of all reconstructed pixels. This sum is normalised by the targets volume. The amplitude response should be constant for all regions if there is an equal sensitivity. Amplitude response (AR) is defined in Eq. (17):(17)AR=1Vt∑xkwhere Vt is the number of reconstructed data points and xk is the value of every pixel in the reconstructed data points.

Position Error: This shows the difference between the centroid of where a target is expected and where it is in the reconstruction as defined in Eq. (18):(18)PE=rt-re

where rt is the radial position of the target and re is the radial position of the centroid of the reconstructed image.

Ringing: Ringing is an artifact that is often present in the reconstructed image where an area of pixels has a negative value when a target is placed in the phantom. To determine the level of ringing in an image two thresholds are applied, one to identify the positive area of the target and one to identify any negative pixels. The area of these threshold sections is found, and the ratio is the level of ringing, Eq. (19):(19)RNG=AnegAposwhere Aneg is the number of pixels with a value of less than zero and Apos is the number of pixels with a value greater than a quarter of the max reconstructed value.

Detectability: Detectability is the ability of a device to identify a single object. This is done by defining a Region of Interest (ROI) around the target by thresholding the image at a quarter of the maximum value in the reconstruction. This is used to find the signal amplitude xq which is the mean pixel value inside the ROI. This is then compared to the noise amplitude, s, which is the standard deviation of the image. The equation for detectability is defined, Eq. (20):(20)z=xqswhere xq is the mean pixel value inside the ROI and s is the standard deviation between a set of reconstructed images.

Results

To quantify the EIT system against the above metrics two different tests were completed. All tests were conducted using a saline phantom pictured in Fig. 13. The saline phantom used stainless steel point electrodes instead of strips of metal to allow for 3D effects. A steady state 10 kHz, 1 mA injection current was used with a 0.2 S/m saline solution.Fig. 13The saline phantom used to conduct quantification tests on the device.

The saline phantom used to conduct quantification tests on the device.

The first test was conducted on a homogenous saline tank to identify the SNR, Accuracy, and Drift. The device collected 300 frames followed by a five-minute delay repeatedly for an hour. The first group of frames was used to compute the SNR and accuracy for each of the 208 measurement channels. The final group of measurements was used to calculate the total drift for each channel. The results from these tests can be seen in Fig. 14.Fig. 14Homogenous characteristics of the measurement quality.

Homogenous characteristics of the measurement quality.

The second test was conducted by taking 10 frames with a 30 s delay before repeating. The first 10 frames were taken of a homogenous tank as a reference frame. The following groups had an 18 mm diameter cylindrical target, Fig. 15, placed into the tank at different radial positions. Each of these groups was reconstructed using the open source GREIT algorithm. Reconstructions for each case can be seen in Fig. 16. Fig. 16 shows the reconstructed impedance maps with the target at different radial positions. The reconstructions at 30 and −30 mm show a circular target with several artifacts in the rest of the image. There are areas of negative impedance that show the presence of ringing. There is also a ghost of the target near the centre of the tank. These artifacts are likely due to differences in the electrodes modelled in the FEM and the real electrodes. The modelled electrodes are assumed to be flush with the wall of the tank which is not the case in the test subject. All other reconstructions show a single target with minimal artifacts. Fig. 17 shows a similar result when comparing the GREIT figures of merit. The PE, AR, and RNG are much higher, and the DTC value is much lower when the target is near the edge of the tank. This indicates the higher levels of reconstruction artifacts in the map. For the other reconstructions, there are significantly lower PE and RNG values and a higher DTC value indicating accurate reconstructions. The AR values are very consistent for these reconstructions which is desirable.
