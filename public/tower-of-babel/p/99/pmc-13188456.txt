Progressive loss of glycemic control during chronic DPP-4 inhibitor therapy despite preserved β-cell function: a case report suggesting acquired incretin resistance
Authors: Sai Prasad, Aarushi Ahuja, Shreya Sharma, Jay Nagda, Shaily Agrawal, Vidhi Parmar, Harsh Vardhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors are widely prescribed second-line agents for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Secondary treatment failure with DPP-4 inhibitors is conventionally attributed to progressive β-cell dysfunction. Acquired resistance to DPP-4 inhibitor therapy, characterised by progressive glycemic deterioration despite preserved endogenous insulin secretion, remains an under-recognised and poorly documented clinical phenomenon. We report a case of secondary DPP-4 inhibitor failure with preserved β-cell function, raising the hypothesis of acquired incretin resistance as an alternative mechanism of treatment failure.Case presentationA 52-year-old non-obese man (body mass index 24.9 kg/m²) with a 4-year history of T2DM developed progressive glycemic deterioration after 22 months of stable glycemic control with metformin and sitagliptin combination therapy. His glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) increased from 6.7% to 8.4% over an 8-month period, with corresponding elevations in fasting plasma glucose (168 mg/dL) and postprandial glucose (248 mg/dL). Comprehensive evaluation revealed preserved β-cell function with normal fasting insulin (14 µIU/mL), C-peptide (2.1 ng/mL), and homeostatic model assessment of β-cell function (HOMA-β) of 68%. Insulin resistance was mild (HOMA-IR 2.3). Autoimmune markers were negative, hepatic and renal function were normal (AST 28 U/L, ALT 32 U/L, eGFR 92 mL/min/1.73 m²), and secondary causes of hyperglycemia were systematically excluded. Sitagliptin was discontinued and replaced with low-dose basal insulin (insulin glargine 10 units at bedtime) while metformin was continued. At 6-month follow-up, HbA1c decreased to 6.9% without hypoglycemia or weight gain.ConclusionsThis case raises the hypothesis that acquired resistance to DPP-4 inhibitor therapy, potentially mediated by desensitisation of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor signalling pathways, may represent an under-recognised mechanism of secondary treatment failure. Although a single case cannot establish causality, this observation may prompt clinicians to consider DPP-4 inhibitor resistance when encountering unexplained glycemic deterioration in non-obese patients with preserved β-cell function.

Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) represents a progressive metabolic disorder characterised by insulin resistance and declining β-cell function, affecting over 537 million adults worldwide [1]. The pathophysiology of T2DM necessitates a stepwise therapeutic approach, with pharmacological escalation when lifestyle modifications and metformin monotherapy prove insufficient to achieve glycemic targets [2]. Among second-line agents, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors have gained widespread acceptance owing to their favourable safety profile, weight neutrality, and minimal risk of hypoglycemia [3].

DPP-4 inhibitors, including sitagliptin, vildagliptin, saxagliptin, and linagliptin, function by inhibiting the degradation of incretin hormones, particularly glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) [4]. This mechanism enhances glucose-dependent insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells while suppressing inappropriate glucagon release from α-cells, thereby improving glycemic control without increasing hypoglycemia risk [5]. Pivotal clinical trials have demonstrated that DPP-4 inhibitors typically reduce glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) by 0.5–1.0% when added to metformin therapy, with maintained efficacy over 2-year follow-up periods [6, 7].

Despite initial therapeutic success, secondary treatment failure remains a recognised clinical challenge across all antidiabetic drug classes [8]. The United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) demonstrated that progressive β-cell dysfunction necessitates treatment intensification in approximately 50% of patients within 3 years of diagnosis, regardless of initial therapy [9]. While β-cell failure represents the predominant mechanism of secondary treatment failure, emerging evidence suggests additional mechanisms may contribute specifically to incretin-based therapy failure [10].

Preclinical investigations have revealed that chronic exposure to elevated GLP-1 concentrations can induce desensitisation of GLP-1 receptors through multiple mechanisms, including receptor internalisation, downregulation of surface expression, and uncoupling from intracellular G-protein signalling cascades [11, 12]. In rodent models, sustained GLP-1 receptor activation has been associated with reduced insulin secretory responses to subsequent GLP-1 stimulation, suggesting tachyphylaxis [13]. However, the clinical relevance of these findings in patients receiving DPP-4 inhibitors remains poorly defined, as the modest elevation in endogenous GLP-1 levels achieved with DPP-4 inhibition differs substantially from the pharmacological GLP-1 concentrations used in experimental models [14].

Importantly, the clinical response to DPP-4 inhibitors is known to vary with patient phenotype. Patients with obesity tend to derive less glycemic benefit from DPP-4 inhibitors compared to lean individuals, likely reflecting differences in incretin sensitivity and the relative contribution of β-cell dysfunction versus insulin resistance [15, 16]. Thus, patient body habitus represents a clinically relevant variable when interpreting DPP-4 inhibitor efficacy and treatment failure patterns.

The clinical manifestation of acquired resistance to DPP-4 inhibitor therapy would be expected to present as progressive glycemic deterioration despite continued medication adherence, in the absence of identifiable confounding factors such as weight gain, intercurrent illness, psychological stress, or introduction of diabetogenic medications [17]. Recognition of this phenomenon is clinically important, as it may influence therapeutic decision-making regarding whether to intensify incretin-based therapy or switch to a mechanistically distinct drug class. However, published case reports documenting DPP-4 inhibitor resistance remain limited, and diagnostic criteria have not been systematically established [18].

We present a case of a 52-year-old non-obese man with T2DM who experienced progressive loss of glycemic control following 22 months of stable disease management with metformin and sitagliptin combination therapy. Comprehensive evaluation excluded alternative causes of glycemic deterioration and demonstrated preserved endogenous insulin secretory capacity. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old man with a 4-year history of T2DM was evaluated for progressive deterioration of glycemic control over the preceding 8 months. His anthropometric parameters were as follows: height 170 cm, weight 72 kg, body mass index (BMI) 24.9 kg/m², and waist circumference 88 cm (below the Asian-specific cut-off of 90 cm for abdominal obesity) [29]. He was a non-smoker with no history of alcohol consumption. His blood pressure was 128/82 mmHg. Family history was notable for T2DM in his mother, diagnosed at age 58.

At the time of diagnosis, adequate glycemic control was achieved with metformin monotherapy (1000 mg twice daily). Due to subsequent suboptimal glycemic control (HbA1c 7.6%), sitagliptin 100 mg once daily was added to metformin 30 months prior to presentation. The choice of DPP-4 inhibitor over a GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) was guided by the treating physician’s clinical judgment, considering the patient’s preference for oral therapy, the modest degree of hyperglycemia at the time of escalation, and cost considerations in the Indian healthcare setting where GLP-1 RAs remain substantially more expensive and less readily accessible than DPP-4 inhibitors [30]. Following initiation of combination therapy, his HbA1c remained stable between 6.5% and 6.8% for approximately 22 months, with self-monitored blood glucose records demonstrating consistent glycemic control.

Despite continued adherence to metformin and sitagliptin, the patient subsequently developed a gradual and unexplained rise in fasting and postprandial glucose levels, with HbA1c increasing from 6.7% to 8.4% over an 8-month period. He denied missed doses, and medication adherence was corroborated through pharmacy refill records and pill counts. He denied dietary indiscretion, recent weight change, intercurrent illness, psychological stress, or use of medications known to worsen glycemia, including glucocorticoids or sympathomimetics. There was no history of pancreatitis, alcohol misuse, or autoimmune disease. Body weight was stable at 72 kg throughout the period of glycemic deterioration.

Biochemical evaluation demonstrated elevated fasting plasma glucose (168 mg/dL) and 2-hour postprandial glucose levels (248 mg/dL). Endogenous insulin secretion was preserved, with normal fasting insulin (14 µIU/mL) and C-peptide (2.1 ng/mL) levels, and a calculated HOMA-β of 68%. Insulin resistance was mild (HOMA-IR 2.3). Autoimmune markers, including glutamic acid decarboxylase-65 (GAD-65) antibodies, insulinoma-associated antigen-2 (IA-2) antibodies, and insulin autoantibodies, were negative. Thyroid function tests (TSH 2.1 mIU/L), morning cortisol levels (12.4 µg/dL), renal function (serum creatinine 88 µmol/L, eGFR 92 mL/min/1.73 m²), hepatic transaminases (AST 28 U/L, ALT 32 U/L), and lipid profile were within normal or near-normal limits. At presentation, the patient was receiving metformin 1000 mg twice daily and sitagliptin 100 mg once daily. Given the progressive glycemic deterioration despite preserved endogenous insulin secretion, sitagliptin was discontinued while metformin was continued. Rather than switching to another incretin-based agent (such as a GLP-1 RA), low-dose basal insulin therapy was initiated with insulin glargine at 10 units at bedtime and titrated according to fasting plasma glucose levels. The rationale for choosing basal insulin over a GLP-1 RA was to test the hypothesis that incretin-pathway resistance was contributing to treatment failure; selecting another agent acting through the same pathway would not have provided the diagnostic clarity achieved by switching to a mechanistically distinct drug class. The patient was advised to maintain his usual diet and physical activity without modification.

At 6-week follow-up, fasting plasma glucose improved to 118 mg/dL and postprandial glucose to 162 mg/dL without hypoglycemic episodes. At 3 months, HbA1c decreased to 7.1%, with preserved fasting C-peptide levels (2.3 ng/mL). By 6 months, HbA1c stabilised at 6.9% on insulin glargine 12 units at bedtime and metformin 1000 mg twice daily, without further escalation of therapy. Body weight remained stable at 73 kg and no adverse events were reported during follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

This case describes an unusual clinical course of progressive glycemic deterioration in a non-obese patient receiving DPP-4 inhibitor therapy, occurring despite preserved β-cell function and the absence of conventional explanations for secondary treatment failure. While we cannot definitively prove acquired incretin resistance based on a single case observation, the clinical course—characterised by sustained glycemic control for 22 months followed by progressive deterioration over 8 months, and the prompt response to discontinuation of the DPP-4 inhibitor with substitution of a mechanistically distinct agent—raises this possibility and merits consideration.

Secondary treatment failure in T2DM is conventionally attributed to progressive β-cell dysfunction, as documented in landmark studies such as the UKPDS and A Diabetes Outcome Progression Trial (ADOPT) [9, 17]. In these studies, deteriorating glycemic control was invariably associated with declining β-cell function, as evidenced by reduced HOMA-β values and decreased C-peptide levels. Our patient, however, maintained normal β-cell function throughout the period of glycemic deterioration (HOMA-β 68%, fasting C-peptide 2.1 ng/mL). Furthermore, his normal BMI (24.9 kg/m²) and mild insulin resistance (HOMA-IR 2.3) make progressive insulin resistance an unlikely explanation. A comprehensive evaluation excluded alternative causes, including medication non-adherence (corroborated through pharmacy records), dietary factors, weight gain, intercurrent illness, psychological stress, endocrinopathies, hepatic or renal impairment, and introduction of diabetogenic medications.

The role of patient phenotype in DPP-4 inhibitor response

The patient’s non-obese phenotype (BMI 24.9 kg/m²) is noteworthy in interpreting this case. DPP-4 inhibitors are generally considered more effective in lean and Asian patients, in whom β-cell dysfunction predominates over insulin resistance [15, 16]. The fact that this patient initially responded well to sitagliptin (consistent with his phenotype) but subsequently lost glycemic control despite preserved β-cell function strengthens the hypothesis that a change in incretin sensitivity, rather than a change in patient phenotype, underlies the treatment failure.

Mechanistic considerations

The concept of acquired resistance to GLP-1-based therapies is supported by preclinical studies of GLP-1 receptor biology. Chronic GLP-1 receptor activation has been demonstrated to induce receptor desensitisation through multiple cellular mechanisms [19]. Following ligand binding, GLP-1 receptors undergo rapid internalisation via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, with subsequent trafficking to either recycling pathways or lysosomal degradation [20]. Prolonged agonist exposure shifts this equilibrium toward degradative pathways, resulting in a net reduction in surface receptor expression. Additionally, sustained receptor activation promotes phosphorylation of intracellular domains by G-protein receptor kinases, leading to β-arrestin recruitment and uncoupling of receptors from G-protein signalling cascades [21]. These mechanisms collectively attenuate cellular responsiveness to GLP-1.

Experimental evidence supporting GLP-1 receptor desensitisation has predominantly been generated in rodent models. Studies in isolated rat pancreatic islets demonstrated that continuous exposure to supraphysiological GLP-1 concentrations resulted in progressive attenuation of insulin secretory responses over 24–48 h [22]. Similarly, chronic GLP-1 receptor agonist administration in diabetic mice led to diminished glucose-lowering efficacy over time, coincident with reduced pancreatic GLP-1 receptor expression [23]. It is important to acknowledge that the translational relevance of these findings to clinical DPP-4 inhibitor use remains uncertain, as DPP-4 inhibition produces only modest 2–3-fold elevations in endogenous GLP-1 levels, substantially lower than the pharmacological concentrations employed in experimental models or achieved with GLP-1 receptor agonists [24]. Nevertheless, even modest but sustained elevations in GLP-1 over prolonged periods could theoretically accumulate sufficient receptor-level effects to produce clinically meaningful desensitisation in susceptible individuals.

Why was a GLP-1 receptor agonist not used?

A legitimate question raised during peer review is why a GLP-1 RA was not substituted for sitagliptin to directly test whether the incretin pathway remained responsive at pharmacological GLP-1 levels. Several considerations informed this decision. First, GLP-1 RAs achieve plasma GLP-1 concentrations 5–10-fold higher than those produced by DPP-4 inhibition and activate the receptor through a distinct pharmacokinetic profile [24, 31]. A positive glycemic response to a GLP-1 RA would not necessarily exclude DPP-4 inhibitor-specific resistance, as it could simply reflect the ability of supraphysiological agonism to overcome partial receptor desensitisation. Conversely, failure of a GLP-1 RA would suggest more generalised incretin resistance but would not distinguish between DPP-4 inhibitor-induced desensitisation and intrinsic incretin pathway defects.

Second, the clinical priority was to restore glycemic control efficiently in a patient with HbA1c 8.4%, and basal insulin—with its well-established efficacy and predictable dose-response relationship—offered a reliable and mechanistically distinct alternative [28]. Third, practical considerations including cost, availability, and patient preference for non-injectable therapy favoured basal insulin over GLP-1 RAs in this clinical context [30]. We acknowledge that a trial of GLP-1 RA therapy would have provided additional mechanistic insight and discuss this as a limitation.

Limitations of β-cell function assessment

We acknowledge that β-cell function was assessed primarily using fasting indices (fasting insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-β). Ideally, a mixed-meal tolerance test (MMTT) or oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with serial insulin and C-peptide measurements would provide a more comprehensive and dynamic assessment of β-cell secretory reserve and, critically, of the incretin effect itself [32]. Measurement of endogenous GLP-1 and GIP levels (both fasting and stimulated) would also have strengthened the mechanistic argument considerably, as demonstration of elevated GLP-1 levels concurrent with reduced insulinotropic effect would provide direct evidence of incretin resistance [33]. These assessments were not performed due to resource constraints in the clinical setting but are recommended for future case series investigating this phenomenon.

Clinical evidence of DPP-4 inhibitor resistance in humans remains limited. Long-term efficacy studies of DPP-4 inhibitors have generally demonstrated sustained glucose-lowering effects over 2–4-year periods, without evidence of systematic attenuation of therapeutic response [25, 26]. However, these studies relied on population-level analyses and may not have detected individual cases of treatment resistance. Furthermore, patients experiencing treatment failure were typically managed with therapy intensification rather than DPP-4 inhibitor discontinuation, potentially obscuring the recognition of incretin-based therapy resistance. One previous report documented similar findings of progressive glycemic deterioration despite preserved β-cell function in a patient receiving vildagliptin [27].

The temporal pattern observed in our patient—initial efficacy for 22 months followed by progressive failure—provides insight into the potential mechanisms. This timeline is consistent with the gradual development of receptor desensitisation rather than acute pharmacokinetic or compliance-related phenomena. Alternative mechanistic hypotheses include the development of compensatory counter-regulatory responses, altered incretin processing, or genetic polymorphisms affecting GLP-1 receptor function, although these remain speculative in the absence of specific testing.

Clinical implications and educational value

Despite the inherent limitations of a single case report, we believe this observation serves an important educational function for clinicians managing T2DM. First, it reminds clinicians that secondary treatment failure in patients receiving DPP-4 inhibitors should not be automatically attributed to progressive β-cell dysfunction; assessment of endogenous insulin secretion may identify cases where the β-cell is not the culprit. Second, when unexplained glycemic deterioration occurs with preserved β-cell function, switching to a mechanistically distinct drug class (such as basal insulin, SGLT-2 inhibitors, or thiazolidinediones) rather than escalating within the incretin pathway may be more rational. Third, this case highlights the need for prospective studies—ideally case series or cohort studies with serial measurements of GLP-1, GIP, and dynamic β-cell function—to systematically investigate the prevalence and mechanisms of incretin resistance in clinical practice.

Limitations

This case report has several important limitations that must be explicitly acknowledged. First, as a single patient observation, generalisability is limited, and we present this case as hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence of a novel phenomenon. The diagnosis of DPP-4 inhibitor resistance remains inferential rather than proven through mechanistic studies. Second, direct measurement of GLP-1 receptor expression or signalling capacity in pancreatic tissues was not feasible, nor were circulating GLP-1 or GIP levels measured. Third, β-cell function was assessed using fasting indices only; a meal challenge test would have provided more comprehensive evaluation of stimulated insulin secretion and the incretin effect. Fourth, the lack of rechallenge with sitagliptin following insulin therapy prevents confirmation that glycemic deterioration would recur upon DPP-4 inhibitor reintroduction, although such an approach would be ethically questionable given the successful therapeutic response. Fifth, the absence of a trial with a GLP-1 RA limits our ability to distinguish between DPP-4 inhibitor-specific resistance and broader incretin pathway dysfunction. Finally, although alternative explanations have been thoroughly investigated, subclinical changes in diet, physical activity, or other unmeasured confounders cannot be definitively excluded.

In conclusion, this case raises the hypothesis that acquired resistance to DPP-4 inhibitor therapy, manifesting as progressive glycemic deterioration despite preserved β-cell function, may represent an under-recognised mechanism of secondary treatment failure in a subset of patients. While a single case cannot establish a new disease entity, this observation—supported by preclinical evidence of GLP-1 receptor desensitisation—may alert clinicians to consider this possibility when conventional explanations for treatment failure are absent. Prospective studies with dynamic assessments of incretin levels, GLP-1 receptor function, and β-cell secretory capacity are needed to determine the prevalence and clinical significance of this phenomenon.
