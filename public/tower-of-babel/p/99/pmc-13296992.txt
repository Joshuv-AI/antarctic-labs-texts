Virtual and in-person interpretation: a qualitative study from the perspective of professional medical interpreters
Authors: Kathryn Freeman, Marin Melloy, Faiza Aziz, Chloe Botsford, Sherri Fong, Lauren McPherson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeProfessional medical interpreters aim to provide high-quality interpretation for non-English language preference (NELP) patients; however, the impact of various interpreter modalities (virtual versus in-person) is unclear. This project explored the impact of interpreter modality on the in-person care experience through focus groups with medical interpreters to identify strengths, limitations, and clinical situations where in-person or virtual interpretation modalities are most beneficial.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study using focus groups with professional medical interpreters. Sessions were recorded, transcribed, and anonymized. Transcripts were then coded and analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsWe conducted five focus groups with 24 professional medical interpreters and identified four themes: relationship development; understanding and accuracy; accessibility, flexibility, and flow; and care quality. Interpreters found many functions are best served through in-person interpreters. However, there are certain circumstances in which virtual interpreters can be effectively utilized to increase access and allow flexibility.ConclusionsInterpreters primarily promote in-person interpreters for increased understanding, equity, and human connection to best serve patients. They provided salient examples and a deep understanding to inform the utility of different modalities in various clinical situations. There are opportunities for health systems, clinicians, and families to improve access to interpretation and ensure interpreters can use their full skill set to improve patient care and equity.

Introduction

Over 68 million US households speak a non-English language, with 29 million of these having limited English proficiency [1]. Significant health outcome disparities exist for non-English language preference (NELP) patients, or those who prefer a non-English language with respect to their care [2]. NELP patients access fewer preventative services [3], experience poorer chronic disease management [4, 5], have higher hospital readmission rates [6], and encounter more safety events [7–9] than patients with English language preference.

As the USA grows increasingly multilingual, access to professional medical interpretation is necessary to ensure quality medical care for all patients. Language assistance is required by federal law [10]. We know that, across multiple settings, bilingual clinicians or use of trained medical interpreters improve patient outcomes [11, 12], increase patient safety, and prevent adverse events [13–16].

Access to medical services through virtual care, via telephone and video, including medical interpretation, expanded in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic [17, 18]. These changes improved patient access to medical interpreters [19–22], but there is mixed evidence regarding how interpreter modality (in-person vs virtual) impacts patient experience [15] and care quality [23]. Some studies show high levels of patient satisfaction with virtual interpreters, including in the clinic, hospital, and emergency room [18, 24–27]. Others suggest that patients and clinicians prefer in-person interpreters [28–32].

Interpreter modality affects the flow and efficiency of healthcare visits. Access to virtual interpreters increases frequency of interpreter use, decreases patient and clinician wait time [20, 33, 34], and increases on-time case rates in surgical settings [35]. In contrast, outpatient studies show that in-person interpreters reduce visit times, and decrease no-show and late arrival rates [30]. In the emergency room, patient length of stay was longer and mortality rates higher with virtual interpreters [36].

There is limited qualitative research comparing healthcare visit experience with in-person versus virtual interpreters, including from interpreters themselves [37, 38]. Patients indicate improved relationship development and trust building with in-person interpreters [29, 32, 39, 40], and suggest a loss of body language and cultural cues with virtual interpreters [32, 41]. In-person interpreters may be particularly valuable in situations with high medical complexity [15, 40], multiple educational components [40, 42], complex psychosocial aspects [15, 29], or hearing impairment [41]. In-person interpreters foster a safe environment for diverse NELP patients, improving patient compliance and clinical outcomes and reducing disparities [11, 42, 43].

Our study aims to contextualize in-person and virtual interpretation modalities from the viewpoint of professional medical interpreters to understand variations in experience and identify clinical situations where each modality is most beneficial.

Materials and methods

This study was deemed Not Human Research by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board.

Participants

For this qualitative study, we conducted five focus groups with 24 professional medical interpreters. Participants were recruited via electronic recruitment flyer. We utilized professional interpreter organizations, interpreter training programs, employment agencies, and health systems, as well as personal networks and snowball sampling for recruitment via emailed and posted flyers. Interested participants were directed via QR code to an electronic screening tool.

Eligible participants included professional medical interpreters in Minnesota with greater than three years' experience, employed more than 20 h per week, and with experience interpreting using both in-person and virtual modalities. Recruitment was broad to capture participants from a variety of languages, cultural backgrounds, geographic locations, and clinical settings.

Seventy-five screening surveys were opened; 39 were deemed eligible and contacted. 24 participated in focus groups, with the majority of non-participants limited by time and scheduling constraints.

Data collection

Five virtual focus groups with three to six participants each were held between March and June 2023 and conducted using a semi-structured moderator guide. The guide (Supplement A) was not pilot tested but developed from clinical experiences, literature review, conversations with interpreters, and reviewed by experienced qualitative researchers. Moderator guide development [44] incorporated principles of trauma-informed research [45] and cultural humility [46], with aim to balance power dynamics [47] and support peer-to-peer conversation. Questions focused on participants' views of the interpreter role, benefits and challenges of working in-person and virtually, and experience during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Focus groups were 90–120 min and hosted virtually via Zoom [48], utilizing audio, video and chat components, with consent confirmed verbally and via chat. Sessions were moderated by two authors (C.B., K.F.) and assisted by three authors (F.A., L.M., M.M.). Moderators included active MDs (K.F., L.M.), MPHs (C.B., L.M.), medical students (F.A., M.M.), and former interpreters (F.A., M.M.), all female. Participants were aware of the researchers' professions, background, and frequent work with NELP patients and interpreters. Some participants and moderators had on occasion worked together, but none were actively employed within the same organization. Participants completed a demographic survey and received a $50 gift card for their participation.

Analysis

Sessions were recorded and transcribed via Zoom and transcripts were reconciled with audio and video files. All moderators kept field notes, which were consolidated and discussed post-session. Transcripts were coded for thematic analysis using an inductive approach [48]. The initial coding structure was developed during interim analyses after completion of the first three focus groups. Once codes were developed, a minimum of three separate authors coded each transcript using NVivo 14 [49]. Six total coders were utilized, including all five moderators. An iterative process of paired debriefing followed by large group discussion was used to refine codes from each transcript and themes were identified. Summaries were developed for each thematic category. Each coding summary was written by one author and reviewed by a second author to ensure encapsulation of key concepts. Focus groups were conducted until no unique themes were identified.

Results

Professional medical interpreters (n = 24) participated in five focus groups. Twelve languages were represented, with Spanish as the majority language. Participants interpreted in a variety of clinical settings, employment models, and community sizes. All interpreters worked using both in-person and virtual modalities, with two-thirds working 50% or more in the in-person setting. Key themes emerged centered on relationship building, accuracy, accessibility, quality and equity. See Table 1 for demographics and Table 2 for illustrative quotes.

Theme 4: high quality and equitable care

As interpreters reflect on the difference between interpreting in person versus virtually, many commented on the unpredictability of clinical interactions; the challenge of anticipating visit complexity and individual patient needs.In an ideal world we will have plenty of interpreters for all…because I can’t tell you instances where I looked at my schedule and I was like “this should be simple,” …And I arrive and I’m so glad I was there in person, because it was such a complicated environment that you cannot foresee by just looking at the notes on the appointment or the specialty. It's a really hard call to make…because you’re not always gonna [sic] be right.—Participant 59, Session 5Participants view interpreter access in all languages and clinical situations as essential to high quality, equitable care. Participants highlighted the key role of just-in-time virtual interpretation yet saw limitations. They had concerns about qualifications and training with interpreter services outsourced from other states and countries. Virtual interpretation, with more limited input, requires increased concentration and focus; their ability to serve patients well decreases. Many described virtual interpretation as “better than nothing,” as they observe lower quality conversations and interactions.

Factors influencing interpreter modality

Participants highlighted multiple situations best suited for each modality, emphasizing that interpreter, clinician, and patient modality should match whenever possible. Virtual interpreters are generally appropriate for patients with chronic conditions who need follow-up or brief consultations, to review results or refill medications. Straight-forward visits (labs, imaging, immunizations) are easily interpreted virtually. For hearing impaired patients, in sensitive encounters, or for those processing past trauma, virtual visits can increase accessibility (Fig. 1).

In-person interpreters can be prioritized for more complex visits; those with new diagnoses, lengthy written materials, frequent and complex physical exam maneuvers, and high risk of harm. In-person interpreters are important with multiple participants, including large healthcare teams and multilingual families requiring simultaneous interpretation. In-person interpreters increase safety and promote patient input in unpredictable situations, such as the emergency room, labor and delivery, intensive care unit, and mental health crises (Fig. 2).

Conclusions

Leveraging focus groups with professional medical interpreters, we found that interpreter modality impacts multiple aspects of the clinical experience, including relationship development, patient and clinician understanding and accuracy, interpreter access, flow and flexibility, and quality of care. Virtual interpretation can play a key role for routine, well-established medical needs and emergent services. In-person interpretation should be prioritized for high risk, complex visits—new patients, new diagnoses, undifferentiated complaints—as well as large groups and emotional situations.

Interpreters identify in-person as the gold-standard. Building on previous qualitative studies, which identified in-person benefits including enhanced relationship development [29, 32, 39], increased utilization of visual cues [32, 41], and improved encounter flow [31, 36], our focus group participants outlined additional benefits. In-person interpreters better integrate cultural cues, instruct testing and physical exam maneuvers, and support written questionnaires and paperwork, enhancing the experience and comprehension for all.

In-person interpreters identify opportunities to support comprehension, advocate for patient needs, and protect against bias and racism [32]. Interpreters see increased care quality with in-person interpreters, when interpreters can clarify, debrief, and educate care team members. This improves trust in the healthcare system and decreases unnecessary healthcare visits due to misunderstandings. Interpreters see this as a critical equity issue; access to an in-person interpreter levels the field for NELP patients.

Interpreters find it challenging to use their full skill set in the virtual setting, and yet they see the value of virtual interpreters. Previous studies emphasize that professional interpreters, regardless of modality, are key to quality NELP patient care [12, 32, 37, 40], and technology increases professional interpreter access and utilization [20]. This is particularly critical in acute situations, as a bridge to in-person care, and with situations of geographic or linguistic isolation. Interpreters highlight the need to improve clinician, staff, and interpreter training and virtual interpretation best practices.

We see opportunities for enhanced integration of interpreters within the healthcare team as an important theme and future direction. Interpreter input has value in improving care delivery, including care planning, coordination across specialties, development of clinical protocols and patient education. Previous work suggests opportunities to embed interpreters into care teams [42, 43], potentially expanding their role to support patient navigation and education similar to community health workers. This would require discussion of interpreter scope with significant input from interpreters themselves, with potential to improve care.

Our focus groups highlight the impressive skill set that interpreters bring to the medical encounter; as well as how their toolbox expands when present in person. Even as technology and systems improve, in-person interpreters enhance visit quality.

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of few to focus on the interpreter perspective and included a diverse group of interpreters in regards to language, location, practice, and employer, providing a wide range of viewpoints. Interpreters routinely observe patients and clinicians and are uniquely qualified to speak to the impact of each modality.

However, both number of participants and focus groups were small—one included only three participants—and favored those working in the urban Twin Cities, with better access to in-person interpreters. The majority of study participants worked primarily in person or via hybrid models. Similarly, our research team members all practice within large academic health centers with a physician-heavy perspective, with minimal community, private clinic, or rural experience, limiting generalizability of results. Multiple participants and physicians worked together previously, impacting the power differential and increasing risk for bias.

Future directions

Better understanding of interpreter roles and best practices, especially virtually, is needed to ensure appropriate, effective, and equitable interpreter utilization by the healthcare team. This includes education on requesting interpreters, communication flow, and scope of practice. Many interpreters feel excluded from aspects of the healthcare environment and worry these attitudes are felt by and impact patients.

NELP patient input on the impact of interpreter modalities on healthcare experience, level of understanding, trust in clinicians, willingness to seek care, and views on privacy is critical. Clinician input would also provide insight, including regarding visit flow, information sharing, and impacts on clinician wellbeing and comfort serving diverse patients.
