The Inflammatory Response in the Murine Ovalbumin‐Induced Skin Inflammation Model Is Robust Despite Major Gut Microbiota Differences
Authors: Ida Wang Henriksen, Christina Bartholdy, Janne Koch, Denitsa Vladimirova Stefanova, Camilla Hartmann Friis Hansen, Axel Kornerup Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTValid pre‐clinical animal models are essential for effective drug development in inflammatory skin diseases like atopic dermatitis. Most murine models exhibit low‐to‐moderate inflammatory responses with significant variability, undermining their validity and contributing to high drug attrition rates. Previous studies showed that microbiota transfer and early‐life microbial exposure affect inflammatory phenotypes and allergy development. This concept might extend to other pre‐clinical inflammatory models. We tested the impact of different microbiotas on ovalbumin (OVA)‐induced skin inflammation in mice, by using BALB/c substrains from different vendors. Additionally, germ‐free mice were co‐housed for 2 weeks with specific‐pathogen‐free (SPF) mice at 6 weeks of age, resulting in delayed gut colonization. Thereafter, mice were sensitized with OVA in aluminum hydroxide and challenged with OVA on the ear. Skin inflammation was assessed through ear cytokine and serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels. Different T cell populations were analyzed in the spleen and ear‐draining lymph node with flow cytometry. Microbiota composition was analyzed via 16S rRNA sequencing. Despite substantial differences in microbiota composition as well as T cell proportion across vendors, similar inflammatory responses to OVA were observed. An early sterile environment resulted in elevated serum IgE and fewer T cells in ear‐draining lymph nodes compared to SPF mice but did not affect ear tissue cytokines. These studies demonstrate that the OVA‐induced skin inflammation model is robust to microbiota differences, enhancing reproducibility while highlighting persistent issues of variability and low effect size.

Introduction

Skin diseases, such as atopic dermatitis, are a global challenge [1] with a significant unmet need for effective and safe treatments [2]. To optimize target validation and pre‐clinical efficacy assessment of potential new drug candidates, valid and robust murine models are indispensable.

The “Ova‐Alum model,” previously developed and implemented at Leo Pharma A/S, inspired by Kasaian et al. [3], serves as an example of a pre‐clinical mouse model commonly used in the pharmaceutical industry. It is based on immunization with chicken egg‐derived ovalbumin in 2% aluminum hydroxide gel adjuvant. A subsequent intradermal challenge with the same antigen in the ear provokes a delayed type (I) hypersensitivity reaction resulting in increased levels of inflammatory proteins such as IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, eotaxin, TARC, IL‐1β, TNF‐α, pSTAT‐6 and RANTES in ear tissue 24 h after challenge as well as increased serum levels of Immunoglobulin E (IgE) [4]. Yet, this model expresses low‐to‐moderate skin inflammatory responses and varies substantially in inter‐individual as well as inter‐experimental magnitudes. Additionally, a more Th2‐biased response is desired to better reflect the human atopic dermatitis phenotype [5].

These translational and reproducibility issues are not limited to the Ova‐Alum model but are pervasive across many pre‐clinical models used in the pharmaceutical industry [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. A major contribution to this may be the way that the most commonly used animal in preclinical research, specific pathogen‐free (SPF) mice [12], have been increasingly sanitized over time. Several studies have highlighted that mice housed under SPF conditions very poorly mimic the immune responses observed in humans due to a lack of microbial stimulation, especially in their early life period [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. This results in an underdeveloped immune system that does not fully mimic the complex interactions between the human immune system and its diverse microbiota [19]. Consequently, immune responses and the effect of drug candidate treatment in SPF mice can be completely different to the clinical settings, leading to a lack of efficacy or even toxicological outcomes in the clinical setting [20, 21]. These obstacles in pharmacological development result in substantial financial costs, waste of animal lives, and a poor bench‐to‐bedside rate, compromising treatment options for severely affected patients [22].

A major factor contributing to the variation in phenotypic expression of rodent models is inter‐individual differences in gut microbiota caused by the environment of origin, including different vendors, rooms or even cages, despite being of the same inbred strain [23]. Rasmussen et al. [24] showed significant distinct gut bacterial and viral profiles between C57BL/6N mice originating from either Taconic, Charles River or Janvier, which persisted even after being fed the same diet (high‐fat vs. low‐fat) for 13 weeks. Additionally, the effect of diabetes treatment in C57BL/6N mice has been shown to depend on breeder in a microbiota‐dependent manner [25]. Oppositely, Siersbæk et al. [26] reported that diet (high‐fat vs. western) explained more variation in microbiota than vendor in C57BL/6J sub‐strains. The phenotypic profile of an airway inflammation model in C57BL/6N mice has been linked to microbiota differences in mice from different vendors but only for one breeding generation [27]. On the other hand, Wolff et al. [28] found no differences on host responses to a pulmonary LPS challenge, despite comprehensive vendor differences on the gut microbiota.

It has been demonstrated that the first intestinal colonizers of mice pups are crucial for the development of oxazolone‐induced skin inflammation later in life, highlighting a specific “window‐of‐opportunity” for early life microbial shaping of the immune system and that the bacterial family Rikenellaceae was positively correlated to an allergy‐prone phenotype [29]. Al Nabhani et al. [14] demonstrated a vigorous immune response—a “weaning reaction”—induced by the microbiota shift at 3 weeks of age. Inhibition of that weaning reaction led to pathological imprinting and increased susceptibility to colitis, allergic inflammation, and cancer later in life due to reduced generation of RORγt+ regulatory T cells, which required bacterial and dietary metabolites—short‐chain fatty acids and retinoic acid [14]. Previous studies have utilized this early‐life “window‐of‐opportunity” to transfer a high‐ or low‐responding inflammatory phenotype in an oxazolone‐induced skin inflammation model to germ‐free mice through fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), thereby increasing the effect size and potential for pharmacological drug evaluation [30, 31].

The Ova‐Alum model expresses high inter‐individual variation in inflammatory responses resulting in large group sizes which is expensive, impractical, and problematic from a reduction point of view. Falkenberg et al. [4] managed to increase the general inflammatory response in the Ova‐Alum model and enhance systemic memory T cell populations through pre‐immunization with inactivated murine pathogens. This improved the pharmacological window, but the high inter‐individual variation persisted. The impact of gut microbiota or vendor has never been investigated for the Ova‐Alum model. Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether the Ova‐Alum model is influenced by varying microbiota compositions at different life stages, with the ultimate goal of enabling gut microbiota manipulation—such as that demonstrated by Zachariassen et al. [30]—to enhance effect size, minimize variability, and improve the potential for pharmacological drug evaluation.

We hypothesized that the ovalbumin‐induced inflammatory response, measured by ear tissue biomarkers, would differ between mice from different vendors due to different microbiota compositions, and that it would be increased in mice with 6‐weeks delayed gut colonization. This was tested with two different approaches: whole‐life microbiota differences by using three different vendors (Study 1) and more extreme differences specified to early postnatal life by delaying gut colonization to 6‐weeks‐of‐age (Study 2). The primary read‐out in this model was ear tissue levels of IL‐5 and IL‐4 in both studies.

Methods

This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines [32]. The animal experiment was approved by the Animal Experiments Inspectorate, Ministry of Food, Fisheries and Agriculture, Denmark (no. 2022‐15‐0201‐01306). All aspects of housing, maintenance, and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the framework of the EU directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and The Danish Animal Experimentation Act (LBK 474 from 15/05/2014).

Experimental Design

Study 1

48 BALB/c mice (7 weeks old at arrival) originating from three different vendors: Taconic (8 male and 8 female BALB/cJBomTac; Taconic Biosciences, Germantown, NY, USA), Janvier (8 male and 8 female BALB/cAnNRj; Janvier Labs, Le Genest‐Saint‐Isle, France) and Charles River (8 male and 8 female BALB/cAnNcrl; Charles River, Sulzfeld, Germany) were, within each vendor group, block‐randomized according to sex, in two treatment groups: The “OvaAlum” group (n = 12/vendor) were induced with skin inflammation and the “Vehicle” group (n = 4/vendor) were treated similarly with saline after a week of acclimatization (Figure 1A,B).

The sample size was based on a power analysis for a general full factorial design (Minitab, Coventry, UK) with 90% power and 5% significance based on effect size and standard deviations from a previous study with the same model [4].

The mice were co‐housed, according to vendor and sex, in open‐type 1284 L Eurostandard II L cages (Techniplast; Varese, Italy) containing three OvaAlum mice and one Vehicle mouse per cage as well as aspen bedding and chewing block, disposable shelters, mini fun tunnels, Nestlet nesting material, EnviroDri (Brogaarden, Lynge, Denmark). The mice had ad libitum access to Altromin 1324 (Altromin, Sage, Germany) and fresh water and were housed in our AAALAC accredited barrier protected facility with a mean temperature of 22.0°C, a mean relative humidity of 49.7% and a light/dark cycle of 12 h/12 h (University of Copenhagen, Copenhagen, Denmark).

Study 2

6 BALB/cJBomTac (designated “SPF”) female mice (Taconic Biosciences, Lille Skendsved, Denmark), reared under specific pathogen‐free conditions, and 6 BALB/c germ‐free (designated “ISO”) female mice, reared in our AAALAC accredited germ‐free facility (University of Copenhagen, Frederiksberg, Denmark), were at the age of 6 weeks, randomly allocated and co‐housed in our AAALAC accredited barrier protected facility with similar conditions as described for Study 1 but with a mean temperature of 22.7°C, a mean relative humidity of 40.3%, and including “dirty” bedding and nesting material from the SPF mice. After 2 weeks of co‐housing, they were all induced with skin inflammation (Figure 5A,B). The sample sizes were limited, as there were only a few germ‐free mice available for this study. Consequently, the conclusions should be interpreted as indications rather than definitive outcomes.

Induction of Skin Inflammation

Skin inflammation was induced with an intra‐peritoneal sensitization with 200 μL 0.05 mg/mL ovalbumin (Albumin A5503; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louise, Missouri, USA) in 1% aluminum hydroxide (Alhydrogel Adjuvant 2%; InvivoGen; Toulouse, France) and 10 days later an intra‐dermal challenge on the right ear, dorsal side, with 10 μL 1 mg ovalbumin/mL NaCl (OVA Endofit; InvivoGen) under transient anesthesia with Isoflurane/O2 (Baxter, Søborg, Denmark). Vehicle mice were “sensitized” with saline and aluminum hydroxide and “challenged” with saline (Figure 1B).

All mice were offered Dietgel Recovery (ClearH2O, Westbrook, ME, USA) 3 days before and 3 days after sensitization to help recovery. Additionally, all mice received treats (meal worms, sweetcorn kernels, and sunflower seeds [Brogaarden]) after sensitization and challenge to distract from the negative experience. Besides visual observation, body weight was registered at Day 0, 1, 2 post sensitization and at termination. Weight losses ≥ 15% were a predefined humane endpoint. None of the mice fell within this criterion.

Sample Collection

24 h after challenge, the mice were anesthetized with a 1:1 Midazolam (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) and Hypnorm (Skanderborg Apotek, Skanderborg, Denmark) mixture and as much blood as possible was collected by puncturing the retro‐orbital sinus and transferred to Eppendorf tubes, stored at room temperature for approx. 1 h to allow coagulation and thereafter at 4°C until centrifugation (5000 × g, 10 min, 5°C). The supernatant (serum) was transferred to 1.4 mL non‐coated u‐bottom bulk Micronics tubes (Micronic, Lelystad, Netherland) and stored at −80°C until analysis. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation.

An 8 mm biopsy was collected from the centre of the right ear, weighed, and cut in four pieces. Two of the pieces (1/2 biopsy) were transferred to 0.5 mL Precellys standard tubes, soft tissue homogenizing CK14 with 1.4 mm ceramic (zirconium oxide) beads (Precellys Lysing Kit, Cat. No. KT03961‐1‐203, Bertin Technologies SAS, Montigny‐le‐Bretonneux, France) and snap‐frozen in dry ice and stored at −80°C until analysis.

The spleen and ear‐draining lymph node (ear‐LN) were sampled and placed in ice‐cold PBS and kept on wet ice until analysis by flow cytometry the same day.

Fecal samples were collected by voluntary defecation prior to sensitization and termination as well as before co‐housing for Study 2, in sterile 1.5 mL Biosphere SafeSeal tubes, snap frozen and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Inflammatory Protein Analysis

Tissue from a half ear biopsy was lysed in ceramic bead containing tubes from the Precellys kit #03961–1‐203 (Bertin Technologies SAS) with 150 μL lysis buffer prepared by mixing 9 mL milliQ water with 1 mL of 10× Cell Signaling Lysis Buffer (9803, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), 1 protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (11873580001, Roche Diagnostics GmbH), 100 μL halt phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (8159680747, Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 100 μL 100 mM vanadate (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Precellys tubes with ear tissue, beads and lysis buffer were inserted to a Precellys 24 tissue homogenizer (Bertin Technologies SAS) for 4 × 30 s at 6800 rpm. Thereafter, incubated on ice for 20 min and centrifuged at 15,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatant was transferred to 1.4 mL non‐coated u‐bottom bulk Micronics tubes (Micronic). Protein determination was performed with the Pierce Detergent Compatible Bradford Assay Kit (YB37471, Thermo Fischer Scientific). The following cytokines were measured in undiluted ear lysate with the MSD U‐Plex platform: IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐10, IL‐13, IL‐17A, IL‐12p70, IL‐31, IL‐1β, eotaxin, TARC, RANTES, MMP‐9, KC/GRO, and TNF‐α on a MESO QuickPlex SQ 120MM instrument with corresponding instructions (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD, USA).

Serum IgE Analysis

Serum IgE was assessed using ELISA MAX Deluxe Set (432404; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) by following the manufacturer's protocol. Serum was diluted 1:1000 and tested in duplicates. Optical density (OD) was measured on an Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer at 450 and 570 nm, and a standard curve was calculated using duplicates of standards with the following concentrations: 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625, 0.313, 0.156, and 0 ng/mL respectively. By using the standard curve, the samples' concentrations were calculated with Gen5.

Immune Cell Analysis

Cells were isolated from the spleen and ear‐LN by squeezing and subsequently filtering through a 70 μm nylon Corning cell strainer (431751; Corning, NY, USA). Filtered cell suspensions were centrifuged (800 × g, 5 min, 4°C) and re‐suspended with PBS. Leukocytes (size equivalent to 4–12 μm) were counted (TC20 Automated Cell Counter, BIO‐RAD Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and suspensions containing approx. 1,000,000 leukocytes were transferred to a sterile 96 well plate for extracellular staining for 20 min in darkness with selected antibodies (Figure S1A). Flow cytometry was performed on a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Kongens Lyngby, Denmark). Gates were applied based on single staining and fluorescence minus one staining. Gating strategy is shown in Figure S1B. Samples with < 5000 cells in the viable cell count were excluded from statistical analysis.

Gut Microbiome Analysis

DNA Purification and 16S rRNA Amplification

Fecal DNA was purified with DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (47016; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) by following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA quality and concentration were assessed with Varioskan Flash Multimode Reader using an Invitrogen Qubit 1× dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and samples were diluted to 5 ng/μL with sterile MilliQ water. DNA amplification was performed on a SureCycler 8800 Thermal Cycler attaching the following unique molecular identifiers (UMI) primers to the DNA: UMI338abF/UMI1391R and UMI27abF/UMI1540R. The PCR mixture containing 12 μL 2 × PCRBIO Ultra mix, 6 μL MilliQ water, 2 μL primer mix (5 μM each), and 5 μL of extracted DNA (5 ng/μL), giving a total volume of 25 μL, was treated as follows: 95°C for 5 min; 2 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 48°C for 30 s, 65°C for 10 s; 72°C for 45 s; and 72°C for 4 min. The PCR product was cleaned with magnetic AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). A second round of DNA amplification attaching unique UMI sample barcodes was performed on a SureCycler 8800 Thermal Cycler (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), where a mixture containing 11 μL of cleaned PCR product, 12 μL 2 × PCRBIO Ultra mix, and 2 μL of unique barcodes was treated as follows: 95°C for 2 min; 33 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 55°C for 20 s, 72°C for 40 s; and 72°C for 4 min. DNA was validated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel for 45 min at 120 V. Finally, 5 μL of all barcoded amplicons were pooled in one Eppendorf tube followed by beads clean‐up (AMPure XP beads; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and library preparation according to the ligation sequencing kit and protocol SQK‐LSK114.

16S rRNA Amplicon Sequencing and Bioinformatics

The library was sequenced on a GridIONX5 platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) using an R10.4.1 flow cell. Sequencing data was collected using MinKnow (v.23.07.12) and basecalled with Guppy (v.3.3.3) in high‐accuracy mode. An abundance table was generated from raw FASTQ files using the Long Amplicon Consensus Analysis pipeline (LACA, GitHub: https://github.com/yanhui09/laca). Quality‐corrected reads were assigned taxonomy using the SILVA database (v.138.1) [33].

The subsequent analysis was conducted using R, in which only bacterial domain sequences were selected for the downstream analysis as well as singletons were removed. The relative abundance of different taxa was determined through dividing the number of sequencing reads assigned to different taxa in each sample by the total number of sequencing reads and then OTUs below 0.01% relative abundance of total reads were removed from the phyloseq object used for further downstream analysis. Data from Study 1 was rarefied using phyloseq [34] (sampling without replacement) for the same reading depth of 10,000 reads per sample, removing 4 sample and 112 OTUs (Figure S2A), whereas data from Study 2 was rarefied for the same reading depth of 19,000 reads per sample, removing no samples and 505 OTUs (Figure S2B).

After the preprocessing, further downstream analysis was performed inspired by a customized workflow scripted by Panah [35]. Alpha diversity was estimated based on Shannon diversity metrics from rarefied data after singleton removal. Beta diversity was estimated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity coefficients obtained through the distance function in the phyloseq package.

Microbial data at phylum, family, genus and species taxonomic levels were normalized and differentially abundant taxa analyzed in R using the DESeq2 [36] package. OTUs classified as “uncultured,” “uncategorized,” “NA,” or “unassigned” at the family level were removed prior to genus agglomeration. Additionally, to ensure unique identification at the species level, uncultured genera were uniquely labeled, and any remaining duplicates at the species level were further resolved by incorporating higher taxonomic ranks. Before estimating the variability (dispersions) in the data, we first calculated the geometric means of the counts for each sample. These geometric means were then used to normalize the data and to estimate the effect sizes of different factors in the analysis. We adjusted the p‐values using the Benjamini‐Hochberg (BH) method to control for the False Discovery Rate (FDR). Only the results with adjusted p‐values below 0.05 were included in the visualizations. These visualizations displayed the differences between groups using Log2FoldChange.

Statistics

Data analysis and visualization were conducted using the free statistical software R. All datasets were initially tested for homogeneity and normal distribution using residual and QQ‐plots respectively. If datasets were considered non‐normal, they were ranked prior to statistical inference.

For statistical testing in Study 1 a multiple linear regression ANOVA was performed on the different outcomes with vendor, treatment and sex as variables with Tukey's post hoc tests. In Study 2 a Welch's unpaired t‐test was performed comparing the ISO group with the SPF group.

Alpha diversity between groups was compared by Welch's unpaired t‐test. Beta diversity indices, such as the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix, were analyzed using Distance‐based Redundancy Analysis (dbRDA) in R. This analysis was performed with the dbrda function from the vegan package [37], using 999 permutations, with “time” as a blocking variable and group, treatment and sex as explanatory variables. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix was generated on log‐transformed rarefied OTU counts and before using it for the dbRDA model, it was examined for the homogeneity of variance around the centroids between the three vendors, concluding that some of the variation in our observations derive from this independent variable. The variance dispersion test was done using the betadisper function in the vegan package [37] with time being set as a constraining block for the permutation (999 total permutations). Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the rcorr function from the Hmisc [38] package to calculate correlations between various biomarkers and microbial taxa at genus, family, and species levels. Heatmaps of the top 50 taxa were generated with the pheatmap package [39] to visualize significant correlations (p > 0.05, FDR corrected), with hierarchical clustering.

To investigate whether a high‐, medium‐, or low‐responding inflammatory phenotype had different microbiota clusters, mice were divided into responder groups based on IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, and TARC levels in ear tissue, and clustering was assessed with dbRDA, with responder group as explanatory variable and vendor as blocking variable.

Results

BALB/c mice from different vendors showed significant differences in gut microbiota composition, while their skin inflammatory responses remained similar. We purchased BALB/c mice from either Taconic, Charles River, or Janvier to investigate the impact of potential gut microbiota differences on ovalbumin‐induced skin inflammation (Figure 1A,B). Before model induction, the alpha‐diversity was similar between the mice; yet after model induction, just 11 days later, alpha‐diversity was slightly increased for Charles River and Janvier mice, resulting in significantly higher diversity compared to Taconic mice (Figure 1C). Looking at diversity in microbiota composition between mice (beta‐diversity), vendor contributed with far the most variation as indicated by the highest inertia and F‐value compared to timepoint, treatment, and sex (Figure 1D).

Ovalbumin‐induced skin‐inflammation, 10 days after sensitization, increased ear biopsy weight (Figure 1E), OVA‐specific IgE and serum IgE (Figure 1F,G), total ear biopsy protein content (Figure 1H) as well as ear tissue biomarkers IL‐1b, IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, Rantes, IL‐10, TARC, IL‐17A, TNF‐α, CXCL‐1, MMP‐9, and eotaxin (Figure 1I–T) but not IL‐31 (Figure 1U). Of all these biomarkers, only serum IgE was significantly different among vendors driven by higher levels in Charles River female mice (Figure S3). However, post hoc tests revealed the most significant treatment effect in Charles River mice compared to Janvier and Taconic mice for OVA‐IgE (Figure 1F), IgE (Figure 1G), total ear biopsy protein concentration (Figure 1H), IL‐1β (Figure 1I), IL‐4 (Figure 1J), IL‐5 (Figure 1K), IL‐13 (Figure 1L), RANTES (Figure 1M), IL‐10 (Figure 1N), TARC (Figure 1O), IL‐17A (Figure 1P), TNF‐α (Figure 1Q), CXCL‐1 (Figure 1R), and MMP‐9 (Figure 1S).

Vendor Choice Affected the Proportion of T Cells in the Spleen

ANOVA testing showed that vendor choice affected the proportion of cells in the spleen: T cells, T helper cells, and cytotoxic T cells after OVA‐Alum treatment (Figure 2A–C). Post hoc testing revealed that this was driven by significantly higher proportions in Taconic mice for T cells and T helper cells, while no significant difference was evident by post hoc testing for cytotoxic T cells. Oppositely, Charles River mice possessed higher proportions of effector memory cytotoxic T cell subsets (Figure 2D), yet not statistically detectable with post hoc tests. Ova‐Alum treatment decreased the proportion of T cells, T helper cells and cytotoxic T cells (Figure 2A–C) but increased the proportion of central memory cytotoxic T cells (Figure 2E) primarily driven by significant changes in Charles River mice, whereas such effects were not statistically detectable in the mice from the other vendors. Sex affected some T‐cell populations, with female mice having higher proportions of T cells, T helper cells, cytotoxic T cells, and naïve helper T cells (Figure 2A–CF), while male mice had higher proportions of effector memory cytotoxic T cells and central memory helper T cells (Figure 2D,G).

Microbiota Differences Did Not Correlate With Inflammatory Biomarkers

Mice from different vendors differed significantly in gut microbiota profile (Figure 4A). Especially Taconic mice differed from the other two vendors with significantly higher abundance of Akkermansiaceae, Eggerthellaceae, and Enterobacteriaceae and significantly lower abundance of Bacteroidaceae and Prevotellaceae (Figure 4B–F). Janvier mice had the highest abundance of Marifilaceae (Figure 4G), whereas Charles River mice exhibited relatively low abundances of Rikenellaceae compared to Janvier and Taconic mice (Figure 4H).

To further investigate a potential impact of microbiota on the model outcome, we allocated mice to either a high‐, medium‐, or low‐responding group based on IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, and TARC concentrations in inflamed ear tissue. A distance‐based redundancy analysis, with vendor as a blocking variable to control for variation introduced by this parameter, revealed no significant effect of responder group on gut microbiota composition (Figure 4I).

We also tested for correlations between biomarkers IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, TARC, eotaxin, and IgE and bacteria at either family level (Figure 4J), genus level (Figure S6), or species level (Figure S7) but with no indication of any strong correlations.

Two Weeks of Co‐Housing With SPF Mice Was Not Sufficient to Normalize the Gut Microbiota of Germ‐Free Mice

The aim of co‐housing germ‐free mice with SPF mice at 6 weeks of age was to delay gut colonization, thereby mimicking early‐life dysbiosis and blocking the “weaning reaction”, which is known to impact immune development and allergy susceptibility [14] (Figure 5A,B). To be able to isolate the potential impact of microbiome differences in early‐life and not adulthood, with the co‐housing we aimed to colonize the germ‐free gut with SPF mouse gut microbiota. However, feces samples before (week 8) and after (week 10) induction of skin inflammation, analyzed by 16S rRNA nanopore sequencing, revealed that these 2 weeks of co‐housing were not enough to secure complete SPF gut microbiota colonization. Visualizing relative abundance at phylum level in a stacked bar plot showed that the distribution of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia appeared more similar between the two groups after model induction compared to before (Figure 5C). Shannon diversity metrics also indicated significantly lower alpha diversity in mice originating from isolators compared to SPF mice after 2 weeks of co‐housing (Figure 5D). However, after induction of skin inflammation, just 11 days later, the alpha diversity was significantly increased in this group, whereas this time frame did not alter alpha diversity in the SPF group. A distance‐based redundancy analysis plotting beta diversity using Bray–Curtis distances and time as a blocking variable confirmed significant distinct clustering between mice originating from isolators or SPF facility but the clusters between these groups were closer after model induction (W10) than before (W8) (Figure 5E). A network analysis before and after model induction indicated that the two groups were completely separated from each other before model induction (Figure 5F) whereas three of the ISO mice were more related to the SPF group after model induction (Figure 5G). Interestingly, the dispersion of variance around the Bray–Curtis centroid was significantly larger in the ISO group compared to the SPF group indicating higher plasticity in less diverse gut environments (Figure 5H). Before model induction, 41 bacterial genera were differentially abundant between the ISO and SPF group (Figure 5I), whereas only 21 genera were differentially abundant after a further 11 days of co‐housing (Figure 5J). At the time of induction of skin inflammation, some of the less abundant genera in the ISO mice included Candidatus Arthromitus, Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group, Prevotellaceae UCG‐001, and different Lachnospiraceae genera, whereas higher abundant genera included Enterobacter, Akkermansia, Enterococcus, Clostridioides, Blautia, and Escherichia‐Shigella (Figure 5I).

Germ‐Free Mice Co‐Housed With SPF Mice Had Unaltered Skin Inflammatory Responses but Increased Serum IgE and Had Fewer T Cells in the Ear‐Draining Lymph Node

Despite remarked early life dysbiosis as well as distinct gut microbiota profiles later in life, the primary outcome of the Ova‐Alum model, ear tissue cytokines and chemokines (Figure 6A–O), as well as OVA‐specific serum IgE (Figure S8) was unaffected. However, serum IgE levels were significantly increased in the ISO group (Figure 6P).

Discussion

We investigated the impact of gut microbiota on the Ova‐Alum model by using substrains from three different vendors. 16S rRNA sequencing data revealed that the vendor was the primary factor explaining variation in gut microbiota composition, surpassing sex, treatment, and timepoint. This finding aligns with Rasmussen et al. [24], who reported that vendor choice influenced both bacterial and viral profiles more significantly than high‐ vs. low‐fat diets. Despite these notable microbiota differences, our hypothesis was not confirmed as vendor choice did not affect skin levels of IL‐4 and IL‐5 in the Ova‐Alum model and no correlations between gut microbiota and biomarkers were observed. We noted considerable inter‐individual variation in inflammatory responses, with some mice exhibiting stronger responses to ovalbumin than others. To determine if mice with a generally high response had a distinct gut microbiota profile, we categorized the mice into three responder groups based on their ear‐tissue levels of Th2‐related biomarkers, that is, IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, and TARC. By controlling for significant vendor variation, a distance‐based redundancy analysis concluded that there was no microbiota clustering attributable to these responder groups.

We also explored the effect of delayed gut colonization on the model, given that early life dysbiosis is linked to increased allergy susceptibility later in life [14, 29, 40, 41]. We hypothesized that germ‐free mice for their first 6 weeks of life, subsequently co‐housed with SPF mice, would develop a normalized gut microbiota similar to SPF mice by the time of Ova‐Alum induction at 8 weeks of age. However, microbiota characterization showed that the ISO group did not fully acquire the SPF mouse gut microbiota after 2 weeks of co‐housing, which has otherwise been indicated as sufficient [42]. The optimal window for microbial manipulation is at weaning [42, 43], which may explain the slower or less efficient colonization at 6 weeks of age. Nevertheless, after an additional 11 days of co‐housing during the Ova‐Alum model, the ISO group exhibited a gut microbiota profile more similar to SPF mice, suggesting that a longer co‐housing period was necessary to normalize the gut microbiota and isolate early life period differences. Despite this, the Ova‐Alum model remained robust to extreme microbiota manipulation in early life, as none of the skin biomarkers were affected. In a similar experiment with 1 or 8 weeks delayed gut colonization in mice with oxazolone‐induced skin inflammation, only the 1 week delayed colonization, and not the 8 weeks, resulted in increased ear tissue inflammatory biomarkers, despite significantly lower alpha diversity in the eight‐week group [29]. Rikenellaceae, previously associated with a high‐responding phenotype in mice with oxazolone‐induced skin inflammation [30], did not colonize in the eight‐week group, potentially explaining the lack of effect on the model outcome. Similarly, in the present study, the ISO mice failed to colonize Rikenellaceae before model induction (Figure 5I), which could contribute to the lack of effect on the Ova‐Alum model. Falkenberg et al. [4] highlighted a potential interplay between effector memory CD8+ T cells in the spleen and Ova‐Alum‐induced inflammatory responses. Both in the vendor study (Study 1) and the co‐housing experiment (Study 2), the total numbers of effector memory CD8+ T cells in the spleen were unaltered between groups, and it is, therefore, in accordance with Falkenberg et al. [4] that we did not observe differences in Ova‐Alum read‐outs. However, the ISO group had significantly fewer effector memory CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in the ear‐LN, possibly due to early germ‐free life or increased migration of cells to the inflamed ear tissue. Nevertheless, these differences in T cell levels in ear‐LN did not influence the cytokine production in the inflamed ear.

The Ova‐Alum model effectively induced ear‐tissue inflammatory biomarkers in treated mice compared to vehicle‐treated mice, as expected. However, the effect of the Ova‐Alum model on cellular composition is less understood. Our findings suggest that Ova‐Alum treatment reduced the frequency of effector memory CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and increased the frequency of naïve T cells in ear‐LN. Although not significant, this trend appeared to apply to total numbers as well (Figure S5). This may be due to the dynamic recruitment of effector memory cells to the ear tissue, where inflammation was induced, while a continuous recruitment of naïve cells occurred simultaneously from the spleen, where the frequency of T cells, T helper cells, and cytotoxic T cells was significantly reduced in Ova‐Alum treated mice.

We hypothesized that gut microbiota would impact the Ova‐Alum skin inflammation model, as reported for other disease models [23, 30, 44], potentially allowing for gut microbiota manipulation to address challenges with high variation and poor translatability. Contrary to the oxazolone‐induced skin inflammation model, as demonstrated by Zachariassen et al. [30], the Ova‐Alum model was robust to microbiota and other biological substrain differences. Al Nabhani et al. [14] showed that inhibiting the “weaning reaction” (a significant microbiota shift at 3 weeks of age due to solid food intake) led to pathological imprinting and increased susceptibility to colitis, allergic inflammation, and cancer later in life. We delayed gut colonization for 6 weeks in the ISO group of Study 2, inhibiting this weaning reaction, altering ear‐LN T cell levels, and generating abnormally high IgE levels. Yet, this did not affect Ova‐Alum induced IL‐4 and IL‐5 in inflamed skin. Compared to other mouse models of allergic skin inflammation, the Ova‐Alum model is a simple, short‐term mechanistic model with no clinical signs of dermal damage but is practically efficient, fast, and animal welfare‐friendly. Our research demonstrates that the Ova‐Alum model is robust to microbiota differences, which is advantageous for reproducibility and flexibility in vendor choice. Additionally, sex did not affect the model and only minimally impacted gut microbiota, further enhancing the model's flexibility. However, challenges with low effect size and high variability persist and may not be resolved by microbiota transplantation from a high‐responding phenotype donor, as demonstrated by Zachariassen et al. [30].

It is important to note that the microbiota of SPF mice is characterized by very low diversity compared to wild mice [17, 19]. It may be speculated that this limited diversity may result in microbiota differences that are too insufficient to significantly impact a robust mechanistic inflammation model. In contrast, “wildlings,” developed by Rosshart et al. [17], possess a much more diverse microbiota and a greater presence of pathogens, which has been shown to increase airway inflammation [44]. Additionally, gut microbiota diversity is inversely associated with variation in biomarkers [30]. Therefore, future studies should investigate the impact of a more diverse and natural microbiota, as well as pathogen exposure, on the Ova‐Alum model. Furthermore, to better reflect the human disease phenotype, a more Th‐2 biased immune response is desired, potentially achievable by examining inactivated parasitic stimulation in mice.

It is a limitation that the study did not specifically isolate the effect of gut microbiota in the absence of substrain genetic differences, and there might also be genotypic differences between mice of the same strain from different breeders, that is, in reality these are sub‐strains. Also, it is a limitation that the cohousing study was performed using only one of the SPF microbiota and that there was an unequal distribution of SPF and germ‐free mice in the gages during co‐housing, which may have biased the microbial colonization in mice from different cages.

In conclusion, the Ova‐Alum model is robust to microbiota differences, including an early sterile environment, vendor choice, and sex, supporting its reproducibility. However, challenges with high inter‐individual variation and a low‐to‐moderate response for many of the mice persist and may require alternative approaches to address.
