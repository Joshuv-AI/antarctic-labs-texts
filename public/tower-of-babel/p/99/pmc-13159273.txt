TBK1-NQO1 negative feedback loop regulates antiviral innate immunity
Authors: Hyuncheol Lee, Asela Weerawardhana, Kiramage Chathuranga, W A Gayan Chathuranga, Tae-Hwan Kim, Duk‐Jae Jang, Aleysha Chen, David Schaffer, Jae U Jung, Chul-Ho Lee, Jong-Soo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) is an essential kinase that phosphorylates transcriptional factors, which induce antiviral interferons and inflammatory cytokines against viral infection. Tight regulation of TBK1 is critical for innate immune responses and maintenance of homeostasis. Here, we report that NAD(P)H: quinone-oxidoreductase-1 (NQO1) forms a negative feedback loop with TBK1 to regulate innate antiviral immune responses. At the late time stages of virus infection, NQO1 specifically interacts with TBK1 and is phosphorylated at S82 of NQOI by TBK1. Interestingly, S82-phosphorylated NQO1 interferes with the self-association and phosphorylation of TBK1, resulting in reduced type I IFN signaling cascades. Depletion or reduced expression of NQO1 increases secretion of antiviral cytokines and resultes in less viral replication. In particular, NQO1−/− mice are more resistant to viral infection due to the enhanced secretion of antiviral cytokines. Taken together, these finding provide new insight into the molecular regulation of NQOI and TBK1 and suggest a critical role for NQOI in the homeostatic control of antiviral response and innate immune responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02850-y.Keywords: NQO1, TBK1, Innate immunity, Virus infection

Introduction

TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) was first identified as a novel IκB kinase (IKK)-related kinase that activates NF-кB in a kinase-dependent manner [1]. Activation of TBK1 is enhanced significantly upon recognition of viral RNA by retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) and melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5). In addition, the detection of viral DNA by cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and recognition of viral double-stranded RNA by Toll-like receptor 3 contribute to the robust activation of TBK1 [2–4]. Activated TBK1 regulates phosphorylation of transcription factors such as interferon (IFN) regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), interferon-regulatory factor 7 (IRF7), and NF-кB, which then translocate into the cell nucleus to promote the secretion of type I IFNs and proinflammatory cytokines [5–7].

TBK1 plays an indispensable role in innate immune response and antiviral defenses because it is central to multiple pathways that control expression of numerous genes (e.g., those encoding type I and type III IFNs). According to the known hypothesis so far, several combined structural and biochemical studies show that activation of TBK1 is dependent on transautophosphorylation [8, 9]. In its native state, TBK1 forms stable homodimers via kinase active sites that are oriented away from each other. This configuration prevents spontaneous activation. However, signaling events trigger changes to the scaffolding of TBK1, resulting in a high local concentration of TBK1 molecules. This proximity promotes interdimer interactions, leading to the phosphorylation of serine 172 (S172) within the activation loop. The phosphorylated form of TBK1 (pTBK1) then rapidly phosphorylates the remaining pool of TBK1 molecules, culminating in the formation of fully-activated kinase dimers and amplifying the immune response through a cascade of signaling events [8, 9]. Although various positive or negative regulators for TBK1 activation have been studied and known to date, there are still unknown regulatory mechanisms.

NQO1 is a FAD-containing flavoprotein located primarily in the cytoplasm and transcriptionally induced by a variety of stimuli, including oxidants, ultraviolet light, xenobiotics, and ionizing radiation [10, 11]. NQO1 is known for its capacity to reduce quinones, quinoneimines, and nitroaromatics by donating two electrons, hence decreasing the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [12]. NQO1 is also critical for the stability of several molecules, including p53 and p73 [13, 14]. Although the role of NQO1 in cancer and chemoprevention has been studied extensively, its role in the immune response is less clear. Previous research indicates that NQO1-null mice show a reduction in peripheral B-lymphocyte, lower germinal center response, altered B-cell homing, and impaired primary and secondary immune responses [15]. Moreover, NQO1 is involved in protecting human monocytes against excessive inflammatory responses [16, 17]. However, the direct role of NQO1 in modulating antiviral immune response and the underlying mechanism remains unknown.

In this study, we found that NQO1 negatively regulates antiviral immune responses during virus infection in vitro and in vivo. Upon virus infection, NQO1 specifically interacts with TBK1 at a late time point and is phosphorylated by TBK1. Phosphorylated NQOI then inhibits TBK1 self-association and activates a negative feedback loop that controls TBK1 activation. Ultimately, NQO1-deficient mice showed enhanced innate immune response against DNA and RNA viruses. These findings presented herein suggest an additional role of NQO1 as a novel regulator for maintenance of homeostasis in antiviral immune response.

All animal experiments were managed in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (published by the US National Institutes of Health) and performed in BSL-2 and BSL-3 laboratory facilities with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chungnam National University (Reference number CNU-00765, 202112 A-CNU-197) and Bioleaders Corporation (Reference number BLS-ABSL-14-013).

For the virus infection experiments, sex-matched mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were infected intranasally with H1N1 (106 TCID50/mouse), intravenously with VSV-GFP (2 × 108 PFU/mouse), or injected with poly(I: C) (200 µg/mouse). Serum samples were collected at the indicated time points (Fig. 1) to examine the level of cytokines in an ELISA. Lungs were also collected to determine the virus titer.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T (ATCC-11268), RAW264.7 (ATCC TIB-71), HeLa (ATCC CCL-2), Vero (ATCC CCL-81), and A549 (ATCC CCL-185) cells were cultured in DMEM (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone) and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic (Gibco). THP-1 (ATCC TIB-202) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (PAN-biotech) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with the indicated plasmids using polyethylenimine (Polyscience inc.), whereas RAW264.7 cells and HeLa cells were transfected with Lipofectamine2000 (Invitrogen).

Preparation of stable cell lines

To prepare the NQO1-overexpressing stable cell line, RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well culture plates. At 12 h post-seeding (60% confluent), the cells were transfected for 6 h with a pIRES-Flag-NQO1 plasmid using Lipofectamine2000 (Invitrogen). Next, the culture medium was exchanged for complete DMEM medium, followed by a 12 h incubation prior to transfer to a 100 mm culture dish. After the cells were attached, the medium was replaced with complete DMEM containing 2 µg/mL puromycin. The medium was replaced with fresh puromycin-containing 10% DMEM every 2 days until resistant colonies appeared. Expression of Flag-NQO1 was confirmed by immunoblotting.

Stable knockdown of NQO1

NQO1-specific shRNA and the pGIPZ lentiviral vector gene set were purchased from Dharmacon. Packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.VSV-G were purchased from Addgene. Lentivirus was generated in HEK293T cells by transfecting the cells with the plasmids for 72 h. Virus titers were measured using a Lenti-X p24 rapid titer kit (Clontech). To generate stable cell lines, RAW264.7 cells were infected with the virus using polybrene (Sigma), followed by selection with puromycin.

Construction of NQO1 mutant cell lines

HEK293T cells were transfected with a plasmid encoding Cas9-NLS, an NQO1-targeted sgRNA (target region, 5’-CCATCTGAGCCCAGATATTG-3’), and each of the HDR templates (S82D, GCTTATAAAGAAGGCCATCTGGACCCAGATATCGTTGCTGAACAAAAGAAG; or S82A, GCTTATAAAGAAGGCCATCTGGCCCCAGATATCGTTGC TGAACAAAAGAAG) using Lipofectamine2000. At 36 h post-transfection, cells were selected with puromycin and verified in a T7E1 assay. A monoclonal cell line was isolated from the verified cell population and then sequence-verified by PCR of the NQO1 S82 gDNA region using the following primers: Forward, 5’-ACGTCCTTCAACTATGCCAT-3’; Reverse: 5’-ATGCAGTAAAGTGGGTA ACTG-3’.

Isolation and culture of primary cells

BMDMs were isolated as previously described [18]. Briefly, femurs and tibias were excised aseptically from NQO1−/− and NQO1+/+ mice. The muscles were removed from the bones, which were then flushed with DMEM to extrude the bone marrow. Ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysis buffer (Gibco) was used to lyse the red blood cells, and the remaining cells were cultured for 6 days in DMEM containing 10% FBS, antibiotics, and mGM-CSF (10 ng/mL) to allow differentiation into BMDMs.

Primary peripheral blood CD14 + monocytes (normal, human; ATCC PCS-800‐010) were cultured in Alpha MEM (Welgene) supplemented with 10% FBS and 500 U/mL of human GM‐CSF (Enzynomics). Complete medium replacement was performed every 3 days. On the ninth day, CD14 + MDMs were harvested and used for subsequent experiments.

Plasmids

To generate the different NQO1 constructs, NQO1 (NM_000903.2) was amplified from template DNA (Sino biological, HG12046-CF) using PCR, and then cloned into pIRES-Flag, pEXPR-Strep, or pEBG vectors. Site-directed PCR mutagenesis was carried out using high-fidelity DNA polymerase (ThermoFisher, F-530 S) to generate the NQO1-S82A and NQO1-S82D mutant plasmids. To generate different TBK1 constructs, WT TBK1 was amplified from template DNA by PCR and then cloned into the pEBG and pIRES-V5 vectors. Similarly, the different TBK1 domain fragments (1–300 aa, 1–385 aa, 300–729 aa, and 385–729 aa) were amplified by PCR and cloned into the pEBG vector.

Reagents and antibodies

Poly(dA:dT), poly(I:C), and 5’ppp-dsRNA were from Invivogen; Lipofectamine2000 and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX were from Invitrogen; puromycin was from Gibco; A/G PLUS-Agarose was from Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Sepharose 6B and glutathione-conjugated Sepharose 4B (GST) beads were from GE Healthcare Life Science; and strep-Tactin Sepharose (120−0590) was from IBA Lifesciences.

Generation of the NQO1 anti-pS82 antibody

Anti-pS82 antiserum was generated by immunizing a 10-week-old female New Zealand white rabbit with a phosphorylated peptide (AYKEGHLpSPDIVAEQC) containing the phosphorylation site, as previously described [19]. Briefly, the rabbit was immunized subcutaneously with peptide-keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH) emulsified with Freund’s complete adjuvant. A booster dose containing peptide-KLH emulsified with Freund’s incomplete adjuvant was given at 14 and 28 days after the initial immunization. Serum was collected 33 days after the initial immunization, and the antibody was purified by affinity resin chromatography, as previously described [20].

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells or 1 mg of mice tissue using the Macherey-Nagel NucleoSpin RNA extraction kit (790955), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the SmartGene Mixed cDNA synthesis kit (SG-cDNAM100). The different levels of cDNA were quantified by quantitative real-time PCR using a SmartGene SYBR green qPCR master mix (SG-SYBR.500) kit in a Rotor-gene Q (Qiagen) instrument. The sequences of the primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Luciferase reporter assay

A luciferase reporter plasmid, a TK-Renilla (thymidine kinase promoter-Renilla) plasmid, and other indicated plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells using polyethylenimine. For ISRE luciferase assay, cells were stimulated with human IFN-β (1000 U/mL). After 12 h, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline and lysed for 20 min in passive lysis buffer (Promega). Luciferase activity was measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega; E1980). Luciferase activity in cells expressing only IFN-β reporter or ISRE reporter and Renilla plasmids was measured as a control. Data are presented as the relative activity of firefly luciferase normalized against that of Renilla luciferase.

Plaque assay

Virus-infected cell culture supernatants collected at the indicated times, and/or extracts were obtained from freeze-thawed virus-infected cells, were used for the plaque assay. Homogenates of freeze-thawed tissue extracts were used for the plaque assay when measuring the virus titer in the in vivo experiments. Briefly, a monolayer of A549 or Vero cells was infected with serially diluted supernatants and/or extracts, and then incubated for 2 h. The medium was replaced with DMEM containing 0.1% agarose (Sigma-Aldrich), and plaque formation was observed under an optical microscope at 200× magnification. The virus titer was calculated as the number of plaque-forming units (taking into account the dilution factor).

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants, tissue homogenates, and mouse serum samples were used for ELISA to measure cytokine secretion levels. Mouse IFN-β (R&D systems, 42400-1), mouse IL-6 (BD Biosciences, 555240), mouse IFN-ʎ (BD Biosciences, 555138), mouse MCP-1 (BD Biosciences, 555260), and human IFN-β (Cusabio biotech, CSB-E09889h) commercial ELISA kits were used.

MAVS aggregation assay

MAVS aggregation was assessed as previously described [18]. Briefly, mitochondria were isolated from cells using a mitochondrial isolation kit (Sigma-Aldrich). The mitochondrial fraction was resuspended in 1× sample buffer (0.5× TBE, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, and 0.0025% bromophenol blue) and subjected to semi-denaturing detergent agarose gel electrophoresis (SDD-AGE). Samples were resolved on a 1.5% vertical agarose gel in running buffer containing 1× TBE and 0.1% SDS at 100 V for 50 min at 4 °C. Proteins were then transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore) and analyzed by immunoblotting using an anti-MAVS antibody.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were harvested 36 h post-transfection and then lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% IGEPAL, 1 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma). For immunoprecipitation, whole cell lysates were pre-cleared for 2 h at 4 °C with Sepharose 6B (GE life sciences). After clearing, whole cell lysates were incubated overnight with 2 µg of antibody specific for the target protein, and 50% slurry of glutathione-conjugated Sepharose (GST) beads (Amersham biosciences) or strep beads (IBA Lifesciences), with agitation at 4℃. A/G PLUS-agarose (Santa Cruz biotechnology) was added only to samples incubated with antibodies for 3 h at 4℃.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance between two groups was determined by a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney test, depending on the normality test result. In the animal survival study, log-rank was performed for statistical significance. A p-value under 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Deficiency of NQO1 increases antiviral response in vivo

Mycobacterium tuberculosis increases expression of NQO1, and knockdown of NQO1 activates NF-кB and triggers production of proinflammatory cytokines [21]. This knowledge prompted us to investigate whether NQO1 plays a physiological role in virus infection in vivo. As shown in Fig. 1A, NQO1−/− and NQO1+/+ mice were infected intranasally with a lethal dose of influenza A virus (H1N1) and then monitored for 15 days (Fig. 1A). NQO1+/+ mice were more susceptible to H1N1 infection than NQO1−/− mice, with a significantly lower survival rate (Fig. 1B). A TCID50 assay was conducted to investigate virus replication and the viral load in mouse lungs was consistent with the survival results; specifically, NQO1−/− mice had lower lung virus titers than NQO1+/+ mice at 3 and 5 days post-infection (dpi) (Fig. 1C). Next, we intranasally infected NQO1−/− and NQO1+/+ mice with low virulence influenza A virus H1N1 and measured IFN-β and IL-6 levels in serum and bronchial lavage fluid (BALF) to determine the effects of NQO1 deficiency on viral clearance. At 3 dpi, the concentration of both IFN-β and IL-6 in serum and BALF fluid from NQO1−/− mice was significantly higher than in those from NQO1+/+ mice (Figs. 1D and S1A). Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin staining of lung sections revealed greater inflammatory cell infiltration in NQO1+/+ mice than in NQO1−/− mice (Fig. 1E). To confirm that NQO1 is involved in the negative regulation of host immune response, we intravenously infected NQO1−/− and NQO1+/+ mice with VSV-GFP, and obtained blood samples at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 18 h post-infection (Fig. 1F). Similar to the results obtained after H1N1 infection, we found that NQO1+/+ mice showed higher virus titers and lower cytokine levels than NQO1−/− mice (Figs. 1G, H and S1B). In addition, we examined the role of NQO1 in cytokine induction by poly(I:C), a synthetic dsRNA (Fig. 1I). Intravenous injection of poly(I:C) caused the rapid and robust induction of IFN-β and IL-6 in NQO1−/− mice; however, induction of these cytokines was significantly less robust in NQO1+/+ mice (Fig. 1J). These results indicated that NQO1 plays a vital role in the antiviral immune responses by suppressing the production of IFNs and proinflammatory cytokines.

Role of NQO1 in the antiviral response to virus infection

The role of NQO1 in virus replication and cytokine secretion was evaluated in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) obtained from NQO1−/− and NQO1+/+ mice. Virus replication in NQO1−/− and NQO1+/+ BMDMs was measured after infection with VSV-GFP, influenza A virus (PR8-GFP), or Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-GFP). As shown in Fig. 2A, virus replication in NQO1+/+ BMDMs was consistently higher than that in NQO1−/− BMDMs. Measurement of IFN-β, IL-6 and IL-12 secretion by BMDMs after virus infection, or after poly(I:C) and poly(dA:dT) treatment, revealed that IFN-β, IL-6, and IL-12 production were lower in NQO1+/+ BMDMs than in NQO1−/− BMDMs (Figs. 2B and C, and S1C). Next, we used human CD14+ monocyte-derived macrophages (hCD14+ MDM) in which NQO1 was knocked down to assess the replication of PR8-GFP and HSV-GFP. Interestingly, we found that virus replication in NQO1-knockdown CD14+ MDM cells was significantly lower than that in the control cells, and NQO1 knockdown enhanced IFN‐β and IL‐6 production markedly (Fig. 2D and G).

To further investigate whether NQO1 induces viral replication, and to confirm the results observed in NQO1−/− BMDM cells, we next knocked down NQO1 in murine macrophages (RAW264.7) using shRNA (Figures S2A and S2B). PR8-GFP, VSV-GFP, and HSV-GFP infected RAW264.7 cells exhibited low virus titers after shRNA knockdown of NQO1 (Figs. 2 H, I, and S2C). In addition, NQO1-knockdown RAW264.7 cells secrete significantly higher levels of cytokines upon infection with RNA or DNA viruses (Figs. 2 J, K, and S2D). Similar results were obtained following poly(I:C), 5’ppp-dsRNA, and poly(dA:dT) treatment, which shows NQO1 knockdown cells secreted significantly higher levels of IFN-β and IL-6 than control cells (Fig. 2 L–N). Figure S2 shows that NQO1 siRNA-mediated knockdown of human monocyte cells (THP-1), human lung epithelial cells (A549), and human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T) yielded similar results to those obtained for NQO1−/− BMDMs or NQO1 shRNA knockdown RAW264.7 cells in that virus replication in siRNA knockdown cells infected with VSV-GFP was lower than that in control cells (Figures S2E-S2H). This suggests that the effect of NQO1 on virus replication is not limited to a specific cell type.

Next, we conducted immunoblot analysis of RAW264.7 cells to confirm stable overexpression of NQO1 (Figure S3A). NQO1- and control vector-overexpressing cells were infected with RNA virus (VSV-GFP or PR8-GFP) and DNA virus (HSV-GFP), followed by measurement of GFP absorbance and virus titer. As expected, overexpression of NQO1 resulted in higher virus replication (Figures S3B-S3D) and lower levels of cytokine secretion (Figures S3E and S3F). Collectively, these results suggest that NQO1 negatively regulates the antiviral response in immune and epithelial cells.

NQO1 negatively regulates the type-I IFN signaling and expression of antiviral genes

Upon virus infection, viral pathogen-associated molecular patterns are recognized by host pattern recognition receptors, which activate key molecules in host immune signaling pathways. Because phosphorylation of specific amino acids within these key signaling molecules is essential for the activation, we examined phosphorylation of IRF3, P65, STAT1, and TBK1 following infection of NQO1−/− and NQO1+/+ BMDM cells with PR8-GFP. As shown in Fig. 3A, phosphorylation of these molecules was significantly higher in NQO1−/− BMDM cells than in NQO1+/+ BMDM cells. These results were confirmed by infection of NQO1 shRNA knockdown RAW264.7 cells and control cells with PR8-GFP, which showed that knockdown of NQO1 led to a marked increase in phosphorylation of these signaling molecules (Fig. 3B). Corresponding results were obtained when using NQO1 and control vector stably overexpressing RAW264.7 cells, where NQO1 overexpression inhibits the phosphorylation of key signaling molecules following infection by PR8-GFP virus (Figure S4A).

Next, we measured expression of mRNA encoding IFN-related genes to determine whether transcription of type I IFN and related genes was affected by knockdown and reconstitution of NQO1 (Figs. 3C and D, and S4B). Expression of mRNA encoding IFN-related genes was higher in NQO1−/− BMDMs stimulated with the PR8-GFP virus (Fig. 3C). Similar data were observed for NQO1-knockdown RAW264.7 cells, which showed higher levels of IFN-related gene expression compared to control cells (Fig. 3D). NQO1 stably-overexpressing RAW264.7 cells also showed lower expression of IFN-related gene expression than control vector stably expressing cells (Figure S4B). Next, to determine whether NQO1 influences IFN-triggered downstream signaling, we assessed IFN-β–stimulated ISRE and ISG54 luciferase activities in the presence of NQO1. Dose-dependent transfection of NQO1 had no significant effect on either ISRE or ISG54 reporter activity (Figures S5A and S5B). Consistent with the luciferase assay results, IFN-induced transcription of downstream genes was also unaffected by NQO1 expression (Figure S5C). Collectively, these findings suggest that NQO1 functions as a negative regulator of the type I IFN induction pathway, not the IFN-triggered downstream signaling pathway.

NQO1 binds to TBK1 upon viral infection

Upon viral infection, host pattern recognition receptors detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns and activate signaling cascades to initiate the host immune response. The above results show that NQO1 regulates this signaling cascade to control the host immune response to virus infections. Therefore, to identify a specific target within the type I IFN signaling cascade that is downregulated by NQO1, we conducted a large-scale pulldown assay using HEK293T cells overexpressing GST-tagged NQO1. Mass spectrometry analysis identified TBK1 as a candidate molecule for binding to NQO1 (Fig. 4A). Next, to clarify these data, we conducted an IFN-β luciferase reporter assay by co-expressing NQO1 and several molecules belonging to the type 1 IFN signaling pathway. The results showed that NQO1 reduced IFN-β luciferase activity in response to co-transfection with poly(I:C), RIG-I, MAVS, STING, and TBK1 in a dose-dependent manner, however, there were no significant changes in IKK-ε mediated induction of IFN-β luciferase activity after co-transfection with different doses of NQO1 (Fig. 4B), suggesting that the target molecule(s) of NQO1 reside immediately upstream of IKK-ε. These data correspond with the mass spectrometry analysis results, leading us to hypothesize that NQO1 interacts with TBK1. To check this, we performed an immunoprecipitation assay using an overexpression system in HEK293T cells. Figure 4C and S5A show a clear interaction between NQO1 and TBK1. Furthermore, overexpression of NQO1 in HEK293T and RAW264.7 cells triggered an interaction with endogenous TBK1 (Figs. 4D and S6B). Next, we investigated the timing of the interaction between endogenous NQO1 and TBK1 in response to infection by RNA and DNA viruses. Immunoprecipitation experiments revealed endogenous binding of NQO1 to TBK1 in RAW264.7 cells after infection with PR8-GFP, NDV-GFP, and HSV-GFP. Importantly, NQO1 interacted with TBK1 at the late stage of virus infection (Figs. 4E, F, and S6C).

TBK1 comprises several domains, a kinase domain (KD), a ubiquitin-like domain (ULD), and coiled-coiled domains 1 and 2 (CC1 and CC2) [22, 23]. To identify the domain within TBK1 that interacts with NQO1, we constructed GST-tagged TBK1 KD-, KD:ULD-, ULD:CC1:CC2- and CC1:CC2-expressing plasmids (Fig. 4G). GST pulldown assays of each construct in the presence of NQO1 showed that NQO1 bound strongly to the KD:ULD (1-385 aa) fragment of TBK1 (Fig. 4H). Next, to identify the NQO1 domain responsible for interaction with TBK1, Strep pull-down assays were performed using each NQO1 construct in the presence of TBK1. The results revealed that TBK1 specifically interacts with the 100–225 amino acid region of NQO1 (Fig. 4I and J). Collectively, these results suggest that NQO1 targets TBK1-mediated IFN signaling to downregulate the host immune response to virus infections.

NQO1 attenuates self-association of TBK1

Self-association and phosphorylation of TBK1 Ser172 are essential for its activation [8]. This knowledge prompted us to ask whether NQO1 attenuates activation of TBK1 by inhibiting its self-association. To answer this question, we examined the impact of NQO1 on self-association between GST-tagged and V5-tagged TBK1. The results showed that self-association of TBK1 decreased when expression of NQO1 increased (Fig. 5 A and B). We further assessed the virus infection-induced self-association of TBK1 in the NQO1 knockdown condition. As shown in Fig. 5C, NQO1 knockdown significantly induced the self-association of TBK1 compared to control. These results were confirmed by confocal microscopy analysis of HeLa cells in which the number of self-associated TBK1 foci was significantly lower in the presence of NQO1 than in cells transfected with TBK1 alone (Figs. 5D and S6C). Because studies suggest that the self-association of TBK1 triggers its transautophosphorylation [8, 24], we transfected HEK293T cells with V5-tagged TBK1 along with different doses of flag-tagged NQO1, followed by immunoprecipitation with an anti-V5 antibody. Immunoblot analysis with an antibody specific to Ser172 phosphorylated TBK1 revealed that NQO1 inhibited phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5E and F). DNA virus infection activates STING, which then recruits TBK1, which then mediates phosphorylation of STING and IRF3 [25, 26]. Since NQO1 inhibits phosphorylation of TBK1, we examined its effect on phosphorylation of STING and IRF3. NQO1 inhibited phosphorylation of STING and IRF3 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5G-J). Next, to assess whether NQO1 affects signaling upstream of TBK1, we performed a MAVS oligomerization assay. The results showed that MAVS oligomerization, which is essential for the activation of MAVS-mediated downstream signaling, was not affected by NQO1 (Fig. 5K).

The P187S polymorphism is known to abolish NQO1 enzymatic activity by markedly reducing FAD-binding affinity and accelerating proteasome-mediated protein turnover [27, 28]. To determine whether NQO1 enzymatic activity is required for its IFN regulatory function, we first examined the interaction between TBK1 and the NQO1 P187S mutant. As shown in Figure S6E, TBK1 interacted with NQO1 P187S at a level comparable to that observed with WT NQO1. Consistent with the preserved TBK1 interaction, HSV-GFP and PR8-GFP replication, as well as virus-induced cytokine secretion, were not significantly different between cells expressing WT NQO1 and NQO1 P187S (Figures S6F–S6I).

TBK1 phosphorylates S82 of NQO1

Because we observed an increased expression of NQO1 protein upon interaction with TBK1 (Fig. 4H), we hypothesized that NQO1 might undergo post-translational modification (PTM) upon such an interaction. Addition of a phosphate group to serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues is one of the earliest PTMs described in eukaryotes [29, 30]. Since TBK1 is also a kinase responsible for phosphorylation of serine/threonine residues within its substrates [31–33], we used phospho-serine- and phospho-threonine-specific antibodies to check the ability of TBK1 to mediate phosphorylation of NQO1. The data in Figs. 6A and S7A suggest that TBK1 phosphorylates serine but not threonine residues. To identify the specific serine residue within NQO1 that is phosphorylated by TBK1, we conducted PTM analyses in the presence of TBK1. The results in Fig. 6B show that NQO1 is phosphorylated at S82, which is consistent with the previous results.

To investigate TBK1 inducible S82 phosphorylation of NQO1 by immunoblotting, we generated an NQO1 anti-pS82 antibody by immunizing rabbits with a phosphopeptide containing the NQO1 residue S82. We then conducted a co-transfection assay using this NQO1 anti-pS82 antibody to confirm involvement of TBK1 in NQO1 phosphorylation; the results showed that phosphorylation of NQO1 S82 increased as the dose of TBK1 increased (Fig. 6C). Since virus infection induces activation of TBK1, we further examined whether virus infection affects the phosphorylation of NQO1. Immunoblot analysis of HEK293T cells infected with PR8-GFP virus showed that phosphorylation of NQO1 S82 was enhanced significantly at late time points after virus infection (i.e., 18 and 24 h; Fig. 6D). To assess the specificity of TBK1 as the kinase responcible for the phosphorylation of NQO1, we checked the virus infection induce NQO1 S82 phosphorylation in TBK1 knockdown cells. As shown in Fig. 6E, NQO1 does not undergo S82 phosphorylation in TBK1 knockdown cells compared to control.

To further assess the specificity of TBK1-mediated phosphorylation of NQO1 S82, we co-transfected cells with TBK1 along with NQO1 wild type (WT), a NQO1 non-phosphorylatable mutant (S82A), and NQO1 phospho-mimicking mutant (S82D) plasmids, followed by immunoblotting with the NQO1 anti-pS82 antibody. The results confirmed that only NQO1 WT and NQO1-S82D are phosphorylated by TBK1 (Fig. 6F), suggesting that the kinase activity of TBK1 is specific for S82 of NQO1. Next, we examine the behavior of S82-phosphorylated NQO1 using S82A and S82D plasmids. The results showed that, unlike the non-phosphorylatable NQO1-S82A mutant (Figs. 6G and S7B), the phospho-mimicking NQO1-S82D mutant inhibited phosphorylation and self-association of TBK1, similarly to NQO1 WT (Figs. 6 H and S7C).

Since NQO1 is involved in the negative regulation of TBK1 self-association, and TBK1 mediates the phosphorylation of NQO1 S82, we next assessed the effect of NQO1 phosphorylation on its function as a negative regulator of host immune responses to virus infection. The results shown in Fig. 6I and J suggest that replication of PR8-GFP in NQO1-S82A-transfected HEK293T cells was significantly lower than that in NQO1-WT- and NQO1-S82D-transfected cells (Fig. 6I and J). Corresponding results were observed when we measured secreted IFN-β: NQO1-S82A-transfected cells secreted more IFN-β than NQO1-WT- and NQO1-S82D- transfected cells (Fig. 6K).

Phosphorylation of NQO1 regulates TBK1

To confirm involvement of NQO1 S82 phosphorylation on NQO1 mediated-immune regulation, we constructed NQO1-S82A- and NQO1-S82D-mutant HEK293T cell lines using CRISPR-Cas9 technology (Figure S8A). Infection of these mutant cell lines with PR8-GFP virus revealed that virus replication in cells harboring the NQO1-S82A mutation was lower, and IFN-β secretion was higher, than in NQO1-S82D-mutatant cells (Fig. 7 A-C). To further assess the effect of NQO1 S82 phosphorylation on the TBK1-induced type I IFN pathway, we performed an IFN-β luciferase assay using NQO1 mutant HEK293T cells stimulated with TBK1. Figure S8B shows that IFN-β luciferase activity in NQO1-S82A mutant cells was significantly higher than in NQO1-WT and NQO1-S82D mutant cells. Next, we confirmed the specificity of TBK1 as the kinase that phosphorylates NQO1 S82 in NQO1 mutant cell lines. The results were similar to those from the experiments using mutant plasmids; i.e., S82A-mutated HEK293T cells do not undergo phosphorylation by TBK1 (Fig. 7D). Finally, we used the NQO1 mutated cells to confirm the ability of S82 phosphorylated NQO1 to inhibit TBK1 phosphorylation and self-association (Fig. 7E). Taken together, these results suggest that at late time points after virus infection, TBK1 phosphorylates NQO1 on S82; phosphorylated NQO1 in turn downregulates host immune responses to virus infection by targeting TBK1 through a negative regulatory loop.

Discussion

NQO1 is a FAD-containing flavoprotein located primarily in the cytoplasm. The chemo-protective activity of NQO1 is due to its property as an obligate two-electron reductase. Utilizing NADH or NADPH as a cofactor, NQO1 reduces quinone and decreases the amount of one-electron reduction, which results in the formation of ROS from highly reactive semiquinone [12]. A previous study reports that the nuclear IκB protein IκB-ζ undergoes NQO1-dependent ubiquitination and degradation by E3 ligase PDLIM2, which inhibits TLR-mediated immune response [16]. Another study describes involvement of NQO1 in autoimmunity and suggests that NQO1 has a negative regulatory role on expression and activation of NF-кB [15]. Although involvement of NQO1 in immune regulation has been reported, no information or detailed mechanism regarding the association between NQO1 and host innate immune responses to virus infection has been reported.

The present study provides several lines of evidence that NQO1 is an essential regulator of TBK1-mediated antiviral immune responses. First, NQO1−/− mice were much less susceptible to a lethal dose of H1N1 than their WT counterparts. Furthermore, infection by H1N1, VSV-GFP, and poly(I:C) resulted in stronger antiviral responses in NQO1−/− mice than in WT mice. Second, knockout or knockdown of NQO1 in human CD14 + MDMs and other immune or epithelial cells resulted in increased IFN-β production and proinflammatory cytokines in response to RNA and DNA viruses, and reduced viral replication. Third, NQO1 specifically interacted with both kinase and ULD domains of TBK1, and TBK1 catalyzed phosphorylation of NQO1 S82 at a late stage after virus infection. Finally, phosphorylation of NQOI S82 inhibits self-association of TBK1, resulting in decreased phosphorylation of TBK1. Overall, the data suggest that NQO1 is an important regulator that suppresses self-association and phosphorylation of TBK1 to inhibit TBK1-mediated antiviral immunity.

The ability of host cells to successfully transduce downstream signaling via receptors, adapter molecules, kinases, and transcription factors is vital for defense against pathogens [26, 34]. Among the various pathway molecules involved in innate immunity, TBK1 is critical because it is one of two non-canonical kinases that regulate activation of the IFNs and NF-кB signaling pathways. Thus, it is important to efficiently regulate TBK1 to manage viral infections and preserve immunological homeostasis. TBK1 is regulated by a variety of processes and specific host regulators during the antiviral cascade, including autophosphorylation, K63-linked ubiquitination, self-association, deacetylation, dephosphorylation, deubiquitination, K48-linked ubiquitination, and disruption of dimerization [35–42], leading to the well-regulated activation of transcription factors IRF3, IRF7 and NF-кB which in turn induce the production of type I IFNs and proinflammatory cytokines.

Among processes of TBK1, reports suggest that the self-association of TBK1 is promoted by molecules such as GSK3β, which upregulate innate immunity by increasing self-association by binding to the DD of TBK1 [43]. The short form of TRIM9 undergoes K63 ubiquitination and provides a platform for TBK1-GSK3β association, ultimately inducing the transautophosphorylation of TBK1 to activate IRF3 signaling [38]. Also, TRIM23 mediates virus-induced autophagy by facilitating the self-association of TBK1 [44]. By contrast, Lck, Hck, and Fgr are Src family kinases involved in phosphorylation of TBK1 at Tyr354/394, which suppresses TBK1 dimerization and activation [45]. Although negative regulation of TBK1 by inhibition of self-association and phosphorylation has been studied [46], no study has reported inhibition of TBK1 self-association and phosphorylation through a negative feedback loop induced by TBK1 itself. To investigate the role of NQO1 in TBK1 self-association, we performed various experiments and showed that the unique function of phosphorylated NQOI is to suppress TBK1 self-association through a negative regulatory loop (Figs. 5 and 6).

NQO1 undergoes post-translational phosphorylation, which modulates the NQO1 stability and function [47, 48]. Kinase Akt mediates NQO1 phosphorylation at T128 residue, which induces ubiquitination and degradation and ultimately, inhibition of the antioxidative properties of NQO1 [47]. Additionally, phosphorylation of NQO1 at S82 is known to alter its flavin binding affinity, but the kinase responsible for phosphorylation of NQO1 S82 has not been identified [48]. Here, we found that NQO1 is phosphorylated at S82 by TBK1 (Fig. 6B C). Experiments using an NQO1 S82-specific phospho antibody confirmed that NQO1 undergoes S82 phosphorylation at late time points following viral infection (Fig. 6D). This phosphorylation event coincides with the time points at which TBK1 interacts with NQO1 upon viral infection, suggesting a link between NQO1 phosphorylation and its regulatory effects on TBK1 activity. That is, cells transfected with the phosphorylation-inhibited mutant NQO1 S82A and NQO1 S82A mutated cells did not show the inhibition of TBK1 self-association and phosphorylation, and ultimately could not enhance virus replication unlike NQO1 transfected cells and NQO1 WT cells, respectively. These findings suggest that phosphorylation of NQO1 S82 plays a critical role in modulating TBK1 activity and consequently the antiviral response.

In vivo evidence supporting the physiological function of NQOI in response to virus infection suggests that NQO1−/− mice had a lower viral load and were less susceptible to challenge with a lethal dose of influenza virus H1N1 than their wild-type counterparts, suggesting that NQO1 is critical for regulating antiviral immune responses. The lower susceptibility of NQO1−/− mice to H1N1 challenge is likely due to enhanced production of IFNs and cytokines (Fig. 1). Additionally, when NQO1−/− and NQO1+/+ mice were infected with VSV-GFP or treated with poly(I:C), serum cytokine levels in NQO1−/− mice were significantly higher than those in NQO1+/+ mice at late time points after virus infection (Fig. 1I and H). These results were corroborated by the in vitro NQO1 knockout, knockdown, or overexpression experiments, suggesting that NQO1 is critical for regulating the antiviral immune response (Figs. 2 and S1-S3). Furthermore, we found that human CD14 + monocyte-derived macrophages, other immune cells, and epithelial cells showed similar responses, indicating that the effect of NQO1 on maintaining the host’s innate immune response is a phenomenon also observed in human cells and is not cell type specific.

Viral infection activates numerous positive regulators of IFN signaling, and unchecked or prolonged activation of these pathways is well known to contribute to immunopathology and chronic inflammation. In this context, NQO1-mediated suppression of TBK1 signaling may represent a protective feedback mechanism to restrain excessive inflammation.

In summary, at a later time point following DNA and RNA virus infection, NQO1 specifically interacted with TBK1 and was phosphorylated at S82 by TBK1. Phosphorylated NQOI then inhibits self-association of TBK1, thereby abolishing transautophosphorylation and activation of TBK1, ultimately preventing type I IFN signaling cascades and IFN production. NQO1−/− mice were less sensitive to virus infection, and NQO1 knockdown altered innate immune responses both in vivo and in vitro. These findings suggest that NQO1 effectively serves as a checkpoint within the innate immune signaling pathway to maintain immune homeostasis.
