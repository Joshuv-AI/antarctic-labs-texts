Effect of Vibration, Electrical Stimulation and Other Non-Pharmacological Interventions on Restless Leg Syndrome Severity and Sleep Quality: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Bhupesh Gupta, Ramita Goel, Kirtika Gupta, Arpitkumar Thakor, Anshu Mittal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:This study aims to determine the effect of nonpharmacological treatments, such as vibration, electric stimulation, acupressure, massage therapy, cryotherapy, and exercise, on the restless legs syndrome (RLS) severity score.Methods:Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed for selecting studies to be included in the meta-analysis. The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) registered this study. Many databases, including Google Scholar, Embase, PubMed, and Scopus, were utilized to locate randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The analysis employed Cochrane’s risk of bias tool 2.0 to evaluate the quality of the incorporated RCTs. Standardized mean difference (SMD) figures were employed for all outcomes to assess the impact of every nonpharmacological intervention. The aggregate effect of each was expressed using forest plots. A random-effects model was used for pooling. The heterogeneity of the included studies was evaluated using I2 values. All relevant statistical calculations were carried out using R Studi version 4.3.0.Results:A total of 17 studies were selected for meta-analysis. There was a significant reduction in RLS severity after vibration/stimulation (SMD −1.09, 95% CI −2.45 to −0.27, P = 0.014), and a non-significant trend for acupressure (SMD −0.87, 95% CI −2.21 to 0.47, P = 0.203), massage therapy (SMD −3.49, 95% CI −10.81 to 3.83, P = 0.350), cryotherapy (SMD −1.27, 95% CI −3.72 to 1.18, P = 0.309), and exercise (SMD −2.05, 95% CI −23.95 to 19.84, P = 0.856). There was an improvement in sleep quality measured using the pooled Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index after the nonpharmacological intervention (SMD: -0.51, 95% CI: -0.73 to -0.28). However, this improvement was not found to be significant (P = 0.73).Conclusions:The study demonstrated significant reductions in RLS severity with nonpharmacological interventions, offering viable alternatives to medication. However, the improvement in sleep quality was not statistically significant. Further research is needed to optimize these treatments.

Introduction

The neurological and sleep problem known as restless legs syndrome (RLS) is typified by painful leg sensations and an overwhelming need to move the legs.[1] RLS symptoms usually manifest in the evening and at night, when one is resting or not moving, and they cause disruptions in sleep.[2] RLS is linked to a higher risk of suicide, self-harming behaviors, and medical conditions, as well as daytime sleepiness, lower quality of life, poor memory, cognitive functioning, and decreased work performance.[3456]

Despite the possibility of RLS being primary (genetically connected), it is also linked to several illnesses, such as peripheral neuropathy, kidney disease, iron insufficiency, and neurodegenerative diseases like Parkinson’s disease (PD).[7]

Levodopa was the first medication to be employed for treating RLS in 1982,[8] although, despite its effectiveness, augmentation occurs at all dosages.[8910] Between 2004 and 2009, dopamine agonists such as pramipexole, ropinirole, and rotigotine received approval for the treatment of RLS,[1112] and they are currently utilized in the first-line scenario.[13] However, dopaminergic medications still cause amplification to varying degrees. Neurologists have tried to find alternatives, like α2δ ligands[14] and opioids,[15] or optimize drug use to address this issue. Since 2000, gabapentin, enacarbil, pregabalin, and other medications have been used to treat RLS; however, they are administered less commonly than dopaminergic medications. Since 2010, pregabalin, a relatively new medication, has only been the subject of a small number of clinical investigations.[161718] However, because of their effectiveness and the fact that α2δ ligands have promise for treating RLS because they do not produce enhancement. In 2013, opioids received approval as a second-line treatment for refractory RLS.[19]

The dopaminergic mechanism, iron insufficiency, or genetic imprinting have been the main subjects of studies on the pathogenic processes of RLS.[20212223] Even though dopaminergic medications are effective, nothing is known about how they work to treat RLS. A study using positron emission tomography (PET) revealed that RLS has fewer dopamine transporters,[22] and another PET study suggested that mesolimbic D2/3 receptor binding may be decreased. These findings are corroborated by the finding that pramipexole, a D2/3 receptor agonist, is an effective treatment.[24]

However, postmortem studies of patients with RLS have not shown dopaminergic neurone loss in the substantia nigra (SN) or associated A11 hypothalamic region, which is a characteristic of neurodegenerative disorders like PD that are treated with dopaminergic medications.[2526] The level of iron in the SN was also observed to be lower in RLS,[26272829] in contrast to PD.[30] The central nervous system’s hypoxia pathway is activated by iron deficiency.[31] Tyrosine hydroxylase requires iron as a cofactor, and D2 receptor activity depends on it.[32] RLS may therefore be exacerbated by changes in the dopaminergic systems brought on by iron shortage.

Nineteen RLS risk gene loci were found by a meta-analysis of three genome-wide association studies involving 15,126 patients with RLS.[33] RLS has been linked to rare variations in the Meis homeobox 1 (MEIS1) gene; MEIS1 has been demonstrated to affect the Early Leg Syndrome (ELS) in mice, pointing to a potential mechanism behind the pathophysiology of RLS.[34] The BTB domain-containing 9 (BTBD9) gene has recently come to light as having a function in the pathophysiology of RLS. Mutations in BTBD9 are known to be closely linked to an increased risk of RLS.[35] In contrast, animals that have BTBD9 deletion may exhibit RLS-like symptoms and dopaminergic system malfunction, both of which can lead to the development of RLS.[36] Nevertheless, nothing is known about the mechanisms underlying BTBD9. Additionally, new treatments for RLS are being developed, such as adenosine or glutamatergic therapy.[37]

However, not every patient responds well to these drugs, and side effects are associated with every medicine. Consequently, there is a growing need for nonpharmacological treatment.[38] Pregnancy may make RLS worse, which could result in difficulties, including disturbed sleep. Certain RLS drugs might not be suitable for the developing foetus.[39] Increased medication levels and possible negative effects can result from impaired drug excretion in patients with renal failure. Additionally, several RLS drugs may be nephrotoxic.[40] Patients on dialysis are more likely to develop RLS, and dialysis may have an impact on the drugs used to treat it. Additionally, RLS symptoms may intensify due to some dialysis-related diseases.[41]

Before receiving first medical care, nonpharmacological remedies may be considered, or they may be used if the recommended drug is not effective.[4243]

This study aimed to determine the effect of nonpharmacological treatments like vibration, electric stimulation, acupressure, massage therapy, cryotherapy, and exercise on RLS severity score and sleep quality of patients suffering from RLS.

Information sources and study selection

Many databases, including Google Scholar, Embase, PubMed, and Scopus, were utilized to locate RCTs that were completed no more than 10 years ago as of March 1, 2024. To find new papers, the bibliographies of pertinent articles were also examined.

Search strategy

The search terms used were “restless leg syndrome” or “RLS severity” as an outcome variable and “nonpharmacological interventions” or “Vibration” or “Electrical stimulation” or “Acupuncture” or “Massage” or “Exercise” or “Cryotherapy” as an exposure variable. The research articles were limited to the English language only. The type of articles included was RCTs only.

Selection process

BG, AT, and RG separately identified all the studies, and any discrepancies were resolved with thorough discussion later. Only the studies including patients with pure RLS syndrome were included.

Study risk of bias assessment

The analysis employed Cochrane’s risk of bias tool 2.0 to evaluate the quality of the incorporated RCTs.[61] The instrument evaluates bias resulting from the randomization procedure, bias resulting from deviations from the planned interventions, bias resulting from incomplete outcome data, bias resulting from outcome measurement, and bias resulting from the choice of the published result. By having in-depth discussions, BG and RG evaluated the possibility of prejudice.

Data analysis

Standardized mean difference (SMD) figures were employed for all outcomes to assess the impact of every nonpharmacological intervention. Study characteristics are expressed in Table 1. The aggregate effect of each study was expressed using forest plots. For pooling, a random-effects model (REM) was used. The included studies’ heterogeneity was evaluated using I2 values. All relevant statistical calculations were carried out using R Studio version 4.3.0.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Table 1 lists the attributes of the studies that were included. Among the 17 articles included, 6 are from Iran, 5 from the USA, 2 from Korea, and 1 each from Turkey, Taiwan, Germany, and Australia. RLS was treated with the following types of interventions: exercise (n = 2), massage (n = 2), acupuncture (n = 3), cryotherapy (n = 2), and vibration/stimulation (n = 8).

Risk of bias in studies

The risk of bias is shown in Figure 2. It was found that the overall risk of bias of the included trials was low. Out of all the included trials, 10 studies had a low risk of bias. There were some concerns in 3 of the included studies, and 4 trials had a high risk of bias.

As per the heterogeneity test, P < 0.01 and I2 = 96% were discovered. After that, analysis was done using the REM. The effect of vibration/stimulation on RLS severity was SMD −1.09 (95% CI −2.45 to −0.27) P < 0.05. As a result, the RLS severity score significantly decreased (P < 0.01) after interventional therapy using vibration/stimulation of the specified kinds.

The effect of acupressure on RLS severity was reported in three publications that were part of the current investigation. According to the heterogeneity test, P = 0.11 and I2 = 54% were found. Analysis was then conducted using the REM. The meta-analysis results showed that SMD was − 0.87 (95% CI −2.21 to 0.47). Consequently, following interventional treatment with acupressure of the given types, there was a reduction in the RLS severity score.

The effect of massage therapy on RLS severity was reported in two publications that were part of the current investigation. According to the heterogeneity test, P = 0.04 and I2 = 76% were found. Analysis was then conducted using the REM. The meta-analysis results showed that SMD (Standardised Mean Difference): -0.87 (95% CI -2.21 to 0.47). Consequently, following interventional treatment with massage therapy of the given types, there was a reduction (P < 0.01) in the RLS severity score.

The effect of cryotherapy on RLS severity was reported in two publications that were part of the current investigation. According to the heterogeneity test, P = 0.29 and I2 = 12% were found. Analysis was then conducted using the REM. The meta-analysis results showed that SMD: −1.27 (95% CI −3.72 to 1.18). Consequently, following interventional treatment with cryotherapy of the given types, there was a reduction in the RLS severity score.

The effect of exercise on RLS severity was reported in two publications that were part of the current investigation. According to the heterogeneity test, P < 0.01 and I2 = 89% were found. Analysis was then conducted using the REM. The meta-analysis results showed that SMD: −2.05 (95% CI −23.95 to 19.84), P < 0.01. Consequently, following interventional treatment with exercise of the given types, there was a significant reduction (P < 0.01) in the RLS severity score.

The effect of nonpharmacological interventions on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was reported in five publications that were part of the current investigation [Figure 3.2]. According to the heterogeneity test, P = 0.76 and I2 = 0% were found. Analysis was then conducted using the REM. The meta-analysis results showed that SMD: −0.51 (95% CI −0.73 to − 0.28). Consequently, following nonpharmacological interventional treatment, there was improvement in sleep quality.

Discussion

This meta-analysis was intended to assess the effect of various interventions like vibration, electrical stimulation, acupressure, massage, cryotherapy, and exercise on treating RLS patients. Overall, the analysis comprised a total of 17 RCTs exhibiting the effect of the mentioned interventions on RLS severity and sleep quality.

The majority of studies included patients suffering from primary RLS, while the rest (n = 6) included hemodialysis patients with secondary RLS. RLS severity score was the primary outcome of the study, and sleep quality using the PSQI index was the secondary outcome of the study. Since it was a multinational study, it therefore enhanced the generalizability of the study.

The meta-analysis found a significant improvement in RLS severity score after treatment by vibration, electrical stimulation, acupressure, massage with a variety of oils, cryotherapy, and physical exercises. According to Roy et al.[44] and Bogan et al.,[45] tonic motor activation was found to significantly reduce RLS symptoms. Electronic stimulation (YW Cho et al.) and noninvasive neuromodulation (MJ Buchfuhrer et al.) were effective too.[4647] Vibration therapy (Hosseini H et al., UH Mitchell et al.) has therefore proven to help in reducing RLS severity through this meta-analysis.[4849] Other stimulation strategies like transcranial magnetic stimulation (YS Koo et al., Altunrende B et al.) also lead to a reduction in RLS severity.[5051]

Other than vibration and stimulation, acupressure therapy was found by Tsai et al.,[52] Mohammadi et al.,[53] and Raissi et al.[54] to be beneficial in patients with RLS. A total of 13 bilateral acupoints, including Shenshu (BL23), Ganshu (BL 18), Chengjin (BL 56), Chenshan (BL57), Jinggu (BL 64), Zusanli (ST 36), Liangqiu (ST34), Shangqiu (SP5), Xuehai (SP10), Shanyijiao (SP 6), Taixi (Ki3), Yanglingquan (GB 34), and Xuanzhong (GB 39) were selected.[54] This meta-analysis also found that massage therapy (Oshvandi et al.,[55] Mirbagher Ajorpaz et al.[56]) with a variety of oils reduces symptoms in RLS. Cryotherapy with cold water (H. Jafarimanesh et al.[57]) and reduced temperature (S. Happe et al.[58]) gives relief in aggravating symptoms of RLS. Regular physical activity and supervised exercise (Cederberg et al., Harrison EG et al.)[5960] lead to increased recovery in RLS severity.

PSQI was used to find out the pooled effect of nonpharmacological interventions on the sleep quality of RLS patients before and after treatment. Five articles reported PSQI after nonpharmacological intervention. The meta-analysis of the same found improvement in sleep quality.

Strengths and limitations

This preliminary study attempts to measure the usefulness of multiple nonpharmacological interventions as a treatment for RLS in one study. No other study has been able to perform the same. However, the study was not able to present a pooled meta-analysis of a variety of other indices for RLS severity and sleep quality. Dietary interventions are a major part of nonpharmacological intervention in RLS, which have been completely omitted by the authors. This happened due to the insufficiency of research articles in the mentioned databases about the same.

Future implications

More research articles need to be published to achieve better clarity on the effect of nonpharmacological interventions on RLS patients who are not responding to pharmacological treatment or are unable to receive the same. More interventions with standard indices need to be incorporated in future publications for a better understanding of the same.

Conclusions

The study found that there is a significant improvement in the severity score of RLS upon treatment with various nonpharmacological interventions like vibration, electrical stimulation, acupressure, massage, cryotherapy, and exercise.
