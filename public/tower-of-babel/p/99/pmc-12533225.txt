Polyhydroxybutyrate laminated trilayer films with enhanced barrier and mechanical properties for active food packaging
Authors: Shu Xu, Kuang-Hao Cheng, Andrew Erwin, Meltem Urgun-Demirtas, Wei Chen, Chaoyi Ba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolysaccharides such as starch and chitosan have been explored for biodegradable food packaging films. However, their inherent hydrophilicity and brittleness cause poor water vapor barrier performance and limited mechanical properties. To address these issues, starch was first functionalized with acetoacetate groups and subsequently blended with chitosan. Dynamic cross-linking occurred between the acetoacetate groups and the primary amine groups of chitosan, forming vinylogous urethane linkages that reduced the water solubility of the films while preserving high mechanical strength. With 30 wt% of glycerol as a plasticizer, the resulting films showed a tensile strength of up to 24.68 MPa and an elongation at break of up to 83.96%. To improve the barrier properties, poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (PHB), plasticized with 5–15 wt% epoxidized vegetable oil, was laminated on top of the chitosan/modified starch films, resulting in a significantly reduced water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of 66%. Additional components, such as food colors like β-carotene were incorporated into this layer to make UV-protective films. A third layer of PHB without active additives was then coated to further reduce WVTR by 99%. Therefore, this multilayer lamination strategy effectively enhanced the barrier and mechanical properties of the polysaccharide-based packaging films.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20239-8.Keywords: Biopolymers, Dynamic cross-linking, Active packaging, Polysaccharides, Poly(3-hydroxybutyrate), Multilayer filmsSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Plastic materials play a crucial role in the packaging industry, which accounts for approximately 40% of global plastic production, equivalent to 146 million tons annually1. Despite their widespread use, conventional petroleum-based plastics are environmentally problematic due to their slow degradation and release of harmful chemicals such as microplastics and aldehydes. These growing environmental concerns are driving increased interest in exploring environmentally friendly alternatives, such as bio-based plastics from biomass waste2,3. The global bioplastic food packaging market is projected to reach USD 29.8 billion by 2027 with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 18.2% from 2022 to 2027 4. Meanwhile, approximately 1.3 billion tons of food are wasted annually, which represents one-third of global food production5. The massive food waste crisis presents an unique opportunity to utilize food waste as a renewable feedstock for the production of bioplastic packaging materials6. This approach can reduce reliance on fossil-derived plastic waste while enhancing the circular economy of food resources7,8.

Polysaccharides, the primary component (over 90%) of natural carbohydrates, have been employed as feedstock for food packaging applications9. Their high natural abundance and the efficient extraction process from biomass sources, including agricultural and food waste, contribute to their sustainability and cost-effectiveness10. Among them, starch is a major film-forming polysaccharide, commonly derived from food or food waste sources11. Derived from chitin in seashell waste, chitosan offers good film-forming ability, solubility, and biodegradability12. Blends of starch and chitosan have been widely reported to develop packaging films with improved mechanical properties13. However, the inherent hydrophilic nature of these polysaccharides poses significant challenges, resulting in poor barrier properties against gases and moisture, reduced mechanical strength, low chemical resistance, and limited overall stability compared to traditional petroleum-based plastics14.

To overcome the limitations of polysaccharides for food packaging applications, reversible crosslinking has emerged as an effective approach15. Dynamic covalent bonds can form reversible crosslinking which enables self-healing and reprocessability at elevated temperatures16. Vinylogous urethane linkage formed by reaction between acetoacetate groups and amines is known for building covalent adaptable networks which enhance mechanical properties, thermal stability, and barrier properties of bio-composite films17. Chitosan contains primary amine functional groups, and starch contains hydroxyl groups that can be functionalized into acetoacetates18. Therefore, films prepared from the modified starch and chitosan can leverage the advantages of reversible crosslinking to enhance the performance and durability of biopolymers as biodegradable alternatives to conventional plastics in active food packaging.

Poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (PHB) is a polyester synthesized by bacteria19,20. It has demonstrated biodegradability in multiple receiving environments including freshwater, soil, industrial composting, marine water, and anaerobic conditions21. The application of PHB in food packaging has been well studied and reported in literature22,23. PHB is a semicrystalline polymer and shows excellent barrier properties and comparable mechanical properties to petroleum-based polymers such as low-density polyethylene or polypropylene24. However, the inherent stiffness and brittleness of PHB require the incorporation of plasticizers for producing packaging films with desired flexibility25. Epoxidized soybean oil (ESO) has been reported as an effective plasticizer for improving the elongation at break and thermal stability of PHB films26.

Lamination is commonly used to enhance the barrier properties of packaging films27. By integrating multiple polymer layers, multilayer films can combine the unique characteristics of each polymer component to produce packaging films with enhanced performance28. In particular, combining food waste-derived polysaccharide films with moisture-resistant PHB layers can significantly improve the moisture barrier performance29. In our previous study, chitosan/starch film was laminated with a PHB layer resulting in an 85% reduction in water vapor permeation rate. 30. However, the tensile properties of these laminated films still need improvement for application as packaging materials (e.g., tensile max stress > 10 MPa, elongation at break > 50%). Therefore, a better lamination technique is needed for the lamination of PHB to produce multi-layer films with sufficient structural integrity and durability.

While traditional food packaging research has focused on preserving food quality and extending shelf life, there is a growing trend toward the development of active and intelligent packaging systems31. Active packaging uses active ingredients to interact with the food or its environment to enhance shelf life, whereas intelligent packaging provides real-time information about food quality, such as freshness or spoilage indicators32. Food colorants function as active ingredients by providing visual cues and absorbing certain wavelengths of light. Changes in the chromophore structure, often caused by external stimuli (e.g., pH, temperature, or oxidation), can alter the color33. Carotenoids, known for their orange, red, or yellow colors, have good antioxidant and photoprotective properties through effective reducing and quenching of singlet radicals and oxygen species34. Among them, β-carotene has demonstrated promising antibacterial effects against gram-positive bacteria and has been utilized for active packaging applications35–37.

In this work, we aim to develop multilayer biodegradable films with enhanced barrier and mechanical properties for active food packaging applications. The first layer was fabricated by blending acetoacetate-functionalized starch, chitosan and a plasticizer (glycerol). The composition was optimized to achieve the desired tensile strength and elongation at break. The formation of dynamic covalent networks through vinylogous urethane was confirmed by FTIR analysis. The second layer was formed by laminating a PHB layer, prepared by casting a PHB/chloroform solution containing a small amount of ESO to enhance flexibility. To address the poor interfacial adhesion between the hydrophobic PHB layer and the hydrophilic base layer, a small amount of acetic acid was added to the PHB solution to improve bonding. However, as acetic acid is nonsolvent for PHB, its presence during the chloroform evaporation process induced phase inversion resulting in a porous PHB layer. In addition, β-carotene was incorporated into the second layer to provide a distinct yellow coloration and enhance UV resistance through its photoprotective and antioxidant properties. To further improve the film’s moisture barrier performance, a third dense PHB layer, plasticized with ESO but free of acetic acid and active components, was laminated on top. This outer PHB layer acts as a barrier to moisture and oxygen, while also preventing the migration of internal components such as β-carotene. The resulting trilayer films successfully combine strong mechanical integrity, enhanced moisture and gas barrier properties, and active functionality, offering great potential for use in biodegradable food packaging systems.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. Poly[(R)-3-hydroxybutyrate] (PHB, natural origin), epoxidized soybean oil (ESO), glycerol (> 99.0%), β-carotene (> 93%), chloroform (> 99.5%), and acetic acid (glacial, > 99%) was purchased from Millipore Sigma (St. Louis, MO). Chitosan (CS, flake, low molecular weight) was obtained from Tidal Vision (Bellingham, WA). Native potato starch (PenPure 10) was obtained from Ingredion (Westchester, IL), tert-butyl acetoacetate (t-BAA, 97%) was obtained from Ambeed (Arlington Heights, IL).

Methods

Synthesis of starch-acetoacetate (SAA)

Potato starch (103 g, anhydroglucose unit: 0.63 mol) was dispersed in 100 mL of tert-butyl acetoacetate (95 g, 0.60 mol) in a 250 mL three-necked flask under a nitrogen atmosphere with stirring. The mixture was then heated to 120 °C and maintained for 2 h. During the reaction, a distillation setup was employed to remove the by-product tert-butyl alcohol (t-BuOH). After completion, the products were precipitated with ethanol, washed with ethanol, and dried at 80 °C for 18 h. The final product was obtained as a white powder.

Preparation of chitosan/starch-acetoacetate (CS/SAA) films

Figure 1 illustrates the structure of the laminated trilayer composite film and the components in each layer. To prepare the bottom layer, chitosan was dissolved in 1% v/v acetic acid in deionized (DI) water to obtain a 4% w/w stock solution. Separately, SAA was gelatinized in DI water to form a 2% w/w stock solution. The two stock solutions were then mixed according to the predetermined SAA-to-CS ratio. To each formulation, glycerol was added as a plasticizer, at a content of 30–40% w/w relative to the total dry mass of chitosan and SAA. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 h to ensure homogeneity. The resulting suspension was then cast into a custom-made 6-inch × 6-inch glass mold. The solvent was evaporated at 40 °C for 2 h to form uniform films.

Structure of the laminated trilayer composite films and the components in each layer.

Preparation of PHB/ ESO middle layer with a food coloring agent

PHB was dissolved in chloroform to prepare a 3.2% w/v stock solution. ESO was added as a plasticizer at 10% w/w relative to PHB. To improve adhesion to the underlying hydrophilic film, acetic acid was added at 2% v/v relative to PHB stock solution. Additionally, β-carotene was incorporated as a food-safe coloring agent at 1% w/w relative to PHB. The formulation mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h and the well-mixed suspension was then cast onto the pre-dried CS/SAA film in the 6-inch × 6-inch mold. The solvents were allowed to evaporate at ambient temperature for 1 h to form the PHB/ESO layer.

Preparation of PHB/ ESO top barrier layer

The casting solution was prepared by combining the 3.2% w/v PHB stock solution with ESO at 10% w/w relative to PHB. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h and then cast onto the pre-dried PHB-based middle layer in the 6-inch × 6-inch mold. The solvent was allowed to evaporate at ambient temperature for 1 h to form the outer PHB barrier layer.

Characterization techniques

Film properties were characterized using a range of analytical techniques, including tensile testing38 water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) measurement39 chemical structure analysis via nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR), thermal behavior assessment through thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and morphological evaluation using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Detailed methodologies for these characterization methods are provided in Supplementary Information (SI), Sections S1-9. The procedure of the film biodegradation test in freshwater receiving environment is described in Section S10.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of films

Synthesis of starch acetoacetate (SAA)

As shown in Fig. 2a, SAA was synthesized via solvent-free transesterification of starch hydroxyl groups with t-BAA. The reaction proceeded without the use of a catalyst, indicating the high reactivity of starch toward t-BAA. Successful functionalization was confirmed by 1H-NMR (Fig. 2b), which exhibited characteristic peaks at 2.08 ppm for methyl protons and 3.51 ppm for methylene protons of the acetoacetate group. The anhydroglucose unit (AGU) displayed signals at 3.53–4.0 ppm (CH 2–5, and CH2 6/7) and 5.0–5.2 ppm (CH 1), with hydroxyl proton peaks observed at 5.49 (OH-3), 5.40 (OH-2) and 4.57 (OH-6).

In this study, the DS was determined to be 0.54, indicating that on average 0.54 acetoacetate groups were introduced per AGU. Given that each AGU has three hydroxyl groups available for substitution, this value reflects a moderate level of functionalization, which is suitable for dynamic cross-linking with chitosan. A relatively low degree of cross-linking was intentionally targeted to balance performance and flexibility, minimizing film brittleness while effectively reducing water solubility and enhancing the mechanical strength of the resulting films.

Preparation and characterization of the CS/SAA films

CS/SAA blend films plasticized with glycerol were prepared via solution casting and their properties are summarized in Table 1. To determine the optimal SAA content, four film formulations were prepared with CS: SAA ratios of 100:0, 95:5, 90:10, and 85:15. All films were transparent with a light-yellow color. The WVTR values ranged from 46.7 ± 1.7 to 54.5 ± 0.1 g/h·m2, indicating minimal variation with SAA addition. The corresponding water vapor permeability coefficients (WVTR x thickness) were calculated to be 77.5–95.3 g·mm/m2·day, which was more than 100 times higher than those of typical petroleum-based films40. Therefore, the polysaccharide-based films had poor water vapor barrier properties for food packaging and thus were used as the support films for PHB barrier coatings. Their mechanical properties are critically important in this application.

Tensile testing revealed that the maximum tensile stress of these films decreased with increasing content of SAA from 0% to 15%, attributed to the lower tensile strength of starch41. Similarly, the elongation at break decreased compared to the pure chitosan film, which was likely due to the dynamic crosslinking that restricted chain mobility. Nevertheless, all CS/SAA films maintained tensile stress above 10 MPa, sufficient for typical food packaging applications. Among them, the CS/SAA 90:10 film demonstrated the best flexibility with an elongation at break of 44.30%. To further improve film flexibility, the glycerol content in the 90:10 ratio film increased from 30% to 40%. As expected, the elongation at break improved from 44.3 ± 11.0% to 84.0 ± 6.5% although the tensile stress decreased from 17.7 ± 4.0 MPa to 7.1 ± 0.9 MPa. WVTR values also increased slightly from 46.7 ± 1.7 to 50.8 ± 1.8 g/h·m2 due to increased hydrophilicity from glycerol. Considering all measured properties, the CS: SAA 90:10 film plasticized with 40% glycerol (CS/SAA 90:10 film) was identified as the most promising composition for use in food packaging.

Tensile stress-strain curves (Figure S1) demonstrated a strain hardening effect across all CS/SAA films, consistent with previous observations in starch and carboxymethyl chitosan blend films42. This behavior suggests the presence of crosslinking bonds between chitosan and modified starch in the blend films. At 30% glycerol, the elastic modulus decreased from 594.3 ± 126.5 MPa to 90.2 ± 7.1 MPa as the SAA content increased from 0% to 15% (Table S1). Further increasing glycerol from 30% to 40% reduced the modulus of the CS/SAA 90:10 film from 115.4 ± 58.7 MPa to 7.2 ± 0.4 MPa. These results highlight the significant influence of both SAA and glycerol on film stiffness due to the inherent brittleness of starch and the plasticizing effect of glycerol, which weakens intermolecular interactions between polysaccharide chains in dried films.

To further verify the presence of cross-linking structures, a gel fraction test was conducted to assess the degree of cross-linking in the films. All samples were immersed in milli-Q water at ambient temperature for 24 h. The insoluble fractions were collected by filtration, followed by drying in an oven to determine the remaining mass. As shown in Table 1, the pure CS film completely dissolved, whereas the CS/SAA blend films retained insoluble fractions, confirming the presence of cross-linking networks. The relatively low gel fraction values suggest partial cross-linking, likely due to the reversible interactions between starch acetoacetate and chitosan. These dynamic bonds became unstable at elevated temperatures as all films were observed to dissolve in water at 80 °C. This thermal reversibility implies that the CS/SAA blend films can be reprocessed or recycled after initial use, offering a potential route toward more sustainable and circular material applications.

Preparation and characterization of the PHB/ESO blend films

A series of PHB films plasticized with ESO was prepared and their WVTR and tensile properties are summarized in Table 2. With increasing addition of ESO plasticizer from 3% to 10% (by weight relative to PHB), the WVTR slightly increased from 0.48 ± 0.06 to 1.05 ± 0.18 g/h·m2. However, further increases in ESO content from 10% to 30% resulted in no significant change in WVTR. This trend suggests that the ESO phase, being slightly more permeable to moisture than PHB, contributed modestly to moisture transport, particularly at lower loading levels. Notably, all PHB/ESO films exhibited WVTR values approximately 50 times lower than those of the CS/SAA films, confirming their effectiveness as moisture barrier layers. The corresponding water vapor permeability coefficients (WVTR x thickness) were calculated to be in the range of 0.30–0.98 g·mm/m2·day, comparable to those of typical petroleum-based films such as polyethylene (0.1–0.59 g·mm/m2·day)40. As shown in Figure S2, the contact angles of the standalone CS/SAA film (90:10 ratio, plasticized with 40% glycerol) and the PHB/ESO film (100:10 ratio) are 80.7° and 95.1–97.0°, respectively. The higher contact angle confirms that the PHB/ESO film is more hydrophobic than the CS/SAA film.

In terms of mechanical performance, the maximum tensile stress of PHB/ESO films gradually decreased from 34.5 ± 2.2 MPa (0% ESO) to 11.3 ± 1.7 MPa (30% ESO) while the elongation at break increased from 1.8 ± 0.1% to 6.6 ± 0.7% with increasing ESO from 0% to 10%. Beyond this point, additional ESO did not lead to further improvement in elongation. The elastic modulus also decreased from 2668.0 ± 275.8 MPa at 0% ESO to 831.6 ± 33.6 MPa at 10% ESO due to the plasticizing effect (Table S2). These results suggest that the PHB/ESO film with 100:10 ratio offers the best combination of mechanical flexibility, adequate tensile strength and stiffness, and low WVTR, making it a promising candidate for use as a moisture barrier layer in food packaging applications26.

Bilayer films prepared via lamination

A lamination method was utilized to combine chitosan-based films with a biodegradable moisture-resistant PHB-based film. The CS/SAA film (90:10 ratio, plasticized with 40% glycerol) was first prepared with a thickness of approximately 50 μm. To achieve strong adhesion with the PHB/ESO (100:10 ratio), acetic acid was added to the PHB/ESO solution in chloroform as it can swell the surface of the underlying CS/SAA layer, promoting interdiffusion and bonding. Upon drying, the two layers bonded tightly to form a bilayer film30.

As summarized in Table 3, the bilayer film exhibited an elongation at break of 33.0 ± 6.3%, an intermediate value between that of the individual CS/SAA (84.0 ± 6.5%, Table 1) and PHB/ESO (6.6 ± 0.7%, Table 2) films. No delamination was observed during the tensile testing, indicating a strong interface bond. The maximum tensile stress was 6.0 ± 0.6 MPa, comparable to that of the CS/SAA film (7.1 ± 0.9 MPa, Table 1). Notably, the WVTR of the bilayer film was reduced by 66% compared to the single-layer CS/SAA 90:10 film, dropping from 50.8 to 17.3 g/h·m2. This result confirms that the PHB lamination significantly improved the moisture barrier properties of the CS/SAA films. However, the WVTR value of the bilayer film remained substantially higher than that of the standalone PHB/ESO film (1.05 ± 0.18 g/h·m2). This discrepancy is likely due to partial porosity induced in the PHB/ESO layer during drying, as acetic acid acts as a nonsolvent for PHB, causing phase inversion and microvoid formation. These findings suggest that incorporating an additional dense PHB top layer may be necessary to further reduce WVTR and fully realize the moisture barrier potential of the multilayer film for food packaging.

Trilayer films prepared via lamination

To make a dense top layer, a PHB/ESO (100:10 ratio) casting solution was prepared without acetic acid and applied on top of the bilayer film. Two trilayer films were fabricated with top layer thickness of approximately 25 μm (trilayer-1) and 50 μm (trilayer-2), respectively. To further demonstrate the barrier performance and potential functionality of the trilayer films, a food colorant, β-carotene, was incorporated into the middle PHB/ESO layer, yielding active packaging films with a light orange color.

As shown in Table 3, the trilayer-1 and trilayer-2 films exhibited significantly reduced WVTR values of 0.96 ± 0.14 and 0.56 ± 0.06 g/h·m2, respectively, comparable to the standalone PHB/ESO film (1.05 ± 0.18 g/h·m2). The trilayer-1 film showed a maximum tensile stress of 11.1 ± 0.8 MPa and an elongation at break of 45.0 ± 3.5%, outperforming the bilayer film (6.0 ± 0.6 MPa and 33.0 ± 6.3%) and closely matching the standalone PHB/ESO film (13.3 ± 3.3 MPa). In contrast, the trilayer-2 film showed a significantly reduced elongation at break of 11.1 ± 3.0% and a lower tensile strength of 7.9 ± 1.6 MPa, indicating increased brittleness due to the thicker PHB/ESO top layer. These results suggest that a top layer thickness greater than 25 μm may compromise film flexibility. In addition, as shown in Table S3, the bilayer film exhibited an elastic modulus of 145.2 ± 12.7 MPa, significantly higher than that of the support film (7.2 ± 0.4 MPa). The trilayer-1 and trilayer-2 films demonstrated even greater moduli of 267.0 ± 28.2 MPa and 377.4 ± 183.2 MPa, respectively, confirming that the incorporation of PHB/ESO layers enhanced the stiffness of the composite films. Overall, the trilayer-1 (trilayer) film offers the optimal balance of mechanical strength, flexibility, and moisture resistance for effective and sustainable food packaging applications.

Characterization of the multilayer films

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal stability of the multilayer films was evaluated by TGA (Fig. 3a). The CS/SAA 90:10 film showed an initial weight loss of approximately 15% at 120 °C, corresponding to the evaporation of absorbed moisture. Subsequent thermal degradation occurred at 200–300 °C, primarily due to the decomposition of chitosan, resulting in a 44% char residue at 500 °C. In comparison, The PHB/ESO 100:10 film showed significantly higher thermal stability, with an onset degradation temperature of 265 °C, attributing to β-elimination of ester linkages in the PHB backbone43. The residue at 500 °C was minimal (0.5%), indicating nearly complete thermal degradation.

(a) TGA, (b) DSC, (c) XRD, and (d) FTIR spectra of the films.

The bilayer film displayed a combination of degradation behaviors including an initial moisture loss of 17% at 120 °C, followed by distinct degradation steps associated with chitosan and PHB, resulting in a residue mass of 16% at 500 °C. This residue reflects the composite nature of the bilayer film and partial thermal stability contributed by the chitosan component. The trilayer film exhibited a similar degradation profile to the bilayer film, with initial moisture loss of 3–7% at 120 °C, followed by sequential degradation of the chitosan and PHB layers. These results indicate that the incorporation of PHB slightly enhanced the thermal stability of the multilayer films, making them suitable for moderate thermal processing.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal transition behaviors of these films were analyzed by DSC, with the second heating ramp from − 40 °C to 180 °C displayed in Fig. 3b. For the CS/SAA 90:10 film, no glass transition or melting phase transition was observed, consistent with the literature reports on glycerol-plasticized polysaccharide films44. This behavior suggests an amorphous structure with high chain mobility and no significant crystalline domains. In contrast, the standalone PHB/ESO 100:10 film exhibited a single melting point at 173 °C, confirming that PHB maintained its semi-crystalline nature despite the presence of ESO. The high crystallinity accounts for the excellent moisture barrier properties and inherent brittleness of the PHB/ESO films.

For the bilayer film, a noticeable decrease in melting temperature was observed, with two distinct melting peaks appearing at 153 °C and 162 °C. This double melting behavior is characteristic of PHB and has been attributed to recrystallization and remelting during the heating process as well as crystallographic polymorphism in PHB structures45. Additionally, thermal degradation of PHB during melt processing can generate species with a broader molar mass distribution, further contributing to this behavior46. The reduced peak intensity suggests a lower degree of crystallinity compared to the standalone PHB/ESO film, likely due to the presence of microvoids. In the trilayer film, a broader melting transition in the range of 162–166 °C was observed, with a larger peak area compared to the bilayer film. This indicates a higher degree of crystallinity in the dense top PHB layer, consistent with the improved moisture barrier performance observed in the trilayer films relative to the bilayer configuration.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The XRD patterns of these films are presented in Fig. 3c. The CS/SAA 90:10 film showed characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 11.2°, 16.5°, and 18.2°, corresponding to the semi-crystalline structure of chitosan films. Additionally, several broad peaks in the 10° − 50° range indicated the presence of the amorphous regions, consistent with the plasticized nature of the polysaccharide matrix. For the PHB/ESO 100:10 film, distinct peaks were observed at 2θ = 13.6°, 16.0°, and 30.4°, which are characteristic of the semicrystalline structure of PHB. These peaks confirm the retention of crystallinity in the PHB phase, even in the presence of ESO.

For the bilayer and trilayer films, the diffraction signals were dominated by the PHB/ESO layers, and characteristic chitosan peaks were no longer detectable, likely due to the overlay and relative mass ratio of the PHB layers. The PHB peaks at 2θ = 13.6° and 30.4° were still observed but showed reduced intensity, proportional to the content of PHB in the entire film. Notably, the relative intensity of peaks at 2θ = 16.7° and 20.3° increased in the multilayer films. These peaks were related to the (020) and (110) planes in the PHB blends with ESO26. Moreover, the peak intensity at 2θ = 22.8° in the trilayer film was slightly higher compared to the standalone PHB/ESO film. This peak has been previously associated with the β-crystalline form of PHB47 which may result from interfacial interactions and constrained crystallization in the multilayer structures. The emergence of this β-phase peak may also explain the dual melting transitions observed in the DSC thermograms. These results confirm that lamination and multilayer design not only preserve but also modify the crystalline structure of PHB, with potential implications for the thermal and mechanical performance of the packaging films.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The characteristic functional groups of the films were identified using FTIR, as shown in Fig. 3d. In the CS/SAA 90:10 film, an absorption band at 1736 cm− 1 was attributed to the C = O stretching of the acetoacetate group, while a distinct peak at 1643 cm− 1 corresponded to the C = N stretching of the vinylogous urethane linkage. These characteristic peaks confirmed the formation of reversible vinylogous urethane dynamic bonds. An additional band at 1545 cm− 1 was assigned to the symmetric bending vibration of the protonated NH3+ group from the primary amines in chitosan48.

In the PHB/ESO 100:10 film, characteristic absorption bands of PHB were observed. These included C-H stretching at 2963 and 2922 cm− 1, C = O stretching at 1722 cm− 1, CH2 bending at 1452 cm− 1, CH3 bending at 1380 cm− 1, CH bending at 1278 cm− 1, and C-O-C stretching at 1181 cm− 1. These spectral features are consistent with literature-reported spectra for pure PHB29,49. In addition, a small shoulder peak at approximately 1740 cm− 1 was attributed to the stretching of triglyceride carbonyl groups from ESO50confirming the presence of the plasticizer in the PHB matrix. The FTIR spectra of the multilayer films (bilayer and trilayer) showed no significant differences from the PHB/ESO film, as the PHB/ESO layer constituted the outer surface in both configurations.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

Figure 4 presents the surface and cross-sectional SEM images of the bilayer and trilayer films. The surface of the PHB/ESO layer in the bilayer film (Fig. 4a) appeared relatively rough, with irregular nodular structures and small pores. These features were likely a result of phase inversion during film formation caused by acetic acid. In contrast, the surface of the PHB/ESO layer in the trilayer film (Fig. 4d) was dense and smooth. These morphological differences correspond to the observed differences in moisture barrier performance between the two films. The CS/SAA surfaces in both the bilayer (Fig. 4b) and the trilayer (Fig. 4e) films showed rough textures, consistent with the hydrogel-like nature and inherent heterogeneity of plasticized polysaccharide films. Cross-sectional SEM images confirmed the layered architecture of the films. The bilayer film displayed a well-defined interface between the CS/SAA layer (~ 41 μm) and the PHB/ESO layer (~ 50 μm), with visible microvoids within the PBH/ESO layer. In the trilayer film, a clear three-layer structure was observed, consisting of a CS/SAA base layer (~ 60 μm), a PHB/ESO middle layer (~ 51 μm), and a PHB/ESO top layer (~ 15 μm). These morphological observations support the mechanical and barrier property data, emphasizing the importance of surface density and layer integrity in achieving high-performance biodegradable packaging films.

Preparation of active packaging films with food color additive

To evaluate the protective capability of the multilayer structure, a standalone PHB/ESO film containing β-carotene and a trilayer film with β-carotene incorporated in the middle layer were prepared and subjected to thermal and sunlight exposure tests. The results are presented in Fig. 5. When exposed to heat at 80 °C or sunlight at ambient temperature for just one day, the single-layer film became discolored and transparent, indicating significant degradation of β-carotene. In contrast, the trilayer film showed little color change even after one month of exposure to the same conditions.

pictures of (a) single-layer PHB/ESO/β-carotene films and (b) trilayer films with β-carotene in the middle layer, and their color change under thermal oxidative or sunlight treatments.

β-carotene is known to be more resistant to thermal degradation than to oxidative degradation by oxygen51. Elevated temperatures alone do not significantly degrade it, while molecular oxygen can oxidize β-carotene through a free radical mechanism to produce low molecular weight carbonyl-containing degradation products52. The enhanced stability of β-carotene in the trilayer film may be attributed to its encapsulation in the middle layer, where it is protected from direct exposure to oxygen. These findings also highlight that the trilayer films had excellent barrier performance to oxygen, which is another essential feature for food packaging applications. Under sunlight, β-carotene is prone to isomerization into multiple cis isomers which leads to the discoloration53. However, with shielding layers on both sides in the trilayer film, the β-carotene in the middle layer became more stable as PHB has low UV transmittance and thus functions as an effective UV barrier54.

Overall, the trilayer films demonstrated strong protective capabilities for active ingredients during food storage. With the addition of β-carotene or other active ingredients in the middle layer, the multilayer films have the potential to offer antioxidant, antibacterial, and photoprotective functionalities, making them promising candidates for active food packaging applications.

Biodegradability of the trilayer film in freshwater receiving environments

The biodegradation test of the trilayer film was conducted in freshwater receiving environments following ISO 14,851 standard method. Biodegradable glucose and cellulose were used as positive controls. According to the standard, the biodegradation of the positive control must exceed 60% by the end of the test for the results to be considered valid. As shown in Fig. 6, glucose reached 100% biodegradation within 10 days, while cellulose achieved ~ 100% biodegradation after 30 days, confirming validity of the experiment setup. Under the same test conditions, the trilayer film reached ~ 98% biodegradation by the end of the test. Through first-order kinetics (Figure S3), the trilayer film exhibits a half-life of 5.2 days and an end-of-lifetime, defined as the time to reach 90% biodegradation, of 18.6 days in freshwater (Table S4). These results demonstrate that the trilayer films are highly biodegradable in freshwater environments. This is expected because the major film-forming components, chitosan, starch, and PHB, are all known to degrade readily under environmental conditions.

Conclusions

Multilayer films were successfully developed by laminating two ESO-plasticized PHB layers onto a CS/SAA base film. The CS/SAA layer formed reversible vinylogous urethane linkages, as confirmed by FTIR and gel fraction analysis, providing dynamic crosslinking and mechanical support. A CS: SAA 90:10 ratio plasticized with 40% glycerol yielded optimal tensile strength (7.1 ± 0.9 MPa) and elongation at break (84.0 ± 6.5%), making it suitable as the support layer in multilayer structures. The trilayer films exhibited significantly enhanced moisture barrier performance, comparable to polyethylene, due to the dense and semi-crystalline structure of the top PHB/ESO layer. XRD and DSC analyses confirmed the presence of crystalline PHB, while SEM images revealed well-defined layer interfaces and a smooth, non-porous surface. Incorporation of β-carotene into the middle layer demonstrated excellent color stability under heat and sunlight, highlighting the films’ strong UV and oxygen barrier capabilities. Furthermore, the trilayer film achieved ~ 98% biodegradation within 30 days in freshwater, confirming its environmental sustainability. These findings demonstrate that the trilayer films possess the required mechanical strength, barrier functionality, and biodegradability for advanced active food packaging applications. This multilayer approach represents a promising strategy to reconcile the environmental benefits of bio-based degradable materials with the performance requirements of modern packaging applications.
