Laser microdissection-based multi-omics integration unveils the pathological atlas and tumor differentiation network of adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma
Authors: Bowen Wu, Chunming Xu, Chenxing Yang, Shenhao Xie, Suping Duan, Jiye Ye, Jinshi Zhang, Shaoyang Li, Furong Liang, Zhicheng Ye, Hai Luo, Rilege Su, Laisheng Pan, Zhizheng Liu, Shizhou Xing, Tianming Dong, Yiguang Chen, Like Chen, Suyue Zheng, Jie Wu, Tao Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma (ACP) is a histologically benign intracranial tumor, its invasive growth pattern, characterized by finger-like protrusions, leads to a poor prognosis. In-depth analysis of the heterogeneity of the pathological structure of ACP and the development and evolution of tumor cells is crucial for expanding precision treatment strategies. However, a detailed understanding of the complex pathological structural characteristics of ACP is lacking. Here, we used laser microdissection to obtain 40 samples from 6 different pathological structures for Smart-Seq2 sequencing and conducted in-depth analysis of 6 single-nucleus RNA sequencing samples and 4 spatial transcriptome samples. We also performed comprehensive validation using 3 external single-cell databases. Through histopathological observation, multiomic analysis, and immunofluorescence staining, we resolved the single-cell expression profiles of two distinct functional subpopulations of the whorl-like cell clusters, providing insights into their potential biological roles in tumor progression. Additionally, we discovered the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) or partial EMT development direction of the stellate reticulum and different cell fates related to terminal keratinization. Moreover, we reported the presence of previously neglected cyst-wall epithelium and the constitutive epithelium within ACP. The latter was found to possess basal cell characteristics and act as a potential progenitor cell pool for tumor maintenance and evolution. Through pseudotime analysis combined with co-expression network analysis, we constructed a differentiation regulatory network based on the constitutive epithelium. Our research results complement existing knowledge and provide a comprehensive analysis of the pathological features of ACP, providing new concepts and targets for precision targeted therapy. Introduction

Adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma (ACP) is a rare intracranial epithelial tumor that originates from the remnants of Rathke’s pouch and is among the most common intracranial non-neuroepithelial tumors in children [1–4]. Owing to its frequent involvement of the hypothalamic–pituitary axis, patients often suffer from severe endocrine and hypothalamic dysfunction [2, 5–7]. Currently, surgical resection is the preferred treatment for ACP. Although proton beam therapy and radiotherapy have been proven effective for residual lesions after surgery [2, 7], their application is mostly limited to adjuvant therapy because of radiation-related side effects and limited efficacy [2, 8]. At the histological level, ACP often forms finger-like protrusions that invade normal structures such as the pituitary stalk and hypothalamus [5, 6, 9, 10], making complete surgical resection more difficult and resulting in a high postoperative recurrence risk and postoperative neurological damage and endocrine disorders [11]. Given its invasive biological behavior, the treatment strategy for ACP should be guided by the principles of precision medicine for malignant tumors; thus, it is critically important to systematically analyze its pathogenesis and tumor characteristics.

In terms of histological structure, ACP shows significant heterogeneity. The typical pathological features include wet keratin (KRT), palisading epithelium (PE), stellate reticulum (SR), and whorl-like cell clusters (WC) [1, 12]. Among them, keratin is a common pathological structure in ACP and is considered not only one of the factors contributing to increased tumor volume but may also participate in calcification formation [13–17]. WC contains stem cells with β-catenin nuclear aggregation and is speculated to regulate the biological behavior of surrounding cells through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms [18, 19]. The PE exhibits stress adaptation characteristics and has strong proliferative potential [20]. Using laser microdissection technology, Apps JR revealed that WC is enriched in genes related to enamel knot, whereas the PE possesses the molecular phenotype of cap-stage enamel epithelium [12]. However, that research failed to capture the panoramic view of various pathological structures of the tumor, making it a key aspect that needs to be supplemented urgently in this field. Our previous single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) study also revealed high heterogeneity within ACP and identified potential epithelial groups involved in terminal keratinization [17]. Wang X first reported the existence of cell clusters with germinal center characteristics within tumors using spatial transcriptomic (ST) technology [21]. Although bioinformatics studies have fully confirmed the existence of different molecular expression characteristics of cell populations in ACP [17, 20, 22, 23], current cell type identification methods based on single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) still have limitations. The conventional analysis strategy usually classifies a certain cell subgroup as a specific pathological structure as a whole. This “one group for one structure” classification method may not fully reflect the potential cell type diversity within the same subgroup, and clearly explaining the developmental association between different pathological structures remains difficult. This situation highlights the necessity of systematically analyzing the cell states corresponding to each pathological structure at a higher resolution level.

In this study, we performed a morphology-guided laser microdissection technique combined with Smart-Seq2 to obtain the gene expression profiles of the main pathological structures of ACP and integrated ST and snRNA-seq data to analyze the cell clusters corresponding to the pathological features at the single-cell level. Moreover, we collected three sets of ACP snRNA-seq or scRNA-seq transcriptomic data from public databases for external validation. This study innovatively revealed the unique molecular properties of each pathological structure, systematically depicted the evolutionary path from tumor progenitor cells to different structures, and successfully constructed an integrated multiomic atlas of ACP, providing a theoretical basis and target clues for the development of precise treatment strategies for this disease.

Materials and methods

ACP sample collection

Forty Smart-Seq2 samples were collected from six patients in the Neurosurgery Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. All patients were diagnosed with ACP based on the pathological examination after surgery. The specimens were immediately stored in liquid nitrogen after they were removed via surgery. The snRNA-seq and ST data were from our previous research [17] and have been deposited in the NCBI database (GSE215932). This study followed the ethical standards set by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients prior to the study.

External datasets

Three external validation data were used in this study: the scRNA-seq dataset downloaded from Zenodo (DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7199080) [20], the scRNA-seq dataset downloaded from GEO (GSE292083) [22], and the snRNA-seq dataset obtained from Chen Y [23]. In addition, a pituitary snRNA-seq dataset (GSE178452) [24] was used as a reference control for copy number variation analysis.

Laser microdissection

The slide was inverted and placed in a Leica LMD7 laser microdissection system. The target pathological structure was identified under a 5x objective lens, and it was ensured that the structure was represented in at least three consecutive sections. A 20x objective lens was used for cutting. To avoid damaging the target cell clusters, the laser trajectory slightly extended beyond the actual cell boundary, ensuring that the laser passed closely adjacent to the cells to be cut. Three to ten cell clusters were taken from the same position of the consecutive sections (depending on the total area), and the total number of collected cells did not exceed 500. The cut cell clusters fell by gravity into a 0.2-ml RNase-free PCR tube cap. Then, 12 µl of lysis buffer was added (purchased from BGI, China) to completely cover and evenly distribute the cell clusters on the tube cap. After a 10-minute reaction, the sample was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 30 s at 4 degrees Celsius and then frozen on dry ice for storage. Detailed information regarding material preparation is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Smart-Seq2 library construction, sequencing, and expression preprocessing

In the Smart-Seq2 analysis, RNA samples were used as the template and amplified using the Discover-sc WTA Kit V2 (VAZYME, China). The resulting product was then subjected to cDNA synthesis via reverse transcription with Oligo dT primers, followed by final amplification into full-length cDNA products using Smart-Seq2 technology. The qualified amplified cDNA then served as input for the final library construction using a transposon-based method. Finally, sequencing reads with a PE length of 100 bases were generated on the T7 platform using combinatorial probe-anchor synthesis. The aforementioned experimental procedures were performed by BGI, China. The raw sequencing data were aligned to the human reference genome (NCBI/GCF_000001405.39_GRCh38.p13) to generate gene quantification files. Gene expression levels were normalized using FPKM and TPM to account for sequencing depth and gene length. The expression matrix was filtered by removing genes with NA expression values and retaining only those genes whose expression was greater than zero in at least 75% of the samples. The detailed experimental procedures and library construction protocols for both snRNA-seq and ST are described in the Methods section of our previous publication [17].

Basic bioinformatics analyses

Routine analyses were conducted in R, Python, and Linux environments using established frameworks: DESeq2 [25] and edgeR [26, 27] for differential expression; gene set variation analysis (GSVA) [28] for pathway scoring; clusterProfiler [29, 30] and Metascape [31] for functional enrichment; Seurat[32] for snRNA-seq and ST processing; and DoubletFinder [33] for snRNA-seq quality control. Additional analyses included cell-cell communication (CellPhoneDB) [34], transcription factor analysis (decoupleR) [35], temporal analysis (Monocle2 [36], Monocle3 [37], RNA velocity38), developmental potential analysis (CytoTRACE) [39], and network analysis (weighted gene co-expression network analysis [40] and high dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis [41]), among others. Further details are available in Supplementary Material 1.

Identification of phenotype-associated cell subpopulations with the Scissor algorithm

The Scissor algorithm [42] was applied to identify cell subpopulations related to specific pathological structures in tumor snRNA-seq data. The previously defined binary pathological phenotypes were used (e.g., whether it was of the WC structure or not, yes vs. no) as input, and the Smart-Seq2 expression profiles were mapped onto an annotated tumor single-cell expression matrix. The mapping results for different pathological structures were subsequently integrated into a unified single-cell matrix, and cells marked as having multiple phenotypes were excluded to ensure that each cell was uniquely associated with one pathological structure. Ultimately, a single-nucleus dataset with pathological structure as a group annotation was constructed.

Cell type deconvolution with robust cell type decomposition

To conduct cell type deconvolution analysis on the ST data, we used the robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) algorithm [43], aiming to resolve or validate the distribution of different pathological structures in spatial situational contexts. The analysis was built upon a reference dataset based on the annotated snRNA-seq (or scRNA-seq) data to estimate the relative proportions of each spatial spot. The deconvolution results of cell type proportions were integrated back into the spatial transcriptomic objects, enabling direct visualization of the spatial distribution patterns for each pathological structure. Based on the RCTD results, we further performed bivariate Moran’s I analysis using the MERINGUE R package to assess the spatial colocalization [44]. Detailed procedures are described in Supplementary Material 1.

Acquisition of functional gene sets

To investigate the functions of different pathological characteristics, phenotypic gene sets from the CancerSEA [45] database were used, and only the positive genes were selected as characteristic genes. Gene sets for partial epithelial–mesenchymal transition (pEMT) and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) were consistent with those used in our previous study [17]. The genes in the basal cell carcinoma signature, WNT signaling pathway, and MYC target gene sets were adapted from “BASAL_CELL_CARCINOMA” (KEGG: hsa05217), “WNT_SIGNALING_PATHWAY” (KEGG: hsa04310), and “MYC_TARGETS_V2” (MSigDB Hallmark: M5928), respectively. We also obtained gene sets for WC and the PE from an earlier laser capture microdissection study [12] to validate our cell subpopulation classification. The gene sets are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

The ACP tissues obtained through surgery were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. After dehydration, the samples were paraffin-embedded to prepare 3-µm sections. The paraffin sections were dewaxed in water and subjected to antigen retrieval in EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH 8.0) in a microwave. The sections were subsequently blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated sequentially with different types of primary antibodies and corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies to achieve multiple immunofluorescence labeling. All primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Finally, the cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the sections were mounted with anti-quenching mounting medium and observed and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. The specific indicators, production companies, and working concentrations of all primary antibodies used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Material 3.

Results

Establishment of the pathological architecture in ACP and identification of a novel cell population

In this study, we described a previously undefined cell population: unlike the PE composed of closely packed columnar cells, the SR formed by low aggregates of stellate cells, the KRT nodules containing anuclear ghost cells, and the WC arranged in nodular whorls, this group consists of medium‑sized cells lacking typical morphological characteristics. These cells are usually located beneath the PE, and are frequently found surrounding WC and SR. This group of cells is moderately dense, with blunt, round, and deeply stained nuclei, and is contiguous with the surrounding epithelial cell layer. Given their relatively unremarkable morphological appearance and function as filler cells within the tumor space, we refer to them as the constitutive epithelium (CE) (Fig. 1A).

Additionally, we observed that cystic changes in ACP occur not only at the macroscopic level but also at the histological level, manifested by tumor cells forming multiple cystic areas. We confirmed these as microcysts rather than ducts through consecutive sections (Supplementary Material 4). The cells of the wall layer of these microcysts can present with a tall columnar (resembling PE) or cuboidal epithelial appearance. Therefore, we systematically described this wall cell population for the first time and named it the cyst-wall epithelium (CW) (Fig. 1A).

In subsequent analyses, based on this novel morphological classification, we performed laser microdissection, multi-omics profiling, and multiplex immunofluorescence staining to systematically validate the molecular and functional heterogeneity of the two newly defined pathological structures and others, thereby establishing a complete evidence loop of “morphological observation–bioinformatics inference–molecular verification”.

Heterogeneity and interrelationships of pathological structures: A high-resolution transcriptomic atlas based on Smart-Seq2

After strict quality control of the laser microdissection domains and RNA, we retained 40 laser microdissection samples from 6 patients, including 6 pathological features: PE, SR, WC, CW, KRT, and CE (Fig. 1B and C). The clustering heatmap indicated significant heterogeneity among the pathological structures (Fig. 1D). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed a grey module specifically enriched in apoptosis regulation, DNA replication regulation and repair in the CE, and a magenta module in the SR for cell fate regulation, oncogenic signaling pathway regulation, and sphingolipid signaling pathway. The greenyellow, blue, and red modules in both the CE and the SR represent antigen presentation and immune activation, cell cycle, keratinization and extracellular matrix interaction. Genes upregulated in the WC were involved mainly in epithelial-derived tissue mineralization, stemness and the WNT pathway, and malignant tumor association, belonging to the green, yellow, pink, and turquoise modules, respectively. The black module, which was specifically located in the CW, was significantly enriched in immune regulation functions, whereas the brown module, which was located in both the PE and the CW, was significantly enriched in extracellular matrix remodeling and stress response (Fig. 1E, and Suppl. Figure S1).

We found functional similarities among some pathological structures in the co-expression network. Coincidentally, Spearman correlation analysis based on the global gene expression profile also revealed high similarity at the transcriptional level between the PE and CW and between the CE and SR (Suppl. Figure S1). Additionally, the enrichment results for high-confidence DEGs (for DEGs of intergroup comparisons between structures, refer to Supplementary Material 5) obtained through strict intergroup comparisons not only confirmed the conclusions of WGCNA but also revealed an exploratory finding: the expression patterns of a few genes in the CE region suggested a possible association with organ development-related pathways such as GDNF-RET signaling and glycosylation processes (for additional enrichment results, refer to Suppl. Figure S2). Although this result was driven by a limited number of genes, its potential significance warrants further exploration.

Systematic pathological characterization based on a Smart-Seq2 integrated multiomic strategy

By using the expression profiles of various pathological structures obtained through laser microdissection as phenotypic anchors, we successfully located the corresponding tumor cell subpopulations in the snRNA-seq data using the Scissor algorithm (Fig. 2A–D). Evidently, the subpopulations obtained by conventional clustering and those identified by Scissor algorithm exhibited overlapping and nested relationships. Thus, a “one group for one structure” conventional classification method would likely result in the loss of biological information. Further differential expression analysis (Supplementary Material 6), integrating the DEGs from Smart-seq2 data, successfully defined the core marker genes of each microscopic pathological structure: WC-positive (WC+) cells were marked by AJAP1, NOTUM, TGFA, PTPRE, LEF1, etc.; PE-positive (PE+) cells were associated with NR4A1, etc.; and the SR-positive (SR+) cells specifically expressed FTL, TAGLN etc. The unique marker genes of CE-positive (CE+) cells included ERBB4, KRT19, KRT15, ANXA1, ANXA2 and FAM20A, among others. Additionally, genes such as CD47 and WIF1 were specifically expressed in both PE+ and CW-positive (CW+) cells (Fig. 2E and F). The expression patterns of these key marker genes were confirmed through multiplex immunofluorescence staining experiments (Fig. 2G, and Supplementary Material 7).

Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis of each subpopulation revealed that CE+ cells were related to basal polarity and adhesion–skeleton assembly, CW+ cells were specifically enriched in the epidermal spreading process, PE+ cells were involved in both transcriptional regulation and ion channel activity, and SR+ cells were closely related to cytoskeletal reorganization and keratinization function (for volcano plots and additional enrichment results, refer to Suppl. Figure S3 and S4). Notably, the correlation analysis at the single-cell level reproduced the association patterns observed among pathological structures in the laser microdissection data and further revealed the expression similarity between KRT-positive (KRT+) cells and other structural cells (Fig. 2H). Cell-cell communication analysis revealed that WC+ cells emitted the greatest number of ligand–receptor signals and occupied a core position in the intercellular communication network of the entire tumor microenvironment (Fig. 2I and J). Furthermore, through the analysis of genome-wide copy number variations (CNVs), we found that chromosome 15 (chr15) amplification events were present in the majority of WC+ cells (Fig. 2K; similar results were obtained using the pituitary dataset as a reference, see Suppl. Figure S5). These two independent findings suggest that WC+ cells are not only communication centers in the tumor microenvironment but also hotspots of genomic instability.

To validate the reliability of the cell subpopulations mapped by Scissor, we adopted three independent strategies for verification. First, we deconvoluted this group of Scissor-defined subpopulations back to the ST using the RCTD algorithm, and the results revealed that their spatial localization was highly consistent with the pathological structures defined during LMD (Suppl. Figure S6). Second, we used the marker gene set obtained from the intersection of LMD structures to score the Scissor subpopulations with AddModuleScore, and the violin plots indicated that the specific high expression of the gene set was largely confined to the target subpopulations (Suppl. Figure S7). Third, we used the published gene sets of WC and PE characteristics from Apps JR’s research to score the subpopulations, and the results were highly consistent with the cell subgroups we defined (Suppl. Figure S8). These three validations from the dimensions of spatial location and transcriptional profile characteristics jointly confirmed the accuracy of the Scissor mapping results, laying a solid foundation for all subsequent analyses based on these subpopulations. For Scissor mapping results in other datasets, please refer to the Supplementary Figure S10–S12.

SR+ cells: Coexistence of keratinization and epithelial–mesenchymal transition

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of DEGs in SR+ cells revealed significant enrichment in keratinization and actin cytoskeleton-related pathways (Fig. 3A). We further utilized the gene sets to functionally score this cluster and found that SR+ cells exhibited high activity in terms of angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and partial EMT (pEMT) (Fig. 3B; effect sizes refer to Supplementary Material 9). We inferred the activity of transcription factors using the decoupleR algorithm, and the results indicated that NR3C1 activity was high in SR+ cells (Fig. 3C), demonstrating their association with the EMT phenotype [46]. Through gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), we found that the expression of genes related to cell adhesion junctions, cell-cell communication, and cornified envelope formation were upregulated in SR+ cells and that the expression of genes related to the WNT signaling pathway was significantly downregulated (Fig. 3D).

To verify these findings in situ, we analyzed a ST sample enriched with SR regions (Fig. 3E). The association between spatial clustering and pathological features suggested that SR region exhibited high activity in terms of EMT and pEMT than WC and PE regions (Fig. 3F). Notably, the expression profile of the SR region was highly similar to that of nontumor regions and cell clusters scattered within keratinized substances (Fig. 3G). To enhance the conclusion, we conducted bivariate Moran’s I analysis between SR+ and KRT+ feature scores based on snRNA and ST deconvolution in two independent ST samples, both of which consistently showed a significant positive spatial colocalization (I_AB = 0.4/0.31, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3H–K), statistically confirming the stable correlation between the SR phenotype and the keratinization phenotype in multiple samples.

In summary, the SR region simultaneously exhibited strong terminal differentiation signals of keratinization, an active EMT/pEMT status (also supported by Smart-Seq2 scoring in Suppl. Figure S9), high angiogenic potential, and significant enrichment of cell junction structures that maintain epithelial adhesion, suggesting a highly plastic intermediate state.

KRT+ cells: A terminal keratinization fate

We further analyzed the subclustering of KRT+ cells into two distinct functional subpopulations (Fig. 4A). Cell cycle analysis revealed that the KRT+_1 subpopulation had a greater proportion of cells in the G1 phase (Fig. 4B), suggesting a more quiescent cell cycle. Based on the gene set scoring of the CancerSEA cohort, the KRT+_2 subpopulation exhibited increased levels of angiogenesis, EMT, and metastasis; however, both subpopulations exhibited low levels of proliferation characteristics (Fig. 4C). GO enrichment analysis indicated that the KRT+_1 subpopulation was enriched in cartilage development, negative regulation of WNT signaling, and some synaptic-related pathways (Fig. 4D), whereas the molecular characteristics of the KRT+_2 subpopulation seemed more extensive and prominent, with significant enrichment in neuronal adhesion, extracellular matrix, actin cytoskeleton, axonogenesis and signal transduction pathways (Fig. 4E and F). The deconvolution of ST confirmed these dichotomous structures: the KRT+_1 subpopulation was strictly localized to keratin and adjacent squamous epithelial cells, whereas the KRT+_2 subpopulation was diffusely distributed at the keratinized edge, even in nontumor regions (Fig. 4G–K). Therefore, we proposed that the KRT+_2 subpopulation was not in a terminal state but represented a transitional cell population with high plasticity.

Overall, KRT+ cells bifurcated into a terminally keratinized subpopulation (KRT+_1) and a more plastic, transitional subpopulation (KRT+_2), with the latter exhibiting enhanced motility and microenvironment interaction.

CW+ cells: Local regulation of the WNT signaling pathway

Based on the analysis of cell-cell communication, we found that CW+ cells were critical signaling nodes in the tumor microenvironment. These cells not only respond to external signals through key receptors such as FGFR2 but also possess the unique potential for bidirectional regulation of the WNT signaling pathway (Fig. 4L): they can activate the pathway by secreting WNT3A and can simultaneously release antagonists such as WIF1 (Fig. 2F) and SOSTDC1 to precisely inhibit the signal (for more multiplex immunofluorescence images of cell-cell communication interaction network, refer to Supplementary Material 10). We found specific enrichment of the latter within the cystic cavity (Fig. 2G), which is highly consistent with the discovery that the CW+_2 subgroup is enriched in secretory granule cavities and nuclear receptor binding pathways in the transcriptome (Fig. 4M–O).

Together, this evidence suggested that the CW, as a cell population with active secretion and signal transmission functions, plays a key role in the regulation of WNT signaling in the tumor microenvironment.

WC+ cells: A signaling engine driving tumorigenesis

Various ligands are released by WC+ cells and regulate the functions of surrounding cells through paracrine signaling (Fig. 5A). We divided WC+ cells into two functionally distinct subgroups (Fig. 5B and C). WC+_1 cells were identified as a typical nuclear cluster, showing the highest LEF1/CTNNB1 transcription factor activity and expression (Fig. 5D and E), with a greater SASP score, and enrichment in WNT, Hippo and other signaling pathways (Fig. 5F and G). However, WC+_2 cells exhibited different characteristics. We found that the cell cycle of WC+_2 cells was more active (with a greater proportion of cells in the G2/M and S phase) (Fig. 5C), the metastasis potential score was increased, and these cells were enriched in cell development and actin and cytoskeleton assembly (Fig. 5F and H). The binary classification of WC+ cells was verified in two other external datasets (Suppl. Figure S10 and S11), and the enrichment of upregulated Rac2/RhoA GTPase signaling in WC+_2 cells was verified by GSEA (Fig. 5I). These features collectively suggest that WC+_2 cells have stronger motility and cytoskeletal remodeling capabilities [47]. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a clear spatial pattern presented by WC+_2 cells surrounding WC+_1 cells (Fig. 5J), suggesting close functional collaboration between these two clusters. Further analysis of cell-cell communication within the subgroups of cells indicated that WC+_1 cells were the communication core of the entire tumor microenvironment responsible for the main signal output function (Fig. 5K and L; for more multiplex immunofluorescence images of cell-cell communication interaction network, refer to Supplementary Material 10).

CE+ cells: A reservoir of highly plastic tumor cells

The newly defined CE+ cell clusters in this study can be divided into two groups at the single-cell level (Fig. 6A). We found that the cell cycle of CE+_1 cells was more active (Fig. 6B) and that their signaling pathways were enriched in cell development, neurodevelopment, and various oncogenic pathways (such as the Rap1 and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways) and strongly associated with basal cell carcinoma (Fig. 6C and D). The CE+_1 subpopulation exhibited increased levels of MYC target and stemness (Fig. 6E). This basal-like cell feature at the single-cell level corresponded to the finding that the CE was rich in developmental regulatory signals, as revealed by the Smart-Seq2 data. From different dimensions, these findings provide a biologically coherent picture of the developmental plasticity of this cell population. This discovery was also demonstrated in the RCTD results of ST, where CE+_1 cells were indeed located in areas without a specific morphology (Fig. 6F and I). Surprisingly, the results of the deconvolution of GFAP and astrocyte revealed that the score of CE+_1 cells was greater in regions near the hypothalamus (Fig. 6F–H). We believe that CE+_1 cells are likely to play an important role in tumor invasion. In another independent sample, we verified the location of CE+_1 cells again and found that CE+_2 cells were specifically located in keratinized substances (Fig. 6I and J). Bivariate Moran’s I analysis of the CE+_2 and KRT+_1 feature scores derived from the ST deconvolution further revealed a significant positive spatial colocalization (Fig. 6K). Therefore, it was inferred that CE+_2 cells may possess keratinization potential. Moreover, TNFA–NF-κB pathway activity and the SASP were greater in the CE+_2 group than in the CE+_1 group (Fig. 6L–N), suggesting that the CE+_2 group may play different roles in tumor development. The above characteristics of the CE+ cell cluster were also verified by similar conclusions in another two external datasets (Suppl. Figure S10 and S11).

Through single-cell reclustering, we further analyzed the heterogeneity of PE+ cells (Fig. 7C–F). Among these clusters, PE+_1 cells were the basic subgroup constituting the main body, while the PE+_3 subgroup showed a very high S-phase proportion (Fig. 7D). Further analysis of PE+_3 cells revealed that the core nodes of their protein interaction network were POLA1, BRCA2, RRM2 and other DNA replication and repair factors (Fig. 7K), and transcription factor activity enrichment and a decoupleR algorithm both confirmed the importance of the E2F family (especially E2F1) of the cell cycle in this cluster (Fig. 7L and M); moreover, GSEA further confirmed high activity in the cell cycle, DNA replication and E2F target pathways (Fig. 7N). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining revealed a scattered distribution of PE+_3 cluster within the PE (Fig. 7P). The above characteristics of the PE+ cell cluster were corroborated by similar conclusions in another two external datasets (Suppl. Figure S10 and S11).

Together, PE+ cells served dual functions as an immune barrier and a proliferative front: PE+_2 cells exhibited WNT pathway activation, while PE+_3 cluster acted as a highly proliferative subgroup with active DNA replication and cell cycle machinery, together supporting both immune evasion and tumor expansion.

Lineage dynamics of the CE+ cluster as a core evolutionary origin

In the overall UMAP chart that integrated all the subgroups, we observed the specific distribution of each functional subgroup (Fig. 8A). Each subgroup had different cell cycle proportions and DEGs (Fig. 8B and C). Moreover, we found that PE+_3 cells exhibited the strongest cell cycle activity in all clusters (Fig. 8B and D); SR+ cells had the strongest EMT characteristics (Fig. 8D); WC+_1 had a high score for the WNT signaling pathway (Fig. 8D); CE+_2 and WC+_1 cells had the greatest SASP score (Fig. 8D), and CE+_2 and SR+ cells together showed a greater inflammatory response (Fig. 8D). With respect to the results of GSVA (Fig. 8E), Scissor mapping (Fig. 8A and F), and average expression profile similarity analysis (Fig. 8G), we detected similarity in the states of different structural subgroups, suggesting that there may be widespread intermediate or transitional cells within the tumor, and this was supported by the results of spatial deconvolution colocalization, such as significant correlations between CE+_1 and CW+_1 (Fig. 8H and I), between SR+ and KRT+_1 (Fig. 8J and K), between CE+_1 and SR+ (Fig. 8L and M), and between CE+_2 and KRT+_1 (Fig. 8N and O). This evidence collectively indicates that the different pathological structures of ACP are not composed of isolated cell populations but are interrelated through a series of functionally continuous and state-overlapping cell subgroups and that there may be evolution between the structures.

To explore the developmental roots of different pathological structures, we conducted cell trajectory inference. Therefore, we conducted Monocle2 pseudotemporal trajectory analysis based on CE+ cells as the root (Fig. 9E), and the results revealed two clear cell fate branches: cell fate 1 (dominated by WC+) and cell fate 2 (dominated by PE+ and CW+) (Fig. 9F). In combination with the CNVs results, the CE+ cells constituting the temporal root showed limited chromosomal alterations, whereas the WC+ cells along cell fate 1 exhibited concentrated high-variation signals on chromosome 15 (Fig. 2K), suggesting the accumulation of chromosomal rearrangement events during evolution. The analysis of time DEGs along the trajectory further resolved the evolutionary process into four functional modules: before the branching point, cells were active in basic programs such as p38 MAPK signaling and skin development; subsequently synaptic regulation and cell connection were favored in fate 1, whereas axonogenesis and axon development were enriched in fate 2 (Fig. 9G). Finally, the key marker genes of each pathological structure showed specific activation patterns along the pseudotime axis (Fig. 9H), clearly outlining the molecular pathway from the basic state of CE+ cells to different pathological structures. To dynamically capture the transformation of cell states, we conducted RNA velocity analysis. The results revealed that the CE+ cluster was a key hub of cell fate flow, with its velocity vector clearly pointing to multiple important subgroups (Fig. 9I), providing dynamic evidence that the CE+ cluster represents the progenitor cells of ACP.

On this basis, we set the CE+ cluster as the root and constructed a continuous trajectory model of cell state transition using Monocle3 (Fig. 9J and K); through temporal evolution combined with high dimensional WGCNA (hdWGCNA), we found that the differentiation of the CE+ cluster to other clusters could be accompanied by the specific activation of different modules (Fig. 9L), and the HUB genes of different modules were specifically activated on the Monocle2 pseudotime axis (Fig. 9M). To refine the differentiation modules, we combined the two clusters and their double-positive cells and then conducted the temporal module inference again. We found that the transformation process of CE+_1 to CW+ was enriched mainly in the activation of protein synthesis functions, the activation of morphogenetic pathways, and the Hippo and VEGF–VEGFR2 signaling pathways (Fig. 10B, and Suppl. Figure S15). During the differentiation of CE+_2 cells to KRT+ keratinized cells, significant enrichment of the activation of the Hippo signaling pathway, accompanied by the activation of ossification pathway, was detected, as well as the inhibition of metabolic signaling pathways (Fig. 10B, and Suppl. Figure S16). The differentiation of CE+_1 cells to PE+ cells was accompanied by profound cellular remodeling, characterized by significant activation of the autophagy pathway, and was synergistic with the upregulation of cell cytoskeleton dynamic signal transduction and DNA damage repair pathways (Fig. 10B, and Suppl. Figure S17). The CE+_1 to SR+ transition was characterized by the synchronous activation of p53 signal transduction and mRNA splicing mechanisms, accompanied by the upregulation of metabolic reorganization and cell cytoskeleton dynamics (Fig. 10B, and Suppl. Figure S18). Additionally, the differentiation of CE+_1 to WC+_2 cells was characterized by posttranscriptional RNA modification, protein synthesis, and cellular metabolism, which is consistent with its structural and reactive functions. Although a direct connection between CE+_1 and WC+_1 cells was not established in the pseudotime trajectory, the results of the hdWGCNA analysis indicate that the gene network modules are significantly enriched in the WNT signaling pathway, suggesting that the transformation of CE+_1 to WC+_1 cells might be driven by intense activation of WNT signaling pathway (Fig. 10B, and Suppl. Figure S19).

WC+ cluster as a secondary lineage origin

The potential differentiation of the WC+ cluster into the PE+, KRT+ and CW+ clusters was indicated by integrated analysis of Scissor mapping intersection, average expression profile similarity, CytoTRACE prediction and RNA velocity (Figs. 8F and G and 9A and C, and Suppl. Figure S20). Consistent with the analytical method of CE+, we found that the transformation of WC+_2 cells into CW+ cells was characterized by the coordinated regulation of extracellular matrix interactions and TGF-β signaling pathways, along with a significant increase in protein translation function, which established the secretory function of CW+ cells (Fig. 10B, and Suppl. Figure S21). The transition of WC+_1 to KRT+ cells was marked by the fine-tuned regulation of the WNT signaling pathway and the cooperative process of the Hippo signaling pathway, accompanied by the activation of DNA damage repair mechanisms and odontogenesis-related pathways (Fig. 10B, and Suppl. Figure S22). Additionally, the evolution of WC+_2 into PE+ cells was accompanied by active adaptation and microenvironment interaction, involving RHO GTPase cycle activation, focal adhesion assembly, collagen formation and extracellular matrix interaction, activation of the VEGFA–VEGFR2 signaling pathway, and reconstruction of tight junctions, laying the foundation for the physical barrier function of PE+ cells (Fig. 10B, and Suppl. Figure S23).

Discussion

In this study, for the first time, we used integrated multiomic sequencing, including laser microdissection combined with Smart-Seq2, snRNA-seq, ST, and external validation datasets, to construct a comprehensive molecular atlas of the various pathological features of ACP.

In this study, at the whole-structure level and single-cell level, we observed that the SR exhibited distinct EMT and pEMT characteristics, which corresponded to an elongated cell nuclear morphology, and confirmed the previous discovery of our team that keratinocytes, including those in the SR, possess pEMT potential [17]. The EMT phenotype is closely related to a poor prognosis in ACP [49, 50]. We subsequently analyzed an external dataset and found that the proportion of SR cells in the recurrence group samples was significantly greater [22] (Suppl. Figure S12). This finding further supported the association between the SR and tumor progression. In addition to interstitial transformation, another potential fate of the SR was differentiation towards the terminal keratinization direction. Degenerative keratinization cell clusters are often accompanied by calcification, and osteopontin (OPN) and bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP2), known as secreted proteins that promote calcification in ACP, are positively expressed in the SR [13, 14]. Therefore, we believe that the SR may participate in the tumor calcification process through OPN. While our analysis of gene modules indicates that the SR+ to KRT+ cell transition involves cornified envelope formation, macroautophagy, and organelle disassembly (Suppl. Figure S24), the regulatory mechanisms governing the divergence of the SR into distinct cellular fates remain unclear.

The most typical molecular feature of ACP is the CTNNB1 mutation, which leads to the defective degradation of β-catenin, causing its nuclear aggregation and subsequently resulting in continuous activation of the WNT signaling pathway [1, 51–54]. High WNT pathway activity in β-catenin nuclear-aggregated cells may lead to unique whorl-like morphological structural characteristics [21, 55]. Our research revealed that WCs play a dominant role in the interaction network with surrounding cells, indicating their important autocrine and paracrine regulatory functions in the pathogenesis of ACP, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [18, 19]. Interestingly, H&E staining revealed that the β-catenin nuclear-aggregated cells were mostly located in the core area of the nodular whorls, and their rotational sense was weaker than that of surrounding cells, suggesting morphological heterogeneity within the WC. Our previous research revealed different cytokine secretion patterns in single-nucleus subpopulations related to the WNT pathway, providing molecular evidence for this hypothesis [17]. In this study, for the first time, we successfully analyzed the internal heterogeneity of WC at the single-cell resolution level and verified it in external datasets. In addition to the classic β-catenin nuclear-aggregated WC+_1 cells, a group of membrane-localized WC+_2 cells was also identified. The latter cells do not show significant WNT pathway activation and exhibit weaker dominance in cell-cell communication, but their potential mobility may explain the feasibility of the finger-like growth patterns in ACP, which are potentially driven by WC+_2 cells. We detected high expression of SLC20A2 in WC+_2 cells, which regulates intracellular phosphate homeostasis and may affect ATP synthesis and cell motilit2y [56].

Although cells in the WCs are considered to possess stem cell characteristics and exert regulatory effects on tumors [18, 19, 57], the cells themselves are in a low-proliferation state and the ultimate fate of these cells seems to be closely related to the formation of wet keratin [58]. In this study, we found strong similarity in expression profiles between KRT+_1 and WC+_1 cells. In addition, RNA velocity analysis revealed the evolution from WC+ cells to KRT+ cells, further suggesting the keratinization fate of WC. This keratinization may be regulated by the activity of the WNT pathway, as Jiang Y reported that MUC1 (a protein that can stabilize nuclear β-catenin) is colocalized with β-catenin nuclear-aggregated cells [20]. However, we further discovered that in WC+_1 cells, the negative regulatory factor of the WNT pathway, AJAP1 [59], is highly expressed (Supplementary Material 7), suggesting that although WC+_1 cells are characterized by β-catenin nuclear aggregation, there is also an endogenous negative feedback regulatory mechanism within the cells. The balance of WNT signaling pathway regulation may be the key to cell fate. Given the core role of the WNT signaling pathway in the maintenance of stem cell functions in normal organisms, off-target effects lead to unsatisfactory results when related inhibitors are used. However, our identification of the precise distinction between WC+_1 and WC+_2 cells provides a key theoretical basis for the development of specific strategies targeting WC in the future.

Our cell-cell communication analysis revealed that immune regulatory pathways based on CD47 and PVR (for multiplex immunofluorescence staining, refer to Supplementary Material 10) may exist between the PE and immune cells. Although there is an immune activation pathway involving PVR–CD226, the immune escape mediated by PVR–CD96, PVR–TIGIT [60–62], CD47–SIRPA [63, 64], and CD47–SIRPG [65] may be dominant, suggesting that the PE primarily functions as an immunoevasive hub within the tumor microenvironment. In previous studies, CD47 was confirmed to be a marker of the PE [20, 66]. As a classical immune escape signal, it can inhibit the phagocytic function of microglia in ACP [66]. Our research refined the mechanism of this process. Additionally, consistent with the finding of previous study [20], we confirmed the presence of a high-proliferation subpopulation in the PE. We found that this group partially retained the gene expression profiles and some basic characteristics of the PE, while its transcription factors were significantly enriched in the E2F family (especially E2F1). Notably, we discovered that cell-cell communication between WC and the PE was significantly enriched in the FGF9–FGFR2 pair (Fig. 5A, and Supplementary Material 10), which has been reported to act as an upstream activation pathway of E2F1 in mesenchymal tumors [67]. These findings collectively suggest that the functional heterogeneity of PE cells and their interaction with WC might be key to maintaining their proliferative activity, which provides a new idea for future precision treatment strategies targeting the PE.

Previous studies have revealed a group of cells located on the inner side of the PE, a subset of which are positive for PD-L1 or LIF [68, 69]; however, their biological significance has not been fully elucidated. Our study first described and systematically explored the characteristics of this group of cells, which we termed the constitutive epithelium (CE). We found that the CE specifically highly expresses molecules such as KRT19, KRT15, ANXA2, and ANXA1, which are usually associated with the stem cell state [70–79]; among them, KRT19 has been confirmed to be an indicator of a poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma [80–82]. Furthermore, Wang Y reported that ANXA2 expression is increased in recurrent ACP and that ANXA2+ cells have a stronger proliferative capacity [83]. These studies suggest the stem cell characteristics and malignant potential of the CE, and the enrichment of basal cell carcinoma-related molecular features in CE+_1 cells further supports this hypothesis. Analysis of the GEPIA2 database [84] revealed that the above characteristic genes are highly expressed in squamous epithelial-derived tumors (Suppl. Figure S14), providing further evidence for the malignant potential of the CE. Much more clinically significant, in an external independent dataset, we observed that the proportion of CE+ cells in recurrent patients’ tumors was significantly greater than that in primary tumors and that the proportion was positively correlated with the tumor volume (Suppl. Figure S12) [22], further supporting the key role of the CE in tumor progression. Moreover, we propose that CE+_2 cells exhibit the SASP, possibly as a consequence of CDKN2A upregulation and sustained TNF–NF–κB signaling activation [85–88]. Combined with the terminal differentiation characteristics exhibited by this cluster, we believe that the cell aging program in ACP may be intrinsically associated with the keratinization differentiation process. Notably, a study by Wang X et al. revealed a group of squamous cells lacking an orderly structure but exhibiting the SASP [21], which further supports the importance of the SASP program in ACP. This is further supported by the work of Prince EW et al. [89] and Zhang B et al. [90], who also emphasized the regulatory role of the SASP in their findings. We speculate that CE+_2 might be a cellular stage of CE+_1, exhibiting stronger terminal keratinization and functional execution characteristics, while CE+_1 represents the relatively more plastic progenitor cell stage within this lineage.

With respect to the cellular origin of ACP, WC or PE have tended to be the favored sources in previous views [17, 20, 23, 90]. However, considering the CE’s wide distribution, its basal cell-like molecular characteristics, developmental potential analysis result, and the clinical evidence related to tumor recurrence, we reasonably hypothesize that the CE, rather than the PE or WC, is more likely to be the true origin of tumor cells, playing a fundamental role in maintaining the long-term growth of ACP tumors. Therefore, on the basis of the results of multiple pseudotime analyses, for the first time, we propose a lineage development hypothesis for ACP centered on the evolutionary origin of the CE. We observed that the Hippo signaling pathway was significantly enriched in several key differentiation pathways (such as CE+_1 to CW+ and CE+_2 to KRT+_1). Based on the module genes and enriched genes, further analysis revealed that this enrichment essentially represented activation of YAP/TAZ. Previous study has reported the nuclear aggregation of YAP/TAZ in ACP [91], which once again confirms our hypothesis. However, this seems to contradict the terminal keratinization fate of KRT+_1 cells. Nevertheless, we found that this precisely corresponded with research on cholesteatoma. Previous study has confirmed that the activation of YAP/TAZ can increase the volume of cholesteatoma tumors [92], and keratin is the main component of cholesteatoma tumors. Thus, we propose a reasonable hypothesis: in ACP, CE+_1 cells, as a basal-like cell cluster, transition into CE+_2 cells and eventually form wet keratin. Thus, the continuous generation of wet keratin is an active growth mode for tumor volume expansion rather than a self-limiting process. In addition, the results of other pathway studies support our depicted lineage diagram. For example, the view that autophagy promotes cell differentiation [93, 94] supports the notion that the autophagy pathway is activated during the differentiation of CE+ to PE+ cells. Additionally, during the terminal differentiation stage from WC+ to KRT+ cells, activation of the DNA repair pathway may contribute to maintaining genomic stability and supporting the smooth execution of the keratinization process, which is consistent with the finding that the DNA repair capacity may support the normal terminal differentiation of epidermal keratinocytes [95], thereby providing an explanation for the terminal differentiation path from WC to wet keratin.

In summary, by integrating laser microdissection and multiomic sequencing, we have for the first time systematically constructed a high-resolution molecular map of the core pathological structures of ACP, including the WC, PE, SR, wet keratin, and two newly described structures, the CW and CE. This work provides potential new targets for the precise treatment of specific cell populations. However, more importantly, through trajectory inference and cell communication analysis, we have defined the CE as the cell evolution hub of the tumor and have drawn a blueprint of lineages originating from these cells.

Despite these contributions, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, notably, the conclusions of this study are based mainly on bioinformatic inference, though we performed bivariate Moran’s I analysis to assess the spatial colocalization of pathological structures and attempted to construct a tumor differentiation network, these analyses still cannot provide direct causal evidence for the structure differentiation. Secondly, although multiple pieces of evidence support the progenitor status of the CE, functional validation of the above lineage relationships and tumorigenic capacity remains unattainable at present due to the lack of well-established primary culture systems for ACP cells. In addition, although we validated the spatial colocalization of selected receptor–ligand pairs by multiplex immunofluorescence staining, these findings mainly reflect correlation and do not directly prove the causal relationship in terms of regulatory interactions. Therefore, the cell evolution pathways and molecular interaction networks we propose urgently need to be confirmed and explored in the future through advanced in vitro models, organoid culture technology, and lineage tracing experiments. Overall, our findings provide new insights and potential clues for the current therapeutic management of ACP.
