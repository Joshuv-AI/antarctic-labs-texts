Abnormal brain iron deposition in patients with new daily persistent headache: a prospective quantitative susceptibility mapping study
Authors: Tong Chen, Xun Pei, Xiaoyan Bai, Xue Zhang, Yuanbin Zhao, Cunxin Lin, Qi Yang, Shengjun Sun, Yonggang Wang, Binbin Sui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe underlying pathogenesis of New daily persistent headache (NDPH) is currently unknown. Abnormal iron deposition in specific brain regions has been reported to be associated with the pathogenesis of migraine. This study aimed to investigate the role of iron metabolism disorders in the pathogenesis of NDPH using quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM).MethodsTwenty-five NDPH patients and 43 gender- and age-matched healthy controls (HCs) were recruited. 3D T1-weighted images (T1WI) and QSM sequences were performed at a 3.0 T MRI scanner. Voxel-based analysis was used to compare differences in iron content between NDPH and HC groups after QSM maps were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute space. The correlations between QSM values and clinical assessment scale scores were also assessed.ResultsThere were significant differences in iron levels in the right cerebellar lobule VIII, right insula, and left gyrus rectus between the NDPH patients and the HC group. Compared to the HC group, patients with NDPH showed higher iron deposition in these three regions. There was no significant correlation between the QSM values and the scores of the clinical scales in these three regions.ConclusionsOur findings indicated that the metabolic disorder of iron deposition in specific areas of the brain may be related to the underlying pathogenesis of patients with NDPH. These findings should be regarded as preliminary exploratory results, suggesting a possible involvement of iron metabolism in the mechanisms of NDPH, which requires further validation in subsequent studies.Keywords: New daily persistent headache, Magnetic resonance imaging, Quantitative susceptibility mapping, Iron deposition, Iron metabolism

Introduction

New daily persistent headache (NDPH) is a rare primary headache characterized by a clear memory of the onset of the headache and persistent headache for more than three months [1]. Previous studies indicated that it may be associated with infections, emotional stress, or other physiological and psychological factors [2, 3]. However, the exact pathophysiological mechanisms of NDPH remain poorly understood. Increasing the understanding of its pathogenesis may facilitate more accurate diagnosis and improved treatment strategies for patients with NDPH.

Iron metabolism is an essential element that participates in numerous physiological processes, including neurotransmitter synthesis, myelin formation, and oxidative metabolism [4]. Disruption of iron homeostasis may result in excessive oxidative stress, microglial activation, neuroinflammation, ultimately leading to neuronal injury [4–6]. These pathophysiological processes are increasingly recognized as critical mechanisms contributing to the development of headache disorders [7–10]. Recently, several studies have reported abnormal iron deposition in the brains of patients with various types of headaches [11–13]. The abnormal iron deposition may be associated with multiple processes, including migraine development and chronicity [13, 14].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a powerful neuroimaging technique for the noninvasive and quantitative evaluation of brain iron deposition. Among these techniques, quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) provides superior reliability and sensitivity, with susceptibility values accurately reflecting local iron content in brain tissue [15, 16]. Although our previous study has reported multiple functional and perfusion abnormalities in the brain of NDPH patients [17–21], few studies have explored the brain iron metabolism in patients with NDPH. Hence, investigating iron deposition in NDPH patients may help advance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of the disorder and may drive advancements in the efficacy of therapeutic interventions.

This study aimed to quantitatively assess brain iron alterations and to explore the potential role of iron deposition in the pathogenesis of NDPH using QSM sequence. Additionally, we also evaluate the correlations between iron content in significant brain regions and clinical characteristics.

Materials and methods

Participants

Thirty patients with NDPH were prospectively recruited from the Headache Centre of the Department of Neurology, Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University, from January 2021 to August 2022. All NDPH patients were diagnosed by two experienced headache physicians according to the diagnostic criteria of the International Classification of Headache Disorders, Third version (ICHD-3) [1]; Inclusion criteria: (1) no claustrophobia and other contraindications to MRI examination; (2) no other neurological or psychiatric disorders; (3) no history of cerebral surgeries or trauma. Exclusion criteria: (1) obvious infarct lesions in the brain; (2) poor MRI image quality; (3) incomplete data.

Forty-six age- and gender-matched healthy controls (HCs) were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria for the HC group were as follows: (1) no history of any headache and other neurological or psychiatric disorders; (2) no claustrophobia and other contraindications to MRI examination; (3) the quality of the MRI images could satisfy the subsequent analysis. The subjects were excluded if they had poor MRI images, or incomplete data.

Demographic and general clinical information, such as age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and disease duration, were recorded for all the participants. Multiple clinical scales have been used to assess the clinical characteristics of NDPH patients. The headache intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) prior to the MRI examination [22]; the impact of headache on daily life was evaluated with the Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6) [23]; the sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleepiness Quality Index (PSQI) [24]; the anxiety levels were determined through the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [25]; and depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [26].

This study was a sub-study of the ongoing Chinese Headache Disorders Registry Study (CHAIRS, trial registration: NCT05334927). All participants signed an informed consent form, and the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital reviewed and approved the study (KY-2022-044).

MRI images acquisition and processing

All participants underwent examination on a 3.0T MR scanner (Signa Premier, GE Healthcare, USA) with a 48-channel head coil. To minimize motion, participants’ heads were immobilized with foam pads, and they were instructed to keep their heads as still as possible.

3D T1 MPRAGE sequence was performed to obtain structural data using the following parameters: sagittal acquisition, echo time (TE) = 3 ms, repetition time (TR) = 7.2 ms, with 1-mm isotropic resolution, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2; acquisition matrix = 256; slice number = 192; flip angle (FA) = 8°; preparation time = 880 ms; recovery time = 400 ms; acceleration factor = 2; acquisition time = 4 min.

A multi-echo 3D gradient recalled echo (GRE) sequence was performed to acquire the QSM maps with the following parameters: TE = 4.0/8.9/13.7/18.5/23.4 ms, TR = 27.4 ms, matrix = 240 × 240, slice thickness = 1.8 mm, FA = 20°, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, and voxel size = 0.9 × 0.9 × 1.8 mm3. The acquisition time was 2 min and 34 s.

The QSMbox 2.0 software package (https://gitlab.com/acostaj/QSMbox) [27] was performed to reconstruct the QSM map in MATLAB (The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Initially, phase images were extracted to unwrap and estimate the local magnetic field. Subsequently, the background field was removed by the Laplace boundary value method alongside the variable spherical mean value algorithm. The Multi-Scale Dipole Inversion (MSDI) algorithm (https://github.com/fil-physics/PublicationCode) was then applied to convolve the dipole field with the susceptibility in k-space.

Subsequently, the QSM maps were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space (1 mm isotropic resolution) using FMRIB Software Library (FSL, www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl, v6.0). For skull stripping, the automated Brain Extraction Tool (BET) algorithm was employed to remove non-brain tissues, including skull and scalp. The registration pipeline employed FSL’s linear registration tool (FLIRT) for co-registration between T1WI and the first echo of QSM magnitude images. For spatial normalization, both FLIRT and FSL’s non-linear image registration tool (FNIRT) registration algorithms were utilized to align individual T1-WI images with the MNI standard space. Subsequently, the derived transformation matrices were applied to register QSM data to the MNI space. The detailed registration pipeline is depicted in Fig. 1.

The registration pipeline involves the following steps. First, the magnitude image from the first echo of the QSM raw data is extracted and co-registered with the T1-weighted image. Voxel‑based QSM analysis

Voxel-wise comparisons of the normalized QSM maps between the NDPH and HC groups were performed within a gray matter mask using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) 12, with age, gender, and BMI as covariates [28]. Whole-brain analyses identified significant gray matter differences between NDPH and HCs using a voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.001 after false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons, combined with a cluster-extent threshold of 800 mm³ (equivalent to 100 contiguous voxels).

ROI‑based QSM analysis

After identifying whole-brain QSM alterations in NDPH patients, the clusters showing significant differences were mapped onto the corresponding brain regions defined by the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas. The mean QSM values within the ROIs corresponding to these regions were then extracted. In addition, we extracted the QSM values of the cerebrospinal fluid as a reference to correct the QSM values of these three brain regions. The corrected QSM values for these regions were obtained for comparison.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of quantitative variables. Quantitative data were expressed using mean ± standard deviation (SD), and median and quartiles according to the distribution of data. Categorical variables were expressed using frequencies and percentages. Differences in gender distribution between groups were assessed using the chi-squared test. Group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. The mean QSM values between the two groups were compared using a two-sample independent t-test, with post-hoc analysis adjusted by the Bonferroni correction. Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation was utilized to analyze the correlation between QSM value and clinical characteristics. p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Finally, 25 NDPH patients (age, 37.0 [17.00–58.50], 12 males) and 43 HCs (age 34.0 [21.0–45.0], 17 males) were included in the analysis. The correlation analysis explored the relationship between QSM values in these three brain regions and clinical characteristics (disease duration, VAS, HIT-6, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and PSQI). The QSM value of right cerebellum lobule VIII tended to be negatively correlated with the PHQ-9 scores (r=−0.367, p = 0.085). After Bonferroni correction, there was no correlation between QSM values and clinical characteristics in these three regions (all > 0.05/18).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the abnormal changes in iron deposition within the brains of NDPH patients. Our results revealed significantly higher iron deposition in left gyrus rectus, right insula, and right cerebellum lobule VIII of NDPH patients. These findings may provide preliminary clues regarding the involvement of iron deposition in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying NDPH.

The role of iron metabolism disorders in the mechanism of migraine has received increasing attention [9, 29]. Neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter metabolism are thought to be involved in the development of migraine, and iron metabolism is closely related to these processes. In recent years, a number of studies have reported a strong association between iron deficiency anemia and migraine [30, 31]. The increased iron deposition in some specific brain regions is thought to be associated with the development and chronicity of migraine [13, 32, 33]. Abnormal iron deposition in the periaqueductal gray (PAG) was also found to be associated with the therapeutic outcome of medication [34, 35]. Our previous study on patients with medication overuse headache found that abnormal iron metabolism in striatum may be associated with medication overuse behavior [14]. Therefore, as a type of headache with a long duration and significant impact on the patient’s health, it is imperative to investigate the altered iron metabolism in the brain of NDPH patients.

In present study, the iron deposition in the cerebellum of NDPH patients was significantly higher than that of HCs. In prior research, Zhang et al. also reported abnormal iron deposition in the cerebellum of migraine patients [12]. The cerebellum, as an important center for movement regulation, has received increasing attention in recent years for its role in pain regulation [36]. The cerebellum has functional connections with multiple pain-processing regions (such as the thalamus, anterior cingulate gyrus, insula, etc.), and abnormalities in these connections may disrupt pain perception and regulation. Previous studies have further reported significant alterations in cerebellar functional connectivity and neurotransmitter metabolism in patients with NDPH [20, 37].

The activation in cerebellum lobules VIII is thought to be associated with nociceptive signaling, fear conditioning, and sensory integration [36]. In previous studies, the cerebellum lobule VIIIa was reported to be involved in painful stimuli processing, and the lobule VIIIb was related with pain-related fear conditioning, pain, and motor processing [38–41]. Interestingly, the cerebellum lobule VIII has also been suggested to have a potential interrelation with the PAG [39]. Moreover, patients with NDPH have been reported to exhibit abnormal functional connectivity in the PAG region [19]. In this study, no abnormal iron deposition was observed in the PAG region of NDPH patients, whereas previous studies on migraine have reported iron accumulation in PAG region [34]. This discrepancy may further highlight potential differences in the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms between migraine and NDPH.

Meanwhile, our findings found that the iron deposition in the right insula of NDPH patients was significantly higher than that of the HC group. Consistent with our findings, Chen et al. also identified abnormal iron deposition in the insula of chronic migraine (CM) patients [42]. The insula is highly interconnected throughout the brain [24], and is associated with multiple brain structures such as the limbic system, limbic subregions, visceral sensory areas, visceral motor areas, motor association areas, vestibular areas, as well as having connections with the amygdala and the thalamus [43]. These connections form the basis of their participation in various functions. Given its functional role and extensive connections with cortical and subcortical brain regions, the insula has been proposed to play an important role in neuropsychiatric disorders [44]. Meta-analyses of structural changes in the brain of CM patients, and studies on NDPH have identified abnormalities in the insula. The structural and functional changes in the insula appear to be a common feature of headache patients [19, 45]. However, the insula is also referred to as the “multidimensional integration site of pain”, as it not only participates in the integration and transmission of pain, but also in the emotional responses triggered by pain [46]. Therefore, its exact role in the pathogenesis of NDPH remains to be further explored.

Interestingly, we also observed abnormal iron deposition in the left gyrus rectus of patients with NDPH. This area is considered to be closely connected to structures such as the nucleus accumbens, which belongs to the limbic system [47]. In previous studies, it has been suggested to be involved in the regulation of emotional processing, motivation, and personality behaviors [48, 49]. In patients with NDPH, long-term headache may cause mood changes and significantly increase the risk of depression [50]. Whether its elevated iron content is related to mood regulation or to the pathogenesis of headache is still unknown. Its role in the pathogenesis of NDPH still requires further exploration.

In addition, there was no correlation between QSM values and the clinical characteristics of NDPH patients in three regions. Similar results have been reported in patients with episodic migraine [51]. Nevertheless, it has also been found that the iron deposition may be related to some clinical characteristics of CM patients such as VAS score, HIT-6 score, and PSQI, etc [13, 42]. Although both CM and NDPH are chronic headache disorders, they differ markedly in their clinical features and onset patterns [1]. The potential associations between QSM values in specific brain regions and clinical characteristics of NDPH patients remain to be further explored.

There were some limitations in our study. This study was conducted as a single-center, cross-sectional analysis with a relatively small sample size. Our findings should be regarded as preliminary results and treated with caution, as they still require validation through larger cohort studies. Considering the rarity of NDPH, these findings may provide preliminary insights that may contribute to a better understanding of this uncommon disorder. In this study, NDPH phenotypic analysis was not performed. In future research, we will expand the sample size to explore differences among different phenotypes. Additionally, the patient’s medication history was not documented or tracked in detail. The heterogeneity of pharmacological treatments may affect our findings, because their potential influence on iron deposition could not be fully accounted for. Further longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the impact of pharmacological treatments on iron metabolism. Finally, abnormal iron metabolism is thought to be associated with the disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). This study was limited to single QSM sequence, and future investigations incorporating multimodal MRI analysis, including BBB assessment, are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding.

Conclusions

These findings demonstrated that the iron deposition in right cerebellum lobule VIII, right insula, and left gyrus rectus of NDPH patients was significantly higher than that of the HCs. The abnormal iron metabolism in specific regions of the brain may contribute to the pathogenesis of NDPH patients. In the future, more studies are needed to further explore the role of iron metabolism in the pathogenesis of NDPH.
