Microenvironment-driven satellite cell regeneration and repair in aging-related sarcopenia: mechanisms and therapeutic frontiers
Authors: Na Li, Yushu Chen, Qiong Wang, Xiaoqin Liu, Chao Han, Chao Qu, Xin Guan, Wei Zou, Xiaomin Wang, Ang Li, Jing Liu, Yanfu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcopenia, a progressive age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and function, is closely linked to impaired regenerative capacity of satellite cells (SCs), also known as satellite cells. Age-dependent SCs dysfunction, driven by intrinsic senescence and niche dysregulation, disrupts activation, proliferation, and differentiation, thereby exacerbating regenerative deficits in sarcopenia. The SCs niche undergoes age-related remodeling, characterized by immune cell infiltration, ECM stiffening, and aberrant FAPs differentiation toward Fibro-Adipogenic lineages. Immune subsets orchestrate inflammation resolution and SCs activation during regeneration, while FAPs exhibit dual roles: transient pro-regenerative WISP1 secretion and chronic fibrotic conversion. Concurrently, vascular-neural networks sustain SCs quiescence and neuromuscular junction integrity, with age-related degradation of these systems exacerbating regenerative decline. Single-cell omics and 3D genomic studies have revealed heterotypic interactions and chromatin structural changes underlying SCs dysfunction in aging. Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting SCs rejuvenation and niche restoration—including metabolic regulation, endocrine interventions, and cell-based therapies—are complemented by advances in single-cell omics and 3D modeling technologies, which offer unprecedented opportunities to dissect niche complexity and identify novel therapeutic targets for sarcopenia. This review synthesizes recent advancements in understanding the role of SCs and their dynamic niche microenvironment in sarcopenia pathogenesis, exploring novel therapeutic strategies while underscoring the critical importance of deciphering their bidirectional interplay for developing effective interventions against age-related muscle loss.Keywords: Muscle stem cells (MuSCs), Satellite cells (SCs), Satellite cell niche, Fibro adipogenic precursors (FAPs), Extracellular matrix (ECM)

Introduction

Sarcopenia, first formally recognized by Rosenberg in 1989 [1], has evolved from a geriatric observation to a codified disease entity (ICD-10-CM code M62.84) affecting 10–27% of adults over 60 globally [2]. Currently, as defined by the criteria of the Asian Working Group, sarcopenia is regarded as a degenerative syndrome closely associated with aging, characterized by progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function [3]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the significant age-related deterioration of skeletal muscle regenerative capacity serves as a central driver of sarcopenia. The underlying mechanisms involve a complex interplay of cellular senescence mechanisms, including inflamm-aging (age-related chronic inflammation), mitochondrial bioenergetic collapse, proteostatic network dysregulation, and neuromuscular junction destabilization [4, 5]. Crucially, these processes converge to disrupt the functional integrity of muscle stem cells (MuSCs)–the tissue-resident sentinels for myogenic repair–thus establishing MuSCs dysfunction as both a biomarker and a mediator of sarcopenic pathology.

Mitochondrial dysfunction in aged SCs manifests as reduced oxidative phosphorylation, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and impaired mitophagy, directly contributing to regenerative decline. In aged murine SCs, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations accumulate, leading to dysfunctional electron transport chain complexes and ATP depletion [6]. This bioenergetic collapse activates the unfolded protein response (UPRmt), triggering p53-mediated senescence via p21CIP1 induction [7]. For example, in senescence-accelerated mouse prone 8 (SAMP8) models, SCs exhibit fragmented mitochondria and reduced mitophagy, correlating with impaired myogenic differentiation [8]. Nicotinamide riboside (NR) supplementation in aged mice restores NAD + levels, improves mitochondrial bioenergetics, and reduces SC senescence markers (p16INK4a, p21) [9]. Aged ECM undergoes pathological remodeling, increasing stiffness and activating YAP/TAZ signaling in SCs. This leads to mitochondrial fragmentation via Drp1 phosphorylation, impairing oxidative capacity. The study finds that H3K27ac significantly increases in senescent human and mouse skeletal muscles, leading to enhancer activation and upregulation of ECM genes, altering the microenvironment of satellite cells, reducing their myogenic potential and causing fibrogenic conversion, while JQ1 can inhibit this process and restore the myogenic differentiation potential of satellite cells [10]. Chronic inflammation in the aged niche disrupts mitochondrial dynamics [11]. Through single-cell transcriptome analysis of ischemic and non-ischemic muscles from CLTI patients and mouse models, this study found that the failure of skeletal muscle regeneration in ischemic-damaged limbs is associated with the signature of pro-inflammatory macrophages and premature differentiation of satellite cells [12]. 3D bioengineered muscle organoids recapitulate age-related mitochondrial dysfunction when cultured in stiff, pro-inflammatory niches, validating cross-talk mechanisms [13]. Age-related vascular decline reduces oxygen and nutrient delivery to SCs, inducing hypoxia-inducible factor 1α and switching metabolism to glycolysis. This metabolic shift impairs mitochondrial biogenesis. Aging-induced DNA damage in satellite cells (SCs) activates the ATM/ATR-Chk2-p53 pathway, leading to p21CIP1-mediated cell cycle arrest. In aged murine SCs, γH2AX foci (DNA damage markers) increase by 2.3-fold, correlating with reduced myogenic potential [14]. Aged SCs show reduced proteasome activity and accumulation of misfolded proteins, including p62 aggregates. This triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR) and SC senescence [15].

Skeletal muscle repair critically relies on resident MuSCs, also known as SCs, which reside within a specialized niche confined between the myofiber plasma membrane and the laminin-rich basement lamina and constitute a self-renewing reservoir supporting muscle maintenance and repair [16]. Their biological trajectory progresses through tightly regulated phases. Under homeostatic conditions, SCs remain quiescent, with sporadic asymmetric divisions replacing daily activity-damaged myofibers to maintain the stem cell pool. Upon tissue injury, SCs switch to symmetric divisions, generating self-renewing SCs and myoblasts that differentiate into myotubes to reconstruct myofibers and drive skeletal muscle regeneration [17]. Post-injury transcriptional reprogramming sequentially activates SCs, governing their activation, proliferation, differentiation, and skeletal muscle regeneration [18]. Upon activation, Pax7 expression is downregulated, with concomitant upregulation of myogenic differentiation protein MyoD driving proliferation, while myogenin (MyoG) induction mediates terminal differentiation [19]. This differentiation cascade–from activated SCs to proliferating myoblast and ultimately multinucleated myotube–exemplifies an evolutionarily conserved repair paradigm. Cellular senescence underlies functional decline in aged SCs. Both extrinsic factors (inflammatory responses, extracellular components, cell-cell interactions) and intrinsic stressors (oxidative stress, DNA damage, signaling pathway alterations, proteostatic disruption, metabolic dysregulation) induce SCs senescence, impairing their regenerative capacity [20, 21].

Recent studies reveal that aging disrupts core signaling cascades essential for SCs activation. Quiescent SCs maintain low metabolic activity through Notch signaling and p21-mediated cell cycle arrest, while activation requires coordinated downregulation of quiescence factors and upregulation of myogenic regulators. In quiescent SCs, Notch1 receptors bind to Jagged1 expressed by neighboring myofibers, activating the RBP-Jκ transcription complex to suppress MyoD expression and maintain the Pax7 + state [22]. Notably, a recent study further demonstrates that spermidine emerges as a key regulator in this process: it activates SCs and promotes muscle regeneration in mice by leveraging hypusinated eIF5A. Specifically, eIF5A orchestrates the translation of MyoD to facilitate spermidine-mediated SC activation. Moreover, spermidine exhibits the capacity to reactivate aged SCs and enhance muscle function in aged mice [23]. In aged SCs, hypermethylation of the Wnt9a promoter reduces its expression by 50%, accompanied by decreased nuclear localization of β-catenin and impaired proliferation [24]. In aged SCs, elevated TGF-β signaling aberrantly sustains quiescence by suppressing MyoD via miR-206-dependent inhibition of HDAC4, blocking myogenic commitment [25]. This pathway underscores how aging-induced disruptions in TGF-β homeostasis directly antagonize the myogenic activation programs that spermidine aims to restore, highlighting the complex interplay between pro-quiescent signals and regenerative interventions in aged muscle tissue.

Comparative studies under matched regenerative conditions demonstrate that aged SCs exhibit diminished repair capacity relative to young counterparts, attributable to accumulated intrinsic cellular damage [26, 27]. Emerging mechanistic insights implicate transcription factors TRIM32 and GSK-3α in MuSCs senescence, with murine models demonstrating that their suppression accelerates aging trajectories and sarcopenia progression [28, 29]. In replicative senescence murine models, SCs regenerative impairment correlates with aberrant DNA damage response (DDR) and compromised myotube differentiation, while DDR inhibition rescues differentiation capacity [14]. SCs persist postnatally but exhibit heightened sensitivity to cumulative intracellular damage. Aging-associated depletion of SCs, coupled with mutational accrual and regenerative decline, correlates strongly with sarcopenia pathogenesis [30, 31]. Mechanistic studies implicated dysregulated JAK/STAT3, FGF2, IGFBP5, WNT3α, and AMPK/SIRT1 pathways in driving SCs senescence, while microRNA-based therapies emerge as promising strategies to counteract pathological aging and enhance myoregeneration [15, 32].

SCs play a pivotal role in regulating skeletal muscle homeostasis, including growth, aging-related maintenance, and post-injury regeneration. The regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle is dependent on the dynamic regulation of SCs, and the functionality of SCs is tightly governed by their specialized niche [33]. This niche comprises a multicellular ensemble, including myofibers, the extracellular matrix, immune cells, Fibro Adipogenic Precursors (FAPs), and the vascular neural network, which collectively orchestrate SCs activation, proliferation, and differentiation [34] (Fig. 1). Crucially, niche homeostasis in SCs necessitates stringent maintenance of the quiescence-activation equilibrium. Concurrently, their dynamic behavioral shifts are precisely orchestrated by multicellular regulatory networks involving immune cells and FAPs through complex intercellular signaling cascades. Emerging evidence posits that SCs not only respond to niche-derived signaling cues but also actively orchestrate neighboring cell behaviors through paracrine/autocrine signaling [33]. SCs coordinate their activation, proliferation, differentiation, and self-renewal through intrinsic regulation and microenvironmental crosstalk, interacting with Notch, Wnt, and mTOR signaling cascades to govern skeletal muscle development, homeostasis, and repair [35, 36]. Transcriptomic profiling of SCs across ten murine age cohorts revealed progressive declines in population dynamics and differentiation potency during skeletal muscle maturation, with aged SCs exhibiting impaired myoregenerative and self-renewal capacities linked to TGFβ2, WNT9a, and FGFR4 signaling perturbations [37]. Aging concurrently drives niche remodeling marked by cellular composition shifts and transcriptional heterogeneity. Recent studies employing single-cell RNA sequencing and proteomics have elucidated niche alterations in aged animals—driven by immune cell infiltration and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling—as a key mechanism impairing SCs functionality [24]. These findings underscore the critical role of SCs and niche dynamics in skeletal muscle repair.

The field of sarcopenia research is currently experiencing a significant paradigm shift. Historically, muscle loss has been predominantly regarded as an inevitable consequence of atrophy that comes with aging. However, the current research trend is pivoting towards a more proactive approach, focusing on enhancing the regenerative competence of muscles. This is being achieved through the rewiring of the relationship between SCs and their surrounding niche. This comprehensive review aims to offer a detailed exploration of multiple aspects related to sarcopenia. First, it delves into the fundamental biology of SCs. Understanding SCs is crucial as they are the key players in muscle repair and regeneration. The review then moves on to examine the signaling dysregulation associated with aging that is mediated by the SCs niche. The niche, which is the microenvironment where SCs reside, plays a vital role in regulating their function. Aging can disrupt the normal signaling within this niche, leading to impaired muscle regeneration. In addition to the biological aspects, the review also presents various therapeutic strategies targeted at SCs for the treatment of sarcopenia. By exploring these strategies, it advances a systems-level understanding of age-related muscle degeneration. This holistic view takes into account not only the individual components of muscle biology but also how they interact with each other in the context of aging. One of the strengths of this review is its ability to synthesize three decades of research on SC biology. It combines this long - standing body of knowledge with the latest and most cutting-edge niche reprogramming strategies. Based on this synthesis, it proposes an integrative framework. This framework is comprehensive, spanning from the molecular circuitry that governs SC function to the potential therapeutic interventions that can be used to treat sarcopenia. In conclusion, this review serves as a valuable resource for researchers and clinicians in the field of sarcopenia. It not only summarizes the current state of knowledge but also provides a forward - looking perspective on potential future directions in research and treatment.

Satellite cell niche composition

Role of myofibers in the satellite cell niche

Myofibers, as core structural components of the SCs niche, dynamically isolate SCs from niche elements (e.g., immune cells, growth factors) via the basement membrane’s physical barrier while mediating direct contact-dependent mechanochemical signaling [38]. Functionally, myofibers secrete pleiotropic factors to orchestrate SCs quiescence-activation equilibrium. Study demonstrated that myofiber-derived oncostatin M suppresses SCs proliferation and differentiation via the JAK-STAT3 pathway to preserve quiescent stemness [39]. Systemic administration of the Yamanaka reprogramming factors (OSKM) activated muscle stem cells, promoted their proliferation, dynamically remodeled the niche microenvironment, and markedly improved skeletal muscle regeneration in murine models [40]. Furthermore, aberrant activation of oncostatin M signaling is shown to exacerbate age-related declines in myogenic differentiation capacity [41]. Beyond oncostatin M, myofibers secrete a spectrum of growth factors and cytokines that critically regulate SCs dynamics. Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), released by myofibers during mechanical loading or injury, activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in SCs to promote proliferation and suppress apoptosis [42]. Notably, aging reduces IGF-1 bioavailability, contributing to impaired SCs activation and delayed regeneration [43]. Similarly, fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) secreted by myofibers binds to FGFR1 on SCs, driving their exit from quiescence via ERK signaling; age-associated ECM stiffening disrupts FGF2-FGFR1 interactions, further compromising regenerative capacity [17]. Mechanical signals generated by myofiber contraction also modulate SCs behavior. Stretch-induced activation of the YAP/TAZ pathway in SCs enhances their proliferation and differentiation by upregulating MyoD and myogenin [44, 45]. Additionally, myofiber-derived extracellular vehicles (EVs) enriched with miRNAs (e.g., miR-206, miR-133) regulate SCs fate by targeting Pax7 and Notch signaling, highlighting a novel mode of intercellular communication [46]. Age-related declines in lactate secretion and MCT1 expression impair SCs bioenergetics, exacerbating sarcopenic pathology [47]. Moreover, ATP released from damaged myofibers acts through P2 × 7 receptors on SCs to induce calcium influx, triggering rapid activation and inflammatory cytokine production [48]. The ECM-myofiber interface also plays a pivotal role. Myofibers dynamically remodel the surrounding ECM by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade collagen IV to facilitate SCs migration during repair [49].

These findings collectively establish myofibers as dual-functional niche hubs—providing physical anchorage and dynamically regulating stem cell fate through secretory networks—thereby informing therapeutic strategies targeting myofiber-SCs crosstalk in sarcopenia intervention.

Dynamic regulation of the extracellular matrix in the satellite cell niche

The basal lamina, serving as the core extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffold of the SCs niche, is primarily composed of type IV collagen, laminins, and proteoglycans, providing physical anchorage for SCs and mediating mechanochemical signal transduction [50]. During aging, ECM undergoes pathological remodeling (increased collagen crosslinking, reduced elasticity), which suppresses MuSCs function via YAP/TAZ signaling, while targeting ECM biomechanics—the physical properties of the extracellular matrix and the mechanobiological processes through which cells perceive and respond to these properties—partially reverses senescence-associated phenotypes [51]. Integrins, transmembrane receptors that bind ECM components, form focal adhesions linking the ECM to the cytoskeleton. Mechanical forces alter integrin clustering and conformation, activating downstream signaling pathways such as FAK and Rho GTPases. These pathways regulate SCs quiescence, proliferation, and differentiation by modulating cytoskeletal dynamics and transcriptional programs [52, 53]. Stiffened ECM in aged muscle hyperactivates RhoA/ROCK signaling, stabilizing YAP/TAZ in the nucleus to drive SCs toward fibrogenic differentiation rather than myogenesis [54]. The transcriptional co-activators YAP and TAZ are central mediators of ECM biomechanical signaling. In soft ECM (physiological niche), YAP/TAZ remain cytoplasmic and inactive, maintaining SCs quiescence. In stiffened ECM (aged niche), mechanical stress triggers YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation, where they bind TEAD transcription factors to promote cell cycle entry and proliferation [55]. Paradoxically, chronic YAP/TAZ activation in aging disrupts SCs self-renewal by suppressing Pax7 expression and inducing premature differentiation [56, 57]. SCs actively remodel the ECM through secreted proteases (MMP2/9) and exosomes containing miRNAs [58]. These factors degrade excessive collagen and inhibit fibroblast activity, maintaining niche elasticity. In aging, diminished SCs secretory capacity disrupts this feedback loop, leading to ECM stiffening and impaired regeneration. Strategies to reverse age-related ECM dysfunction include Hydrogel-Based Biomaterialsand MMP Activation [59]. These interventions highlight ECM biomechanics as a pivotal target for rejuvenating aged muscle stem cells and combating sarcopenia.

Mechanotransduction through actin-integrin complexes enables SCs to sense ECM biomechanical cues, converting mechanical forces into intracellular signals that regulate proliferation and differentiation [60]. Notably, ECM composition exhibits developmental stage-specificity: Tierney team identified fetal SCs-enriched tenascin-C, fibronectin, and type VI collagen as epigenetic reprogrammers of adult SCs, conferring enhanced regenerative potential [61]. During aging, ECM undergoes pathological remodeling (increased collagen crosslinking, reduced elasticity), which suppresses MuSCs function via YAP/TAZ signaling, while targeting ECM biomechanics partially reverses senescence-associated phenotypes [55]. Furthermore, SCs secrete miR-206-enriched exosomes that suppress fibroblast collagen biosynthesis by targeting Rrbp1, establishing a negative feedback loop to maintain ECM homeostasis and prevent excessive fibrotic deposition [62]. The ECM acts not merely as static structural support but as a dynamic biomechanical-biochemical signaling hub governing stem cell fate and tissue repair. Such bidirectional regulatory mechanisms unveil novel avenues for ECM-targeted interventions in sarcopenia.

Neural regulation of the satellite cell niche

Neuromuscular junctions (NMJs), as critical niche components, regulate SCs proliferation, differentiation, and muscle homeostasis via neurotrophin secretion [63, 64]. Bidirectional SCs-NMJ crosstalk ensures muscle regeneration and functional integrity: SCs secrete neurotrophic factors (NTFs) to sustain NMJ synaptic plasticity, while NMJ-derived signals (e.g., acetylcholine receptor activation) reciprocally modulate SCs quiescence-activation dynamics [65, 66]. Aging drives NMJ-associated SCs subpopulation depletion and dysfunction, with disrupted Schwann cell (SC) communication exacerbating NMJ structural degeneration and muscle atrophy [67]. Mechanistically, SC-SCs interactions via the Notch/Jagged1 axis spatiotemporally coordinate NMJ repair, whereas premature SCs activation disrupts this network, inducing YAP-dependent signaling dysregulation and impaired regeneration [68]. Beyond neural regulation, nutritional modulation emerges as a pivotal determinant of MuSCs dynamics through growth factor-mediated pathways. The nutrient-growth factor-MuSCs axis represents a critical regulatory framework wherein systemic and local nutrient availability synergizes with growth factor signaling to govern MuSCs dynamics. Central to this axis is the interplay between metabolic substrates and signaling molecules: branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), particularly leucine, activate mTORC1 signaling in MuSCs through Sestrin2-mediated disinhibition [69]. Activated mTORC1 promotes proliferation and suppresses autophagy, a process essential for stem cell pool maintenance. Aging-associated declines in circulating BCAAs impair mTORC1 activity, driving MuSCs quiescence and regenerative decline [70]. For instance, leucine-enriched diets reactivate mTORC1 in aged MuSCs, improving muscle mass [71]. This metabolic regulation converges with IGF-1/PI3K-Akt pathway integration. IGF-1 secretion, potentiated by protein intake, binds MuSCs receptors to activate PI3K-Akt signaling, enhancing MyoD expression while inhibiting FoxO-mediated apoptosis [72]. Therefore, positioning the nutrient-growth factor-MuSCs axis as a key frontier for developing interventions targeting age-related sarcopenia and neuromuscular degeneration.These insights pioneer neural niche-targeted strategies to counteract sarcopenic muscle decline.

The MTJ, a specialized interface between muscle fibers and tendons, undergoes age-related degenerative changes that parallel sarcopenic pathology. Notably, MTJ integrity relies on bidirectional interactions between muscle fibers, tendon fibroblasts, and SCs [73]. Aging-induced structural remodeling of the MTJ—marked by folding loss, collagen disorganization, and reduced mechanical stability—impairs force transmission and exacerbates muscle atrophy, while molecular alterations in MTJ-specific proteins and ECM components disrupt mechanotransduction and drive satellite cell senescence, ultimately correlating with clinical manifestations of reduced muscle strength and functional decline, particularly in load-bearing muscles of older adults [74]. Young and aged mice were injected with saline or human LM-111, and then the myotendinous junction of the gastrocnemius muscle was removed to apply chronic mechanical stimulation to the plantaris muscle. It was found that LM-111 increased the protein expression of α7B integrin in aged muscle and caused its clustering near the cell nuclei, reduced CD11b + immune cells, enhanced repair, and improved the growth response of aged plantaris muscle to loading [75]. Satellite cells (SCs) and the MTJ engage in bidirectional crosstalk, where age-related MTJ stiffening (e.g., collagen crosslinking) impairs SCs mechanosensitivity, leading to aberrant cell cycle arrest and diminished regeneration [76].

Role of endothelial cells in the satellite cell niche

Research demonstrated that proliferating SCs predominantly localize near vascular structures, where endothelial cells (ECs) promote SCs proliferation through secretory factors including IGF-1, HGF, PDGF-BB, and VEGF [77]. Conversely, differentiated myogenic cells exhibited pro-angiogenic effects in vitro. Using fluorescent labeling and three-dimensional imaging in murine and human muscle tissues, researchers revealed that SCs recruit capillary networks via VEGFA secretion, establishing a microenvironment conducive to their quiescence and self-renewal [78]. Beyond their conventional roles in hemodynamic regulation and metabolic exchange, skeletal muscle vasculature interacts with myogenic precursor cells and SCs through microvascular architecture to modulate muscle development and regeneration [79]. Pericytes, specialized perivascular cells, critically regulate the MuSCs niche. Through lineage tracing and quantitative analysis in human muscle, researchers demonstrated that pericytes coordinate myogenic differentiation and SCs maintenance via IGF-1 and ANGPT1 secretion, respectively [80]. Pericyte ablation disrupted SCs functionality and impaired regeneration, highlighting their indispensable role in muscle homeostasis. These findings elucidated the spatiotemporally coupled nature of endothelial-SCs interactions, providing a mechanistic foundation for sarcopenia therapies targeting vasculo-myogenic.

Immune cell dynamics in the satellite cell niche

Macrophages affect satellite cells by secreting various cytokines and growth factors. For example, the decrease in the secretion of growth differentiation factor-3 (GDF3) by macrophages due to aging has a negative impact on adult myogenesis, reducing the number of satellite cells and their myogenic capacity. SCs reciprocally induced macrophage oxidative phosphorylation via IGF-2 secretion, establishing a pro-regenerative anti-inflammatory feedback loop [81]. Genetic ablation models confirmed that macrophage depletion caused regenerative failure, underscoring their indispensability [82]. This spatiotemporal coupling of phenotypic switching and metabolic reprogramming pioneered macrophage-SCs axis-targeted therapies for muscle degenerative diseases. On the other hand, the increase in the secretion of osteopontin (OPN) by macrophages disrupts the normal inflammatory response to muscle injury, leading to regenerative defects [83–85]. Study further demonstrated that neutrophils coordinated macrophage activation and SCs function through paracrine signaling, establishing a regulatory axis for regenerative cascades [86]. This temporally phased immune remodeling provides critical insights into regenerative failure in aging and disease contexts.

T cells with immunomodulatory functions contributed to satellite cell niche homeostasis by modulating inflammatory response intensity and duration [87]. T cell-derived cytokines activated the STAT3/NF-κB axis to drive SCs expansion in vitro, while IL-1α/TNF-α signaling curbed inflammation-mediated stem cell pool exhaustion, collectively forming a bidirectional immunoregulatory network that integrated immune surveillance with pro-regenerative signaling [88, 89]. CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) can attract macrophages by secreting chemokine ligand-2 (CCL2), indirectly promoting the proliferation of satellite cells, and may also directly act on myogenic cells to promote muscle regeneration [89, 90]. The interleukin-10 (IL-10) secreted by CD4 + regulatory T cells (Treg cells) can promote the transformation of macrophages into M2 type and can also secrete amphiregulin to directly act on myogenic cells, increasing their differentiation ability [91]. Aging may affect the functions and numbers of these lymphocytes, thereby influencing satellite cells.

Aging is accompanied by systemic changes in the immune system, affecting many myeloid and lymphoid cell populations that can influence muscle regeneration, leading to a state of chronic inflammation. The expression of various cytokines and molecules in chronic inflammation changes [92]. For example, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα) and interferon-γ (IFNγ) increases, while the expression of factors beneficial to muscle regeneration such as insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF1) decreases [93]. These changes affect the function of satellite cells and the muscle regeneration process, which may lead to the progressive loss of muscle mass and function.

Role of faps in the satellite cell niche

Fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), multipotent mesenchymal progenitor cells residing in skeletal muscle, exhibit differentiation potential into fibroblasts, adipocytes, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes [94]. Dysregulated inflammatory responses triggered pathological FAPs accumulation, driving muscle fibrosis, adipogenesis, and impaired regeneration—a finding that implicated FAPs in sarcopenia pathogenesis [95]. Given their pleiotropic functions, FAPs have emerged as a focal point in muscle regeneration research over the past decade. In 2019, through single-cell sequencing analysis, a sub-population of FAPs characterized by the expression of TIE2 and VCAM1 in muscles was found to enable the muscle to adapt to physiological and pathological stimuli of growth and injury. Macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses were regulated and muscle regeneration was promoted by injury - activated VCAM1 + FAPs [96].

FAPs are mesenchymal progenitors governing ECM remodeling and immune-SC crosstalk. In aging, chronic inflammation fundamentally reprograms FAP behavior. Pro-inflammatory cytokines drive FAPs toward fibro-adipogenic differentiation via STAT3/PPARγ activation. This shift is self-reinforcing—senescent FAPs overproduce TGF-β, which further activates fibrotic SMAD signaling in SCs and suppresses myogenesis while promoting collagen deposition [97].

A randomized controlled trial study was conducted with older adults aged 70 to 89 as the research subjects [98]. They were divided into a physical activity group and a health education control group, aiming to explore the effects of physical activity on the strength and skeletal muscle fat infiltration in older adults. The results showed that moderate physical activity could prevent the further decline of muscle strength and the increase of muscle fat infiltration in older adults, and the effect of physical activity on muscle fat was site-specific. FAP-SCs interdependencies have been further delineated by recent advances [99]. FAP-SCs interdependencies have been further delineated by recent advances. In PDGFRα CreER murine models, FAPs depletion was shown to impair SCs proliferation, reduce their regenerative pool, and exacerbate muscle atrophy—directly substantiating FAPs’ essential role in myogenic maintenance [100]. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that activated FAPs upregulated WNT1-inducible signaling pathway protein 1 (WISP1) to enhance SCs expansion and differentiation [101]. Additionally, regeneration was facilitated by FAPs through matrix remodeling [102] and orchestration of SCs-immune interactions [103–105]. Notably, Osr1—a recently identified FAP-specific transcription factor—was demonstrated to integrate inflammatory modulation, matrix secretion, and SCs-driven regeneration, highlighting FAPs as master regulators of muscle homeostasis [106].

Single-cell sequencing revealed the inherent heterogeneity of FAPs, identifying FAP-like adipose stromal cells (ASCs) in subcutaneous adipose tissue (ScAT). Both ASC infiltration blockade and ScAT-derived ASC depletion impaired muscle regeneration [107]. Breakthroughs in single-cell transcriptomics enabled systematic characterization of FAP heterogeneity and their context-dependent molecular interactions during muscle regeneration and disease progression. This cellular diversity underscored FAPs’ multifaceted regulatory roles: in acute injury, FAPs rapidly entered the cell cycle and secreted trophic factors supporting myogenic activity of SCs. Conversely, dysregulated FAPs activation was mechanistically linked to fibroadipogenic tissue accumulation in pathological conditions such as muscular dystrophy and aging [108].

Satellite Cell-Mediated regeneration and Aging-Associated sarcopenia

The degenerative pathology of sarcopenia is intrinsically linked to the functional decline of SCs, wherein aging drives a triad of detrimental adaptations: (1) marked reductions in regenerative potential due to impaired damage sensing and myogenic commitment [21]; (2) destabilization of the quiescence-activation equilibrium, characterized by aberrant Notch/Wnt signaling crosstalk [32]; (3) a self-reinforcing vicious cycle of cell-autonomous damage coupled with niche deterioration (chronic low-grade inflammation, ECM stiffening via collagen crosslinking) [109, 110]. Recent advances in niche-centric studies have elucidated intricate regulatory networks involving SCs and their niche-associated components—including FAPs, macrophages, and endothelial cells [111]. These spatiotemporally coordinated signaling systems not only orchestrate muscle repair processes but also provide novel therapeutic strategies to enhance SCs regenerative capacity, offering promising avenues to combat sarcopenia.

Fundamental advances in satellite cell repair mechanisms

Ex vivo strategies to enhance satellite cell regenerative capacity

Therapeutic strategies targeting SCs functional rejuvenation focused on precision interventions in aging-associated signaling pathways. The cell cycle regulator p16INK4a, a core senescence marker, drove regenerative impairment by accumulating aberrantly to block SCs self-renewal and asymmetric division [112]. Pharmacological inhibition of p38α/β MAPK and mTORC1 signaling downregulated p16INK4a expression and restored stem cell activity [27, 113]. Mechanistically, the zinc-finger transcription factor Slug, a direct transcriptional repressor of p16INK4a, was markedly reduced in aged SCs; its functional loss underlay p16INK4a-dependent senescence, while exogenous Slug overexpression reversed regenerative deficits, nominating it as an epigenetic therapeutic target [114]. These findings provided the molecular basis for developing small-molecule inhibitors or gene-editing therapies targeting the p16INK4a/Slug axis.

Cellular senescence in SCs is a key driver of sarcopenia, governed by interlinked pathways including p16INK4a/pRB and p53/p21CIP1. While p16INK4a has been highlighted for its role in blocking SC self-renewal, emerging evidence underscores the critical contributions of p53 and p21 to age-related SC dysfunction. In aged SCs, DNA damage and oxidative stress activate p53, leading to p21 upregulation and impaired myogenic differentiation [115]. Overexpression of p21 in mouse skeletal muscle induces hallmark senescence features, including DNA damage foci, mitochondrial dysfunction, and SASP factor secretion (e.g., IL-6, MMPs). This leads to muscle atrophy, fibrosis, and impaired regeneration, phenocopying sarcopenic pathologies [7]. In chronic kidney disease models, p21 expression correlates with muscle wasting, and its inhibition by senolytic drugs restores SC proliferative capacity [116].

Rejuvenation strategies based on SCs reprogramming advanced to preclinical validation: small-molecule inhibitors (e.g., p38 MAPK blockers, SETD7 methyltransferase antagonists) enhanced aged SCs expansion in vitro via epigenetic remodeling, while transient mRNA transfection of nuclear reprogramming factors (OCT4/SOX2/KLF4) reversed senescence in human SCs and restored muscle strength in aged mice post-transplantation, validating allogeneic cell therapy feasibility [117, 118]. However, clinical translation was hindered by critical bottlenecks—bioengineered culture systems mimicking the biophysical niche properties (e.g., stiffness, topography) were required to preserve SCs stemness and lineage commitment. Future studies had to integrate microfluidic organ - on - chip platforms and mechanosignaling modulation to optimize ex vivo expansion protocols for personalized cell therapies.

Biomimetic models for human skeletal muscle regeneration

The inherent limitations of traditional animal models in recapitulating complex pathological phenotypes of human aging and disease drove innovations in muscle organoids and bioengineering technologies. Human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived 3D artificial skeletal muscle organoids modeled pathological features (e.g., muscular dystrophy) accurately, providing humanized platforms for mechanistic studies [119]. Tissue-engineered models integrated myogenic cells with biomimetic scaffold materials to replicate muscle contraction in vitro while maintaining cellular polarity, transitioning muscle regeneration research from 2D cultures to 3D physiological contexts [120]. Notably, bioengineered skeletal muscle platforms have been specifically employed to study aging-related mechanisms, such as senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASP) and impaired regenerative capacity. For instance, Madden et al. demonstrated that human myobundles recapitulate age-dependent declines in satellite cell activation and myofiber hypertrophy, offering a translational model to test pro-regenerative therapies [121]. Similarly, Rajabian et al. developed a bioengineered skeletal muscle system to investigate aging-associated ECM remodeling and mitochondrial dysfunction, identifying mechanosensitive pathways as key targets for intervention [13].

Further advancements include dynamic hydrogel technologies that adapt to injury biomechanics, spatiotemporally releasing stem cells and suppressing fibrotic signaling to achieve niche remodeling [122]. Hicks further refined these models by identifying ERBB3/NGFR-marked progenitors in human skeletal muscle, enabling precise lineage tracing in engineered tissues [123]. Future research would focus on multimodal biofabrication strategies—combining electroactive hydrogel-mediated neuro-muscular co-cultures, optogenetically controlled mechanostimulation devices, and AI-powered drug screening platforms—to advance muscle organoids toward translational clinical applications.

Satellite Cell-Targeted therapeutic strategies for sarcopenia

In vivo modulation of the niche microenvironment

Recent advances in localized and systemic interventions in murine models demonstrated promising potential to reverse age-related muscle regenerative decline, offering hope for sarcopenia treatment. Inhibition of mTORC1 signaling remained the most effective intervention to delay age-associated pathophysiological changes [124, 125]. Although mTORC1 was essential for muscle growth, its hyperactivation in aged muscle and SCs aligned with observations in rodent sarcopenia models, suggesting partial mTORC1 suppression might mitigate disease progression [126, 127]. Genetic studies confirmed that rapamycin/rapalog treatment enhanced aged muscle regeneration by augmenting SCs functionality through autophagy activation and senescence prevention [113]. However, chronic mTORC1 inhibition reduced myofiber diameter and mitochondrial content. Mechanistically, rapamycin inhibits mTORC1-driven cap-dependent translation, reducing myofibrillar protein accretion [127, 128]. MTORC1 maintains postsynaptic AChR clustering, rapamycin exacerbates age-related NMJ fragmentation, disproportionately affecting type II fibers [129]. MTORC1 coordinates PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial gene expression, its inhibition downregulates TFAM and NRF1 [130]. Thus, while mTORC1 inhibition rescues SC regenerative capacity, its impact on myofiber hypertrophy and mitochondria necessitates precision dosing or combinatorial approaches to fully leverage therapeutic potential in sarcopenia.

Additionally, rapamycin/rapalog treatment delayed murine skeletal muscle aging and improved muscle function and architectural integrity, though it reduced myofiber diameter and mitochondrial content—a negative effect counterbalanced by the identification of neuromuscular junctions as critical nodes of mTORC1-driven muscle atrophy, potentially serving as therapeutic targets for muscle wasting.

Using genetic engineering and viral vectors, researchers expressed pluripotency-inducing transcription factors (OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, c-MYC) in murine myofibers or SCs, demonstrating that myofiber-specific expression suppressed Wnt4 transcription via the p53-p21 axis to promote MuSCs activation and proliferation [40]. Despite these promising advances, technical limitations persist. The hierarchical organization of dysregulated pathways within SCs and their local/systemic environments remains incompletely understood, necessitating further mechanistic exploration.

Growth factors represent a promising class of agents to rejuvenate the aged SC niche by recapitulating youthful signaling dynamics. HGF promotes SC activation and migration by binding c-Met receptors, stimulating myogenic differentiation and suppressing fibrosis. This study performed single-cell RNA sequencing on porcine skeletal muscles, combined with various bioinformatics analyses and functional experiments, revealed a novel interaction between muscle satellite cells and fibro-adipogenic progenitors mediated by FGF7-FGFR2, and found that FGF7 can promote the proliferation of satellite cells, which is beneficial to muscle regeneration and counteracts age-related myopathies [131]. Metrnl acts on macrophages via Stat3, inducing IGF-1 to promote satellite cell proliferation, offering a therapeutic target for muscle regeneration in aging and myopreveals [132]. MSCs transplanted via porous alginate cryogels can secrete bioactive factors affecting muscle progenitor cell functions. Stimulation with IGF-1 and VEGF165 enhances their paracrine effects. The synthetic niche of multifunctional alginate cryogels regulating MSC paracrine signaling improves muscle function in skeletal muscle injury models, outperforming standalone cell or growth factor delivery [133]. One study, by means of single-cell RNA sequencing and other methods, has found that quiescent MSCs in the inflammatory microenvironment after soft tissue injury can transform and differentiate. A variety of immune cells affect this process, and immune cells activate relevant signaling pathways. This achievement is expected to advance the treatment of HO [133]. While these strategies demonstrate preclinical efficacy, challenges remain in optimizing delivery systems (e.g., sustained-release biomaterials, targeted viral vectors) and minimizing off-target effects. Combinatorial approaches integrating growth factors, stem cells, and biomechanical cues may unlock synergistic benefits, paving the way for precision therapies against age-related muscle decline.

Metabolic and dietary modulation therapies

Metabolic modulation in preclinical models is achieved via caloric restriction or pharmacological mimetics. Short-term caloric restriction or rapamycin analogs enhanced aged SCs quantity and post-transplantation regenerative capacity by suppressing mTORC1 signaling, a mechanism involving mitophagy activation and epigenetic reprogramming [134]. Similarly, intermittent fasting regimens in aged mice improved SCs self-renewal by activating AMPK/SIRT1 pathways, mitigating age-related mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [9]. Intriguingly, rodent studies employing rodent models of caloric restriction revealed that both acute and chronic regimens failed to alter mitochondrial function or glycolytic capacity, but consistently enhanced myogenic colony formation—indicating that satellite stem cell functional adaptations are not universally dependent on metabolic remodeling [135]. This dissociation between functional SCs adaptation and canonical metabolic remodeling underscores the existence of non-metabolically driven pathways governing satellite cell plasticity [76]. While short-term caloric restriction enhances SC function in mice, human compliance remains a major challenge. Long-term caloric restriction in humans may lead to malnutrition, bone density loss, and hormonal imbalances, which could counteract muscle preservation.

Beyond direct metabolic modulation, nutritional interventions exhibit synergistic potential in combating sarcopenia. Multi-component supplements containing bioactive compounds such as whey protein have demonstrated efficacy in promoting type II fiber hypertrophy by augmenting SCs activation post-injury [136]. Furthermore, dietary supplementation with 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 (25OHD3), calcium-phosphorus complexes, glutamine, and short-chain fatty acids enhances SCs abundance and functional competence, synergistically promoting skeletal muscle hypertrophy through multimodal mechanisms. Some research has delved into the influence of dietary interventions on aged rodent models of sarcopenia. Research investigated the effect of a specific nutrient-rich diet on muscle mass and strength. The results showed that the diet rich in certain amino acids and antioxidants could mitigate the decline in muscle mass and improve muscle function in these aged mice, which was associated with the regulation of muscle protein synthesis and the reduction of oxidative stress - key factors in sarcopenia development [137].

Mitochondrial-targeted therapies are gaining traction. Nicotinamide riboside (NR), a NAD + precursor, improved SCs function in aged mice by rescuing mitochondrial bioenergetics and reducing SASP factor secretion [6]. Similarly, glutamine supplementation in geriatric rodent models enhanced SCs proliferation and attenuated muscle atrophy via mTORC1 activation, demonstrating its potential as a nutraceutical intervention [138]. These interventions optimize calcium signaling, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and epigenetic regulation to bolster myogenic differentiation and attenuate age-related stem cell senescence, offering translatable nutritional strategies for muscle mass preservation and sarcopenia mitigation.

Exercise protocols as satellite cell rejuvenation strategies

Emerging evidence delineates that exercise modality, intensity, and duration differentially modulate SCs dynamics [139]. Resistance training, for instance, induces age-dependent delays in SCs activation kinetics, with geriatric populations exhibiting protracted responsiveness compared to younger counterparts—a phenomenon attributed to blunted mechanotransduction signaling and impaired niche-derived growth factor secretion [140]. Notably, high-intensity interval training (HIIT) counteracts streptozotocin (STZ)-induced muscle atrophy and myonuclear depletion by promoting SCs-mediated myonuclear replenishment. This process restores nuclear morphology and spatial positioning within regenerating myofibers, thereby rescuing transcriptional homeostasis [141]. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of tailored exercise regimens to rejuvenate SCs functionality, though individualized protocols must account for age-related molecular desensitization and comorbidities.

In parallel, muscle contraction and endurance training exert distinct yet complementary effects on SCs biology. Muscle contraction promoted SCs recruitment and differentiation via the apelin/APJ axis, while endurance training improved SCs self-renewal and damage responsiveness through mitochondrial oxidative stress mitigation and niche microenvironment optimization. Exercise augments SCs-mediated repair mechanisms: Joanisse demonstrated that exercise improves aged SCs responsiveness to injury [142]. Muscle contraction-induced apelin secretion promotes SCs-dependent muscle repair by activating APJ receptor signaling [143]. Abreu further showed that endurance training expands the SCs pool and enhances post-injury regeneration, correlating with suppressed mitochondrial oxidative stress and optimized niche microenvironments that sustain SCs self-renewal [144].

Combined melatonin supplementation and exercise training reversed skeletal muscle functional decline and obesity-induced atrophy by improving mitochondrial bioenergetics, expanding Pax7 + satellite cell pools, reducing cellular senescence, and promoting myogenic differentiation in preclinical models [145]. Thus, lifestyle modifications targeting cellular metabolism —including caloric restriction, exercise, and nutraceutical adjuvants—represent low-risk, high-efficacy strategies to boost SCs activity, improve muscle regenerative potential, and mitigate sarcopenia progression. Critically, future research must prioritize precision dosing regimens and combinatorial therapies to overcome interindividual variability and maximize clinical translatability.

Endocrine therapies to enhance satellite cell regenerative capacity

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) offered a viable option for exercise-intolerant elderly individuals, serving as a classical strategy to mitigate muscle atrophy [146]. The androgen receptor ligand testosterone, a canonical anabolic regulator, enhanced muscle regeneration by promoting protein translational efficiency and driving SCs cell cycle progression. However, its clinical application was limited by adverse effects, including thrombotic events and elevated prostate cancer risk [147, 148]. Melatonin, a low-risk alternative, suppressed muscle atrophy via the IGF-1/AKT-mTOR axis while balancing adipose-muscle metabolic crosstalk, demonstrating multi-target protective effects in obesity and aging models [149].

Estrogen exerted multidimensional protective effects in sarcopenia prevention and treatment by coordinating skeletal muscle regeneration, metabolic homeostasis (promoting protein synthesis/inhibiting catabolism), and inflammation resolution (via NF-κB pathway suppression) through ERα/ERβ-mediated genomic and non-genomic signaling [150, 151]. Mechanistically, estrogen deficiency disrupted SCs activation (Notch/Wnt dysregulation) and impaired exercise-induced regeneration [152, 153]. The phytoestrogen genistein enhanced SCs terminal differentiation by suppressing miR-221/222-mediated epigenetic repression, highlighting the therapeutic potential of natural compounds [154]. Preclinical studies demonstrated that estradiol replacement therapy significantly expanded the SCs pool and improved regenerative capacity [155–159]. A randomized controlled trial by Dam team [160] further revealed that estrogen combined with resistance training reversed SCs depletion in postmenopausal women. However, clinical translation of estrogen therapy was hindered by dose-dependent oncogenic risks (breast/endometrial cancers) and interindividual response variability. Future research had to focus on developing tissue-selective estrogen receptor modulators and precision delivery systems to optimize efficacy-safety profiles.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of sarcopenia has evolved from a linear narrative of age-related muscle wasting to a multidimensional dialogue between cellular senescence and niche dysregulation (Fig. 2). This synthesis of three decades of SCs biology and niche reprogramming research reveals that sarcopenia is not merely a passive outcome of aging but an active disruption of the regenerative ecosystem. This discussion aims to synthesize the findings presented in the manuscript, offering a comprehensive analysis of the mechanisms underlying SCs dysfunction in aging, the role of the niche in regulating SCs behavior, and the therapeutic potential of targeting SCs and their niche to combat sarcopenia. By integrating these insights, we propose a systems-level understanding of age-related muscle degeneration and highlight future research directions.

Molecular and Cellular Dynamics of Satellite Cell-Mediated Muscle Regeneration and Sarcopenia Progression: Schematic representation of satellite cell (SC) differentiation, niche interactions, and pathological perturbations in sarcopenia. The diagram illustrates SC activation, myogenic differentiation, and age-related dysfunction. Quiescent SCs reside adjacent to myofibers (red), maintaining low metabolic activity via Notch signaling and p21 expression. Activation triggers include mechanical stress, growth factors (HGF, WNT4), and macrophage-derived cytokines. Asymmetric division generates self-renewing SCs and activated myoblasts, marked by MyoD induction. Proliferating myoblasts express MyoD and cyclin D1, transitioning to myocytes via upregulation of MyoG. Terminal differentiation involves fusion into myotubes, characterized by MHC (myosin heavy chain) expression and sarcomere assembly. Mature myofibers integrate into muscle tissue, supported by endothelial cells and Schwann cells for vascular and neural connectivity. Senescence markers accumulate in aged SCs, impairing self-renewal and differentiation. Downstream consequences involve reduced myonuclear accretion, myofiber atrophy, and impaired regeneration, culminating in sarcopenia

The functional decline of SCs in sarcopenia has long been attributed to cell-autonomous defects—telomere attrition, mitochondrial dysfunction, and epigenetic drift. However, emerging evidence positions niche dysregulation as an equally critical driver. Aged SCs are not isolated victims of intrinsic damage but are entrapped in a maladaptive microenvironment that amplifies senescence while suppressing regeneration. The role of niche extends beyond passive signaling to active reprogramming of SCs fate. This pathological niche remodeling establishes a self-reinforcing loop: SCs depleted by senescence reduce trophic support for niche homeostasis, further destabilizing the microenvironment. Thus, sarcopenia emerges not from SCs failure alone but from the collapse of bidirectional SCs-niche crosstalk—a concept demanding therapeutic strategies that co-target both compartments.

The SCs niche, composed of myofibers, ECM, immune cells, and vasculature, offers multiple targets for therapeutic intervention. The immune system’s dual role in muscle repair—orchestrating regeneration while priming chronic inflammation—exemplifies the niche’s complexity. Single-cell omics, particularly scRNA-seq, has fundamentally transformed our understanding of SCs heterogeneity and dynamic niche remodeling. For instance, Cutler et al. [161] employed scRNA-seq to decipher niche dynamics during muscle repair, revealing how macrophage and endothelial subsets direct activated SCs back to quiescence via secreted factors like SPP1—demonstrating that niche remodeling drives SC fate heterogeneity. Similarly, Zhang et al. [162] combined scRNA-seq with spatial transcriptomics to uncover a MPC macrophage and SC signaling cascade in hypertrophy: mesenchymal progenitors (MPCs) secrete Granulin to polarize macrophages, which subsequently activate SCs through IGF1, elucidating multicellular coordination of SC behavior. Additionally, Hong et al. [163]synthesized scRNA-seq evidence showing that aging reshapes both SC heterogeneity and niche cells, causing bidirectional communication failure that drives functional decline and fibrotic propensity. Collectively, these advances highlight scRNA-seq’s indispensable role in resolving SC heterogeneity, decoding niche interactions, and exposing pathological mechanisms—providing a molecular blueprint for targeted interventions. In youth, neutrophils and macrophages execute temporally precise roles: early M1 macrophages clear debris and activate SCs proliferation via IL-6/STAT3, while late M2 macrophages resolve inflammation and support differentiation [164]. Aging corrupts this coordination, fostering a pro-inflammatory milieu where M1 macrophages persist, secreting TNF-α that induces SCs senescence via p38 MAPK hyperactivation [165]. Notably, SCs themselves modulate immune behavior—senescent SCs release SASP factors that recruit cytotoxic T cells, exacerbating inflammatory niche damage. This vicious cycle underscores the need for immune-targeted therapies that preserve regenerative inflammation while curbing chronicity [166].

However, translating these strategies to humans requires addressing species-specific differences in niche composition and signaling dynamics. Current strategies to rejuvenate SCs face a paradox: interventions effective in murine models often exhibit limited translatability or adverse effects in humans. For example, mTORC1 inhibitors like rapamycin enhance SCs autophagy and mitigate senescence but concurrently suppress myofiber hypertrophy, potentially accelerating atrophy. Similarly, OSKM reprogramming factors rejuvenate SCs but risk teratoma formation if delivered systemically [167]. These challenges demand precision approaches that spatially and temporally restrict therapeutic activity. Notably, FAPs emerge as key regulators of niche homeostasis. Their dual role in supporting regeneration and driving fibrosis necessitates context-specific targeting. Strategies to polarize FAPs toward a pro-regenerative phenotype, such as inhibiting PDGFRα signaling, may mitigate sarcopenia progression [168]. Additionally, neural regulation via NMJ-SCs crosstalk presents an underexplored avenue, with acetylcholine receptor agonists potentially enhancing SCs activation in aged muscle [169].

Current interventions for sarcopenia target SCs through diverse approaches, each with distinct advantages and limitations. First, metabolic and dietary strategies hold potential for addressing sarcopenia but are confronted with substantial translational obstacles. For instance, individual tolerance to caloric restriction differs significantly, with some patients finding it difficult to persist because of adverse effects such as hypoglycemia, while the prolonged use of rapamycin analogs—effective in animal models—may hinder muscle fiber hypertrophy. Moreover, there is currently a lack of unified standards for the optimal dosage and duration of metabolic and dietary interventions, and their mechanisms of action can vary remarkably among individuals, thus making the precise application of such strategies particularly challenging. Second, exercise interventions can improve muscle stem cell function, yet the exercise capacity of elderly patients is often constrained by age-related physiological decline. For example, patients with severe arthritis or cardiovascular diseases may be unable to tolerate high-intensity resistance training. Additionally, the diversity of exercise modalities and individual differences pose significant challenges in formulating standardized exercise protocols. Notably, the current lack of effective biomarkers to precisely identify different subtypes of sarcopenia limits the development of precision exercise medicine, making it difficult for clinicians to design targeted exercise regimens based on patients’ specific conditions.​ Finally, endocrine therapies hold potential for improving muscle stem cell function but are associated with safety risks. For instance, traditional hormone replacement therapies may cause local concentration spikes due to uneven systemic distribution, leading to adverse effects like increased thrombosis risk or prostate hyperplasia. Efficacy is significantly influenced by patient biological heterogeneity, with pronounced interindividual variability in hormonal responses. Furthermore, the current regulatory framework for endocrine therapies is incomplete, hindering large-scale clinical implementation.​.

To bridge the gap between research and clinical application, we suggest the following clinical trial designs to test the proposed interventions: First, conduct RCTs to evaluate the efficacy of personalized nutritional plans in improving muscle mass and function in sarcopenic patients. Include long-term follow-up to assess sustainability and safety. Additionally, design trials that combine metabolic interventions with exercise or endocrine therapies to determine synergistic effects. Meanwhile, implement adaptive exercise trials that adjust exercise intensity and type based on patient progress and feedback. To further personalize interventions, include biomarker assessments to stratify sarcopenia subtypes and tailor treatments. For elderly populations with limited mobility, conduct trials to test the efficacy and safety of exercise-mimetic drugs in improving muscle function. Moreover, conduct phase II/III trials to evaluate the efficacy and safety of muscle-selective androgen receptor modulators and tissue-selective estrogen receptor modulators in sarcopenic patients. Finally, use biomarkers to guide patient selection and monitor treatment response in endocrine therapy trials, ensuring personalized and effective interventions.

Satellite cells (SCs) are central to muscle development and regeneration, offering significant therapeutic potential for combating degenerative muscle disorders. This review underscores the critical role of the SC niche in regulating SC functionality and highlights the need to address the bidirectional interplay between SCs and their niche in developing effective interventions for sarcopenia. Notably, emerging technologies, including single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, are revolutionizing our understanding of SC heterogeneity and niche complexity. Although challenges remain, the convergence of molecular biology, bioengineering, and precision medicine heralds a new era in sarcopenia management. Thus, future breakthroughs will likely emerge from interdisciplinary collaborations, translating bench discoveries into clinical solutions for this pervasive age-related condition.

Despite advances, several gaps remain. The molecular mechanisms underlying SCs-niche crosstalk in humans require clarification, particularly regarding species-specific signaling pathways. Additionally, the role of non-coding RNAs and epigenetic modifiers in age-related SCs dysfunction warrants further exploration. Longitudinal studies are needed to validate the efficacy of interventions in diverse populations, accounting for comorbidities and genetic predispositions. Technological innovations, such as single-cell multi-omics and AI-driven modeling, will likely revolutionize sarcopenia research. These tools can map dynamic SCs-niche interactions in real time, identifying novel therapeutic targets. Moreover, CRISPR-based gene editing holds potential for correcting age-associated genetic aberrations in SCs, though off-target effects and ethical considerations must be addressed.

Conclusions and perspectives

SCs as the central cellular orchestrators of muscle development and regeneration, hold pivotal therapeutic potential for combating degenerative muscle disorders. This review delineates the intrinsic regulatory mechanisms of SCs and the impact of niche homeostasis on their functionality, revealing that aging disrupts core signaling pathway equilibrium within the SCs niche—a pathological hallmark driving myogenic potential attenuation and regenerative failure. Niche-targeted interventions may thus offer transformative strategies to reverse muscle degeneration in sarcopenia. Emerging technologies, including single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, have revolutionized our capacity to decode SCs heterogeneity, dynamic behaviors, and multidimensional regulatory networks. While challenges persist, the convergence of molecular biology, bioengineering, and precision medicine heralds a new era in sarcopenia management. Future breakthroughs will likely emerge from interdisciplinary collaborations, translating bench discoveries into clinical solutions for this pervasive age-related condition.
