A Breed‐Specific SNP Panel With Higher Theoretical Exclusion Power and Practical Equivalence to International Standards for Parentage Testing in Pura Raza Española Horses
Authors: Nora Laseca, Antonio Molina, Tristan Marshall, Ismael González, Julia Poyato‐Bonilla, Mercedes Valera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAccurate parentage assignment is essential for managing livestock breeding and genetic improvement. This study evaluated a breed‐specific SNP panel (375 SNPs) for the Pura Raza Española (PRE) horse, comparing its performance against the traditional STR reference panel (18 microsatellite) and the international ISAG SNP core panel (378 SNPs) across population scenarios differing in levels of inbreeding and kinship. The PRE‐specific SNPs were selected from the Axiom EQUIGENE array based on high genotyping reliability (call‐rate > 99%), Mendelian error minimization, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p > 0.05), minor allele frequency (MAF > 0.495) and linkage disequilibrium pruning. Using genomic data from 1000 individuals and random mating simulations via CERVUS software, we assessed non‐exclusion probabilities (NEP) and assignment accuracy under idealized, current, and highly inbred population models using varying proportions of sampled candidate parents. Both SNP panels showed markedly higher theoretical power than the STR panel (3 × 10−8), with the PRE‐specific panel achieving the lowest NEP (1.7 × 10−54). Simulation results confirmed that the PRE‐specific and ISAG SNP core panels showed similar parentage assignment accuracy. In both cases, assignment rates approached the theoretical maxima imposed by candidate parent sampling proportions, regardless of inbreeding level. While the PRE‐specific panel exhibited slightly lower theoretical non‐exclusion probabilities, both panels provide a robust framework for parentage assignment in PRE horses. These results support transitioning to SNP‐based technologies to enhance the integrity of genealogical records.

Introduction

Accurate parentage assignment is fundamental to the mating management and genetic improvement of livestock breeds. Within animal breeding, programs, maintaining accurate pedigree records is essential, as these underpin selection decisions, guide breeding strategies, and ensure the integrity of genetic evaluations. However, pedigree errors can occur due to missing data or human error, among others. These errors in parentage can lead to incorrect estimates of breeding values, misdirected selection, and ultimately, a slower rate of genetic progress within a population (Banos et al. 2001; Zhang et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2021). In any of these cases, DNA‐based parentage testing is an indispensable tool for correcting pedigree errors. The ability to confirm ancestry with a high degree of confidence significantly helps the breeding programe and increases the power and validity of genetic research. It has therefore become an important tool in both breeding practices and research studies (Hu et al. 2021; Strucken et al. 2014).

Traditionally, microsatellite markers (STRs; short tandem repeats) have been successfully used in parentage testing in many species (Jones et al. 2010), including horses (Bowling et al. 1997; Tozaki et al. 2001; van de Goor et al. 2011; Sargious et al. 2021; Park et al. 2022). Their widespread use has led to the establishment of standardized panels, such as those recommended by the International Society for Animal Genetics (ISAG), which facilitate international comparisons and data exchange (ISAG 2025). However, despite their usefulness, STRs have certain limitations. For example, genotyping is not well automated, and it can be difficult to identify alleles due to stutter artifacts, multiple peaks, allelic dropout, null alleles, or other issues. These can sometimes lead to inconsistencies between parents and offspring, increasing the risk of misclassification (Hoffman and Amos 2005).

The advent of next‐generation sequencing technologies and genotyping with SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphism) chips have revolutionized genomic research, making large‐scale genotyping economically viable. SNPs offer high density across the genome and represent a powerful alternative for traceability and parentage analysis (Hirota et al. 2010; Holl et al. 2017; Nolte et al. 2022; Aminou et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024). Furthermore, SNPs offer several advantages over STRs. For example, they have a lower mutation rate, which simplifies allele standardization. Additionally, SNP genotyping is more amenable to automation, leading to reduced costs and higher data throughput in the laboratory (Vignal et al. 2002). Numerous studies indicate significant advances in the usefulness of SNPs for horse pedigree testing in recent years, and most present preliminary panels for their breeds (Hirota et al. 2010; Holl et al. 2017; Aminou et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024; Flynn et al. 2021; Ishige et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2025). However, the informativeness of an SNP set can vary considerably across populations, the development of SNP panels for parentage testing requires balancing statistical optimization with biological representativeness and long‐term robustness across breeding lines or subpopulations.

The Pura Raza Española (PRE) horse, a native Spanish breed, with an active population of 290 407 horses (Ministerio de Agricultura, Pesca y Alimentación (MAPA) 2025) has a global presence in more than 71 countries. The Royal Purebred Spanish Horse Breeders' Association (ANCCE) plays a central role in the management of the breed, in the management of the studbook and in the development of the official breeding programme for the breed. Since its establishment in 1912, the PRE studbook has been entirely closed, exclusively registering foals from already registered parents. Parentage testing in PRE horses began in the early 1980s, employing various biochemical and molecular techniques such as blood typing, serum biochemical polymorphism, and DNA microsatellites. Since 2005, ANCCE has been carrying out paternity controls with a panel of 18 STR markers, recommended for paternity testing and individual identification by the International Society of Animal Genetics. Consequently, the PRE breed now benefits from a comprehensive pedigree record, encompassing over 40 years of established parental information.

Within this framework, the present study evaluates the theoretical performance of a newly defined SNP panel for parentage testing in the PRE horse population. This work aims to (i) assess the potential exclusion power of a PRE‐specific SNP subset, (ii) compare its performance with both the currently applied ISAG STR panel and the internationally recommended ISAG SNP core panel, and (iii) evaluate its behavior under various simulated population scenarios characterized by different levels of kinship and inbreeding.

Materials and Methods

Animals, Genotyping Data and Quality Control

A total of 4000 animals were genotyped using the Axiom EQUIGENE SNP array. Raw genotypes were analyzed with Axiom Analysis Suite software, employing default parameters (Dish quality control > 0.82) and call rate (CR) > 0.95. The 80 932 SNPs that passed quality control were subsequently used for further analysis. All SNP positions and genomic coordinates were defined according to the EquCab3.0 reference genome assembly (GCA_002863925.1). From the initial sample of 4000 animals, 3000 were used for the selection of the SNP panel, while the remaining 1000 were reserved to validate the panel through simulations.

To characterize the genetic structure of the PRE population and evaluate the representativeness of the sampled individuals, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the SNPs that passed quality control and a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01. The PCA was executed on both the full set of genotyped individuals (3000) and a subset of minimally related horses (1000) to visualize population structure and explore potential subpopulation patterns. These analyses were conducted using PLINK software (Purcell et al. 2007).

From this cohort of 3000 genotyped animals, genomic data from 646 PRE trios (sire‐dam‐offspring) were utilized for Mendelian error analysis. Mendelian errors, defined as variant calls inconsistent with Mendelian inheritance rules, were assessed using PLINK software (Purcell et al. 2007) with the ‐‐mendel command. The number of SNPs exhibiting Mendelian inconsistencies was determined to evaluate the quality of the SNP data.

Selection of SNPs Markers for Parentage Panel

After excluding SNPs showing Mendelian inconsistencies, markers were filtered based on high genotyping reliability (CR > 99%), Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (hwe > 0.05) and a MAF > 0.495 to maximize heterozygosity in the target population using PLINK software (Purcell et al. 2007). Finally, linkage disequilibrium pruning was applied using a sliding window of 50 SNPs, advanced by 5 SNPs per step, to reduce marker redundancy and ensure approximate genomic independence among retained loci. In addition to these quality‐control measures, only SNPs exhibiting all three expected genotypes in the population were retained in the final panel, in order to reduce the risk of including loci located in duplicated or paralogous genomic regions. In total, 375 SNPs were retained, which were then designated as the specific SNP markers for the parentage panel (PRE‐specific panel) (Table S1).

For the validation of the selected SNP panel and subsequent parentage potential analyses, genotypic data from 1000 PRE horses selected to minimize close pedigree relationships were used. These animals were independent from the trio dataset and were chosen to represent a broad genetic background of the breed by avoiding direct relatives whenever possible and prioritizing individuals from different studs. From these individuals, genotypes corresponding to the 375 SNPs of the PRE‐specific panel and to the SNP set defined in the international ISAG reference core panel (378 SNPs) were extracted (ISAG 2025).

Additionally, microsatellite genotyping data for these animals were obtained from the ANCCE using the standard 18 STR panel (AHT4, AHT5, ASB17, ASB2, ASB23, CA425, HMS1, HMS2, HMS3, HMS6, HMS7, HTG4, HTG6, HTG7, HTG10, LEX3, LEX33, and VHL20). This panel is routinely used for parentage testing carried out by ANCCE.

Statistical and Simulation Analysis

The theoretical potential of the different marker panels (the PRE‐specific SNP panel, the ISAG SNP core panel, and the STR panel) was evaluated using CERVUS v.3.07 software (Kalinowski et al. 2007). For this analysis, the genotypes of the 1000 minimally related individuals were used to estimate parameters related to their power, reliability and discriminatory capacity. This subset was independent from the trios used for Mendelian error assessment and was employed exclusively for simulation to validate analysis. Expected heterozygosity (HE) and polymorphic information content (PIC) were calculated as descriptive measures of marker informativeness. The combined non‐exclusion probability (NEP) for the first parent (NEP1), the second parent (NEP2), and both parents (NEP3), as well as the probability of identity (PI) and sibling identity (PI2), were estimated using CERVUS 3.0 software (Kalinowski et al. 2007).

To further compare the performance of the PRE‐specific SNP panel and the ISAG SNP core panel under different population structures, simulation analyses were conducted using CERVUS 3.0 software. Simulation‐based comparisons were designed to evaluate relative performance under controlled demographic assumptions rather than to replicate specific real‐world pedigrees. Three distinct population scenarios were modeled using predefined population inbreeding values: (1) an ideal noninbred population, with unrelated breeding individuals and F pop (population inbreeding) = 0.00; (2) a scenario representing the current global PRE population, with average relatedness between breeders = 0.12 and F pop = 0.08; and (3) a highly inbred population scenario, with average relatedness between breeders = 0.25 and F pop = 0.12. Average relatedness between breeders was used to describe the relatedness structure among candidate breeding individuals, whereas population inbreeding (F pop) represented the assumed average inbreeding level of each simulated population scenario. In the CERVUS simulations, F pop was specified independently and was not calculated as one‐half of the average relatedness among breeders.

For each scenario, the ability to correctly assign parentage was assessed without defining predefined candidate trios, representing an extreme and conservative scenario in population‐based parentage testing.

Simulations were performed with different known candidate mothers/fathers ratios in the sample (0.5, 0.9, and 0.95) and two confidence levels (Δ): relaxed (95%) and strict (99%). Allele frequencies from the reference PRE population were used to simulate random matings, generating 1000 offspring from 500 dams and 100 sires across two independent breeding seasons. The selected candidate‐parent sampling proportions (0.5, 0.9, and 0.95) were chosen to represent a range of realistic sampling intensities in studbook‐based breeding systems, from incomplete coverage to high, but not exhaustive, parental sampling. A value of 1.0 was deliberately avoided to prevent forcing parentage assignment when true parents may be absent from the candidate pool, a situation that can realistically occur in studbook management and recording scenarios.

Results and Discussion

The use of SNPs for individual identification and parentage assignment has increased over the past decade, largely due to advancements in high‐throughput genotyping technologies for livestock. Currently, the ISAG promotes the progressive adoption of SNP‐based systems as a robust alternative to STRs for equine parentage verification (ISAG 2025). Previous empirical studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of SNPs in both exclusion and parentage verification (Hirota et al. 2010; Holl et al. 2017). Accordingly, this research aimed to evaluate the theoretical performance of a PRE‐specific SNP subset for equine parentage analysis and to compare it with both the STR panel currently used by ANCCE and the internationally recommended ISAG SNP core panel.

The selection of the sampling population for both SNP panel development and marker validation is critical to avoid ascertainment bias. To ensure the robustness of the panel, the validation was conducted on an independent subset of individuals, following the recommendation to avoid upwardly biased assignment power (Anderson 2010). To this end, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to assess the population structure and possible stratification (Patterson et al. 2006). The eigenvectors corresponding to the two largest eigenvalues (60.65% and 30.08% for PC1 and PC2, respectively) defined the Cartesian reference plane for the analysis. The PCA results revealed a structured yet interconnected population, where the study sample of 3000 individuals captured a broad proportion of the genetic variability of the breed. Furthermore, the validation set of 1000 individuals showed a clear spatial overlap with the reference study group (Figure S1), supporting its representativeness of the simulation dataset. This overlap verifies the robustness of the population inferences and validates the applicability of the evaluated parentage panels designed in this study.

Quality Control for SNP Selection for Parentage Testing

The analysis of all SNPs with 646 trios revealed a low average Mendelian error rate of 0.002 across all SNPs. A large proportion of markers (79.8%) showed no Mendelian errors. In contrast, a small proportion of SNPs (7.58%) exhibited more than five Mendelian errors and 6.15% had more than 10 errors in the trios analysed. This observed Mendelian error rate in the initial set of SNPs suggests a high level of data quality, which is crucial for reliable parentage analysis.

Following the removal of SNPs showing Mendelian inconsistencies (error rate > 0.001), additional quality filters were applied to define a specific SNP panel. This panel, composed of 375 SNPs, was defined by selecting highly informative markers with allele frequencies close to 0.5 in the PRE population. This approach is theoretically justified because markers with high minor allele frequencies maximize expected heterozygosity and, consequently, exclusion power in parentage testing. This ensures that the resulting panels remain highly informative across the entire breed rather than being limited to specific sub‐lines (Strucken et al. 2016). This SNP panel was evaluated for parentage analysis in the PRE population under the specific conditions of this study. The average HE for the SNPs was 0.498 and the average PIC was 0.375. In contrast, the SNP markers selected from the ISAG‐recommended panel exhibited an average HE of 0.442 and an average PIC of 0.342. In contrast, the microsatellite panel showed average values of 0.605 and 0.566 for HE and PIC, respectively. These results are summarized in Table 1.

After removing SNPs markers with Mendelian inconsistencies, the error rate improved significantly. This error rate decreased to 0.0006. This highlights the importance of thorough quality control during development and selection of the marker panel. Judicious selection and the potential exclusion of underperforming SNPs are crucial steps to ensure the robustness and accuracy of genetic analyses (Saunders et al. 2007; Wang 2018). These rigorous quality control measures are critical to establishing reliable marker panels. Moreover, all SNPs retained in the final panel showed the three expected genotypes in the population dataset. This observation supports their behavior as segregation bi‐allelic markers and reduces the likelihood of artifacts associated with duplicated or paralogous genomic regions.

Previous studies in other equine breeds have designed SNP panels for parentage testing encompassing between 53 and 561 SNP markers (Hirota et al. 2010; Holl et al. 2017; Aminou et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024; Flynn et al. 2021; Ishige et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2025). For example, the 144‐SNP panel for Moroccan horses presented an average PIC value of 0.351 (Aminou et al. 2024), which is comparable to the value observed for the PRE‐specific SNP panel (0.375). In addition, its average expected heterozygosity was 0.457, close to PRE‐specific SNP panel 0.498 (Aminou et al. 2024). Similarly, the 71‐SNPs panel designed for the Thoroughbred, Jeju horse, Quarter horse, American Miniature, and Mongolian horse breeds showed PIC values ranging from 0.349 (American Miniature) to 0.364 (Mongolian horse), which were comparable to those obtained with our panel (Kim et al. 2025). Furthermore, mean HE values ranged between 0.468 (American Miniature horse) and 0.491 (Jeju horse) similar to our PRE‐specific SNP panel (Kim et al. 2025). However, the two panels of 147 and 561 SNPs designed for the Percheron and Breton breeds showed slightly lower PIC values (0.296 to 0.308) and HE values (0.381–0.397) (Ishige et al. 2023). Conversely, the panel designed for Korean horse breeds exhibited a higher HE of 0.56 (Lee et al. 2024).

Theoretical Potential of the Panels for Paternity Testing

The estimated theoretical potential parameters for the three panels are shown in Table 1. These parameters showed markedly lower non‐exclusion probabilities for both SNP panels compared to the STR panel currently used by ANCCE, reflecting a higher theoretical exclusion power.

The probability of combined non‐exclusion for the first parent (NEP1) and second (NEP2) ranged from 4.982 × 10−18 to 1.593 × 10−34 for the SNP panels, while the probability of non‐exclusion for both parents (NEP3) ranged from 3.567 × 10−50 to 1.749 × 10−54. In the STR panel, the non‐exclusion probabilities ranged from 0.0031 for NEP1 to 3 × 10−8 for NEP3. These extremely low NEP values indicate a very high level of reliability for paternity testing with either SNP panel (PRE and ISAG core). These findings are consistent with analyses conducted in other breeds that compared proposed SNP panels with STRs, demonstrating the significant potential of SNPs in equine parentage verification, especially in terms of exclusion probabilities (Hirota et al. 2010; Holl et al. 2017; Aminou et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024; Flynn et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2025; Ishige et al. 2023). Our NEP values were lower than those reported in several multi‐breed studies (Flynn et al. 2021; Holl et al. 2017; Hirota et al. 2010; Kim et al. 2025; Ishige et al. 2023) and comparable to values reported for Moroccan horses (1 × 10−7 to 7.099 × 10−20) (Aminou et al. 2024).

When comparing the PRE‐specific SNP panel with the ISAG SNP core panel, the PRE‐focused subset showed slightly lower NEP values, reflecting a marginal increase in theoretical assignment power. However, given that both SNP panels already achieve non‐exclusion probabilities close to zero, the practical impact of this difference is expected to be limited under routine parentage testing conditions. These findings align with those reported by Ishige et al. (2023) who compared their 561‐SNP panel to a 147‐SNP ISAG SNP panel and observed increased robustness.

Regarding the probability of identity (PI) values (Table 1), it ranged from 1.225 × 10−14 in the STR panel to 2.197 × 10−160 in the PRE‐specific SNP panel, and the probability of sibling identity ranged from 2.23 × 10−6 to 1.46 × 10−85 for the STR panel and the SNP‐specific panel, respectively. Our panel exhibited lower PI values compared to other previously studied SNP panels in different breeds (ranging from 3.10 × 10−35 to 5.722 × 10−57) (Holl et al. 2017; Aminou et al. 2024). The only exception was the 561‐SNP panel for the Breton and Percheron breeds (Ishige et al. 2023), which showed even lower PI values (1.01 × 10−178 and 8.53 × 10−183, respectively). These low PI values indicate that the markers used are highly discriminatory, making it extremely unlikely for unrelated individuals to share identical genotypes and thereby minimizing the risk of misidentification or incorrect parentage assignment when using SNP based panels.

Simulation Analysis for Paternity Assignment

The results of paternity assignment simulations, conducted for the ISAG core and PRE‐specific SNP panels within a simulated population under various parametric assumptions, are shown in Table 2. The simulation analyses under different population scenarios (ideal, global PRE, and highly inbred) consistently showed a high rate of correct parentage assignment for both the PRE‐specific and the ISAG core SNP panels. The assignment rates were close to the theoretical maximum given the parameters used, regardless of the stringency of the confidence level (95% or 99%) or the level of inbreeding in the simulated populations. Importantly, these maxima are primarily determined by the assumed sampling proportions of candidate parents. Specifically, assignment rates are constrained by the proportion of candidate sires and dams sampled; for instance, when both proportions are set at 0.95, the theoretical maximum assignment rate is approximately 0.952 ≈90%. This mathematical constraint largely explains the plateau observed across both panels (Table 2). Minor deviations from these theoretical maxima may occur due to sampling variance and occasional incorrect assignments, particularly when utilizing lower confidence thresholds.

These analyses allowed us to model diverse population structures, allele frequencies, and relatedness levels, providing a comprehensive understanding of each marker set's power to correctly exclude nonparents and assign true parents. The comparable performance of both SNP panels across different simulation parameters indicates that each provides a high level of accuracy for paternity assignment under a wide range of demographic scenarios. These results further suggest that, once a sufficiently informative SNP density is reached, gains in assignment success may plateau, particularly under the simulated conditions explored here. Beyond this point, sampling design and population coverage become the dominant factors influencing assignment success rather than further increases in marker number. Notably, assignment success rates remained highly similar across the three simulated population inbreeding levels evaluated in this study (F pop = 0.00, 0.08, and 0.12). This indicates that both SNP panels maintained robust parentage assignment performance within the range of inbreeding conditions modeled here, including the scenario representing the current PRE population and the highly inbred scenario.

Although the PRE‐specific panel showed slightly improved theoretical exclusion power, the practical impact of this difference is likely to be minimal under routine parentage testing conditions, given the similarly high assignment accuracy achieved by both SNP panels. Therefore, the choice between panels may depend more on operational considerations, such as compatibility with international standards, laboratory workflows, and integration with existing genotyping platforms, than on differences in assignment accuracy.

In this context, although this study relies on theoretical parameters and simulation‐based analyses, these approaches are well suited to evaluate parentage testing performance. Importantly, the simulation scenarios applied here reflect key features of real studbook‐based breeding systems, including incomplete sampling of candidate parents, variable levels of relatedness, and differing degrees of inbreeding. The consistently high assignment rates observed across all simulated scenarios therefore support the expectation that both SNP panels would perform reliably under real‐world conditions, provided that candidate‐parent sampling coverage, genotyping quality, and pedigree recording accuracy are comparable to those modeled here. Nevertheless, empirical testing against confirmed pedigree discrepancies would be valuable in future implementation studies.

Beyond confirming the well‐known advantages of SNP‐based parentage testing over STR markers, the main contribution of this study lies in its PRE‐specific evaluation within a closed studbook population. The PRE‐specific panel was developed using population‐specific allele frequency information and was evaluated together with the ISAG SNP core panel under simulation scenarios reflecting key structural characteristics of the PRE breeding system, including relatedness, inbreeding, and incomplete parental sampling. These results highlight the importance of considering population structure when designing or selecting parentage testing panels for closed studbook breeds such as the PRE. From a practical perspective, this work supports the implementation of SNP‐based parentage testing within the PRE breeding program, showing that both a PRE‐specific panel and a standardized international ISAG panel can provide robust and reliable parentage assignment under realistic simulated breeding conditions. This is particularly relevant for routine studbook management, where marker performance, standardization across laboratories, and compatibility with international SNP‐based parentage testing frameworks must be considered jointly.

Finally, SNP‐based parentage testing offers additional practical advantages, including high‐throughput genotyping, reduced allele‐calling ambiguity, and increased standardization across laboratories. The use of SNP panels compatible with internationally recognized standards, such as the ISAG SNP core panel, facilitates data exchange and interoperability, while breed‐specific panels can be incorporated into existing genotyping workflows. When integrated into routine genotyping pipelines, these features may improve operational efficiency and support the practical implementation of SNP‐based parentage testing in registry and breeding program operations.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the high potential of SNP markers for parentage testing in the PRE breed. The theoretical power parameters indicate that SNP panels offer a significantly more reliable tool for parentage verification compared to the currently used microsatellite markers. Furthermore, the PRE‐focused SNP subset showed a slight advantage over the ISAG SNP core panel in terms of theoretical power under the assumptions explored in this study. Simulation analyses confirmed the robustness of both SNP panels for parentage assignment, even under conditions of high inbreeding and varying proportions of candidate parents. Overall, these findings support the suitability of SNP‐based technologies for parentage testing in the PRE breed.
