“Intranasal clozapine sol-gel formulation induces an antipsychotic effect at a fraction of a comparable oral therapeutic dose”
Authors: DW Eyles, HS Parekh, S. Alexander, P. Pandey, D. Siskind, M. Ali, M. Langguth, R. Lohman, X. Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clozapine is arguably the most effective antipsychotic in the treatment of schizophrenia. However, clozapine comes with a substantial and even sometimes lethal side-effect burden. The vast majority of side-effects are peripherally induced, requiring additional pharmacotherapy. An approach to diminishing such debilitating side-effects would be to minimize drug access to the periphery through consideration of other dosage forms and routes of administration. Although a number of intranasal formulations have been developed for clozapine, their antipsychotic potential and distribution across the brain remains unknown. We have developed a poloxamer-based clozapine-infused sol-gel engineered for sustained and controlled nose-to-brain drug delivery. Here in rats, we show that intranasal clozapine delivered via sol-gel achieves high concentrations in brain with minimal distribution to blood. Importantly, we also show that an industry-standard antipsychotic effect is achieved at a fraction (3.5%) of the dose required orally to achieve the same effect. These studies also show a greatly reduced potential of clozapine-infused sol-gel administered intranasally, to elevate blood glucose. The substantive reduction in dose achieved, through olfactory mucosa resident sol-gel restricts drug access to the periphery and therefore associated metabolic side-effects, making intranasal clozapine sol-gel an attractive option for further clinical investigation.

Introduction

Clozapine is arguably the antipsychotic with greatest efficacy [1]. However, it is the only recommended therapy for treatment resistant schizophrenia (TRS) [2]. TRS is common, affecting 25–33% of patients with schizophrenia, and has a poor prognosis. Among people with TRS, clozapine improves positive symptoms, reduces hospitalisations [3], suicides and all-cause mortality [4]. However, many practitioners are hesitant to prescribe clozapine due to a wide range of debilitating adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and extensive therapeutic monitoring requirements [5]. This leads to delays in commencing clozapine, which can contribute to poorer TRS outcomes.

Amongst the most common side-effects is metabolic syndrome reflecting a combination of increased weight, elevated triglyceride levels, low levels of high density lipoproteins, hypertension and elevated blood glucose [6]. Potentially life-threatening neutropenia occurs in up to 1.2% of patients receiving clozapine, overwhelmingly occurring in the first months after initiation [7]. Clozapine-induced neutropenia mandates regular haematological monitoring. Severe neutropenia and other serious ADRs such as myocarditis [8] are major barriers for prescribers [9]. The most common fatal ADR is secondary to constipation [10]. Again, clozapine has the highest rate of severe constipation, with a 3-fold increased risk compared to other antipsychotics [11]. Many of the proposed mechanisms for these ADRs have origins in the periphery. Given the lack of other effective treatments for TRS, reducing the burden of ADRs is crucial to improving the physical health of people taking clozapine. An ideal strategy to address this would be adoption of a route of administration that minimizes drug access to the periphery.

Intranasal (IN) delivery of CNS drugs is one such strategy that maybe particularly appropriate. The advantages of such an approach include mimimizing drug exposure to the periphery, increasing drug availability via reduced 1st pass metabolism and direct access to the brain via the olfactory neurons. All of these advantages can lead to the use of lower doses to achieve antipsychotic effects. Clozapine would appear to be a particular focus for IN delivery with considerable effort being directed towards developing formulations specifically for IN delivery [12–15]. To-date focus has remained on clozapine gaining access to the brain but few have shown convincing evidence of antipsychotic drug action.

In contrast to conventional solution/suspension nasal sprays with their poor retention and reliability in nose-to-brain delivery applications, we propose a poloxamer-based clozapine-infused sol-gel formulation, tailored for IN delivery through optimisation of gelation and rheomechanical properties [16, 17]. Unlike conventional nasal sprays, clozapine is solubilized via a phase-transforming formulation in a stabilized nanoparticulate system, in the absence of alcohols/oils, and employing US-FDA approved generally regarded as safe (GRAS) excipients [18]. The sol-gel delivery system employed has been rheomechanically engineered to rapidly transform from a liquid to a gel. Its mucoadhesive properties impart sustained and controlled drug delivery characteristics, which surpasses the capabilities of conventional nasal formulations. Detailed rheological optimisation with mathematical modelling to predict drivers of drug release have been undertaken [16, 17]. This confirms drug partitioning from the sol-gel platform at the mucosal surface occurs via water diffusion into the sol-gel from the mucosa resulting in swelling of the nanomicellar polymer network, which promotes controlled drug transport into and through mucosal tissue.

Suppression of the conditioned avoidance response (CAR) is a relatively discrete property of antipsychotic drugs (APD) [19–21] amongst all other psychotropic agents. CAR suppression has been established as a property of 32 APD candidates [19]. CAR suppression is the gold standard for acute APD action in wild type animals representing an acute loss of function due to blockade of 5HT2A/C and D2 receptors [22, 23]. Clozapine most likely suppresses CAR by blocking these receptors. Laboratory animals can be conditioned to respond to aversive stimuli (mild foot shock), which is performed in two chamber active avoidance boxes. Animals are conditioned to learn that a light and tone cue will predict forthcoming foot shock. The animal learns to avoid the foot shock by relocating to the non-shocked chamber. This constitutes a conditioned avoidance response (CAR). If the animal fails to move, it receives a mild aversive foot shock prompting an escape response to the non-shocked chamber. If the animal fails to respond after 20 s of foot shock, this is recorded as an escape failure. Animals are trained to reach 80% CAR. All APDs robustly impair this learned response in a dose dependent manner [19]. It is thought that CAR suppression occurs when between 60–80% of D2 receptors are occupied (similar to the occupancy rate suggested for successful APD action). At higher doses when APDs achieve >80% D2 blockade this induces escape failures, which is a read out of catalepsy [24, 25] reflecting an undesirable tranquilizing effect in experimental animals and by analogy extrapyramidal side-effects in patients.

This study shows intranasally delivering clozapine in sol-gel with olfactory targeting allows clozapine to be concentrated in the brain with minimal distribution to the periphery. Furthermore, IN clozapine produces an industry-standard CAR-suppression antipsychotic effect, at a fraction of the comparable oral dose used to achieve the same pharmacological outcome.

Methods

Animals

For all blood/brain distribution and behavioural studies, Male Sprague-Dawley rats were used. For acute effects of clozapine on blood glucose, female animals were used. Animals were ordered anywhere between 9 and 20 weeks of age and were allowed to habituate to the behavioural facility for 1 week. Rats were housed in groups of 4 in wire top cages with standard bedding with a hut and ad lib access to food & water under 12 /12 lighting with lights on/off @ 6am/pm. This study was approved by the University of Queensland Animal Ethics committee, permit no. 2021/AE000549. All behavioural and pharmacokinetic studies were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Clozapine preparation

Although clozapine’s suppression of CAR behaviour has been well characterized in Sprague-Dawley rats, clozapine’s effects on CAR behaviour delivered by oral gavage or IN routes was unknown. Therefore, a range of doses were prepared (10–40 mg/kg) for oral and (0.23–1.15 mg/kg) for IN administration. To prepare for oral administration, clozapine was 1st dissolved in 1N HCl followed by a 1/10 dilution in sterile saline. IN clozapine in sol-gel at various concentrations was freshly formulated as previously described [16, 17].

Clozapine delivery

Oral clozapine (10–40 mg/kg) and vehicle (0.1N HCl in saline) was gavaged at 1 mL/kg under brief anesthesia (1–2 mins 4% isoflurane). For IN delivery, a 1.5% w/w clozapine infused sol-gel and its vehicle (placebo sol-gel) were kept at 2–8 °C, drawn up into a flexible plastic catheter (polyethylene tubing (OD: 0.96 mm, ID: 0.58 mm, PE9658, Microtube Extrusions Pty Ltd, NSW, Australia) and placed on ice. Animals underwent the same brief anesthesia with the variation that the catheter attached to a 50 uL Hamilton syringe was inserted 1.5–2 cm into a single nare approximating the position of the cribiform plate to allow direct access to the olfactory nerves. IN administration took on average 30 s per dose and was administered to the right-sided nare with animal in a supine position, CAR behaviour was assessed 30 min post drug administration. Volumes of between 10–50 uL of 1.5% (w/v) were delivered to achieve various doses. 1–2 min prior-anethesia clearly had no effect on baseline (non-drug) CAR behaviour. CAR training/testing regime

Two-way avoidance chambers (Med Associates Inc,. VT, USA) were used to assess CAR.

Training

Animals are trained for a single session once daily for 7 days. Training consists of habituating animals to the testing arena for 10 mins. Forty trials were then conducted. Each trial started with the presentation of a tone (Conditioned Stimulus, CS) 10 s, followed by mild footshock (Unconditioned Stimulus, US) 10 s, followed by a random inter-trial-interval (20–40 s). The rat learns to associate the CS with the US and moves into the adjacent chamber to avoid the footshock upon presentation of the CS. This is a conditioned avoidance response (CAR). If the animal fails to move during the CS but only during the US this is recorded as an “Escape” response and is a measure of CAR suppression. Failure to move during both CS & US counted as an “Escape failure” indicating an undesirable cataleptic effect of treatment. Only animals which successfully attain a 80% CAR rate during training are used. A separate group of 8 animals /route (IN or oral) were used for each clozapine dose. Separate animals were required as clozapine is well known to induce tolerance to CAR suppression with repeated exposure [26, 27].

Testing

On the day of testing, trained rats 1st undergo a baseline CAR session to ensure baseline CAR was intact (this represents a 0 (time) point prior to drug administration). They then receive either clozapine (IN/oral) or the appropriate vehicle via the same route. CAR is assessed at 30, 100 and 250 min and 24 h post administration.

Clozapine measurements in brain and sera

After establishing the respective IN and oral doses that successfully suppressed CAR, a separate cohort of rats was dosed to measure the kinetics of IN clozapine administration in blood and brain. These animals were chosen from those that did not achieve 80% CAR suppression during training. These animals were dosed with clozapine either intranasally (0.7 mg/kg) or orally (20 mg/kg) and then sacrificed at various time points and blood and brain collected after exactly 30 min. Trunk blood was drawn and sera obtained. Animals were then perfused with cold saline, and brains rapidly dissected to obtain forebrain and striatum and all samples frozen. Clozapine, nor-clozapine and clozapine N-oxide were assessed according to the HPLC method of Fadiran [28]. The only modification was that 100 mg brain samples were 1st sonicated in methanol on ice containing the internal standard (N-methylspiperone) followed by centrifugal evaporation. This extract was then subjected to solid phase extraction as per the published method.

To examine the broader distribution of intranasally administered clozapine across multiple brain sites, a separate cohort of five male animals were administered IN clozapine in sol-gel at 0.7 mg/kg and were then sacrificed exactly 30 min later given this time represents peak brain clozapine levels independent of route of administration [28–31] brains were dissected into 7 rostro-caudal regions. From each section three dorsal/ventral tissue punches (approx. 50ug) were obtained from both hemispheres representing 42 separate samples/brain. Although the HPLC technique produced reliable data it was not sensitive enough for such low tissue amounts. Therefore, a tandem mass spectrometry method was employed for this assay which was optimized for clozapine only not its metabolites. Briefly, brain samples from IN-dosed animals and a non-clozapine exposed control brain were dispersed in acid-washed sand (approx. same weight as tissue sample) followed by 9 volumes deionised water, vortexed then extracted into 1:4 methanol, centrifuged and the supernatant diluted in 50% methanol prior to injection into a Shimadzu 8060 triple-quad LC/MS/MS coupled to a Nexera series high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) unit. A Shim-pack Scepter Phenyl-120 (75 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm particle size) was used. Aqueous phase (solvent A) was hypergrade water with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid, and the organic phase (solvent B) was LCMS hypergrade methanol. A simple linear gradient was employed. Injected sample volume was 1 μL. All multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) LCMS protocols had been optimised prior to the study. A 5-point standard calibration curve for clozapine only (10 nM to 1 pM) was prepared for each sample batch and run consecutively with the samples.

To examine whether the IN and oral doses chosen that produce similar antipsychotic readouts produced comparable levels in brain we dosed 8 male animals with 0.7 mg/kg IN clozapine and another 8 with 20 mg/kg oral clozapine. After exactly 30 mins animals were sacrificed and striatum only obtained, and clozapine and its metabolites examined using the HPLC assay. To examine the differences in blood levels we 1st dosed 8 male animals with 0.7 mg/kg clozapine and obtained 100 uL sera via tail vein. After allowing 2 days for drug washout we dosed these same animals with 20 mg/kg oral clozapine and again obtained sera via tail vein and using the HPLC assay, determined concentrations of clozapine and its metabolites.

Finally, we examined fasting blood glucose concentrations after a single acute dose of either IN (0.7 mg/kg) or oral (20 mg/kg) clozapine or their appropriate vehicles. Ten female rats / each group were used for this experiment as females have been shown to be more liable to the effects of clozapine on blood glucose levels [31, 32]. One drop of whole blood was repeatedly obtained by tail vein over a period of 3 h. Blood glucose was measured using a simple hand-held glucose monitor (Blood Glucose Plus B-Ketone Monitor ;LS-946N, Genesis Biotech Pty Ltd., QLD, Australia).

Data analysis

Clozapine’s effects on CAR suppression and number of crossings was assessed by 2-way ANOVA across the 0, 30, 100 and 250 min periods as this reflects an “on drug” effect. Undesirable escape failures were recorded but not analysed. Most measures have returned to baseline at 24 h reflecting a drug-free timepoint. The profile of clozapine and its metabolites in brains of intranasally dosed rats is reported but not analysed. The distribution of IN clozapine between hemispheres analysed by paired t-test. The rostrocaudal distribution of IN clozapine in brain was assessed by linear regression. Concentrations of clozapine and its metabolites compared between orally and intranasally-dosed striatum were analysed by ANOVA. Concentrations of clozapine and its metabolites compared between orally and intranasally-dosed sera were analysed by paired t-test. Fasting blood glucose measures in orally and intranasally-dosed animals were assessed using 2-way ANOVA assessed for time and treatment. Statistical significance was observed at P < 0.05. In all experiments, animals are coded and researchers blinded to treatment.

Results

IN clozapine produces an industry-standard antipsychotic action at a dose representing 3.5% of that used orally to achieve the same effect

Oral and IN doses of 20 and 0.7 mg/kg respectively were chosen for comparison as they achieved a similar profile of CAR suppression. Here we show that of 30 µL of sol-gel administered clozapine (0.7 mg/kg, n = 7) delivered intranasally produces a similar degree of CAR suppression over 4 h (F (2, 97) = 26.76 P < 0.0001) as 20 mg/kg oral clozapine (n = 8) by oral gavage; (F (2, 96) = 27.13 P < 0.0001) with minimal escape failures indicating an industry standard antipsychotic effect. Post hoc tests reveal CAR suppression was significant at the 100 and 250 min periods (P < 0.0001) for the intranasally administered clozapine and at the 30, 100 and 250 min periods (P < 0.001 for the 30 min and P < 0.05 for the 100 and 250 min periods respectively) for orally administered clozapine. This effect washes out over 24 h consistent with the elimination of drug over that period. Lower doses by either route (10 mg/kg oral, n = 8 and 0.23 mg/kg IN, n = 8) produced minimal escape failures but also did not lead to appreciable CAR suppression (Fig. 1A, B).

In assessing CAR suppression we also obtained the number of chamber crossings during testing. As clozapine can also affect locomotion this measure may be affected. We show oral administration of clozapine at 20 mg/kg also retards locomotion (F (2,20) = 3.30, P < 0.05). This was not significant for the lower dose (10 mg/kg). Locomotion was not affected by any IN clozapine dose (F (2,20) = 0.35 P > 0.05; Fig. 1C, D). Escape failures were observed with all oral doses 10 mg/kg or higher. Higher doses of either formulation (25–40 mg/kg) oral and (1.15 mg/kg) IN were not used further as they produced substantial initial escape failures indicating an undesirable cataleptic or tranquilising effect (Fig. 1E, F).

Clozapine levels in brain and blood

Based on the CAR data for all measures of clozapine in brain and blood, doses of 0.7 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg (n = 4) were used for IN and oral administration, respectively. Clozapine administered intranasally reached peak levels in both brain regions examined after 30 mins. The pharmacologically inert metabolite clozapine-N-oxide was also measurable whilst the active metabolite nor-clozapine was virtually absent (Fig. 2A, B). A small amount of clozapine had also reached the peripheral circulation peaking at 30 mins at 33 ± 15 ng/mL (Fig. 2C). We have also calculated area under curve for the relative distribution of clozapine and its metabolites in these same samples and these are presented in Table 1. As the drug was administered to the right nare we examined distribution in the right and left hemispheres but found no difference (t = 0.73 P > 0.05, n = 5). We also examined the rostro-caudal distribution over the 7 sections taken and found a significantly higher level of drug in rostral compared to caudal areas in both the left (r2 = 0.22, F(1,32) = 9.3 P = 0.0047) and right (r2 = 0.27, F(1,33) = 11.9, P = 0.0015) hemispheres (Fig. 2D). Discussion

Although there have been a number of reports of IN delivery of clozapine in experimental animals, most investigators have simply described access to the brain. Here we have advanced this field in five important ways. First, IN delivery of clozapine via the sol-gel delivery system shows the drug gains rapid access across the brain to produce an industry standard, APD-relevant effect in laboratory rats. Second, we have achieved this APD effect using a dose that represents a 28-fold reduction compared to the comparable CAR-reducing oral dose. Third, even though our IN dose is dramatically lower than the oral dose, we achieve comparable clozapine levels in the brain. Fourth, IN-clozapine delivered via this sol-gel platform to the olfactory cleft minimises access to the peripheral circulation. Fifth, IN-administration greatly reduces clozapine’s well-known effects on blood glucose.

Intranasal Clozapine produces an industry standard measure of antipsychotic action

CAR suppression represents a unique property of all current APDs [20]. Other psychoactive compounds i.e. antidepressants, mood stabilizers have no effect on CAR [19]. Pharmacologically, CAR suppression by APD-like agents reflects antagonism primarily at D2 and 5HT2A and 5HT2C receptors [23]. Clozapine has moderate affinity for these receptors. Whilst there are many new APD agents being developed, D2 antagonism still represents a necessary condition for a drug to have APD potential. In this study, we carefully titrated both oral and IN drug doses to achieve a similar profile of acute CAR suppression over time. It is interesting to note that while significant escape failures indicating unwanted cataleptic effects, could be produced at higher doses for both IN- and orally-administered clozapine, at the respective doses chosen to similarly impair CAR (0.7 and 20 mg/kg), the oral dose also produced some unwanted cataleptic effects. This was also the case even for an oral dose that was inadequate to suppress CAR (10 mg/kg). Catalepsy means an animal is not capable of performing a conditioned avoidance and so erroneously contributes to the overall CAR suppression score. One recent study where authors administered clozapine at 2.5 mg/kg via the oral, intranasal and intravenous routes showed that all routes of administration adversely affected motor function [15]. Importantly escape failures were completely absent in our intranasally treated rats at the dose examined here, 0.7 mg/kg.

Another factor that can possibly confound CAR response is an impairment in locomotion. All drugs that are antagonists at D2 receptors have the capacity to impair locomotion. Even though clozapine has relatively low potency as a D2 antagonist it can still reliably impair locomotion in rodents [33]. We assessed locomotion during CAR testing as the number of crossings between the two chambers. We report an overall effect of the orally-administered dose of 20 mg/kg on reducing the number of chamber crossings reflecting a reduction in locomotion. A recent study also examined locomotion after IN-clozapine. This group used the psychomimetic drug ketamine to elevate locomotion in rats and then examined whether clozapine administered via the intranasal, oral or intravenous routes could suppress this. Indeed, they confirm that at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg IN-clozapine did apparently reduce ketamine mediated locomotion [15]. This finding however is difficult to interpret as no control data showing whether 2.5 mg/kg IN-clozapine itself could impair locomotion was presented. Indeed, we think this is likely given the drug-induced motor impairments these authors observed as noted above. This study also used a dose that was four times greater than used here. It is worth mentioning that we observed significant clozapine induced catalepsy at intranasal doses of only 1.15 mg/kg. No such effect was observed with 0.7 mg/kg IN-clozapine in sol-gel.

Intranasal clozapine gains access to the brain with minimal peripheral distribution

The clearance of IN-clozapine from the brain was similar to the two other studies so far to measure this in IN-dosed rats [14, 34]. We administered clozapine into the right nare, yet the drug clearly distributed evenly between the brain hemispheres. We did note a rostro-caudal distribution of the drug with slightly higher levels found rostrally potentially driven by the high levels close to the site of administration, the olfactory bulbs.

The IN-clozapine dose represented a 28-fold reduction compared to the dose used orally. Therefore, one may expect drug levels in brain to also be far lower. Despite a nominal reduction (618 ± 507 vs 225 ± 159 ng/g mean ± SD P > 0.05 n = 8) this was not statistically significant. Given the low levels of clozapine in sera it is not surprising that there was a marked distribution of clozapine to the brain relative to sera in the IN-dosed group. Brain levels of the inactive clozapine-N-oxide metabolite however, were higher in the orally dosed animals (434 ± 297 vs 145 ± 94 ng/g mean ± SD, P < 0.05, n = 8). Drug metabolism is obviously rapid as the time point measured is only 30 min after administration. Notably a significant proportion of N-oxidation must occur in brain as at this time point the intranasally administered drug is at an order of magnitude lower levels in sera. The pharmacologically active metabolite nor-clozapine was observed at comparable levels to the parent compound in blood from orally-dosed animals, yet this did not appear to lead to partitioning into the brains of these animals. Importantly nor-clozapine has been shown to increase blood glucose itself [35]. Therefore, the relative absence of nor-clozapine from the blood of intranasally dosed animals may in part explain the much lower elevation of blood glucose relative to the orally-dosed animals.

Notably in contrast to our study, all previous studies that have administered clozapine intranasally have shown substantial levels of clozapine in blood [12, 14, 34]. Indeed, one study showed peak blood levels of IN-administered clozapine even exceeded those seen in orally-dosed animals [15]. This implies that other preparations facilitate substantial drug access to the periphery, most likely a result of their delivery throughout the nasal cavity, with limited drug retention in the olfactory cleft - the most direct, rapid and non-invasive route to brain tissue delivery of therapeutics. Earlier studies used solution/suspension-based formulations, which result in distribution preferentially to the highly vascularised respiratory region of the nasal cavity, explaining the high levels of drug observed in sera. Additionally, these formulations are more easily tracked posteriorly by active cilia movement in the nare potentially resulting in swallowing and delivery into the gut. Our goal is to achieve a delivery system for clozapine that largely avoids the periphery given a number of its debilitating side-effects are peripherally-induced. Our canularised delivery method and sol-gel gelation properties enhance drug retention on olfactory mucosa to increase direct-to-brain bioavailability, while avoiding the rich blood supply underlying the respiratory region (c.f. olfactory cleft). In addition, the fact that nor-clozapine is virtually absent from the blood of our intranasally dosed rats confirms this mitigation of clozapine exposure to the periphery. Our IN delivery system via the patented sol-gel platform provides a distinct advantage over conventional IN delivery systems and may prove crucial for future clinical trials in patients.

Clozapine’s ability to increase fasting blood glucose levels in patients is well known and considered a primary contributor to the high incidence of diabetes in patients [36]. This is largely due to clozapine’s actions on pancreatic β-cells to prevent insulin release though other mechanisms have been proposed [37–39]. Indeed, another very recent study confirmed chronic oral clozapine increased blood glucose and reduced insulin [15]. Importantly this same study also confirmed no affect from intranasal administration. Although in our study IN-administered clozapine did appear to elevate blood glucose this was not significantly different to either control exposure. In stark contrast, oral clozapine had a dramatic and prolonged effect elevating blood glucose over the 3 h testing period while the small elevation of glucose in IN-dosed animals had returned to baseline levels. The combination of the greatly reduced IN-dose and minimal presence of clozapine or its glucose inducing metabolite nor-clozapine in the blood of our IN-dosed animals likely explains this difference.

Conclusion

Our key innovation has been to solubilise in an aqueous vehicle and safely deliver, in a targeted manner, a low dose of clozapine via the nasal mucosa to achieve high concentration in the brain, minimise access to the periphery, and to establish that an intranasally-delivered APD produces an internationally-recognized preclinical APD effect. The fact that the IN dose used is dramatically lower than that required by oral delivery to achieve a comparable APD effect and that we minimise one of clozapine’s most well recognised peripherally-induced adverse effects suggests IN delivery of clozapine has enormous potential to reduce the debilitating/life-shortening side effects that are the greatest barriers to this important drug’s use. The sol-gel delivery platform has been developed with human translation in mind. Its composition, using US-FDA approved excipients that are GRAS (generally regarded as safe) classified, renders them safe for human use. Furthermore, unlike conventional sprays that are rapidly cleared from the nasal cavity, sol-gels’ mucoadhesive properties support extended residence and drug delivery after a single dose. Studies to pair the sol-gel platform with a nasal spray device are also well-advanced (commercial in-confidence), which will serve to bridge the preclinical-to-clinical translation gap. Tolerability trials have now begun in human volunteers with no reports yet of any adverse outcome. It must be acknowledged that one potential limitation of translating this work is the greatly reduced ratio of nasal surface area to brain volume in humans compared to rats which could decrease clozapine’s access to patient’s brains. Our unique gelating delivery system allowing prolonged access of drug to olfactory epithelium may overcome such a translational barrier. Should future pre-clinical studies show that chronic IN-clozapine sol-gel retains APD actions whilst dramatically reducing most side-effects it is not hyperbole to suggest that success in this endeavor combined with good tolerability outcomes in patients could revolutionize APD therapy.
