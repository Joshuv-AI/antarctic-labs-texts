Long-term exposure to wildfire smoke particulate matter and incident stroke: a US nationwide study
Authors: Hua Hao, Ke Xu, Danlu Zhang, Yanling Deng, Tarik Benmarhnia, Qiang Pu, Varun K Pattisapu, Kyle Steenland, Howard H Chang, Alvaro Alonso, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsWildfire smoke is a growing public health concern, particularly due to its high fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations. While short-term exposure has been linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, long-term effects remain understudied. This study examined the association between long-term wildfire smoke PM2.5 exposure and incident stroke among older US adults.MethodsA national open cohort study was conducted among Medicare Fee-for-Service beneficiaries aged ≥65 years from 2007 to 2018. Stroke cases were identified using validated Medicare claims. Wildfire smoke PM2.5 exposure was estimated at a 1 km2 resolution using a machine learning model distinguishing smoke-specific PM2.5 from other ambient sources. Stratified Cox proportional hazards models with generalized estimating equations were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) per 1 µg/m3 increase in wildfire smoke PM2.5, adjusting for non-smoke PM2.5, meteorological variables, socio-economic status, and healthcare capacity.ResultsAmong ∼25 million beneficiaries, ∼2.9 million incident stroke cases occurred. Long-term wildfire smoke PM2.5 exposure was significantly associated with increased stroke risk. Stronger associations were observed for longer exposure windows. For instance, per 1 µg/m3 increase in 3-year average wildfire smoke PM2.5 was associated with a 1.3% increase in stroke risk (HR: 1.013; 95% CI: 1.006, 1.020). Associations for wildfire smoke PM2.5 were generally stronger than for non-smoke PM2.5 per unit exposure.ConclusionsThis study provides national-scale evidence that chronic exposure to wildfire smoke PM2.5 increases stroke risk in older adults. These findings underscore the long-term health risks of wildfire smoke and support policies aimed at reducing exposure, especially among vulnerable populations.Keywords: Wildfire, Stroke, Fine particulate matter, Long-term exposure, Elderly

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) stands as one of the primary causes of death in the US and worldwide,1 and approximately every 1 in 3 American adults have some form of prevalent CVD.2 Stroke, a major type of CVD, is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the USA, with 795 000 people experiencing a new or recurrent stroke each year, resulting in ∼140 000 annual deaths.3,4 The economic burden of stroke in the USA is estimated to be around $40 billion annually.5,6 While stroke incidence among elderly has decreased over recent decades, this decline has begun to plateau compared with the steep decreases seen in the 1990s suggesting a change in the potential combination of risk factors.7 Conventional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking do not fully explain the variations in stroke risk over time.8 Emerging evidence suggests that environmental factors, particularly air pollution, may play a critical role in CVD risk.9 The American Heart Association has identified a causal relationship between long-term exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5; particle diameter < 2.5 µm) and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality including stroke.10 Furthermore, several reviews have documented the association between chronic PM2.5 exposure and increased stroke incidence, likely mediated by oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombotic activation, with animal studies also suggesting its role in blood–brain barrier disruption and neuroinflammation.11,12

While emissions control policies have successfully reduced ambient PM2.5 levels in the USA over the past few decades,13 this trend is being reversed in some parts of the USA14 given the growing frequency, intensity, and duration of wildfires—exacerbated by climate change.15 Wildfires emit vast quantities of smoke that can travel over long distances, resulting in sharp increases in PM2.5 concentrations in populated areas that can persist over extended periods, contributing to long-term air quality deterioration.16 These recurrent pollution events raise concerns about the long-term health impacts of wildfire smoke exposure,17 as episodic spikes in pollution can accumulate over time, causing chronic health conditions.

Recent studies suggest that short-term exposure to wildfire smoke PM2.5 has stronger health effects than PM2.5 from other sources at the same concentration, likely due to its higher oxidative potential and unique composition.18,19 Similar patterns have been observed for long-term exposure, including increased dementia risk,20 raising concerns about potential cerebrovascular effects such as stroke. While short-term wildfire smoke exposure is well-documented, growing evidence suggests long-term exposure may have distinct and underestimated health effects.21,22 A recent model estimates that nearly 40% of air quality monitoring sites experience over 30 days of smoke-related pollution annually,23 underscoring the urgency of studying its long-term health implications as wildfire seasons lengthen and intensify. However, many previous studies have relied on proxy measures such as total PM2.5 concentrations or classified exposure based on smoky days vs non-smoky days, introducing large exposure errors.21,24,25 Addressing these limitations requires high-resolution, wildfire-specific PM2.5 exposure data to accurately quantify long-term health risks, particularly for stroke.

To bridge the above knowledge gaps, we conducted the first national, population-based open cohort study to quantify the associations between long-term wildfire smoke PM2.5 exposure and stroke risk among US older adults, from 2007 to 2018. Leveraging nationwide Medicare claims data, we utilized high-resolution, smoke-specific PM2.5 estimates generated by a two-stage machine learning model to ensure precise exposure assessment.

Methods

Study population

We constructed our study cohort using the national Medicare denominator file and the Medicare Chronic Conditions Warehouse (CCW) dataset from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). The CCW claims data contains predefined indicators for chronic conditions among Medicare Fee-for-Service (FFS) patients, and it provides the year of the initial event and the corresponding diagnosis code for a specific disease. The denominator file contains each Medicare beneficiary’s enrolment information, which was updated annually. This information includes demographics, the death date (if any), the beneficiary’s ZIP code, and Medicaid insurance enrolment, serving as a proxy indicator for socio-economic status (SES).

The study included all Medicare beneficiaries continuously enrolled in both the Medicare FFS programme and Parts A (hospital insurance) and B (medical insurance) in the contiguous USA from 2007 to 2018. We tracked each enrollee’s annual residential zip code changes over time. The year of initial stroke diagnosis was identified per individual and linked to the denominator. A 3-year washout period was applied after the first enrolment, during which no stroke diagnoses occurred. This approach helped ensure that only new stroke cases were captured. By removing prevalent cases of stroke during the initial 3 years of follow-up, we improved our ability to approximate stroke incidence in later life. We determined 3 years to be a reasonable timeframe to reduce the likelihood of including pre-existing stroke cases. This decision is consistent with previous Medicare-based studies on chronic disease outcomes,26–29 which used clean periods ranging from 1 to 5 years. While longer clean periods may improve confidence in capturing incident cases, they also substantially reduce sample size and statistical power, especially in older adult populations. Since we limited enrolment to beneficiaries aged 65 and older at baseline, applying a 3-year washout period resulted in a study population that was at least 68 years old at the beginning of follow-up. In the final cohort, participants entered the study the year following the washout period and were followed until stroke diagnosis, death, or end of the study period. To avoid immortal time bias,30 the washout period was excluded from follow-up time, meaning follow-up started as early as 2010. The Medicare dataset has been preserved and processed in a secure computing infrastructure compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) at the Rollins School of Public Health of Emory University.

Outcome classification

Stroke cases were identified through an algorithm within the CCW that utilizes data from various Medicare claims, including hospital inpatient, outpatient, and carrier claims (such as physician visits), all coded with valid International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) or Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes.26,27 The year of the first stroke diagnosis was obtained from the CCW following the beneficiary’s enrolment in the Medicare FFS programme and both Part A and Part B. Detailed ICD codes and the algorithm used for identifying stroke conditions in Medicare CCW can be found in Supplementary data online, Table S1.

Exposure assessment

Daily wildfire smoke PM2.5 and non-smoke PM2.5 concentrations for the contiguous United States (CONUS) from 2007 to 2018 were estimated at 1 km2 spatial resolution using a two-stage, twin-model machine learning modelling framework. A comprehensive description of this framework is published elsewhere and summarized here.23 On a daily basis, 1 km2 grid cells in the CONUS domain were classified as smoke-impacted or smoke-free based on fire smoke contours from the NOAA Hazard Mapping System31,32 and wildfire-attributable contributions to total PM₂.₅ simulated by the Community Multiscale Air Quality Modeling System (CMAQ). The CMAQ provided a mechanistic estimate of how much PM2.5 in a given grid cell and day could be attributed to biomass burning emissions, enabling classification of smoke-impacted areas.33 Two separate random forest models were trained: one using data from smoke-impacted grid cells to predict total PM2.5 and another using data from smoke-free grid cells to predict background non-smoke PM2.5. The smoke-specific PM2.5 concentration was then derived by subtracting the predicted non-smoke PM2.5 from the predicted total PM2.5 within smoke-impacted cells. The modelling framework integrated multiple data sources including satellite Aerosol Optical Depth, meteorological variables, ground-based regulatory monitors, and low-cost sensors (e.g. PurpleAir). The total PM2.5 model demonstrated strong predictive performance, with a cross-validated R2 of .9 at monthly and annual scales. The smoke-specific PM2.5 model also performed well, with a daily cross-validated R2 of .75 in smoke-impacted areas, as reported in Zhang et al.23

To link the exposure with the Medicare data, we calculated annual wildfire smoke and non-smoke PM2.5 concentrations at the ZIP code level by averaging the daily 1 km2 PM2.5 concentrations whose centroids fall within the ZIP code boundary and for each year of the study period. Exposure levels were then estimated for each participant based on their residential ZIP code. Moving averages for wildfire smoke and non-smoke PM2.5 were calculated for past 1-, 2-, and 3-year windows for each follow-up year, representing long-term exposure. Building on previous research17 to assess long-term wildfire PM2.5 exposure, we calculated three additional metrics to capture key wildfire characteristics: frequency, duration, and intensity. Specifically, we calculated the number of days with wildfire smoke PM2.5 > 0 µg/m3, >1 µg/m3, and >2.5 µg/m3 using 1-, 2-, and 3-year moving averages to characterize long-term exposure to varying smoke intensities. The >0 µg/m3 threshold was used to capture any detectable smoke exposure, while >1 µg/m3 and >2.5 µg/m3 correspond approximately to the 75th and 95th percentiles, respectively, of the daily wildfire smoke PM2.5 distribution in our dataset. These empirically derived thresholds allowed us to characterize a range of smoke exposure conditions, from moderate, frequent events to rarer, higher-intensity episodes.

Covariates

Individual-level data on age, sex, race, Medicaid status, and date of death were obtained from the Medicare denominator file. For each beneficiary, based on their ZIP code of residence and follow-up year, we incorporated information on US geographic regions (i.e. Northeast, Southeast, Midwest, Southwest, and West); ZIP code-level meteorological variables (relative humidity and temperature) from the North American Land Data Assimilation System (NLDAS); ZIP code-level healthcare capacity-related information (total number of beds regularly maintained, total number of active medical doctors per 1000 people, and the number of hospitals) from the American Hospital Association Annual Survey Database; ZIP code-level behavioural risk factors (smoking rate) from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS); and ZIP code-level SES factors, including population, poverty, education, median household income, per cent Black, per cent White, per cent using auto transportation, and per cent renting obtained from the US Census and the American Community Survey. Alcohol use disorder, drug use disorder, and tobacco use disorder at the individual level were derived from the ‘Other Chronic or Potentially Disabling Conditions’ segment of the CCW.

Statistical analysis

We applied stratified Cox proportional hazards models with generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for clustering of individuals within ZIP codes, recognizing that residents in the same area may share similar environmental exposures, socio-economic characteristics, healthcare access, and other contextual factors that could influence stroke risk. By using GEE, we adjusted for within-ZIP code correlation, ensuring more robust standard errors and reliable inference when estimating hazard ratios (HRs) of incident stroke risk among Medicare enrollees exposed to long-term wildfire smoke PM2.5. Long-term exposure was measured using nine metrics, which included the moving averages of wildfire smoke PM2.5 concentrations over the past 1, 2, and 3 years, as well as the number of days with wildfire smoke PM2.5 exceeding 0 µg/m3, 1 µg/m3, and 2.5 µg/m3 over the same time periods. All models were adjusted for non-smoke PM2.5, meteorological variables (including annual mean of relative humidity and temperature), SES variables, behavioural risk factors, and healthcare capacity-related covariates. Non-smoke PM2.5, temperature, and relative humidity were assigned using the same 1-, 2-, and 3-year moving average windows as wildfire smoke PM2.5 to ensure temporal consistency. All exposure and covariate variables were linked annually by ZIP code and follow-up year and modelled as time-varying covariates. To account for potential residual temporal and spatial patterns, categorical indicators of the calendar year and geographic regions were also added to the model. To avoid collinearity, only covariates with correlations below .7 were included (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1). The final model adjusted variables of non-smoke PM2.5, relative humidity, temperature, calendar year, geographic region, total number of beds, smoking rate, population, per cent Black, per cent poverty, per cent using auto transportation, and per cent renting. All covariates were included as linear terms unless noted otherwise. Person-year observations with missing values in any covariates were excluded. The overall proportion of missingness was low (4.14%) and therefore unlikely to introduce substantial bias. To allow for flexible baseline hazard functions, models were stratified by sex, race (White, Black, other), Medicaid eligibility, and age at study entry in 1-year increments. Hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) per 1 µg/m3 increase in smoke or non-smoke PM2.5 concentrations were reported. We further quantified the stroke burden attributable to smoke PM2.5 exposure using two indicators: the number of attributable cases and the population attributable fraction (PAF), both of which reflect the population-level impact of exposure.34 Additionally, penalized spline models were used to test for potential non-linear concentration–response (C-R) relationships between smoke PM2.5 and stroke, controlling for the same covariates across models. We further evaluated effect modification by entry age, sex, and socio-economic indicators, including Medicaid dual enrolment and ZIP code-level median household income, by fitting separate models within each subgroup. These modifiers were chosen based on prior research indicating their relevance in influencing vulnerability to PM2.5-related cardiovascular outcomes.35

We conducted a number of sensitivity studies to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we repeated the analyses using a washout period of 5 years. Compared with the primary analysis, which used a 3-year washout period, this would enhance the likelihood of capturing incidents rather than prevalent diagnosis. Second, we conducted a non-mover cohort analysis by only including enrollees who did not relocate during the follow-up period to reduce potential measurement errors related to address change. Third, although we used ambient air pollution as the exposure metric in this analysis, which is more closely associated with area-level factors and less influenced by individual-level confounders, we still accounted for potential confounding by including personal-level variables such as alcohol use disorders, drug use disorders, and tobacco use disorders in the model. These variables were excluded from the primary models because they were sourced from the ‘Other Chronic or Potentially Disabling Conditions’ segment of the CCW, which employs a different diagnostic identification approach than the standard annual algorithm used for other covariates. Additionally, these variables are more likely to represent severe clinical diagnoses rather than general behavioural tendencies, which could introduce misclassification. As a result, we used them only in sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our results. Fourth, we implemented a censoring-by-death weighting (CDW) approach to account for death as a competing risk, using stabilized inverse probability weights derived from logistic regression models of mortality.36,37 Last, to account for potential state-level heterogeneity in baseline stroke risk and unmeasured contextual factors, we further stratified the models by US state.

The Rollins High Performance Computing Cluster at Emory University was used for all computations, and all statistical analyses were implemented in R (version 4.0.2).

The mean concentrations for wildfire smoke PM2.5 for the past 1-, 2-, and 3-year exposure metrics were .53 μg/m3, .50 μg/m3, and .51 μg/m3, respectively. The corresponding mean non-smoke PM2.5 concentrations were ∼8.55 μg/m3, 8.56 μg/m3, and 8.61 μg/m3. The average number of days with wildfire smoke PM2.5 > 1 μg/m3 over the past 1-, 2-, and 3-year were 83, 82, and 83 days (Table 1). Health effects estimates

Figure 2 and Supplementary data online, Table S5 present the associations between long-term exposure to wildfire smoke and non-smoke PM2.5 and stroke incidence among older adults in the USA. Both wildfire smoke and non-smoke PM2.5 were associated with elevated stroke risk. For the past 1-year exposure window, the HR per 1 µg/m3 increase in wildfire smoke PM2.5 was lower than for non-smoke PM2.5. However, at longer exposure windows, HRs for wildfire smoke PM2.5 increased, while HRs for non-smoke PM2.5 remained relatively stable. Specifically, each 1 µg/m3 increase in 3-year average wildfire smoke PM2.5 was associated with a 1.3% increase in stroke risk (HR: 1.013; 95% CI: 1.006–1.020), compared with a .7% increase for non-smoke PM2.5 (HR: 1.007; 95% CI: 1.006–1.008), suggesting potentially greater toxicity of smoke-derived particles. Additionally, long-term exposure—measured as 1-, 2-, and 3-year moving averages—of days with wildfire smoke PM2.5 > 1 µg/m3 and > 2.5 µg/m3 was associated with a higher risk of stroke (see Supplementary data online, Figure S4). Assuming a baseline annual stroke incidence rate of 2211 per 100 000 Medicare beneficiaries,26 this corresponds to an estimated 17 226 (95% CI: 7,830, 26,100) additional stroke cases per year and a PAF of 1.30% (95% CI: .60%, 1.96%) among US older adults.

Stratification analysis

We examined effect modification by individual and community-level characteristics and observed variation in the associations between wildfire smoke PM2.5 and stroke incidence risk. Among all subgroups, Medicaid-eligible individuals—a proxy for lower personal SES—consistently showed stronger associations across all three exposure windows (moving average of past 1, 2, and 3 years). In contrast, other modifiers showed less consistent patterns across time windows (see Supplementary data online, Table S6).

Sensitivity analysis

Relationships between long-term exposure to wildfire smoke PM2.5 and CVD outcomes were consistent in multiple sets of sensitivity assessments. First, a more rigorous ‘cleaner period’ that excluded anyone diagnosed with stroke in their first 5 years of follow-up showed similar results to the primary analysis (see Supplementary data online, Table S7). Second, after we restricted the analysis to participants who did not move throughout the follow-up period to adjust for address change bias, the results agreed with our main findings, suggesting statistically precise and positive associations between exposure to wildfire smoke PM2.5 and stroke incidence (see Supplementary data online, Table S8). Third, our model yielded similar results even after incorporating personal-level covariates (see Supplementary data online, Table S9). Fourth, when accounting for death as a competing risk using CDWs, the results remained consistent with those of the main analysis (see Supplementary data online, Table S10). Last, stratifying the model by US state yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (see Supplementary data online, Table S11).

Discussion

Our study revealed significant associations between long-term exposure to wildfire smoke PM2.5 and an increased risk of stroke incidence (Structured Graphical Abstract). This is the first study focusing on long-term effects of wildfire smoke PM2.5 on stroke incidence. Our findings aligned with recently published systematic reviews by Reid et al.38 and Chen et al.39 that exposure to wildfire smoke is associated with acute cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes. However, most prior research on stroke morbidity linked to wildfire smoke has focused on transient effects, such as emergency department visits and hospitalizations under acute exposure. Our study, in contrast, extends this knowledge by illuminating the potent impact of long-term wildfire smoke PM2.5 on stroke. While the increase in stroke risk per unit of wildfire smoke PM2.5 was relatively small at the individual level, the potential population-level burden is substantial given the widespread exposure among older adults. We estimate that a long-term increase of just 1 µg/m3 in wildfire smoke PM2.5 could result in approximately 17 226 additional stroke cases annually in the US elderly population. These findings underscore the public health significance of chronic wildfire smoke exposure and the urgency of effective mitigation strategies.

Interestingly, we observed stronger wildfire smoke PM2.5 effects on stroke than non-smoke PM2.5 per 1 μg/m3 change in exposure, suggesting that wildfire smoke PM2.5 impacts cerebrovascular health more than PM2.5 from other sources, with the most marked difference seen in the moving average of past 3-year results. This aligns with previous evidence showing similar patterns for other outcomes.20 While our study contributes to the limited research comparing wildfire smoke and ambient PM2.5, direct comparisons of toxicity remain challenging due to differences in exposure distributions and chemical composition. Recent in vivo animal studies indicated that particles in wildfire smoke are more harmful to the lungs than equal dosages from ambient air pollution sources.40 This observed toxicity has manifested in increased neutrophil count and protein concentration within the pulmonary lavage fluid.38 Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that neutrophils can exacerbate cardiac damage through the release of granular components, reactive oxygen species, and pro-inflammatory mediators.41 Wildfire smoke PM2.5 possesses a different chemical composition, characterized by a higher proportion of polar organic compounds,42 containing a substantial organic carbon component (at least 50%) and a notable element carbon content (5%–20%).39 This composition renders it more susceptible to oxidation when compared with ambient PM2.5. Moreover, the polar organic compounds in wildfire smoke have the propensity to produce additional free radicals, which raises the risk of increased inflammation and oxidative stress and further triggers CVD onsets.43 Additionally, variations in the duration and intensity of particulate matter exposure may underlie disparities in the magnitude of the inflammatory response.10 Unlike PM2.5 generated from urban resources, wildfire smoke PM2.5 can exhibit dramatic fluctuations in concentration levels, contingent on the durations and intensities of wildfire events.44 Finally, the physical and mental stress produced by the fear of a wildfire, evacuation, possible unemployment, and lack of healthcare access after the disaster might increase CVD incidence. Several prior studies have established links between CVD and natural disasters, such as hurricanes, typhoons, and earthquakes,45 and those effects can sometimes last for up to 6 years after the disaster.46

Our research also found that long-term exposure to non-smoke PM2.5 is associated with an increased risk of stroke incidence. Although previous meta-analyses11,12 concluded that long-term ambient PM2.5 exposure is linked to incident stroke, findings from US studies have been inconsistent. Alexeeff et al.’s meta-analysis,12 which pooled 14 studies, included five from the USA. Among these, Lipsett et al.47 conducted a prospective cohort study of over 100 000 California teachers, with a median follow-up of 8 years, reporting a marginally significant association between long-term PM2.5 exposure and stroke [HR: 1.14 (95% CI: .99, 1.32)]. Similarly, Hart et al.48 followed 114 537 women in the Nurses’ Health Study and also found a marginal association [HR: 1.18 (95% CI: .96, 1.45)]. Conversely, Puett et al.’s study49 of 17 545 men with higher SES living in the midwestern and northeastern USA reported no association [HR: .69 (95% CI: .39, 1.21)]. Another prospective cohort study of 6575 Ohio residents who underwent coronary angiography also found no association [HR: 1.34 (95% CI: .23, 7.93)].50 However, Miller et al.’s study,51 which followed 65 893 postmenopausal women without previous CVD for a median of 6 years, reported a significant association between long-term PM2.5 exposure and stroke risk [HR: 1.28 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.61)].

In our study, we observed that the adverse effects of wildfire smoke PM2.5 on stroke risk became stronger with longer exposure windows and higher smoke-day thresholds—reflecting both increasing cumulative concentrations and more frequent or intense smoke days over time. This pattern was not observed with non-smoke PM2.5, which showed relatively stable associations across different exposure durations. The trend for wildfire smoke PM2.5 may be explained by a combination of cumulative exposure burden and intensity-driven toxicity.52 Longer durations of wildfire smoke exposure may promote systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and neurovascular stress—key mechanisms in stroke development.38,53 In addition, repeated exposure to days with higher smoke concentrations may lead to disproportionate biological effects, as more intense wildfire PM2.5 has been shown to contain elevated levels of reactive and toxic constituents.39,42 Epidemiological evidence on the chronic effects of smoke PM2.5 on cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes has been limited. One recent study showed that long-term smoke PM2.5 exposure is linked to cardiovascular mortality,54 but no research has examined the effects of long-term cumulative exposure to wildfire smoke. For short-term effects, Requia et al.55 conducted a national study in Brazil with more than 2 million cardiovascular hospital admissions but found no specific patterns with 1–5 day moving averages. Future research is warranted to reproduce our findings on cumulative effects in different populations and settings. While several studies have explored the cumulative effects of long-term exposure to ambient PM2.5 on cardiovascular outcomes, their conclusions remain inconclusive. For example, a national study in the USA suggested that cardiovascular mortality risk increases with longer exposure windows.56 However, another national study from South Korea reported no cumulative effect of ambient PM2.5 exposure on cardiovascular cause-specific deaths.57 Additionally, a Danish cohort study with more than 15 years of follow-up reported that shorter ambient PM2.5 exposure windows (5 years and 10 years) showed similar results for CVD-related deaths compared with a 15-year exposure window.58 Our findings on non-smoke PM2.5 align with the South Korean study and the Danish study.

The assessment of the dose–response relationship is crucial for informing public health policies. We observed linear relationships with no threshold of smoke PM2.5 exposure windows for stroke, suggesting that there is no safe dose of wildfire smoke. A previous multi-country study reported approximately threshold type C-R curves for stroke incidence and long-term ambient PM2.5,59 but an Indian national study reported a linear relationship.60 These disparities underscore the need for future studies to validate our findings.

Our study has several strengths. First, this is the first large-scale national cohort study to explore the associations between long-term wildfire smoke PM2.5 exposure and stroke incidence in the contiguous USA. About 25 million enrollees with millions of incidence cases were included in our analysis and provided ample statistical power. Second, the high-resolution smoke PM2.5 exposure data enabled us to evaluate the effect of wildfire smoke PM2.5 on stroke more precisely compared with most previous research that rely on highly uncertain proxies, such as ambient total PM2.5 concentrations during fire seasons. Third, the CCW database collected data from various healthcare settings like inpatient and outpatient settings. Unlike previous studies that used hospital data, our study cohorts, coupled with a 3-year clean period in data collection, more accurately represent the incidence (rather than prevalence) occurrence of stroke.

Our study also has a few limitations. First, despite excellent model performance, our predicted smoke PM2.5 concentrations may still include measurement errors. Since the estimated smoke PM2.5 concentration error is presumably independent of the disease outcome status, any biases are expected to be towards the null. Second, there may be unmeasured confounding due to the lack of control for individual-level stroke risk factors. However, since we used ambient air pollution as the exposure metric rather than personal exposure, it is more closely associated with area-level factors, which we have already controlled for, reducing the potential for confounding.61 Furthermore, sensitivity analyses showed that the model results remained consistent even after incorporating personal-level covariates. Third, while our findings suggest a stronger association per unit increase in wildfire smoke PM2.5 compared with non-smoke PM2.5, we acknowledge that these two pollutant types differ in their distribution patterns and chemical composition. Therefore, direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution, and additional toxicological and epidemiological research is warranted to better characterize the relative toxicity of different PM2.5 sources. Finally, our results only account for the Medicare FFS enrollees. We potentially missed outcome cases in our data because we do not include the Medicare-HMO (Medicare managed care plan). Therefore, our results cannot be generalized to the entire Medicare population.

In conclusion, our study provides strong evidence that chronic exposure to PM2.5 from wildfire smoke increases the risk of developing stroke. Our findings indicate that regulations leading to effective wildfire management and prevention can have important health benefits for the elderly American population.
