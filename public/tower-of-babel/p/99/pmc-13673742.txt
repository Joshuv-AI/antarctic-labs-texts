Risk factors for delayed gait recovery after primary total hip arthroplasty in patients with crowe type I/II developmental dysplasia of the hip
Authors: Wei Xin, Cong Wang, Zhipeng Yue, Shu Chen, Yunli Zhu, Peiliang Fu, Qirong Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gait recovery process after total hip arthroplasty (THA) in patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is complex. However, systematic evidence regarding risk factors for delayed early postoperative gait recovery in patients with Crowe type I/II DDH remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective study included 283 patients with Crowe type I/II DDH who underwent primary THA at our institution between January 2020 and December 2024. The primary outcome was delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months after surgery, defined as persistent limping or Trendelenburg gait after full weight-bearing was permitted and/or a Harris Hip Score (HHS) limp subscore < 11; this threshold indicates failure to reach the “no limp” score of 11 in the HHS limp subscore. Outcomes were independently adjudicated by two uniformly trained investigators, and inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ). Model 1 included age, bilateral DDH status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status class II/III, symptom duration, preoperative HHS, preoperative need for a walking aid, preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe classification, preoperative leg length discrepancy (LLD), operative time, and intraoperative blood loss. Model 2 further included postoperative residual LLD, height of the acetabular center of rotation, cup abduction angle, and femoral offset change. The primary analysis was based on multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE; 20 imputations), with sensitivity analyses using the unilateral DDH subgroup, complete cases, alternative outcome definitions, a parsimonious model, an all-prespecified-covariate model, collinearity testing between preoperative and postoperative LLD, and LLD threshold analyses.ResultsDelayed gait recovery at 3 months occurred in 92 patients (32.5%). Multivariable analysis (Model 1) showed that a positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign (odds ratio [OR] = 2.87, 95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 1.55–5.31), Crowe type II (OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.18–3.91), preoperative LLD (per 1-mm increase: OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03–1.26), age (per 1-year increase: OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.05), and symptom duration (per 1-year increase: OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06) were independent risk factors for delayed gait recovery. The extended model (Model 2) showed that postoperative residual LLD was also independently associated with delayed gait recovery (per 1-mm increase: OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.02–1.41), whereas height of the acetabular center of rotation, cup abduction angle, and femoral offset change showed no independent associations. Sensitivity analyses restricted to unilateral DDH and complete-case analyses were consistent with the primary analysis.ConclusionsIn this predominantly female Chinese single-center cohort of patients with Crowe type I/II DDH undergoing primary THA, delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months was not uncommon. Positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe type II, greater preoperative LLD, older age, and longer symptom duration were independently associated with delayed early clinical gait recovery; postoperative residual LLD provided additional explanatory value in the extended model. These factors may help identify patients at higher risk of delayed early clinical gait recovery before surgery, but instrumented gait analysis was not performed in this study, and long-term three-dimensional gait-kinematic prognosis requires further validation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03970-5.

Introduction

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is an important cause of secondary hip osteoarthritis and total hip arthroplasty (THA) in adults. Adult DDH is often characterized by insufficient acetabular coverage, superolateral displacement of the femoral head, abnormal proximal femoral morphology, and adaptive changes in periarticular soft tissues. These structural abnormalities may lead to abnormal distribution of joint stress, pain, gait compensation, and end-stage degeneration. For adult patients with DDH who have persistent pain or functional impairment after failed conservative treatment, THA remains the principal treatment option for restoring joint stability, relieving pain, and reconstructing lower-limb alignment [1, 2].

Radiographic diagnosis and deformity stratification form the basis of preoperative assessment in DDH. The lateral center-edge angle (LCEA), Sharp angle, continuity of Shenton’s line, and acetabular coverage can reflect the degree of acetabular underdevelopment; the Crowe classification stratifies deformity severity according to the extent of proximal femoral-head displacement. Crowe type I/II disease usually represents relatively mild or moderate displacement, but this population may still have long-standing abductor insufficiency, pelvic compensation, leg length discrepancy (LLD), and soft-tissue contracture [3].

Previous studies have mainly focused on reconstruction techniques, osteotomy strategies, implant selection, and mid- to long-term implant survival in Crowe type III/IV high dislocation, whereas evidence regarding early functional recovery after THA in Crowe type I/II patients remains relatively limited [4, 5]. Although bony deformity is milder in this subtype, long-term abnormal loading and impaired hip abductor function may continue to affect early postoperative gait recovery. Prior literature has more frequently reported Harris Hip Score (HHS), revision rate, or radiographic reconstruction outcomes, and fewer studies have treated early postoperative clinical gait recovery as the primary outcome or systematically analyzed its risk factors [6].

Gait recovery is an important component of patient-perceived function after THA. Persistent limping or Trendelenburg gait not only affects daily activities, but also suggests potential abductor dysfunction, inadequate biomechanical reconstruction, or delayed rehabilitation progress. Three months after surgery usually corresponds to an early clinical assessment point after completion of basic weight-bearing, transition to out-of-hospital rehabilitation, and routine outpatient follow-up after primary THA. Persistent clinical gait abnormality at this stage may indicate a need for intensified rehabilitation and review of radiographic reconstruction; however, its clinical meaning differs from mid- to long-term functional prognosis or persistent three-dimensional kinematic deficits [7, 8].

Trendelenburg sign and LLD represent two key clinical dimensions that may affect gait recovery after THA. The former reflects hip abductor insufficiency, whereas the latter may influence early gait through pelvic obliquity, trunk compensation, and step-length asymmetry. Existing studies have usually discussed these factors separately, and an explanatory predictive framework that simultaneously incorporates preoperative function, deformity stratification, LLD, surgical complexity, and postoperative radiographic reconstruction parameters in Crowe type I/II DDH remains lacking [9, 10].

This study aimed to build an explanatory model based on preoperative and intraoperative factors to identify factors associated with delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months after primary THA in patients with Crowe type I/II DDH and to stratify patients at high early risk. An extended model further assessed the additional explanatory value of early postoperative radiographic reconstruction parameters, and the stability of the results was examined using alternative outcome definitions, a unilateral DDH subgroup, complete-case analysis, a parsimonious model, an all-prespecified-covariate model, and LLD threshold analyses.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study included adult patients with DDH who underwent primary THA at our institution between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. The study was approved by the institutional medical ethics committee. Because this was a retrospective analysis of de-identified medical records and radiographic data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

During the study period, adult DDH cases undergoing primary THA that initially met the eligibility criteria were screened according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) framework. A total of 421 cases were screened, and 138 were excluded: 54 with Crowe type III/IV disease, 27 with previous surgery on the affected hip, 25 with diseases independently affecting walking function, 11 with simultaneous bilateral THA, 4 with severe perioperative complications requiring reoperation and directly affecting gait assessment, and 17 lost to follow-up or missing primary outcome/key exposure data. Finally, 283 patients with Crowe type I/II DDH were included. The patient selection process is presented according to the STROBE framework (Fig. 1) [11].

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a definitive diagnosis of adult DDH based on standard anteroposterior pelvic radiographs combined with clinical data and classification as Crowe type I or II; (3) primary THA on the affected side for DDH-related end-stage hip disease with persistent pain and/or functional impairment after failed standard conservative treatment; and (4) complete perioperative data and completion of 3-month postoperative follow-up.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Crowe type III or IV DDH; (2) previous surgery on the affected hip, including but not limited to osteotomy, internal fixation, hip arthroscopy, or revision arthroplasty; (3) concomitant diseases that could independently affect walking function, including osteonecrosis of the femoral head, hip infection, rheumatoid arthritis, post-traumatic deformity, neuromuscular disease, sequelae of stroke, and symptomatic lumbar, knee, or ankle disorders that substantially affected walking function; (4) simultaneous bilateral THA; (5) severe perioperative complications requiring reoperation and directly affecting postoperative gait assessment; and (6) loss to follow-up or missing primary outcome/key exposure variables.

For patients with bilateral DDH who underwent staged bilateral THA during the study period, only the side that first underwent THA was included as the index hip. The diagnosis of adult DDH was determined comprehensively according to standard anteroposterior pelvic radiographs and clinical data; Crowe classification was determined according to the degree of proximal femoral-head displacement and checked for consistency using standardized classification descriptions and literature on DDH-THA reconstruction [12, 13].

Radiographic diagnosis mainly referred to accepted criteria for insufficient acetabular coverage, including an LCEA < 20° and/or Sharp angle > 45°, combined with findings such as discontinuity of Shenton’s line and a shallow acetabulum. Crowe classification was determined using the original Crowe method, namely the percentage of proximal displacement of the femoral head-neck junction relative to the true acetabulum in relation to pelvic height on standard anteroposterior pelvic radiographs; Crowe type I was defined as < 10%, and Crowe type II as 10%-15%.

Surgical technique

All operations were primary THA procedures on the affected side and were performed by the institutional arthroplasty team under general anesthesia or neuraxial anesthesia. Standard preoperative anteroposterior pelvic radiographs were routinely obtained, and hip computed tomography (CT) or three-dimensional reconstruction was added when necessary for individualized preoperative templating and implant planning. The surgical principles were to reconstruct the hip center of rotation within the true acetabulum as much as possible and to restore limb length, femoral offset, and soft-tissue tension while ensuring joint stability and initial implant fixation.

The surgical approach, acetabular cup and femoral stem specifications, use of screw-assisted fixation, and necessary soft-tissue release or other reconstruction measures were determined by the surgeon according to the patient’s anatomic deformity, bone stock, bone quality, and intraoperative stability, and were fully documented in the operative record.

All patients received standardized perioperative management and rehabilitation protocols at our center. Bedside standing and gait training were usually initiated on postoperative day 1–2, and ambulation was gradually advanced with assistive support. Once initial implant stability was confirmed intraoperatively, full weight-bearing as tolerated was usually allowed. Routine use of a cane, crutches, or walker at 3 months after surgery was recorded as a secondary exploratory recovery outcome and was not included in the adjudication of the primary outcome. If medical records clearly indicated that the use of an assistive device was caused by reasons unrelated to the operated hip, it was not counted as this secondary outcome [14, 15].

Perioperative variables further included implant fixation method (cementless total hip arthroplasty or hybrid/cemented fixation). Because the vast majority of primary THA procedures for Crowe type I/II DDH at our center used cementless fixation, implant fixation method was included as a potential confounder in a supplementary model rather than in the primary model [16, 17]. When necessary, three-dimensional planning or robot-assisted strategies were used for preoperative templating and verification of implant positioning [18]. Postoperative activity restrictions and assistive-device use were recorded as supplementary rehabilitation information and were not used as criteria for adjudicating the primary outcome [19, 20].

Data collection and outcome definitions

Study data were obtained from the hospital electronic medical record system, anesthesia records, operative records, nursing records, the picture archiving and communication system (PACS), and postoperative outpatient/rehabilitation follow-up records. All data were independently collected by two uniformly trained investigators using a predesigned case report form. Disagreements were reviewed and adjudicated by a third investigator.

The collected candidate variables included: (1) general characteristics: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), affected side, bilateral DDH status, smoking history, comorbidities such as hypertension/diabetes mellitus, and ASA physical status class; (2) preoperative clinical variables: symptom duration (time from patient-reported onset of persistent hip pain or gait abnormality to surgery, cross-checked using outpatient history, admission records, and preoperative assessment records), visual analog scale (VAS) pain score, HHS, preoperative need for a walking aid, preoperative Trendelenburg sign, and hip range of motion; (3) preoperative radiographic variables: Crowe classification and preoperative LLD; (4) perioperative variables: surgical approach, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion, acetabular screw-assisted fixation, and implant fixation method; and (5) early postoperative radiographic variables: residual LLD, height of the acetabular center of rotation, cup abduction angle, and femoral offset change.

The prespecified variables for the primary analysis were preoperative and intraoperative factors, including age, sex, BMI, Crowe classification, bilateral DDH status, ASA physical status class, symptom duration, preoperative HHS, preoperative need for a walking aid, preoperative Trendelenburg sign, preoperative LLD, surgical approach, implant fixation method, operative time, and intraoperative blood loss. Early postoperative radiographic indicators (residual LLD, height of the acetabular center of rotation, cup abduction angle, and femoral offset change) were included in the extended model to explore the relationship between the quality of biomechanical reconstruction and the outcome.

Preoperative Trendelenburg sign was extracted in a standardized manner from preoperative outpatient physical examination records. Before data extraction, two investigators received uniform training covering interpretation criteria for the single-leg stance test, HHS limp subscore rules, keyword extraction from medical records, and procedures for handling ambiguous cases. The sign was judged positive only when the record clearly described a positive standardized single-leg stance test, contralateral pelvic drop, or an explicit positive Trendelenburg sign documented by the attending physician. Non-specific descriptions such as “difficulty walking” or “poor gait” alone were not directly considered positive and were instead submitted to a third investigator for review. In a random sample of 60 records selected for repeat extraction, agreement for preoperative Trendelenburg sign data extraction was κ = 0.84 (95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 0.77–0.91).

Three months after surgery was prespecified as the primary outcome assessment time point. This time point corresponds to the early functional assessment phase after completion of basic weight-bearing, transition to out-of-hospital rehabilitation, and routine clinical follow-up after primary THA. Recent THA rehabilitation literature also regards the early postoperative period as an important window for evaluating walking ability, gradual discontinuation of assistive devices, and functional training goals [21, 22]. This study used this time point to identify patients who still had clinical limping or had not reached the “no limp” state on the HHS limp subscore, and the scope of the conclusions was restricted to early clinical gait recovery status.

The primary outcome was delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months after surgery. Patients were classified as having delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months if they met either of the following criteria: (1) persistent limping or Trendelenburg gait clearly recorded in outpatient or rehabilitation records after full weight-bearing was permitted; or (2) concurrent HHS limp subscore < 11. In the HHS limp subscore, “no limp” corresponds to 11 points, “mild limp” to 8 points, “moderate limp” to 5 points, and “severe limp” to 0 points; therefore, a score < 11 indicates the presence of any observable limp. This sensitive threshold was used to identify patients who had not reached a “no limp” state during early recovery; a strict definition requiring both criteria and a single-component definition of “HHS limp subscore < 11” were used in sensitivity analyses [23, 24].

The primary outcome was independently adjudicated by two investigators on the basis of de-identified outpatient records, rehabilitation records, and concurrent HHS assessments. During outcome adjudication, the investigators did not review preoperative radiographic measurements or intraoperative records. Disagreements were resolved by a third investigator. Cohen’s kappa (κ) was used to assess inter-rater agreement for the primary outcome and its two component indicators, and 95% CIs were reported.

Radiographic measurements were based on the most recent preoperative digital standard anteroposterior pelvic radiograph and early postoperative digital standard anteroposterior pelvic radiographs obtained within 1 week after surgery. Measurement methods for LLD, height of the acetabular center of rotation, cup abduction angle, and femoral offset change are illustrated in Fig. 2. Preoperative and postoperative LLD were defined as the absolute difference in the vertical distances from the highest points of the bilateral lesser trochanters to the inter-teardrop line; postoperative residual LLD was measured using the same method. For patients with bilateral DDH, the inter-teardrop line was used as a pelvic horizontal reference, but neither hip was used as a normal control. This method provides a relative radiographic estimate of limb-length difference, whereas bilateral pelvic compensation, spinal inclination, and patient-perceived inequality should still be interpreted together with clinical information [25, 26].

Femoral offset was defined as the perpendicular distance from the anatomical axis of the femoral shaft to the center of the femoral head; femoral offset change was defined as postoperative femoral offset on the affected side minus preoperative femoral offset on the affected side. Cup abduction angle was defined as the angle between the opening plane of the acetabular cup and the inter-teardrop line [27, 28].

To assess radiographic measurement error, preoperative and early postoperative anteroposterior pelvic radiographs from 60 randomly selected patients were independently and repeatedly measured by two investigators for preoperative LLD, postoperative residual LLD, height of the acetabular center of rotation, cup abduction angle, and femoral offset change. Each investigator measured the same set of images again after a 2-week interval, with the measurement order re-randomized. Interobserver and intraobserver intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated using a two-way random-effects absolute-agreement model, and the standard error of measurement (SEM) and minimal detectable change (MDC95 = 1.96 × √2 × SEM) were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Descriptive statistics were first generated for all variables. Distributional characteristics of continuous variables were evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plots. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared between groups using the independent-samples t test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range [M (Q1, Q3)] and were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared between groups using the χ² test or Fisher exact test.

Delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months after surgery was used as the dependent variable. Univariable analyses were first performed to describe crude associations between candidate variables and the outcome. Multivariable analyses were then performed using binary logistic regression. To preserve temporal ordering as far as possible, two models were prespecified: Model 1 included preoperative and intraoperative variables to identify clinical factors associated with the outcome; Model 2 further included early postoperative radiographic reconstruction parameters based on Model 1 to explore the independent association between the quality of biomechanical reconstruction and the outcome.

Sample size was determined by the number of consecutive cases that met the inclusion criteria and had available primary outcome and key exposure variables during the study period. A total of 283 patients were finally included, with 92 primary outcome events. Model 1 contained 11 free parameters, and the events per variable (EPV) ratio was approximately 8.4, below the traditional recommended threshold. Model 1 was used to describe independent associations between preoperative/intraoperative clinical factors and early outcomes. To assess the risk of overfitting, a parsimonious model including only age, symptom duration, preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe classification, and preoperative LLD was additionally constructed, and an all-prespecified-covariate supplementary model including sex, BMI, surgical approach, and implant fixation method was also provided.

Covariates in the primary model were not selected solely according to P values in univariable analyses, but were prespecified according to clinical relevance and previous literature. In principle, continuous variables entered the model in their original continuous form. Restricted cubic splines were used to test the linearity between continuous variables and the logit; when obvious deviation from linearity was detected, appropriate transformation or categorization was performed according to clinical interpretability. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 5 indicating obvious collinearity. Regression results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs.

Relationships among candidate variables were described using Spearman correlation, point-biserial correlation, or the φ coefficient, and collinearity was assessed using VIF. For missing data, the number and proportion of missing values for each variable were reported, and Little’s missing completely at random (MCAR) test was used to assess the missing-data mechanism. Except for the primary outcome and key exposure variables, the overall proportion of missing covariates was low and no clear departure from the MCAR assumption was indicated; therefore, MICE with 20 imputations was used. Predictive mean matching was used for continuous variables, logistic regression for binary variables, and multinomial logistic regression for multicategory variables. Regression results from the imputed datasets were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. Sensitivity analyses included the unilateral DDH subgroup, complete cases, alternative outcome definitions, threshold analyses for postoperative residual LLD ≥ 5 mm and ≥ 10 mm, subgroup interaction tests by age/sex/Crowe classification, and collinearity and alternative-model tests for preoperative LLD and postoperative residual LLD.

Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test combined with residual and influential-point diagnostics. Adjusted ORs and 95% CIs from the primary and extended models were presented with a forest plot. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient screening, missing data, primary outcome, and component indicators

During the study period, 421 adult patients with DDH who underwent primary THA were screened. A total of 138 patients were excluded according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 283 patients with Crowe type I/II DDH were finally included. Among them, 92 patients had delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months after surgery, and 191 had no delayed clinical gait recovery (Fig. 1).

Among the 283 included patients, there were no missing data for the primary outcome, Crowe classification, preoperative Trendelenburg sign, preoperative LLD, or major surgical variables. Twenty-two cases had at least 1 missing non-key covariate, and 261 were complete cases. The missing rate for any individual variable was low, and Little’s MCAR test did not indicate a clear departure from the MCAR assumption (Supplementary Table 1).

The raw adjudications of the primary outcome by the two investigators showed good agreement. Assessor A classified 94 patients as having delayed gait recovery, whereas assessor B classified 90 patients as having delayed gait recovery. Both assessors classified 86 patients as delayed and 185 as not delayed; 12 cases had disagreements and were adjudicated by a third investigator. Ultimately, 92 patients were classified as having delayed gait recovery and 191 as not having delayed gait recovery. The observed agreement rate for the primary outcome was 95.8%, with Cohen’s κ = 0.90 (95% CI: 0.85–0.95, P < 0.001). Agreement for the two component indicators was also good (Supplementary Table 2).

The incidence of delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months was 32.5%. Among patients with delayed recovery, persistent limping or Trendelenburg gait records and HHS limp subscore < 11 both accounted for high proportions, and most patients met both criteria. A smaller proportion met only a single criterion (Table 1).

Comparison of general characteristics and preoperative clinical and radiographic features between groups

Compared with the no-delayed-recovery group, the delayed-recovery group was older, had a higher ASA physical status class, longer symptom duration, more severe preoperative pain, lower preoperative HHS, and smaller preoperative hip range of motion. The proportions of bilateral DDH, preoperative need for a walking aid, positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe type II, and greater preoperative LLD were also higher (all P < 0.05). The distributions of sex, BMI, affected side, smoking history, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus were similar between the two groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Comparison of perioperative data, early postoperative radiographic results, and recovery-related indicators between groups

Perioperative data showed that the delayed-recovery group had longer operative time and greater intraoperative blood loss (both P < 0.05), whereas the distributions of surgical approach, intraoperative transfusion, acetabular screw-assisted fixation, and implant fixation method were similar between the groups (all P > 0.05). Early postoperative radiographic results showed that the delayed-recovery group had greater postoperative residual LLD and smaller femoral offset change (both P < 0.05), whereas differences in height of the acetabular center of rotation and cup abduction angle were small between groups (both P > 0.05). As a secondary exploratory recovery outcome, the proportion of routine walking-aid use at 3 months after surgery was higher in the delayed-recovery group (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis and model diagnostics

Correlation analysis showed that preoperative Trendelenburg sign had low-to-moderate correlations with preoperative HHS, preoperative need for a walking aid, and preoperative hip range of motion. The maximum VIF in Model 1 was 2.72, indicating no serious collinearity (Supplementary Table 3). After sex, BMI, surgical approach, and implant fixation method were included in the all-prespecified-covariate model, the effect directions of the core variables remained consistent with the primary model (Supplementary Table 4). In the parsimonious model, the EPV was 18.4, and the directions and statistical significance of the core variables remained stable (Supplementary Table 5).

Model 1 was the primary analysis model and was based on pooled results after 20 MICE imputations. Model 1 included preoperative and intraoperative variables. All covariates had VIF values < 3.0, indicating no obvious multicollinearity; the Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed χ² = 6.41 and P = 0.602. In Model 1, age, symptom duration, positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe type II, and preoperative LLD were associated with delayed gait recovery at 3 months after surgery (all P < 0.05).

Model 2 was the extended exploratory model. After early postoperative radiographic parameters were further included based on Model 1, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed χ² = 7.18 and P = 0.517. In Model 2, age, symptom duration, positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe type II, preoperative LLD, and postoperative residual LLD remained associated with delayed gait recovery (all P < 0.05), whereas height of the acetabular center of rotation, cup abduction angle, and femoral offset change showed no clear associations (all P > 0.05) (Table 5).

Sensitivity analyses and secondary exploratory outcome

Sensitivity analyses showed that after restriction to the unilateral DDH subgroup and complete cases, the association directions of positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe type II, preoperative LLD, age, and symptom duration with the outcome remained consistent (Table 6). In analyses using alternative outcome definitions, preoperative Trendelenburg sign remained associated with the outcome when the outcome was limited to HHS limp subscore < 11 or when a strict definition requiring both criteria was used. LLD threshold analyses showed that postoperative residual LLD ≥ 5 mm was associated with a higher risk of delayed recovery, whereas cases with residual LLD ≥ 10 mm were few and did not reach statistical significance. Preoperative LLD and postoperative residual LLD showed a low-to-moderate correlation, and both VIF values in Model 2 were < 1.5; alternative models showed consistent directions (Supplementary Table 7). Logistic regression using routine walking-aid use at 3 months after surgery as a secondary exploratory outcome showed that positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign and preoperative LLD were associated with assistive-device use (Table 7).

Discussion

Based on 283 patients with Crowe type I/II DDH who underwent primary THA, this study systematically evaluated the incidence of delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months after surgery and its associated factors. The main findings were as follows: the incidence of delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months was 32.5%; age, symptom duration, positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe type II, and preoperative LLD were independently associated with delayed early recovery; the extended model showed that postoperative residual LLD provided additional explanatory value, whereas height of the acetabular center of rotation, cup abduction angle, and femoral offset change showed no independent associations. Sensitivity analyses, the all-prespecified-covariate model, the parsimonious model, and alternative outcome analyses all supported the stability of the core findings.

Positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign was the clinical factor with the largest effect size in this study. This sign indicates hip abductor insufficiency and may reflect the combined consequences of long-standing insufficient acetabular coverage, lateral displacement of the femoral head, and failure of compensation in the tendon-muscle unit. Recent literature suggests that structural lesions of the hip abductors and insufficient recovery of muscle strength can affect early biomechanical recovery after THA, and patients with poorer preoperative muscle status are more likely to have reduced gait efficiency and pelvic compensation during early postoperative recovery. The higher risk of persistent clinical gait abnormality at 3 months after surgery among patients with a positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign in this study is consistent with these mechanisms [29, 30].

Preoperative Trendelenburg sign and Trendelenburg gait recorded in the 3-month postoperative outcome are not repeated measurements at the same time point. The former reflects abductor insufficiency during the preoperative single-leg stance test, whereas the latter reflects persistent clinical gait abnormality after completion of early weight-bearing and rehabilitation. Alternative outcome analyses showed that the association direction of preoperative Trendelenburg sign remained stable when the outcome was limited to HHS limp subscore < 11 or when the strict definition requiring both criteria was used, suggesting that conceptual overlap had a limited effect on the results. Nevertheless, this variable was derived from retrospective outpatient records, and the execution and completeness of physical examination documentation may influence effect estimates. Prospective standardized physical examination, abductor strength testing, and objective gait analysis could further strengthen the evidence.

Crowe type II was associated with a higher risk of delayed gait recovery than Crowe type I. Even within the relatively mild-to-moderate deformity spectrum of Crowe type I/II, increased femoral-head displacement may still be accompanied by a shallow acetabulum, adaptive soft-tissue contracture, greater difficulty in true-acetabulum reconstruction, and delayed postoperative muscle-strength recovery. Recent DDH-THA literature suggests that greater deformity severity increases the technical demands of reconstruction and uncertainty in functional recovery. This study further indicates that Crowe type II retains identifiable value for early functional risk stratification even in a cohort excluding Crowe type III/IV disease [31, 32].

Preoperative LLD was independently associated with delayed recovery, suggesting that pelvic obliquity, trunk compensation, and step-length asymmetry caused by long-standing limb-length inequality may persist in the short term after THA. Postoperative residual LLD also showed a statistical association in the extended model, but the median between-group difference was only 1 mm and was smaller than the MDC95 for radiographic measurement in this study. The statistical association of residual LLD as a continuous variable is more appropriately interpreted as an overall trend indicating reconstruction quality, whereas the clinical meaning of a single 1-mm difference is limited. Threshold analyses showed that postoperative residual LLD ≥ 5 mm was associated with a higher risk of delayed recovery, whereas cases with residual LLD ≥ 10 mm were few and statistical power was insufficient. This finding is exploratory and requires further validation together with patient perception, pelvic compensation, and long-term functional outcomes [33, 34].

The effect sizes for age and symptom duration were small but directionally stable. Increasing age may reflect reduced muscle mass, decreased neuromuscular coordination, and lower rehabilitation tolerance. Longer symptom duration may indicate more prolonged pain inhibition, disuse muscle atrophy, and consolidation of compensatory gait patterns. The clinical meaning of ORs for continuous variables should be interpreted according to the actual scale; symptom duration reflects chronic symptom burden, and the clinical effect of a 1-year change is small. For patients with a long disease course, poor preoperative function, or impaired abductor function, perioperative rehabilitation may place greater emphasis on muscle strength, balance, and gait-control training [35, 36].

Among perioperative factors, operative time and intraoperative blood loss were associated with the outcome in univariable analyses but were no longer significant in multivariable models, suggesting that these indicators more likely reflect deformity complexity and surgical difficulty. Surgical approach and implant fixation method showed no independent effect in the all-prespecified-covariate model. Recent studies comparing different approaches have not been fully consistent, but under standardized technique and rehabilitation pathways, the influence of approach on early clinical gait recovery may be smaller than that of preoperative abductor function, deformity stratification, and LLD [37, 38].

Femoral offset change showed some association in univariable analysis but did not show independent statistical significance in the extended model. Descriptive statistics showed that femoral offset change was concentrated within a relatively narrow range, and its interobserver ICC was lower than that of other radiographic indicators. This study cannot exclude a potential effect of femoral-offset reconstruction on gait; however, under the measurement method, range of variation, and model specification used in this sample, femoral offset change did not provide independent explanatory value. More precise three-dimensional reconstruction assessment, dynamic muscle-arm analysis, and long-term gait parameters may help clarify this issue further [39, 40].

The interpretation of this study was supported by dual-assessor outcome adjudication, agreement analysis, missing-data reporting, MICE imputation, the all-prespecified-covariate model, the parsimonious model, alternative outcome definitions, LLD threshold analyses, and radiographic measurement-error assessment. Nevertheless, the primary outcome was still based on retrospective clinical records and the HHS limp subscore rather than objective kinematic deficits confirmed by three-dimensional gait analysis. Therefore, the results are mainly intended for early clinical risk stratification. Future studies may incorporate spatiotemporal gait parameters, ground reaction forces, three-dimensional kinematics, abductor electromyography, and muscle-quality assessment, and should verify whether delayed early clinical recovery predicts persistent gait impairment at 6 months, 1 year, and longer follow-up [41, 42].

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design was inevitably affected by documentation quality and selection bias. Second, symptom duration was based on patient recall and may have been subject to non-differential measurement error, potentially biasing effect estimates toward the null. Third, the contralateral hip was not used as a normal reference in patients with bilateral DDH. Although results in the unilateral DDH subgroup were consistent, interpretation of LLD in the context of bilateral morphologic abnormalities still requires consideration of pelvic compensation and patient perception. Fourth, the sample came from a single center in China, and female patients accounted for 84.1%; caution is therefore needed when extrapolating the findings to populations with a higher proportion of male patients, different ethnic or genetic backgrounds, different rehabilitation resources, or different surgical systems. Fifth, the EPV of Model 1 was below the traditional threshold; although supplementary models yielded consistent results, external validation remains necessary. Three months after surgery remains a dynamic recovery stage, and some patients may continue to improve during subsequent follow-up. Because this study did not include 6-month, 1-year, or long-term instrumented gait analyses, it cannot determine whether delayed early clinical recovery persists as a long-term kinematic abnormality.

Conclusions

In this predominantly female Chinese single-center cohort of patients with Crowe type I/II DDH undergoing primary THA, delayed clinical gait recovery at 3 months after surgery was not uncommon. Positive preoperative Trendelenburg sign, Crowe type II, greater preoperative LLD, older age, and longer symptom duration were independently associated with delayed early clinical gait recovery; postoperative residual LLD provided additional explanatory value in the extended model. These factors may be used to identify patients at higher risk of delayed early clinical gait recovery before surgery, but this study did not perform instrumented gait analysis, and long-term three-dimensional gait-kinematic prognosis requires further validation.
