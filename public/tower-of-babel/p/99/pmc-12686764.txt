Measuring Anxiety and Depression in Adolescents: Psychometric Properties of the Overall Anxiety and Depression Severity and Impairment Scales
Authors: Inês Maçãs‐Carvalho, Nélio Brazão, Marco Pereira, Maria Cristina Canavarro, Daniel Rijo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnxiety and depression are among the most prevalent mental health disorders in adolescence. However, there is a lack of efficient instruments that simultaneously assess both the severity and impairment associated with these symptoms. This study aims to adapt and further validate the Overall Anxiety Severity and Impairment Scale (OASIS) and the Overall Depression Severity and Impairment Scale (ODSIS) for adolescents.MethodsThe psychometric properties of the OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A were examined in a community sample of 598 adolescents (51% male) from Portuguese public schools. Analyses included factor structure, internal consistency, measurement invariance, construct validity, gender‐based comparisons and cutoff scores.ResultsFactor analysis supported a unidimensional structure for both the OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A. The internal consistency was excellent for both scales (Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω for the OASIS‐A were 0.90 and for ODSIS‐A were 0.94) and measurement invariance across gender was established. The OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A showed significant correlations with measures of anxiety, depression, quality of life and mental well‐being, in theoretically expected directions. Additionally, girls reported significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression. Optimal cutoff scores were identified as 7 for OASIS‐A and 5 for ODSIS‐A.ConclusionsThis study presents the first psychometric evaluation of the OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A, underscoring the utility of brief self‐report measures for the severity and impairment associated with anxiety and depression in adolescents. Findings also support the use of these tools for screening purposes, particularly in school settings.Keywords: adolescents, anxiety, depression, prevention, screening, validation

Introduction

Anxiety and depression are among the most common mental health problems in adolescence (Essau et al. 2018; Merikangas et al. 2010) and often co‐occur, with 75% of depressed youth also experiencing anxiety (Cummings et al. 2014). These symptoms lead to significant impairment across various domains of life (Rapee et al. 2009), including social and academic functioning (Campbell‐Sills et al. 2009; Chiu et al. 2021; Mohammadi et al. 2020), with a negative impact on quality of life (Lyneham et al. 2013). If left untreated, these problems will likely persist into adulthood (Penninx et al. 2021; Skokauskas et al. 2019; Wiebenga et al. 2021).

According to the World Health Organization (Freeman 2022), half of the mental disorders diagnosed in adulthood have their onset before the age of 14. Research also indicates that the average age of onset for mental disorders is approximately 14.5 years (Solmi et al. 2022). Adolescence is a developmental period characterized by fundamental biological, cognitive, social and emotional changes (Stephanou et al. 2016; Theurel and Gentaz 2018), which increase vulnerability to psychological stress and the risk for psychopathology (Desatnik et al. 2017). Approximately 30% of European adolescents exhibit subclinical symptoms of anxiety and depression (Balázs et al. 2013), and globally, one in seven adolescents lives with a mental disorder (Freeman 2022). Anxiety and depressive disorders constitute the most common mental health conditions among adolescents (Essau et al. 2018). However, many young people do not seek help early, often due to difficulties in recognizing their symptoms and the need for professional intervention (Radez et al. 2021). Help‐seeking behaviour increases when multiple mental health problems are present, and this tendency is more common for behavioural than for emotional disorders (Chavira et al. 2004, 2009). The significant short‐ and long‐term consequences of untreated mental health problems during adolescence, as well as the lack of diagnosis and intervention, underscore the critical importance of early assessment and detection of mental health problems in this age group (Radez et al. 2021).

Schools often serve as the first point of contact and support for adolescents' mental health, making them an ideal and accessible setting for prevention efforts (Cosma et al. 2023). Prevention strategies can be classified as universal (i.e., delivered to all individuals regardless of risk level or existing symptoms), selective (i.e., delivered to those at heightened risk for developing a specific disorder) or indicated (i.e., delivered to individuals exhibiting subclinical symptoms; Mrazek and Haggerty 1994; Werner‐Seidler et al. 2017). Following a systematic review, the Community Preventive Service Task Force (2024) recommends universal, school‐based cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) programs to prevent depression and anxiety symptoms among school‐aged children and adolescents. These programs aim to strengthen problem‐solving skills, enhance emotion regulation and promote adaptive patterns of thought and behaviour (Community Preventive Services Task Force 2024). Universal prevention within school settings offers several advantages over selective or indicated approaches: It is delivered to all students, thereby reducing stigma; it allows for the early identification of adolescents who may not currently show symptoms but are at risk of developing them; and it is easier to schedule and implement within the school context (Werner‐Seidler et al. 2017). A stepped‐care model—where universal CBT programs are implemented first, followed by more targeted interventions for students who continue to show symptoms—has also been recommended (Community Preventive Services Task Force 2024). Furthermore, international guidelines highlight the importance of increasing mental health screenings in schools, as adolescents spend much of their time in this setting and can therefore be efficiently reached (Lustig et al. 2023). The combination of school‐based screening and CBT interventions has been identified as one of the most cost‐effective strategies for preventing mental disorders in this population, compared to non‐CBT interventions, psychoeducation programs or parenting programs. This approach integrates early detection, evidence‐based intervention and scalable delivery within a context that maximizes reach while minimizing cost (Le et al. 2021).

Universal mental health screening using validated questionnaires is an effective strategy for the early identification of adolescents in need of mental health support, enabling preventive interventions (Braund et al. 2024). When combined with appropriate interventions, screening can be helpful in reducing symptoms and enhancing positive mental health outcomes (Siu and U.S. Preventive Services Task Force 2016). Universal school‐based screening has been shown to identify and refer significantly more adolescents to mental health services compared to targeted approaches (Husky et al. 2011), with adolescents being nearly six times more likely to be detected through universal screening (Sekhar et al. 2021).

Several validated self‐reported measures have been shown to effectively assess specific emotional disorders (i.e., anxiety and depressive disorders) in youths (Gotlib and Hammen 2008; Schniering et al. 2000). However, applying multiple questionnaires in school settings is time‐consuming, which represents a disadvantage. Furthermore, many instruments focus only on symptom severity, overlooking daily life impact (Ito, Oe, et al. 2015), which is one of the main reasons individuals seek mental health support (Creswell et al. 2021; Rapee et al. 2012). While severity refers to the frequency, extent and duration of behavioral or emotional symptoms, impairment denotes the extent to which these symptoms interfere with daily functioning (Etkin et al. 2024). Impairment has also been conceptualized as a socially and ecologically valid indicator of psychopathology (Fabiano and Pelham 2016). These dimensions are related but not always aligned (Pickles et al. 2001), as they depend on factors like individual's coping style, social support and personal characteristics (Sandín et al. 2020). Young people may experience severe symptoms with minimal interference in daily life, or conversely, less severe or subclinical symptoms that nonetheless cause significant functional impairment (Pickles et al. 2001). Both severity and impairment are essential when assessing emotional disorders (Rapee et al. 2012). Particularly in prevention, evaluating both symptom severity and functional impairment (beyond merely assessing symptoms or diagnostic criteria) is crucial, as adolescents may not meet full diagnostic thresholds yet still experience symptoms that significantly interfere with daily life.

Various efforts have been made to develop instruments assessing depressive and anxiety symptoms in adolescence. The Adolescent Life Interference Scale for Internalizing Symptoms (ALIS‐I; Schniering et al. 2023) is one of the measures assessing the functional interference of anxious and depressive symptoms in adolescents. However, this scale is considered too long (26 items) to be used in school settings, namely for screening purposes. Another example is the Depression and Anxiety Interference Scale for Children and Adolescents (EIDAN), the first measure designed in Spain to assess the interference of anxiety and depression across the life domains of children and adolescents (Espinosa et al. 2022). Despite being shorter (10 items), it assesses impairment alone, ignoring symptom severity. In school settings, transdiagnostic measures would be preferable due to their brevity and their design to capture core features of various emotional disorders, rather than focusing on specific symptoms or individual disorders (Campbell‐Sills et al. 2009). Given the impact of anxiety and depression on adolescents, and the importance of assessing their interference with daily functioning, it is essential to develop instruments that capture these dimensions during adolescence (Schniering et al. 2023), especially considering the limitations of existing measures.

Two short measures were designed to assess both severity and impairment of anxiety and depressive symptoms in adults: The Overall Anxiety Severity and Impairment Scale (OASIS; Norman et al. 2006) and the Overall Depression Severity and Impairment Scale (ODSIS; Bentley et al. 2014). The OASIS and ODSIS are brief, five‐item measures that are time‐efficient and easy to administer. The OASIS was developed for use in both community and clinical settings, targeting a range of anxiety disorders as well as subthreshold anxiety symptoms (Comer et al. 2022). Based on this instrument, Bentley et al. (2014) developed the ODSIS to assess transdiagnostic depressive symptoms and associated functional impairment.

The OASIS has been validated in multiple languages, including English (Norman et al. 2006), Spanish (Osma et al. 2019), Japanese (Ito, Oe, et al. 2015), Dutch (Hermans et al. 2015) and Farsi (Farrahi et al. 2020). Across these validations, a single‐factor structure has been consistently confirmed, and the scale has demonstrated good internal consistency (Bragdon et al. 2016; Campbell‐Sills et al. 2009; Ito, Oe, et al. 2015; Moore et al. 2015; Osma et al. 2019). In both clinical and nonclinical samples, suggested cutoff scores for the OASIS typically ranged between 8 and 9 (Ito, Oe, et al. 2015; Norman et al. 2011).

The ODSIS has similarly been validated in English (Bentley et al. 2014), Spanish (Mira et al. 2019; Osma et al. 2019) and Japanese (Ito, Bentley, et al. 2015). Like the OASIS, it has demonstrated a single‐factor structure and excellent internal consistency (Bentley et al. 2014; Ito, Bentley, et al. 2015; Osma et al. 2019; Sandora et al. 2021). Across studies with clinical and nonclinical samples, proposed cutoff scores have ranged from 7 to 11 (Bentley et al. 2014; Ito, Bentley, et al. 2015). However, to date, neither measure has been validated specifically for adolescents. The closest attempt was conducted by Comer et al. (2022) who adapted the adult version of the OASIS into a brief caregiver‐report format (OASIS‐Y) to assess youth anxiety severity and impairment. In this adaptation, all items were reworded to reflect the caregiver's perspective, and two additional items were introduced to assess interference with family functioning and caregiver functioning. This adaptation demonstrated good psychometric properties, comparable to findings from adult self‐report versions (Comer et al. 2022).

To our best knowledge, this is the first study aimed at adapting and validating the OASIS and the ODSIS for adolescents (i.e., OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A). This adaptation was designed to address the need for brief, transdiagnostic and psychometrically robust instruments capable of assessing both the severity and functional impairment associated with anxiety and depression symptoms in Portuguese adolescents, particularly within school settings. Specifically, we sought to (1) examine the factor structure of the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A, testing whether the unidimensional structure found in the adult versions is replicated in an adolescent population; (2) assess internal consistency using Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega to evaluate the reliability of the adapted scales; (3) evaluate measurement invariance across gender, ensuring that the scales measure the intended constructs equivalently in boys and girls; (4) investigate convergent and divergent validity by examining correlations with other relevant measures of mental health, including anxiety, depression, quality of life and mental well‐being; (5) compare mean scores between genders, exploring potential differences in self‐reported anxiety and depression symptoms among male and female adolescents and (6) determine cutoff scores for each scale to identify clinically meaningful levels of symptom severity and functional impairment, thereby supporting their use as screening tools.

Method

Participants

The sample consisted of 598 adolescents (305 boys, 293 girls) attending public schools located in the northern, central and southern regions of mainland Portugal. Participants had a mean age of 13.52 (SD = 1.01; range = 11–18). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) enrolment in the 7th, 8th or 9th grade and (2) proficiency in Portuguese. Data collected through the sociodemographic questionnaire indicated that most participants were enrolled in regular education (94%), were in the 8th grade (66.1%), were not currently receiving psychological treatment (80.5%), and were not taking psychiatric medication (87.9%). Gender differences were examined using chi‐square analysis for past psychological therapy, current psychological therapy and current pharmacological treatment. The only significant difference was found for current therapy, χ2 (2) = 8.96, p = 0.011, with girls reporting higher rates than boys. Additional demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Procedures

The sample was collected through the dissemination of the study across 25 public schools in mainland Portugal. Participants were included if they were enrolled in the 7th, 8th or 9th grade and demonstrated proficiency in Portuguese. No additional exclusion criteria were applied. Written informed consent was sought from the adolescents' parents/legal guardians under 18 years old, as well as from the adolescents themselves. Participants completed OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A scales and the remaining research protocol through LimeSurvey software. Data were collected during school hours and administered in person by a clinical psychologist from the research team. All participants completed the full assessment battery. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Research of the [Blind for Review], as well as by the executive boards of schools.

For this study, we used the translation of the original OASIS adult scale (Pereira et al. 2023) through a back translation process. The OASIS was translated into European Portuguese by two independent researchers fluent in English. The Portuguese version was then translated into English by an independent researcher who was fluent in Portuguese and English and who had no previous knowledge of the scale. No significant differences were found between the original and the translated version for adults. In the present study, to enhance adolescents' comprehension and to better tailor the scale to this developmental stage, several modifications were made to the final version. For the adolescent version of the OASIS, we used the informal form (see Appendix 1). Changes were also made in item 4, which refers to the interference of anxiety at work, school or home. The term ‘at work’ was removed and the terms ‘at school or home’ were maintained. This change was made to both the item and the response options where it appeared (i.e., the first and second options). We also changed the fifth answer option, which in the original version of the scale mentions that ‘I was unable to complete my tasks and had to drop out of school, quit or was fired from my job, or I was unable to complete tasks at home and suffered consequences such as eviction, bill collectors.’ We replaced this phrase with ‘I was unable to complete my tasks at school or at home, and suffered consequences because of this, such as being punished or getting poor grades.’

For this study, we also used the Portuguese translation of the original ODSIS validated for adults (Pereira et al. 2023) through the same translation process that can be found in the OASIS description above. The same changes were added to the final European Portuguese version of the ODSIS for adolescents. Specifically, we changed item 4 in the same way described in OASIS. To increase adolescents' understanding of the term ‘depression,’ in the original initial instruction (i.e., ‘The following items ask about depression’), we added an explanation of what depression is in parentheses ‘feeling sad most of the time, for several days in a row or no longer having the desire and pleasure to do the things you liked to do before.’

Instruments

Sociodemographic Questionnaire

Sociodemographic and clinical information were collected using a self‐report questionnaire developed by the authors. The questionnaire included items on age, gender, education and area of residence. Clinical information covered past attendance at psychological therapy, current psychological therapy and current use of pharmacological medication.

Overall Anxiety Severity and Impairment Scale

The OASIS (Norman et al. 2006) is a five‐item self‐report measure that assesses the frequency and severity of anxiety, the degree of avoidance and the extent to which anxiety interferes with functioning at work, school, home and in social domains. Items (e.g., ‘In the past week, how often have you felt anxious?’) refer to the past week and are rated on a five‐point scale ranging from 0 (Little) to 4 (Extreme). The total score is calculated by summing the items (range = 0–20), with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity and impairment. In the present sample, both Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω were 0.90.

Overall Depression Severity and Impairment Scale

The ODSIS (Bentley et al. 2014) consists of five items that assess the frequency and severity of depressive symptoms, difficulties engaging in or finding interest in enjoyable activities, and the extent to which depressive symptoms interfere with functioning at work, school, home and social situations. Similarly to the OASIS, the ODSIS items (e.g., ‘In the past week, how often have you felt depressed?’) refer to the past week and are rated on a five‐point scale ranging from 0 (Little) to 4 (Extreme). The total score is calculated by summing all items (range = 0–20), where higher scores indicate a greater severity and impairment of depression. In the present sample, both Cronbach's α and McDonald's Omega were 0.94.

The Multidimensional Emotional Disorder Inventory

The MEDI (Rosellini and Brown 2019) is a self‐report questionnaire assessing the following nine dimensions: (1) neurotic temperament; (2) positive temperament; (3) depressed mood; (4) autonomic arousal; (5) somatic anxiety; (6) social anxiety; (7) intrusive cognitions; (8) traumatic re‐experiencing and (9) avoidance. It comprises 49 items that are answered in a nine‐point scale ranging from 0 (Not all characteristic of me) to 8 (Totally characteristic of me), with higher scores indicating higher levels of the dimension assessed. For this study, we used the Portuguese adult version (Pereira et al. 2025), which was adapted and is currently being validated for adolescents by the authors. In this adaptation, several modifications were made to the items in collaboration with the author of the Portuguese version to enhance clarity for adolescents. In the original study, all MEDI dimensions had acceptable reliability (ρ range = 0.68–0.93), as well as in the Portuguese study (α and ω range = 0.70–0.91). In the present study, only the dimensions of depressed mood (α and ω = 0.82), autonomic arousal (α = 0.87 and ω = 0.88), somatic anxiety (α = 0.61 and ω = 0.54) and social anxiety (α and ω = 0.88) were used.

Mental Health Continuum‐Short Form

The MHC‐SF (Keyes et al. 2008; Portuguese Adolescent Version by Matos et al. 2010) is a 14‐item self‐report scale designed to assess positive mental health, operationalized as the presence of high levels of emotional, social and psychological well‐being. Each item is rated on a six‐point scale ranging from 0 (Never) to 5 (Every day). The total score can be computed as a continuous variable (range = 0–70), with higher scores indicating higher levels of mental well‐being, or categorized into the following three categories: flourishing, moderate mental health and languishing. In the original study, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability (α > 0.80) and adequate discriminant validity among adolescents. The Portuguese version has also shown high reliability for the total scale (α = 0.90), as well as good internal consistency for each subscale (all Cronbach's α above 0.80). In the present sample, both Cronbach's α and McDonald's Omega were 0.92.

KIDSCREEN‐10 Index

The KIDSCREEN‐10 (Ravens‐Sieberer et al. 2010; Portuguese Version by Matos et al. 2012) is a self‐report measure designed to assess perceived quality of life in children and adolescents. It comprises 10 items rated on a five‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Never/Not at all) to 5 (Always/Extremely), with total scores ranging from 10 to 50. Higher scores indicate greater subjective well‐being and satisfaction across various key life domains, including family relationships, peer interactions and school experiences (Matos et al. 2012). The original version demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.82) and test–retest reliability. The Portuguese version confirmed the unidimensional structure of the instrument and showed acceptable reliability (α = 0.78). In the present sample, Cronbach's α was 0.84 and McDonald's ω was 0.83.

Data Analysis

Analyses were performed using IMB SPSS Statistics Version 27 software and Mplus v8.3 (Muthén and Muthén 2017). Sociodemographic characteristics, means and internal consistency for all measures were analysed. Additionally, the distributional characteristic of the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A items was examined. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted in Mplus, using the maximum likelihood estimator (MLM). Model fit for the OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A was evaluated using multiple fit indexes: the Chi‐square test, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI) and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Modification indices were examined when adjustments were necessary to improve model fit. A good model fit was indicated by RMSEA and SRMR values below 0.06, while values below 0.08 (RMSEA) and 0.09 (SRMR) were considered acceptable. CFI and TLI values above 0.95 indicated excellent fit, and values above 0.90 were considered acceptable (Hu and Bentler 1999). Loading values of items were analyzed and considered adequate when > 0.50 and good when > 0.70 (Hair et al. 2010). The reliability of the scales was estimated using Cronbach's α and McDonald's omega.

To investigate gender invariance, multigroup confirmatory analyses were conducted using Mplus. Following established procedures (Dimitrov 2010), the following three levels of invariance were sequentially tested: configural, metric and scalar invariance. Configural invariance assesses whether the same factor structure holds across groups, indicating that latent constructs are conceptualized similarly. Metric invariance tests whether factor loadings are equivalent across groups, suggesting that participants interpret the items similarly. Scalar invariance evaluates the equivalence of item intercepts, which is necessary for meaningful comparisons of latent means across groups. Model comparisons were based on changes in fit indices. Metric invariance was considered acceptable when changes in CFI (ΔCFI) were ≤ 0.01, combined with changes in RMSEA (ΔRMSEA) ≤ 0.015 or SRMR (ΔSRMR) ≤ 0.030. Scalar invariance was supported when ΔCFI was ≤ 0.01, along with ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 or ΔSRMR ≤ 0.010 (Chen 2007).

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the associations between the OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A scores and other measures of depression, anxiety, mental health well‐being and quality of life. Differences between boys and girls were analyzed with an Independent samples t test, and the effect size was quantified using Cohen's d, with 0.20, 0.50 and 0.80 indicating small, medium and large effects, respectively (Cohen 2013).

To provide useful screening information, a receiver operating curve (ROC) for the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A was used to determine the optimum cutoff point that maximizes the sensitivity and specificity in anxiety and depressive symptoms severity and impairment. The 75th percentile of the dimensions of the MEDI Autonomic Arousal (score = 18) and Depressed Mood (score = 20) was used as the validity criterion of the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A, respectively. Evidence of Validity Based on Internal Structure and Estimation of Reliability of OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A

For the OASIS‐A, a one‐factor model was tested, and the initial model demonstrated suboptimal fit, χ2 (5) = 51.42, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.125, 90% CI = [0.095–0.157]; CFI = 0.947; TLI = 0.894; SRMR = 0.033. However, modification indices suggested correlating the error variances of items 1 and 2—a modification also recommended in previous studies (Campbell‐Sills et al. 2009; Osma et al. 2019). After allowing this correlation, model fit improved substantially, χ2 (4) = 4.04, p = 0.401; RMSEA = 0.004, 90% CI = [0.000–0.062]; CFI = 1.000; TLI = 1.000; SRMR = 0.009, indicating excellent fit. All factor loadings were significant and considered large (Table 3). Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω for the OASIS‐A were excellent (α and ω = 0.90).

Concerning the ODSIS‐A, consistent with findings from previous research, correlating the error variances of items 1 and 2 was also necessary to achieve adequate model fit. In the present study, the initial model without this correlation showed suboptimal fit (χ2 (5) = 41.90, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.111, 90% CI = [0.081–0.143]; CFI = 0.958; TLI = 0.917; SRMR = 0.025). All factor loadings were significant and of large magnitude (Table 3). Internal consistency was excellent, with both Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω equal to 0.94.

Measurement Invariance of the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A

Configural gender invariance was established for the OASIS‐A, indicating that the overall factor structure was equivalent across male and female participants. Full metric invariance was also found (ΔRMSEA = −0.008, ΔCFI = 0.000, ΔSRMR = 0.008), suggesting equivalence of factor loadings across genders. Full scalar invariance was further confirmed (ΔRMSEA = 0.003, ΔCFI = −0.004, ΔSRMR = 0.002), indicating that items' intercepts were invariant across groups.

Similarly, the ODSIS‐A demonstrated configural invariance across gender groups. Full metric invariance was also found (ΔRMSEA = 0.007, ΔCFI = −0.005, ΔSRMR = 0.022), as well as full scalar invariance (ΔRMSEA = −0.003, ΔCFI = −0.001, ΔSRMR = 0.002).

Gender Differences

The average score for the OASIS‐A was 5.83 (SD = 5.01, range 0–20) and for the ODSIS‐A was 3.85 (SD = 4.82, range 0–20). In the OASIS‐A, an independent sample t test showed a significant difference in anxiety levels between boys and girls, t(596) = −4.57, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.37, indicating a small‐to‐medium effect size, with girls (M = 6.77; SD = 4.86) scoring significantly higher on anxiety than boys (M = 4.93; SD = 4.99). Similarly, a significant difference in depression levels, as measured by the ODSIS‐A, was found, t(596) = 9.96, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.37, indicating a small‐to‐medium effect size, with girls (M = 4.74; SD = 4.98) scoring significantly higher on depression than boys (M = 2.99; SD = 4.51).

Sensitivity, Specificity and Cutoff of OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A

According to the ROC curve, the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A indicated a good discrimination of adolescents reporting anxiety symptoms and depressive symptoms, respectively. The area under the curve was 0.84, 95% CI = [0.80–0.88] and 0.78, 95% CI = [0.74–0.83], respectively (see Figures 1 and 2). The result of the Youden index for the OASIS‐A was 0.53, with an optimal cutoff of 6.5. For the ODSIS‐A, two cutoffs were identified (4.5 and 5.5) with the same Youden index value (0.46). Given the aim of maximizing sensitivity for early identification in a school setting, the lower cutoff of 4.5 was selected. Considering these cutoffs, the sensitivity and specificity were 0.81 and 0.73 for the OASIS‐A and 0.69 and 0.77 for the ODSIS‐A. Since the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A use whole number scoring (i.e., 0–4 per item), the cutoff values were rounded up for 7 and 5, respectively. With these cutoffs, 41% of adolescents of this community sample were identified with anxiety and 29.3% with depression.

Discussion

The effective prevention and treatment of emotional disorders in adolescence rely heavily on the availability of psychometrically sound assessment tools, as well as their appropriate use in relevant contexts. In the field of prevention, routine screenings, particularly universal screenings conducted in school settings, hold significant potential for identifying adolescents at risk, including those presenting subclinical symptoms that might otherwise go unnoticed. Schools are uniquely positioned to serve as a platform for early detection and intervention, as they offer consistent access to youth and are ideal environments for promoting mental well‐being and preventing mental health problems (Fonseca‐Pedrero et al. 2023).

Importantly, research has shown that functional impairment (rather than the symptom's presence and severity alone) is often the key factor prompting individuals to seek professional help (Creswell et al. 2021). Thus, assessment instruments that explicitly evaluate the degree to which symptoms interfere with daily functioning are essential, especially during adolescence (Espinosa et al. 2022). Despite this need, there is a lack of validated tools that assess symptoms interference in adolescent populations. The present study addresses this gap by examining, for the first time, the psychometric properties of the OASIS and the ODSIS in a community sample of Portuguese adolescents within a school setting.

Consistent with the findings from the adult versions of the OASIS and the ODSIS (Bentley et al. 2014; Norman et al. 2006, 2011; Osma et al. 2019), our results support a unidimensional structure for both the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A, with all items loading strongly and significantly onto a single factor. As reported in previous research (e.g., Campbell‐Sills et al. 2009), a correlation of error terms of items 1 and 2 was necessary to achieve adequate model fit for both scales. This modification likely reflects the conceptual proximity of these two items: Item 1 assesses the frequency of anxiety/depression symptoms, while item 2 captures their intensity—two aspects that are naturally interrelated (Campbell‐Sills et al. 2009; Osma et al. 2019). These dimensionality findings support the use of the total score of OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A as brief, reliable self‐report instruments for assessing the severity and functional interference of anxiety and depression severity symptoms in adolescents.

Internal consistency was considered excellent for both measures (α = 0.90 for OASIS‐A; α = 0.94 for ODSIS‐A). These results were consistent with previous research on the adult versions of the instruments, which reported Cronbach's α coefficients ranging from 0.80 to 0.96 (Bentley et al. 2014; Bragdon et al. 2016; Ito, Bentley, et al., 2015; Moore et al. 2015; Norman et al. 2006, 2011, 2013). Regarding the average scores of the OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A, no prior normative data are available for adolescent populations, as this is the first study to examine these measures in a community sample of adolescents. Therefore, the current findings provide an important initial reference point for future research in this age group.

Measurement invariance was supported for both the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A. These instruments appear to be appropriate measures of the severity and impairment associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms for both genders. This is congruent with the two studies that reported measurement invariance among adults (Moore et al. 2015; Sandora et al. 2021).

Both the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A were positively associated with measures of anxiety and depression and negatively associated with dimensions of quality of life and mental well‐being. These findings are consistent with previous research showing that anxiety and depressive symptoms are negatively associated with adolescents' quality of life and mental well‐being (Bartels et al. 2013; Dickson et al. 2024; Freire and Ferreira 2018; Salum et al. 2014). Regarding the OASIS‐A, the strongest association was observed with autonomic arousal, defined as the experience of symptoms related to sympathetic nervous system activation (i.e., panic symptoms), which is common in the general population (Kessler et al. 2006; Rosellini and Brown 2019). An interesting, and perhaps unexpected, finding was that the second strongest correlation of the OASIS‐A with other measures was with depressed mood, rather than with somatic or social anxiety. However, this result is consistent with studies (Campbell‐Sills et al. 2009; Norman et al. 2006, 2013) indicating that high correlations between OASIS‐A and measures of depression are not surprising, given the high comorbidity between anxiety and depression (Brown and Barlow 2009). Another plausible explanation is that the social anxiety and somatic anxiety dimensions capture more specific manifestations of anxiety, whereas autonomic arousal may overlap more with generalized distress and physiological activation, which are core features captured by the OASIS‐A. As expected, the ODSIS‐A showed its strongest correlation with depressed mood, and weaker correlations with social anxiety, somatic anxiety and autonomic arousal. These results are consistent with the study of Osma et al. (2019), conducted in an adult sample, in which the ODSIS showed stronger associations with depressive symptoms compared to the anxiety measure used.

Gender differences were found, with girls scoring significantly higher in both the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A. These results are consistent with the literature, which indicates that anxiety and depression symptoms are more intense in girls than in boys (Sandora et al. 2021; WHO 2017). Furthermore, adolescent girls tend to experience higher depressive mood and anxiety compared to boys (Kuehner 2017; McLean et al. 2011). Similarly, previous validations of the adult OASIS and ODSIS reported gender differences, with females scoring significantly higher than males on both instruments (Rojas et al. 2023). Additionally, this finding is consistent with the observed gender difference in current therapy, with girls reporting higher rates than boys. This suggests that, in addition to exhibiting higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms, girls are also more likely to seek professional help.

Our findings suggest a cutoff of 7 for the OASIS‐A (81% sensitivity) and of 5 for the ODSIS‐A (69% sensitivity). This is an innovative finding, as it is the first study to establish cutoff scores using a nonclinical adolescent sample. These results are consistent with previous adult validations that suggested cutoff scores between 5 and 15 for the OASIS and between 5 and 12 for the ODSIS (Rojas et al. 2023). They are also in line with research using community samples, such as the study by Osma et al. (2021), which proposed a cutoff of 4 for the OASIS and of 5 for the ODSIS. As mentioned in several studies (Hermans et al. 2015; Norman et al. 2013; Osma et al. 2019), this variability in cutoffs may be due to differences in sample characteristics.

The significance of the present study lies in addressing the need for assessment tools specially validated for adolescents that not only evaluate symptoms of anxiety and depression but also capture the extent to which these symptoms interfere with daily functioning in different life domains. Assessing functional impairment is essential, as it helps identify adolescents who may present subclinical symptomatology but still experience meaningful disruptions in their lives (individuals who might otherwise go undetected in traditional symptom‐based screenings and who may nonetheless benefit from early intervention). Early detection through valid and reliable instruments is crucial, as it can contribute to the prevention of full‐blown psychological disorders, promote mental well‐being, and ultimately yield positive social and economic outcomes. This perspective is aligned with the recommendations of the US Preventive Services Task Force (Siu and U.S. Preventive Services Task Force 2016), which support the use of validated screening instruments in adolescents aged 12 years and older. The present study showed that 41.0% of participants reported having received therapy in the past. Although the specific reasons for seeking therapy were not assessed, this finding suggests that a substantial proportion of adolescents may have experienced clinically relevant symptoms that interfered with their functioning, prompting them to seek professional support at an early age. This finding underscores the potential utility of the OASIS‐A and ODSIS‐A in detecting impairing symptomatology even in younger populations, allowing for early identification and intervention that may help prevent the development of more severe symptoms. Moreover, the OASIS‐A and the ODSIS‐A may serve as valuable tools for evaluating the efficacy of prevention and intervention programs targeting anxiety and depression. This is particularly relevant in the context of transdiagnostic approaches that simultaneously address symptoms across multiple emotional disorders (such as anxiety and depression) within a unified treatment framework (García‐Escalera et al. 2016).

The present study is not without limitations. First, although the sample included adolescents from various geographical regions in Portugal, all participants were recruited exclusively from public schools. As such, the findings may not fully generalize to the broader Portuguese adolescent population, particularly those attending private or alternative educational settings. Second, the temporal stability of the scales was not assessed, and the measures used to establish the cutoff points and assess convergent validity were not the most conventional or widely used.

This is the first study to adapt and validate both the OASIS and the ODSIS for use with adolescents; thus, further research is needed to replicate and extend these findings. Future studies should include a clinical sample of adolescents diagnosed with emotional disorders, which would allow for the assessment of the scales' sensitivity to change (a key criterion for their use in treatment outcome research). Additionally, future studies should aim to include more diverse educational contexts to enhance the external validity of the findings and should also examine the test–retest reliability of both measures using appropriate time intervals. The validation of the OASIS and the ODSIS among adolescents supports their use as screening tools for assessing the severity and impairment of symptoms of anxiety and depression, highlighting their value for early mental health assessment in school settings; thus, helping improve the quality of intervention delivery.
