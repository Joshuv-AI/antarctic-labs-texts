Polysaccharide-engineered mitochondria reprogram macrophages to resolve diabetic wound inflammation and promote repair
Authors: Chenyan Yu, Qian Feng, Yuheng Liao, Meijun Tan, Yanzhi Zhao, Lang Chen, Wenqian Zhang, Chuanlu Lin, Ruiyin Zeng, Fawwaz Al-Smadi, Haochen Wang, Longyu Du, Xin Zhang, Ying Hu, Guodong Liu, Zhiyong Hou, Hang Xue, Guohui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic diabetic wounds are characterized by persistent inflammation, defective resolution and impaired tissue regeneration, in which macrophage dysfunction and mitochondrial damage play central roles. Here, we developed a macrophage-targeted engineered mitochondrial transplantation system by coating adipose-derived stem cell (ADSC) mitochondria with triphenylphosphonium-modified konjac glucomannan (Mito-TPP-KGM). This design preserves mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production while reducing ROS generation, and provides a mannose-rich corona for lectin receptor-related uptake. In RAW264.7 macrophages exposed to high glucose plus H2O2 or LPS, Mito-TPP-KGM is efficiently internalized, restores mitochondrial homeostasis, rebalances glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, and shifts inflammatory profiles toward a less inflammatory and more reparative phenotype. Engineered mitochondria also restore efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophil-like cells and enhance the pro-angiogenic capacity of macrophage-conditioned media, thereby improving endothelial tube formation, migration and proliferation. Blocking experiments with mannan and anti-CD206/anti-DC-SIGN antibodies, together with species-specific mtDNA quantification, indicate that mannose-type lectin receptors contribute to the uptake and immunomodulatory effects of Mito-TPP-KGM. In a db/db mouse full-thickness wound model, local delivery of Mito-TPP-KGM promotes wound repair, improves histological healing, reduces oxidative damage, enhances angiogenesis, and modulates wound macrophage phenotype, leading to accelerated wound closure; these therapeutic benefits are partially attenuated by local CD206 blockade. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that polysaccharide-engineered mitochondria can reprogram diabetic wound macrophages via targeted mitochondrial transplantation, offering a promising immunometabolic strategy for chronic wound therapy.Keywords: Mitochondrial transplantation, Engineered mitochondria, Macrophage polarization, Immunometabolism, Diabetic wound healing

Introduction

Chronic diabetic wounds, including diabetic foot ulcers, remain a major cause of morbidity, amputation and mortality worldwide despite advances in debridement, off-loading, dressings and adjunctive therapies [[1], [2], [3]]. A hallmark of these wounds is failure to transition from the initial inflammatory phase to the proliferative and remodeling phases, resulting in persistent inflammation, impaired angiogenesis and disorganized extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition [4,5]. Macrophages play a key role in this process: they typically transition from an initial pro-inflammatory state to a healing state. However, in diabetic wounds, they frequently remain in a prolonged inflammatory state with defective efferocytosis, which continues to cause tissue damage [[6], [7], [8]].

Growing evidence indicates that macrophage polarization is tightly coupled to immunometabolism [9]. Classically activated, M1-like macrophages are typically associated with enhanced glycolysis and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), whereas alternatively activated, M2-like macrophages are more closely associated with intact mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation [10,11]. In the diabetic microenvironment, high glucose and oxidative stress induce mitochondrial depolarization, impaired ATP production and excessive mitochondrial ROS in macrophages, which together sustain M1-biased transcriptional programs, suppress M2 transition and weaken efferocytosis and pro-angiogenic functions [[12], [13], [14]]. Restoring mitochondrial function in wound macrophages is therefore an attractive strategy to break the chronic inflammatory loop and re-establish a pro-healing milieu.

Over the past decade, mitochondrial transplantation has emerged as a promising therapeutic concept [[15], [16], [17]]. Exogenous mitochondria delivered by direct injection, stem cell-mediated transfer or mitochondria-containing extracellular vesicles have been shown to rescue bioenergetics, reduce ROS and improve functional outcomes in models of myocardial ischemia, stroke and lung injury [[18], [19], [20]]. These studies suggest that viable mitochondria can act as “metabolic therapeutics” to support stressed cells. However, current mitochondrial transplantation approaches face several limitations. Most efforts have focused on parenchymal cells such as cardiomyocytes or neurons, whereas the concept of macrophage-centered mitochondrial transplantation for immunomodulation and wound repair has not been systematically explored. Free mitochondria are unstable in vivo, prone to nonspecific uptake and rapid clearance, and their cell-type specificity is difficult to control. Moreover, receptor-level mechanisms governing mitochondrial uptake and downstream immunometabolic reprogramming remain poorly defined, and the potential of mitochondrial transplantation to resolve chronic inflammation in diabetic wounds is still insufficiently understood.

Mannose/polysaccharide ligands are widely used to bias uptake toward macrophages via lectin receptors such as CD206, but most platforms deliver signaling cargos such as small molecules or nucleic acids, rather than functional organelles. These considerations motivate a macrophage-directed mitochondrial transplantation strategy that can more directly support the immunometabolic capacity required for inflammation resolution programs, including efferocytosis.

Macrophages express multiple carbohydrate-recognizing lectin receptors, most notably the mannose receptor CD206 and, in human cells, DC-SIGN, which can recognize and internalize mannosylated polysaccharides and thereby provide an intrinsic handle for macrophage-biased delivery [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. Konjac glucomannan (KGM) is a neutral, biodegradable polysaccharide enriched in glucose and mannose residues and has been widely used as a hydrogel and drug carrier with good biocompatibility [27,28]. It is thus a plausible ligand for lectin receptor-related uptake. Triphenylphosphonium (TPP) is a classical lipophilic cation that accumulates on the negatively charged mitochondrial inner membrane and has been extensively exploited as a mitochondrial targeting moiety for small molecules and probes [29,30]. Conceptually, conjugating TPP to KGM could allow KGM to anchor on isolated mitochondria, forming a mannose-rich corona around exogenous mitochondria. Yet, to our knowledge, it remains unclear whether such a polysaccharide-engineered mitochondrial platform can effectively target wound macrophages and, more importantly, whether this form of macrophage-directed mitochondrial transplantation can reprogram diabetic wound macrophages.

In this study, we develop a macrophage-targeted engineered mitochondrial transplantation system, denoted Mito-TPP-KGM, by coating isolated adipose-derived stem cell (ADSC) mitochondria with TPP-modified konjac glucomannan (Scheme 1). We first characterize the physicochemical properties and bioenergetic stability of Mito-TPP-KGM and show that it preserves mitochondrial membrane potential while reducing ROS generation. We then demonstrate that Mito-TPP-KGM is efficiently internalized by RAW264.7 macrophages and THP-1-derived macrophages, modestly restores mitochondrial ATP production, rebalances glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, and reprograms LPS- or high-glucose/H2O2-challenged macrophages toward a less inflammatory and more reparative phenotype with enhanced efferocytosis and pro-angiogenic paracrine activity. Mechanistically, we provide evidence that mannose-type lectin receptors contribute to the uptake and immunomodulatory effects of engineered mitochondria, with CD206 and DC-SIGN serving as representative receptors in murine and human macrophages, respectively. Finally, in a db/db diabetic wound model, we show that local delivery of Mito-TPP-KGM promotes wound repair, attenuates oxidative damage, enhances angiogenesis, improves histological healing, and accelerates wound closure; these therapeutic benefits are partially attenuated by local CD206 blockade. Our work thus integrates polysaccharide receptor-mediated targeting with mitochondrial transplantation and macrophage immunometabolic reprogramming, offering a new strategy for treating chronic diabetic wounds.

Schematic illustration of the preparation of Mito-TPP-KGM and its proposed mechanism for diabetic wound repair. TPP-modified KGM chains were used to coat ADSC-derived mitochondria to construct engineered mitochondria (Mito-TPP-KGM). After local administration around diabetic wounds, Mito-TPP-KGM is preferentially internalized by macrophages through lectin receptor-mediated uptake. In inflammatory macrophages, engineered mitochondrial delivery alleviates ROS accumulation, restores mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), improves respiratory impairment, and rebalances cellular metabolism. These changes promote macrophage transition from an inflammatory phenotype toward a reparative phenotype, enhance efferocytosis, and ultimately facilitate inflammation resolution, angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, and diabetic wound healing.

Materials and methods

Materials

(5-Carboxypentyl) triphenylphosphonium bromide (TPP-COOH), 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl), and fluorescein 5(6)-isothiocyanate (FITC) were purchased from Adamas (Shanghai, China). 4-Dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) was obtained from Heowns (Tianjin, China). Anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and deuterium oxide (D2O) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Konjac glucomannan (KGM) was purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Antibodies against CD86, CD206, iNOS, CD163, CD31, TOM20 and 8-OHdG, as well as isotype controls and secondary antibodies, were obtained from commercial suppliers (details and catalog numbers are listed in Table S1). JC-1, DCFH-DA and MitoSOX™ Red probes, EdU assay kits, ATP determination kits, mitochondrial isolation kits, and ELISA kits for TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-10 were purchased from standard suppliers (Table S1, Table S2). All other reagents were of analytical grade and used as received.

RAW264.7, THP-1, HUVEC and HL-60 cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). db/db (BKS-Lepr^db/db) and age-matched non-diabetic control mice (M/M) were purchased from an accredited animal facility.

Synthesis and characterization of TPP-KGM

Preparation of TPP-KGM conjugates

TPP-KGM was synthesized by EDC/DMAP-mediated coupling of TPP-COOH to KGM, as previously reported with minor modifications. Briefly, TPP-COOH, EDC·HCl and DMAP were dissolved in anhydrous DMSO and stirred for 5 min to activate the carboxyl groups. The activated solution was then added dropwise to an aqueous KGM solution under continuous stirring and reacted overnight at room temperature. The reaction mixture was transferred into dialysis tubing (MWCO 3.5-7 kDa) and dialyzed against deionized (DI) water for 3 days, with frequent water changes, to remove unreacted small molecules and DMSO. The dialysate was then frozen at −80 °C and lyophilized to obtain TPP-KGM powder, which was stored at −20 °C until use.

FITC labeling of TPP-KGM

To enable fluorescence tracking, TPP-KGM was conjugated with FITC. Briefly, TPP-KGM was dissolved in DI water and mixed with FITC dissolved in DMSO at a molar ratio optimized in preliminary experiments. The reaction was allowed to proceed overnight in the dark at room temperature. The mixture was dialyzed against DI water to remove unbound FITC, and the FITC-TPP-KGM conjugate was obtained by lyophilization.

1H NMR and zeta potential

The successful conjugation of TPP to KGM was confirmed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR). KGM, TPP-COOH and TPP-KGM were dissolved in D2O and analyzed on a 400 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker AVANCE NEO 400, Bruker, Germany). Characteristic aromatic proton peaks of TPP and backbone peaks of KGM were compared to confirm the presence of TPP moieties on the KGM chains (Fig. S1a).

The surface charge of KGM and TPP-KGM was evaluated by measuring their zeta potential using a dynamic light scattering instrument equipped with a zeta potential module (e.g., Malvern Zetasizer Nano). Samples were dispersed in DI water at room temperature, and zeta potential values were recorded as the mean of at least three measurements (Fig. S1b).

Isolation of ADSC-derived mitochondria

Human and mouse adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) were cultured in standard growth medium (F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. When cells reached 80-90% confluence, mitochondria were isolated using a commercial mitochondrial isolation kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Briefly, ADSCs were washed with ice-cold PBS, harvested by trypsinization, and pelleted by centrifugation. The cell pellets were resuspended in ice-cold mitochondrial isolation buffer and homogenized on ice with a glass/Teflon homogenizer until >80% cells were disrupted. The homogenate was subjected to differential centrifugation to remove nuclei and cell debris, and the supernatant was further centrifuged at high speed to pellet mitochondria. The mitochondrial pellet was washed and resuspended in ice-cold storage buffer. Protein concentration was determined by a BCA assay, and mitochondrial suspensions were kept on ice and used within the same day.

For some experiments, mitochondria were pre-stained with MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos according to the manufacturer's protocol prior to engineering or confocal imaging.

Preparation of engineered mitochondria (Mito-TPP-KGM)

To prepare engineered mitochondria, freshly isolated ADSC mitochondria were incubated with TPP-KGM; for fluorescence-based coating analysis, FITC-TPP-KGM was used in parallel, at various concentrations (0.1, 0.2, 0.5 and 1.0 mg/mL) under gentle shaking at room temperature for 30-60 min. Unbound TPP-KGM was removed by centrifugation and washing with PBS. The resulting Mito-TPP-KGM complexes were resuspended in appropriate buffer for subsequent experiments.

For colocalization analysis, Mito-TPP-KGM containing MitoTracker™ Red-labeled mitochondria was placed on glass-bottom dishes and imaged by confocal laser scanning microscopy. The overlap between red (mitochondria) and green (FITC-TPP-KGM) signals was quantified using ImageJ to calculate mitochondrial encapsulation efficiency (%).

pH-dependent functional stability of Mito-TPP-KGM

To evaluate the functional stability of engineered mitochondria under different pH conditions, Mito-TPP-KGM was incubated in buffer at pH 7.4 or pH 6.0 for 4 h. After incubation, mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP level, and ROS level were assessed using JC-1 staining, ATP assay, and ROS assay, respectively, according to the corresponding protocols described above.

In vitro degradability of TPP-KGM in simulated wound fluid

To assess the degradability of TPP-KGM, the material was prepared at 10 × working concentration and incubated in simulated wound fluid at 37 °C. At the indicated time points, samples were collected, lyophilized, and weighed to determine the remaining dry mass. The degradation profile was expressed as the percentage of residual dry weight over time.

Cell culture and treatments

RAW264.7 macrophages and HUVECs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. THP-1 monocytes and HL-60 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

For macrophage experiments, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in plates or on glass coverslips and allowed to adhere overnight. THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophage-like cells with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 100 nM) for 24 h, followed by washing and resting in fresh PMA-free medium for an additional 24 h before subsequent experiments.

To mimic diabetic oxidative stress, a combined high-glucose and H2O2 model (H2O2(HG)) was used. Cells were cultured in high-glucose medium (33 mM glucose) and treated with H2O2 (100 μM) for 24 h. For inflammatory activation, cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) with or without ATP (5 mM) for IL-1β secretion assays.

The following treatment groups were used unless otherwise indicated: Control (PBS or vehicle); H2O2(HG) alone; H2O2(HG) + Mito (free mitochondria); H2O2(HG) + TPP-KGM; H2O2(HG) + Mito-TPP-KGM. For polarization and cytokine assays: Control; LPS; LPS + TPP-KGM; LPS + Mito; LPS + Mito-TPP-KGM. The doses of Mito, TPP-KGM and Mito-TPP-KGM were selected based on preliminary biocompatibility and efficacy screening (100 μg mitochondrial protein/mL and 0.5 mg/mL TPP-KGM).

Assessment of mitochondrial function and ROS in macrophages

JC-1 staining

Mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) was evaluated using the JC-1 probe. After treatments, cells grown on coverslips were incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20-30 min, washed with buffer, and imaged immediately by confocal microscopy. Red (J-aggregates) and green (monomers) fluorescence were recorded, and the red/green ratio was quantified using ImageJ. For quantitative analysis, JC-1 staining was also performed in 96-well plates and read by a microplate reader.

Total and mitochondrial ROS

Total intracellular ROS were measured using DCFH-DA. Cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μM) at 37 °C for 30 min, washed, and either imaged by fluorescence microscopy or analyzed in a plate reader to obtain mean fluorescence intensity.

Mitochondrial ROS were quantified with MitoSOX™ Red by flow cytometry. After exposure to H2O2(HG) and/or mitochondrial treatments, cells were incubated with MitoSOX™ Red (5 μM) for 10-15 min at 37 °C, washed, and analyzed using a flow cytometer. The percentage of MitoSOX-positive cells and mean fluorescence intensity were recorded.

ATP assay

Cellular and tissue ATP levels were determined using a luciferase-based ATP assay kit. Cells were lysed in ATP lysis buffer, and supernatants were collected after centrifugation. ATP content was quantified according to the kit protocol using a luminometer and normalized to total protein content.

Seahorse analysis of mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis

Mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic function were assessed using a Seahorse XFe96 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies). RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded into Seahorse XF cell culture microplates at a density of 2 × 10^4 cells/well and cultured overnight to allow complete attachment. On the day of assay, culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF assay medium and cells were equilibrated at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator for 45-60 min before measurement.

For mitochondrial respiration analysis, OCR was measured using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test according to the manufacturer's instructions. OCR was recorded under basal conditions and after sequential injection of oligomycin (1 μM), FCCP (1.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM each). Basal respiration and maximal respiration were calculated using Seahorse Wave software. Basal respiration was defined as the OCR before oligomycin injection after correction for non-mitochondrial respiration, and maximal respiration was defined as the FCCP-stimulated OCR after subtraction of non-mitochondrial respiration.

For glycolytic analysis, ECAR was measured using the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test according to the manufacturer's instructions. ECAR was recorded under basal conditions and after sequential addition of glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 μM), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG; 50 mM). Glycolysis and glycolytic capacity were calculated using Seahorse Wave software.

Unless otherwise specified, OCR and ECAR values were normalized to cell number and are presented as OCR (pmol/min) and ECAR (mpH/min), respectively.

Macrophage polarization: flow cytometry, qPCR and ELISA

For flow cytometric analysis of surface markers, treated macrophages were harvested, blocked with Fc receptor blocker, and stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD86 and CD206 for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After washing, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry, and the percentages of CD86+ and CD206+ cells were quantified.

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol or a similar reagent, and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green master mix on a real-time PCR system. Gene expression of Nos2, Tnf, Il1b, Il6, Arg1, Il10, Tomm20, Tfam, Pink1, Dnm1l (Drp1), Mertk, Cd36 and Mrc1 (Cd206) were normalized to β-actin and analyzed by the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

For cytokine quantification, cell culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged to remove debris, and subjected to ELISA for TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-1β according to the manufacturers’ protocols. For IL-1β measurement, cells were primed with LPS and briefly stimulated with ATP prior to collection to promote inflammasome activation.

Efferocytosis assay

Efferocytosis was evaluated using apoptotic HL-60 cells as targets. HL-60 cells were induced to undergo apoptosis by UV irradiation and incubated for an additional period to allow apoptosis development before use, stained with the lipophilic dye Dil, and washed thoroughly. RAW264.7 cells were labeled with FITC-phalloidin to visualize the actin cytoskeleton.

Dil-labeled apoptotic HL-60 cells were co-incubated with treated RAW264.7 macrophages at a defined ratio (4:1) for 1-2 h at 37 °C. After washing away non-engulfed HL-60 cells, samples were fixed and imaged by confocal microscopy. The number of Dil+ HL-60 cells engulfed per macrophage or the percentage of Dil+ area per field was quantified using ImageJ.

Receptor blocking and cross-species mtDNA qPCR

To investigate receptor involvement, RAW264.7 and THP-1-derived macrophages were pretreated with soluble mannan or β-glucan (laminarin) for 30-60 min to competitively block lectin receptors, or incubated with blocking antibodies against CD206 and/or DC-SIGN (THP-1 only) at appropriate concentrations. Cells were then exposed to Mito-TPP-KGM prepared from human ADSC mitochondria (for RAW264.7) or mouse ADSC mitochondria (for THP-1) for 4-6 h. After extensive washing to remove extracellular mitochondria, total DNA was extracted, and donor mitochondrial uptake was quantified by cross-species mtDNA qPCR using species-specific primers (Table S3). Specifically, human MT-ND1 was amplified in RAW264.7 cells to selectively quantify donor human mtDNA and normalized to murine nuclear genomic DNA (mouse Gapdh). Conversely, mouse mt-Nd1 was amplified in THP-1-derived macrophages to selectively quantify donor mouse mtDNA and normalized to human nuclear genomic DNA (human GAPDH). Relative donor mtDNA abundance was calculated using the ΔCt method (ΔCt = Ct donor mtDNA − Ct nuclear) and presented as 2^−ΔCt (equivalently, 2^(Ct nuclear − Ct donor mtDNA)) to reflect relative mtDNA copy number per cell.

For functional readouts, macrophage polarization-related gene expression and cytokine production were assessed as described. For RAW264.7 cells, Nos2, Arg1, Tnf, Il6, Il1b, and Il10 were quantified; for THP-1-derived macrophages, the corresponding human genes NOS2, ARG1, TNF, IL6, IL1B, and IL10 were quantified.

Endothelial tube formation, migration, proliferation and transwell assays

To assess the paracrine effects of treated macrophages on endothelial cells, conditioned media (CM) were collected from different groups of RAW264.7 cells. After treatment under H2O2(HG) or LPS conditions with or without Mito-TPP-KGM, culture supernatants were harvested, centrifuged and mixed with fresh endothelial growth medium at a defined ratio (1:1) to prepare CM.

For tube formation, HUVECs were seeded on Matrigel-coated 96-well plates in CM and incubated for 4-8 h. Tube-like structures were imaged under a phase-contrast microscope, and total tube length and node number were quantified using ImageJ (Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin).

For scratch wound assays, confluent HUVEC monolayers in CM were scratched with a pipette tip, washed, and imaged at 0 and 24 h. Wound closure area (%) was quantified.

For EdU incorporation, HUVECs were cultured in CM for a defined period, incubated with EdU according to the kit instructions, and stained. EdU+ nuclei were counted and expressed as a percentage of total nuclei.

For transwell migration, HUVECs were seeded in the upper chamber of transwell inserts and allowed to migrate toward CM in the lower chamber for 12-24 h. Migrated cells on the underside of the membrane were fixed, stained (crystal violet) and counted in several random fields.

Live/dead staining for cytocompatibility evaluation

To evaluate cytocompatibility, HUVECs and RAW264.7 macrophages were incubated with different concentrations of TPP-KGM (0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL). After the indicated incubation period, cells were stained using a live/dead viability kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence images were acquired under a fluorescence microscope, and the percentage of viable cells was quantified.

Animal experiments and wound model

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and performed in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines. Male db/db mice (8-10 weeks old) and age-matched non-diabetic M/M mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water.

After anesthesia, the dorsal hair was removed and the skin was disinfected. A 6-mm full-thickness excisional wound was created on the dorsum of each mouse using a sterile biopsy punch. Wounds were left uncovered. db/db mice were randomly assigned to the following groups: Control, Mito, Mito-TPP-KGM, Mito-TPP-KGM + isotype IgG, and Mito-TPP-KGM + anti-CD206. Non-diabetic M/M mice served as a physiological reference group.

Treatments were administered by six-point periwound intradermal injection around the wound margin immediately after wounding (day 0) and then every 3 days (days 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12). The total injection volume was 50 μL per wound (approximately 8 μL per site). For CD206 blockade, anti-CD206 antibody was locally administered at 10 μg per wound per injection, and an isotype-matched IgG was used at the same dose in the control blockade group. The mitochondrial dose in the Mito and Mito-TPP-KGM groups was adjusted to the same mitochondrial protein amount per wound.

Wound photographs were taken on days 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 with a scale reference. Wound area was quantified using ImageJ, and wound closure (%) was calculated as (A0 − At)/A0 × 100, where A0 is the initial wound area and At is the wound area at the indicated time point.

At selected time points, mice were euthanized and wound tissues together with a margin of surrounding skin were harvested for histological, immunohistochemical, immunofluorescence, ATP, and flow cytometric analyses. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were also collected for biosafety evaluation.

In vivo fluorescence tracing of DiR-labeled TPP-KGM

For in vivo tracing, TPP-KGM was labeled with DiR and locally injected around the wound. Free DiR was used as a control. Fluorescence images were acquired on days 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 using an in vivo imaging system. Fluorescence intensity at the wound site was quantified and normalized to the initial signal to evaluate local retention and clearance kinetics.

Histology and quantitative morphometry

Wound tissues and organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4-6 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using standard protocols to assess general morphology and re-epithelialization. Masson's trichrome staining was performed to visualize collagen deposition and organization.

For quantitative analysis, wound sections were imaged under a light microscope. Wound fill (%) and neo-epidermal thickness at day 7 were measured from H&E sections. Collagen-positive area (%) was quantified from Masson-stained sections at day 7. At day 14, neo-epidermal thickness and a Masson-based scar index (ratio of scar collagen area to entire dermal area, combined with fiber orientation scores) were calculated. Detailed definitions of morphometric parameters are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded wound tissues were sectioned at 4-6 μm, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2, and non-specific binding was blocked with serum.

For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD31 and 8-OHdG, followed by appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were visualized using DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. CD31-positive vessels per field and the percentage of 8-OHdG-positive nuclei were quantified using ImageJ.

For immunofluorescence, wound-edge tissues collected on days 1 and 3 were stained with antibodies against F4/80 and CD206 to evaluate the presence of CD206-positive macrophages during the early stage of wound healing. In separate day-3 wound sections, antibodies against iNOS and CD163 were used to assess wound macrophage phenotype. After incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and images were acquired under a fluorescence microscope. The proportion of F4/80+CD206+ cells among F4/80+ macrophages and the ratio of iNOS-positive to CD163-positive macrophages were quantified from multiple random fields per section.

Flow cytometric analysis of in vivo targeting

To evaluate in vivo targeting of Mito-TPP-KGM to wound macrophages, db/db mice received local injections of FITC-labeled Mito-TPP-KGM at day 3 post-wounding. Four hours later, wound tissues were harvested, minced, and enzymatically digested in collagenase-containing buffer supplemented with DNase I at 37 °C for 30-45 min to obtain single-cell suspensions. The digested samples were passed through a 70 μm cell strainer, washed, and processed for flow cytometry.

Cells were stained with antibodies against CD45, CD64, and Ly6G. After washing, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry. Gating was sequentially performed on live singlets, CD45+ leukocytes, and FITC+ cells. Within the FITC+ population, CD64+Ly6G− cells were defined as macrophages and Ly6G+ cells were defined as neutrophils; the remaining FITC+ leukocytes were classified as other cells. The distribution of FITC signal among wound leukocyte subsets was quantified to evaluate macrophage-biased uptake in vivo.

Serum biochemistry and histological biosafety evaluation

To assess systemic biosafety after local treatment, blood samples were collected from mice at day 14, and serum was isolated for biochemical and inflammatory analyses. Serum levels of ALT, AST, BUN, creatinine, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were measured according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E for histological examination.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests was applied. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact n values and statistical tests used for each experiment are indicated in the figure legends. ns, not significant, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results

Construction and basic characterization of Mito-TPP-KGM

To endow free mitochondria with a polymer corona that can later mediate macrophage targeting, we first synthesized a TPP-modified konjac glucomannan (TPP-KGM) and used it to coat isolated mitochondria (Fig. 1). Triphenylphosphonium (TPP) is a classical lipophilic cation that accumulates at the negatively charged mitochondrial membrane and is widely used as a tethering group for mitochondrial drug delivery systems [31]. Konjac glucomannan (KGM) is a neutral, biocompatible polysaccharide that has been broadly explored as a hydrogel or carrier matrix for biomedical applications [32]. In our design, TPP-COOH was conjugated to the KGM backbone to generate a water-soluble polymer bearing multiple TPP pendants, which can adsorb onto the surface of isolated mitochondria through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, forming so-called Mito-TPP-KGM.

The TPP-KGM synthetic route is outlined in Fig. 1a, and successful conjugation was confirmed by ^1H NMR (Fig. S1a). Compared with native KGM and TPP-COOH, the spectrum of TPP-KGM shows the characteristic aromatic proton signals of the TPP phenyl rings in the 7-8 ppm region, superimposed on the carbohydrate proton envelope between 3 and 5 ppm, together with new linker-related peaks, indicating that TPP has been grafted onto the KGM backbone. Based on the theoretical structure of TPP-KGM, the theoretical proton integral ratio of site a to site b should be 1:15 when all available substitution sites are fully grafted with TPP. However, ^1H NMR analysis showed that the actual integral ratio of a to b in TPP-KGM was 1:3 (Fig. 1a–S1a). Comparison between the theoretical and experimental ratios suggested that the actual grafting efficiency of TPP onto KGM was approximately 20%. This result confirmed the successful conjugation of TPP to the KGM backbone and indicated a relatively stable substitution level, providing a structural basis for the subsequent fabrication of engineered mitochondria. Transmission electron microscopy showed that the isolated mitochondrial preparation exhibited intact particulate morphology, and after incubation with TPP-KGM, the engineered mitochondria displayed an altered peripheral appearance with a surrounding low-density layer compared with naked mitochondria (Fig. 1b), consistent with polymer coating on the mitochondrial surface. A schematic illustration of the preparation process for Mito-TPP-KGM is shown in Fig. 1c. In addition, FT-IR spectra showed that KGM exhibited the characteristic broad O-H stretching band around 3400 cm−1, the C-H stretching peak around 2920 cm−1, and the polysaccharide-related absorption band near 1035 cm−1, whereas TPP displayed a characteristic carbonyl absorption peak at 1714.7 cm−1. Compared with KGM, TPP-KGM retained the characteristic peaks of the polysaccharide backbone and showed a new absorption peak at 1723.5 cm−1, indicating the successful conjugation of TPP to KGM (Fig. S2a). High-resolution XPS further supported the association between mitochondria and TPP-KGM. Compared with naked mitochondria, Mito-TPP-KGM showed a markedly attenuated N 1s signal, together with the presence of a P 2p signal derived from TPP-KGM, supporting the successful assembly of TPP-KGM on the mitochondrial surface (Fig. S2b). We next assessed the interaction between FITC-labeled TPP-KGM and isolated mitochondria. Confocal imaging showed progressively increased overlap between the green FITC signal and the red mitochondrial signal with increasing FITC-TPP-KGM concentrations (0.1-1.0 mg/mL), indicating enhanced association of TPP-KGM with mitochondria (Fig. 1d). Quantitative analysis revealed that the encapsulation efficiency increased from 0.1 to 0.5 mg/mL and then reached a plateau, with no significant difference between 0.5 and 1.0 mg/mL (Fig. 1e). Therefore, 0.5 mg/mL was selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments. Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed the successful coating of mitochondria by TPP-KGM: compared with naked mitochondria, Mito-TPP-KGM exhibited a markedly increased FITC-positive fraction, reaching 85.2% on average at the working concentration of 0.5 mg/mL (Fig. S3).

We then evaluated whether TPP-KGM coating affected mitochondrial functional integrity over time. JC-1 staining showed that both naked mitochondria and Mito-TPP-KGM exhibited predominantly red fluorescence at 0 h, whereas with prolonged incubation the uncoated mitochondria displayed a progressive increase in green fluorescence and decline in red fluorescence. In contrast, Mito-TPP-KGM maintained stronger red JC-1 aggregate signals and weaker green monomer signals at 4 and 8 h (Fig. 1f), indicating better preservation of mitochondrial membrane potential. Consistently, time-course analysis demonstrated that Mito-TPP-KGM retained higher ATP levels (Fig. 1g) and generated less ROS (Fig. 1h) than naked mitochondria during 0-8 h incubation, suggesting that TPP-KGM coating improved mitochondrial stability and alleviated functional deterioration under cell-free conditions.

Finally, we assessed the cytocompatibility of the TPP-KGM polymer itself. Live/dead staining of HUVECs and RAW264.7 macrophages treated with a series of TPP-KGM concentrations (0-1.0 mg/mL) for 24 h (Fig. S15a and b) showed that cell viability remained high across all groups. Quantitative comparison with the 0 mg/mL group revealed only a slight, statistically significant decrease in HUVEC viability at 1.0 mg/mL, while all other concentrations in both HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells were not significantly different from control. These data indicate that TPP-KGM is well tolerated by endothelial cells and macrophages at ≤0.5 mg/mL, the concentration used to construct Mito-TPP-KGM.

Collectively, Fig. 1, Appendix A, Appendix A demonstrate that we successfully synthesized TPP-KGM, efficiently coated isolated mitochondria to form Mito-TPP-KGM without disrupting their membrane potential, better preserved ATP levels and attenuated ROS accumulation in vitro, and that the carrier polymer exhibits acceptable cytocompatibility at the working dose. This provides a robust physicochemical basis for using Mito-TPP-KGM in subsequent mechanistic and in vivo studies.

Time-dependent macrophage uptake, intracellular fate, and exploratory mechanistic profiling of engineered mitochondria

To determine whether engineered mitochondria (Mito-TPP-KGM) could be efficiently internalized by macrophages, we first monitored their cellular uptake over time using confocal microscopy. As shown in Fig. 2a, Mito-TPP-KGM-derived red fluorescence was already detectable around RAW264.7 cells at 10 min, became more evident at 30 min, and markedly accumulated by 1 h, indicating rapid and time-dependent cellular association and internalization.

We next quantified this uptake process by flow cytometry. Representative PE fluorescence histograms (Fig. 2b) showed that both free mitochondria and Mito-TPP-KGM could be taken up by RAW264.7 cells, but Mito-TPP-KGM induced a stronger rightward fluorescence shift at the early time point. Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that the median PE fluorescence intensity in the Mito-TPP-KGM group was significantly higher than that in the free mitochondria group at 1 h, and remained higher at 4 h (Fig. 2c). Notably, the uptake signal of Mito-TPP-KGM at 1 h had already reached a level comparable to or approaching that of later time points, suggesting that TPP-KGM coating accelerated macrophage recognition and early internalization of exogenous mitochondria.

To further explore the intracellular fate of internalized engineered mitochondria, we assessed their colocalization with endogenous mitochondria and lysosomes at different time points. Confocal imaging showed partial overlap between Mito-TPP-KGM and endogenous mitochondria at 1 h, which gradually decreased from 4 to 24 h (Fig. 2d). Quantification confirmed that the colocalization ratio with endogenous mitochondria was highest at the early stage and then progressively declined over time (Fig. 2e). In parallel, Mito-TPP-KGM also exhibited limited colocalization with lysosomes after uptake, with a modest overlap observed at early time points but no progressive accumulation within lysosomal compartments over time (Fig. 2f and g). These findings suggest that internalized engineered mitochondria were not subjected to rapid and complete lysosomal sequestration, but instead displayed a dynamic intracellular trafficking pattern after macrophage uptake.

After confirming cellular entry and preliminary intracellular trafficking, we performed an exploratory qPCR panel to profile genes related to mitochondrial homeostasis, inflammatory polarization, and phagocytic receptors (Fig. 2h). Under H2O2(HG), mitochondrial homeostasis-associated genes including Tomm20, Tfam, Pink1, and Dnm1l were generally suppressed, whereas M1-associated inflammatory genes Nos2, Tnf, Il1b, and Il6 were markedly elevated. In contrast, M2-associated genes such as Arg1 and Il10, as well as phagocytic/efferocytic receptor genes including Mertk, Cd36, and Mrc1, showed an overall downward trend. Treatment with free mitochondria only partially alleviated these changes, while TPP-KGM alone exerted limited effects. By comparison, Mito-TPP-KGM more effectively restored the expression of mitochondrial homeostasis-related genes, reduced pro-inflammatory gene expression, and increased the expression of reparative and phagocytic receptor genes, yielding a transcriptional profile shifted toward a mitochondria-recovered, inflammation-resolving, and clearance-supportive state.

In addition, supplementary dose-response experiments showed that Mito-TPP-KGM improved intracellular ATP levels and reduced ROS accumulation in H2O2(HG)-injured macrophages in a concentration-dependent manner, with the protective effect tending to plateau at medium-to-high doses (Fig. S4). Together, these results indicate that Mito-TPP-KGM can be rapidly internalized by macrophages, exhibits dynamic intracellular trafficking after uptake, and initiates a coordinated transcriptional and metabolic response associated with mitochondrial recovery and macrophage functional reprogramming.

Metabolic rescue of oxidatively stressed macrophages by Mito-TPP-KGM

Under high-glucose oxidative stress, RAW264.7 macrophages exhibited evident mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic disturbance, whereas Mito-TPP-KGM treatment partially restored mitochondrial homeostasis and bioenergetic activity.

JC-1 staining first showed that control cells predominantly displayed red J-aggregates, indicating a preserved mitochondrial membrane potential, while H2O2(HG) stimulation markedly increased green monomers and reduced red fluorescence, consistent with severe mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 3a). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant decrease in the JC-1 red/green ratio in the H2O2(HG) group compared with the control. Both free mitochondria and Mito-TPP-KGM increased the JC-1 red/green ratio relative to the H2O2 (HG) group, whereas TPP-KGM alone showed no obvious restorative effect (Fig. 3b). Notably, the recovery was more pronounced in the Mito-TPP-KGM group.

Consistent with the change in mitochondrial membrane potential, cellular ATP production was markedly impaired after H2O2 (HG) treatment. Supplementation with free mitochondria partially restored intracellular ATP levels, while Mito-TPP-KGM achieved a stronger recovery; TPP-KGM alone again produced only limited improvement (Fig. 3c). These findings indicate that engineered mitochondria more effectively rescued mitochondrial energy production under oxidative stress conditions.

We next assessed intracellular oxidative stress. Quantification of DCFH-DA fluorescence showed that H2O2 (HG) markedly elevated total intracellular ROS levels, whereas both free mitochondria and Mito-TPP-KGM significantly reduced ROS accumulation (Fig. 3d; representative images shown in Fig. S5). In parallel, MitoSOX flow cytometry demonstrated that mitochondrial ROS was strongly increased in the H2O2 (HG) group, but was markedly suppressed after treatment with Mito-TPP-KGM; free mitochondria also reduced mitochondrial ROS, although to a lesser extent, while TPP-KGM alone had only a modest effect (Fig. 3e and f). Together, these results suggest that Mito-TPP-KGM alleviates oxidative stress at both the global and mitochondrial levels.

To further determine whether mitochondrial respiration was functionally restored, Seahorse analysis was performed. The OCR profile showed that H2O2(HG) substantially impaired mitochondrial respiration, as evidenced by decreased basal and maximal respiration compared with the control group (Fig. 3g–i). Treatment with free mitochondria partially improved these parameters, whereas Mito-TPP-KGM produced a more substantial restoration. In contrast, TPP-KGM alone had only minor effects.

ECAR analysis further revealed that H2O2 (HG) drove macrophages toward a more glycolytic state, with increased glycolysis and glycolytic capacity (Fig. 3j–l). Free mitochondria partially attenuated this metabolic shift, while Mito-TPP-KGM more effectively normalized both glycolysis and glycolytic capacity. These data indicate that engineered mitochondrial delivery not only rescues oxidative phosphorylation but also alleviates the compensatory glycolytic bias induced by oxidative stress.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Mito-TPP-KGM effectively mitigates mitochondrial depolarization and ROS accumulation, restores ATP production and respiratory capacity, and rebalances mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis in oxidatively stressed macrophages, showing an overall stronger metabolic rescue effect than non-engineered mitochondria.

Mito-TPP-KGM reprograms LPS-activated macrophages toward a reparative phenotype

Building on the above finding that Mito-TPP-KGM restores mitochondrial homeostasis and rebalances glycolysis/OXPHOS in RAW264.7 macrophages, we next investigated whether this metabolic rescue was accompanied by changes in macrophage inflammatory phenotype. Because macrophage polarization is closely coupled to cellular metabolic state, we examined surface markers, phenotype-related genes, and cytokine secretion profiles in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells.

Flow-cytometric analysis revealed a clear phenotypic shift after Mito-TPP-KGM treatment (Fig. 4a). LPS stimulation markedly increased the proportion of CD86-positive cells compared with the control group, indicating robust pro-inflammatory activation. Both free mitochondria and TPP-KGM alone partially reduced CD86 expression, whereas Mito-TPP-KGM produced the most pronounced decrease among all LPS-treated groups. In contrast, CD206 expression showed a different pattern: compared with the control group, the LPS group did not exhibit an obvious reduction, while treatment with free mitochondria or TPP-KGM alone led to relatively lower CD206 positivity. Notably, Mito-TPP-KGM significantly increased the proportion of CD206-positive cells and showed the highest CD206 level among all treatment groups. These findings suggest that engineered mitochondrial delivery not only suppresses LPS-induced pro-inflammatory activation but also favors acquisition of a more reparative surface phenotype.

We next quantified representative phenotype-related genes by qPCR (Fig. 4b–g). LPS strongly upregulated the pro-inflammatory genes Nos2, Tnf, Il1b, and Il6 compared with the control group. Treatment with TPP-KGM or free mitochondria partially attenuated the expression of these genes, whereas Mito-TPP-KGM reduced them more markedly, bringing their levels closer to baseline. In parallel, the reparative markers Arg1 and Il10 were increased after Mito-TPP-KGM treatment and reached levels higher than those in the LPS, LPS + TPP-KGM, and LPS + Mito groups. Together, these transcriptional results support a shift away from an LPS-driven pro-inflammatory program toward a reparative phenotype following treatment with engineered mitochondria.

Finally, we examined secreted cytokines at the protein level by ELISA (Fig. 4h–k). To more closely mimic inflammasome activation, IL-1β release was measured in macrophages primed with LPS and then pulsed with ATP. Compared with PBS, LPS(ATP) dramatically increased IL-1β secretion, whereas addition of free mitochondria, TPP-KGM or Mito-TPP-KGM all significantly reduced IL-1β levels, with Mito-TPP-KGM showing the strongest inhibition (Fig. 4h). In parallel, TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-10 were quantified in cells stimulated with LPS alone (Fig. 4i–k). LPS robustly elevated TNF-α and IL-6 relative to PBS, while free mitochondria or TPP-KGM partially blunted this increase and Mito-TPP-KGM reduced their levels most markedly (Fig. 4i and j). By contrast, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was only modestly affected by LPS, but was significantly upregulated by Mito-TPP-KGM compared with both the LPS group and the free-mitochondria/TPP-KGM groups (Fig. 4k).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that Mito-TPP-KGM effectively suppresses LPS-induced inflammatory activation and promotes a reparative macrophage program, as evidenced by reduced CD86, Nos2, Tnf, Il1b, Il6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, together with increased CD206, Arg1, and IL-10. This phenotypic reprogramming is consistent with the metabolic rescue shown in Fig. 3 and provides a functional basis for the pro-healing effects of engineered mitochondria in diabetic wound repair.

Mito-TPP-KGM-reprogrammed macrophages enhance efferocytosis and paracrine pro-angiogenic activity

We next investigated whether metabolic and phenotypic reprogramming of macrophages by Mito-TPP-KGM translated into improved efferocytosis and pro-angiogenic paracrine activity.

Under high-glucose oxidative stress, RAW264.7 macrophages exhibited a marked defect in efferocytosis of apoptotic HL-60 cells. Compared with the control group, the H2O2(HG) group showed fewer merged signals between apoptotic HL-60 cells and RAW264.7 macrophages and a significantly reduced macrophage efferocytosis index, indicating impaired engulfment capacity (Fig. 5a and b). Treatment with free mitochondria partially restored efferocytosis, whereas TPP-KGM alone showed only a limited effect. Notably, Mito-TPP-KGM treatment most effectively enhanced apoptotic cell clearance and significantly increased the macrophage efferocytosis index compared with the H2O2(HG) group, suggesting that engineered mitochondrial delivery efficiently rescues macrophage efferocytic function under high-glucose oxidative stress.

Consistent with these findings, scratch-wound assays showed that H2O2(HG)-CM significantly delayed endothelial wound closure, whereas conditioned media from free mitochondria-, TPP-KGM-, and especially Mito-TPP-KGM-treated macrophages accelerated HUVEC migration into the wound area (Fig. 6d and e). EdU staining further demonstrated that H2O2(HG)-CM suppressed HUVEC proliferation, while conditioned media derived from mitochondrial intervention groups restored the percentage of EdU-positive cells, with the strongest proliferative effect observed in the Mito-TPP-KGM-CM group (Fig. 6f and g). Similarly, transwell assays showed that endothelial migration was impaired by H2O2(HG)-CM but was significantly improved after exposure to conditioned media from Mito-TPP-KGM-treated macrophages (Fig. 6h and i).

To further test whether this pro-efferocytic and pro-angiogenic effect could also be observed under inflammatory stress, we performed parallel validation experiments in an LPS model. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S6, LPS stimulation likewise impaired macrophage efferocytosis, whereas Mito-TPP-KGM treatment significantly restored apoptotic HL-60 engulfment. In addition, conditioned media from LPS-stimulated macrophages suppressed HUVEC tube formation, wound closure, proliferation, and migration, while these endothelial impairments were substantially reversed by conditioned media from Mito-TPP-KGM-treated macrophages (Fig. S7).

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Mito-TPP-KGM not only restores macrophage efferocytosis under stress conditions but also reprograms the macrophage secretome to support endothelial angiogenic activities, including tube formation, proliferation, and migration, thereby linking macrophage metabolic rescue to a pro-repair microenvironment.

Mito-TPP-KGM reprograms macrophages via mannose-type lectin receptors in both murine and human cells

Given that KGM is a glucomannan enriched in mannose residues, we hypothesized that Mito-TPP-KGM may be preferentially internalized by macrophages through mannose-type lectin receptors, thereby facilitating mitochondrial transfer and subsequent phenotypic reprogramming. To test this possibility, receptor-blocking experiments were performed in both murine RAW264.7 macrophages and human THP-1-derived macrophages.

In RAW264.7 cells, immunofluorescence staining showed that LPS stimulation induced a marked iNOS-positive inflammatory phenotype and reduced ARG-1 expression, whereas treatment with Mito-TPP-KGM significantly decreased iNOS and increased ARG-1, consistent with a shift toward a reparative phenotype (Fig. 7a and b). However, this effect was attenuated when cells were pretreated with mannan, anti-CD206 antibody, or laminarin. Compared with the LPS + Mito-TPP-KGM group, all three blocking conditions increased the proportion of iNOS-positive cells and reduced ARG-1-positive cells, indicating that lectin receptor-associated recognition contributes to the phenotypic effects of engineered mitochondria.

To directly evaluate cellular uptake, we quantified human mitochondrial DNA in RAW264.7 macrophages after incubation with human ADSC-derived Mito-TPP-KGM. Mito-TPP-KGM markedly increased intracellular human mtDNA levels relative to the LPS group, whereas mannan, anti-CD206, and laminarin each significantly reduced this increase (Fig. 7c), supporting the involvement of mannose-recognizing and lectin-related uptake pathways in mitochondrial internalization.

Consistent with the immunostaining results, qPCR analysis showed that Mito-TPP-KGM significantly suppressed the pro-inflammatory genes Nos2, Tnf, Il1b, and Il6, while upregulating the reparative markers Arg1 and Il10 in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 7d–i). Blocking with mannan, anti-CD206, or laminarin partially reversed these transcriptional changes, leading to re-elevation of inflammatory genes and reduction of reparative markers. ELISA results showed the same overall trend at the protein level: Mito-TPP-KGM decreased secretion of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, while increasing IL-10, whereas these effects were weakened by receptor blockade (Fig. 7j–m). Together, these data indicate that in murine macrophages, efficient uptake and downstream reprogramming of Mito-TPP-KGM depend, at least in part, on mannose-type lectin receptor-related pathways.

We next performed parallel validation in THP-1-derived macrophages to examine whether a similar mechanism operates in human cells. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S8, Mito-TPP-KGM likewise reduced iNOS, increased ARG-1, enhanced intracellular mouse mtDNA accumulation, suppressed inflammatory genes, and promoted a more reparative cytokine profile in LPS-stimulated THP-1 derived macrophages. Importantly, these effects were attenuated by mannan, anti-CD206, or anti-DC-SIGN treatment, indicating that human mannose-binding lectin receptors also participate in engineered mitochondrial uptake and functional reprogramming.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which Mito-TPP-KGM is internalized through mannose-type lectin receptor-related pathways in both murine and human macrophages, thereby promoting mitochondrial delivery and shifting macrophages away from an inflammatory state toward a reparative phenotype.

Although our in vitro data suggest that multiple lectin-related receptors participate in Mito-TPP-KGM uptake, CD206 was chosen as a representative receptor for subsequent in vivo validation because it is a macrophage-associated mannose-recognizing surface receptor that can be directly and locally blocked in the wound environment.

Macroscopic quantification showed that wound closure was significantly delayed in diabetic control mice compared with non-diabetic M/M mice, whereas treatment with free mitochondria accelerated wound healing to some extent and Mito-TPP-KGM produced a more pronounced improvement throughout the observation period (Fig. 8b). Importantly, the beneficial effect of Mito-TPP-KGM was partially attenuated by local anti-CD206 treatment, while isotype IgG had little influence, indicating that CD206-related recognition contributes to the therapeutic efficacy of engineered mitochondria in vivo. Representative gross wound images are shown in Fig. S9.

Histological analysis further supported the wound closure results. On day 7, H&E staining showed that wounds treated with Mito-TPP-KGM exhibited improved wound fill and re-epithelialization compared with diabetic control wounds, whereas anti-CD206 treatment weakened this improvement (Fig. 8c and d). Consistently, Masson staining at day 7 revealed enhanced collagen deposition in the Mito-TPP-KGM group, which was diminished after CD206 blockade (Fig. 8e and f). By day 14, H&E staining demonstrated a more normalized epidermal architecture in the Mito-TPP-KGM group, while this benefit was partially reduced in the anti-CD206 group (Fig. 8g and h). Masson staining at day 14 similarly suggested improved collagen remodeling after Mito-TPP-KGM treatment, whereas local CD206 blockade weakened this effect (Fig. 8i and j). Together, these findings indicate that Mito-TPP-KGM promotes diabetic wound repair in vivo and that this therapeutic advantage is at least partly associated with CD206-related targeting.

We next examined whether the in vivo therapeutic benefit of Mito-TPP-KGM was accompanied by changes in wound angiogenesis, oxidative damage, and macrophage-related inflammatory status. Immunofluorescence staining of wound-edge tissues showed that F4/80+CD206+ cells were already detectable during the early stage of repair, and their proportion among F4/80+ macrophages was higher on day 3 than on day 1 (Fig. 9a and b), supporting the rationale for selecting CD206 as a representative receptor for in vivo blocking validation. At day 14, CD31 staining showed that Mito-TPP-KGM increased the number of CD31-positive vessels compared with diabetic control wounds, whereas anti-CD206 treatment reduced this pro-angiogenic effect (Fig. 9c and d). At day 7, 8-OHdG staining revealed marked oxidative damage in diabetic wounds, which was alleviated after treatment with free mitochondria or Mito-TPP-KGM; this reduction was weakened by anti-CD206 blockade (Fig. 9e and f). In parallel, immunofluorescence analysis of iNOS and CD163 at day 3 showed that Mito-TPP-KGM decreased the ratio of iNOS-positive to CD163-positive macrophages in wound tissues, consistent with modulation toward a less inflammatory and more reparative macrophage phenotype, whereas anti-CD206 treatment partially reversed this change (Fig. 9g and h). Collectively, these results suggest that the in vivo wound-healing benefit of Mito-TPP-KGM is accompanied by enhanced angiogenesis, reduced oxidative damage, and altered macrophage phenotype, and that these effects are partially dependent on CD206-related targeting.

Additional supporting experiments further strengthened these in vivo findings. Flow-cytometric analysis supported preferential uptake of FITC-Mito-TPP-KGM by wound macrophages in vivo (Fig. S10). Moreover, macrophage-rich regions at diabetic wound edges displayed enhanced oxidative damage and mitochondrial abnormalities compared with non-diabetic controls, further supporting the pathological relevance of mitochondrial-targeted macrophage intervention (Fig. S11). Additional supplementary data also showed that Mito-TPP-KGM retained basic mitochondrial function under mildly acidic conditions, while TPP-KGM exhibited progressive degradability in simulated wound fluid and gradual in vivo clearance after local administration (Fig. S12–S14). Meanwhile, TPP-KGM showed acceptable cytocompatibility in vitro and no evident systemic toxicity in vivo, as supported by live/dead staining, serum biochemistry, inflammatory cytokine analysis, and histological examination of major organs (Fig. S15–S17).

Discussion

Macrophage-targeted mitochondrial transplantation as a strategy for chronic diabetic wounds

Chronic diabetic wounds are characterized by a prolonged inflammatory phase, defective resolution, and impaired transition into a pro-repair milieu [33]. A central pathological feature is macrophage dysregulation: diabetic wounds show excessive and persistent accumulation of inflammatory macrophages, defective clearance of apoptotic cells, and delayed emergence of reparative populations, all of which contribute to non-resolving inflammation and fibrosis [[34], [35], [36]]. Our in vivo data in db/db mice are consistent with this paradigm, showing delayed wound closure, increased oxidative damage, impaired angiogenesis, and a higher ratio of iNOS-positive to CD163-positive macrophages in untreated diabetic wounds, whereas Mito-TPP-KGM treatment shifted the wound microenvironment toward improved histological healing and reduced inflammatory burden.

In parallel, mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated as an upstream driver of impaired wound repair in diabetes, by amplifying ROS production, lowering ATP availability, and skewing macrophage immunometabolism toward a pro-inflammatory state [[37], [38], [39]]. Therapeutic mitochondrial transplantation has therefore emerged as a promising approach to rescue bioenergetic failure in ischemia-reperfusion injury, cardiac arrest, and donor heart preservation, with mounting preclinical and early clinical data demonstrating that exogenous mitochondria can restore oxidative phosphorylation, limit cell death, and improve organ function [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. However, most studies have focused on direct delivery of mitochondria to parenchymal cells, whereas the concept of macrophage-centered mitochondrial transplantation for immunomodulation and wound repair has not been systematically explored [45].

ADSCs were selected as the mitochondrial donor source in this study because they represent a practical and regenerative cell type that can be expanded under standardized conditions and is widely used in tissue-repair research. Our aim was not to identify the most mitochondria-rich donor cell type, but to establish a proof-of-concept macrophage-directed mitochondrial transplantation strategy using an experimentally accessible and translationally relevant donor source.

By engineering isolated ADSC-derived mitochondria with a glucomannan-based polysaccharide (TPP-KGM) and directing them to macrophages, our work attempts to bridge these two lines of research. On one hand, we harness the organelle-replacement logic of mitochondrial transplantation; on the other hand, we target a pivotal immune orchestrator of wound healing rather than the structural cell compartment alone. The enhanced uptake of Mito-TPP-KGM by macrophages in vitro, the restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis in RAW264.7 cells, and the downstream effects on efferocytosis, angiogenesis, and diabetic wound repair together support the notion that macrophage-directed mitochondrial transplantation can serve as an immunometabolic strategy complementary to previously reported parenchymal-cell-focused approaches.

Engineered mitochondria restore mitochondrial homeostasis and immunometabolism in stressed macrophages

Mitochondria are now recognized as central hubs coupling macrophage phenotype to metabolic state [46,47]. In general, inflammatory macrophages are associated with enhanced glycolysis, elevated ROS, and impaired mitochondrial function, whereas reparative macrophages are more closely associated with intact oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and lower oxidative stress [[47], [48], [49]]. In our model, combined high glucose and H2O2 recapitulated several features of diabetic oxidative stress in macrophages, including mitochondrial depolarization, elevated total and mitochondrial ROS, ATP depletion, and impaired respiratory function, together with a shift toward a more glycolytic metabolic profile. These findings are in line with previous studies showing that hyperglycemia and oxidative stress reprogram macrophages into a metabolically inflexible and inflammatory state.

Importantly, both free mitochondria and Mito-TPP-KGM partially reversed this dysfunctional phenotype, but the engineered mitochondria generally exerted a more pronounced effect. Mito-TPP-KGM more effectively restored mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced global and mitochondrial ROS, and increased ATP levels compared with free mitochondria, while TPP-KGM alone produced only minor changes. These data suggest that the major bioenergetic rescue derives from mitochondrial transfer rather than the polysaccharide carrier itself.

Seahorse analysis further reinforced this conclusion. H2O2(HG) treatment suppressed basal and maximal respiration while increasing glycolysis and glycolytic capacity, indicating impaired OXPHOS with compensatory glycolysis. Mito-TPP-KGM shifted this profile toward a more balanced respiratory and glycolytic state, whereas free mitochondria also improved these parameters but to a lesser extent. Together with the JC-1 and ROS data, these findings indicate that engineered mitochondria not only provide bioenergetic support but also re-establish a more physiological coupling between mitochondrial function and macrophage immunometabolism. Such metabolic normalization is likely to underpin the downstream shift toward a less inflammatory and more reparative macrophage phenotype. Although the present study did not directly dissect downstream signaling pathways, candidate regulators such as AMPK, mTOR, and HIF-1α may plausibly connect mitochondrial uptake and redox rescue to the observed immunometabolic reprogramming of macrophages. Defining these pathway-level links will be an important objective for future mechanistic studies. Notably, the current data do not support a suppressive effect of donor mitochondria on host endogenous mitochondrial function. Instead, the observed improvement in mitochondrial homeostasis-related genes, ATP production, ROS burden, and metabolic readouts is more consistent with a functional complementation/rescue effect in stressed macrophages. Nevertheless, dedicated long-term studies will still be required to define how exogenous mitochondria influence endogenous mitochondrial dynamics and mitonuclear coordination after uptake.

Lectin receptor-related uptake and the rationale for CD206-focused in vivo validation

A distinctive feature of our system is the use of glucomannan-based TPP-KGM to decorate isolated mitochondria. Glucomannan and related polysaccharides have been widely exploited to bias uptake toward macrophages through carbohydrate-recognizing lectin receptors, including CD206, Dectin-1-related pathways, and, in human cells, DC-SIGN [50,51]. Previous studies have shown that KGM-based materials can interact with macrophages and modulate inflammatory status depending on composition and context [[52], [53], [54]]. Our design extends this concept from conventional molecular cargos to organelle-level delivery.

Our data in RAW264.7 and THP-1-derived macrophages support the view that mannose-type lectin receptors contribute to both the uptake and functional activity of Mito-TPP-KGM. In murine RAW264.7 cells, pretreatment with mannan, anti-CD206, or laminarin reduced intracellular donor mtDNA accumulation and attenuated the effects of Mito-TPP-KGM on inflammatory and reparative markers. In human THP-1-derived macrophages, mannan, anti-CD206, and anti-DC-SIGN produced a similar attenuation. Together, these findings suggest that uptake and downstream macrophage reprogramming are not mediated by a single receptor, but rather involve multiple lectin receptor-related pathways.

This interpretation also helps resolve the apparent paradox that CD206 is often regarded as a reparative macrophage marker whereas diabetic wounds are enriched in inflammatory macrophages. CD206 expression in vivo is not binary, and our wound-edge staining showed that F4/80+CD206+ macrophages were already detectable during the early stage of repair, with a higher proportion on day 3 than on day 1. Moreover, in vitro data suggest that initial lectin receptor-related binding may facilitate mitochondrial transfer and subsequently reinforce a more reparative macrophage state. In this context, CD206 was selected for in vivo blocking experiments not because it is the only relevant receptor, but because it represents a macrophage-associated mannose-recognizing surface receptor that can be directly and locally blocked in the wound environment. This makes CD206 a practical and biologically relevant representative node for testing the contribution of lectin receptor-related targeting in vivo.

From efferocytosis to angiogenesis: linking macrophage reprogramming to tissue-level repair

In addition to metabolic and transcriptional changes, an effective pro-repair macrophage therapy must restore key effector functions, especially efferocytosis and the capacity to support angiogenesis. Efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils and other dying cells is a critical step in inflammation resolution, limiting secondary necrosis and promoting a transition toward a reparative state [55]. Defective efferocytosis has been repeatedly documented in both experimental and clinical diabetic wounds and is now considered a major bottleneck in wound resolution [7,56]. Our efferocytosis assays demonstrate that both high-glucose oxidative stress and inflammatory stimulation impaired macrophage engulfment of apoptotic HL-60 cells, whereas treatment with free mitochondria and especially Mito-TPP-KGM restored this capacity. This functional rescue is consistent with the observed shift toward a less inflammatory and more reparative transcriptional and secretory profile. Together, these data support a model in which engineered mitochondria act upstream to correct metabolic and redox defects, thereby enabling macrophages to regain efficient efferocytosis.

The consequences of this macrophage reprogramming were further reflected in the endothelial assays using macrophage-conditioned media. Conditioned media from stressed macrophages impaired HUVEC tube formation, migration, and proliferation, whereas conditioned media from Mito-TPP-KGM-treated macrophages significantly improved these angiogenesis-related behaviors under both high-glucose oxidative stress and inflammatory conditions. These findings are consistent with the idea that metabolically restored macrophages can generate a more pro-angiogenic and pro-resolution secretome.

At the tissue level, Mito-TPP-KGM improved multiple wound-healing readouts in db/db mice, including faster closure, improved histological architecture, enhanced collagen deposition and remodeling, increased CD31-positive vessels, reduced 8-OHdG staining, and a lower ratio of iNOS-positive to CD163-positive macrophages. Although not every readout was completely normalized, the overall pattern supports a coherent link between macrophage-centered mitochondrial rescue, improved clearance of inflammatory debris, a more favorable angiogenic microenvironment, and enhanced tissue repair in diabetic wounds.

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite the comprehensive in vitro and in vivo data presented here, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, although our data provide supportive evidence regarding the uptake and fate of engineered mitochondria, the ultimate intracellular fate of transplanted mitochondria remains incompletely resolved. Fig. 2 shows rapid macrophage uptake of Mito-TPP-KGM, partial colocalization with endogenous mitochondria, and limited lysosomal colocalization over time, suggesting that a fraction of the delivered mitochondria may associate with the host mitochondrial network rather than being immediately degraded. In addition, DiR-based in vivo tracing supported gradual local retention and clearance of the carrier-associated signal after wound administration. Nevertheless, these data do not definitively determine whether exogenous mitochondria are functionally integrated into the endogenous mitochondrial network, persist as partially independent organelles, or undergo progressive intracellular degradation after uptake by wound macrophages. Future studies combining higher-resolution live-cell imaging, organelle-specific lineage tracing, and spatially resolved analyses will be needed to clarify these processes more precisely [[57], [58], [59], [60], [61]].

Second, our receptor-blocking experiments support the involvement of mannose-type lectin receptor-related pathways in the uptake and immunomodulatory actions of Mito-TPP-KGM, but they do not establish a single dominant receptor mechanism. In murine RAW264.7 macrophages, mannan, anti-CD206, and laminarin all attenuated mitochondrial uptake and phenotypic reprogramming, while in human THP-1-derived macrophages, mannan, anti-CD206, and anti-DC-SIGN produced similar inhibitory effects. These findings suggest that multiple lectin receptor-related pathways may participate in recognition of the glucomannan corona. Accordingly, CD206 was selected for in vivo validation as a representative and experimentally accessible macrophage mannose-recognizing receptor, rather than being interpreted as the sole receptor responsible for targeting. More definitive mechanistic studies using genetic perturbation and receptor-specific imaging will be required in future work.

Third, although both RAW264.7 and THP-1-derived macrophages were used to strengthen mechanistic consistency across murine and human systems, these cell models cannot fully recapitulate the heterogeneity of macrophage populations present in diabetic wounds. Likewise, the db/db mouse model captures key pathological features of diabetic wound healing but does not fully represent the complexity of human chronic ulcers. Validation in primary wound macrophages, more advanced humanized or organotypic systems, and larger animal models will therefore be important for further translational development. In addition, the in vivo flow-cytometric analysis in the present study was performed at the level of the broad wound macrophage compartment and did not further distinguish tissue-resident from infiltrating macrophage subsets. Future studies using more refined subset-resolved flow cytometry or single-cell approaches will be needed to define the core macrophage populations targeted by engineered mitochondrial transplantation.

Fourth, the present study focused on macrophages as the primary therapeutic target and mechanistic hub, but diabetic wound healing is inherently a multicellular and clinically heterogeneous process involving fibroblasts, keratinocytes, endothelial cells, neutrophils, peripheral nerves, and, in some cases, bacterial biofilm-associated infection. It therefore remains to be determined to what extent the beneficial effects of Mito-TPP-KGM arise from direct macrophage rescue versus secondary remodeling of the broader wound microenvironment. In addition, from a translational perspective, allogeneic mitochondrial transplantation may raise concerns regarding immune recognition and other long-term biosafety issues. In the present study, preliminary systemic safety was supported by the absence of obvious abnormalities in serum liver/kidney function markers, circulating inflammatory cytokines, and major-organ histology after local administration. The use of local wound delivery may also help reduce systemic exposure relative to systemic infusion. Moreover, TPP-KGM showed progressive degradation in simulated wound fluid and gradual local clearance in vivo after DiR labeling, supporting the biodegradability and limited persistence of the carrier under the current conditions. However, rare or delayed events, including long-term immune responses, the fate of exogenous mitochondrial genetic material, carrier-related immune activation such as splenic lymphocyte responses, and the behavior of this platform in biofilm-associated or infected wound settings, were not directly assessed here and will require dedicated investigation in future studies. Further studies dissecting cell-cell communication and integrating mitochondrial delivery with other regenerative or anti-infective strategies may expand the therapeutic potential and translational relevance of this platform.

In summary, our current findings support polysaccharide-engineered mitochondrial transplantation as a promising macrophage-centered immunometabolic strategy for diabetic wound repair, while also highlighting the need for deeper mechanistic tracking, broader translational validation, and longer-term safety assessment.

Conclusion

In this study, we engineered ADSC-derived mitochondria with triphenylphosphonium-modified konjac glucomannan to construct a macrophage-targeted mitochondrial delivery system, Mito-TPP-KGM. In vitro, Mito-TPP-KGM was efficiently internalized by macrophages, restored mitochondrial homeostasis, reduced oxidative stress, and rebalanced glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, thereby shifting macrophages toward a less inflammatory and more reparative state with enhanced efferocytosis and pro-angiogenic paracrine activity. Mechanistically, our data support the involvement of mannose-type lectin receptor-related pathways in the uptake and immunomodulatory actions of engineered mitochondria. In a db/db diabetic wound model, local delivery of Mito-TPP-KGM improved histological repair, reduced oxidative damage, enhanced angiogenesis, and accelerated wound closure; these therapeutic benefits were partially attenuated by local CD206 blockade. Collectively, these findings indicate that polysaccharide-engineered mitochondrial transplantation represents a feasible macrophage-centered immunometabolic strategy for diabetic wound repair and provides a conceptual basis for further development of organelle-level therapeutics in chronic inflammatory diseases.
