Multivisceral arterial compression by the median arcuate ligament in a 33-year-old woman
Authors: Taleen A. MacArthur, Manju Kalra, Melinda S. Schaller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Compression of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) by the median arcuate ligament (MAL) is a rare cause of MAL syndrome. This is a case of a 33-year-old woman with a history of postprandial abdominal pain who was found to have compression of the celiac artery and SMA by the MAL. In this case, both arteries were otherwise healthy, and release of the MAL resulted in resolution of the celiac artery and SMA stenosis and sustained symptomatic relief.

Median arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS) typically refers to compression of the celiac artery (CA) by the MAL. Symptoms of MALS can vary, but patients often present with postprandial abdominal pain, nausea, weight loss, and other gastrointestinal symptoms. These patients often experience abdominal symptoms for a prolonged period before being diagnosed with or treated for MALS.1 In rare circumstances, the MAL can also compress the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). In this case, the authors discuss the open surgical treatment of symptomatic compression of both the CA and SMA by the MAL. Informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication and presentation of this case, including images.

Case report

This patient is a 33-year-old woman who presented to the vascular surgery clinic with postprandial epigastric abdominal pain, bloating, and nausea that had worsened over the last 6 months, leading to food fear and weight loss. The pain would come on immediately after or while eating. Before presentation she had undergone a full workup with gastroenterology for these symptoms, including upper endoscopy with biopsies to evaluate for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, which was normal, without changes of chronic ischemia. She also underwent an endoscopic ultrasound which showed numerous collaterals to the head of the pancreas, but no other abnormalities. She had no objective evidence of delayed gastric emptying. She underwent cross-sectional imaging with computed tomography angiography (CTA), which demonstrated a near occlusive stenosis vs focal occlusion of the proximal CA with a hypertrophied gastroduodenal artery off the SMA. Compression of the SMA during expiration was also seen on CTA with respiratory phases. She then had a mesenteric arterial duplex ultrasound performed, which also revealed elevated velocities about the proximal SMA, with a peak systolic velocity of 349 cm/s, consistent with high-grade stenosis. This result prompted a referral to vascular surgery. Her medical and surgical history was significant for renovascular hypertension owing to fibromuscular dysplasia, Hashimoto thyroiditis, and fibromyalgia. She had undergone a prior robotic-assisted partial right nephrectomy to address her renovascular hypertension, which resolved this issue.

On physical examination, her abdomen was soft and nontender, and she had a normal peripheral pulse examination. A dynamic CTA of the abdomen and pelvis with respiratory maneuvers was obtained. This demonstrated fixed, high-grade narrowing of the CA secondary to compression by the MAL, as well as high-grade stenosis of the SMA, worse on expiration, secondary to compression from the MAL (Fig 1). She underwent a fluoroscopically guided celiac plexus block resulting in significant relief of her symptoms for several hours, allowing her to eat without difficulty. Given the imaging findings on both a high-quality CTA and mesenteric duplex ultrasound examination, her otherwise negative gastroenterology workup, and relief of her symptoms with the celiac plexus block, a diagnosis of MALS was made. The patient was offered evaluation by general surgery for consideration of a laparoscopic or robotic MAL release, but she preferred an open approach. Under general endotracheal anesthesia, an upper midline laparotomy was performed and the supraceliac aorta was exposed anteriorly. The celiac trunk was identified and the common hepatic, splenic, and left gastric arteries were circumferentially dissected free and controlled with silastic vessel loops, taking care to clear these entirely of any overlying neuronal tissue. The celiac trunk was similarly entirely cleared of all surrounding tissue, anteriorly and posteriorly (Fig 2). Dissection was carried along the anterior surface of the celiac trunk. A right angle was then placed between the celiac trunk and the previously exposed supraceliac aorta. The thick MAL was divided and resected on either side of the aorta (Fig 3). A complete and circumferential dissection was performed of the celiac trunk, freeing it of all compression and a complete neurolysis was undertaken. The bulky celiac ganglion was identified and resected on the right and left aspects of the celiac trunk. Debulking of the MAL and diaphragmatic crura caudal to the CA origin resulted in release of the SMA compression. Appropriate Doppler signals were confirmed in the celiac trunk, each of its branches, and the SMA. Both the CA and SMA appeared to be healthy without any concerning changes owing to their chronic compression by the MAL; therefore, reconstruction was not needed for either vessel. There was also no concern for mesenteric fibromuscular dysplasia on intraoperative evaluation of the mesenteric vessels. Hemostasis was ensured and the abdomen was closed in the standard fashion. The patient was extubated in the operating room and admitted to the vascular surgery floor. An epidural catheter was placed to assist with postoperative pain control. Her postoperative course was uncomplicated, and she was discharged from the hospital on postoperative day 5, tolerating a general diet without any significant abdominal pain. On 1-year follow-up the patient remained asymptomatic. Discussion

Compression of the SMA by the MAL is a very uncommon source of abdominal pain. This has been reported in only a few cases in the literature. Management of the SMA compression has been variable under these circumstances. In one instance with confirmed chronic CA occlusion and high-grade SMA stenosis in the setting of MAL compression, open aortomesenteric revascularization after MAL release was performed.2 Additional surgical approaches reported in the literature include laparoscopic MAL release with subsequent SMA stenting for ongoing symptoms and CT evidence of persistent external compression,3 and open MAL release via a retroperitoneal approach to improve the SMA stenosis and provide symptomatic relief.4 In these authors’ experience, suspected occlusion of the CA adequately resolves after MAL release confirmed by imaging postoperatively and rarely requires reconstruction. However, the surgeon must ensure complete release of the MAL to avoid the need for subsequent procedures such as stenting. Relief of both CA and SMA compression from the MAL can be achieved from a standard anterior supraceliac/paraceliac approach with better visualization of neuromuscular structures to the right of the CA origin and does not require a retroperitoneal dissection. Additionally, the authors advocate for a complete celiac ganglionectomy in these cases, because this technique addresses the issue of celiac plexus irritation contributing to postprandial pain in patients with MALS. This is this reason that a preoperative celiac plexus block is a helpful diagnostic tool.

Conclusions

MALS with compression of the SMA is uncommon, and there is no standard for management of the SMA in these circumstances. In cases such as this one, where the patient's SMA is otherwise healthy, release of the MAL itself from a standard supraceliac approach is sufficient to resolve the SMA compression and lead to sustained symptomatic relief.
