Hardware in context

The use of Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT), a geophysical method, has become widespread over the past few decades [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. Today, it is one of the most commonly used and frequently cited methods in the geophysical literature [1], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. The scope of its application extends well beyond the academic sphere. ERT is widely used in many different contexts, such as civil engineering [13], hydrogeology [3], archaeology [14], agriculture [1], the study of contaminant transport [15], and environmental investigations [16]. It is also widely used in non-profit contexts, for example by NGOs for humanitarian and environmental projects [17].

Initially applied to the simple survey of subsurface structures, in particular for mining exploration, the method underwent major expansion in the early 1990 s [18]. The advent of multiplexed resistivity meters enabled automated 2D imaging along aligned electrode profiles and 3D surveys [9]. During this period, the first 2D and 3D inversion software packages that could be used on personal computers with reasonable computation times were developed [18], [19], [20]. These parallel advances in hardware and software have fostered the rapid dissemination of the method and the emergence of new applications, particularly in time-lapse monitoring [21].

ERT is now increasingly used to acquire repeated image series (time-lapse), enabling the monitoring of various dynamic processes, such as water infiltration [22], [23], [24], temperature variations [25], slope deformation and stability [26], and the surveillance of human-made environments [27]. The method is used in many different contexts, including natural soils [28], engineered grounds [29], waste storage facilities [30] and constructed wetlands (reed bed filters) [31].

Numerous inversion tools [20] were developed during the 2000 s, progressively incorporating new options for image reconstruction [8]. However, the first open-source codes dedicated specifically to geophysical inversion appear after 2006 [32]. Since then, the open-source movement has kept gaining momentum, encouraging the ongoing development of a growing number of image reconstruction tools [33], [34]. In contrast, the field of instrumentation has lagged behind. While robust industrial instrumentation suitable for a wide range of applications has become the standard, its high associated cost limits its accessibility, particularly for less profitable activities or for teams with limited resources. Monitoring of soil moisture or water table often requires daily measurements over several months. In this scenario, the immobilisation of an expensive commercial instrument is not practical. The first open-source and open-hardware resistivity meter was published in 2020, initially appearing in the HardwareX journal under the name OhmPi [29]. This initiative demonstrated the feasibility of developing accessible, reproducible and user-repairable electrical resistivity instruments. This breakthrough was made possible by the widespread availability of rapid prototyping platforms, such as the Arduino microcontroller and the Raspberry Pi microcomputer, which provide flexible, cost-effective tools that are well suited to the development of scientific instrumentation. The OhmPi V1.00 version had the advantage of being based mainly on commercially available electronic breakout boards that could be easily sourced from the best-known manufacturers for less than 500 euros. One of its interesting features was that it offered the possibility of controlling measurements using an external trigger, such as a water content probe, pressure sensors, or even remote triggering via a Raspberry Pi. This feature was very useful in the field of geophysical instrumentation for time-series monitoring.

However, this first version also had several inherent limitations:·complex assembly of the various electronic boards, which was not well suited to operational needs, particularly for multiplexing management;·use restricted to laboratory applications or very small-scale experiments, due to the inability to exceed an injection voltage of 12 V and only a few milliamps, thus limiting the depth of investigation and applicability in the field;·the limited modularity, particularly in terms of multiplexers.

Hardware description

Principle of measurement

Electrical resistivity measurement is one of the most extensively documented geophysical methods in the specialized geoelectrical literature [12], [18], [36], [37]. Electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) is considered a reference technique for subsurface exploration. Numerous studies have provided a detailed description of the principles of electrical resistivity data acquisition, emphasizing both the instrumental characteristics of the measurements and the impact of noise on the acquired data [1], [19], [20], [38], [39].

Physically, the method is based on Ohm's law, which relates the measured potential difference to the intensity of the injected current and the resistivity of the medium traversed [18].

The measurement is based on a quadrupole configuration (Fig. 1), consisting of two current injection electrodes, labelled A and B (or C1 and C2 depending on the nomenclature used), and two potential measurement electrodes, labelled M and N (or P1 and P2). The application of a controlled voltage (VAB) creates an electric current (IAB) between the current electrodes that causes a potential distribution in the ground, part of which is measured between the potential electrodes (VMN). Based on the ratio between the measured potential difference and the injected current, and taking into account a geometric factor depending on the configuration of the electrodes, it is then possible to calculate the apparent resistivity (ρa) of the medium.Fig. 1Measuring resistance with a quadrupole. R is the transfer resistance, RAB is the resistance viewed by the transmitter, the apparent resistivity of the medium is computed from the transfer resistance and a geometric factor (Modified from [29]).

Measuring resistance with a quadrupole. R is the transfer resistance, RAB is the resistance viewed by the transmitter, the apparent resistivity of the medium is computed from the transfer resistance and a geometric factor (Modified from [29]).

In order to obtain the spatial distribution of soil resistivity, this operation is repeated using multiple combinations of four electrodes [37]. A multiplexer is employed to automatically select the required electrode combinations without manual intervention, thereby enabling the acquisition of a complete measurement sequence. This dataset forms the basis of electrical resistivity tomography (ERT), whose interpretation relies on inversion algorithms designed to reconstruct the subsurface distribution of resistivity based on the measured resistances [32], [33], [34], [37], [40].

In addition to resistivity, the same acquisition system can be used to measure the chargeability of the subsurface through the induced polarization (IP) method [18], [41]. Chargeability reflects the ability of geological materials to temporarily store electrical charges when subjected to an external electric field. Physically, this phenomenon is mainly associated with electrochemical polarization mechanisms occurring at grain–fluid interfaces, particularly in the presence of metallic minerals, clays, or other electrically active materials.

In time-domain induced polarization (TDIP), a direct current is injected into the ground for a given duration, after which the current is abruptly switched off. While the injected current flows, charges accumulate within the medium. When the current is interrupted, the measured potential difference does not instantaneously return to zero but instead decays progressively over time. This decay curve is recorded between the potential electrodes and analysed over a defined time window.

The apparent chargeability (Ma) is generally defined as the normalized integral of the voltage decay curve over a specified time interval, divided by the primary voltage measured during current injection. It is commonly expressed in milliseconds (ms) or in dimensionless units (mV/V). By repeating the measurement sequence over multiple electrode configurations, as performed for resistivity, it is possible to construct a dataset of apparent chargeability values.

Similarly to resistivity data, chargeability measurements are inverted using dedicated algorithms to obtain a spatial distribution of subsurface chargeability. The joint interpretation of resistivity and chargeability models provides complementary information: while resistivity is mainly sensitive to lithology, porosity, and fluid content, chargeability is particularly sensitive to the presence of disseminated metallic particles, clay content, and certain geochemical conditions. Consequently, the combined use of ERT and induced polarization significantly improves the characterization of subsurface materials and enhances the reliability of geological and environmental interpretations.

Advances over the OhmPi V1.00 architecture

The new OhmPi version (v2024.0.2) represents a substantial advancement over the original architecture published in 2020 (OhmPi V1.00). Unlike the previous version, which relied predominantly on commercially available off-the-shelf electronic boards, this new version constitutes an entirely new instrument, incorporating both hardware and software developments. It introduces a dedicated measurement board and a custom multiplexer board that requires assembly, providing greater control over the instrument's performance and expanding its capabilities.

Table 1 summarizes the key improvements between the two versions. The most notable hardware advance concerns the maximum injection voltage, which has been increased from 12 V to 50 V through the new measurement board, significantly extending the instrument's capability to operate in high-resistivity environments. A dedicated voltage injection system allows injection up to 50 V and 40 mA, depending on the shunt resistance selected (2 O). It is worth noting that the measurement board itself supports input voltages up to 200 V, meaning that users who develop their own current injector can take full advantage of this extended range, further increasing the flexibility of the system. The operating temperature range has also been extended down to −20°C, making the instrument suitable for deployment in cold climate conditions. The Vmn input impedance has been increased from 36 MOhm to 1000 MOhm, greatly reducing measurement errors in challenging field conditions. On the software side, the acquisition framework has evolved significantly, with numerous parameters now accessible to the user, as well as the ability to record full waveform data, opening new possibilities for advanced signal processing and time-domain induced polarization analysis. The codebase has also been redesigned with a stronger emphasis on flexibility, allowing users to more easily adapt and extend the software to meet the specific requirements of their applications.

Together, these improvements substantially broaden the range of applications for which OhmPi is suited, while maintaining the low-cost and open-source philosophy that underlies the project.

Functional component of the OhmPi measurement board.

Voltage measurement is typically performed using an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC). In the OhmPi, a 16-bit ADC component (ADS1115) is used. The ADS1115 is also equipped with programmable gain control (PGA), meaning it can scale up the measured voltage before digitising it. The gain can vary between 2/3 and 16. With a gain of 1, the ADS1115 can measure voltages between 0 and 5 V with a precision of 5/(21⁶) = 0.076 mV. Electrode N is connected to the analogue ground of the ADS1115. At electrode M, a single ended voltage reading is measured. The signal must lie within the ± 2.5 V or ± 5 V range depending on the gain of the circuit (either 1 or 1/2, controlled by the DG411 analog switch). First, an operational amplifier (OP27) is used in 'follow-up' mode to ensure the MN part has a high input impedance. If current leaks in the MN part while measuring, it will affect the measurement. The next stage is a gain adapter, if the signal is within ± 2.5 V, unity gain is applied; if the signal is higher, a gain of 1/2 is applied using a resistor divider bridge. As the ADS1115 cannot measure negative voltages in this configuration, a + 2.5 V DC offset is added to the signal to obtain a 0–5 V signal at the A0 input of the ADS1115 (see Fig. 5). Note that for the MN part, N is used as a 'floating ground'. This allows the ADS1115 to perform a differential VMN measurement. To understand this better, if we inject a 50 V VAB voltage into our power supply, we could obtain a VM voltage of 40 V and a VN voltage of 43 V referenced to the power supply ground. However, for the VMN measurement, OhmPi sees a voltage of −3V across its terminals since floating ground is electrode N. This voltage can then be processed by applying a gain of 1/2 to give −1.5 V, followed by an offset of 2.5 V to give a voltage of 1 V, which is acceptable for the ADS1115. (See Fig. 5).Fig. 5Electronic signal conditioning circuit for Vmn from M/N front-end to the ADC (ADS1115).

Electronic signal conditioning circuit for Vmn from M/N front-end to the ADC (ADS1115).

The OhmPi injection unit is based on an H-bridge topology driven by four electromechanical relays, which enable the generation of bipolar injection cycles. The system is flexible enough to allow configurable pulse durations ranging from 50 ms to 10 s, with an accuracy of 2 ms, enabling it to adapt to specific signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) requirements and subsurface polarisation time constants. A safety dead time of at least 10 ms is strictly observed between each polarity reversal. This guard interval is imperative to prevent shoot-through (cross-conduction) phenomena during relay switching. This ensures the integrity of the power circuits part. Meanwhile, the injected current is measured using a precision shunt resistor (0.1 to 10 Ω ± 0.1 %) and the resulting differential signal is processed by a Mikroe® Current Click 7 module implementing an INA282 amplifier (Fig. 6). The INA282 applies a fixed gain of 50 on the voltage measured at the shunt, enabling it to reach the dynamic range of the 16-bit ADS1115 analogue-to-digital converter (ADC). This acquisition chain provides high metrological resolution, which is essential for accurately converting potential measurements into current values while minimizing systematic errors. Given the value of the shunt resistor (e.g., 2 Ohms), one can compute the maximum current that can be measured by the device as: Imax = 5 V/(50 * 2 Ohm) = 0.05A. Note that with a 2 Ohm shunt resistor the device can measure up to 50 mA, meaning that if larger currents are injected, which is possible, the instrument will keep showing 50 mA. If there is a need to measure larger currents, a smaller shunt resistor (e.g., 1 Ohm for up to 100 mA) can be used.Fig. 6Functional diagram of the H-bridge injection unit and current acquisition chain.

Functional diagram of the H-bridge injection unit and current acquisition chain.

Multiplexer boards (mux.2024)

The mux.2024 boards consist of electromechanical relays that are designed to route signals (A, B, M or N) from the measurement board to specific electrodes (Fig. 7). Each board features 32 OMRON G5LE-1-VD 12 VDC power relays that are connected to two MCP23017 I/O expanders. As the MCP23017 supports eight unique I2C addresses, up to four mux.2024 boards (each supporting a 32-electrode system) can be connected directly to the measurement board.Fig. 7Architecture of the Multiplexer board: (a) Electronic schematic of the driving circuit for a single relay, and (b) View of the assembled electronic board showing the relay matrix and the connection interfaces to the AB in this case.

Architecture of the Multiplexer board: (a) Electronic schematic of the driving circuit for a single relay, and (b) View of the assembled electronic board showing the relay matrix and the connection interfaces to the AB in this case.

For larger scales, an I2C extension board based on the TCA9548A multiplexer can manage up to 32 mux.2024 boards, enabling a 256-electrode system with one TCA9548A board. As the TCA9548A can have up to 8 addresses, this allows the use of eight I2C extension boards in parallel to further reach a total number of electrodes that can possibly be addressed to 2048. While the system is theoretically scalable to 2048 electrodes, practical constraints on the I2C bus length have currently limited testing to systems of up to 512 electrodes. To ensure supply chain resilience, the PCB supports dual footprints for the relay-driving MOSFETs (ZVN4206A or STP16NF06L). Likewise, the board offers versatile connectivity, featuring 16-way IDC connectors or screw terminals for electrode take-outs, 6-way IDC connectors for the I2C bus and screw terminals for power and role wiring. Finally, the board can be physically configured for either two-role (16 electrodes) or four-role (8 electrodes) operation, offering users the flexibility to optimise their setup for assembly simplicity or more complex measurement protocols.

Power supply

In the OhmPi Lite configuration, the digital power supply module (specifically the Joy-IT© DPH5005) regulates the output voltage (VAB) for the transmitter (Tx). The resulting injected current (IAB) is governed by the relation IAB = VAB/RAB, where RAB represents the total resistance between electrodes A and B. This module supports power ratings up to 50 V, 5 A, and 250 W, communicating with the Raspberry Pi via USB using the ModBus protocol by adding the JT-DPH-USB module. Since this module is provided as a commercial kit, detailed assembly instructions are not covered in this article. Readers should refer to the manufacturer’s documentation for guidance on integration. Within the OhmPi framework, the DPH5005 serves as a reference implementation, though the architecture remains modular, allowing users to integrate alternative power modules or standard batteries depending on their specific field requirements.

Software description

Architecture overview

The instrument operates using code that runs on a Raspberry Pi. Early versions of OhmPi were controlled using a Python script employing functional programming. This has evolved into an object-oriented module including several classes and a configuration file. The current software architecture (see Fig. 8) has three main aims: (1) to facilitate the development and evolution of hardware by focusing on the atomic functionalities of its components; (2) to make software more stable and easier to maintain by decoupling system operation from the way hardware components are controlled, and (3) to improve the exposure of the user interface by separating low-level functionalities at the system scale from higher-level, user-oriented tools.Fig. 8Overview of the OhmPi software architecture and its relationship with hardware components and interfaces. This architecture is based on classes designed to represent the three levels of description and operation of the OhmPi: the hardware component level, the assembled system level, and the higher-level operations level with its user interfaces.

Overview of the OhmPi software architecture and its relationship with hardware components and interfaces. This architecture is based on classes designed to represent the three levels of description and operation of the OhmPi: the hardware component level, the assembled system level, and the higher-level operations level with its user interfaces.

At the most basic level, the five main hardware components are represented by distinct classes that expose the components' atomic functionalities. Abstract classes are defined in order to provide a common interface for the various implementations of a component. Derived classes such as those required to interface the OhmPi Lite components are implemented from these abstract classes, representing the default properties, actual capabilities, and ways to interact with the physical implementations of the components. These classes are stored in the directory ohmpi/hardware_components.

At the medium level, the OhmPiHardware class facilitates the logical assembly and operation of the acquisition system. Its methods orchestrate the atomic operations of the system components to expose basic functionalities, such as cross-mux switching, square-wave voltage injection and full-waveform voltage and current readings during injection cycles. These functionalities are implemented using synchronisation mechanisms between threads to ensure that each component keeps in step with the others. This part of the software architecture should remain stable unless the system's overall functionalities evolve. However, introducing new functionalities at the system level or making radical changes to the way the components work together would require adaptations at this level. The OhmPi class exposes the OhmPi functionalities, particularly for interacting with the OhmPi instrument via a web interface, IoT communication protocols (e.g., MQTT), and/or the Python API.

This new OhmPi software includes a logging system that enables you to monitor operation execution (exec_logger), system health (soh_logger), and data flow (data_logger). These logs are stored locally using a rotating logging system and can be sent as MQTT topics for shared and remote monitoring purposes. The system may subscribe to an additional MQTT topic (ctrl_topic) to interface. This version also introduces event messages as an additional feature that leverages the logging system used throughout the code to track system operations at any level. Transfer resistance measurement

Transfer resistance is measured at the OhmPi Hardware class level using methods from the Tx and Rx component classes dedicated to measuring IAB and VMN. Injection pulses are created using an OhmPiHardware method that is based on the relay switching methods of the mux components.

VAB must be chosen so that PAB (power at the transmitter side: PAB = VAB * IAB), VMN and IAB remain within the operating range of the OhmPi system. However, R (VMN/IAB is the transfer resistance of the quadripole and depends on the soil and the array used.) and RAB (soil resistance plus contact resistances to current path between A and B) are both unknown prior to measurement. In order to obtain a good measurement of R, we need to inject enough current (IAB) between A and B, meaning we need to apply a large enough VAB to force current through RAB. However, we also do not want to inject too much current as it might lead to a VMN too large to measure. Several strategies are available to select the optimal VAB voltage. Apart from strategies in which the user specifies VAB a priori, the search for optimal VAB to apply is performed through successive iterations during the initial phase of the measurement. After this first phase, the measurement of R is performed using the VAB deduced from the search phase (cf. Fig. 10).Fig. 10A typical transfer resistance measurement is based on the measurement of IAB and VMN on a series of pulses. Short pulses are first created to find the optimal VAB according to a given strategy. Afterwards, this VAB is used to create longer pulses dedicated to the measurement of the transfer resistance.

A typical transfer resistance measurement is based on the measurement of IAB and VMN on a series of pulses. Short pulses are first created to find the optimal VAB according to a given strategy. Afterwards, this VAB is used to create longer pulses dedicated to the measurement of the transfer resistance.

On OhmPi Lite v2024, VMN must be within the range [-5V, +5V]. To ensure that it remains within this range at the first step, a low initial value of VAB is selected (e.g., 5 V). At each step, two alternating pulses of VAB are created and the optimal VAB is computed, taking into account the uncertainties in IAB and VMN. This process is repeated until an adequate VAB is found or a predefined number of steps has been reached. Fig. 10 on the right part of the graph shows the successive steps in the search for the optimal VAB.

For example, the Vmin strategy will try to find the minimum VAB to inject in order to be able to reliably measure VMN by attempting to reach a given voltage at the receiver. This saves energy. On the contrary, the Vmax strategy will try to find the maximum VAB that can be injected while keeping IAB within bounds and still being able to measure VMN. This maximises the signal to noise ratio.

System specification and configuration

The specifications for each component model are described in the files for the associated derived class. Depending on the characteristics described, the specifications may include minimum, default and maximum values. The constructor of each component class calls a method that enforces the specifications to remain within the bounds of the component's specifications. The default specifications can be further tightened at the system level. For instance, for the component “power supply”, the model “battery” will have a minimum voltage of 12 V and a maximum voltage of 12 V while the model “dph5005” will have a minimum voltage of 0 V and a maximum voltage of 50 V as specifications.

The configuration specifies which components are used together in a system. It specifies which version of the component, its specifications (e.g., max 5 V), interface (e.g., I2C, Modbus) and other parameters. The configuration file also specifies the options for the loggers and the MQTT interface. A config.py configuration file example for an OhmPi Lite is presented below. Interfaces

The OhmPi can be operated through several interfaces (external layer in Fig. 8). At the lower level, a Python API provided through the methods of the OhmPi class enables custom scripts to be written. On top of that, the MQTT server can send and receive commands that are processed by the Python API. A Node-Red interface can be used to send and receive commands from/to the MQTT server. In addition, a graphical web interface can also be used to send and receive commands from/to the MQTT server and operate the OhmPi. Examples of interfaces will be given in the Operating instructions section.

Build instructions

Step one: Mounting the boards

High-quality soldering is critical to ensure long-term device reliability and prevent intermittent failures due to false contacts. Each solder joint should be visually inspected and verified for continuity using a multimeter. If a joint appears cold or insufficient, it should be melted again to ensure a proper mechanical and electrical connection. Additionally, strict ESD (Electrostatic Discharge) precautions must be followed when handling sensitive components to prevent latent damage. Detailed soldering instructions for the mb.2024 and mux.2024 boards are not provided in this text; instead, users should refer to the Interactive BOMs available in the OSF repository. A comprehensive guide covering soldering procedures and component placement is also maintained on the official website at https://ohmpi.org.

Measurement board

Components should be soldered in increasing order of height, starting with the smallest surface-mount or discrete parts and ending with the tallest elements. Note that the Raspberry Pi header must be soldered to the underside of the measurement board while all other components are soldered on the top side of the board. To enhance repairability and simplify maintenance, most integrated circuits (ICs) and the DC-DC power supplies (THD series) are mounted on DIP sockets. This modular design approach ensures that as many components as possible can be reused across successive OhmPi iterations or replaced if needed, reducing the environmental footprint and overall manufacturing costs.

Mux board

The assembly of the mux.2024 board also followed a progression based on component height. This progression initiates with low-profile parts and is concluded with larger elements, such as the G5LE-1A relays. A key feature of this board is its design flexibility, which is evident in its provision of two switching transistor footprint options (STP16NF06L or ZVN4206A). This feature enables the board to be adapted to the availability of components without the need for modifications to the printed circuit board (PCB). In order to facilitate maintenance, integrated circuits such as the MCP23017 I/O expanders are mounted on DIP sockets, thus ensuring straightforward replacement. The utilisation of a modular architecture, in conjunction with the employment of standard 16-position IDC connectors, facilitates the reusability of modules across diverse OhmPi iterations. This approach serves to minimize manufacturing costs and the environmental impact of the hardware.

The electronics of the OhmPi are sensitive to humidity and temperature change. High humidity associated with cold temperature can cause condensation on the electronics parts, potentially causing short-circuit and damaging the instrument. Overheating of the system can cause the Raspberry Pi to work at a reduced rate or shut down. Protections against humidity and temperature can include: weatherproof enclosure, silicate gel to absorb excessive moisture, conformal coating of PCB, ventilator on the Raspberry Pi, internal electric radiator for cold regions or sun-shield on the entire system for hot conditions. It is up to the user to ensure optimal operating conditions of the instrument for their specific applications. Inspiration for installation can be found in the ‘Example of applications’ section of https://ohmpi.org. The toolbox setup proposed here is adequate for one-time field surveys and if connectors are installed on the side, the box can even stay closed during the acquisition.

Contributing to new hardware developments

The OhmPi system is designed for the integration of alternative or newly developed components. The documentation provides advice and best practices to help developers create new components that work within an OhmPi system and can be adopted by the community.

The simplest case is the development of a new hardware component that complies with the current way the OhmPi Hardware System works. Such developments typically focus on improving an existing component by reducing costs, improving component performance and/or adapting its specifications for specific applications, or designing with easily available parts. The newly created hardware component will expose the minimum functionalities required by the hardware system for this type of component.

Once the hardware has been designed, the next step is to write a new component class based on the relevant abstract or existing derived class. Finally, the new component and its characteristics hava to be added in a configuration file describing the system. For the community to benefit from the contribution, detailed documentation of the hardware and software is essential. Open-source third-party contributions may be proposed for integration into the OhmPi system via the GitLab repository.

To integrate an external component, such as another digital power supply, simply derive a custom class from the original abstract class or a similar component and modify the methods. To improve not only a single component but also how the system functions, it is recommended that an early-stage discussion is initiated with the OhmPi developers. This will help identify the most effective design for seamless integration.

Operating instructions

General safety provision

The OhmPi instrument is able to send lethal current (> 25 mA), please ensure proper use and avoid touching electrodes and cables when the instrument is operating. Situation where electrodes A and B are in short-circuit should also be avoided as the large current that will result from the short-circuit will burn the shunt and damage the measurement board.

Environment and software installation

After all hardware checks have been successfully completed (see online documentation www.ohmpi.org), the battery can be connected. If all is well, the 3 green LEDs (2 on the I2C isolator click and 1 on the current click) should illuminate on the mb.2024. If that’s not the case, check the click boards are well in place and the Traco Power well in their sockets. The activity LED on the raspberry pi will also flicker if it is powered (Fig. 4). Before you can configure OhmPi, you need to install the OS on the Raspberry Pi SD card and clone the OhmPi code from the Gitlab repository. The software has been tested on a Raspbian Bookworm (32-bits) with or without graphical desktop interface. The OhmPi code is a Python code that runs within a virtual Python environment (named ‘ohmpy’ with a ‘y’). It relies on several external Python packages (e.g., Adafruit busio). All packages and the virtual environment are created by running the original./install.sh script located in the cloned repository. This script also enables the I2C interface of the Raspberry Pi and creates a default config.py based on an interactive prompt. This configuration can then be updated with ‘python setup_config.py’ or by manually editing ohmpi/config.py. We redirect the reader to the online documentation for more details.

Troubleshooting

As a fully repairable system, help is provided to troubleshoot issues that can be encountered during operation. With the help of the full-waveform (i.e., all VMN and IAB samples recorded during the current injection and off-time), common issues such as current overflow, voltage overflow, large noise or other issues can be diagnosed. More information is provided in the online documentation for the different cases that can be encountered.

Thermal stability of resistance measurements

To assess the thermal stability of OhmPi, the instrument was placed in temperature-controlled rooms at four different temperatures (approximately −25°C, 8°C, 20°C and 45°C) for a minimum of 5 h to ensure full thermal equilibration of the electronics. Measurements were then performed on a resistive test circuit maintained at a stable temperature of 20.3°C ± 0.5°C throughout the experiment, so that any variation in the measured quantities could be attributed solely to the thermal behaviour of the instrument itself.

Fig. 31 shows the evolution of the injected current (Iab), the measured voltage (Vmn), and the computed resistance (R = Vmn / Iab) as a function of OhmPi‘s operating temperature. As expected, both Iab and Vmn exhibit a slight but inconsistent increase with temperature, reflecting the low thermal dependence of the electronic components involved in current injection and voltage measurement. Notably, however, these two small effects partially compensate each other, so that the resulting resistance R remains remarkably stable across the entire tested temperature range. The measured resistance values consistently fall within ± 0.5% of the true value (100.8 Ω, shown as a dashed red line), with no systematic trend as a function of temperature.Fig. 31A) evolution of the injected current (iAB), b) measured voltage (VMN), and c) computed resistance (R = VMN/ IAB) as a function of OhmPi operating temperature.

A) evolution of the injected current (iAB), b) measured voltage (VMN), and c) computed resistance (R = VMN/ IAB) as a function of OhmPi operating temperature.

These results demonstrate that OhmPi provides thermally stable resistance measurements over a wide operating temperature range, which is a critical requirement for long-term field deployments in varying environmental conditions.

Validation of time domain induced polarization on R-RC circuit

To validate the ability of the Ohmpi system to accurately measure induced polarization, an equivalent RRC electrical circuit was designed and experimentally tested (Fig. 37) to reproduce a polarizable medium. The circuit consists of a series resistor R1=330Ω connected to a parallel branch composed of a resistor R=1000Ω and a capacitor C=260μF. Such a configuration reproduces the transient behavior of a polarizable medium and mimics the charge/discharge response commonly observed in time-domain induced polarization (TDIP) measurements.

The current injected by Ohmpi system corresponds to a square-wave sequence including positive and negative current injections separated by zero-current intervals (Fig. 34a). Processing of resistivity and chargeability dataset

The OhmPi resistivimeter, in addition to providing real-time measurements of injected current and potential differences, also records full time series, allowing for more accurate post-processing. Electrical resistivity tomography was performed at Boissy site in France using a Wenner array configuration. The electrode spacing was set to 1 m, for a total of 64 electrodes. A 50% duty cycle was used, meaning that current was injected for half of the injection period, while the remaining half corresponded to a current-off phase. The injection time was set to 0.5 s. Fig. 36 illustrates an example of a time series acquired at the Boissy site. Fig. 36a shows the measured current as a function of time between electrodes 1 and 10. Current injection starts after a dead time of approximately 0.2 s. The time series highlights an efficient current regulation, as evidenced by stable plateaus and short switching times. The effective injection duration was set to 0.5 s. For this acquisition, the mean effective injection time was 0.4964 s, with a minimum of 0.491 s and a maximum of 0.5 s. This discrepancy is explained by the fact that the effective injection time depends on the timing of the Raspberry Pi control requests. Fig. 36 b shows the resulting potential difference measured between electrodes 4 and 7. The noise level on the potential difference was estimated during the dead time. The measured signal also reveals a non-linear drift of spontaneous polarization. The non-linear drift is mainly caused by residual polarization effects induced by previous current injections, reflecting the slow relaxation of electrochemical and interfacial processes in the subsurface.

Fig. 36c shows the processed potential difference. The potential difference time series were processed using a recursive plus–minus–plus (PMP) filtering approach applied during both current-on and current-off periods, following an adaptation of the time-series processing framework proposed by [42]. This time-domain processing operates directly on the measured current and voltage waveform and aims at reducing slow, non-linear drift associated with spontaneous polarization and residual effects from previous current injections. By combining voltage samples from consecutive half-periods, the recursive PMP filter efficiently suppresses low-frequency trends while preserving the injected signal. This processing is particularly critical for chargeability estimation, as the post-injection potential differences are significantly smaller than the primary voltage used for apparent resistivity computation. The recursive approach allows this processing to reduce the noise level.

After time-series processing, the injected current and the corresponding potential difference were extracted using a fixed delay time (Vdly), ensuring that measurements were taken after transient effects had decayed. In time-domain induced polarization measurements, such delay times are commonly used to avoid early-time transients related to current switching and electrode polarization effects [43]. Apparent chargeability was subsequently computed from the post-injection voltage decay using a chargeability delay time (Mdly) and 20 linearly spaced time windows during the current-off period, following standard time-domain IP methodology [43]. The global chargeability is calculated from these 20-time windows.

Fig. 37 presents the results obtained after time-series processing of the electrical data. Fig. 37 a shows the apparent resistivity pseudosection, which displays a relatively smooth spatial distribution with increasing values at shallow depths and moderate lateral variability along the profile. Fig. 37 b illustrates the apparent chargeability, characterized by overall low amplitudes and a higher sensitivity to noise compared to resistivity, reflecting the weak post-injection voltage levels used for its estimation. In this field experiment, apart from the near-surface layer, chargeability values are low. Fig. 37 c shows the estimated noise level on the measured potential difference, highlighting increased noise at greater pseudo-depths and near the edges of the profile, primarily due to larger potential dipole separations, which result in weaker voltage amplitudes and increased sensitivity to noise. Finally, Fig. 37 d presents the signal-to-noise ratio associated with apparent resistivity measurements, which decreases with depth and electrode separation, reflecting signal attenuation and increasing geometric spreading.Fig. 37Data obtained after time-series processing: (a) apparent resistivity, (b) apparent chargeability, (c) noise level on the measured potential difference, and (d) signal-to-noise ratio, where the signal corresponds to the measured potential difference.

Field monitoring experiment

To further validate the OhmPi system in natural field conditions, we conducted a long-term field monitoring experiment. The main purpose of such experiments is to acquire scheduled, repeated ERT datasets on a same electrode array in order to record changes in resistivity that can be attributed to environmental changes in the subsurface. An OhmPi system was deployed at the Rochefort Cave Observatory (RCO) in Belgium, a site where hydrological research has been conducted for several years. The acquisition system was installed in a shed supplied with mains power. The Rx module was powered via a 220 VAC to 12 VDC power supply, while the Tx module was powered via a 12 V battery, connected to a battery charger. An external relay switch ensured the battery was charged only when the system was not acquiring data, to avoid any issue on the battery charger.

A 16-electrode array was deployed at the surface of the forested soil along a 6.0 m long transect with 0.4 m electrode spacing. The electrodes consisted of 10 cm long stainless-steel threaded rods, mechanically connected via single-wire cables using two stainless steel nuts. All electrode cables were laid out in a shallow trench and routed back to the OhmPi system.

Python scripts were scheduled via the Raspberry Pi built-in scheduler crontab, to collect individual acquisitions every 3 h over a 14-month period. Each individual acquisition consisted of 384 injections using a multi-gradient array configuration, which resulted over 3000 datasets collected during this monitoring period, exceeding 1 million single injections. Fig. 38 summarises the ERT results at daily intervals, i.e., a subset comprising 393 datasets acquired every day at 00:00 UTC. Data processing, including filtering and time-lapse inversion was done using the open-source software ResIPy [33]. The baseline resistivity, i.e., resistivity distribution of the initial time-step, shows a thin conductive layer (blue tones) overlying a more resistive, thicker layer (yellow tones). This is consistent with existing knowledge of the site and corresponds to a thin soil layer over a weathered limestone bedrock, as already reported for the RCO site [35].Fig. 38A. baseline resistivity model of the initial time-step acquired on the 15 june 2024 at the rco site. b) Daily rainfall and soil moisture at two different depths acquired at the rco site. c) Percent change in resistivity as compared to the baseline resistivity for the superficial layer (average resistivity for all model cells < 20 cm deep) and a deeper layer (average resistivity for all model cells > 50 cm deep). d) Percent change in resistivity as compared with the baseline resistivity during four 1-week periods.

The temporal evolution of resistivity, as shown in Fig. 38c and d, follows a seasonal pattern, with increasing resistivity during the summer months (typically from May to October at the RCO) and lower resistivity during the winter months (November to March). Short-term, more pronounced resistivity drops are also observed following rainfall events. Both the seasonal resistivity variations, driven by vegetation activity and evapotranspiration, and the short-term resistivity variations, driven by rainfall events, are strongly linked to soil moisture dynamics, as evidenced via data from co-located soil moisture sensors installed at two depths (Fig. 38b). Overall, these results demonstrate the reliability of the OhmPi system for long-term acquisition and its applicability for field-scale ERT monitoring experiments. Limitations related to the power injection capability (50 V, 5 A) of the DPH5005 module mean that the signal-to-noise ratio of the measured voltages can become too low as the size of the electrode array increases. Therefore, careful consideration is required when designing experiments at larger scales.

Conclusion

The OhmPi project and the OhmPi Lite configuration presented in this manuscript are the result of a collaborative effort of core developers and user feedback. Started as a DIY instrument with the OhmPi V1.00, OhmPi v2024 with its upgraded specifications and broader range of applications, aims to bridge the instrumentation gap suited for environmental monitoring surveys; hence, providing information at relevant spatial and temporal scales. The modularity of the OhmPi v2024 combined with its extensive documentation, puts users first and allow to build, deploy, repair and upgrade their instrument. The low-tech (and often low-cost) nature of the OhmPi, along with open-source inversion codes, further increases the accessibility of geoelectrical imaging technology. Further development of the OhmPi hardware will focus on upgrading the ADC to possibly a multi-channel system to improve the imaging of fast processes happening in the subsurface.
