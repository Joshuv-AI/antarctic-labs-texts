Primary biliary cholangitis mortality in Europe: a temporal and spatial view across the period 2002–2022
Authors: Angelica Ortega-Torres, Germán Sánchez-Díaz, Ana Villaverde-Hueso, Greta Arias-Merino, Verónica Alonso-Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a rare autoimmune disease of multifactorial origin with an uneven incidence across countries. This study sought to identify temporal trends in PBC mortality and describe possible spatial patterns across Europe.MethodsData on PBC mortality (ICD-10 K74.3) and European populations by age group in 20 countries were sourced from the World Health Organization for the period 2002–2022. We calculated age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) for the period 2012–2022, both for individual countries and overall. Trends were assessed using Joinpoint regression analysis. To ascertain the geographical distribution, standardised mortality ratios (SMR) were calculated for the periods 2002–2011 and 2012–2022.ResultsA total of 12,934 PBC deaths (81.65% women) were recorded during the period 2002–2022. Mortality showed a downward trend in most countries except for Hungary, Türkiye, Romania and Poland. Lithuania registered the highest AAMR, at 3.47 per 1,000,000 population (95% CI 2.86–4.19), followed by Hungary and the United Kingdom (UK). During the period 2002–2011, the risk of death in northern countries was twice as high as expected (Finland, Lithuania and the UK with SMRs of 2.09 to 2.14). The differences between northern and southern Europe persisted during 2012–2022, albeit with some variations.ConclusionsPBC mortality is decreasing or remains stable in most European countries. Risk of death is higher in countries in the north and north-west. There is a need for further in-depth study of the causes of the geographical variability detected.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28178-3.

Background

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), formerly known as primary biliary cirrhosis until 2014 [1], is characterised by the slow, autoimmune-mediated destruction of the bile ducts, which may eventually lead to non-alcoholic liver cirrhosis [2]. It is a rare disease (RD), as it has a prevalence of fewer than five cases per 10,000 population in the European Union [3]. It mainly affects middle-aged women and can progress to terminal liver diseases [4]. PBC is progressive, progressing from the initial bile duct lesion to cirrhosis, and the disease course can be divided into three periods: (1) an asymptomatic stage, with a duration of over 20 years; (2) a symptomatic stage, with a duration of five to ten years, marked by the existence of pruritus, asthenia and/or slightly elevated bilirubin levels; and (3) a pre-terminal stage of short duration, characterised by intense icterus [5]. The most characteristic immunological alteration is the presence of antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA) [6]. The diagnosis is confirmed when the patient fulfils at least two of the following criteria: elevated serum alkaline phosphatase levels, presence of highly specific AMA in serum, and/or liver histology consistent with the disease [7]. Ursodeoxycholic acid is the only accepted treatment that prolongs survival without liver transplant [8]. Although the median survival of those affected is less than that of the general population, survival among those affected by PBC is longer in asymptomatic than in symptomatic patients [9].

Global studies on PBC incidence indicate a value of 1.8 per 100,000 population, while prevalence is 14.6, though estimates differ due in great measure to study characteristics or geographical inequalities [10]. Sequential studies have reported a general increase in prevalence, but vary with regard to incidence depending on the geographical area [11, 12]. Most estimates of epidemiological indicators have been made in Europe and North America, the regions with the highest prevalence and incidence, albeit with substantial differences between countries [10, 13–16].

The growing interest in advances in the diagnosis, treatment and epidemiological indicators of PBC can be seen in many epidemiology articles published in the last decade. Even so, few studies have focused on analysing the mortality trends, a concept closely linked to survival [17]. Furthermore, there are differences of opinion about the aetiology of this RD, with both genetic and environmental factors having been proposed [15]. The possible role of environmental triggers in the development of the disease could account for substantial geographical differences, which are already known through certain epidemiological indicators [10]. Hence, calculating spatial inequalities in epidemiological indicators and mapping the results obtained is a valid approach to investigating the aetiology of this RD. Understanding the geographical distribution and temporal trends of PBC mortality is essential for identifying potential disparities in diagnosis, healthcare access, and disease management across countries. Moreover, analysing these patterns provides insights into the epidemiology of the disease, helps to generate hypotheses about potential risk factors, and supports the development of targeted public health strategies.

The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate trends in PBC mortality in European countries, compare the age-adjusted mortality rates, and identify spatial patterns in the risk of death.

Methods

Data on mortality and European population data by 5-year age groups were obtained from the most recent World Health Organization (WHO) update, issued in February 2026 [18]. We selected all records with data on PBC deaths coded as K74.3 according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). In line with the availability and consistency of annual data reported to the WHO, information on deaths was retrieved for the following 20 European countries: Austria, Belgium, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Norway, Romania, Sweden, Switzerland, Spain, Poland, Türkiye, and the United Kingdom (UK) (see Supplementary Materials S1 and S2 for details of the country selection criteria based on WHO data).

The temporal analysis of mortality, both overall and by sex, was conducted by country for the period 2002–2022, with the exception of Finland (1996–2015) and Italy (2003–2022) and Türkiye (2009–2022), due to differences in data availability. Time trends were analysed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (desktop version 5.3.0.0; National Cancer Institute, USA), which identifies points where statistically significant changes in trend occur and quantifies their magnitude over time [19]. Annual percent changes (APC) and average annual percent changes (AAPC) were estimated [20]. Model selection was performed using the Grid Search method, allowing for a maximum of three joinpoints. The optimal number of joinpoints was determined using a Monte Carlo permutation test. A minimum of two observations was required between each joinpoint and the ends of the data, and at least four consecutive observations were required between joinpoints. The selection of the final model was based on statistical significance, with an overall significance level set at p < 0.05. Countries were included in this analysis only if they had at least one recorded case of PBC per year throughout a consecutive period, which ensured the stability of the estimates. This modelling approach allows for changes in temporal trends by fitting piecewise log-linear models, which are particularly suitable for our data given the non-normal distribution of the data and the relatively small number of cases. Moreover, log-linear models express trends as constant APC, providing an interpretable metric that facilitates comparisons across populations with markedly different baseline rates, which would be less appropriate under linear models [19, 21]. This approach is particularly useful in the context of RD or small numbers.

Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were calculated for the period 2012–2022 for each country (except Finland) and for all countries combined using the ageadjust.direct function from the epitools R package, using a shorter time frame to maximise the inclusion of European countries with available annual data. Direct standardisation was applied to allow comparability of mortality rates across countries with different age structures by removing the confounding effect of the age distribution. Age-specific mortality rates, calculated for standard 5-year age groups, were applied to the 2013 European Standard Population (ESP) [22], using predefined age-group weights, with results expressed per 1,000,000 population. The same function was used to compute 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) based on a gamma distribution. This approach was selected because age-adjusted rates are derived from Poisson-distributed counts and may exhibit skewness, particularly when the number of events is small. Gamma-based confidence intervals provide better coverage and avoid negative lower bounds, making them more appropriate than normal approximations in this context [23].

To assess the geographical distribution, standardised mortality ratios (SMRs) were calculated for two periods (2002–2011 and 2012–2022) using an indirect standardisation approach. Expected deaths were estimated by applying age-specific mortality rates from the reference population to the age structure of each country, defined in standard 5-year age groups, and SMRs were obtained as the ratio of observed to expected deaths [24]. 95% CIs were calculated using the exact Poisson method, which provides more accurate interval estimation than normal approximation methods, particularly when the number of observed events is small [25]. These results were then mapped using QGIS software (version 3.40.15).

Results

A total of 12,934 PBC deaths (10,560 women, 81.65%) were recorded in the analysed countries. The UK was the country with the most recorded cases (n = 3,257), while Germany, Italy, France and Spain had more than one thousand cases. These five countries represented 65.93% of deaths. Among men, the UK and Germany had the highest number of cases (30.75% of the total). Among women, the UK, Germany and France recorded over 5,000 cases (49.55% of the total).

Table 1 shows significant AAMR trends by country for the period 2002–2022, with the exception of Finland, Italy and Türkiye, which had different study periods. In both sexes, Türkiye registered an annual 13.46% increase (p = 0.016), Romania 9.40% (p < 0.001), Hungary 5.44% (p < 0.002) and Poland 3.15% (p < 0.024) in PBC mortality. In contrast, the greatest decreases were observed in Finland -4.38% (p < 0.001), France − 3.01% (p = 0.008), the UK -2.64% (p < 0.001), Sweden − 2.39% (p = 0.016), and Germany − 1.86% (p = 0.032). Figure 2 shows the spatial variability in the SMR per country, over two periods (2002–2011 and 2012–2022). Geographically, the highest risk of death due to PBC was observed in northern Europe. In the first period, Lithuania, Finland, and the UK recorded SMRs approximately twice those expected for Europe as a whole (ranging from 2.09 to 2.18). In contrast, the lower mortality risks were detected in Austria, Romania, and the Netherlands (SMRs ranging from 0.23 to 0.43). Across the period 2012–2022, differences between northern and southern Europe persisted, albeit with some variations: the highest risks continued to be observed in the UK, Lithuania and Hungary, with SMRs ranging from 1.94 to 2.79; the lowest were found in Austria, the Netherlands and Belgium, with values ranging from 0.34 to 0.57.

In women, the spatial distribution was similar to that observed for both sexes in the period 2002–2011. Among men, the distribution was different. In the first period, the highest risks were observed in Lithuania (SMR = 4.05), the Czech Republic and Sweden (both with SMRs of around 2.00). The lowest risks were observed in Austria, Romania and Spain (0.34, 0.50, and 0.60, respectively). During the period 2012–2022, there were substantial variations in the risk of death, with Romania, Hungary and Lithuania showing SMRs of 2.54–3.78. In contrast, Austria, the Netherlands, and Latvia had the lowest risk of death due to PBC, with SMRs of 0.39, 0.41 and 0.47, respectively.

Discussion

The national mortality statistics supplied by the WHO are an excellent data source for calculating epidemiological indicators, analysing time trends, and making comparisons between countries, as has already been done for other RDs at the European level [21]. Epidemiologically speaking, there are increasingly more studies and comparative analyses on PBC prevalence and incidence. However, no studies have focused on mortality and its spatial distribution in Europe until this study. This study provides a comprehensive overview of the geographical and temporal patterns of PBC mortality in Europe, contributing to a better understanding of potential inequalities and underlying determinants of the disease. These findings offer relevant insights into the epidemiology of PBC and may help inform future research and public health strategies aimed at reducing disparities across countries.

In Europe, PBC is more common in women, with the male-to-female ratio of 1:4. A breakdown by country shows that these differences are minimal in Romania (1:1) and greatest in Latvia and the UK (1:9 and 1:6, respectively in 2012–2022) [17]. Similarly, in our study, mortality reflects this predominance in women, perhaps explained by genetic factors linked to the X chromosome, hormonal mechanisms, and/or specific humoral responses in females [16]. A meta-analysis revealed that these differences have also been observed in prevalence and incidence, which were four to six times higher in women [14].

Our study shows evidence of a reduction in PBC mortality at the European level. The majority of countries experienced a decrease or remained stable throughout the period, with Finland, France and the UK showing the largest decreases. For instance, mortality in the Netherlands showed a stable trend, in contrast to the increase in incidence and prevalence observed from 2000 to 2008 [13]. In Sweden, where mortality decreased by 2.39% annually, prevalence increased significantly from 1987 to 2014 according to other studies [26]. This downward trend in mortality could be related to improvements in diagnostic tools and the availability of highly effective treatments, such as ursodeoxycholic acid and emerging treatments [27–30], which, if administered in the early stages of the disease, may improve the prognosis of this RD. In any case, the increase in PBC prevalence has also been observed globally for other autoimmune liver diseases and has had a significant impact on disease-related costs, due in particular to exacerbation of cirrhosis and the need for liver transplant [31–33]. The increase in mortality observed in Türkiye, Romania, Hungary and Poland in the total population, as well as in sex-specific analyses, might reflect an improvement in PBC coding practices in these countries. Another factor to consider is that the mortality patterns may differ between patients with or without symptoms, since a study in Japan reported that asymptomatic patients over 55 years of age displayed a mortality pattern similar to that of the general population [34]. In contrast, it has been reported that diagnosis of PBC in symptomatic patients is associated with rapid disease progression and worse prognosis. This might be reflected in countries like Türkiye or Romania, where healthcare expenditure is lower than in other European countries with more advanced health systems, and where delays in diagnosis and late treatment may contribute to this rise in mortality.

The geographical analysis shows variability in the risk of death due to PBC in Europe. The highest mortality was observed in northern Europe, particularly in the Scandinavian Peninsula and the UK. In the latter case, a retrospective cohort study has shown that the incidence of both PBC and autoimmune hepatitis rises with an increase in latitude in the UK, with incidence thus being higher in Scotland than in the south of England [35]. This finding raises the question of whether variations in exposure to ultraviolet light cause lower vitamin D levels and, in association with other genetic risks, might modulate the risk of developing PBC. Nevertheless, exposure to sunlight is greater in Mediterranean Basin countries like Spain, Italy and Türkiye. Even so, women in Spain have a higher-than-expected mortality risk than those in other countries situated farther north, such as Germany or the Netherlands, which may suggest a multifactorial trigger not exclusively related to latitude. In Spain, plotting the risk of death due to PBC at a regional level shows considerable territorial variation, though it does not appear to indicate a higher risk with increasing latitude [36]. On the other hand, studies conducted in Greece and Northern Ireland report important geographical differences in epidemiological indicators [37, 38]. As yet, very little is known about the environmental factors that may influence the development of PBC, with some studies reporting a higher risk associated with urinary tract infections, use of hair dyes, exposure to smoking, or poor environmental sanitation conditions in childhood [39, 40].

Genetic predisposition and ethnicity are factors that have also been studied at a continental and regional level. In the latter case, some studies suggest that First Nation populations in British Columbia (Canada) have a higher incidence of PBC than those in other regions [11, 41]. It would be of great interest to identify the different factors involved and their degree of influence on variations in mortality at the country level in Europe, in order to hypothesise possible differences in disease behaviour. The observed geographical differences, particularly in Eastern European countries, should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect not only true epidemiological variation but also differences in healthcare access, diagnostic practices, and disease awareness. Previous studies have highlighted that outcomes in PBC depend on early diagnosis and timely treatment initiation, which are influenced by socioeconomic and healthcare provider-related factors, with ursodeoxycholic acid remaining the first-line treatment for PBC [10]. Hence, larger-scale and/or nationwide studies are needed to investigate and explain the geographical differences found.

With respect to the limitations of this study, it should be noted that the year of adoption of the ICD-10 for mortality coding was not the same for all countries. It should be stressed that PBC is a disease coded at the four-digit level in ICD-10, but that some countries only report data to the WHO at the third-digit level, thus limiting the number of countries included. Furthermore, PBC diagnosis relies on specific immunological and biochemical markers and, in some cases, histological confirmation, which may not always be available or accurately recorded in death certificates. This diagnostic complexity may lead to misclassification or underreporting in mortality data. Although ICD-10 code K74.3 specifically identifies PBC, some degree of misclassification cannot be excluded due to limitations in cause-of-death attribution and diagnostic accuracy on death certificates. The change in nomenclature from “primary biliary cirrhosis” to “primary biliary cholangitis” may also affect temporal comparability, as coding systems such as ICD-10 do not fully reflect this distinction [1, 42]. These factors may introduce a degree of classification bias and should be considered when interpreting the observed patterns. Lastly, the availability at the country level meant that internal regional inequalities in the risk of death could not be analysed. Moreover, the relatively small number of deaths may introduce statistical instability, particularly when stratified by country and sex, increasing random variability and reducing the reliability of both crude counts and age-adjusted rates. Consequently, some of the observed temporal patterns may reflect random fluctuation rather than true epidemiological changes, and SMR-based comparisons should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Finally, one strength of this study is the use of a uniform methodology, thanks to reliance on a single database for death and population statistics. This ensures stability over time and will make it possible to increase both the number of cases and the scope of research on this RD as more countries adopt four-digit ICD-10 coding.

Conclusions

PBC mortality is higher in women, though with a ratio lower than that reported in prevalence and incidence studies. Most countries show a downward or stable mortality trend, probably due to improvements in earlier diagnosis and better treatment. Countries in the north and north-west show a higher risk of death due to PBC than that expected for Europe as a whole. These geographical differences might be linked to a combination of genetic and environmental factors, thus making it essential to continue investigating through additional epidemiological studies to better understand the aetiology and geographical distribution of this disease.
