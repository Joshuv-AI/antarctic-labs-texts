Scaling laws in confined media applied for biomarker detection
Authors: Yuhua Cai, Benjamin Cressiot, Mathias Winterhalter, Sébastien Balme, Juan Pelta, Laurent Bacri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biological processes often imply dynamics in crowded or confined media. Here, we focus on the entry of molecules into nanopores relevant for applications in health, biotechnologies, or the environment. Polymers in the semi-dilute regime that are excluded from the nanopore generate osmotic pressure, allowing controlled entry of analytes and facilitating their detection and identification inside a confined media. Using solid-state nanopores, we first study the entry dynamics of neutral polymers in a semi-dilute solution at the single-molecule level. We investigate the influence of polymer concentration, chain length and nanopore size. Second, we study the detection and identification of polysaccharides of various molecular weights in the presence of large polymers. Third, we discriminate between two peptide biomarkers with an enantiomeric difference in only one amino acid (D- or L-) for different stoichiometric ratios. From these examples, we experimentally demonstrate the universal behavior that drives large polymers into a nanopore. When the mesh size of the semi-dilute solution is smaller than the nanopore size, the osmotic energy overcomes the confinement energy, allowing the chain to enter the confined media. We found that the power-law dependency of the threshold concentration for polymer entry on the ratio between nanopore and monomer size is predicted theoretically by Pierre-Gilles de Gennes. Additionally, the depletion layer thickness depends on the screening length (mesh size), revealing three regimes that rely on the ratio between the pore size and the screening length. The application of polymer scaling laws represents a universal approach to control the dynamics of biomolecules through nanopores.

Introduction

Crowded or confined media cause deviations from simple linear models and thus require more complex descriptions. Understanding the dynamics of confined media has long interested physicists and biologists, resulting in significant amounts of theoretical and experimental work related to societal challenges1. For instance, a current challenge is understanding the movement of molecular species in confined environments to solve the differences by several orders of magnitude between the theoretical expectation and the collected energy, making osmotic energy generation (blue energy) possible and becoming a promising energy source for the transition toward zero emissions2. Related problems with diffusion in confinement influence biological functions and pathological processes3–7, involving water and ions8,9, neutral macromolecules10 or charged molecules11–13. Different kinds of confined media have been tested in this context, such as microfluidic systems14, intracellular environments15, or nanopores16. The increasing age of the population combined with advanced nanotechnology leads to new rapid, affordable, sensitive, real-time, portable diagnostic methods to prevent human diseases or monitor their progression. Understanding confined media also helps to address significant health challenges. Recently, a protein channel was used to discriminate different conformations of peptide biomarkers in a complex media, serum17. Nanopores or micropipettes under crowding conditions showed that detecting biomolecules such as DNA18,19, proteins20, RNA conformations21, peptides22, and amyloids23 could be enhanced20,22. The polymer that creates crowding conditions modifies interactions between the analytes and the pore and their transport properties.

Here, we extend previous theories in polymer physics and experimentally investigate the entry of biomolecules into the confined environment of a nanopore10,13,24. From a polymer physics point of view, crowding at the nanopore entrance corresponds to semi-dilute polymer solutions where polymers overlap and are highly entangled. In this regime, the polymer chains adopt a new conformation of a chain network that is characterized by a mesh size ξ. In these conditions, volume-excluded interactions are screened25. The mesh size ξ of the network can be related to monomer concentration c, using de Gennes’ theory. If the mesh size ξ is smaller than the nanopore size D (ξ ≤ D), the osmotic force overcomes the confinement force, allowing the chain to enter the nanopore. The threshold concentration cth required for chain entry and subsequent detection in the nanopore equals (D/a)−4/3, where a is the monomer size. Despite early work using a protein channel (α-hemolysin) with a fixed pore opening, the predicted scaling law as a function of pore size has never been experimentally validated, nor has the concept of a depletion layer in the nanopore26.

We first investigate the dynamics of neutral polymers in the semi-dilute regime entering a solid-state nanopore as a function of the polymer concentration, chain length, and nanopore size. We confirmed that for mass concentrations above c% ≅ cth where the mesh size ξ is smaller than the nanopore size D, the polymer chain enters the nanopore25. One experimentally accessible parameter is the reduction in ion current due to the chain entry, which is directly related to the polymer depletion layer inside the channel. As predicted by De Gennes’ hypothesis of a depletion layer in the channel, we further find experimentally that its thickness depends on the screening length ξ.

In a second application, we use polymers in the semi-dilute regime to enhance the detection of both short and long polysaccharide chains. We analyze their conformations, adopting the model of blob sequences within the pore25. The flexibility of the long polymer in the nanopore creates a void volume necessary for the ion flow.

In a third demonstration, we discriminate between short peptide biomarkers differing by a single amino acid (d- or l-) and for different stoichiometric ratios. We evaluate the decrease in the confinement energy barrier for these peptides due to the osmotic energy. These findings open new perspectives on using solid-state nanopores to identify biomarkers, peptides, proteins, and sugars with subtle modifications in an environment that mimics a macromolecular medium.

Results and discussion

Polymer entry into SiNx nanopore under semi-dilute conditions

In the first series of experiments, we studied polymer entry in the semi-dilute regime (Fig.1). To elucidate the pore size effect, we used commercial 12 nm thin SiNx membranes and fabricated single pores with sizes in the 2–8 nm range using dielectric breakdown (Fig. S1)27–30. The SiNx membrane separates two compartments, filled with electrolyte. Application of a transmembrane voltage drives ions through the nanopore, which can be recorded. When a macromolecule interacts with or enters the nanopore, we can detect a transient decrease of the ionic current31 with a typical amplitude, duration, or frequency characteristic (Fig. S2)17. The conformation of polymer chains strongly depends on their concentrations as described in Fig. S3. Briefly, at low concentrations in the dilute regime, polymer chains adopt a coil conformation25,32. The concentration increase leads to the overlapping of the polymer chains. Figure 1c shows the case where the pore size D = 2 nm is smaller than the mesh size ξ, whereas Fig. 1d–e shows the situation where D > ξ (respectively D = 4 nm and D = 7.8 nm). At this mass fraction of PEG, we did not observe any current blockades when the pore is 2 nm in diameter. On the other hand, we observe an increasing number of blockades for pores larger than 4 nm.

To understand these observations, we varied the concentration and chain size of the neutral polymer for three pore sizes. First, we focused on the blockade frequencies to better understand the confinement mechanisms. We determined the event frequency by fitting the distribution of the inter-event times ti with an exponential function (Figs. S4 and S5). Figure 1f–h describes the results when the polymer concentration (mass fraction) is increased up to 25 wt%. We found the same behavior for each experimental condition in Fig. 1f–h: (1) we do not observe any blockades at low PEG concentrations (Fig. S6), even though a high voltage (–700 mV) is applied. In this situation, the electroosmotic flow is insufficient to overcome the energy barrier of confinement. (2) Above the threshold concentration cTh, we observe several blockades. We use a –700 mV voltage to optimize the number of blockades (Fig. S5), hereby obtaining, on the one hand, sufficient blockades to achieve a good statistical sample and, on the other, a good signal-to-noise ratio. The blockade frequency increases linearly with c independent of the nanopore size (Fig. S7). The observed linear behavior is independent of the cation nature Li+ or K+. These results show a characteristic osmotic energy dominating the entry of molecules inside the pore, and interestingly, it is not dominated by interactions with the pore. Although the slopes extracted are the same and independent of the size of the nanopore, they vary slightly according to the electrolyte nature: 0.60 ± 0.03 Hz in LiCl and 0.40 ± 0.03 Hz in KCl. As the Debye length is larger than the mesh size, we can suppose that the interactions between the PEG chains and K+ cations could reduce their mobility. The presented data suggest that the polymer chain entry into the nanopore can not be described by a simple diffusion law.

The concentration asymmetry between the compartments filled with polymer chains outside the nanopore and the empty region inside the nanopore leads to an osmotic pressure. We plotted the threshold concentration cTh as a function of the ratio D/a in Fig. 2b. A power law fit resulted in cTh = (1.00 ± 0.08) (D/a)(–1.32 ± 0.01), which is in surprisingly good agreement with the theoretical hypotheses from Eq. 2. In this model, the nanopore is supposed to be neutral. Nevertheless, the SiN nanopores are negatively charged, leading to an electroosmotic flow33,34. In Fig. S9, we detail the calculation of the electroosmotic force, whose magnitude is fEO = 4.5 ± 1 pN. Since this force is smaller than the osmotic force, the confinement barrier is mainly overcome by the osmotic pressure, as suggested by the experimental results. The same behavior is observed in both LiCl and KCl electrolyte solutions33,34.

From the force balance, we conclude that the mesh size ξ must be similar or smaller than the pore size D to allow the chains to enter the nanopore. To show this relationship, we calculated on one hand the mesh size from Eq. 2 at the transition ξTh ≅ ξ(cTh) and on the other hand, the pore size D by solving Eq. S1. This equation shows the relationship between the nanopore conductance G and the pore size D. Figure 2c shows that the mesh size ξTh linearly increases with the pore size D. In light of these findings, we wondered whether the study of the blockade durations could be exploited to better understand the dynamics of these polymer chains in a semi-dilute regime.

We explored how the nanopore or polymer sizes could influence the dwell times. The dwell time is defined as the duration between the start of a blockade’s current drop and the start of its rise (Fig. S2)35. Figure S11 shows the normalized blockade of each blockade according to its dwell time. We observe two populations. The first one represents the majority conformation, which we take into account in this work. In the second minority population, the polymer chains could adopt a second conformation characterized by dwell times mainly shorter than 100 µs, which could be attributed to bumping events. Fig. S8 shows the dwell time distribution of the 25 wt% 35 kDa PEG. It is fitted by two exponential functions ∝ exp(−t/τ) characterized by two characteristic times: a short one τ1 = 77 ± 35 µs attributed to bumping events12,17 and a long one τ2 = 402 ± 65 µs attributed to the polymer chains inside the nanopore26. We observe that dwell times increase with the polymer concentration in Fig. 2d. In contrast, we could expect a decrease attributed to the increase of the osmotic force. The viscosity of the solution could explain this counterintuitive behavior. In a semi-dilute regime, the chains are entangled in a dense network and can move by reptation (insert in Fig. 2d). As the polymer chain length is longer than the membrane thickness, we can suppose that the reptation time τrep dominates the dwell times25,26. When the chain dwells inside the nanopore, the osmotic pressure between the inside and outside of the nanopore vanishes. As the SiN is negatively charged, the influence of the electroosmotic flow during the dwelling of the polymer chain should be discussed. As demonstrated by the experimental data, the dissipation mainly occurs outside the nanopore, meaning that the dynamics are primarily governed by reptation. We can therefore assume that the influence of the electroosmotic flow is not dominant. This was supported by recent simulations showing that the electroosmotic flow is reduced when a molecule dwells in the nanopore36. Thus, we could suppose that a similar behavior occurs in the SiN nanopore. We now focus on the conformation of the chains inside the nanopore during the dynamics.

Given that the blockade frequency, f, can be described by an Arrhenius law11,12, f = f0 fV fΠ exp(–U⋆/kBT). The pre-exponential term represents the frequency factor, and the exponential factor is the confinement energy barrier, U⋆, which molecules must overcome to enter the nanopore. The frequency factor fV is given by the exponential function: fV = exp(q|V|/kBT), V being the applied voltage, q the effective charge of the Dextran Sulfate, and kB is the Boltzmann constant11. This frequency factor represents a reduced barrier due to the applied voltage. Similarly, the frequency factor fΠ is also related to a reduction factor EΠ of the barrier due to the osmotic pressure, represented by the function fΠ = exp(EΠ/kBT). The energy EΠ can be evaluated by calculating the ratio between the blockade frequencies designated as fi in the diluted and ff in semi-dilute conditions. Subsequently, this energy can be expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{\varPi }={{\mathrm{ln}}}\left(\frac{{f}_{f}}{{f}_{i}}\right){k}_{B}T$$\end{document}EΠ=lnfffikBT, leading to EΠ= (2.0 ± 0.3) kBT for DS8kDa.

We observe relatively long blockade durations of τ8kDa = 935 ± 20 μs. This could be understood as being caused by a hard sphere of chains packed in a tube of diameter Dt and length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L=9.4\, \textrm{nm}$$\end{document}L=9.4nm (detailed calculation in Supplementary Fig. S12). This result is similar to the one obtained above and shows that the osmotic pressure energy does not depend on the length of the dextran sulfate chains. As previously observed, the semi-dilute PEG chains screen the confinement barrier (≈ 2 kBT).

These blockade levels correspond to a completely unfolded chain inside the nanopore. We suppose that the polysaccharide chains migrate by reptation within the network of PEG chains, forming a series of blobs within a depletion cylinder inside the pore (Fig. S12). Similar blockade amplitudes for the two chain lengths suggest the chains have the same conformation within the nanopore, leading to the same reduction of the ion current. Only the 8 kDa DS has some additional, deeper blockades (13 ± 4%) corresponding to 2 monomer blobs (Fig.S11). This data suggests that the dynamics of the DS chains are not dominated by dissipation in the nanopore, unlike the long PEG chains above. Then, these dynamics of DS chains are governed by the dissipation in the PEG network outside the nanopore. The semi-dilute chain conformation outside the nanopore primarily governs the dynamics of the polyelectrolyte chain within the nanopore.

In biology, it is crucial to discriminate enantiomers because biological systems are chiral. Proteins recognize and interact with molecules based on their spatial orientation. An l-peptide may be biologically active, while its d-enantiomer might be inactive or have entirely different effects38. Can the same approach based on the osmotic pressure be used to sense small peptide biomarkers that differ by a single amino acid (l- or d-Arg)17? In such a scenario, the transport process should be predominantly governed by dissipation within the nanopore.

The depletion layer proposed by P. G. de Gennes25 can explain these unexpected results on the broader distribution of events for l-AVP. Due to this depletion, each peptide must cross against high friction during transport. As previously described, the peptide must first overcome an energy barrier to enter the nanopore to be transported13. Consequently, peptide transport is governed by applied potential. Depending on the peptide-nanopore interactions, each peptide must explore its energy landscape. d-AVP explores fewer conformations during transport than l-AVP, which is distributed over a broader range. Such a broadening was also observed using an aerolysin nanopore17.

In this work, we generate an osmotic force in solid-state nanopores using semi-dilute solutions of polymer chains. When the mesh size ξ is smaller than the nanopore size, the osmotic force overcomes the confinement force, allowing the polymer chains to enter the nanopore. We demonstrated experimentally de Gennes’s prediction on the threshold concentration as a function of the pore size. We confirmed the chain reptation dynamics through the pores. Furthermore, we experimentally verified the concept of a depletion layer inside the nanopore thanks to single-molecule ion current characterization. This mechanism allowed us to apply osmotic pressure that favors the detection of complex biomolecules such as polysaccharides and peptide biomarkers, hardly detectable in diluted conditions, using solid-state nanopores. By doing so, we confirmed the transport of charged macromolecules adopting blob conformations, as reported previously for related polymers12,41. The semi-dilute experimental conditions allowed us to discriminate l-AVP and d-AVP differing by a single enantiomeric amino acid, which has never been achieved using solid-state nanopores.

This approach could be universally applied to detecting hardly detectable complex biomolecules for diagnosis purposes. Decreasing the confinement energy barrier facilitates their detection by increasing blockade frequency. By this approach, the number of events is enhanced, leading to a more accurate identification or analysis of the biomolecules. One of the most significant challenges for protein/peptide detection through solid-state nanopores is their fast translocation, which creates a loss of blockade events42. The approach we developed might provide a better temporal resolution with an increase in the dwell times compared to dilute conditions, allowing a better identification of molecules.

Methods

Solid-state nanopore fabrication by automated controlled dielectric breakdown (CDB)

Nanopores are drilled in 12 nm thick SiNx membranes (NXDB-50H405 V122, Norcada). The SiNx membranes are cleaned after immersion in a piranha solution (H2SO4:H2O2, 3:1) at approximately 90 °C for 15 min. After cleaning, the chips are rinsed with Milli-Q water. The cleaned SiNx chips are then mounted into microfluidic cells sold by Northern Nanopore. This device is flushed by 60 μL of Propan-2-ol to remove air bubbles before filling with the “fabrication buffer” (see below).

The setup is connected to the current amplifier via Ag/AgCl electrodes and placed inside a Faraday cage before starting the nanopore fabrication. Nanopore fabrication is achieved through a controlled dielectric breakdown method using the Spark-E2 system (Northern Nanopore Instruments)29. We follow a two-step process:Fabrication Step: The fabrication buffer consists of a solution of 1 M KCl, 8.3 mM HEPES pH 8. Firstly, a voltage ramp from 0 V to –5 V is applied for 1 min 30 s. Secondly, a second ramp from –5 V to –14 V (slope −3 V/min) is applied until a breakdown is automatically detected by the software.Conditioning Step: The conditioning buffer consists of a solution of 1.5 M NaCl and 8.3 mM HEPES pH 8.5 cycles of a triangular signal (±3 V, period of 4 s) are applied repeatedly until the target nanopore diameter (2–7.8 nm) is reached29.

After each fabrication, each nanopore is characterized by measuring its conductance in a 1 M KCl solution with 5 mM HEPES, pH 7.4.

Nanopore characterization

After each fabrication, the nanopore is characterized using I-V curves from –200 mV to +200 mV (Fig. S1). Current measurements and data acquisition were performed with the eONE Plus (Elements, Cesena, Italy) at a sampling rate of 200 kHz. The signal was filtered with a 5 kHz cut-off frequency. Two Ag/AgCl electrodes are used to apply the voltage and measure the ionic current. The cis chamber is connected to ground. Note: We placed the flat side of the SiNx in the short side of the microfluidic holder and connected it to the ground side of the amplifier. All asymmetric additions were added to the ground side of the microfluidic holder.

Current trace recording

The current traces are measured by an Axopatch 200B amplifier. The signal is filtered by a 4-pole Bessel filter with a cut-off frequency of 10 kHz. Data acquisition is performed using a PCIe-6251 card (National Instruments, USA) at a sampling rate of 250 kHz (4 μs sampling time). All experiments were performed at an ambient temperature of 23 ± 2 °C. Long neutral polymer transport through the nanopore is performed by adding 60 μL of 35 kDa PEG to the cis and trans chambers to a final mass concentration of 0.5 wt% –25 wt% (100 mM LiCl or 100 mM KCl, both in 5 mM HEPES pH 7.5). We perform experiments with six different nanopore diameters (2.0 ± 0.1 nm, 3.0 ± 0.2 nm, 4.0 ± 0.1 nm, 5.0 ± 0.1 nm, 6.0 ± 0.2 nm, 7.8 ± 0.2 nm). For 100 kDa and 200 kDa PEG, the osmotic pressure is controlled by the 35 kDa PEG26. Experiments are performed by mixing 100 kDa: 35 kDa = 2%: 98% and 200 kDa: 35 kDa = 2%: 98% (100 mM LiCl 5 mM HEPES pH 7.5) (Fig. S11). The applied voltage is −700 mV.

For the transport of polysaccharides, we mix semi-dilute PEG chains (3 wt% mass concentration) with 8 kDa or 500 kDa DS (0.3 wt%). We also performed experiments without mixed PEG chains and only with 8 kDa or 500 kDa DS (100 mM LiCl 5 mM HEPES pH 7.5). We added DS into the cis chamber.

Vasopressin peptides, including l-Arg vasopressin (l-AVP) with the sequence Nter−C−Y−F−Q−N−C−P−(l)R−G−NH2, and D-Arg vasopressin (d-AVP) with the sequence Nter-C-Y-F-Q-N-C-P-(d)R-G-NH2, were synthesized and purified using HPLC by Proteogenics (Schiltigheim, France). Both peptides have a disulfide bond between residues C1 and C6. We mix semi-dilute PEG chains (3% mass concentration) with 0.5–20 µM l-AVP or d-AVP to transport peptides. We also perform experiments with 0.5–20 μM l-AVP or d-AVP in 100 mM LiCl 5 mM HEPES pH 7.5. To identify two chiral peptides, l-AVP and d-AVP, we mixed semi-dilute PEG chains (3 wt% mass concentration) with 20 µM l-AVP and d-AVP. We added a peptide into the cis (GND) chamber17.

Data analysis

Data analysis is performed using Igor Pro 9.05 software (WaveMetrics, Portland, USA) with homemade code. The average open pore current (I0) and standard deviation (σ) are calculated from the current trace. Each blockade is determined using a threshold approach at I0 –5 σ17. 300 s-long recordings are processed and analyzed to detect blockades and extract their characteristic parameters: the minimum current blockade levels within each event (Ib), the dwell time, and the inter-event time between two consecutive blockades (Fig. S2). The dwell times are calculated from the exponential fit of the corresponding distribution to avoid the limitation of the 10 kHz low-pass filter, which attenuates blockades shorter than twice the rise-time of the filter35,43.

The fits are performed five times by varying the fitting range, and we show the average of these fit values. The error bars correspond to the standard deviation of these fit values. The fitting ranges are defined based on 1) the total range of interest [Xmin, Xmax] where the curve is fitted 2) the range between Xmin and the middle of the range (Xmin + Xmax)/2 3) the range between Xmax and the middle of the range (Xmin + Xmax)/2 4) the range between the first and the last quartile (Q1 and Q3 respectively) 5) the range between the middle between Q1 and (Xmin + Xmax)/2 and the middle between (Xmin + Xmax)/2 and Q3.
