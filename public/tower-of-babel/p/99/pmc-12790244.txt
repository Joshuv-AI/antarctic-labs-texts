Multivalvular Disease
Authors: Syed Zaid
Published: 2025

Multivalvular disease (MVD) has shifted from the rare to the routine.1,2 Signals distort, thresholds move, and outcomes hinge on small choices: how we measure severity; which lesion we treat first; and how we interpret the response. This Editor's Page offers a framework for presenting and learning from MVD without prescribing definitive solutions. It highlights where precision matters, how sequencing shapes trajectory, and how shared learning turns a single-center experience into comparable, pragmatic evidence.

Challenges and Consequences

In MVD, physiology is interdependent and the driver lesion is not always obvious. Loading can mask or magnify severity and treating one valve affects another by mechanism and over time. In aortic stenosis with concomitant mitral regurgitation (AS + MR), the most common left-sided combination, MR can depress forward flow and underestimate AS at baseline. After aortic therapy, MR response can be a coin toss even when predictors look favorable.3,4 With tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and left-sided disease, outcomes also depend on right-sided reserve and pulmonary vascular load, details that are often omitted.5 The greatest value comes from transparent reasoning: what was measured; why a path was chosen; when reassessment will occur; and what benefit would justify proceeding or pausing. These interdependent mechanisms and shifting thresholds demand a structured framework to improve reasoning and reporting in MVD (Figure 1). At JACC: Case Reports, we highlight cases with integrated assessment, a clear sequencing plan, a prespecified reassessment window, and patient-centered outcomes to enable cross-center comparison and shared learning.

Getting the Diagnosis Right

Precision starts with how we measure. Single-parameter grading is fragile when lesions coexist.6 In AS + MR, low forward stroke volume from MR can depress transaortic gradients, whereas AS-related loading distorts MR assessment at rest. Documenting rhythm, blood pressure, and loading conditions prevents false reassurance. In TR with left-sided disease, assessment should also make right-sided physiology explicit: right ventricular (RV) size and function; simple RV–pulmonary artery coupling surrogates; pulmonary pressures/resistance; and when needed, invasive hemodynamics at rest or with exercise. A multiparametric, multimodality approach (comprehensive echo with computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging when helpful) reduces misclassification that drives mis-sequencing. We would prioritize an integrated assessment snapshot that pairs key echo/computed tomography findings with hemodynamic context so readers see how physiology shaped the plan, not just the numbers.

Sequencing and Strategy

Sequencing should follow mechanism, feasibility, and tolerance for residual disease. In AS + MR with primary MR, treating AS alone seldom changes MR; persistence is common and clinically relevant, so a predefined checkpoint is essential.3,4 In TR with left-sided disease, options are often framed as left-sided first, concomitant, or TR first. What helps is seeing why a strategy was chosen and the checkpoint for reassessment. We ask authors to include a brief reasoning box that identifies the driver lesion, states the intended sequence, specifies the reassessment window and metrics, and lists advance triggers to proceed or to pause. This turns a case into a small, testable story rather than a post hoc justification.

The Interval as Information

What follows the index step is part of the therapy. A clear view of interval change such as symptoms, heart failure events, lesion grades, and chamber dimensions often teaches more than procedural detail. In AS + MR, a checkpoint at 30 to 90 days after aortic therapy shows who improved, plateaued, or worsened despite technical success. In TR with left-sided disease, a compact trajectory mini-set could include TR grade, a simple RV trend (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, fractional area change, or RV global longitudinal strain when available), right-atrial size or venous congestion markers and diuretic requirement. This turns the interval into analyzable data rather than anecdote.

Hybrid Care and Lifetime Planning

Care is often hybrid, not purely surgical or transcatheter. Choices today should preserve tomorrow's options: reliable coronary access after transcatheter aortic valve replacement; a path for valve-in-valve or mitral redo; and room-to-treat TR without lead entrapment. In AS + MR/mitral stenosis, doing transcatheter aortic valve replacement without a defined mitral strategy can complicate later care; if the patient is not a transcatheter candidate, mitral valve surgery with a transcatheter aortic valve in situ can often be far more challenging. Left-sided double-valve surgery outside high-volume centers carries higher in-hospital risk than isolated procedures do,7,8 reminding us that “Can we?” and “Should we now?” are different questions. In TR with left-sided disease, lifetime planning often turns on lead strategy (extract, revise, or work around) and atrial fibrillation management. A brief lifetime-lens statement should note what the choice preserves, what it forecloses, and how it affects future reinterventions.

The Discipline of Restraint

Not every escalation serves patient goals. Frailty, mitral annular calcification, advanced RV dysfunction, poor RV–pulmonary artery coupling and fixed high pulmonary vascular resistance often limit the benefit of multivalve intervention.9 In these settings, symptom relief and quality-of-life endpoints often take precedence. It is important to apply the same rigor as escalation to explain why further therapy is unlikely to change outcomes and define patient-reported endpoints that will guide care. Concise accounts that identify preprocedural cues, state the plan and checkpoint, and note what the team would change next time can clarify futility signals, sharpen stop rules, improve consent, and help avoid procedural momentum.

Learning From Cases: Patterns Without Overreach

With integrated assessment, a clear sequencing plan, a prespecified reassessment window, and patient-centered outcomes, patterns become visible across centers without overclaiming. This comparability is especially useful for newer therapies with thin evidence. JACC: Case Reports has showcased patients ineligible for conventional therapy, to introduce and contextualize emerging platforms and techniques. For example, consistent reporting can clarify when leaflet modification is appropriate for selected high-risk patients, practical cues for chordal repair, transcatheter annuloplasty, and newer tricuspid systems, including device interactions and implications for reintervention. Because multivalvular patients are often excluded from trials, evidence is limited and guidance sparse; case-based learning is therefore a primary route to pragmatic knowledge. Consistent reporting can lay the groundwork for future guidance and careful aggregation can support bedside recognition without overreach.

Presenting MVD Cases: Essentials at a Glance

Effective teaching often comes from clear visuals and a steady cadence. Figures that stay simple yet rich can help readers: for example, a single panel that shows the hemodynamic interplay, the initial strategy, a concise if/then path for reassessment and the lifetime lens. A report can open with a clear clinical question, present the integrated assessment, state the planned sequence, and return to it at a defined interval. Patient-centered outcomes can be shown next to gradients/images and near-misses or not-yet decisions alongside technical successes. For consistency, consider 4 brief elements: 1) clinical summary with key echo/computed tomography findings; 2) why the plan was chosen; 3) what will be checked and when; and 4) how today's choice may affect future options.

Conclusions

By elevating integrated assessment, making sequencing logic transparent, honoring the interval, and presenting lifetime trade-offs openly, JACC: Case Reports can turn complex cases into clear lessons. The commitment is simple: clarity over volume; reasoning over ritual; and shared learning that compounds, case by case, into better care, especially where evidence is sparse and guidance limited.
