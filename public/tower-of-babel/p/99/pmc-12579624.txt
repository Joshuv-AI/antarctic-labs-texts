Calcium sodium phosphosilicate toothpastes impact on the treatment of dentin hypersensitivity: an umbrella review
Authors: Sara Ziaaddini, Negin Riahi, Sara Majidinia, Alireza Sarraf Shirazi, Vahideh Motamedosanaye, Mohammad Mostafazadehbakhtiyary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesensitizing toothpastes are considered an effective treatment option for managing dentin hypersensitivity (DH). This review evaluated existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses to determine the effectiveness of toothpastes containing calcium sodium phosphosilicate (CSPS) in managing DH. A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane, and Web of Science databases. Two reviewers performed study selection, data extraction, methodological quality assessment (using the AMSTAR 2 tool) and risk of bias (RoB; using the ROBIS tool) evaluation. The primary randomized clinical trials overlap was assessed using a citation matrix and calculating the overlap percentage, covered area (CA), and corrected covered area (CCA). A total of 5681 studies were identified and the full text of 85 studies were assessed. Finally, nine reviews were included in the umbrella review. A very high overlap was observed across primary studies. Eight studies had a low or critically low quality. Two reviews demonstrated a low RoB across all domains. CSPS-containing toothpastes were found to be more effective in relieving DH than placebo and negative controls. CSPS-containing toothpastes may be effective in relieving the pain associated with DH. However, the considerable heterogeneity and generally low methodological quality of the available evidence warrant cautious interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44445-025-00079-y.Keywords: Dentine hypersensitivity, Toothpaste, Umbrella review, Meta-analysis, Systematic review

Introduction

Dentin hypersensitivity (DH) is described by acute, intense pain following dentin exposure to thermal, physical, or chemical stimuli. The global prevalence of DH among adults ranges from 1.3% to 92.1% (Favaro Zeola et al. 2019; Ramli et al. 2022). DH affects patients' quality of life, necessitating a comprehensive treatment strategy to improve both oral and general health (Douglas-de-Oliveira et al. 2018; Soares et al. 2021). Although the underlying mechanisms of DH are not fully understood, the hydrodynamic theory is generally acknowledged (Liu et al. 2020). It suggests that increased fluid movement within the dentinal tubules alters pressure, resulting in the pain associated with dentinal sensitivity. Consequently, the most effective therapies for DH should aim to block nerve responses, reduce fluid flow, or achieve both (Liu et al. 2020).

At-home treatments for DH may include the use of desensitizing toothpastes, gentle brushing techniques with a soft-bristled toothbrush, and the avoidance of acidic foods. Professional treatments provided by dentists may involve fluoride varnishes, resin bonding, or low-intensity laser therapy. Additionally, lifestyle modifications-such as wearing a night guard to address bruxism-may contribute to positive outcomes (Marto et al. 2019; Porto et al. 2009). It is generally recommended to begin treatment with at-home options, such as desensitizing toothpastes (Schmidlin and Sahrmann 2013; Sowinski et al. 2001). Different formulations that contain potassium, strontium, arginine, stannous fluoride, nano-hydroxyapatite, and calcium sodium phosphosilicate (CSPS) are successful in alleviating DH, mainly by blocking the dentinal tubules (Marto et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2018; West et al. 2015). CSPS is a bioactive glass that produces hydroxycarbonate apatite (HCA) upon contact with bodily fluids (Asadi et al. 2024). HCA occludes exposed dentin surfaces and forms a mineralized layer that reduces permeability, particularly under acidic conditions (Wang et al. 2010; Athanasiadou et al. 2024). CSPS and stannous fluoride appear to provide the most rapid and robust desensitization. Stannous fluoride can deliver relief within days (He et al. 2011; Thrash et al. 1992), while CSPS offers immediate-to-rapid relief with sustained improvements over several weeks (West et al. 2015). Arginine and strontium are also effective, though their action may be slower.

According to the previous systematic reviews, CSPS-containing toothpastes reduce DH (Hu et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2015). A network meta-analysis with moderate to high certainty of evidence further revealed that CSPS outperformed fluoride in response to tactile, cold, and air stimuli (Martins et al. 2020). However, some studies did not find significant differences between toothpastes containing CSPS, potassium, and strontium (Hu et al. 2019). Several systematic reviews have examined the effects of CSPS-containing toothpastes compared to placebo or non-desensitizing toothpastes in managing DH, with conflicting findings. The present umbrella review aimed to determine whether CSPS-containing toothpastes are more effective than placebo or negative controls in DH management by evaluating systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Methods

Registration and protocol

This umbrella review was carried out following the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Further, its protocol has been documented in the PROSPERO database (code: CRD42024617640). The checklist for PROSPERO can be found in Appendix 1.

Inclusion of studies

The PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Control, Outcome, and Studies) for this review is summarized in Table 1. Across the included studies, comparison groups varied and included placebos (inert toothpastes), negative controls (non-desensitizing regular toothpastes containing low concentrations of fluoride, such as 1450 ppm), and positive controls (other desensitizing agents such as potassium nitrate, stannous fluoride, and arginine). However, for the purposes of the present review, only comparisons of CSPS-containing toothpastes with placebos and negative controls were considered. Systematic reviews published in English that evaluated the effects of CSPS-containing toothpastes on DH were included. Non-systematic reviews, clinical trials, animal studies, experimental studies, case reports, and case series were excluded.

Search strategy

PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched up to February 2025. The strategy for the search was formulated by utilizing a mix of keywords and MeSH terms as detailed below:

(teeth OR tooth OR oral OR mouth OR dent*) AND (hypersensitivity OR sensitivity) AND (Desensiti* OR Dentifrices OR Toothpastes OR Mouthwashes OR (In-office treatment) OR Fluorides OR Arginine OR Calcium OR Phosphates OR Silicates OR Potassium OR Nitrates OR Oxalates OR Strontium OR Titanium OR Aluminum OR Hydroxyapatites OR Nanohydroxyapatite OR Durapatite OR Casein OR Glutaraldehyde OR Gluma OR (Bioactive glass)) AND (Pain OR VAS OR (Verbal analog scale) OR (Prevention & control) OR Therap* OR Outcome OR Effectiveness) AND (review OR meta-analysis OR meta analysis).

Study selection and data collection

The title and abstract of all selected papers were evaluated by two reviewers (S.Z. and N.R.). When uncertainty arose, the full text was examined. A third reviewer (S.M) resolved any disagreements. Decisions were documented using EndNote 20, and the Rayyan AI web application was also employed during screening. The screening and selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart.

Data extraction was independently conducted by two reviewers (S.Z and N.R), with disagreements resolved by the third reviewer (S.M). An Excel spreadsheet was used to design the data collection form, which included the following details: author names, year of publication, journal name and SJR quartile, type of review, objectives, databases searched, search timeframe, number and type of included studies, quality assessment method, number and age of participants, CSPS concentration in the toothpaste, follow-up duration, results, and presence of heterogeneity.

Calculating the overlap of primary studies

To minimize bias, the overlap among primary studies was assessed in two phases. In Phase I, all primary studies within systematic reviews evaluating CSPS were included, irrespective of the comparison group. Phase II focused exclusively on primary studies that included placebo or negative control groups. For each phase, a citation matrix was used as a visual tool to assess overlap. Three indices were calculated: overlap percentage, covered area (CA), and corrected covered area (CCA) with the following formulae:

% Overlap = (original studies appearing in more than one systematic review) / r

% Overlap = (original studies appearing in more than one systematic review) / r

CA (covered area) = N / rc

CA (covered area) = N / rc

CCA (corrected covered area) = (N – r) / (rc – r)

CCA (corrected covered area) = (N – r) / (rc – r)

The total number of published primary studies, including repetitions, was denoted as N, while r represented the number of unique primary clinical trials, and c indicated the number of systematic reviews. Compared to CA, CCA is more affected by the number of primary studies. Reporting CCA in umbrella reviews is recommended as it provides the most accurate measure of overlap (Pieper et al. 2014). A CCA overlap of 0–5 was considered slight, 6–10 was moderate, 11–15 was high, and values greater than 15 was considered to be very high (Pieper et al. 2014).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included reviews was evaluated using the AMSTAR 2 tool. A checklist consists of 16 questions, each assessed independently by two reviewers (S.Z. and N.R.), with responses recorded as yes, partial yes, or no. Disagreements were resolved by consulting the third reviewer (S.M.). Each review was classified as having a high, moderate, low, or critically low quality (Shea et al. 2017).

Risk of bias assessment

The potential risk of bias (RoB) of the reviews was assessed using the ROBIS tool, which involves three phases. In the first phase, relevance was examined by determining whether the research question of this umbrella review was consistent with the studies’ PICO framework. The second phase focused on identifying possible issues in the review process and was structured into the following domains: study eligibility criteria, identification and selection of studies, data collection and appraisal, and synthesis and findings. Each domain contained five to six signaling questions, with responses recorded as “Yes” (Y), “Probably Yes” (PY), “Probably No” (PN), “No” (N), or “No Information” (NI). Reviews were categorized as having a low RoB when all responses were marked Y or PY; an uncertain RoB when one response was marked PN, N, or NI; and a high RoB when more than one response fell into these categories. The third phase provided an overall judgment of RoB for each review, based on three additional questions scored using the same response options (Whiting et al. 2016).

Results

Search result

As shown in Fig. 1, 5681 articles were retrieved and 2803 duplicates were removed. During the initial screening, 2793 studies were excluded and the full text of 85 studies was assessed. Following a detailed evaluation, 76 were excluded. Exclusion rationale are shown in Appendix 2. Ultimately, nine systematic reviews were included.

Studies characteristics

The features of the included reviews are summarized in Tables 2 and 3. Five reviews included a meta-analysis. These reviews were published between 2014 and 2021, and the primary clinical trials analyzed were conducted between 2008 and 2018. Search timeframes ranged from inception to 2020 across several databases. The RoB was assessed using either the Cochrane tool or the GRADE framework. The randomized controlled clinical trials count on CSPS ranged from 2 to 15. CSPS concentrations in toothpastes varied from 2.5% to 15%. Follow-up durations ranged from immediate assessment post-treatment to 6 months.

Overlap calculation

The overlap analysis was conducted in two phases. In both citation matrices, the systematic reviews were arranged in columns in ascending order of publication year. Of the 29 original studies included across the systematic reviews, 13 used placebo as the comparison group. Accordingly, the Phase I citation matrix (Table 4) comprised 29 rows, while the Phase II citation matrix (Table 5) comprised 13 rows. The specified formulas were applied to calculate %Overlaps, CA, and CCA in each phase. In Phase I, which assessed the overlap of all primary trials, a total of 79 study instances (N) were identified across 29 unique trials (r) and 9 systematic reviews (c). The percentage overlap was 65.51%, with a covered area (CA) of 30.26% and a corrected covered area (CCA) of 21.55%. In Phase II, which considered only primary trials with negative control or placebo comparison groups, 46 study instances were identified across 13 unique trials and 9 systematic reviews. The percentage overlap was 61.53%, with a CA of 39.31% and a CCA of 31.73%.

Risk of bias assessment

The outcomes from the ROBIS tool are summarized in Table 7. In Phase I, the target question aligned with the review question. The RoB in the Phase II part one was low for all included reviews. For the second part, six reviews demonstrated a low RoB, while the remaining three showed an unclear RoB. The main concerns in these three reviews were the limited range of databases searched and the absence of supplementary search methods beyond databases. The RoB of the third part was unclear or high for three reviews. The primary issue was the failure to have two reviewers independently extract data and assess the RoB. Regarding the forth part, nine reviews had an unclear or high RoB because they did not adequately address heterogeneity and biases in primary studies, nor did they use funnel plots or sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of their findings. All studies had a low RoB for Phase III.

Main outcomes

The results of the included reviews generally indicated that CSPS-containing toothpastes were more effective than placebo and negative controls in reducing DH. According to Martins et al. (2020), CSPS was the most effective toothpaste against tactile, air, and cold stimuli compared with all other formulations. With respect to CSPS concentration, Talioti et al. (2014) discovered that a concentration of 7.5% CSPS yielded the most significant decrease in DH, while both the placebo and 2.5% CSPS showed comparable effects over an eight-week period. In contrast, Freitas et al. (2021) and Zhu et al. (2015) reported that toothpastes containing 5% CSPS or 5% bioglass 45S5 (NovaMin) were more effective than controls throughout all follow-up periods.

Heterogeneity was present across all nine systematic reviews, preventing quantitative subgroup analysis. This heterogeneity was related to study design, patient populations, CSPS concentrations, placebo or control groups, outcome measures, and follow-up durations. Such variability reduced the precision of pooled estimates and limited the certainty of translating these findings directly into clinical recommendations. Consequently, while clinicians may consider CSPS a potentially effective option, the magnitude and consistency of its benefits may vary across practice settings and patient subgroups.

Discussion

Summary of findings and methodological critique

In the last decades, more than 15 products have been created using CSPS for the management of DH, which are now sold in over 20 different countries (Zhu et al. 2015). In order to capture all relevant studies, the search strategy of this umbrella review considered all non-invasive approaches for DH. Among different treatment methods, CSPS was the one necessary to carry out an umbrella review on because of the numerous systematic reviews examining its effect on DH, which had questionable conclusions. This is the first umbrella review to evaluate the effect of CSPS-containing toothpastes on DH. CSPS-containing toothpastes were considered as the intervention group and placebo or negative control as the comparison group. Although negative control toothpastes do not contain specific active desensitizing ingredients, they often contain fluoride. Fluoride can reduce DH by blocking fluid movement within dentin tubules. High-fluoride products alleviate DH, while low-fluoride formulations offer minimal sensitivity relief. This can lead to therapeutic effects, undermining their role as true placebos (Zhu et al. 2015; Hu et al. 2019; Pradeep et al. 2012).

A major challenge in this review was the considerable heterogeneity among the included studies, which made subgroup analysis not possible. Heterogeneity refers to the variability or differences between studies included in a systematic review. Clinical heterogeneity arises from aspects such as variations in CSPS concentration, differences in control groups, assessment methods for DH, and follow-up periods. For example, the comparison groups varied across the studies, with controls ranging from placebos, to negative or positive control groups.

Due to the high heterogeneity of the comparison groups in clinical trials of the included systematic reviews, a citation matrix for all primary studies on CSPS (Phase I) and for only the primary studies considering a negative or placebo control (Phase II) were provided. The obtained CCA percentage (more than 15%) in both phases indicated a very high overlap across primary clinical trials. This degree of overlap demonstrates an unnecessary repetition of systematic reviews, which leads to bolding the outcomes of duplicate primary studies and decreasing the evidence reliability. However, the lower CCA of Phase I (21.55%) compared to Phase II (31.73%) makes the results of Phase I more reliable. Studies of this Phase generally showed positive effects of CSPS when compared to placebo or negative controls. Furthermore, the assessment of methodological quality and RoB revealed considerable concerns regarding the reliability and internal validity of the evidence, making cautious interpretation of the results necessary.

Interpretation of results and clinical discussion

According to the AMSTAR 2 evaluation, only one review received a high quality score (Zhu et al. 2015). Zhu et al. (2015) reported that most of the included clinical trials suggested CSPS worked better than the negative control in reducing DH. The other eight reviews had a low or critically low quality, mainly because of the absence of a registered protocol or to the lack of explanations for deviations from planned methodologies, incomplete search strategies, lack of subgroup analyses or meta-regressions, inadequate handling of heterogeneity, unclear criteria for excluded studies, and limited consideration of RoB. While most reviews assessed RoB within individual studies using tools such as Cochrane or GRADE scores, few explicitly discussed how this bias influenced their results. Merely reporting the RoB without integrating it into the interpretation reduces the rigor of a systematic review. For example, high-risk studies may have overstated the effectiveness of CSPS-containing toothpastes, but this was rarely acknowledged in the included reviews.

The results of Phase I of the ROBIS tool indicated that all nine systematic reviews were relevant to the target question. All the systematic reviews had a low RoB in the first domain of Phases II and III due to the appropriate eligibility criteria designed for the review and conclusions supported by evidence. Within the second and third domains of Phase II, 66.66% of the studies had a low RoB, while others had an unclear or high RoB due to minor flaws such as limited database sources and the failure to perform data collection and RoB assessment in duplicate. The majority of shortcomings were found in the fourth domain of Phase II, where only two reviews demonstrated a low RoB (Hu et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2015). Zhu et al. (2015) conducted a meta-analysis showing that, based on moderate evidence, 5% CSPS toothpaste outperformed the negative control in managing DH. Similarly, Hu et al. (2018) reported that certain desensitizing toothpastes, including those containing CSPS, provided greater DH relief than the negative control.

The findings consistently highlight that CSPS-containing toothpastes outperform placebo, negative controls, and some positive controls in reducing DH (Martins et al. 2020; Freitas et al. 2021). CSPS toothpastes demonstrated significant reductions in pain associated with dentinal sensitivity from tactile, cold, and air stimuli. These results confirm the bioactive properties of CSPS, which forms an HCA layer upon contact with saliva, effectively occluding dentinal tubules and reducing fluid movement (Asadi et al. 2024). The results of network meta-analyses further strengthen the evidence, showing CSPS outperformed placebo or fluoride-based formulations and provided rapid, sustained relief when used consistently. On the other hand, some studies reported comparable outcomes between CSPS, potassium, and strontium toothpastes (Zhu et al. 2015; Hu et al. 2019).

Martins et al. (2020) reported CSPS as the most effective agent among desensitizing toothpastes. Talioti et al. (2014) emphasized the superior performance of the 7.5% CSPS formulation compared with lower concentrations (2.5%) and placebos after eight weeks. Two other reviews reported that toothpaste containing 5% CSPS had greater impact on DH than the control at all follow-up times (Zhu et al. 2015; Freitas et al. 2021). The difference in the effective percentage of CSPS among the reviews may be due to heterogeneity among the studies and differences in publication years, as the more recent studies were included by Freitas et al. and Zhu et al. Multiple factors caused the high heterogeneity among these reviews including different study designs, population characteristics, comparison group dentifrices and follow-up periods. The inadequate number of primary studies on 7.5% CSPS formulation also decreases evidence reliability. Because of the high heterogeneity, no synthesis could be performed on whether a dose–response relationship exists, and the contradictory results between 5% and 7.5% CSPS concentrations highlight a research gap. However, at lower concentrations, 2.5% CSPS showed only marginal benefits over placebos, suggesting a potential dose–response relationship. Overall, interpretation of the findings must be approached with caution due to the low or critically low quality and high heterogeneity among most of the studies. More high quality studies on the correlation between CSPS dose and its effectiveness on DH are recommended to be performed in order to fill this research gap.

Clinical implications

The findings support the routine use of CSPS-containing toothpastes for patients experiencing acute or persistent sensitivity, particularly those with enamel erosion, gingival recession, or post-procedural sensitivity.

Conclusion

CSPS-containing toothpastes were reported to be effective compared with placebo and negative, controls in the treatment of DH. However, the high degree of heterogeneity across the included reviews, together with the low quality of evidence, necessitates cautious interpretation of these findings. Well-designed, standardized studies with pre-registered meta-analysis that meet the standards of available guidelines are needed to provide more reliable and generalizable evidence.
