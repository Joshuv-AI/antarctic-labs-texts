Anesthetic management of tracheal tumor resection with preservation of spontaneous breathing under laryngeal mask airway general anesthesia combined with epidural anesthesia
Authors: Xiaolin Tang, Peng Li
Published: 2025

This approach not only reduces medical costs and minimizes patient harm but also promotes recovery. For future patients with similar or more severe conditions, this anesthetic method may be a more favorable option to consider. Introduction

Primary tracheal tumors are exceptionally rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately one per million, representing only 0.2% of all malignant neoplasms [1, 2]. These tumors frequently present with nonspecific symptoms, with clinical manifestations primarily characterized by airway obstruction and irritation. Common presentations include dyspnea, wheezing, cough, hemoptysis, and dysphagia or hoarseness, which may result from invasion of adjacent structures[3]. At present, surgical resection of the affected tracheal segment followed by reconstruction remains the gold standard for the treatment of tracheal tumors [4]. Given that the trachea serves as both the surgical site and a critical conduit for maintaining respiratory function and oxygenation during anesthesia, the approach to airway management must be meticulously tailored. This necessitates a thorough evaluation of various factors, including the tumor's histopathological characteristics, growth pattern, anatomical localization, degree of airway obstruction, and the selected surgical approach. These challenges require a meticulous balance to ensure optimal oxygenation and the successful outcome of the procedure [5]. This case report details the anesthetic management of a patient undergoing tracheal tumor resection under general anesthesia with a Video Laryngeal Mask Airway (SaCoVLM), in combination with epidural anesthesia, while maintaining spontaneous breathing.

Case presentation

The patient was a 22-year-old Asian female, measuring 162 cm in height and weighing 55 kg, who presented with a history of dyspnea persisting for more than two months. Her medical history was unremarkable, and no abnormalities were detected in her coagulation profile. Initial fiberoptic bronchoscopy identified a lesion in the middle segment of the trachea, characterized by extensive basal growth and significant luminal obstruction (Fig. 1A). Partial resection of the tumor was performed during this procedure. Follow-up bronchoscopy revealed a lesion extending through the middle and lower segments of the trachea, exhibiting wide basal growth and mild-to-moderate luminal narrowing. The lesion measured approximately 2.0 cm in length, with its lower edge located 3.0 cm from the carina and the upper edge 5.0 cm from the vocal folds (Fig. 1B). Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a soft tissue density nodule on the right wall of the upper trachea, corresponding to the level of the upper border of the sternum at the T3-T4 vertebral level. The nodule measured approximately 15 mm × 12 mm and protruded into the tracheal lumen, causing mild narrowing. No significant enhancement was observed on contrast-enhanced imaging (Fig. 1C). The patient was diagnosed with a tracheal tumor, and surgical resection was planned.

B Tracheoscopy showing the lower (distal) margin of the tumor approximately 2.1 cm above the carina. C Chest CT scan confirming tumor location within the thoracic trachea

A surgical approach utilizing spontaneous breathing with a Video Laryngeal Mask Airway (SaCoVLM, Zhejiang UE Medical Corp), combined with general anesthesia and epidural anesthesia, was proposed. In the pre-anesthesia area, left radial artery puncture and catheter placement were performed under local anesthesia. Upon admission to the operating room, standard monitoring was initiated, including measurements of blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), oxygen saturation (SpO2), and bispectral index (BIS), with the initial BIS ranging from 85 to 100. Intravenous administration of 5 µg sufentanil and 1 mg midazolam was administered. Epidural catheter placement was successfully achieved under local anesthesia at the T5-T6 level with the patient in the right lateral position. Following confirmation of proper catheter placement, a test dose of 30 mg lidocaine was administered, confirming the absence of a subarachnoid block. Subsequently, 70 mg of 1% ropivacaine was injected through the epidural catheter. Intravenous dexmedetomidine was administered with a loading dose of 1 µg/kg over 10 min, followed by a maintenance infusion at 0.4–0.7 µg/kg/h. Propofol and remifentanil were infused intravenously at titrated rates. Assisted ventilation was provided as necessary when respiratory rate or tidal volume decreased.

Once the bispectral index (BIS) reached 40–60, a size 3 Laryngeal Mask Airway (LMA) was inserted, and lidocaine was sprayed on the vocal cords to minimize airway reflexes. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy was performed to reassess the tumor’s location and the condition of the trachea. Anesthesia was maintained with a continuous infusion of propofol (3–6 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.1–0.4 µg/kg/min). Throughout the procedure, spontaneous breathing was preserved, and the depth of anesthesia was maintained within a BIS range of 45–60. The respiratory rate varied from 8 to 19 breaths per minute, tidal volume fluctuated between 150 and 350 mL, and partial end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2) levels were maintained between 45 and 66 mmHg. Assisted ventilation was provided as needed to prevent respiratory depression resulting from CO2 accumulation.

The patient underwent thoracoscopic resection of the tracheal tumor and end-to-end tracheal anastomosis in the left lateral position. A pneumothorax was induced with CO2 insufflation at a pressure of 8–10 mmHg to facilitate optimal surgical visualization. After thoracoscopic mobilization of the trachea, the diseased segment was excised, and end-to-end anastomosis was performed (Fig. 2). Following the procedure, respiration was maintained via the distal trachea. During resection and anastomosis, a 14 French disposable sputum suction tube (4.67 mm) was prepared for placement by the surgeon at the tracheostomy site near the cricoid cartilage. The suction tube was externally connected to a ventilator’s backup oxygen source, providing a baseline oxygen flow of 2 L/min, with flow adjustments based on SpO2 levels. The placement of the suction tube also allowed for the aspiration of secretions or blood. Endotracheal tubes (ID 5.5 and 6.0) were prepared for potential intubation in the event of respiratory arrest, ensuring the availability of mechanical ventilation if required. Spontaneous breathing was maintained throughout the procedure, and the distal tracheal opening was kept elevated to prevent the aspiration of secretions or blood from the upper airway into the lungs via the tracheostomy, thereby reducing the risk of asphyxia and choking.

Following tumor resection and tracheal anastomosis, ventilation via the LMA was resumed. At the conclusion of the surgery, a Guardian suture was used to bridge the skin over the chin and sternal regions, maintaining neck flexion to minimize tension on the anastomosis site. Anesthetic agents were gradually tapered, and airway and oral secretions were carefully suctioned to prevent aspiration and choking during recovery. For postoperative analgesia, 15 mg of 0.33% ropivacaine was administered via the epidural catheter. The patient’s head was maintained in a flexed position to avoid tension on the tracheal anastomosis. Once the bispectral index (BIS) exceeded 80, the patient was gently awakened and assessed for respiratory stability and airway patency. After confirming the absence of respiratory difficulty or the need for reintubation, the LMA was removed, and the patient was transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for recovery, ultimately returning safely to the ward. Postoperative pathological examination confirmed the tumor’s salivary gland origin.

Discussion

In certain benign tracheal conditions, such as tracheobronchopathia osteochondroplastica, bronchoscopic surgery may represent a more appropriate treatment option. The tracheal stenosis caused by these lesions constitutes a significant clinical concern that requires prompt management [6]. Furthermore, bronchoscopic evaluation provides more accurate information regarding the degree of airway obstruction, as well as detailed images of the lesion’s surface, shape, and endoluminal extension, which are essential for assessing resectability [7]. However, surgical resection remains the treatment of choice for malignant tracheal conditions whenever feasible. In this case, given the patient’s severe symptoms of dyspnea, we initially opted for bronchoscopic surgery to alleviate the patient’s symptoms and obtain a clearer understanding of the mass’s nature, which will inform the planning of subsequent treatment.

Tracheal tumors are rare and typically associated with low morbidity; however, they are often accompanied by tracheal stenosis. Tracheal resection and reconstruction remain the gold standard in treatment. Anesthetic management strategies for these cases vary considerably, depending on the tumor’s location and the surgical technique employed. Given that the trachea functions both as the airway and the surgical site, a comprehensive and individualized anesthetic approach is essential to ensure optimal airway management for the patient.

Traditionally, tracheal surgery is performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation to facilitate respiratory control. However, this approach may not be suitable in certain clinical scenarios. For example, in cases of severe tracheal stenosis, tracheal occlusion following general anesthesia, or situations where the endotracheal tube is unable to bypass the narrowed segment, alternative strategies are required. Additionally, endotracheal intubation may obstruct the surgeon’s access to the surgical site, complicating tumor resection and tracheal anastomosis.

Kiss et al. [8] demonstrated that non-intubated thoracoscopic surgery with preservation of spontaneous breathing is feasible and effective in selected cases. Some studies suggest that tubeless tracheal surgery is both feasible and safe for tracheal resection and reconstruction in the management of benign stenosis [9]. Thoracic epidural anesthesia offers continuous analgesia, reduces the need for systemic sedatives, and alleviates respiratory depression. The epidural puncture site is typically chosen based on the surgical location, often between T3 and T7, providing effective analgesia without significant respiratory compromise [10]. However, epidural anesthesia alone is frequently poorly tolerated by patients, presenting challenges for performing thoracoscopic procedures, particularly in the lateral position.

LMA offers an alternative ventilation strategy for airway surgeries [11, 12] . By preserving spontaneous breathing, the LMA avoids the complications associated with endotracheal intubation, particularly in patients with severe or high tracheal stenosis. This approach also reduces the risk of airway trauma, bleeding, and other complications related to intubation. Additionally, the LMA facilitates both airway management and visualization of the trachea and surgical site without interference during procedures such as airway inspection and anastomosis.

In cases of tracheal tumors with severe airway obstruction, spontaneous breathing may fail to provide adequate ventilation. In such instances, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or cardiopulmonary bypass may be required to ensure sufficient oxygenation during tumor resection. However, in the present case, the patient had undergone partial tumor resection via preoperative bronchoscopy, significantly alleviating the degree of airway obstruction. The primary objective of utilizing spontaneous breathing is to facilitate safer extubation postoperatively, thereby preventing respiratory depression associated with the use of muscle relaxants during surgery and the potential need for emergency reintubation following extubation. Furthermore, if mechanical ventilation or endotracheal intubation is employed, muscle relaxants are generally required to prevent patient-ventilator asynchrony, which could interfere with the surgical procedure. Although endotracheal intubation can support spontaneous breathing, LMA ventilation offers excellent surgical exposure while avoiding the complications associated with intubation, such as airway injury and disruption of friable tissues. Additionally, the LMA does not irritate the tracheal mucosa and allows for fiber-optic visualization of the anastomosis. The use of LMA significantly reduces the risk of excessive coughing during emergence, thereby protecting the anastomosis from dehiscence. High-frequency jet ventilation (HFJV) is another potential ventilation strategy; however, it carries risks, including difficulty in monitoring ventilation, barotrauma, or mucosal injury (e.g., whiplash injury). Nevertheless, LMA ventilation with spontaneous breathing can result in inadequate ventilation, leading to hypoxemia, hypercarbia, and an increased risk of aspiration. Therefore, precise titration of anesthetic agents is necessary to avoid apnea or coughing. This approach minimizes patient movement, ensures a clean surgical field, and reduces the risks associated with general anesthesia.

A pneumothorax was induced with CO2 insufflation at a pressure of 8–10 mmHg to enhance surgical visualization. During the procedure, the patient did not experience apnea but exhibited a decrease in respiratory rate to 8 breaths per minute. To prevent hypercapnia from suppressing the respiratory center, assisted manual ventilation with a resuscitation bag was provided. Despite this, PETCO2 levels were slightly elevated. We suspect that part of this elevation may have been due to CO2 from the pneumothorax entering the monitoring equipment via the tracheostomy site. Unfortunately, no measures were taken to prevent CO2 from entering the airway, nor was arterial blood gas (ABG) monitoring utilized to assess gas exchange.

At the conclusion of the surgery, a Guardian suture was utilized. This specialized suture is designed to secure medical devices, such as the endotracheal tube (ETT) or supraglottic airway devices, preventing their displacement or movement during the procedure. The Guardian suture is particularly valuable in surgeries involving the neck, especially when long-term airway management is necessary, as it ensures that the airway device remains securely positioned. Indications for the use of a Guardian suture include prolonged airway management, particularly in patients with an endotracheal tube; head and neck surgeries to prevent the displacement or accidental removal of airway devices; and in patients who are unconscious or unable to maintain their airway independently, ensuring the secure positioning of airway devices. In summary, the Guardian suture plays a critical role in stabilizing and securing airway devices during surgery or the postoperative period, thereby helping to prevent airway complications arising from device movement or misplacement.

Postoperative management plays a crucial role in preventing complications, such as anastomotic dehiscence, which may result from patient movement or aspiration. Comprehensive postoperative analgesia must be administered, ensuring optimal pain control. Additionally, all respiratory secretions and blood should be thoroughly aspirated prior to the patient’s recovery phase. It is essential to maintain effective communication with the perioperative team to facilitate early detection of any potential issues and ensure prompt intervention.

Contingency plans for intraoperative complications, such as respiratory depression or airway obstruction, must be meticulously prepared in advance. These plans should encompass provisions for ECMO or extracorporeal circulation, ensuring the maintenance of patient safety throughout the entire surgical procedure.

In conclusion, for patients with tracheal tumors, a combined approach of general anesthesia and thoracic epidural anesthesia, while maintaining spontaneous breathing, proves to be a safe and feasible strategy. This technique effectively ensures patient safety, minimizes the risk of complications, reduces the length of hospitalization, and lowers associated medical costs. Our experience suggests that this approach offers a valuable reference for the management of similar cases.
