Ultrasound-guided modified superior laryngeal nerve block for awake tracheal intubation in patients with difficult airways
Authors: Yan Chen, Wen Du, Wei Wang, Juan Guo, Feng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy of an ultrasound-guided modified superior laryngeal nerve block for awake tracheal intubation in patients with anticipated difficult airways.MethodsThis study included 60 patients with difficult airways who were scheduled for orthopedic surgery under general anesthesia. The study was conducted at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University from January 2024 to December 2025. Patients were grouped according to routine clinical practice. The experimental group received an ultrasound-guided modified superior laryngeal nerve block (n = 30), whereas the control group received topical anesthesia via fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided supraglottic spraying (n = 30). Intubation time and success rate were recorded. Cough response was graded, patient satisfaction was assessed, and peri-procedural complications were documented.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups (p > 0.05). Intubation time was shorter in the experimental group than in the control group (1.73 ± 0.44 vs. 2.70 ± 0.46 min; p < 0.001). The success rate was also higher in the experimental group (100% vs. 83%; p = 0.020). Cough responses were attenuated, with 90% of patients in the experimental group showing Grade I responses, whereas Grade III-IV responses were more frequent in the control group (37%) (p = 0.026). Patient comfort was better in the experimental group, with 90% versus 40% rated as “good” (p < 0.001), and poor comfort occurred only in the control group (37%). The incidences of tachycardia and hypertension were lower in the experimental group, although the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). No laryngospasm, bleeding, or nerve injury was observed in either group.ConclusionUltrasound-guided modified superior laryngeal nerve block was associated with shorter intubation time and attenuated cough response during awake tracheal intubation in patients with difficult airways. Higher procedural success and patient satisfaction were achieved without an increase in complications. This approach appears to be safe and feasible in the described clinical setting.

Introduction

Management of difficult airways remains a high-risk challenge in clinical anesthesia practice. Limited visualization and access increase the likelihood of failure in both mask ventilation and tracheal intubation [1, 2]. Delayed or inadequate airway control may lead to hypoxemia and airway injury. In severe cases, oxygenation cannot be maintained [3, 4]. Although the overall incidence is low, the associated risk is substantial. This imbalance continues to influence routine anesthetic decision-making [5, 6]. Changes in patient demographics, particularly advanced age and increased body mass index, have been associated with a gradual rise in the predicted incidence of difficult airways, adding further variability to airway planning and management [7].

Awake tracheal intubation is commonly used in this setting. Conventional techniques usually rely on fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided topical anesthesia of the glottis. However, this process can be time-consuming and may be poorly tolerated by patients. In addition, strong laryngeal reflexes may still interfere with tube passage [8]. Prolonged or repeated intubation attempts are associated with an increased risk of adverse events [9]. Supraglottic reflexes can be attenuated by a targeted block of the superior laryngeal nerve [10]. Ultrasound enables clearer visualization of the nerve and adjacent anatomical structures, thereby facilitating needle localization [11, 12]. This strategy may improve cough suppression and overall intubation conditions, and has also been reported to improve first-pass success rates [13, 14].

Existing techniques for airway anesthesia show considerable interpatient variability [15]. Topical approaches, such as spraying or nebulization, depend on contact with the airway mucosa and may be affected by secretions [16, 17]. These methods often result in uneven distribution, and the depth of anesthesia is difficult to control [18, 19]. When a superior laryngeal nerve block is performed using landmark-guided techniques, needle positioning remains highly dependent on operator experience. In addition, the presence of nearby vascular structures makes the technique less predictable. Although ultrasound improves anatomical visualization, stable needle placement and uniform spread of local anesthetic are not always achieved in daily practice [20]. In some cases, the supraglottic sensory block may be incomplete because of limited diffusion across the thyrohyoid membrane [21]. These technical limitations reduce reproducibility, particularly in the absence of well-trained operators. This highlights the need for further refinement of the technique.

Given the limitations of current airway anesthesia techniques, particularly the uneven spread of anesthetic and inconsistent suppression of laryngeal reflexes, further optimization of the superior laryngeal nerve block is warranted. In this study, a modified ultrasound-guided approach was used to improve needle positioning and local anesthetic spread. Its performance during awake tracheal intubation was compared with that of conventional fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided topical anesthesia in patients with anticipated difficult airways. The aim was to evaluate intubation time, success rate, cough response, patient comfort, and safety, and to assess its clinical applicability in difficult airway management.

Materials and methods

Participants

This prospective observational study did not involve interventional allocation. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Clinical Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (approved number: IACUC-20231230-13). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study or their legal guardians. Clinical trial number: Not applicable. Patients admitted to the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between January 2024 and December 2025 who were assessed preoperatively as having a difficult airway were screened. All patients underwent awake tracheal intubation followed by general anesthesia for orthopedic surgery, including spinal surgery and total hip arthroplasty.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: age 18–55 years; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-II; predicted difficult airway defined by at least two of the following criteria: neck circumference ≥ 40 cm, inter-incisor distance < 3 cm, Mallampati class ≥ III, thyromental distance < 6 cm, or limited cervical mobility; intact mental status without cognitive impairment; and provision of written informed consent. The exclusion criteria included severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction, hepatic or renal insufficiency, infection at the puncture site, malignancy or other major comorbidities, and inability to cooperate.

Patients were managed according to routine clinical practice and were categorized accordingly into either the ultrasound-guided superior laryngeal nerve block group (experimental group, n = 30) or the fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided topical glottic anesthesia group (control group, n = 30). Efficacy- and safety-related outcomes during awake tracheal intubation were compared between the two groups.

Procedures

Preoperative fasting followed Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) recommendations, with fasting from solids for 8 h and from clear fluids for 2 h. Upon arrival, standard monitoring was initiated, including electrocardiography, continuous pulse oximetry, and noninvasive blood pressure monitoring. All airway procedures were performed by the same senior anesthesiologist.

In the experimental group, a modified bilateral superior laryngeal nerve block was performed under ultrasound guidance. With the patient in the supine position and the head extended, a high-frequency linear probe was placed in the midline over the hyoid bone. The hyoid appeared as an arch-like hyperechoic structure. The probe was then moved laterally and caudally to identify the sternohyoid and thyrohyoid muscles, with the thyrohyoid membrane located deep to the latter. The superior laryngeal artery was visualized deep to the membrane, and the nerve was located medial to the artery. After rotating the probe by 90° with the marker directed cephalad, the hyoid bone (cephalad) and thyroid cartilage (caudad) defined the thyrohyoid membrane between them, and the nerve was identified on its deep surface. A further counterclockwise rotation of approximately 30° at the caudal end created the modified orientation (Fig. 1A). Using an out-of-plane approach along the ultrasound M-line, the needle was advanced at the junction of the thyroid cartilage and the thyrohyoid membrane. After negative aspiration, 2 mL of 2% lidocaine was injected. Downward displacement of the membrane indicated adequate spread. The contralateral side was treated in the same manner (Fig. 1B-C).

In the control group, 4 mL of 2% lidocaine was sprayed onto the glottis via a fiberoptic bronchoscope to provide topical anesthesia (Fig. 1D).

In both groups, ultrasound-guided cricothyroid membrane puncture was subsequently performed. This method primarily targets the tracheal branches of the vagus nerve, including the sensory branches of the recurrent laryngeal nerve (which innervate the airway mucosa below the glottis) and the submucosal vagal nerve endings. It achieves comprehensive sensory blockade of the airway from below the glottis to the carina. The probe was placed over the thyroid cartilage and moved caudally to identify the depression corresponding to the cricothyroid membrane, which appeared as a curvilinear hyperechoic line. An in-plane lateral-to-medial approach was used to advance the needle beneath the membrane. Entry into the tracheal lumen was indicated by loss of resistance and aspiration of air, after which 2 mL of 2% lidocaine was rapidly injected. Coughing was typically elicited, after which the needle was withdrawn. A second puncture was then performed to administer the remaining anesthetic, for a total of 8 mL of 2% lidocaine (Fig. 1E-F).

In both groups, glossopharyngeal nerve block was achieved by topical application of 2% lidocaine using a laryngeal anesthesia atomizer to the base of the tongue, the bilateral palatoglossal and palatopharyngeal arches, and the posterior pharyngeal wall. This sequence was repeated three times at 1-min intervals, with a total dose of 8 mL. Absence of the gag reflex upon insertion of the bronchoscope was considered indicative of adequate blockade.

Based on the minimum body weight of 61 kg recorded in the study population, and in accordance with guidelines from the Difficult Airway Society (DAS) and international expert consensus on anesthetic practice, the recommended maximum safe dose of lidocaine for awake management of the difficult airway is 9 mg/kg. This corresponds to an upper safety limit of 550 mg for a 61-kg individual. The total lidocaine dose administered in the present study was 400 mg, which falls below the 550 mg safety threshold.

Awake tracheal intubation was then performed under fiberoptic guidance. After successful tube placement, general anesthesia was induced with sufentanil 0.4 µg/kg, propofol 2 mg/kg, and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg. Mechanical ventilation was initiated with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg, a respiratory rate of 12 breaths/min, and an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:2. End-tidal carbon dioxide was maintained at 35–45 mmHg. Depth of anesthesia, as measured by the bispectral index (BIS), was maintained at 45–60. Propofol and remifentanil were used for anesthesia maintenance, and doses were adjusted according to blood pressure, heart rate, and BIS values.

In both groups, no routine intravenous sedatives were administered before the airway block or during awake intubation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test. Categorical variables were presented as counts or percentages and compared using the χ2 test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

There were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups, including age, sex distribution, body mass index, and ASA physical status (all p > 0.05). Specifically, the mean age was 43.40 ± 4.62 years in the experimental group and 41.36 ± 6.01 years in the control group (p = 0.148). The sex distribution was comparable between the two groups (p = 0.795). Body mass index was also similar (p = 0.851). Likewise, the distribution of ASA physical status (I/II) did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.739). These findings indicate that the two groups were well balanced and comparable at baseline (Table 1).

Secondary outcomes

The distribution of cough response grades differed significantly between the two groups (p = 0.026). In the experimental group, all patients exhibited minimal or no cough (Grade I-II), and no Grade III or IV responses were observed. In contrast, higher-grade cough responses were more common in the control group, including Grade III in 6/30 patients (20%) and Grade IV in 5/30 patients (17%). Patient-reported comfort also differed significantly between the two groups ( p < 0.001). A higher proportion of patients in the experimental group reported good comfort compared with the control group. Poor comfort was reported only in the control group. The incidences of tachycardia and hypertension were lower in the experimental group than in the control group; however, the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.190). No cases of laryngospasm, bleeding, or superior laryngeal nerve injury were observed in either group. Overall, the experimental group demonstrated reduced airway reflex responses and better patient comfort, without an increase in peri-procedural complications (Table 3).

Discussion

Airway control during general anesthesia is a pivotal determinant of perioperative safety. Failure to manage a difficult airway accounts for a substantial proportion of anesthesia-related mortality. Awake tracheal intubation is often selected when a difficult airway is anticipated, especially in patients with structural abnormalities. This approach preserves spontaneous breathing and reduces the risk of severe hypoxia, although patient tolerance may be limited. In this study, the ultrasound-guided modified superior laryngeal nerve block was associated with shorter intubation time, a higher success rate, reduced cough response, and better patient comfort compared with conventional topical anesthesia, without an increase in complications.

This comparison primarily focused on the ultrasound-guided modified superior laryngeal nerve block. The main differences were observed in intubation time and procedural success. The superior laryngeal nerve provides sensory innervation above the glottis; therefore, incomplete blockade may leave the laryngeal inlet responsive. In the control group, topical glottic anesthesia may have been cleared by secretions and distributed unevenly. This was reflected by more frequent coughing and slower advancement of both the bronchoscope and the tracheal tube. Previous studies have shown that ultrasound-guided superior laryngeal nerve block provides better intubation conditions than landmark-guided techniques and topical anesthetic methods. This may be attributed to more uniform anesthetic spread and reduced reflex activity [22, 23]. In contrast, topical anesthesia administered through a fiberoptic bronchoscope depends on adequate mucosal coverage and is readily affected by airway secretions, resulting in variable effects. These findings are consistent with the higher success rate observed in this study and suggest that improved block quality may facilitate smoother passage through the glottis.

The experimental group showed a significantly lower cough response, with more patients exhibiting lower-grade responses. This is consistent with the role of the superior laryngeal nerve in supraglottic sensation [24]. Reduced reflex activity also contributes to more stable conditions during airway manipulation. Previous studies have shown that effective airway blockade is associated with reduced stress responses and less fluctuation in heart rate and blood pressure [25]. Although tachycardia and hypertension were less common in the block group than in the control group, the differences were not statistically significant. Sympathetic responses may still occur in awake patients, particularly in the absence of additional sedation, which may have reduced the differences between the two groups [26].

There were no cases of laryngospasm, bleeding, or severe superior laryngeal nerve injury. This is noteworthy given the close proximity of the nerve to the superior laryngeal artery. The technique relied on the identification of the thyrohyoid membrane and confirmation of anesthetic spread by observing its downward displacement. Local anesthetic spread above the membrane appeared sufficient to achieve effective blockade while helping to avoid intravascular or intraneural injection. In the control group, several patients with severe coughing required a change in the anesthetic plan. Early intervention may have helped prevent laryngospasm. Previous studies have shown that ultrasound guidance reduces the risk of vascular puncture and improves the accuracy of nerve block compared with blind techniques [27]. In the present study, downward displacement of the thyrohyoid membrane served as an additional indicator of effective and safe anesthetic spread.

Patient-reported comfort was better in the block group. This difference is consistent with the reduced cough response and smoother intubation process. Fewer interruptions and a shorter procedural time likely contributed to less discomfort. These variables are interrelated and may reflect the overall quality of airway anesthesia. Similar findings have been reported in studies of airway nerve block, in which improved anesthetic quality was associated with better patient tolerance and satisfaction [28]. These results underscore the importance of optimizing airway anesthesia to improve both procedural success and patient experience.

In the control group, five patients experienced a severe cough reflex during the attempt to advance the fiberoptic bronchoscope through the glottis, which prevented successful passage. To avoid the risk of laryngospasm due to inadequate anesthesia, only one attempt was made. The failure may be attributed to the presence of excessive secretions at the glottic level, which potentially diminished the efficacy of local anesthetic infiltration and consequently hindered the advancement of the bronchoscope. Subsequently, these five patients from the control group, who had initially failed intubation, were managed electively with awake tracheal intubation using the technique employed in the experimental group, and all were successfully intubated.

Two technical points should be noted. First, rotating the probe by approximately 30° after obtaining the long-axis view provided a more favorable needle trajectory and a clearer view. Second, an out-of-plane approach guided by the ultrasound M-line facilitated more accurate targeting of the midline. The junction of the thyroid cartilage and thyrohyoid membrane, where membrane depression was observed during injection, served as a useful endpoint. Additional airway anesthesia was also administered. A topical glossopharyngeal nerve block was performed by atomization, and a transtracheal injection through the cricothyroid membrane was administered in two small aliquots. The initial aliquot partially anesthetized the tracheal mucosa to attenuate the cough reflex, thereby minimizing needle movement during the subsequent injection. The combination of these techniques enabled consistent suppression of gag and intratracheal reflexes. The modified ultrasound-guided block was performed by a single anesthesiologist with extensive experience in regional anesthesia and difficult airway management. A learning curve likely exists for this technique, particularly for the 30° rotation and out-of-plane M-line approach. Therefore, our results represent a “best-case scenario” and may not be directly generalizable to centers without dedicated training.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the precision of effect estimates and reduce the robustness of the findings, particularly for secondary outcomes. Second, patients were not randomly assigned but were grouped according to routine clinical practice, which may have introduced selection bias. Although the two groups were well balanced in terms of baseline characteristics, which partially mitigates concerns about confounding, the lack of randomization remains a limitation that could affect the internal validity of the study. Third, this study did not prospectively measure the time required for performing the block or topical anesthesia, which may underestimate total procedural time in the experimental group. Additionally, the sample size was not based on a power calculation, and the study may be underpowered for some secondary outcomes. Therefore, the results for secondary endpoints should be considered exploratory. Finally, no long-term follow-up was conducted, and delayed complications were therefore not evaluated.

In conclusion, the modified ultrasound-guided superior laryngeal nerve block improved the efficiency, success, and patient comfort of awake tracheal intubation. It also reduced airway reflex responses without increasing complications. Further randomized studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings and clarify the broader clinical applicability of this technique.
