Structural Racism and Chronic Kidney Disease Among African Americans: A Research Process Guided by Public Health Critical Race Praxis
Authors: Collins O. Airhihenbuwa, Khadijah Ameen, Alexis J. Smith, Nicole M. Franks, Megan Urbanski, Rhonda Harris, Bobby Howard, Janice Lea, Kimberly Jacob Arriola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Structural racism has a profound impact on the ability of Black patients to access needed chronic kidney disease (CKD) treatment and care. Studies that have documented various forms of inequities in CKD diagnosis and treatment of CKD among Black patients are mostly focused on kidney disease outcomes with little attention to the processes of intervention implementation. Focusing on outcomes alone tends to assume the process of research, including study design and implementation, is neutral to the outcome. In actuality, the process, including methods employed by the researcher, all influence and shape the outcomes of research. Thus, closing gaps in differences in health outcomes requires a different approach; one that attends to the critical role of ‘the process’ of investigating these outcomes in research. In this paper, we focus on the processes employed to develop an intervention to address the role of structural racism in CKD treatment and care while acknowledging the importance of documenting disparities in outcomes. We emphasize the need to prioritize engagement with community leaders in the process of research interventions to be implemented in the community.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global public health problem that continues to affect more than 30 million adults in the US.1 Care for CKD exists along a continuum of primary care for early detection, risk management, and treatment initiation, secondary care for CKD complications, and multidisciplinary care for renal replacement therapy.2 Along this continuum exists key medical decision points such as referral to nephrology, initiation of pre-transplant workup, initiation of pre-dialysis care, education of dialysis treatment options beyond the default of in-clinic hemodialysis, and referral to transplant. Patients are ideally referred from various healthcare settings (including but not limited to primary care) to nephrology early in their disease process to maximize organ preservation. According to clinical practice guidelines, patients should receive 6–12 months of pre-dialysis nephrology care before estimated dialysis start date and are referred for preemptive transplant prior to the initiation of dialysis or are offered multiple options for dialysis treatment including home hemodialysis to enhance clinical efficiency and patient satisfaction.3 If done well, this pathway of care improves quality of life, reduces the need for deceased donor transplant, decreases the likelihood of allograft failure, and improves long-term patient survival. However, racial disparities exist at each point along this pathway of care due to the potent effects of structural racism.

Racial disparities in advanced CKD treatment, care, and patient outcomes are profound, persistent, and pervasive. Despite a large volume of studies linking structural racism to a range of health outcomes, only recently has there been a surge in attention to the role of structural racism as a driver of health disparities in CKD.4–10 Specifically, structural racism inhibits Black patients living in the United States from having equal access to wealth, employment, income, and healthcare all of which directly contribute to racial disparities in CKD treatment, care, and patient outcomes. In this paper, we use the racial identity ‘Black’ to include African Americans, Africans and Caribbean immigrants living in the US.

Structural racism is considered a fundamental cause of the origin and persistence of racial disparities in health.11 Relatedly, the Theory of Fundamental Causes posits that there are social factors that determine access to important resources (including knowledge, power, social connections, financial resources, and prestige) that then determine exposure to the risk factors and risk environments that serve as the more proximal determinants of health.12 For CKD in Black populations, these fundamental causes tend to operate through multiple intervening social and clinical mechanisms to influence health outcomes.13

Structural racism impacts CKD care access through social and structural drivers such as residential neighborhood segregation and socioeconomic deprivation that disproportionately affect Black communities. Structural racism also influences clinical decision-making and may result in poorer kidney health outcomes for Black patients in clinical diagnosis and treatment.9,14,15 Using race-correction in clinical decision-making, despite race being a social construct, remains an injustice in healthcare with negative health consequences for Black patients.16 For example, the use of race in clinical algorithms to assess kidney function has led to differential readings for creatinine level as well as poor referral systems for nephrology and kidney transplantation.17,18 As part of an institutional response to addressing structural racism related to kidney health, the National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of Nephrology recommended removing the race-based calculation of the estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR).19 This race-based calculation was found to inflate the eGFR for Black patients, thus leading to delays in treatment for kidney disease, including transplants, for US Black patients.20,21 These equity focused policy changes notwithstanding, the practice of racializing health care treatment remains normative and a contentious issue in clinical decision-making including diagnosing, treating, and managing Black patients.22

Structural racism impacts CKD progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) through increased physical, environmental, and psychological stress, which contributes to accelerated decline in renal function.23 Discriminatory kidney allocation policies like racial inequities in human leukocyte antigens (HLA) matching, performance risk adjustment for race and ethnicity by organ procurement organizations (OPOs), and lack of APOL1 genetic testing of Black living kidney donors adversely affect Black patients’ access to both living and deceased donor kidney transplantation.17,24 Black patients have a prevalence of end-stage-kidney disease that is almost three times that of White patients.25 Black patients are less likely to be referred to nephrology, receive patient-centered dialysis modality education, and undergo planned dialysis initiation than White patients.26 Moreover, access to home dialysis modalities with all its clinical efficiencies and patient benefits is lower for Black patients as is access to preemptive transplant and any type of transplant.27,28 For decades, researchers have documented the cumulative disease burden that structural racism has placed on Black populations, resulting in higher morbidity and mortality in general and CKD treatment and care.4,6,7,14 Despite the pronounced impact of structural racism on kidney health, many interventions to address health disparities in CKD and ESKD have focused only on the patient-level rather than the health systems-level, reenforcing the notion of “patient blaming”.

This paper is centered on describing the detailed methods employed to highlight the methodological significance of the process when conducting antiracism research in interventions to reduce disparities in health outcomes. Major efforts to understand and close racial gaps in the delivery of clinical care typically use data on health outcomes such as mortality and interventions are often framed to focus primarily on outcome variables. Focusing on outcomes alone tends to assume the process of research, including study design, implementation, and evaluation, is neutral to the outcome. In actuality, the process, including methods employed by the researcher, all influence and shape the outcomes of research. Thus, closing gaps in differences in health outcomes requires a different approach; one that attends to the critical role of ‘the process’ of investigating these outcomes in research. In this paper, we emphasize the need to focus on the processes employed in research investigations and health care delivery while acknowledging the importance of documenting disparities in outcomes. To address these gaps in illuminating research processes, a race-conscious approach to research is pivotal in achieving equity in kidney health for Black Americans.29–31 The Public Health Critical Race Praxis (PHCRP) is a critical race and equity-focused approach to address research processes that are central to actions necessary for addressing structural racism.32 In what follows, we describe how we used the PHCRP as a guiding framework in our research focused on addressing structural racism in a health care system to improve kidney health for Black Americans.

The aim of this paper is to illustrate the process of a study that was focused on solutions to structural racism. Dismantling structural racism is considered an important key to reducing kidney health disparities and should not be overlooked in intervention design and implementation aimed at improving kidney health of Black populations.33 Rather than focus exclusively on CKD outcomes, our intervention study was designed to focus on the process as well as outcomes as two necessary elements of kidney health research. By process, we ask three research questions: 1) Who is telling the story; 2) What tools are we using to tell the story; and 3) What is the message? The central hypothesis for our study was that with substantial guidance from a Community Advisory Board (CAB) and a Clinical Advisory Board (ClAB), comprehensive systems change implemented through an anti-racism lens would improve access to key steps of kidney care along the CKD continuum among Black patients, thereby decreasing existing racial disparities.

Methods

To address the first question of who is telling the story, we describe the research team of CLARIFy-KD (Collaborating Leaders to Address Racism with Interventions For Equity-Kidney Disease). CLARIFy-KD was a National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) funded project designed to improve kidney health among Black populations in Atlanta. CLARIFy-KD research team members were mostly Black thus having a shared identity with the study population. This study made visible research actors who initiate the project and community-engaged partnerships that are fully involved in defining, shaping and refining how the study was to be conducted. The study engaged an iterative process in which every step of planning, implementation, and evaluation was shared with a Community Advisory Board (CAB) and a Clinical Advisory Board (ClAB). The CAB, led by a coauthor (Howard), was comprised of 14 Black patients, patient advocates, caregivers, and community organization representatives. The CAB brought a patient-centered perspective to the question of how structural racism drives racial disparities along the CKD continuum. The CAB ensured that our project was founded on the wishes and realities of the people who are most affected by the interventions to have maximal benefit. The voice of the CAB ensured that the interventions were more likely to be acceptable and appropriate. The CAB met on a monthly basis where we solicited input for the interventions. The ClAB was led by a coauthor (Franks), an emergency medicine physician and supported by another coauthor (Lea), a nephrologist. The ClAB was comprised of 18 Emory Healthcare leaders and clinicians of various specializations, 15 of whom identify as Black. The CIAB were drawn from the Emory Healthcare Diversity, Equity & Inclusion (DEI), leaders across the primary study site (Emory University Hospital Midtown) with a strategic plan aimed at improving inclusive culture and addressing health inequities. The range of represented specialties included nursing, care management, obstetrics & gynecology, emergency medicine, hospital medicine, general internal medicine, neurology and nephrology. Represented leadership spanned from senior leaders from the health system, school of medicine division leadership, hospital administration and digital health. The goals of the CIAB were 1) Collaborate across the care continuum to address healthcare disparities by developing meaningful interventions, 2) Use a forum to share ideas, analyze data, develop new and support ongoing projects and spread solutions across populations. The overall role of the CIAB were to provide candid feedback on structural racism, CKD disparities interventions, and advocate and support implementation of solutions. The CIAB met monthly, to learn about the progress of the interventions and provide feedback. The ClAB was also to conduct a rigorous quantitative and qualitative evaluation of process measures, guided by an extension of the Reach Effectiveness Adoption Implementation Maintenance (RE-AIM) Framework, which was to provide critical data to help explain and contextualize results from the effectiveness evaluation according to implementation practices across diverse settings (e.g., emergency room, primary care, nephrology care) and among a variety of users (e.g., physicians, patients).34

The study’s two major components are 1) to screen and identity patients with advanced kidney disease and 2) to deliver patient-centered kidney health coaching. For Screening and identifying patients with advanced kidney disease, we sought to identify patients seen in the Emergency department, the hospital, the primary care setting, and nephrology with lab values within the past year that are indicative of advanced kidney disease. A clinical practice alert in the electronic medical record was developed to prompt providers to refer patients to primary care or nephrology or continue to follow-up with assessing their CKD risk depending on lab values. A CKD Risk order set was to be ordered under certain circumstances, so that patients could undergo lab testing prior to their next appointment, allowing for the calculation of the kidney failure risk equation, which is an individual risk prediction tool. Having the kidney failure risk equation (KFRE) available guides treatment decision-making at that next appointment.35 To deliver patient-centered kidney health coaching, three full-time kidney health coaches (KHCs) were hired and trained to deliver patient-centered health coaching. All of them self-identify as Black or African American. These non-medical or lay individuals were selected based on their interpersonal skills (including being an active listener, having empathy and compassion, being a creative problem solver, and dedicated to supporting patients with kidney disease).36 All three KHC on CLARIFy-KD have had personal experience (either themselves or a family member) with CKD. The KHCs underwent training to deliver patient support that aligns with the constructs of the Chronic Care Model based on the dimensions below: a) Clinical information systems: Use of an electronic medical record platform to document patient interactions and share patient preferences with clinical care providers; b) Patient self-management and treatment decision-making support: CKD educational resources and psychosocial support; individualized care planning using a shared decision-making framework; c) Delivery system redesign: Hiring and training of KHCs who were to be well-integrated into the care delivery system through appointment attendance, facilitating patient/provider communication through the patient portal, and activating patients around their CKD treatment and care; and d) Community resources: Identifying barriers and facilitators to nephrology appointment attendance and CKD self-management in order to provide resources using an existing social determinants of health tool that currently exists within the electronic medical record.37

Regarding the tool we are using to tell the story (our second question), the PHCRP was first published in 2010 as a health equity lens of critical race theory (CRT).38 PHCRP provides a semi-structured process for conducting research and practicing public health that attends to the role of structural racism while maintaining methodological rigor.32,38 Co-developed by the first author (Airhihenbuwa), this framework has four areas of focus: a) contemporary racial relations shape how racism affects health, b) researchers must use knowledge to disrupt the root causes of inequities, c) conceptualization and measurement must rely on social and political explanations for health disparities, and d) the voices of marginalized groups must be centered in the production of knowledge (Figure 1). From within and across the connecting domains of these four foci, 10 principles provide a methodological framework to guide this research with an anti-racism lens. Although PHCRP has been applied to a broad array of public health issues including police violence, sex trafficking, and curriculum development, this type of approach has been largely absent in interventions designed to eliminate racial disparities in CKD.39,40 Moreover, while individual disease outcomes and group-level reporting continue to be necessary, they are not necessarily intentional about focusing on the process and therefore insufficient to address the disproportionate burden of disease borne by Black populations. The new focus must be on the process of conducting research and include questions such as: Who is asking the questions? What questions are being asked? Whose voices are being engaged in the process? What methods are being used to generate data for analysis and interpretations? Addressing these questions is the primary aim of the principles and foci of PHCRP. The four foci of PHCRP asks 1) how does racism operate at the institutional level today; 2) what are some assumptions about what is considered relevant knowledge as applies to the issue being addressed; 3) what methods are being employed to apply what is known to achieve what is expected; and 4) what actions are being taken and whose voices are considered central to these actions.38 In the past few years, the timeliness of PHCRP has become more evident as researchers began to acknowledge the role of racism as a public health problem and the specific need to address structural racism.41

In Table 1, we present an overview of the ways in which the processes of intervention implementation were nested within the four foci of PHCRP. A key part of the process was the ways in which we engaged the CAB as co-researchers in an iterative process of the planning and implementation of the interventions. At the monthly meeting in which project status was presented, the CAB’s constructive and engaged feedback regularly resulted in modifications that made the intervention more responsive to the needs of CKD patients. Every phase and component of the intervention design and implementation process was done with continuous input and revision by the CAB. The iterative process drew on the CAB to better capture the ways in which racism and contemporary racialization (see PHCRP focus 1) of Black kidney patients impacts how they navigate the health system and how the health system interacts with them. For example, the CAB offered critical insights based on their lived kidney health experiences and gave voice (see PHCRP focus 2) to 1) why it was essential to have an individual to coach patients as they navigate the health care system; and 2) why such a trained person should be called a kidney health coach instead of a navigator. In our process of conceptualization, we elevated structural competency, which focuses on institutional change, as an important intervention outcome instead of focusing solely on individual health literacy. We should not rely only on individual capacity and choices without attending to structural and systemic accountability, as provided by the Clinical Advisory Board (ClAB) (see focus 3 on PHCRP). We believe that aspects of institutional impediments should be a component of structural competency, to better illustrate institutional accountability in the degree to which individuals are able to find, understand, and use information and services to inform health-related decisions and actions for themselves and others. Relatedly, health literacy measures remain vital enough that CAB asked that the measurements should be included as part of the planned data collection measures to evaluate the CLARIFy-KD intervention. When addressing structural racism, we wanted to be sure to acknowledge any evident commitment of an institution to be structurally competent in addressing and sustaining changes in health care policy and practices.42,43 Part of this commitment is why we have regular engagement with the ClAB made up of clinicians of different health and medical specializations, including nephrologists. PHCRP also focuses on the process of research since the researcher is the storyteller, and the storyteller influences the messaging of the subject of the story (see PHCRP focus 4). Since structural racism requires a focus on institutional policies and practices, framing the story of kidney health was an opportunity in this study to focus on ways to improve health care policies and practices at Emory Healthcare beyond individual behaviors of patients seeking care.

A key message in CLARIFy-KD (our third question) implementation was that the process of conducting antiracism research is as important as the outcome of the research. Indeed, it is the process that frames the outcome. CLARIFy-KD used PHCRP to guide the development of a kidney health coach intervention. The kidney health coaches represent a critical bridge in a process in which voices of the community become central to decision making. When the study intervention was designed, the initial plan did not include the use of kidney health coaches. When the plan was presented to the CAB, it was the CAB that requested to have trained non-clinical professionals whose guidance would help Black patients navigate and negotiate receiving care in the Emory healthcare system while drawing on community resources as part of the care continuum. The non-clinical professional was first proposed to be a ‘health navigator’. Upon presenting the idea of health navigator to the CAB, the group expressed their concern for the use of the term and instead preferred the term ‘Health coach’ based on their experience. Their reasoning was that the word navigator, even though commonly used for this type of support, makes more sense to the clinicians and research investigators than to community they serve. Moreover, they felt that the responsibility of the health coach should serve as a guide for patients to access resources in their communities and responds better to addressing health-related social needs than the conventional role of a navigator who guides patients in response to only their clinical needs.

In CLARIFy-KD, the majority of the research team and ClAB identify as Black or African American, while all 14 members of CAB are Black. While racial concordance may be a factor in higher quality care for Black patients, we do not assume that such concordance would guarantee elimination of disparities in health outcomes.44 However, it does offer opportunity for an iterative and community-empowered research process whereby the researchers are active listeners to the voices of the community. This is important in research as praxis whereby action drives the conceptualization of the investigation. One example, a form of disciplinary self-critique, is when the PI and coauthor (Arriola) repeatedly reminded all co-researchers within a consortium of this grant that the Emergency Room must be included as a recruitment site given the number of Black patients who end up there due to unmet health-related social needs. CLARIFy-KD study was intentional in its plan to recruit patients through both primary care and the emergency room with the knowledge that Black, uninsured patients are more likely to seek critical care through the ER than a PCP. This commitment to ER recruitment is a result of the aforementioned processes and conversations that reiterate the need to take actions that prioritize those most marginalized by the health care system.

In CLARIFy-KD, the training of three KHC considered both the health literacy of the patient and institutional accountability through actions guided by the ClAB. An eight modules curriculum was developed for KHC and included a second module focused on the “action” foci of PHCRP. The importance of addressing the process of generating data for systemic change even as we focus on CKD outcomes in Black patients was emphasized in the Kidney Health Coaches’ training.

Discussion

Racial disparities in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) treatment, care, and outcomes are profound and pervasive. Structural racism impacts the ability of Black patients to access needed CKD treatment and care. Using an antiracism framework in health disparities research could improve intervention implementation processes and CKD outcomes for Black patients. Structural racism should be addressed in ways that engages both the voices of community members who have experienced kidney disease and treatment as well as clinicians who have treated Black patients with kidney disease. These are the voices needed to amplify research processes that are necessary to address structural racism in health care, including the determined outcomes to assess success and the measures employed to generate those outcomes.

It is worth noting that this project has gone through six months (April-September 2025) of disruptive processes: a) On April 30, 2025, this project was terminated because it was deemed to be misaligned with the new federal priorities; b) On July 11, 2025, it was reinstated as part of the partial final judgment issued from the American Public Health Association vs. NIH lawsuit filed on 4.2.25 and the remainder of year 2 funds were issued; c) On August 21, 2025, a decision by the Supreme Court of the United States relaxed the requirement for an immediate reinstatement of the grant; d) On September 11, 2025, the NOA (notice of award) was issued providing funding for year 03 (i.e., July 1, 2025-June 30, 2026); and finally e) On September 12, 2025 we began the process of rehiring previous staff and hiring new staff.

While we feel some relief that we can continue our project, we do note that the disruption resulted in losing critical staff members, time needed to meet our recruitment targets, and momentum from developing the intervention and study protocol. Staff morale is greatly impacted because of the substantial fear and uncertainty generated by the termination. Universities are now also required to assume greater financial risk due to the uncertainty of this funding stream.

Conclusions

We approached this study emphasizing the central role of research processes, even as we continue to work toward closing gaps in disparate kidney health outcomes that disproportionately burden Black populations. This paper addresses our intentionality of elevating research processes both in ‘Who is asking the questions’ (mostly Black researchers, mostly Black ClAB, and all Black CAB), what method are they using (i.e. PHCRP) and what questions are being asked to potentially improve kidney health for Black patients (process and outcome questions). Our recommendations include the importance of process and how PHCRP can be used as guide to engagement in antiracism process. This study also emphasizes the importance of identifying and addressing racism at the structural, institutional, and systems-level, and not just at the interpersonal and intrapersonal levels. Finally, this study highlights the significance of centering community and patient voices in intervention decision-making processes.
