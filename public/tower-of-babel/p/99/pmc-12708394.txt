NF Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2 Deficiency Enhances the Immunosuppressive Function of Monocytic Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells in a Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor-γ-Dependent Manner
Authors: Yichen Jia, Jiawei Li, Tianying Yang, Ruimin Li, Guowei Tu, Yue Qiu, Xuepeng Zhang, Ruirui Sang, Yi Shi, Shihao Xu, Yin Celeste Cheuk, Jingjing Liu, Ruiming Rong, Yi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are immature myeloid cells which serve to limit T-cell responses. MDSCs are increased in numerous pathologic processes, including cancer, traumatic stress, organ transplantation, and various chronic inflammatory diseases.1 Emerging evidence indicates that MDSCs can exhibit different phenotypes in response to specific environmental conditions. MDSCs typically comprised of monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) and granulocytic MDSCs (G-MDSCs), each exhibiting a different morphology and surface marker expression. It has been accepted that MDSCs are identifiable based on the coexpression of surface marker CD11b but distinct expression of Ly6C and Ly6G, depending on the MDSC subtype, with M-MDSCs displaying Ly6G−Ly6Chi and G-MDSCs displaying Ly6G+Ly6Clo. Although both MDSC subtypes seem to be immunosuppressive, they are associated with different dominant functions, depending on the specific disease. In an allo-skin-transplanted model, the number of both M-MDSCs and G-MDSCs was increased in the allograft. The depletion of mammalian target of rapamycin using rapamycin was found to dramatically alleviate the allograft rejection by decreasing the number of M-MDSCs but not G-MDSCs, implying that M-MDSCs might be more essential in the rejection process.2 However, G-MDSCs represent more than 80% of all MDSCs in tumor-bearing mice and are involved in mediating tumor escape. Therefore, the function of the two MDSC subtypes in the context of different pathologic conditions requires further clarification.

As one of the most important regulators of antioxidant cell defense, many studies have reported that NF erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) participates in MDSC-mediated immunosuppression. Nrf2 induces antioxidant gene expression when activated by reactive oxygen species (ROS), which is implicated in signaling transduction pathways controlling several biologic functions (e.g., immunosuppression). Under homeostatic conditions, Nrf2 is bound to Kelch erythroid-cell-derived protein with CNC homology-associating protein 1 (Keap1) in the cytoplasm. Cellular stimuli, including oxidative stress, results in the release of Nrf2 from Keap1 and subsequent changes in the activation of downstream gene expression. Recent studies by us and others indicate that Nrf2 may interact with NF-κB in various cellular disorders, and a blockade of Nrf2 expression is correlated with changes in the NF-κB signaling pathway.3,4 In addition, Nrf2 has been described as an essential factor in myeloid differentiation and skews the differentiation potential of hematopoietic stem cells toward the granulocyte-monocyte lineage5; however, the role of Nrf2 in the MDSC differentiation and function remains contractionary.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ), known for its anti-inflammatory role, promotes immunosuppressive factors and signaling molecules. Blockade of PPARγ in myeloid cells triggers tumorigenesis and systemic inflammation by reducing T-cell numbers and function.6 The critical role of PPARγ has also been shown in vivo, as a selective PPARγ antagonist was found to completely inhibit the transcriptional activity of PPARγ and MDSC induction.7 Whether PPARγ can further regulate MDSC differentiation and subset functionality remains unclear.

In this study, we used a bone marrow (BM) in vitro induction model, tumor-bearing mice model, and inflammatory mice models to systematically demonstrate that an Nrf2 deficiency enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs. More importantly, the function of M-MDSCs with an Nrf2 deficiency was found to be PPARγ-dependent, which could be dramatically inhibited by GW9662, a specific inhibitor of PPARγ.

Methods

Mice

Male C57BL/6 and Nrf2−/− mice (8−10 weeks of age) were purchased from the Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and Institute of Biology, Nanjing University (Nanjing, China), respectively. All mice were kept under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water. All animal assays were approved by the Animal Ethical Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, Shanghai, China.

Tumorigenesis in Mice

The mouse melanoma cell line, B16-F10, was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection and was routinely cultured in high glucose DMEM (Gibco, NY) supplemented with 10% FBS (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) at 37°C and 5% CO2. Tumor cells (5×105 cells/mouse) were collected and subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank regions of C57BL/6 or Nrf2−/− mice. Tumor nodules were measured every 7 days using calipers and calculated as volume=length×width2/2. Mice were euthanized after 14 days.

Cell Preparations

Mononuclear cells from the spleens of wild-type (WT) mice were obtained by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. CD4+ T-cells were magnetically purified from spleen mononuclear cells in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations (Miltenyi Biotec, Auburn, CA). The purity of the sorted cells in this study was consistently >95%.

T-Cell Proliferation Assays

Freshly isolated CD4+ T-cells from WT mice were labeled with 2.5 μM carboxifluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (Invitrogen) and cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium with Concanavalin A (50 μg/ml; Sigma, St. Louis) to stimulate T-cell proliferation. The sorted M-MDSCs or G-MDSCs from C57BL/6 or Nrf2−/− mice were cocultured in graded doses with CD4+ T-cells (1×105 cells/well) for 3 days. The cells were harvested, and carboxifluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester fluorescence intensities were measured using flow cytometry.

Induction and Treatment of Kidney Ischaemia-Reperfusion Injury

C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneal (i.p.) injected with pentobarbital (40 mg/kg; Sigma) as an anesthetic. After separating the right kidney, ligating the right renal pedicle and the right ureter, the right kidney was removed. The left renal pedicle was clipped with noninvasive microvascular clips. After 30 minutes, the blood vessel clamp was removed, and 1 ml of normal saline was i.p. injected. For the indicated experiments, mice were intravenous (i.v.) injected with 1×106 M-MDSCs or G-MDSCs from C57BL/6 or Nrf2−/− mice 4 hours after the operation.

Induction and Treatment of CKDs

C57BL/6 mice were i.p. injected with pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, sigma) as an anesthetic. The left kidney was separated, two thirds of the kidney was removed, and two swabs were used to stop the bleeding. After 7 days, the right kidney was removed as described above. For the indicated experiments, mice were i.v. injected with 1×106 M-MDSCs or G/MDSCs from WT or Nrf2−/− mice on days 0 and 7.

RNA Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

WT and Nrf2−/− M-MDSC RNA were extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was used, and single-end 75-bp-length reads were generated. The data were aligned to the mouse genome (mm9 version) using the TopHat2 algorithm. The HT-seq and DESeq algorithms were used to measure the level of gene expression and identify the differential expression between the two groups of cells. Genes with a P value ≤ 0.05 and fold change ≥1.5 or ≤−1.5 were considered to be upregulated and downregulated, respectively. Gene ontology analysis, pathway annotation, transcription factor enrichment, and comparison with various immunologic and oncogenic gene signatures were performed using the database for annotation visualization and integrated discovery knowledge base, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis software, and Molecular Signature Database from the Broad Institute.

Cytokine Analysis

The levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in the serum were assessed using commercial ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical Analyses

A Student t test was used to analyze differences between groups. Where appropriate, a one-way ANOVA was initially performed to determine whether an overall statistically significant change existed using a two-tailed paired or unpaired Student t test. All tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Holdings, San Diego, CA) and SPSS version 13.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Other materials and methods are described in the Supplemental Material.

Results

Nrf2 Deficiency Enhances the Immunosuppressive Function of M-MDSCs In Vitro

To investigate the role of Nrf2 in the regulation of MDSCs, BM cells were harvested from WT and Nrf2−/− mice and subsequently induced to differentiate into MDSCs. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of total MDSCs within the CD11b+ cell population from Nrf2−/− mice compared with WT controls (Figure 1, A and B). Specifically, the percentage of CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi M-MDSCs was elevated in Nrf2−/− mice, whereas the proportion of CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clo G-MDSCs remained unchanged (Figure 1C). Furthermore, the G-MDSC/M-MDSC ratio was notably reduced in the Nrf2−/− MDSCs (Figure 1D), indicating a shift toward the monocytic subset.

To assess the functional implications of Nrf2 deficiency on MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, CD4+ T-cell proliferation was stimulated using Concanavalin A in the presence or absence of MDSCs derived from either WT or Nrf2−/− mice. Both WT and Nrf2−/− G-MDSCs were capable of suppressing T-cell proliferation (Figure 1E). However, Nrf2-deficient M-MDSCs exhibited a markedly stronger, dose-dependent inhibition of T-cell proliferation, with even low numbers of M-MDSCs exerting potent immunosuppressive effects (Figure 1F). These findings suggest that Nrf2 deficiency selectively enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs in vitro, highlighting a potential mechanism for modulating MDSC activity in immune regulation.

Nrf2 Deficiency Promotes the Immunosuppressive Function of M-MDSCs In Vivo

MDSCs are key players in tumor progression by suppressing T-cell activity. To investigate whether Nrf2 deficiency influences MDSC differentiation in vivo, we established a tumor-bearing model by subcutaneously injecting B16-F10 melanoma cells into WT and Nrf2−/− mice. Tumor growth was significantly increased in Nrf2−/− mice compared with WT controls 14 days postinoculation (Figure 2A). Histologic analysis revealed enhanced tumor angiogenesis in the Nrf2−/− mice (Figure 2B), suggesting that Nrf2 deficiency plays an essential role in promoting tumor progression. To further study the cellular mechanisms, T-cell infiltration into tumors and the differentiation of MDSCs were examined in tumor-bearing WT and Nrf2−/− mice. Immunohistochemical staining indicated decreased infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells into the tumors of Nrf2−/− mice (Figure 2C). Flow cytometry analysis showed an increase in the percentage of CD11b+ cells in both the spleen and tumor tissues of Nrf2−/− mice (Figure 2D). Moreover, the percentage of G-MDSCs was largely decreased, while M-MDSCs were markedly increased in Nrf2−/− mice (Figure 2, E and F). As a result, the G-MDSC/M-MDSC ratio was reduced in Nrf2−/− mice (Figure 2G). In functional assays, M-MDSCs from Nrf2−/− mice exhibited stronger immunosuppressive activity, significantly inhibiting CD4+ T-cell proliferation compared with G-MDSCs isolated from the same tumors (Figure 2H).

To further confirm the immunosuppressive role of M-MDSCs, we performed adoptive transfer experiments. G-MDSCs or M-MDSCs were isolated from Nrf2−/− tumor-bearing mice and transferred into WT tumor-bearing mice. Histologic analysis showed that Nrf2−/− G-MDSCs promoted tumor growth, while Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs caused a more dramatic increase in tumor size (Supplemental Figure 1A) and tumor angiogenesis (Supplemental Figure 1B). Moreover, the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells into the tumor (Supplemental Figure 1C) was significantly inhibited in mice receiving Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Nrf2 deficiency enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs in vivo, contributing to tumor progression.

Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs Attenuate AKI in Mice

In vivo studies were performed to further validate the immunosuppressive function of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs. WT or Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs were i.v. injected into AKI mice. Notably, although the WT M-MDSCs significantly attenuated the level of inflammatory cell infiltration into the kidneys, dilation of renal tubules, and edema, the Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs were associated with a more obvious therapeutic effect compared with the WT M-MDSCs (Figure 3A). The observed effects of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs were consistent with a substantial decrease in the serum creatinine (SCr) concentration (Figure 3B), as well as a lower percentage of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells in the spleen (Figure 3C). In addition, the level of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration into the kidneys was greatly decreased after the delivery of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs (Figure 3D). Moreover, treatment of Nrf2-deficient M-MDSCs to AKI mice resulted in a substantially lower level of proinflammatory cytokine production, including TNF-α and IL-1β, in the kidney (Figure 3E). Accordingly, the levels of serum TNF-α and IL-1β were also decreased in AKI mice treated with Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs (Figure 3F). Since the NF-κB signaling pathway is essential for regulating inflammatory responses, related proteins located upstream of NF-κB were examined in the kidneys. As shown in Figure 3G, a greater downregulation in NF-κB and inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B kinase subunit alpha expression, as well as decreased phosphorylation of NF-κB and inhibitor of kappa B alpha, was detected in the Nrf2−/− M-MDSC-treated mice. This finding demonstrates that Nrf2-deficient M-MDSCs attenuated AKI in mice due to a powerful immunosuppressive ability.

Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs Ameliorates CKD in Mice

We next sought to determine whether the enhanced immunosuppressive function of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs in chronic inflammatory responses was consistent with that observed in acute inflammatory responses. To this end, WT M-MDSCs or Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs were i.v. injected into mice with CKD. In the histologic analysis, hematoxylin and eosin staining indicated that treatment with WT M-MDSCs attenuated the tissue damage in the kidneys and treatment with Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs elicited a better therapeutic effect compared witih treatment with WT M-MDSCs (Figure 4A). The fibrosis progression of the kidney tissues was also dramatically decreased in CKD mice treated with Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs as examined by Masson trichrome and Sirius red staining (Figure 4A). Accordingly, we observed a decreased concentration of SCr (Figure 4B), lower percentage of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells in the spleen (Figure 4C), and reduced T-cell infiltration into the kidneys (Figure 4D) in CKD mice treated with Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs compared with WT M-MDSCs. In addition, the level of TNF-α and IL-1β mRNA expression in the kidneys (Figure 4E) as well as the secretion of both proinflammatory cytokines in the serum (Figure 4F) were all decreased in CKD mice treated with Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs compared with WT M-MDSCs. Moreover, we found that NF-κB and inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B kinase subunit alpha expression as well as the phosphorylation of NF-κB and inhibitor of kappa B alpha were markedly decreased in the kidneys of CKD mice treated with Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs compared with WT M-MDSCs (Figure 4G). Taken together, these data indicate that Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs may ameliorate chronic inflammatory responses.

Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs Mediate an Immunosuppressive Function in a PPARγ-Dependent Manner

To explore the molecular mechanisms involved in the immunosuppressive function of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs, M-MDSCs derived from Nrf2−/− mice were induced in vitro and the level of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and Arg1 was evaluated. As shown in Figure 5, A and B, both the level of iNOS mRNA and protein expression was upregulated in Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs compared with that of WT M-MDSCs. Intriguingly, the level of Arg1 mRNA and protein expression was significantly downregulated in Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs (Figure 5, C and D), which suggests that the Nrf2−/− M-MDSC-mediated immunosuppressive function is independent of Arg1 expression.

We further performed mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to investigate the difference in gene expression between WT and Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs. Approximately 1022 differentially expressed genes were identified, as shown by the color gradation (Figure 5E). A gene ontology analysis pointed to an enrichment of oxidoreductase activity related to oxidative stress (Figure 5F). Furthermore, 81 differentially expressed genes (50 upregulated genes and 31 downregulated genes) related to oxidative stress were extracted from the RNA-seq results, and a strong correlation with PPARγ was observed (Figure 5G). Next, real-time PCR was performed to validate the expression of Pparγ-related genes. As shown in Figure 5H, the level of PPARγ, PPARγc1a, PPARγc1b, FOXO1, FOXO3, STAT2, STAT3, TRP53, Sirt1, and Sirt3 mRNA expression was increased, whereas FOXO3 expression was decreased in Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs compared with WT M-MDSCs. Similar results were observed by Western blot, revealing that the level of PPARγ, FOXO3, STAT2, STAT3, TRP53, Sirt1, and Sirt3 protein expression was upregulated in Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs (Figure 5I). The data indicate that Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs have an immunosuppressive function in a PPARγ-dependent manner (Supplemental Figure 2).

PPARγ Blockade with GW9662 Inhibits the Immunosuppressive Function of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs In Vitro and In Vivo

Next, we analyzed whether a blockade of PPARγ could affect the enhanced immunosuppressive role of Nrf2-deficient M-MDSCs. BM derived from Nrf2−/− mice was harvested to induce MDSCs with or without GW9662 treatment, a specific inhibitor of PPARγ. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the addition of GW9662 significantly decreased the percentage of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs and G-MDSC/M-MDSC ratio, but not the percentage of Nrf2−/− G-MDSCs (Figure 6, A and B). As a result, the G-MDSC/M-MDSC ratio was increased (Figure 6C). The suppression of T-cell proliferation mediated by Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs was also blocked by GW9662 in a dose-dependent manner in vitro (Figure 6D).

Furthermore, the in vivo study indicated that PPARγ inhibition markedly reversed the effect of Nrf2−/− M-MDSC treatment in AKI mice, as shown by the more severe pathology score and elevated SCr concentration (Figure 6, E and F). In addition to the role of PPARγ in acute inflammatory disease, treatment with GW9662 had a similar therapeutic effect in chronic inflammatory disease. The histologic analysis indicated that GW9662 treatment promoted the level of tissue damage and kidney fibrosis in CKD mice treated with Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs (Figure 6G). Accordingly, an increased SCr concentration was also associated with GW9662 treatment (Figure 6H). Together, these findings indicate that PPARγ plays an essential role in the enhanced immunosuppressive function of Nrf2-deficient M-MDSCs.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that an Nrf2 deficiency enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, an Nrf2 deficiency promotes the differentiation and suppressive machinery of M-MDSCs under inflammatory conditions through the production of iNOS and inhibition of Arg1 in a PPARγ-dependent manner (Supplemental Figure 3).

MDSCs were first identified in patients with cancer and tumor-inoculated mice, with patients with cancer showing up to ten-fold higher MDSC levels in peripheral blood and 40% of splenocytes in tumor-bearing mice comprising MDSCs.8 MDSCs block immune effector cell function, aiding tumor progression. While G-MDSCs have a short lifespan, M-MDSCs, which accumulate in the spleen and tumor sites, are more stable and have been the focus of research due to their immunosuppressive roles.9,10 M-MDSCs can also have therapeutic effects in inflammation-related diseases. For example, Leslie et al. showed that they protect against early pleurisy by suppressing T-cell inflammation in a TNF-α-dependent manner.11 Our work showed that M-MDSCs promote B16-F10 melanoma growth more than G-MDSCs and improve both acute and chronic inflammation. Thus, M-MDSC-based therapy may be a potential target for inflammatory disorders.

We harvested WT or Nrf2-deficient M-MDSCs from BM or tumor-bearing mice and found that M-MDSCs have stronger immunosuppressive abilities than G-MDSCs, with Nrf2 deficiency enhancing this function. Nrf2, a key regulator of antioxidant genes, protects cells from oxidative stress and inflammation. Kim et al. showed Nrf2 promotes MDSC expansion and balances glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism in LPS-induced sepsis.12 Our findings suggest that Nrf2 negatively regulates MDSCs, as its depletion increased both the quantity and function of M-MDSCs. This contrasts with a study showing Nrf2 increased tumor-infiltrating MDSC numbers and their suppressive activity but did not affect circulating MDSC levels in tumor-bearing mice with mammary or colon carcinomas.13 A potential reason for this inconsistency may be attributed to a feedback mechanism, rather than a direct effect on genes, within BM progenitor cells is involved in MDSC homeostasis mediated by Nrf2.

To test the therapeutic role of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs, we used mouse models of AKI and CKD, which are linked by persistent inflammation during the AKI-to-CKD transition, a critical factor in clinical outcomes.14 Studies in AKI models have revealed more severe inflammation infiltration and a higher mortality in Nrf2 knockout mice, suggesting that Nrf2 plays a protective role against ischemic injury.15,16 Conversely, another report showed that accelerating the activity of Nrf2 with the treatment of Keap1 inhibitor aggravated renal damage,15 indicating that there are complex mechanisms involved in Nrf2 regulation. Our findings confirm that Nrf2-deficient M-MDSC treatment alleviates immune pathogenesis in AKI and CKD, although the mechanisms in specific MDSC subsets require further investigation.

M-MDSCs and G-MDSCs suppress immune responses through different mechanisms: M-MDSCs through iNOS and nitric oxide production (STAT1-dependent), and G-MDSCs through STAT3, NADPH oxidase, and ROS. Both subsets express Arg1. In this study, Nrf2-deficient M-MDSCs showed enhanced suppression with increased iNOS and decreased Arg1, suggesting an unconventional activation. RNA-seq revealed a strong correlation with PPARγ, indicating a potential Arg1-independent mechanism in Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs.

PPARγ is a ligand-activated transcription factor that is mainly expressed in epithelial and innate-immune cells, where it primarily plays an anti-inflammatory role.17 On activation, PPARγ promotes M1-phenotypic macrophages to express M2-phenotypic markers through the NF-κB signaling pathway.18 The immune functions of PPARγ were also revealed through studies which observed that mice with a selective PPARγ deficiency have impaired differentiation of tolerant CD103+ dendritic cells in the airways.19 In addition to the two subtypes of myeloid cells, the induction and mobilization of MDSCs from the BM were markedly promoted by cannabidiol-induced PPARγ activation.7 Our data show that PPARγ is central to the enhanced immunosuppressive function of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs, highlighting a novel role in MDSC differentiation. In addition, PPARγ-related genes may protect against ROS. The forkhead box O (FOXO) family is critical for cell survival, and FOXO1 and FOXO3 are directly involved in the transcriptional regulation of antioxidant genes.20,21 FOXO3 regulates transcription by binding to the PPARγ coactivator promoter, dependent on NO production.22 It has also been demonstrated that the overexpression of PPARγ in 293 T-cells can result in the inhibition of FOXO1-mediated reporter activation, creating a PPARγ-FOXO regulatory feedback loop.23 Sirt1, a member of the sirtuin protein family, is regulated by PPARγ, with its expression increasing after PPARγ agonist treatment in human neural stem cells.24 In cellular senescence, PPARγ mediates the binding of Sirt1 to its promoter, sustaining Sirt1 expression.25 As another essential regulator of cellular protection, Sirt3 has also been a reported target of PPARγ model cells.26 Increased PPARγ acetylation suppresses Sirt1 expression, and inhibiting this acetylation limits FOXO activation through deacetylation.27

In conclusion, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to elucidate that an Nrf2 deficiency enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs and ultimately ameliorates acute and chronic inflammatory disease in mice. Furthermore, the suppressive machinery of Nrf2−/− M-MDSCs was PPARγ-dependent. This study provides a solid foundation for the potential application of Nrf2-modified M-MDSCs for the treatment of inflammatory disorders. Further investigation of Nrf2 deficiency-mediated immunosuppressive mechanisms will be crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying the AKI-to-CKD transition.
