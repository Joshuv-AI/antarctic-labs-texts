A 2 to 16 GHz dual polarized end fire ETS array with corrugated edges for unified ECC evaluation framework
Authors: Zihe Wang, Mengxin Li, Zi-Neng Ng, Khuzairi Masrakin, A. R. A. Rahman, Wei Hown Tee, Yu Chen Lee, Chia Chao Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a reusable evaluation framework for broadband dual-linearly polarized end-fire multiple-input–multiple-output (MIMO) arrays based on exponentially tapered slot (ETS) elements. A 2 × 2 ETS subarray operating from 2 to 16 GHz is used as a case study, where three representative edge treatments are compared: a double-sided corrugated edge (Double-CE), a single-sided corrugated edge (Single-CE), and a bare aperture without corrugation. Under unified port reference planes and a common spherical sampling grid, the framework jointly examines S-parameters, 2D/3D radiation patterns, axial-ratio/circular-component descriptors (as auxiliary polarization indicators), and pattern-based envelope correlation coefficient (ECC). Simulated results indicate that all three strategies achieve broadband impedance matching and maintain low inter-port coupling over most of the 2–16 GHz span under the unified reference configuration, while localized resonant features in coupling and correlation are sensitive to the intra-subarray pitch. The end-fire main beam is preserved at representative frequencies, with mid-band peak realized gains on the order of 9–10 dBi. Pattern-based ECC (Port 1–Port 2) further reveals a frequency- and pitch-dependent isolation–correlation trade-off: Double-CE produces the smoothest radiation envelope and a more uniform multiport environment at the nominal layout, but high-ECC cases can emerge at specific nodes when the pitch is perturbed toward tighter spacing; Single-CE can achieve very low correlation at selected mid-band nodes (e.g., around 8.02 GHz) and provides a favorable trade-off under appropriate pitch settings, whereas elevated ECC may still occur at other nodes. Surface-current snapshots link these metric-level trends to boundary-mediated current channels, edge scattering, and cavity-mode participation, providing practical guidance on choosing symmetric versus intentionally asymmetric edge loading. Although demonstrated on a specific ETS topology, the proposed framework is directly extendable to other end-fire array elements, subarray layouts, and boundary treatments for broadband MIMO applications.

Introduction

Sixth-generation (6G) wireless systems are driving the convergence of integrated communication, sensing, and computing (ISAC), which imposes increasingly stringent requirements on front-end antenna arrays in bandwidth, polarization diversity, isolation, and radiation stability1–3. Broadband dual-polarized end-fire arrays present an ideal solution for these requirements, offering compact apertures with high gain along the array axis while enabling multiple-input–multiple-output (MIMO) functionality4–7. Exponentially tapered-slot (ETS) antennas, commonly referred to as Vivaldi-type tapered-slot radiators, operate in a traveling-wave manner and are widely recognized for their inherently wide impedance bandwidth and suitability for planar integration8–11. From an operating-band perspective, multi-octave Vivaldi implementations have been reported at the array level, for example, balanced antipodal Vivaldi arrays have achieved a 10:1 impedance bandwidth over 1.8–18 GHz, which supports the physical feasibility of targeting a three-octave (8:1) 2–16 GHz span for ETS/Vivaldi-based front ends12. In addition, a microstrip-fed Vivaldi design has demonstrated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {S}_{11}\mid$$\end{document} below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-10 \mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}$$\end{document} over 2–18 GHz, while also noting that frequency-dependent microstrip/stripline-to-slotline transitions can practically limit the achievable bandwidth to only a few octaves if not carefully engineered13. Therefore, the 2–16 GHz band selected in this work is not an arbitrary choice but a multi-octave target that is consistent with prior ETS/Vivaldi bandwidth capability. The key challenge is to meet the constraints of a dual-polarized shared-aperture end-fire array over the entire 2–16 GHz span6,7,14. This requires simultaneously achieving impedance matching, port isolation, end-fire stability, and low pattern-based correlation with a simple planar feed topology.

Under the combined constraints of a 2–16 GHz (8:1), dual linear polarization, and arrayed geometry, three key structural challenges arise.

First, a fundamental trade-off exists between achieving broadband impedance matching and phase consistency. While microstrip-to-slotline coupling with near-throat compensation is inherently weakly dispersive, it cannot simultaneously flatten both phase and amplitude at the low and high band edges, thereby introducing a compromise between matching performance and stable end-fire pointing. Second, achieving high port isolation via simple decoupling techniques does not necessarily ensure low correlation across the array. This is especially true when considering the full far-field patterns, which include cross-polarization and side-lobe characteristics15. Thirdly, in shared-aperture arrays, attempts to improve isolation and cross-polarization discrimination (XPD) through local slotting or shielding often disrupt the effective aperture, leading to a degradation in gain and efficiency at one end of the frequency band4,7.

Recent works on wideband end-fire antenna arrays have made substantial advances in addressing key challenges, but they are typically based on relaxed or fragmented constraints, with a focus on specific performance metrics such as operating band, fractional bandwidth, impedance thresholds (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {S}_{ii}\mid$$\end{document}, VSWR), isolation (often assessed at the full-band minimum of coupling terms), peak gain, efficiency, and aperture size normalized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda}_{min}=c/{f}_{H}$$\end{document}8,14,16–20. Many studies have reported designs with good matching and isolation over sub-octave spans, such as 1.8–6 GHz or 6–18 GHz, while others have focused on beamforming and scanning performance up to 20 GHz, but often without comprehensive MIMO-related metrics such as envelope correlation coefficient (ECC) derived from 3-D radiation patterns or consistent XPD over the full band4,7,8,15. Moreover, the combined effects of boundary engineering (e.g., corrugations, slots, absorbing edges) and taper profiles on both MIMO performance and effective aperture size have not been systematically quantified for multi-octave (8:1) dual-polarized ETS arrays9,10,14,16–18.

To address the limitations identified in previous studies, this work develops a comprehensive three-dimensional evaluation framework for 2–16 GHz dual-polarized end-fire ETS arrays, incorporating a novel boundary engineering technique with corrugated-edge (CE) structures. The evaluation framework spans across the frequency domain, radiation patterns, and MIMO performance metrics. The radiating element is based on ETS/Vivaldi slots fed by a microstrip-to-slotline coupling structure, which includes a quarter-wavelength (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda /4$$\end{document}) compensation stub, enabling broadband unbalanced-to-balanced impedance transformation without the need for complex baluns. Dual linear polarizations along the x- and y-directions are achieved through a cross-orthogonal configuration of identical ETS elements. The array is formulated as a 2 × 2 four-port subarray, serving as a canonical and scalable building block that is compact enough to keep the design space controlled while still capturing essential multiport effects in a shared-aperture dual-polarized end-fire array (inter-element coupling, polarization diversity, and pattern correlation). This subarray-level formulation also enables a fair ablation study of boundary strategies (double-sided CE, single-sided CE, and no CE) under a unified reference configuration, avoiding additional degrees of freedom introduced by large-array feed/beam-steering networks. The subarray-as-building-block rationale is consistent with subarray-based synthesis approaches, where larger arrays can be constructed by repeating well-characterized subarrays21.

To improve isolation and reduce surface-wave propagation, periodically slotted corrugated edges are introduced at the boundary, which act as a high-impedance boundary to suppress edge reradiation and enhance the smoothness of the end-fire envelope, thereby improving sidelobe behavior and isolation. Under a unified reference framework, which includes consistent port reference planes, far-field origins, polarization bases, angular sampling grids, and convergence criteria, three boundary configurations—double-sided CE, single-sided CE, and no CE—are evaluated in a comparative study. Frequency-domain performance is analyzed by full-band curves of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {S}_{ii}\mid$$\end{document}, VSWR, and key isolation terms. Radiation performance is assessed using the main-beam direction, half-power beamwidth (HPBW), sidelobe level (SLL), and peak realized gain derived from both two-dimensional cuts and three-dimensional radiation patterns at representative frequencies. Finally, MIMO performance is quantified using pattern-based ECC as the primary metric, with S-parameter-based ECC being reported as a supplementary upper-bound reference20.

The proposed 2–16 GHz (8:1) dual-polarized end-fire ETS array achieves broadband impedance matching under the unified reference configuration, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {S}_{ii}\mid$$\end{document} remaining below − 10 dB over a large portion of the band and VSWR staying below 2 in the evaluated cases. The port coupling is also reduced to a low level for most frequencies, while local peaks may occur at certain nodes depending on the boundary strategy and the intra-subarray pitch. In terms of radiation, the array preserves an end-fire main beam at representative frequencies and provides a peak realized gain on the order of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$9-10 \mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}\mathrm{i}$$\end{document} in the mid-band. The detailed pattern characteristics are discussed in the radiation-result section. Regarding MIMO correlation, the pattern-based ECC (Port 1–Port 2) is evaluated as the primary metric. The results show a clear dependence on both the corrugated-edge strategy and the layout pitch, and the single-sided corrugation can offer a favorable isolation–correlation trade-off under appropriate pitch settings. Methodology

Performance targets and evaluation metrics

For the proposed 2–16 GHz dual-linearly polarized end-fire array, a unified set of performance targets and evaluation metrics is defined under a common port reference plane and far-field coordinate system27. The metrics are applied consistently across frequency-domain, radiation, and MIMO evaluations so that different boundary configurations and parameter sets can be compared on the same basis27,28.

In the MIMO dimension, correlation is quantified using the ECC. The maximum and mean ECC values across 2–16 GHz are reported as compact indicators of broadband correlation.

Radiation performance is evaluated at a set of representative frequency points covering the band (2.00, 4.03, 6.025, 8.02, 10.015, 12.01, 14.005, and 16.00 GHz), using both two-dimensional cuts and three-dimensional patterns of the realized gain27. The main-beam direction in the end-fire region, the HPBW and the maximum SLL on the principal and orthogonal planes are extracted as indicators of end-fire pointing stability and sidelobe control. Peak realized gain at each representative frequency is also recorded as a compact measure of array aperture efficiency. The linear polarization purity in the main-beam direction is quantified by XPD, which serves as an auxiliary indicator.

Microstrip-to-slotline transition and near-throat λ/4 compensation (U–B conversion)

To realize broadband matching of the end-fire ETS element without introducing an external balun, the feeding network adopts a planar microstrip-to-slotline coupling structure that performs unbalanced-to-balanced (U–B) conversion under a geometrically symmetric throat constraint. The microstrip line excites the slotline through a coupling window etched in the common ground plane, and the slotline in turn feeds the exponentially tapered slot. Owing to the weakly dispersive nature of the transition, the amplitude and phase of the input reflection cannot remain flat across the 2–16 GHz multi-octave (8:1) without additional phase equalization.

A standard linear polarization basis aligned with the global Cartesian axes is adopted: the X-polarized port pair (Ports 1–2) predominantly excites fields aligned with the global \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document}-axis in the main-beam region, whereas the Y-polarized port pair (Ports 3–4) predominantly excites fields aligned with the global \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y$$\end{document}-axis. For pattern-based ECC computation, the complex embedded-element far fields \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{F}}_{i}\left(\theta ,\phi \right)$$\end{document} of each port are first represented in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{\theta }/\widehat{\phi }$$\end{document} basis and then consistently projected onto the same linear polarization basis under the unified angular sampling grid defined in Sect. “Electromagnetic simulation and MIMO performance evaluation”.

Geometrically maintaining symmetry of the two ETS elements and equalizing the electrical length of the four feeds facilitates an approximate separation of odd and even coupling modes between the X and Y branches. This reduces common-mode leakage into the cross-polarized ports and suppresses unintended transverse cavity modes, thereby supporting low cross-polarization levels and high port isolation over the entire 2–16 GHz band. The cross-orthogonal dual-polarized configuration and four-port reference described in this subsection form the foundation for the CE engineering in Sect. “Corrugated-edge engineering and reproducible design protocol” and for the unified performance evaluation framework discussed in Sects. “Performance targets and evaluation metrics” and “Electromagnetic simulation and MIMO performance evaluation”.

Corrugated-edge engineering and reproducible design protocol

For a finite ETS aperture, lateral surface currents and edge-guided waves can travel along the long metallic boundaries and scatter at discontinuities. This boundary-mediated radiation may manifest as angular ripples, elevated side/back lobes, and additional coupling paths that are not fully characterized by S-parameters once the full embedded-element patterns and polarization contents are considered. In this study, CE loading is introduced as a boundary-level design variable that intentionally perturbs the edge current path while preserving the feed topology and the exponential flare of the radiating aperture. The CE is realized by copper removal on the existing metallization layer; therefore, it does not introduce extra layers, vias, or lumped components and remains compatible with standard planar fabrication.

A stepped-depth groove profile is adopted for multi-octave (8:1) robustness. Compared with single-shape periodic grooves (e.g., purely sawtooth or purely rounded profiles), a stepped profile provides additional degrees of freedom to distribute the effective boundary reactance along the edge and to avoid an overly narrow, single-frequency stopband behavior. The CE geometry on each long edge is parameterized by the groove period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({p}_{CE})$$\end{document}, groove width \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({w}_{CE})$$\end{document}, the onset offset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({x}_{s})$$\end{document} measured from the throat, the number of grooves \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$( N )$$\end{document}, and a monotonic stepped depth sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\{{d}_{i}\})$$\end{document}. The i-th groove depth is defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{i} = d_{0} + \left( {i - 1} \right)\Delta d, i = 1,2, \ldots ,N,$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({d}_{0})$$\end{document} is the minimum depth and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$( \Delta d )$$\end{document} is the step increment. The onset offset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({x}_{s})$$\end{document} is introduced to keep the first grooves away from the microstrip-to-slotline transition and the near-throat compensation region, so that CE primarily modifies the boundary current channel rather than detuning the feeding network. The groove period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({p}_{CE})$$\end{document} and maximum groove depth \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({d}_{N})$$\end{document} are related to the guided wavelength at the mid-band frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({f}_{GM})$$\end{document} of the 2–16 GHz band, and these relationships ensure proper CE design across the operational frequency range. The guiding wavelength \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({\uplambda}_{g}\left({f}_{GM}\right))$$\end{document} at the geometric-mean frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({f}_{GM})$$\end{document} is given by:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda_{g} \left( {f_{GM} } \right) = \frac{c}{{f_{GM} \sqrt {\varepsilon_{eff} } }},$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$( c )$$\end{document} is the speed of light in vacuum, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({\upvarepsilon}_{eff})$$\end{document} is the effective permittivity of the dielectric material. The slot width \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({w}_{CE})$$\end{document} is selected according to the minimum manufacturable line/space of the PCB process and is kept constant for all slots to simplify fabrication.

To ensure the reproducibility of the CE design process, a structured ‘start-refine-compare’ methodology is employed. This approach systematically iterates through parameterization, simulation, and performance comparison stages, ensuring consistency across all design phases. The process is framed within the unified evaluation framework outlined in Sects. “Performance targets and evaluation metrics” and “Electromagnetic simulation and MIMO performance evaluation”, allowing for objective validation and refinement.

To isolate the net effect of boundary engineering, three boundary strategies are compared in a controlled ablation study: (i) Double-sided CE, where identical CE is applied on both sides; (ii) Single-sided CE, where CE is applied on one side only; and (iii) No CE, where aperture edges are left unmodified. Electromagnetic simulation and MIMO performance evaluation

Rogers Ultra 1250 is selected as the substrate, with relative permittivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({\upvarepsilon}_{r}\approx 2.5)$$\end{document} and loss tangent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mathrm{tan}\updelta \approx 0.0015)$$\end{document}. The metal is modeled as a perfect electric conductor (PEC), and the surrounding medium is modeled as air with free-space parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\left({\upvarepsilon}_{0},{\upmu}_{0}\right))$$\end{document}, consistent with standard PCB fabrication and enabling subsequent validation.

From a fabrication perspective, all slots (ETS aperture, feeding slotline, and corrugated edges) are defined by subtractive patterning on the copper layer, ensuring compatibility with standard PCB processing. The slot width and minimum spacing adhere to standard design rules. CEs are generated by defining the nominal period and depth, and copying the slot pattern along the array boundary. For double-sided CE, the pattern is mirrored along the array centerline to preserve symmetry, as shown in Fig. 6. No additional metal layers or plated-through vias are required, keeping the design compatible with single-layer planar processes.

Electromagnetic simulations are performed using Ansys HFSS in Driven Modal mode. Lumped or wave ports (depending on the feed realization) are defined at the ends of the microstrip feeds, normalized to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(50\Omega )$$\end{document}. All ports are de-embedded to the throat reference plane at the microstrip-to-slotline junction, ensuring consistent electrical reference for the S-parameters. The structure is enclosed in an air box, with a radiation boundary or perfectly matched layer (PML) applied at a distance of at least \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\uplambda /4 )$$\end{document} (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\uplambda$$\end{document} is the free-space wavelength at 2 GHz) from the outermost conductor, suppressing spurious reflections. For PML, multiple layers (e.g., eight layers) are used to ensure absorption across the entire 2–16 GHz band.

For the spacing-sensitivity evaluation, the S-parameter simulations are repeated for each boundary strategy under the same port definitions, de-embedding reference planes, and boundary settings. Results and discussion

Scope and metrics

This chapter reports full-wave simulation results obtained with Ansys HFSS over the 2–16 GHz band under the unified frequency–radiation–MIMO evaluation framework established in Sects. “Performance targets and evaluation metrics” and “Electromagnetic simulation and MIMO performance evaluation”. The element geometry, feed topology, material parameters, port reference planes, and far-field coordinate system are kept identical across all cases. Under this common baseline, three boundary strategies are compared, namely double-sided CE (Design 1), single-sided CE (Design 2), and no CE (Design 3). In addition, a small pitch sweep of the intra-subarray spacing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{x}$$\end{document} is performed (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{x}=$$\end{document} 13.5, 14.0, and 14.5 mm) to assess spacing sensitivity, especially for coupling and correlation trends.

In the frequency domain, the reported quantities include the four-port reflection coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|Sii\right|$$\end{document}, the corresponding VSWR, and selected inter-port coupling terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|Sij\right|$$\end{document}. The evaluation criteria follow Sect “Performance targets and evaluation metrics”. Matching is assessed using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|Sii\right|$$\end{document} and VSWR, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$VSWR < 1.8$$\end{document} treated as a slightly stricter objective than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|Sii\right|<-10dB$$\end{document}. Coupling is assessed using the relevant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|Sij\right|$$\end{document} terms. The worst-case coupling over the band is summarized by

Full-band curves are presented together with extreme-value statistics to separate broadband trends from local resonance features.

Radiation metrics are derived from the complex three-dimensional far fields and their principal cuts under the common coordinate system and polarization basis defined in Sects. “Orthogonal polarization and four-port reference” and “Electromagnetic simulation and MIMO performance evaluation”. At the representative frequency nodes within 2–16 GHz, the end-fire pointing direction, HPBW, SLL, and peak realized gain are extracted. Linear polarization purity in the main-beam direction is characterized by XPD. Circular polarization components and axial ratio are reported only as auxiliary descriptors of the far-field composition and are not used as design thresholds for this dual-linearly polarized array17.

MIMO performance is quantified primarily through the pattern-based ECC \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rho}_{e}$$\end{document}, computed from the embedded-element far fields using an isotropically weighted full-sphere integral on the unified angular grid. At the nominal pitch (Table 2), the three boundary strategies exhibit distinctly different correlation characteristics. Design 3 (no CE) shows highly frequency-selective behavior, with ECC approaching unity at 2.0, 6.025, and 16.0 GHz, while very low ECC values occur around 8.02–10.015 GHz. Design 1 (double-sided CE) yields a moderate correlation level across the band at the nominal pitch, with ECC values ranging from approximately 0.06 to 0.53 at the sampled nodes. Design 2 (single-sided CE) achieves extremely low ECC at 8.02 GHz (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 9.64\times {10}^{-4}$$\end{document}) and relatively low values around 10.015 GHz, but the correlation becomes high at several other nodes (e.g., 12.01–16.0 GHz), indicating that one-sided boundary loading does not guarantee uniformly low correlation across the full 2–16 GHz span.

The pitch-sweep results in Table 3, 4, 5 further show that ECC is sensitive to the intra-subarray spacing and that the observed trends are not artifacts of a single layout choice. For Design 1 (double-sided CE), reducing the pitch by dx_mm = − 0.25 can lead to very high ECC at several nodes (e.g., ECC ≈ 1 at 2.0, 14.005, and 16.0 GHz), whereas increasing the pitch by dx_mm = + 0.25 significantly reduces correlation at the upper-band nodes (e.g., ECC ≈ 0.004–0.032 over 12.01–16.0 GHz). For Design 3 (no CE), the pitch variation can partially mitigate the near-unity correlation observed at the nominal pitch at low frequencies, but high correlation can still appear at the upper-band nodes depending on the pitch setting. For Design 2 (single-sided CE), low ECC is consistently observed around 8.02–10.015 GHz for all three pitch cases, while the low- and high-band nodes remain more sensitive and can exhibit elevated ECC.

From a mechanism perspective, these results indicate that correlation is governed by the combined effect of boundary conditions and the modal content shaped by the subarray pitch. Corrugated-edge loading modifies the edge-guided current channels and the boundary-mediated scattering, which affects the embedded-element patterns beyond what can be inferred from S-parameters alone. Meanwhile, changing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{x}$$\end{document} alters the mutual coupling environment and the relative phase and amplitude distributions along the shared aperture, which can either enhance or suppress pattern similarity depending on frequency. Therefore, low ECC should be interpreted jointly with radiation patterns and surface-current observations under the unified reference configuration.

Finally, the table-based sensitivity study also suggests practical directions to mitigate high-ECC cases while preserving broadband matching and coupling performance. For double-sided CE, targeted mitigation can be pursued by introducing slight left–right asymmetry in the CE degrees of freedom already defined in Sect. “Corrugated-edge engineering and reproducible design protocol”, for example by using different onset offsets (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{0,L}\ne {x}_{0,R}$$\end{document}) and different stepped-depth sequences (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{i,L}\ne {d}_{i,R}$$\end{document}), or by applying different non-uniform period perturbation sequences on the two sides (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta}_{n,L}\ne {\delta}_{n,R}$$\end{document}) while keeping the same nominal p_CE. Such controlled asymmetry aims to reduce excessive pattern similarity in the frequency regions where ECC becomes high. These refinement directions are consistent with the unified pattern-based evaluation framework and can be assessed without changing the excitation, reference planes, or post-processing settings.

At 2.00 GHz, Design 3 (no CE) shows pronounced current spreading along the lateral aperture boundaries and into the surrounding ground region, indicating that boundary-mediated current paths remain strongly connected when the edges are left unmodified. Such broadly distributed edge currents can provide common current channels for the two orthogonal ports, which is consistent with the high correlation observed at this node for the nominal pitch case (Table 2). For Design 2 (single-sided CE), the corrugated boundary on one side perturbs the edge-guided current channel and introduces an intentional imbalance between the two sides of the aperture. This controlled asymmetry redistributes the return-current flow and can reduce the degree of shared edge-current similarity between the two polarization ports, which aligns with the decorrelation tendency observed in the pattern-based ECC results when pitch and frequency conditions are favorable (Table 3, 4, 5). For Design 1 (double-sided CE), currents are more strongly influenced by the bilateral boundary loading and exhibit a more symmetric edge-current organization compared with the no-CE case. This symmetric boundary condition is beneficial for suppressing edge-induced scattering and stabilizing the radiation envelope, while the correlation outcome remains dependent on the frequency and the intra-subarray pitch (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5).

At 8.02 GHz, the current distribution becomes more localized around the slot aperture and the near-throat region, and the edge-current reinforcement is less dominant than in the low band. Across the three boundary strategies, the embedded-element patterns therefore become less likely to be locked by a single common edge-guided channel, which is consistent with the low pattern-based ECC values observed at this node under multiple pitch settings (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5).

Overall, the comparative study demonstrates how the proposed unified evaluation framework can make trade-offs among matching, coupling, radiation-envelope smoothness, and pattern-based correlation explicit. The results also highlight that correlation optimization in broadband dual-polarized end-fire subarrays is inherently frequency- and layout-dependent. Therefore, boundary engineering should be assessed jointly with pitch selection and pattern-based metrics under consistent reference settings, and the same framework can be extended to other ETS/Vivaldi topologies and array configurations beyond the corrugated-edge examples considered here.

Conclusion

This work investigated a broadband dual-linearly polarized end-fire MIMO subarray based on ETS elements over the 2–16 GHz (8:1) frequency span. A unified evaluation framework was established to jointly assess frequency-domain S-parameters, 2D/3D radiation characteristics, auxiliary AR/CP descriptors, and pattern-based ECC under consistent port reference planes, a common angular sampling grid, and unified post-processing settings. Within this framework, three.

boundary strategies were comparatively studied as concrete implementations of CE engineering, namely double-sided CE (Design 1), single-sided CE (Design 2), and no CE (Design 3). Full-wave simulations show that all three strategies achieve broadband input matching under the same reference configuration, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {S}_{ii}\mid$$\end{document} remaining below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-10 \mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}$$\end{document} over the main portion of the band and VSWR staying below 1.8 in the reported cases. The 2D and 3D radiation patterns confirm that end-fire behavior is preserved at representative frequencies. In particular, double-sided CE produces the smoothest and most symmetric radiation envelope at 9 GHz, while no-CE exhibits more pronounced envelope ripples and stronger backward radiation. The peak realized gains at 9 GHz are of the same order (around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$9-10 \mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}\mathrm{i}$$\end{document}), with differences within approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 1 \mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}$$\end{document}, indicating that CE boundary engineering primarily reshapes the radiated-field distribution at the representative frequency rather than fundamentally changing the gain level. AR and circular-component observations at 2 GHz are provided only as auxiliary descriptors for the double-sided CE case and are not treated as design constraints for this dual-linearly polarized array. More substantial strategy-dependent differences arise in the pattern-based ECC. Using the unified pattern-based formulation for the Port 1–Port 2 pair, the nominal-pitch results (Table 2) and the pitch-sweep results (Table 3, 4, 5) together show that correlation is inherently frequency- and layout-dependent for all three strategies. At the nominal pitch, the no-CE case exhibits strong frequency selectivity, including near-unity ECC at several sampled nodes and very low ECC around 8.02–10.015 GHz. The double-sided CE case yields a moderate ECC level at the nominal pitch, while the single-sided CE case achieves extremely low ECC near 8.02 GHz but can exhibit elevated correlation at other nodes. The pitch-sweep results further confirm that ECC can change markedly with small variations of the intra-subarray pitch, and therefore low correlation cannot be inferred from S-parameters alone. These findings highlight that boundary engineering and pitch selection must be considered jointly when correlation is a critical objective. From an engineering perspective, the three strategies represent different priorities. The no-CE configuration provides the simplest baseline and can be used as a reference for assessing boundary-mediated effects. The single-sided CE configuration offers a practical mechanism to introduce controlled asymmetry and can promote decorrelation under favorable frequency–pitch conditions, while maintaining acceptable matching and radiation behavior. The double-sided CE configuration is attractive when radiation-envelope smoothness and boundary stabilization are prioritized, and it also provides a structured starting point for correlation mitigation by deliberately relaxing excessive symmetry (e.g., through small left–right asymmetries in CE onset, depth sequence, or period perturbation) within the same unified evaluation framework.

Beyond the specific ETS and CE implementations studied here, the proposed evaluation framework is intended to be reusable for a broader class of broadband end-fire MIMO arrays. By enforcing consistent references and pattern-based metrics, it enables fair comparison across different element topologies, subarray layouts, and boundary treatments, and makes the trade-offs among matching, coupling, radiation smoothness, and correlation explicit. This study is subject to several limitations. All results are based on full-wave simulations without experimental validation, and the analysis is restricted to a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\times 2$$\end{document} subarray with an idealized simulation environment and no manufacturing tolerances. Future work will include prototype fabrication and measurement, extension to larger apertures and practical feeding/beamforming networks, and more systematic assessment of additional constraints such as efficiency, robustness to platform/chassis effects, and tolerance sensitivity. Coupling the unified evaluation framework with optimization techniques may further accelerate the exploration of high-dimensional design spaces for broadband end-fire MIMO arrays.
