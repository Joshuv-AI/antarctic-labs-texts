Risk factors for cervical artery dissection: a systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Wilco Jacobs, Debbie S Wright, Katherine Pohlman, Rob Rosenbaum, Barbara A Hutten, Gwendolyne GM Scholten-Peeters, Scott Haldeman, Johannes CF Ket, Sidney M Rubinstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical artery dissection (CAD) is a rare cause of stroke. This is an update of an earlier systematic review, which focuses on the risk of CAD in the general population. The objective was to identify the risk factors for CAD.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in MEDLINE, EMBASE and Web of Science on 20 September 2024. Observational studies (cohort, case-control studies and case-crossover studies) that studied patients with CAD and a control group were included. Risk of bias was assessed with the ROBINS-E tool, and certainty of the evidence was assessed using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE). The results were stratified by healthy controls (primary analyses) and other control groups, notably ischaemic non-CAD stroke (secondary analyses).FindingsIn total, 128 study reports were identified, of which 54 used one or more healthy control groups. Of these reports, 49 (91%) used a case-control design. The risk of bias was generally high (93%). For the following categories, effects were identified: (1) genetic factors or factors with a familial predisposition: migraine, methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR), TT homozygosity, matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) concentration, and connective tissue disorders; (2) external factors: recent infection, winter or autumn–winter season and oral contraceptive use; (3) minor trauma; (4) cardiovascular factors: hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, relative vasodilatation of internal carotid, coronary artery disease and other cardiac diseases. For other risk factors (5), there were no significant pooled estimates. The certainty of the evidence was moderate for migraine and MTHFR TT, low for minor trauma and very low certainty for all others.InterpretationThis is the first review that comprehensively examined the risk of CAD in the general population. Genetic factors, cardiovascular risk factors, recent infection and minor trauma are risk factors for CAD. Caution is needed in interpretation as the evidence is overall low to very low certainty, except for migraine and MTHFR TT homozygosity.

Introduction

Cervical artery dissection (CAD) is a vascular condition involving a false, blood-filled lumen between the vessel wall layers, most commonly occurring in the vertebral and carotid arteries.1 Spontaneous CAD refers to occurrence without obvious major trauma. Knowledge about the incidence of CAD is limited due to the underdiagnosis of asymptomatic cases. In the healthy adult population, CAD occurs at a rate of 8.93 per 100 000 person-years,1 while CAD accompanied by stroke occurs at a rate of 2.97 per 100 000 person-years.2 Between 0 and 13.5% of adult CAD patients experience a stroke within the first 4 weeks,36 but this estimate is dependent on the hospital setting with stroke centres reporting higher incidences. CAD is recognised as the leading known cause of ischaemic intracerebral stroke in young-aged adultsand middle-aged adults, accounting for 12.8 to 23.6% of stroke cases in this population.710

Previous research is limited by the number of studies while many of those studies examined the risk of CAD compared with other forms of stroke.11 There has been an increase in studies examining risk for CAD using healthy control groups. Therefore, our aim was to identify the risk factors for CAD in the general population.

Methods

For this review, the guidelines as outlined by the Joanna Briggs Institute, specifically oriented towards aetiological reviews were followed.12 Additionally, the guidelines for reporting as outlined by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement13 and checklists (online supplemental appendix 1) were followed and modified, where necessary, to suit the nature of this review. The review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023486338).14

Search methods for the identification of studies

A comprehensive search was performed in the databases: OVID/Medline, Embase.com and Clarivate Analytics/Web of Science Core Collection from inception to 20 September 2024, in collaboration with a medical information specialist (JK). The full search strategies and methods are presented in online supplemental appendix 2.

Eligibility criteria

Three reviewers (WJ, DW and KP) independently screened titles, abstracts and full texts of the identified references as needed, using the following inclusion criteria:

Types of studies: case-control, case-crossover studies or cohort studies;

Population: subjects with a confirmed or an assumed diagnosis of (recurrent) CAD of the vertebral arteries or carotid arteries;

Population: subjects with a confirmed or an assumed diagnosis of (recurrent) CAD of the vertebral arteries or carotid arteries;

Exposure of interest: any risk factor related to CAD measured in the study population;

Exposure of interest: any risk factor related to CAD measured in the study population;

Outcome: occurrence of CAD;

Full report of studies in the English language.

At least two reviewers (WJ, DW and KP) screened each reference. Consensus was reached during meetings; where necessary, a third reviewer (arbiter, SR) was used for conflicts. We excluded CAD due to major trauma, defined as fracture, penetrating injury or dislocation (eg, motor vehicle accident, CAD due to surgical or angiographic procedures).

Data collection

Data were extracted by a single reviewer (WJ), and a second reviewer (DW) checked all extractions with the original publication. Risk factors were categorised as (1) genetic factors or factors with a familial predisposition; (2) external factors; (3) minor trauma; (4) cardiovascular factors and (5) others (see online supplemental appendix 2). Control groups representative of the general population (healthy controls) were analysed as the primary control group. All others (ie, patients with stroke, patients with vascular disorder and general patients) were analysed as secondary control groups in the sensitivity analysis.

Data synthesis

The results were stratified by healthy controls (primary analyses) and other control groups, including ischaemic non-CAD stroke (secondary analyses). Data were pooled when deemed clinically homogenous (ie, no fundamental differences in the study population or risk factors measured) and where clinically relevant and appropriate. If no OR was presented, crude ORs were calculated from available data and included in the pooled analysis. Continuous measures were analysed with standardised mean differences (SMD) based on the observed difference relative to the studies’ variability. When both crude and multivariable or adjusted estimates were provided, preference was given to the multivariable or adjusted. Variables used to match groups were not included. A random-effects model was used for all analyses based on the DerSimonian and Laird15 approach and was conducted in RStudio version 2024.04.2+764 using package meta. If a meta-analysis was not possible (eg, one study per risk factor or clinical heterogeneity), a narrative approach was used.

Risk of bias

Risk of bias of each risk factor in each study was assessed in duplicate (WJ and RR) using the ROBINS-E tool,16 with an Excel implementation template. Our assumption was that, given the complex nature of the disease, there should be an attempt to control for confounding in multivariable or adjusted analyses. In the ROBINS-E tool, this assumption results in a ‘very high risk of bias’ for all univariable estimates.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of the evidence for pooled results was summarised according to the principles of GRADE for observational studies (https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/), with the resulting levels of evidence categorised as ‘high,’ ‘moderate,’ ‘low’ and ‘very low.’ Based on the research design, we started with ‘low’ level of evidence, which was then downgraded and upgraded as described below. However, the resulting level of evidence could also be influenced by specific findings in the studies. Additional methods used for certainty of evidence are reported in online supplemental appendix 2.

Risk factors were included in Summary of Findings (SoF) tables when two or more studies were identified for a given specific type of risk factor. Furthermore, for reasons of brevity, when there were essentially no differences between variations of a risk factor (eg, migraine with or without aura), we present the main analyses here and all other analyses in the online supplemental appendices.

Explanation of statistical heterogeneity and robustness of the analyses

Statistical heterogeneity: Subgroup analyses were performed using CAD artery (internal carotid artery dissection (ICAD), vertebral artery dissection (VAD) and both arteries).

Robustness of the findings: Sensitivity analyses were performed for risk of bias levels and different analysis approaches (multivariable, adjusted or univariable).

Results

In total, 128 articles were included, of which 54 (represented by 46 studies) were available for our primary analysis using healthy controls.1770 These 54 articles reported on 484 estimates for 130 different risk factors. An additional 74 articles were included for the secondary analysis to examine risk factors for CAD compared with other control groups. The PRISMA flowchart13 is given in figure 1. The following can be found in the online supplemental appendices: characteristics of the included studies (Appendix 3); a complete listing of risk factor estimates (Appendix 4); forest plots for pooled estimates (Appendix 5); risk factors with only one estimate (Appendix 6) and GRADE assessment (Appendix 7).

Genetic factors or factors with a familial predisposition

Genetic factors or factors with a familial predisposition

In general, there is moderate certainty of evidence that migraine and methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFRR) TT homozygosity, and very low certainty of evidence that transforming growth factor β (TGFß) genes and connective tissue disorders result in increased risk for CAD.

Migraine demonstrated an increased risk (table 1), and there was no difference in those with or without aura (online supplemental appendix 4). One study56 reported an increased risk of family history of migraine (OR 8.69, 95% CI 3.22 to 23.5, n=144). Connective tissue disorders also showed an increased risk for CAD (table 1).

Thirteen studies reported on genetic associations. Homocysteine regulating genes included C677T MTHFR genotypes, 844ins68BP CBS and MTHFD1 G195BA. The TT genotype of C677T MTHFR was associated with CAD (table 1) with moderate evidence. There was no association with CAD for CT genotype in one study,57 for 844ins68BP CBS in two studies46 54 and MTHFD1 G195BA in one study.46 Extracellular matrix regulating genes were associated in one study for FBN1 (OR 13.15, 95% CI 1.66 to 104) but not for other polymorphisms (matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) polymorphism21 65 or LOXL1 rs3825942 SNP48). Three studies demonstrated no association for most cytokine regulating genes (TGFß-regulating genes TGBR1, TGFBR2, TGFB2, SMAD3) except TGFBR2, which demonstrated significant association with CAD in two out of three studies,33 55 58 but the number of observations was very low. Endothelial regulating genes were associated with CAD in one study for ICAM-1 E469K EE genotype,49 but not for other genes in two studies (ACTA2, MYH11 MYLK and NOS3 intron 4 a/b VNTR).33 42 Collagen-regulating genes demonstrated an association for COL3A1 in one of two studies,33 64 for COL4A1 in one study,33 but not for COL5A2 in one study.33 No association was found for several other genes, including Alpha1-AT regulating genes,34 coagulation regulating genes (SEPS1,41 FII G20210A,42 FV Leiden G1691A42), lipid regulating gene APOE e442 and glucose transport regulating gene SLC2A10.33

Although MMP polymorphisms were not identified as risk factors, their regulated substance MMP was elevated in two studies (table 1). In line with homocysteine-regulating genes, homocysteine was elevated in three studies.46 53 54 In line with their regulating genes, collagen, Alpha1-AT and Alpha2-MG were not found as risk factors for CAD.

External factors

External factors

There is very low certainty of evidence that oral contraceptive use is a risk factor for CAD.

Recent infection was a risk factor (table 2) but white blood cells were significantly increased in patients with CAD (SMD 1.46, 95% CI: 0.99 to 1.93, two studies, I2=23%). Winter or autumn–winter seasons, which have increased infection prevalences, were also associated with CAD (table 2). One study demonstrated decreasing ORs with increasing periods following respiratory tract infection from 30 to 365 days, with the increased risk only significant after 30 and only marginally after 90 days.40 One study found large increased risks for monocytes (OR=38), lymphocytes (p=0.01) and neutrophils (OR=27).26

Minor trauma

Minor trauma

There is low certainty of evidence that minor trauma is a risk factor for CAD.

Minor trauma was associated with CAD in three studies (table 3). A variety of definitions of trauma were used in the included studies, ranging from heavy lifting to extensive bruising, thereby limiting the meta-analyses. Any trauma was a risk factor in one study (OR 3.7, 95% CI 2.40 to 5.56), and also mild and severe trauma, as well as specific trauma, such as heavy lifting and extreme head movements, were risk factors.30 One study demonstrated an association of extensive bruising with CAD (OR 4.02, 95% CI 1.33 to 11.49).43 One study reported a very high risk of trauma, with 12 of 48 patients with CAD having suffered from trivial trauma versus none of the 96 cases in the control group.42

Four studies24 30 47 67 68 reported on the association of cervical spinal manipulative therapy with CAD at different periods following the intervention. The pooled OR from three studies that provided the risk after 30 days was 0.64 (0.26 to 1.6).30 47 68 Whedon et al68 examined participants seen in medical evaluation and management appointments with the same associated diagnoses as the control exposure and found a reduced OR for those exposed to cervical spinal manipulative therapy in the 30 days prior to the CAD. Cassidy et al24 found an increased risk at 14 days following exposure to cervical spinal manipulative therapy in younger patients (<45 years). Still, this risk was not different from the risk 14 days following primary care physician visits. Kosloff et al did not find a significant association between chiropractic visits and CAD, while the association between primary care physician visits and CAD was significantly present.47

Cardiovascular factors

There is low certainty of evidence that hypertension is a risk factor and very low certainty of evidence that relative vasodilation of the internal carotid artery (ICA), hypercholesterolaemia, coronary artery disease and other cardiac diseases are risk factors for CAD.

Hypertension was consistently associated with CAD (table 4). Elevated systolic and diastolic blood pressure was a risk factor in the pooled effect from two studies.19 36 Heart rate was not a risk factor (table 4).

Relative vasodilation of the ICA was a risk factor for CAD (table 4). One study could not be pooled (only median provided), but also demonstrated an increased risk (p=0.01).20 Several other estimates demonstrated an increasing risk with vasodilation in increasing tertiles for the ICA, the vertebral artery and the common carotid artery (see online supplemental appendix 4).

The common carotid artery diameter and intima-media thickness were not risk factors for CAD (table 4). Also, other diameter factors, such as the vertebral artery diameter, (supra) bulbar carotid artery and ICA were not risk factors (see online supplemental appendix 4).

Smoking was not associated with CAD (table 4). Also, past smoking demonstrated no association in the two studies.17 27 Hypercholesterolaemia was associated with CAD (table 4). Individuals with diabetes mellitus did not have a higher risk of CAD (table 4).

Coronary artery disease was a risk factor (table 4), as well as other cardiac diseases (table 4). The presence of vascular risk factors was a risk factor for CAD in one study,25 as well as a family history of vascular diseases.29

Individuals with a higher body mass index (BMI), especially patients who are obese, had a decreased risk of CAD (table 4). Individuals who were overweight (BMI 25–30) did not have a decreased risk of CAD (table 4). One study demonstrated28 an association of overall lower BMI with CAD, but BMI on a continuous scale was not a risk factor in a pooled analysis of five studies.17 19 36 50 56

There were several risk factors identified in only one study (relative vasodilation of the vertebral artery, spontaneous dilation of the common carotid artery or vertebral artery,19 vasodilation, relative vasodilation of the common carotid artery,20 vasodilation, Young’s elastic modulus, circumferential wall stress,22 lymphocyte,26 vascular risk factors,25 family history of cardiovascular disease,29 relative vasodilation of the common carotid artery,36 hypotension, other biomarkers,43 platelet,44 basilar dolichoectasia,50 pregnancy,59 neutrophil, white blood cells,66 or the measures were presented in insufficient detail (only p-values, unclear measures).

Other factors

Other factors

There were a number of risk factors that were examined in only one study demonstrating a lower risk (hydroxyproline43) or a higher risk (folate,46 orosomucoid,43 sulphated glycosaminoglycans,43 widened atrophic scars on the skin,43 comorbid conditions23 and diseases of the endocrine glands47 (see online supplemental appendix 4) or the measures were presented in insufficient detail (only p-values, unclear measures).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analysis. The most distinct differences were observed between stroke control groups and healthy general population subgroups (online supplemental appendix 8). In general, when compared with stroke control groups, studies that used healthy controls demonstrated higher estimates for most risk factors, but lower estimates for some of the external factors. There were no differences in risk factors between VAD and ICAD. Only vertebrobasilar strokes seemed to be associated with a less pronounced lower risk for manipulative therapy.

Sensitivity analyses. Multivariable or adjusted analyses generally demonstrated lower risk estimates than univariable analyses, but mostly not at a significant level, except for diabetes mellitus and cervical spinal manipulative therapy (14 days and 30 days). Given that most studies had a ‘very high risk of bias’, we were not able to assess the influence of risk of bias on the outcome.

Finally, any of the other predefined subgroup and sensitivity analyses were not conducted because there were too few studies per risk factor to draw any meaningful conclusions.

Discussion

This review found moderate certainty of evidence for a few risk factors (migraine and MTHFR TT homozygosity) and low to very low confidence in the strength of the association for other factors, notably: 1. genetic factors or factors with a familial predisposition (TGFß genes and presence of connective tissue disorders), 2. external factors (oral contraceptive use), 3. minor trauma, 4. cardiovascular factors (hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, relative vasodilatation of ICA, coronary artery disease and other cardiac diseases), and 5. other factors (protective effect of obesity). Numerous other risk factors were examined in single studies, and therefore, it is difficult to assess whether there is a real association with CAD or whether the findings are spurious.

Risk factors associated with genetic/familial predisposition

The evidence on genetic risk factors is very fragmented, and the numbers of CAD cases are low. The strongest evidence was found for TT homozygosity of the homocysteine regulating gene MTHFR. Other reviews on CAD confirm MTHFR TT or C677T polymorphism of MTHFR to be a risk factor,71 72 while a review that included four studies focused exclusively on carotid dissections could not find an association.73 For MMP polymorphisms, there was no increased risk, but MMP serum levels were elevated in patients with CAD .

Several authors identify migraine as a risk factor for CAD.7477 In a systematic review, Rist et al76 found a twofold increased risk for overall migraine that is consistent with our findings and consistent with the findings in other reviews.75 78 79

We were unable to assess the risk of CAD in the general population among individuals with connective tissue disorders, such as Marfan syndrome, Ehler–Danlos, and fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD). This limitation arises because all reviewed studies evaluated these disorders in comparison to stroke or other hospital patients as controls,8083 leaving the association unclear.

Risk factors associated with trauma

Minor trauma was identified as a risk factor, which is also postulated in several reviews.74 77 One study30 found trauma to be a risk factor for CAD, but not an independent predictor of functional outcome. The definition of neck trauma was, however, very heterogeneous and included heavy lifting, sports injuries and cervical manipulative therapy with ORs varying between 0.94 (95% CI 0.28 to 3.11) and 4.2 (95% CI 0.43 to 41.4). These authors concluded that prior cervical trauma seemed to be an important environmental determinant of CAD but was not an independent predictor of outcomes, as it was not associated with functional 3 month outcome after adjusting for age, sex and stroke severity.30 The heterogeneity in the minor trauma types prohibits definite conclusions about minor trauma in general as a risk factor for CAD, and even within the separate subtypes, for example ‘sports-related injuries’, there may be significant heterogeneity.

Our findings suggest that cervical spinal manipulative therapy is not a significant risk factor for CAD. Two studies showed no increased risk,24 47 one found an increased risk30 while another suggested a decreased risk.68 This may be explained by the setting, design and nature of the controls. The first two aforementioned studies used primary care visits as controls, indicating that the increased risk is likely due to care-seeking behaviour in patients with CAD-related presenting symptoms of neck pain and/or headaches rather than the treatment itself. Perhaps one of the most insightful studies is that conducted by Cassidy et al.24 The authors used a case-crossover design and found an increased risk in the younger population with head and neck pain diagnoses from chiropractic visits but also a comparable increased risk compared with primary care visits, suggesting no increased risk due to exposure to a chiropractor. This may best be explained by ‘protopathic bias’—that is, an intervention, such as cervical spinal manipulative therapy, is applied to a condition (headache and/or neck pain) that has yet to be diagnosed for a condition, such as CAD. The one study that showed a significant increased risk from cervical manipulative therapy used healthy controls rather than general physician consultations used in the other studies.30

Risk factors associated with external factors

Oral contraceptive use was identified as a risk factor only after pooling one significant and three non-significant studies, which indicated heterogeneity and inconsistency. The included studies provide no compelling rationale for a possible risk from oral contraceptive use, aside from similarities between CAD-related stroke and ischaemic stroke.84

A recent infection was identified as a risk factor. Additionally, white blood cell count was found to have borderline significance. In case-crossover studies, we observed an increased risk for CAD during the winter season,51 following a recent infection,69 and shortly following respiratory tract infection.40 Since other inflammation parameters (neutrophil, monocyte, lymphocyte and platelet) were risk factors in single studies,26 44 66 a recent infection should be considered a risk factor for CAD and should undergo further scrutiny in future studies.

Cardiovascular risk factors

We found increased risks for hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, coronary artery disease and other cardiac diseases when compared with the general population, which contrasts with our previous review, where we found vascular factors to be protective when compared with stroke control groups. We also observed an opposing risk effect of hypercholesterolaemia compared with the findings of Abdelnour et al.85 However, their review included fewer studies, some of which used hospital-based control groups.

Limitations of the review

Limitations of the review are first, the representativeness of the included ages in the studies, which is higher than the typical patient with CAD (under 45 years old); second, the unclear aetiological pathway and the place of the identified risk factors in this pathway; third, the presence of accompanying stroke; fourth, clinical heterogeneity of the included studies and outcomes; fifth, the statistical approaches varying from univariate to multivariate analyses, and finally, the wide confidence intervals of some estimates limiting the certainty of the evidence on these estimates through downgrading in the GRADE approach.

Limitations of the review process

The GRADE system starts with a low level of evidence for non-randomised, observational studies, making it inherently challenging to achieve high levels of certainty when assessing risk factors. The observational design for assessing risk factors is penalised by two downgrades regardless of whether the study design is rigorous and valid for answering this objective.

The risk of bias tool was used to determine internal validity, which is one of the determinants in the GRADE system. According to the GRADE procedure, any univariate analysis was, by definition, judged as having a ‘very high risk of bias.’ While this may seem overly stringent, studies that do not correct for confounding or mediating factors are likely to overestimate the association between CAD and the central determinant. Notably, most multivariable analyses received this grading when at least two domains were identified as being at high risk of bias. This was often attributed to factors such as the lack of a reliable outcome assessment in the healthy control group, missing data and/or incomplete measurement of exposure.

We did not use duplicate data extraction; however, according to a recent study,86 independent extraction of data does not yield significantly different results than when this is checked as we have done. Importantly, all the major steps, including search and selection of studies, and the risk of bias assessment were performed by two reviewers independent of one another.

We did not search for grey literature such as conference proceedings, dissertations and ongoing studies. Importantly, there is controversy in the literature as to whether the inclusion of grey literature may indeed result in overestimated effects,87 other studies show grey literature has little impact on the results88 or should be weighed against the effort and cost it involved including these studies.89 Additionally, those studies have examined the effect of grey literature in effectiveness studies as one may expect, so while the impact on aetiological studies is unclear, it is not likely to have influenced our results.

We did not use correction of the confidence intervals for multiple testing. Bonferroni correction would widen the CI of the meta-analyses results but would not influence the point estimate of the effect. We performed meta-analyses when two or more studies provided estimates, reducing the possibility for spurious findings from one study. False positive meta-analyses would therefore indicate the combined effect from several studies to be biased which is a methodological flaw that is not solved by correction for multiple testing, but by the risk of bias tool.

Implications for practice and policy

Information on risk factors can assist clinicians in clinical reasoning, helping them determine whether a CAD could be present and whether referral for specific medical diagnostics or treatment is needed. The strongest risk factors, with the highest levels of certainty, should be prioritised in clinical practice. However, despite our findings, it remains unclear how the risk of CAD may be reduced, as it is a rare event. The risk factors we identified are highly prevalent in the population, making the assessment burdensome for screening. Additionally, this makes it difficult to influence the incidence of stroke following CAD.

Additionally, because the certainty of evidence is ‘low’ to ‘very low’ for many of the risk factors, the application of these risk factors for clinical decision making is limited and should be used with caution.

Implications for future research

The certainty of evidence was ‘low’ to ‘very low’ for many of the risk factors. More population-based studies are needed that have sufficient quality to reduce the risk of bias and have the potential to upgrade the certainty of evidence from low or very low, which is inherent with these observational study designs. Studies should include all known relevant risk factors, particularly those that are suspected to be risk factors such as cardiovascular factors, infection, connective tissue disorders, migraine, alcohol use, contraceptive use, manipulative therapy and minor trauma. The analysis should employ multivariable models to account for confounding relationships between these factors. Genetic factors should be studied in conjunction with their regulated substances in studies that provide sufficient case counts.

Conclusions

We found moderate levels of evidence for migraine and MTHFR TT homozygosity as risk factors for CAD in the general population. Low to very low certainty of evidence was found mainly for genetic and cardiovascular risk factors and minor trauma. Future studies should use large cohort designs from the general population and include all relevant known risk factors in multivariable analyses.
