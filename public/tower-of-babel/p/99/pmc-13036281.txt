Nurse Educators’ Perceptions of the Meaning of Nursing Theories in Norwegian Bachelor's Nursing Programmes: A Focus Group Study
Authors: Monica Evelyn Kvande, Tone Glad, Anne Clancy, Gabriele Kitzmüller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo explore how nurse educators in bachelor's nursing programmes perceive the meaning of nursing theories in nursing education.BackgroundNursing is situated between a professional discipline and an academic discipline. Nurses' professional values significantly influence nursing practice, playing a key role in shaping their behaviour and decision‐making and the quality of care provided.DesignA descriptive qualitative study was conducted and reported on in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).MethodsFour focus groups with 17 educators at three universities and one university college were conducted between September 2021 and September 2023. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, in accordance with Braun and Clarke's approach.ResultsThree themes identifying nurse educators’ perceptions of the meaning of nursing theories in Norwegian bachelor's nursing programmes: nursing theories define what nursing is and refer to core elements in nursing practice; nursing theories strengthen nursing students’ self‐understanding and professional identity and nursing theories—a vital yet marginalised part of nursing education.ConclusionsNursing theories strengthen nursing students’ self‐understanding and identity as nurses and provide them with the knowledge needed for empathetic caring and relational competence. While nursing educators acknowledge the importance of nursing theories in nursing education, discipline‐specific nursing knowledge often competes with knowledge from other disciplines. The interdisciplinary approach emphasised in the training of health and social care professionals can overshadow the unique contribution of nursing theories. As a result, nursing theories may appear less important.Keywords: focus groups, nurse educator, nursing education, nursing theory, professional identity, nursing students

Introduction

This study examines the significance of nursing theories in Norwegian bachelor‐level nursing education. Conceptual models and nursing theories provide formal explanations of what nursing is and what the nursing process entails [1]. Furthermore, Fawcett [2] proposed that the metaparadigm of nursing knowledge consists of four components that define the discipline's central focus: human beings, environment, health and nursing. Barrett [3, 4] defines nursing science as discipline‐specific knowledge about the human–universe–health process, articulated in nursing frameworks and theories. Discipline‐specific knowledge resides within schools of thought that reflect distinct philosophical perspectives, which in turn shape the ontological, epistemological and methodological approaches used to develop and apply knowledge about nursing's unique phenomena of concern.

The International Council of Nurses (ICN) [5] states that educators and researchers should ensure that professional values and ideals, ethical frameworks, responsibilities and obligations are included in nursing curricula. World Health Organization (WHO) [6] states that one of the core competencies of nurse educators is to demonstrate professionalism, including legal, ethical and professional values as a foundation for developing nursing education policies, procedures and decision‐making. WHO [6] also states that nurse educators should facilitate professionalisation for learners by encouraging self‐reflection, personal goal setting and socialisation within the role of the nurse.

Studies have shown that nurses’ professional values significantly influence nursing practice, playing a key role in shaping their behaviour, decision‐making and the quality of care provided [7, 8]. Benner et al. [9] highlight the importance of professional nursing values and ethical behaviour as a foundation for nursing practice. However, the value of nursing theories in bachelor‐level education has been widely discussed. Barrett [3, 4] stated that introducing nursing theories in undergraduate programmes can increase nursing students’ awareness of their professional identity and their unique contribution to healthcare systems, as well as to patients’ care and healthcare‐related experiences. Educating undergraduate students in nursing theories helps them appreciate nursing ethics and acquire nursing knowledge [10, 11].

Sandvik and Hilli [12] argue that if clinical education neglects a strong theoretical and philosophical foundation in the core elements of nursing, there is a risk of losing the essence of what it means to be a nurse. However, nurse educators find it challenging to include philosophical and theoretical discourse on nursing theories at all levels of the curriculum due to competing influences from other disciplines, such as the biological sciences and from medical and surgical content [13, 14]. Dahl and Clancy [15] argue that the use of evidence‐based techniques and protocols developed by other professions can weaken nurses’ clinical judgement and professional identity. This may have contributed to the dilution of nursing knowledge and hindered nursing students’ development within nursing education [11].

Benner et al. [9] recommend shifting the focus of nursing roles to the formation of the nurse's professional identity. Sandvik and Hilli [12] argue that clinical nursing education should prioritise learning reflective and critical thinking, as well as exploring different ways of being a nurse, rather than simply drilling specific skills. Jaastad et al. [16] found that becoming a professional caring nurse is an ongoing process and is ‘enabled when nursing students have a sense of self‐awareness, the courage to stand in their vulnerability, and reflect on their responsibility, caring attitude, and inner values and ethics’. Furthermore, Benner et al. [9] explain the concept of formation in nursing as follows: ‘as opposed to socialization, [it] adds an agent‐centred role of the professional in forming the habits, skills and practices necessary for good practice’.

Nursing education in Norway is a 3‐year bachelor's degree programme and consists of 50% integrated clinical studies. In 2020, Norway adopted the National Curriculum Regulations for Norwegian Health and Welfare Education (RETHOS, hereafter referred to as the national regulations). The purpose of the national regulations is to ensure that all nursing candidates achieve a uniformly high academic level and common final competencies, independent of the educational institution [17]. In addition, Norway has national regulations and guidelines that establish a common curriculum and define the national framework for health and social care education [18].

In accordance with regulations issued by the Norwegian Ministry of Education and Research [19] under the Norwegian Universities and University Colleges Act, institutions must ensure that 20% of positions in the academic area of bachelor's programmes are held by staff with senior academic competence, including a relevant PhD or equivalent, educational qualifications and proficiency in Norwegian. Where a PhD is not held, the educator must have a higher degree (master's), demonstrate educational competence, possess relevant professional or teaching experience and have contributed to advanced academic development work in line with international and national standards in the field [19].

According to regulations under the Norwegian Universities and University Colleges Act [19], academic staff must cover the study programme's subjects, courses and supervision; meet senior academic competence requirements; possess higher education pedagogical competence; participate in relevant national collaborations and networks and maintain up‐to‐date professional knowledge and experience. Except for the recommendation of at least 2 years of relevant professional practice within the past 5 years, the Norwegian competency requirements [19] exceed those outlined by the WHO for nursing educators [6].

Norway, along with 47 other European countries, participated in the Bologna Process, which led to the creation of the European Higher Education Area (EHEA). In addition, Norway participates in the European Union's Erasmus+ programme to promote student mobility, institutional collaboration, internationalisation and research cooperation [20].

The Norwegian Regulation on the National Guideline for Nursing Education does not include recommendations for teaching nursing theories [21].

Exploring nurse educators’ perspectives on the meaning of nursing theories in education is essential, as they shape curricular content and reading lists. Nevertheless, there are no recent qualitative studies exploring this issue. The aim of this study was thus to explore how nurse educators in bachelor's nursing programmes perceive the meaning of nursing theories in nursing education.

Methods

Design

The study had a descriptive qualitative design and included four focus group discussions with educators in nursing education. The epistemological starting point is a qualitative interpretive paradigm in which knowledge is generated through reflective engagement with and interpretation of empirical data [22]. To ensure a comprehensive report, the study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [23].

Inclusion Criteria, Setting and Sample

Purposive sampling was chosen for this study [1]. To qualify for inclusion in the study, the educators had to be nurses and university or university college educators in bachelor's programmes in nursing, with experience in teaching nursing theory and developing curricula in nursing education.

As we aimed to include both universities and university colleges, four public universities and two university colleges (one public and one private) were invited to participate. Three universities and one university college (private) were willing to participate. Recruitment was aided by the head of the department at each university and university college, who distributed information about the study; interested educators contacted the first author via email. The recruitment process was prolonged due to challenges in recruiting participants.

Seventeen nurse educators, 15 females and two males aged between 36 and 67, from four Norwegian bachelor's programmes in nursing, agreed to participate. Most participants held a PhD and were engaged in teaching across bachelor's, master's and doctoral programmes. In terms of work experience as lecturers, the majority had more than 11 years of experience, while their experience in teaching nursing theory ranged from 0 to 5 years to more than 11 years. Eight participants had specialised education in nursing theories. (Table 1).

Data Collection

Focus groups were chosen for data collection because they can generate rich data through discussions and interactions in a group sharing experiences on a common topic [24]. We conducted four focus groups with a total of 17 educators (FG1 n = 3; FG2: n = 5; FG3: n = 5; FG 4: n = 4) between September 2021 and September 2023 in undisturbed locations at the educators’ workplace. An interview guide based on previous knowledge [4, 11, 12] was developed to facilitate reflection and dialogue with the participants. All authors contributed to developing the interview guide. Examples of interview questions include, ‘What do you define as nursing theory?’ ‘Do we need nursing theory in the education of nurses, and if so, why?’ and ‘How do you introduce students to nursing theory?’ (Supporting file).

The first author (M.E.K.) and co‐author (G.K.) each moderated two focus groups, and co‐author (T.G.) co‐moderated three focus groups. A research assistant co‐moderated one focus group. All focus groups featured rich discussions that illuminated our research topic. The moderator acted as a facilitator, aiming to include all participants in the discussions and asking follow‐up questions to further elucidate the topic. The co‐moderator took field notes and ensured that all themes outlined in the interview guide were covered. None of the authors or the research assistant had any prior relationship with the participants. The focus groups lasted between 77 and 90 min. All interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data Analysis

The interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, following the six phases described by [22]: familiarising; coding; generating initial themes; reviewing and developing themes; refining, defining and naming themes and writing the report.

Two authors (M.E.K. and G.K.) coded the transcripts and independently organised them into initial themes. All four authors participated in the inductive, reflexive thematic analysis. Divergent interpretations were discussed and the collaborative, creative, interpretive process evolved into a shared understanding of the possible meanings of the participants' statements, as described by Braun and Clarke [22]. Finally, the transcripts were reread to ensure that the interpretations were valid representations of the original transcripts (Table 2).

Ethics

The study was approved by the head of the department at each public university and the private university college and by the Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research (SIKT; reference numbers 949,108 and 208,874). All participants received written information about the study in advance, stating that participation was voluntary, that anonymity and confidentiality would be safeguarded, and that they could withdraw their written consent at any time [25].

Results

The three themes developed during the thematic analysis were as follows: nursing theories define what nursing is and refer to core elements in nursing practice; nursing theories strengthen nursing students’ self‐understanding and professional identity and nursing theories—a vital yet marginalised part of nursing education. To facilitate transparency in the reporting of comments and agreement among educators, each educator is referred to by a unique, nonidentifying study identification number (e.g., Educator (E) 1–17, FG1–4).

Nursing Theories Define What Nursing Is and Refer to Core Elements in Nursing Practice

The educators stated that there is a substantial amount of nursing theory that refers to core elements in nursing practice that distinguish nursing from other disciplines. Nursing theories were seen as a set of guidelines that help students define what nursing is and enable them to understand the essence of nursing.

[N]ursing theories provide substantial answers about what nursing is, and—depending on the level of the theories—they add something to practice … what it is that is significant in nursing. (E4, FG2)

The classical nursing theories are valuable as a tool to think with, to imagine ‘what is nursing’ and ‘what should I do in the setting where I meet the patients.’ (E11 FG3)

I [as an educator] used to say to the students that [nursing] theory is a pair of glasses, or a kind of map, where you see the nursing subject, or part of the subject in a way, from above. (E14, FG4)

The educators pointed out that nursing lacks a language and that nurses struggle to articulate the underlying assumptions and substance of the discipline.

Everything we [nurses] do in a professional context needs a language. Our language shapes our tasks, and our tasks shapes our language. I think we need a clearly formulated language for the things we do in our professional practice. (E13, FG3)

Theories from other disciplines (e.g., coping theories, sense of coherence theory and Kitwood's theory of person‐centred care) were mentioned as important contributions to the discipline of nursing. However, participants pointed out that using knowledge from other disciplines and downplaying nursing theories as a foundation has made it difficult to develop nursing as an independent discipline with its own body of knowledge and nursing‐specific theories.

The negative discourse about nursing theories has ended up with the question, ‘What's in it for nurses with these theories?’ But I think we lack a definition of what nursing really is. We haven’t been able to define ourselves and so it's no wonder that others don’t understand us. (E3, FG1)

Other professions have originated from nursing … and we struggle to communicate what's left; [nursing] is kind of a ‘left over’ profession, essential but without a language—that's challenging. (E11, FG3)

One of the educators stated that the theoretical foundation of nursing is on the decline: ‘I’m left with a deep sadness: Where have [the nursing theories] gone?’ (E4, FG3). Others mentioned that nurses had only themselves to thank for the decline, as many had participated in the derogatory discourse around nursing theories. Most educators agreed on the importance of preserving the traditional nursing philosophies and theories of Nightingale, Orem, Travelbee, Martinsen, Eriksson, Henderson and Benner, as well as Kitson's fundamentals of care. Nevertheless, some questioned the aim of teaching nursing theories:

Do we think that theories form the [nursing students] to be more caring and empathetic persons and better clinicians? Do theories add to their competence and readiness for action? Do they become better defenders of the nursing profession? Does their nursing identity get stronger? What are we aiming for? For me, it's challenging. (E3, FG1)

There was consensus in the focus groups that many of the traditional nursing theories needed refreshing, as nursing is a dynamic subject that should be adapted to a constantly changing society and the contemporary healthcare system.

In one focus group, differing views emerged on the role of theories in interdisciplinary work. One educator admitted that theoretical nursing knowledge tends to fade when collaborating with other professions, whereas another noted that such collaboration heightens their awareness of their own nursing identity.

Nursing Theories Strengthen Nursing Students’ Self‐Understanding and Professional Identity

In the focus groups, caring was seen as a fundamental dimension of nurses’ identity. The educators argued that nursing theories strengthen nursing students’ self‐understanding and identity as nurses and provide them with the knowledge needed for empathetic caring and relational competence. They also stated that nursing theories are crucial to developing nursing students’ understanding of patients’ illness, lifeworld concerns, coping and recovery. Norwegian nurse and philosopher Kari Martinsen's Philosophy of Care was regarded as a fundamental component of nursing education.

Caring knowledge is a core element if we talk about the way we provide care, how we communicate, and how we organise or plan for organising. (E2 FG1)

Martinsen's Philosophy of Care is a theory that may shape students and educate nurses to practice their profession. It tells something about what nurses are meant to be […] what demands the nurse faces when caring for ill and vulnerable people […] There are also other theories on other levels showing the way for concrete actions in practice. (E4, FG2)

The educators stated that being able to define themselves and their areas of responsibility would strengthen nurses’ identity, allowing them to claim their area of practice in relation to other nurses and stand up for their patients:

In all the final evaluations [after students' clinical practice], the nurses expressed that the students have become brave […]. They dare to speak up and to stand up for the patients, they are reflective, and they take part in the discussions. (E3, FG4)

Participants viewed the development of nursing students’ professional identity as a form of scaffolding that provides a foundation for facing the existential and moral challenges inherent in caring for ill and vulnerable patients. Participants asserted that ethical reflection in practice is crucial for fostering the courage nursing students need to advocate for patients within a healthcare system increasingly driven by economic priorities. Although the national regulations in Norway are a guide for professional development in the nursing curricula, the educators stated that nurses’ professionalisation should be based on the ICN's ethical guidelines and on Norwegian law. During the focus group discussions, participants questioned whether today's nursing students genuinely learn to reflect on the quality of their practice or merely follow directives.

In todays' healthcare system, we need nurses who can think past tomorrow to ensure that ill people are taken care of. If we run out of thoughts, we may end up in limbo with a practice no longer in line with humanism […]. (E12, FG3)

In one of the focus groups, the educators articulated their belief that it is essential to focus on nurses’ identity from the beginning of their education; they highlighted that nursing knowledge as the main subject should guide the curriculum, supported by complementary subjects like anatomy and nosology.

Nursing Theories—A Vital Yet Marginalised Part of Nursing Education

Educators observed that nursing students often struggle to comprehend nursing theories, and many choose not to attend the lectures

I apply theories within practical contexts. Nevertheless, [the students] complained that this was not elaborated on enough and [that] practice supervision did not involve discussions on nursing theories due to lack of continuity within the teaching staff, many short‐term temporary appointments, and the educators' lack of familiarity with nursing theories. (E1, FG1)

The educators also described how there is insufficient time for a thorough introduction of nursing theories and how competing subjects leave little room for the teaching of nursing theories. They explained that when students begin working on their bachelor's theses and are expected to use nursing theories, they can no longer remember them. In the focus groups, some participants discussed how focusing on nursing as a discipline has been downplayed in the national regulations for nursing education. The focus is instead on the common features of becoming a health and social care professional, making nursing theories seem less important.

Nowadays, interdisciplinary thinking is predominant. I think when [nursing] was integrated in the health faculty, our autonomy was taken away from us […] I think things would be different if we had a faculty of our own. (E9, FG3).

The participants noted that the term ‘nursing science’ had been replaced with ‘health sciences’ when nursing education was transferred from university colleges to the immense health faculties in universities. They expressed concern that the term ‘nursing science’ is losing legitimacy and noted the difficulty of maintaining a nursing focus within joint programmes for several professions.

I perceive nursing to be a word we should not use in the faculty of health sciences. It is almost a swear word. Almost [like] we can't use it in any setting […] I think if we do not preserve nursing science, other sciences will take over. (E5, FG2)

Participants expressed concern about the lack of collaboration around and discussions about nursing theories, as this has led to conflicts and a lack of united strategies (such as when selecting the literature used in the curriculum).

Talking about nursing is something we should do. We teach nursing, but in our university, we do not talk about what nursing is […] it has become so fragmented, there are so many different subject areas and so many hindrances. We have our directives with nice learning outcomes, but how do we interpret it? (E7, FG2)

However, in one focus group, educators reported that curriculum planning and evaluation at their institution are conducted through group discussions involving all lecturers. In the other focus groups, participants reported that nursing theories were largely absent from official directives and curricula; instead, the selection of theories was shaped by individual lecturers’ interests.

It is a little random… Only the theories that were most familiar and confident in presenting were included in the teaching… I think this is too casual and we need more diversity in nursing theories and not only the traditional theories. (E3, FG1)

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to explore how nurse educators in bachelor's nursing programmes perceive the meaning of nursing theories in nursing education.

Participants perceived nursing theories as lenses or guidelines that help students to define what nursing is and enable them to understand the essence of nursing. In accordance with the ICN's [5] code of ethics, nursing curricula should include values and ideals, ethical responsibilities and obligations as a framework for standards, professional practice and evaluation. Jenkins et al. [11] call for an ontological and reflexive orientation in nursing education that shifts the emphasis towards becoming skillful nurses, with expertise grounded in nursing knowledge. They also promote a thoughtful ontological orientation that focuses on formation, or the ‘being’ and ‘becoming’, of a nurse. Other studies (7, 8) showed that professional values specific to the field of nursing are essential for nurses' behaviour and decision making, and the quality of care provided.

Participants saw caring as the most important value in nursing and argued that nursing theory provides nursing students with the knowledge needed for empathic caring. They expressed that nursing theories are crucial to developing nursing students’ understanding and awareness of patients’ experiences of illness and lifeworld concerns. Alsvåg and Martinsen [26] and Nyström et al. [27] argue that being a caring nurse is fundamentally a way of being—involving sensitivity to human vulnerability and a commitment to alleviating suffering while upholding patients' dignity and inherent worth as human beings. Watson and Browning [28] emphasise that caring is the moral, philosophical and theoretical foundation for nursing. This aligns with Jaastad et al. [16], who argue that becoming a professional, caring nurse requires self‐awareness, the courage to embrace vulnerability and the ability to reflect on one's responsibilities, caring attitude and underlying values and ethics. Martinsen's [29] Philosophy of Caring was described as a fundamental subject in nursing education, playing a prominent role in understanding care as the value base of nursing and promoting person‐oriented professionalism as a foundation for nursing practice. Benner et al. [9] observe that formation and ethical comportment introduce students to the full scope of the profession, grounding them in its fundamental purposes and in what it means to be a good nurse—such as meeting patients as persons, preserving their dignity and personhood, and responding critically to substandard practice. Moreover, introducing nursing theories at the undergraduate level fosters an appreciation of nursing ethics and supports the acquisition of nursing knowledge [10, 11].

In this study, participants experienced that nursing as a discipline is neglected in the national regulations due to the common curriculum for health and social care education [18]. The term ‘nursing science’ has been replaced with ‘health sciences’, and the focus is on being a healthcare professional, making nursing theories within professional nursing education seem less important. In addition, the participants felt that the increased focus on evidence‐based practice in nursing competes with nursing theory in nursing curricula. This aligns with Jenkins et al. [11], who note that pressures to prioritise evidence‐informed practice, address multiple competency requirements and respond to unpredictable events such as the COVID‐19 pandemic have resulted in increasingly crowded nursing curricula. Similarly, Donohue‐Porter et al. [13] report that nursing educators are tempted to exclude nursing theory from the curriculum due to other competing subjects, such as the constant barrage of new information, influences and demands competing for course content. However, WHO [6] stated that a core competency for nurse educators is designing curricula that incorporate transformational and experiential learning strategies to build context‐based nursing knowledge and skills, while promoting socialisation into the nursing role. Brickner et al. [8] stated that it is essential for nurses to internalise professional values such as human dignity, altruism and justice in order to develop and sustain a professional identity.

In this study, participants explained that using knowledge from other disciplines has made it difficult to develop nursing as an independent discipline with its own nursing knowledge. This is in line with other studies [13, 14] who found that nurse educators found it challenging to include theories about nursing care at all levels of nursing curricula due to competing influences from other disciplines. Jenkins et al. [11] question whether these competing forces have contributed to the dilution—or neglect—of nursing knowledge.

Participants reported that nursing students often struggle to understand nursing theories and frequently choose not to attend lectures. They also noted that supervision in clinical practice rarely includes discussions of nursing theory, partly because some educators lack familiarity with various theories or do not consider them necessary. Nevertheless, Helou et al. [30] found that students are interested in a plurality of theories and are able to use theoretical concepts to reflect on their practice. They conclude that teaching nursing theory in undergraduate nursing programmes should support the use of theoretical knowledge relevant to practice and promote its direct application during clinical training. This is in accordance with Benner et al. [31], who argue that tacit and experiential knowledge are best elicited by situations requiring the knowledge as used in similar contexts. Related knowledge from research and theories is recalled when situations demand its use, either through questions or the nature of the situation. In relation to our study, this means that educators must be familiar with various nursing theories and demonstrate their relevance to clinical practice.

Implications for Practice and Future Research

This study offers important insights into nurse educators’ perspectives on the role and significance of nursing theories in education, given their influence on curricular content and reading lists. Increased emphasis is needed on including professional values and ideals when developing nursing curricula. There is a need to adapt nursing theories to contemporary healthcare systems. Future research may focus on how legal, ethical and professional values presented by nursing theories are expressed in nursing curricula.

Strengths and Limitations

The authors are researchers and educators with an interest in nursing education and theories of nursing. The authors were aware that their preunderstanding could potentially influence the findings. Closeness and loyalty to the respondents’ statements, as well as regular discussions among the authors, helped ensure that interpretations were grounded in the participants’ perspectives. The heads of departments at each university and university college recruited the participants, which may have introduced bias by reflecting their own or their department's views on the role of nursing theories in nursing education. Nevertheless, the participants in the focus group shared personal experiences openly, adding richness to the data. A sample of 17 nurse educators from a Norwegian context might be considered small. However, the inclusion of educators from four different bachelor's programmes in nursing—including both public universities and a private university college—provides rich data from educators with substantial and varied experience in teaching nursing theory and developing curricula in nursing education. Consequently, the dataset was considered to possess sufficient information power [32]. We consider it a strength that two authors coded and categorised the data independently and that all authors contributed to the development of the themes.

Conclusions

Nursing theories strengthen nursing students’ self‐understanding and identity as nurses and provide them with the knowledge needed for empathetic caring and relational competence. While nursing educators acknowledge the importance of nursing theories in nursing education, discipline‐specific nursing knowledge often competes with knowledge from other disciplines. The interdisciplinary approach emphasised in the training of health and social care professionals can overshadow the unique contribution of nursing theories. As a result, nursing theories may appear less important.
