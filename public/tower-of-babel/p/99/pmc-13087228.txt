Removal of lead from groundwater using carbide-derived carbon
Authors: Yehia Manawi, Iman Abdel-Hadi, Yongfeng Tong, Anes Ghoul, Simjo Simson, Viktor Kochkodan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the current work, the use of carbide-derived carbon (CDC) as a novel adsorbent to remove lead (Pb2+ ions) from water is presented for the first time. The effect of different adsorption factors, including CDC dose, adsorption contact time, and lead concentration, was studied. The CDC adsorbent was characterized by using advanced characterization techniques such as scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analyzer and zeta potential measurement. It was observed that CDC showed a BET surface area of 1597.15 m2/g and showed a point-of-zero charge (PZC) at pH 6. The batch adsorption experiments showed that CDC has a high removal efficiency of 98.9% for Pb2+ ions at CDC dose of 6.25 mg/L. It was shown that adsorption kinetic is very fast and 98% of lead removal was reached just in five minutes adsorption time. The adsorption data were fitted to the Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Sips isotherms, which showed that the adsorption process follows the Langmuir isotherm with a regression value of 0.9302. The adsorption of Pb2+ ions onto CDC showed a maximum adsorption capacity of 89.16 mg/g. Moreover, the experimental adsorption data were fitted to pseudo-first order, pseudo-second order, the Elovich model, intraparticle diffusion, and liquid film diffusion, kinetic models and the experimental adsorption results were consistent with the pseudo-second order model, with a regression value of 0.9999. The thermodynamic study indicated that the adsorption is spontaneous at temperatures of 25, 35, and 45 ℃. The ionic strength experiment showed that increasing the ionic strength of the solution causes a decrease in adsorption capacity due to decreasing electrostatic interactions between negatively charged CDC surface and lead ions; however, a removal efficiency > 99% was achieved even at 0.1 M NaCl feed solution. Moreover, the mechanisms of adsorption of lead towards CDC were discussed. The treatment of real groundwater spiked with 500 µg/L of lead showed a complete removal of lead at CDC dose of 1 g/L. The study showed the possibility of efficient regeneration of lead-saturated adsorbent for further reuse. These findings support the potential application of CDC for lead removal from water.Keywords: Adsorption isotherms, Carbide-derived carbon, Lead, Groundwater, Water treatmentSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Environmental pollution is posing a major threat to the natural environment. Heavy metals are among the alarming environmental contaminants that are of great concern because of their toxicity to the natural world and living organisms1. Lead is a heavy metal element that poses great negative impacts on human health and the environment. Human exposure to lead can result from various sources. For instance, lead pollution can result from water pipes that supply homes with water and cause high exposure to lead. Lead can also originate from other sources, such as industrial effluents, solid waste dumping, fertilizers, leaching of metals from garbage, and paints2. The danger of lead exposure lies in the high absorption by the human body, where the rate of absorption can reach 20–70%, with even higher absorption in children3. The Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) stated that exposure to lead causes serious cardiovascular effects, which have led to the death of 1.5 million people globally in 20214. This alarming issue indicates that mitigation is required to decrease the lead levels in contaminated water resources.

Lead can exist in groundwater resources due to many factors, such as the leaching of naturally occurring lead in rocks and minerals, which can result in high concentrations of lead in groundwater5. According to a report by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the maximum limits of lead in groundwater should not exceed 10 µg/L6.

Lead removal from polluted water can be performed using various technologies and methods, including precipitation, coagulation-flocculation, ion exchange, and membrane separation7. However, these techniques were found to include some challenges when utilized for water treatment. For instance, the chemical coagulants are costly, and the process of coagulation involves the challenge of generating a large amount of sludge, which involves additional costs for safe disposal8,9. Furthermore, the ion exchange method for water treatment poses similar challenges of high cost associated with the treatment process. For instance, it was reported that the clogging of ion exchange material by particles is one of the main challenges addressed in the ion exchange technique, which makes it less effective for water treatment10. The use of membranes for water treatment can also pose some challenges including membrane fouling, which necessitates frequent cleaning and eventually replacement11,12.

Conversely, the effective utilization of different adsorbents for lead removal from water was studied in previous literature. For instance, Ahmad et al.13 prepared two adsorbents from Mentha piperita and groundnut husk modified with Guar Gum to remove lead from water. Moreover, Li et al.14 used carbon nanotubes, which had a surface area equal of 134 m2/g for lead removal from water and reported a maximum adsorption capacity (qm) which equals of 49.95 mg/g. Additionally, Ding et al.15 prepared biochar from water hyacinth plants for lead removal and reported a maximum adsorption capacity of 128.95 mg/g with a surface area of 51.15 m2/g. Pap et al.16 has explored the removal of lead from water by utilizing activated carbon made using fruit processing industrial waste, where 80 mg/g of adsorption capacity was achieved at 1 g/L of lead.

Carbide-derived carbon (CDC) is an emerging carbonaceous material that has received significant attention from material scientists worldwide due to its exceptional characteristics, including a highly porous structure that can be precisely controlled, excellent electrical conductivity, and a high surface area17. CDC has been found to be used for various applications, for instance, supercapacitors, hydrogen storage, methane storage, and lithium-ion batteries, and it has been utilized as a support for platinum catalysts18. In addition to the previously mentioned applications, CDC has demonstrated significant potential in the treatment of various metal ions from water17,19. Previous studies have shown that CDC effectively removes molybdenum ions with an adsorption capacity of 16.24 mg/g17, while Cr3+ ions with adsorption capacity of 159.1 mg/g19.

The current work targets the use of CDC as an adsorbent material to remove Pb2+ ions from water which has not been studied yet. While CDC was cited in literature for the removal of contaminants from water to our knowledge, the use of CDC to remove Pb2+ ions from water was not covered in literature. The impact of adsorbent amount, adsorption time, and solute concentration on lead adsorption was investigated. Adsorption isotherms, kinetics of adsorption, and thermodynamic parameters of adsorption were discussed as well as the possibility of sorbent regeneration was evaluated. Finally, the adsorptive lead removal from real groundwater was conducted.

Materials and methodology

Materials

The CDC material (nanopore U type) used in this paper was purchased from Carbon-Ukraine Company (Kyiv, Ukraine). Prior to use, the CDC was washed several times with deionized water (DIW) using mechanical shaker (VWR Standard 5000 Orbital Shaker, Pennsylvania, USA) followed by centrifuge until reaching a solution pH of 6.5.

A 1000 ppm lead stock solution was prepared using lead (II) nitrate and was used to prepare lead-containing solutions for the adsorption experiments by mixing the corresponding amount of the stock solution with deionized water.

Methodology

Different characterization techniques were conducted to study the morphological structure and composition of CDC. Field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, QUANTA FEG 650, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Billerica, MA, USA) coupled with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS, Bruker Xflash 6l60, Germany) was utilized to study the elemental composition of CDC. The morphological structure of CDC was evaluated by a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM) operating at 200 kV (FEI Talos 200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Billerica, MA, USA). Before the TEM analysis, CDC was sonicated in isopropyl alcohol for five min, followed by adding 20 μL of the dispersed solution on top of a 300-mesh lacey/copper-carbon matrix and allowed to dry under ambient temperature. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) technique was utilized to conduct surface analysis of the CDC material before and after adsorption. The XPS is conducted using a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250 platform with a monochromated Alka x-ray source and a hemisphere analyzer. The total surface area of CDC was calculated using a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area analyzer (Micromeritics, ASAP 2020, Norcross, GA, USA). The determination of the pore-size distribution was performed using Barrett, Joyner, and Halenda (BJH) method. N2 gas at a temperature of 77 K was employed for adsorption – desorption experiments. FTIR analysis was performed in absorbance mode using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) equipped with a diamond-ATR accessory. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to observe the reduction in the weight of the sample along with temperature. This will provide an insight about the nature andchemical composition of the investigated CDC sample. In this work, SDT Q600 TGA (TA instruments, Delaware, USA) was used at a heating rate of 20 °C/min to heat up CDC sample fromroom temperature to 1000 °C. A Zetasizer (Nano ZS 90, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) with an inner laser of 4.0 MW was used to determine the zeta potential of the used CDC. To find the point of zero charge, the experiment was conducted at a room temperature of 25 °C in a pH range from 1.5 to 10. The evaluation of the lead in water samples was performed using ICP-OES (5800 ICP OES, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Prior to analysis, the solution was filtered using a 0.2 ?m cellulose acetate syringe filter (Minisart, Goettingen,Germany).

Results and discussions

Characterization of CDC

The typical SEM image (Fig. 1) shows the CDC surface morphology at different magnifications. As seen, CDC is characterized with nonuniform and irregular particle shape with smooth surfaces. Moreover, CDC exhibited wide particle size distribution over 200 nm–3 um range. The agglomeration of particular areas could be attributed to the chlorination step27,28.

SEM elemental mapping is shown in Fig. 2a and b for CDC sample before and after lead adsorption, respectively. As seen in Fig. 2a and b, there is no remarkable change in the structure of CDC before and after adsorption experiments. EDS analysis (Fig. 2b) confirms presence of lead at CDC surface after adsorption experiments. The atomic percent of C: O: Cl: Ti for the pristine CDC is 86.75: 10.86: 0.99: 0.27 compared to 92.18: 5.98: 1.07: 0.27 after lead adsorption. 0.1% of Pb was also detected in the CDC sample after lead adsorption. Moreover, the EDS analysis confirms the detection of 0.1% Pb in the CDC sample after lead adsorption.

TEM was also used to analyze the morphological structure of CDC. Figure 3a and b illustrates the TEM analysis for CDC at a magnification of 100 and 2 nm, respectively. The TEM analysis revealed that the CDC samples comprise different particle sizes and structures that alternate between aggregates and sheet-like, resembling a graphene layered structure. As shown in Fig. 3b, the CDC exhibits an interlayer distance of 0.34 nm while Fig. 3b depicts the particle size of CDC. Moreover, Fig. 3c depicts the diffraction patterns of CDC which showed diffused rings that indicate the amorphous structure of CDC. Previous studies showed similar TEM results. For instance, Yushin et al.29 have reported the presence of amorphous graphitic-like structure in TEM images of CDC due to the chlorination.

The peak at 2θ = 26° indicates the weak crystallization of CDC. This was supported by the diffraction patterns of CDC obtained by TEM analysis (Fig. 2c) which showed diffused rings. The adsorption–desorption graph measured at 77 K is shown in Fig. 6a. N2 adsorption–desorption graphs were calculated using the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method. The isotherm exhibits a rapid increase in N2 uptake at low relative pressure region and a rounded knee indicating the presence of wide micropores. Additionally, the isotherm possesses hysteresis at higher relative pressure region, confirming the presence of mesopores; thus, the isotherm demonstrates the combined characteristics of type I and IV isotherms. Moreover, as illustrated in Fig. 6b, the pore size distribution of CDC shows the bimodal pore size distribution centered at 18.6 and 27.6 Å. This further supports the isotherm characteristics and confirms the presence of both micro- and mesopores in the sample. The total pore volume calculated from the N₂ isotherm at P/P0:0.99 was estimated to be 0.73 cm3/g. A similar conclusion was drawn in a previous study investigating the CDC structure19. The CDC surface area estimated by using the BET method was 1597.15 m2/g. The BET surface area correlates well with previous literature, where the CDC exhibited a surface area of 1120 m2/g17.

The zeta potential or surface charge of CDC used in this work was measured over a pH range of 1.5–10, as presented in Fig. 9. It can be seen that the point-of-zero charge (PZC) of CDC occurred at pH 6, indicating that at pH < 6, CDC exhibits a positive charge, while at pH > 6, CDC surface exhibits a negative charge and will be neutral at pH 6. At lower pH, the zeta potential becomes positive because of possible adsorption of hydrogen ions on the CDS surface35. The higher negative charges with an increase in pH might be ascribed to the deprotonation of CDC surface and the increased presence of oxygen-containing groups36.

Effect of CDC dose, adsorption time, Pb2+ loading and pH

In the present work, the effect of CDC dosage, adsorption time, and sorbate concentration on lead removal was studied. Figure 11 shows the lead removal efficiency and CDC adsorption capacity at adsorbent loadings from 0.25 to 4 g/L. It is seen that the removal efficiency remained constantly high at 98% even at a low CDC dose of 0.25 g/L. This is explained by the high capability of CDC to adsorb Pb2+ ions from water. However, the adsorption capacity showed a sharp decline when the adsorbent loading increased. The removal efficiency of CDC towards Pb2+ ions at CDC dose of 4 g/L was as high as 98.92%, which is the highest removal.

Lead removal efficiency (R%) and adsorption capacity (qe) with variations in CDC dose: Lead concentration: 10 mg/L; pH: 6.5; adsorption time: 1 h.

The results were found be consistent with the work reported in previous literature; for example, Tyagi38 reported an increase in lead removal efficiency reaching a maximum removal efficiency of 68.2% when the dose of the plant-based biochar increased from 0.5 to 1.25 g/L. Moreover, Wang et al.39 reported that the increase in biochar dosage enhances the lead removal as a result of the increase in the number of adsorption sites. However, increasing the adsorbent dose results in decreased adsorption capacity. Enaime et al.40 stated that increasing the adsorbent dose causes overlap between the layers of biochar, which would result in covering the active sites on the surface, causing a reduction in available adsorption sites and hence decreased adsorption capacity.

The effect of adsorption time on the removal of Pb2+ ions was explored over a contact time of 5 min to 24 h (Fig. 12). As seen in Fig. 12, the removal efficiency increased from 90 to 96% at adsorption times of 10 min and 24 h, respectively. These results were found to outperform some of the previously published work on the adsorption of Pb2+ ions by other carbon-based adsorbents. For instance, the removal efficiency of biochar made of sugar cane reached about 80% within 30 min of adsorption time at a feed solute concentration of 6 mg/L72. Moreover, the CDC adsorption capacity reached 36.12 mg/g in 10 min, indicating very fast adsorption kinetics. This time is notably shorter compared to 30 min adsorption time, which was reported by Mohan et al.41 to achieve an adsorption capacity of 9 mg/g using commercial activated carbon.

Lead removal efficiency (R %) and adsorption capacity (qe) with adsorption time: Pb concentration: 20 mg/l; CDC dose: 0.5 g/L; pH: 6.5.

The removal efficiency and adsorption capacity of CDC at adsorption time of 1 h did not increase significantly even after increasing the adsorption time to 24 h. Therefore, the optimum adsorption time was set to be 1 h.

The effect of solute concentration on lead removal efficiency and the adsorption capacity was evaluated and shown in Fig. 13. As seen in Fig. 13, the removal efficiency decreased from 100 to 36% with an increase in lead concentration from 5 to 100 mg/L. Conversely, the adsorption capacity increased from 10 to 73 mg/g. A comparable trend was observed in Alizadeh et al.42, who reported that the adsorption capacity significantly increased from 199 to1684 mg/g while the lead removal decreased from 99.82 to 84.2% when the solute concentration changed from 50 to 500 mg/L42. Another study conducted by Dewage et al.43 has also reported an increased adsorption capacity with increasing the lead concentration from 150 to 230 mg/L. However, although the adsorption capacity typically increases at higher adsorbate concentrations, the overall removal efficiency may decrease. This decline is primarily due to the saturation of available adsorption sites, which limits further ion uptake by the adsorbent as adsorption equilibrium is reached42.

Lead removal efficiency (R,%) and adsorption capacity (qe) at different lead concentrations: adsorption time: 1 h; CDC dose: 0.5 g/L.

Likewise, the effect of solute concentration on lead removal efficiency showed that the removal efficiency of CDC at solute concentration of 50 mg/L did not increase significantly even after increasing the solute concentration to 100 mg/L. Consequently, the optimum lead concentration was selected to be 50 mg/L.

Moreover, the effect of solution pH on lead adsorption onto CDC was also investigated in the present work. Figure 14 shows the removal efficiency of lead at acidic, neutral and basic conditions. As seen, the removal efficiency increased along with increase in pH from 12% at pH 3 to 47% at pH 6 until reaching a complete removal at pH 9. This can be attributed to the increase in negative surface charge of CDC due to the deprotonation of H + which was confirmed by the zeta potential of CDC over a pH range of 1.5–10 which showed a PZC of 6. The increase in the attraction between negatively charged CDC and Pb2+ cations along with the increase in pH can give rise to the enhancement in the removal efficiency. Furthermore, the increase in the removal of lead at high pH was also reported in literature to be attributed to the precipitation of lead by the formation of lead hydroxide (Pb(OH)2) which has low solubility in water at basic conditions (pH: 8–10)44–47. Adsorption isotherms

Figure 15 shows the experimental adsorption capacities of CDC against various equilibrium concentrations of Pb2+ ions at constant temperature. As seen, the increase in the equilibrium concentrations of Pb2+ ions from 2.4 to 69.7 mg/L increased the adsorption capacity from 5 to 49 mg/g.

Adsorption isotherms of Pb2+ ions onto CDC.

Adsorption isotherms are of great significance to further understand the process of adsorption of metal onto adsorbent material. These isotherms are defined as the relationship curve between the concentration of solutes in two phases, at equilibrium, conducted at a constant temperature. For any adsorption system under stable temperature as well as equilibrium, the mathematical formula is called the adsorption isotherm. These isotherms help in identifying which adsorption theory explains the adsorption process for a specific adsorbent and adsorbate. This is conducted by comparing the regression coefficients or the degree of proximity of the experimental points with the model52,53. In the current study, four different adsorption isotherms were used to study Pb2+ adsorption to the CDC, namely Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Sips. Figure 13 illustrates the experimental data fitted into the adsorption models.

Table 4 indicates the non-linear fitting parameters of the Pb2+ ions adsorption on CDC. As seen in this Table, the Langmuir isotherm has the highest linear regression value of 0.93025, indicating a monolayer adsorption of Pb2+ ions onto CDC and adsorption capacity of 89.16 mg/g. The separation factor calculated for the Langmuir isotherm has a value that ranges between 0.3 and 0.9, indicating a favorable adsorption reaction17,19,54.

The regression value of Sips isotherm, which is lower than Langmuir’s isotherm but higher than Freundlich’s isotherm, indicates the heterogeneous adsorption. This isotherm assumes that the surface of a given adsorbent has adsorption sites with uniformly distributed binding energies, up to a specific energy level55. Since the regression value of Sips isotherm was quite close to Langmuir’s isotherm, the adsorption of Pb2+ ions onto CDC can be described as monolayer coverage with some heterogeneity of adsorption sites. This was found to correlate with the work reported by Almanassra et al.19, when CDC was used to remove chromium from water, which exhibited monolayer coverage with some potential heterogenous adsorption sites. Furthermore, the Sips model exponent (ns) value in the present work was less than 1 which gave 1/ns value of 1.4. This was reported in literature to indicate heterogenous adsorption sites56.

Moreover, the regression value (R2) of the Freundlich and the Sips isotherms were 0.90445 and 0.91728, respectively indicating that both homogeneous and heterogeneous adsorption are taking place on the CDC surface. The Temkin regression value was the lowest, with a value of 0.86986.

The main findings of the current work were in good agreement with or outperformed those of adsorbents made from carbon, which were used for the removal of Pb2+ ions in previous work. For instance, Ding et al.49 have conducted a study on Pb2+ ions removal using alkali-modified biochar from aqueous solutions and showed that the adsorption process is explained by the Langmuir isotherm, with a regression value of 0.943, and a maximum adsorption capacity of 53.6 mg/g, which is less than the value obtained in the current work.

Adsorption kinetics

The experimental data correlating the amount of Pb2+ ions removed over time is presented in Fig. 16. These experimental data were collected by measuring the adsorption performance over time while fixing the other operational parameters (CDC dose: 0.5 g/L, pH: 6, Pb2+ concentration: 20 mg/L).

Adsorption kinetics for Pb2+ ions removal by CDC: (a) pseudo-first order, (b) pseudo-second order, (c) the Elovich, (d) intraparticle diffusion kinetic models, and (e) Liquid film diffusion.

Five different kinetic models, such as pseudo-first order, pseudo-second order, Elovich model, intraparticle diffusion and liquid film diffusion, were used to study the effect of adsorption time on the adsorption capacity. Figure 16 shows the linear fit of all kinetic models used, while Table 5 displays the kinetic models’ parameters obtained in this study. As shown in Fig. 16, among all the kinetic models studied, the pseudo-second-order model provides the best fit to the experimental data, as confirmed by the regression coefficient of 0.9999. In the pseudo-second order model, it is assumed that the adsorption rate is affected by the interaction between the adsorbate and the active sites on the surface of the adsorbent during the entire adsorption process57. The adsorption capacity calculated for the pseudo-second-order model (modelled qe) was 38.38 mg/g. This value is very close to the adsorption capacity obtained from the experimental data, which was 38.42 mg/g. This indicates that the pseudo-second order model fits well the experimental data.

The initial adsorption rate was calculated using Eq. (9). The initial adsorption rate for the best-fitting kinetic model (pseudo-second order) was 22.1 mg/g/min. The initial adsorption rate of the adsorption of Pb2+ onto CDC in the present work was found to be faster than the adsorption of chromium using CDC which showed initial adsorption rate of 11 mg/g/min corresponding to pseudo-second order19. The higher initial adsorption rate obtained for lead adsorption onto CDC compared to chromium adsorption onto CDC indicates a faster uptake of Pb2⁺ ions during the early stages of adsorption. This behavior suggests a stronger interaction between Pb2⁺ ions and the active sites on the CDC surface, leading to more rapid occupation of available adsorption sites. The higher initial adsorption rate observed in this study highlights the suitability of CDC for rapid Pb2⁺ ions removal, particularly under short contact times.

The half-life time (t1/2) is defined as the time required to reach 50% of the adsorption capacity, according to the pseudo-second order model58. The half-life time, which is defined as the time required to reach 50% of the adsorption capacity58 was 1.72 min , indicating fast adsorption kinetics . Pseudo-first order model, Elovich, and intraparticle diffusion models showed a relatively lower regression with values of 0.6009, 0.4883, and 0.2568, respectively compared to the pseudo-second order kinetic model. These data correlated well with previous studies reported on adsorption of Pb2+ ions by different adsorbents48,50,51.

Effect of temperature and adsorption thermodynamics

The effect of temperature on the adsorption of Pb2+ ions onto CDC was studied at three solution temperatures of 25, 35, and 45°C. As seen in Fig. 17, the adsorption capacity increased from 16.0 to 20.96 and 29.1 mg/g with an increase in temperature from 25 to 35 and 45°C, respectively. Higher values of adsorption capacities are ascribed to higher mass transfer of ions at elevated solution temperature. Similarly, Almanassra et al.19 reported an increase in adsorption capacity with increasing the solution temperature from 22 ℃ to 60 ℃ when removing chromium from water using CDC.

Effect of solution temperature on adsorption capacity (qe) of CDC towards Pb2+ ions a at pH 6.

Thermodynamics study has been conducted to identify the feasibility and spontaneity of the adsorption process. Three thermodynamic parameters have been calculated, which are the enthalpy (∆H), entropy (∆S), and the Gibbs free energy (∆G). The evaluation of ΔH°, ΔS° and ΔG° was based on the dimensionless equilibrium constant (Ke°) at three temperatures which are 25, 35 and 45 °C. Figure 18a shows Van’t Hoff plot which correlates ln Ke° against 1/T with a regression coefficient (R2) of 0.999. Moreover, the nonlinear form of Van’t Hoff plot, depicted in Fig. 18b, produced a straight line with a regression coefficient (R2) of 0.9995. The slope and intercept of the straight line fitted to the experimental data was used to determine ΔH° and ΔS° which corresponded to 19.23 kJ/mol and 134.37 J/mol, respectively which were both positive. Uma et al.59 indicated that the positive values of the enthalpy are attributed to the fact that the adsorption process is endothermic. Moreover, Isaac et al.60 ascribed the positive value of the enthalpy to be endothermic and indicate physical adsorption of metals. Additionally, the positive entropy values indicate an increased randomness of the solution interface during the adsorption61.

Thermodynamic plot of (a) ln Ke° against 1/T and (b) Ke° against T.

Moreover, ΔG° was evaluated at 25, 35 and 45 °C using Van’t Hoff equation which showed values of −20.8, −22.2 and −23.5 kJ/mol at solution temperatures 25, 35, and 45 ℃, respectively. Table 6 lists the thermodynamic parameters at pH 6 at solution temperatures 25, 35, and 45 ℃. The Gibbs free energy was observed to have negative values and increase with increasing temperature, which indicates the highly spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable adsorption process under these conditions. A similar result has been stated in previous work, where an increase in the magnitude of negative Gibbs free energy was observed, indicating a spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable reaction62.

Regeneration of CDC adsorbent

Desorption is commonly implemented to retrieve the adsorbed species out of the exhausted adsorbents, which facilitates the reuse and restoration of various types of adsorbents63. The effective desorption of adsorbates was reported to mitigate the generation of waste, cut down the consumption of adsorbent, and diminish the cost of adsorbent disposal64. In the present work, the regeneration of CDC was performed by adding 0.1 M solution of nitric acid to lead-saturated CDC and shaking a CDC containing solution for 2 h to facilitate the desorption process. Figure 19a shows the adsorption capacity and desorption efficiency of CDC calculated using Equation 15 after 5 adsorption/desorption cycles. As observed, the desorption efficiency after the first regeneration cycle was slightly reduced to 91%. Likewise, the desorption efficeincy after the fourth and fifth adsorption/desorption cycles accounted for 76% and 67%, respectively. The reduction in the adsorption capacity after regeneration might be due to the loss of some CDC after centrifuging as observed by the black color in the decanted solutions. Moreover, the decline in adsorption efficiency over successive regeneration cycles can be attributed to lowering the number of active adsorption sites on the CDC with repeated use. Furthermore, some Pb2+ ions may become trapped within the internal pores of the CDC, making them inaccessible to the desorbing agents.

(a) Removal efficiency (R,%) after 4 regeneration cycles and (b) XPS survey spectra for the CDC sample after lead adsorption and the CDC after regeneration.

Figure 19b illustrates the XPS survey spectra for the CDC sample before and after regenerating the lead saturated CDC, with the detailed deconvolution for the later sample in Fig. 20. No lead was present on the surface of the CDC after regeneration, as shown in Fig. 19b, which confirms the successful reusability of this material. Meanwhile, C1s, N1s and O1s peaks in Fig. 20b–d conserved the same chemical structure as the pristine CDC before regeneration, except the M–O bond missing in the O1s due to the lead removal. These findings confirm the possibility of efficient regeneration of CDC for further reuse.

Effect of ionic strength

The effect of ionic strength of the aqueous solution on lead removal with CDC was evaluated and shown in Fig. 21. As seen in this Fig. 21, the adsorption capacity and lead removal efficiency slightly decrease with ionic strength of the solution. This might be due to the “shielding effect” caused by Na+ and Cl- ions that results in reduction of electrostatic interactions between Pb2+ ions and the negatively charged CDC surface. Similar findings were reported in previously published studies. For instance, Manawi et al.17 showed a decrease in adsorption capacity for the removal of molybdenum ions from water using CDC with an increase in water salinity from 0.05 to 0.6 mg/L NaCl.

Effect of ionic strength of the solution on Pb2+ ions removal efficiency and CDC adsorption capacity.

Removal of lead from groundwater

The investigation of the level of lead in Qatari groundwater by analyzing 205 groundwater samples collected from all over the country was performed by Shomar65 and Kuiper et al.66. Lead levels as high as 76 µg/L and 37 µg/L were detected in Qatari groundwater, which were notably higher than 15 µg/L, which is the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US-EPA)’s standard for safe drinking water67,68. In this work, groundwater collected from one of the wells in Qatar with total dissolved solids content of 3141 mg/l, conductivity of 6.409 mS/cm, pH of 7.48, turbidity of 0.42 NTU, sodium of 1123 mg/L, chloride of 645 mg/L, sulphate of 482 mg/L, magnesium of 157 mg/L, calcium of 462 mg/L, 11.6 mg/L, potassium, 78.8 mg/L and strontium of 11.6 mg/L was spiked with 500 µg/L of Pb2+ ions and used for adsorption experiments with CDC. The removal efficiency of lead is presented in Fig. 22. The lead removal from groundwater at 0.25 g/L CDC dosage was 93% and the removal efficiency increased steadily with CDC dose to achieve complete removal of lead at the CDC dose of 1 g/L. Even 0.25 g/L of CDC was observed to reduce the level of lead in groundwater to 8 µg/L, which is below the US-EPA’s standard for safe drinking water.

Adsorption mechanisms

The adsorption of Pb2+ ions at carbon-based CDC might be due to physical and chemical adsorption. Figure 23 depicts the possible adsorption mechanisms of Pb2+ ions which include surface complexation, ion exchange, electrostatic interaction, and physical adsorption. The surface complexation seems to be one of the main adsorption mechanisms as confirmed by presence of Pb–O and –Pb–O– structures in lead-saturated CDC, shown in XPS spectra (Fig. 8). This is consistent with the observation in Fig. 8b: the deconvolution reveals a C–O and COO content of 12% and 10% for the pristine CDC; while after lead adsorption both Co and COO signal vanished, indicating important role of the surface complexation in the lead adsorption with CDC. This agrees with previous work which reported that surface complexation accounted for 55.1% of the lead adsorption value onto biochar69. Moreover, the FTIR analysis of CDC indicates the presence of hydroxylic groups, which can be involved in surface complexation with Pb2+ ions (Fig. 4).

Adsorption mechanisms of lead removal by the CDC material.

The electrostatic interactions mechanism also contributes to the adsorption of Pb2+ ions onto CDC in the present work. This can be supported by observing the zeta potential of CDC in Fig. 8 which shows negative charge of CDC in water above PZC of 6. This in turn enhances the bonding between negatively charged CDC and positively charged Pb2+ ions. Moreover, the electrostatic interaction mechanism can be confirmed by the effect of ionic strength experiments performed in the present work, which showed a reduction in the CDC adsorption capacity along with the increase in the NaCl concentrations from 0.03 M until 0.1 M due to the shielding effect at high ionic strength of the solution that reduced electrostatic interactions between Pb2+ ions and the negatively charged CDC surface.

In addition, ion exchange might also be responsible for the adsorption of Pb2+ ions onto CDC. It was reported that the ion exchange mechanism is related to the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups and contributed to the lead adsorption from wastewater by biochar70. Moreover, the presence of Pb–O/Pb–O=C groups on the surface of biochar, prepared from banana peels, was related to the ion exchange mechanism for the removal of lead71.

Conclusions

The current study involves utilizing the CDC for removal of Pb2+ ions from water under different experimental conditions. The effect of different adsorption parameters, such as CDC dose, adsorption contact time, solute concentration, temperature and ionic strength of the solution on the lead removal was evaluated. The Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Sips adsorption isotherms were applied to the experimental data which showed that the Langmuir isotherm was the best fit model by having an R2 of 0.9302. Moreover, the various kinetic models (pseudo-first order, pseudo-second order, Elovich, intraparticle diffusion and liquid film diffusion) showed that the experimental adsorption data were best fitted to the pseudo-second order model, with a regression value of 0.9999. The adsorption capacity value of 89.16 mg/g was reached for adsorption of Pb2+ ions on CDC. By studying the influence of temperature on the adsorption, it was shown that the increase in solution temperature from 25 to 45 ℃ increased the adsorption capacity from 16.1 to 29.1 mg/g. The thermodynamic study revealed that the adsorption process is spontaneous as confirmed by the negative Gibbs free energy values under the studied operating conditions.

The ionic strength experiments, which were performed by using 0.03–0.1 M NaCl solutions, showed that increased ionic strength caused decreased of adsorption capacity obviously due to decreasing electrostatic interactions, between negatively charged CDC surface and lead ions but a high removal efficiency of > 99% was achieved even at 0.1 M NaCl concentration. The possible mechanisms for lead adsorption onto CDC, such as surface complexation, ion exchange, electrostatic interactions, and physical adsorption, were also highlighted.

The use of CDC to remove Pb2+ ions from real groundwater spiked with 500 µg/L indicated that at 1 g/L CDC the adsorbent completely removed lead from water. The XPS survey spectra showed no trace of lead was present on the surface of the CDC after regeneration with 0.1 M nitric acid, that confirm efficient CDC regeneration for further reuse. The high efficiency for lead removal in groundwater and the reusability of CDC indicate suitability of CDC in water treatment applications for Pb2+ ions removal.
