Dental anomalies and dental age in children with down syndrome: a comparative retrospective study with healthy controls
Authors: İsmail Cihangir, Esra Kizilci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren with Down syndrome (DS) are known to present various dental anomalies; however, evaluating anomaly types together with dental age remains limited, in Turkish pediatric populations. The aim of this study was to investigate differences in dental anomaly prevalence and dental age between children with DS and healthy controls.MethodsThis retrospective study was conducted at a university dental clinic using panoramic radiographs obtained between 2009 and 2024. A total of 61 children with DS and 61 age and sex-matched healthy controls (5–14 years) were included. Dental anomalies were categorized as size, shape, number and structure on radiographic evaluation. Dental age was estimated using the Nolla method by assessing six mandibular teeth. Statistical analyses were performed using chi-square, Fisher’s exact, Mann–Whitney U tests and p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsDental anomalies were more prevalent in the DS group. Taurodontism was significantly more common in children with DS (36.1%, 95% CI: 24.2–48.0) compared to controls (19.7%, 95% CI: 9.7–29.7; p = 0.043). Hyperdontia (14.8% vs. 1.6%, p = 0.008), hypodontia (42.6% vs. 4.9%, p < 0.001), and combined hyperdontia and hypodontia (p < 0.001) were also significantly higher in the DS group. Structural anomalies (8.2% vs. 0%, p = 0.022), microdontia (59.0% vs. 11.5%, p < 0.001), and macrodontia (54.1% vs. 6.6%, p < 0.001) were observed more frequently in children with DS.Mean dental age was lower in the DS group (8.3±1.4 vs. 8.5±1.2; 95% CI: 7.95–8.65 vs. 8.20–8.80), although the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.605). Age-group analysis showed a significant difference only at 8 years of age (p=0.019).ConclusionsChildren with DS demonstrated a higher occurrence of dental anomalies than healthy peers. Although dental maturation appeared to be delayed in the DS group, the difference was not significant. These findings highlight the importance of individualized radiographic assessment in children with DS and may support more precise timing of preventive and interceptive dental interventions.

Background

First described by Esquirol in 1838, Down Syndrome is a congenital anomaly characterized by delayed mental and physical development. It is most commonly caused by Trisomy 21, affecting approximately one in 600 to 1000 live births. Recent systematic reviews confirm that managing its complex multisystemic manifestations requires standardized multidisciplinary dental protocols to improve long-term outcomes [1–3].

Patients present with characteristic facial features, including mid-facial hypoplasia, depressed nasal dorsum, slanted eyes, and open mouth posture. Patients also have systemic problems such as cardiovascular, hematologic and thyroid disorders. In addition to craniofacial anomalies, dental problems are among the most easily identifiable findings of the syndrome. The most commonly observed oral findings include mouth breathing, open bite, macroglossia, hypotonic and fissured tongue and, angular cheilitis [2, 3].

Dental problems include anomalies in number, size, shape and structural. They can be classified as developmental and acquired according to the timing of occurrence. Deformities that occur in the embryologic stages of teeth are considered developmental, while problems that occur after the development process is completed are called acquired. These anomalies may be observed individually or in association with a syndrome. Developmental dental anomalies can be grouped as size (microdontia/macrodontia), shape (dens invaginatus, talon tubercle, dens evaginatus, gemination, fusion, taurodontism, root dilaceration), number (hyperdontia/hypodontia) and structure [3].

It has been reported that the prevalence of tooth deficiency is 10 times higher in children with Down syndrome compared to healthy children and this rate ranges between 30 and 60%. Nearly half of patients have microdontia; the teeth show a short conical structure. This leads to the formation of multiple diastema and a lower caries prevalence may be observed [3, 4]. Delay in eruption may be seen in both dentition periods and the timing of eruption may vary. Impacted teeth are commonly seen. In addition, supernumerary teeth, conical shaped laterals, taurodontism, enamel hypoplasia and calcification are among the developmental anomalies noted [5].

Dental anomalies have aesthetic and functional effects, and problems in the dental arch may affect eruption. Therefore, clinical management of anomalies is of great importance. Innovations in the field of healthcare, specialized education system and increased acceptance in social life have increased the frequency of dental treatment for patients with Down syndrome. Because of its high prevalence, dentists are likely to encounter patients with Down syndrome [3, 6].

Therefore, knowing the dental anomalies associated with the syndrome will contribute to improving treatment. Considering that patients may have cognitive limitations, taking panoramic radiographs is more feasible than periapical and tomography [7].

Along with chronological age, height, weight, skeletal, dental and menarche age are used to determine the growth and development status of children. It is accepted that dental age shows less variation compared to skeletal or sex-related growth characteristics. There are various methods used in studies to determine dental age appropriately [8–10]. The Nolla method is frequently preferred for its practicality in determining dental development stages by comparing 10 stages defined graphically with radiographs. Recent comparative assessments in Turkish cohorts demonstrate that the Nolla method provides accurate age estimations [10, 11].

Recent studies on children with Down syndrome have focused on specific dental anomalies or limited outcome measures. The comprehensive data integrating multiple anomaly types with dental age assessment is limited [3, 5, 8, 9]. This limitation is even more pronounced in Turkish pediatric populations [4, 10–12]. This manuscript aims to provide a more integrated understanding of dental development patterns in children with Down syndrome by simultaneously evaluating a wide range of dental anomalies together with Nolla-based dental age assessment.

Methods

The Erciyes University Non-Invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee provided ethical clearance for this research. (Approval No: 2022/668). Due to the retrospective design and the use of anonymized radiographic data and the absence of patient intervention, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was not a clinical trial, trial registration was not required.

Sample size calculation

A sample size calculation was conducted utilizing the G*Power program (version 3.1.9.2). Adopting an alpha error level of 0.05, a power of 95%, and an estimated large effect size (0.8), the analysis indicated a minimum requirement of 50 participants. In order to enhance statistical reliability and maintain group balance, a final study population consisting of 61 children with Down syndrome and 61 age- and sex-matched healthy controls was included.

Study design & setting

Our study was carried out by examining the panoramic images of 61 pediatric patients with Down syndrome between the ages of 5–14 years who applied to Erciyes University Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Pedodontics between 2009 and 2024. A control group was formed by selecting the same number of non-syndromic pediatric patients with similar age group. All panoramic radiographs were evaluated independently by a research assistant with 4 years of clinical experience and a faculty member with 14 years of clinical experience. Both examiners assessed the radiographs under standardized viewing conditions using digital imaging software. Prior to the evaluation, both examiners were calibrated using a subset of radiographs. Inter-observer agreement was assessed based on the initial independent evaluations using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with a two-way random-effects model and absolute agreement. The ICC value was 0.84, indicating good agreement between the examiners. In cases of disagreement, the images were re-evaluated jointly until a consensus was reached.

Inclusion criteria

Children aged 5–14 years with available high-quality panoramic radiographs (OPG X-ray machine) (71kv, 16ma, 13.4s; Instrumentarium op200D, Kavo Kerr, Germany) and complete clinical records were included in the study. The Down syndrome group consisted of patients diagnosed with trisomy 21, while the control group included age- and sex-matched healthy children without any syndromia.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with systemic diseases other than Down syndrome that could affect dental development were excluded. A total of 8 radiographs were excluded due to insufficient image quality. Patients who had only undergone intraoral examination without panoramic radiographs were not included in the study.

Radiographic evaluation & anomaly assessment

Since the study was based on radiographic evaluation, only anomalies detectable on panoramic radiographs were included, and no clinical examination findings were considered. When necessary, available periapical radiographs were also reviewed to improve the evaluation of the anterior region. Regardless of the dentition period, dental anomalies were classified as size (microdontia, macrodontia), shape (dens invaginatus, talon tubercle, dens evaginatus, gemination, fusion, taurodontism, root dilaceration), number (hyperdontia, hypodontia), and structure (enamel hypoplasia, dentin dysplasia). The classification criteria were defined a priori based on commonly accepted categories in the literature to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

Dental age assessment

The age of tooth development of both groups was compared by averaging the 6 left mandibular teeth using the Nolla method (Fig. 1). Following the criteria described by Nolla, radiographic images are scored according to the developmental stages. To enhance the precision of the estimation, if the determined developmental status is between two stages, fractional increments (+ 0.2, + 0.5, or + 0.7) are added to the score, a method described in previous studies to increase the sensitivity of the 10-stage scale [8, 10]. In the present study, a + 0.5 value was added if the status was mid-way between stages, while + 0.2 and+ 0.7 were added for cases closer to the preceding or succeeding stage.

Statistical analyses

Statistical evaluations were conducted utilizing the SPSS software package (version 23.0). To determine the distribution of continuous data, the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed. Depending on the data type, categorical parameters were analyzed through either Fisher’s exact test. Due to the non-parametric nature of the continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for both general and age-specific group comparisons. In addition to p-values, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and effect size measures (Cramér’s V for categorical variables and r for Mann–Whitney U test) were calculated for key outcome variables to enhance the interpretation of statistical significance and clinical relevance. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Age and sex were controlled through matching during the study design. Therefore, multivariate regression analysis was not performed, as the primary aim was to compare group-level differences rather than identify independent predictors.

Results

Sixty-one patients with Down syndrome (Group 1) and 61 healthy pediatric patients (Group 2) with adequate image quality as a result of the digital panoramic films of the patients admitted to our clinic were included in our study. The ages of the patients ranged between 5 and 14 years (Group 1 = 9.5 ± 2.3; Group 2 = 9.5 ± 2.4). The gender ratios of the patients were 49.2% girls (n = 31) and 50.8% boys (n = 30) in Group 1 and 47.5% girls (n = 29) and 52.5% boys (n = 32) in Group 2. No statistically significant difference was found between groups in terms of gender distribution (p = 0.856). The rate of shape anomalies in the teeth of the patients was 63.9% in Group 1 and 42.6% in Group 2. The presence of shape anomalies was numerically higher in Group 1 except for dens evaginatus. In taurodontism, this rate was statistically significantly higher in Group 1. (p = 0.043) (Details of the anomalies are shown in Table 1).

The prevalence of hyperdontia, hypodontia, and their combined occurrence was significantly higher in patients with Down syndrome compared to healthy controls (Table 1). The combined occurrence of hyperdontia and hypodontia was also significantly more frequent in the Down syndrome group (p < 0.001). The presence of structural anomalies was higher in Group 1 (p = 0.022), while microdontia and macrodontia were also significantly more frequent in this group (both p < 0.001). Status anomalies (heterotopia, ectopia, transposition) were not observed in any patient in either group.

The distribution of patients according to chronologic age and Nolla dental age is given in Table 2. Although Nolla dental age was slightly lower than chronological age in the Down syndrome group, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.605). Age-group-specific statistical analysis could not be performed for the 5-year age group due to insufficient sample size; however, descriptive data for this group are presented in Table 2. Among the analyzed age groups, a statistically significant difference was observed only at 8 years of age (p = 0.019).

Effect size analysis revealed large effects for hypodontia (Cramér’s V = 0.46), microdontia (Cramér’s V = 0.50), and macrodontia (Cramér’s V = 0.49), moderate effects for hyperdontia (Cramér’s V = 0.24) and taurodontism (Cramér’s V = 0.18), and a small to moderate effect for structural anomalies (Cramér’s V = 0.21). Discussion

This study demonstrated that children with Down syndrome exhibit a higher prevalence of dental anomalies, while no significant difference was observed in dental age between the groups. Since panoramic radiography was used as the primary diagnostic method, only anomalies detectable on radiographic examination were included. Therefore, structural anomalies that cannot be reliably identified radiographically (such as molar-incisor hypomineralization, Turner hypoplasia, and fluorosis) were not evaluated.

Regarding healthy Turkish pediatric populations, Dikmen et al. reported prevalence rates of 3.4% for hypodontia and 3.8% for root dilaceration [13], while Özveren et al. identified hypodontia in 5.17% and taurodontism in 1.59% of their cohort [14]. These values are lower than those observed in our control group (4.9%, 9.8% and 19.7%, respectively). Internationally, the overall prevalence of dental anomalies in pediatric cohorts is reported to range between 5.1% and 7.4% [15, 16]. While our control group’s taurodontism rate (19.7%) appears higher than earlier local reports, it is more consistent with the global average of 11.8% identified in recent meta analyses [17]. Recent studies also demonstrate variability in prevalence rates among healthy populations. Eymirli et al. reported a hypodontia rate of 8.3%, whereas Bayraktar Nahir et al. reported an overall anomaly prevalence of 2.1% [4, 18]. Among these findings, hypodontia and root dilaceration show the closest similarity to our results. The relatively higher values observed in our control group may be attributed to differences in sample size, population characteristics, and the sensitivity of diagnostic criteria [4, 13, 14]. From a clinical perspective, such variability underscores that population-specific reference values should be continuously refined using updated diagnostic criteria to serve as a reliable baseline.

In studies conducted in children with Down syndrome, the anomalies with the highest prevalence are identified as hypodontia, microdontia and taurodontism. Reporting hypodontia rates vary considerably among authors, including Mestrovic et al. (38.6%) [19], Acerbi et al. (60%) [6], Moraes et al. (53.05%) [20], Sekerci et al. (29%) [21] and Bamba et al. (65%) [22]. Boukhalfa et al. reported a higher prevalence of 68.3% [3], while contemporary reviews by Batool et al. emphasize that hypodontia remains the most frequent numerical dental anomaly due to genetic morpho-differentiation disturbances [23]. Microdontia is another frequently observed anomaly; while Cuoghi et al. (16.19%) [24] and Sekerci et al. [21] reported lower rates, 2025 data indicate a prevalence of 51.7% in Down syndrome cohorts [3]. This elevated frequency contrasts with healthy Turkish pediatric populations, where studies such as Bayraktar Nahir et al. reported lower overall anomaly prevalence of 2.1% [4]. Observations from our research, where hypodontia and microdontia emerged as the primary anomalies, are in agreement with these findings. This agreement with recent international data [23] further supports the characteristic impact of Trisomy 21 on dental development and morphology.

Similarly, taurodontism remains a critical finding in Down syndrome populations; studies by Bell et al. [25] (36.4%) and Moraes et al. [20] (85,71) reflect the morphological variations associated with Trisomy 21. In Turkish Down syndrome cohorts, reported rates range from 66% to 81% [12], whereas the prevalence in the present study was 36%. While previous literature has attributed such discrepancies primarily to sample size, a more critical evaluation suggests that methodological differences in diagnostic criteria are the main contributing factor. Higher prevalence rates (e.g., 81%) often result from including “hypotaurodontism” (TI ≥ 20), whereas lower rates reflect stricter criteria focusing on meso- or hyper- taurodont forms [26, 27]. Furthermore, regional genetic variations and the specific tooth groups examined also influence these outcomes. Narasimhan et al. (2025) indicate that prevalence comparisons vary depending on whether maxillary or mandibular molars are evaluated [28]. Supporting this, the prevalence observed in the study (36%) is consistent with recent findings by Boukhalfa et al. (31.7%) [3]. This suggests that contemporary diagnostic approaches, particularly the exclusion of mild forms, provide more conservative and clinically meaningful estimates [23]. Thus, differences between our findings and earlier Turkish reports may reflect methodological refinement rather than true biological variation.

The high prevalence of dental anomalies in individuals with Down syndrome is a well-established consensus in the literature. Cuoghi et al. found that these variations are significantly more common in this population compared to healthy individuals [24], with recent studies reporting a prevalence up to 5 times greater in this cohort [21, 23]. Similarly, the absence of gender-related differences in our results supports the view that the potent genetic influence of Trisomy 21 operates as a systemic factor that transcends biological sex [3]. Since the extra chromosome 21 is an autosomal mutation, its inhibitory effect on cell proliferation during early odontogenesis likely overrides typical sexual dimorphism [23, 24]. This global deceleration of the entire dental formation process is intrinsically reflected in the delayed pace of dental maturation. Consequently, this biological ‘slowdown’ provides a logical foundation for investigating the discrepancy between chronological and dental age in this population.

Given these findings related to altered dental development patterns in individuals with Down syndrome, the evaluation of dental maturation becomes particularly important. Several studies have demonstrated the validity of the Nolla method for dental age estimation in Turkish populations. In addition, the method offers several practical advantages, including reliability comparable to other commonly used methods, incorporation of an additional crown mineralization stage compared to the Demirjian method, and the ability to be applied using panoramic radiographs without requiring additional equipment. Furthermore, panoramic radiography has been reported to be more effective than intraoral radiographs for dental age assessment [8, 10].

In the literature, few studies have examined dental age in individuals with Down syndrome using contemporary methodologies [29–31]. In the present study, dental age was found to be lower than chronological age across all groups, regardless of gender, but the overall difference did not significant. These findings are highly consistent with recent evidence provided by Apriyono, who demonstrated that while individuals with Down syndrome exhibit a persistent trend toward developmental delay, the gap often remains within the spectrum of biological variation in specific pediatric populations [29]. This perspective is fundamentally supported by the work of Moraes et al., who utilized dental calcification stages and reported that a vast majority of individuals with Down syndrome—73.1% of males and 82.8% of females—exhibited dental maturation within the normal range of reference Table [30] Their earlier findings, together with the high correlation between chronological and dental age reported by Diz et al. [31], suggest that although maturation is generally slower, it follows a predictable trajectory that parallels healthy controls during the developmental period.

However, our observation of a significant difference specifically at age 8 (p = 0.019) represents a distinct ‘developmental divergence’ window. This is strongly corroborated by the regional evidence provided by Nur et al. in Northeastern Turkish children—the same demographic as our study. In their validation of the Nolla method, they found that the 8–8.9 age group exhibited highly significant discrepancies between chronological and dental, indicating that this specific window is particularly sensitive to maturation lags [32]. Biologically, this stage coincides with a period of peak mineralization and eruption of permanent teeth. In individuals with Down syndrome, this process is often delayed, typically occurring around 8.75 to 9 years of age. This cumulative delay during high-activity growth periods explains why the divergence reaches statistical significance at this milestone, which may support the concept of a ‘stalled maturation’ pattern in this population [23, 29].

From a clinical standpoint, identifying this age-specific delay is essential for optimizing the timing of interceptive orthodontic and pediatric dental treatments. While many individuals with Down syndrome may fall within the normal maturation range, the 10-stage precision of the Nolla method provides the necessary sensitivity to detect localized maturation gaps [30, 32].

Limitations

Several limitations of this investigation warrant consideration when evaluating the results. Primarily, the retrospective nature of the study could have led to selection bias, since the analysis was restricted to patients for whom panoramic radiographs were accessible.

Second, the evaluation of dental anomalies was based solely on radiographic assessment. Therefore, structural anomalies that cannot be reliably detected radiographically, such as molar–incisor hypomineralization, Turner hypoplasia, and fluorosis, were not included.

Third, although panoramic radiographs are widely used for dental age estimation, the absence of routine cephalometric radiographs limited the ability to assess skeletal maturation, which may have provided additional insights into developmental patterns. Intraoral radiographs were used when available; however, they were not standardized across all patients.

Finally, although age and sex were controlled through matching, multivariate regression analysis was not performed. Therefore, potential residual confounding factors cannot be completely excluded. Subsequent research employing larger cohorts and prospective methodologies would be beneficial to validate and expand upon these observations.

Conclusion

This study showed that the prevalence of dental anomalies was higher in children with Down syndrome compared to healthy controls. Hypodontia and taurodontism were the most common anomalies in both groups, and both were more frequent in the Down syndrome group. No association was found between gender and the presence of dental anomalies or dental age. Although dental age assessed by the Nolla method was slightly lower in children with Down syndrome, the difference was not statistically significant. Overall, dental development patterns were broadly comparable between groups. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings.
