Deep learning radiomics based on multimodal MRI for preoperative prediction of N stage in tongue squamous cell carcinoma: a multicenter study
Authors: Xin Wang, Sen Fu, Jie Ren, Pingqing Tan, Tao Wei, Yikang Liu, Hang Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative discrimination between early (N0/N1) and advanced (N2/N3) nodal stages is critical for treatment planning in tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) due to its well-established impact on prognosis. Nevertheless, conventional imaging techniques show limited accuracy in staging precision. To address this gap, we developed and validated a Deep Learning Radiomics (DLR) model utilizing multimodal MRI, with the goal of enhancing preoperative staging reliability and supporting clinical decision-making in oral oncology.MethodsA total of 579 patients with TSCC from two independent centers were enrolled. Patients from Center 1 were randomly split into training (n = 357) and internal test (n = 153) sets, while patients from Center 2 formed an external test set (n = 69). Handcrafted radiomic features were extracted from primary tumor volumes on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted and T2-weighted images. Concurrently, deep learning features were extracted from the maximum cross-sectional region of interest using three convolutional neural networks (ResNet34, ResNet101, DenseNet121). These features were integrated to build the DLR models. Model performance was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with comparisons made using DeLong’s test. Calibration and decision curve analysis were also employed.ResultsResNet34 DLR model achieved optimal performance, with AUC values of 0.811 (95% CI: 0.76–0.860), 0.806 (95% CI: 0.709–0.903), and 0.807 (95% CI: 0.684–0.931) in the training, internal validation and external test sets, respectively. It significantly outperformed the conventional radiomics model in both the training and internal validation cohorts (all p < 0.05). Models based on ResNet101 and DenseNet121 also consistently surpassed the radiomics baseline across all datasets.ConclusionThe proposed DLR approach outperformed conventional radiomics in preoperative prediction of nodal stages in TSCC, demonstrating superior diagnostic potential based on multimodal MRI. This method may offer a non-invasive decision-support tool for individualized treatment planning in oral oncology, although further prospective validation is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08165-1.Keywords: Deep learning, Radiomics, Tongue squamous cell carcinoma, Nodal staging, Cervical lymph node metastasis, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introdution

Tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is the most common type of oral cavity cancer, accounting for approximately 25–40% of all oral cancers [1, 2]. It is characterized by aggressive clinical behavior, including local invasion and a high propensity for regional lymphatic metastasis, leading to high morbidity and poor prognosis [3–5]. Lymphatic metastasis, particularly cervical lymph node metastasis (CLNM), is a well-established independent prognostic factor associated with significantly reduced 5-year survival and increased recurrence rates [6–9].

The management of cervical lymph nodes is paramount in TSCC treatment. Therapeutic neck dissection is standard for confirmed lymph node metastasis (LNM). However, the role of elective neck dissection (END) in clinically node-negative (cN0) early-stage (cT1–2) TSCC remains controversial. Occult lymph node metastasis (OLNM) occurs in 20–40% of these patients [10–12], consequently, a substantial proportion (60–80%) would derive no benefit from routine END and would instead be exposed to its unnecessary surgical risks, diminished quality of life, and potential increased mortality [13, 14]. This clinical challenge underscores the critical need for accurate preoperative LNM identification to guide personalized therapy.

According to the NCCN Guidelines [15], the nodal (N) stage serves as a critical determinant in surgical planning. For early nodal disease (N0/N1), ipsilateral neck dissection is generally indicated, whereas advanced disease (N2/N3) warrants bilateral neck dissection, frequently combined with adjuvant therapy. This stratification carries significant prognostic weight, as early and advanced nodal involvement have been consistently associated with distinct survival outcomes. Therefore, accurate preoperative differentiation between these stages is of paramount clinical importance. Current preoperative assessment relies on techniques like ultrasonography (US), US-guided fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), CT, and MRI, all of which have inherent limitations. FNAC exhibits limited sensitivity (75–85%) due to sampling variability and anatomical inaccessibility [16], while CT and MRI depend heavily on subjective interpretation of size and morphological criteria, leading to suboptimal accuracy [17–20]. Thus, more objective and reliable tools for nodal staging are urgently needed.

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly radiomics and deep learning (DL), have opened new avenues for cancer imaging analysis [21–23]. Both handcrafted radiomics features and DL models based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated potential in predicting CLNM in TSCC [22, 24]. A key advantage of DL lies in its ability to automatically extract salient features from images, frequently outperforming handcrafted radiomic features [25]. However, most existing studies remain limited to binary classification (presence vs. absence of LNM), and few have addressed the clinically critical task of distinguishing between the prognostically distinct N0/N1 and N2/N3 stages.

Multimodal MRI, including contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (CET1) and T2-weighted (T2WI) sequences, provides a comprehensive representation of intratumoral heterogeneity and has shown great promise in the evaluation of soft tissue tumors like TSCC [20, 26]. Previous research suggests that radiomics(Rad) models integrating multimodal MRI outperform those based on single-sequence images in predicting LNM [27]. However, most MRI-based radiomics studies in TSCC continue to rely on single-sequence protocols, and the full potential of multimodal MRI for precise N-stage classification remains underexplored.

Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate deep learning radiomics (DLR) models based on multimodal MRI for preoperative discrimination between N0/N1 and N2/N3 stages in TSCC. By integrating handcrafted radiomics and DL features derived from CET1 and T2WI sequences, we seek to construct a robust, non-invasive tool to improve N-stage assessment. Such a model could potentially guide more personalized decisions regarding neck dissection and help reduce overtreatment.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and processing of data sets

In the present study, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and corresponding clinical information for patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the GDC portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Processed TCGA-HNSCC datasets were further acquired from the UCSC Xena browser (http://xena.ucsc.edu/). From this cohort, a subset of patients diagnosed with TSCC was identified.

To assess the prognostic significance of nodal staging, Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis was performed comparing patients with early-stage nodal disease (N0/N1) against those with advanced nodal involvement (N2/N3). The log-rank test was used to evaluate statistical significance between the survival curves.

Characteristics of the patients

This study received ethical approval from the ethics committees of the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University (approval No. SBQLL-2024-064) and the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (approval No. 2025-E0785). A clinical trial number was not applicable. We retrospectively enrolled 579 patients diagnosed between January 1, 2020 and December 1, 2024. Eligible patients had pathologically confirmed TSCC, underwent cervical lymph node dissection with subsequent pathological examination, and received preoperative MRI within two weeks before surgery. Exclusion criteria included incomplete T2WI or CET1 sequences, incomplete clinical data, or absence of cervical lymph node dissection. Figure 1 illustrates the patient inclusion and exclusion process.

The pathological N stage (pN) served as the reference standard for all model development and evaluation. This was determined from postoperative histopathology of neck dissection specimens. Advanced nodal disease (N2/N3) was defined according to the AJCC 8th edition staging system, based on the number and laterality of metastatic lymph nodes.

MRI image acquisition and processing

MRI images in DICOM format were obtained from the medical imaging storage systems at two hospitals. All participants’ images were acquired using three different MRI scanners (Siemens Essenza 1.5T, GE Signa HDxt 3.0T, and Siemens Skyra 3.0T). Detailed acquisition parameters for each scanner and sequence are provided in Supplementary Table 1. All images were processed with N4 bias field correction [28] and resampled to a uniform voxel size of 0.5 × 0.5 × 4 mm³.

Segmentation of region of interest

When segmenting region of interest (ROI), images were delineated along the edge of the tumor, and blood vessels and bone were removed. To ensure the repeatability of segmentation features in ROI, we first performed Intra-class correlation (ICC). Twenty patients with N0/N1 and twenty patients with N2/N3 were randomly selected for ROI segmentation. Firstly, two senior radiologists performed double-blind delineation of the primary tumor. Then, the images of 40 patients delineated by two senior radiologists were extracted and ICC was calculated to measure the consistency. Finally, the ROI of the target area was segmented by a senior radiologist.

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

Radiomics features were extracted from three-dimensional (3D) segmented tumors on CET1 and T2WI sequences using PyRadiomics, including shape, texture, and higher-order features. This volumetric approach captured the full spatial heterogeneity of the primary tumor. Features with ICC > 0.75 were retained and normalized using Z-score normalization. To prevent data leakage and ensure unbiased evaluation, all feature selection steps were performed exclusively within the training cohort. The dataset was first split into training and independent test sets. Within the training set only, Spearman correlation analysis was applied to remove redundant features. Features with a correlation coefficient |ρ| > 0.9 were considered highly collinear, and only one from each correlated pair was retained, a widely adopted threshold in radiomics research that balances redundancy reduction with preservation of informative feature diversity [29]. The remaining features then underwent least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression within the training set, with the optimal penalty parameter (λ) determined via five-fold cross-validation. Features with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were selected to construct the radiomics model. This selected feature subset was subsequently applied to the independent test set for evaluation. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) classifier with hidden layer sizes of (61, 128, 64, 32), maximum iterations of 300, stochastic gradient descent solver (‘sgd’), and random state of 0 was used to distinguish N0/N1 from N2/N3 stages.

Deep learning feature extraction and selection

The maximum cross-sectional ROI from CET1 and T2WI sequences was extracted, a standardized 2D approach chosen to balance clinical practicality across multicenter data with computational efficiency [30]. Images were augmented using random horizontal and vertical flips and uniformly cropped to 224 × 224 pixels. Three pre-trained DL methods (ResNet34, ResNet101, and DenseNet121) were employed for feature extraction via transfer learning on the center 1 training set. Training was performed using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with a momentum of 0.9 and weight decay of 1 × 10⁻⁴. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01 and adjusted using a cosine annealing scheduler. The batch size was 32. Models were trained for a maximum of 100 epochs with early stopping (patience of 10 epochs) based on validation loss to prevent overfitting. After feature extraction, DL features with ICC > 0.75 were retained.

To construct the DLR models, these robust DL features were fused with the previously extracted radiomics features. The fused feature sets were then Z-score normalized and subjected to Spearman correlation analysis exclusively within the training set to prevent data leakage. Fused features with |ρ| > 0.9 were removed. The remaining features then underwent LASSO regression for final optimal feature selection. Consistent with the radiomics pipeline, these selected fused features were subsequently fed into the MLP classifier for N0/N1 vs. N2/N3 discrimination.

Statistical methods

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical data were compared using the χ² test, and non-normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The DeLong test was applied to compare AUC values across models, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Prognostic value of N stage in TSCC based on TCGA data

To assess the prognostic impact of nodal staging in TSCC, we performed KM survival analysis stratifying patients into early (N0/N1) and advanced (N2/N3) nodal disease groups (Fig. 3). A significant difference in overall survival was observed between the two groups (p = 0.00067), with patients in the N0/N1 group demonstrating markedly better prognosis. These findings confirm the critical prognostic value of N-stage stratification.

Establishment of deep learning radiomics model

Three DL methods (ResNet34, ResNet101, and DenseNet121) were used for feature extraction, yielding 512, 2,048, and 1,024 features, respectively. After ICC screening (ICC > 0.75), 508, 2,022, and 1,024 features were retained. These DL features were then fused with radiomics features, followed by Z-score normalization, Spearman correlation analysis, and LASSO regression (Fig. 4B–D). This process selected 15, 14, and 15 features for the three respective backbones to construct the DLR models (Fig. 4F–H).

Comparison of deep learning radiomics model and radiomics model

The ResNet34-based DLR model exhibited significantly higher AUCs than the Rad model in both the training set (AUC = 0.811, 95% CI: 0.761–0.860, p = 0.015, Supplementary Fig. 1A) and the internal validation set (AUC = 0.806, 95% CI: 0.709–0.903, p = 0.034, Fig. 5A). Comprehensive pairwise statistical comparisons of the AUCs, evaluated via the DeLong test, are visualized as p-value heatmaps in Supplementary Figs. 2 A and 2B.

This superior performance was consistent in the external test set, where the ResNet34 DLR model also outperformed the Rad model (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the DenseNet121 DLR model significantly surpassed the Rad model in the training set (AUC = 0.810, 95% CI:0.760–0.859, p = 0.028) (Supplementary Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 2A). Overall, all three DLR models achieved higher AUCs than the Rad model in all datasets, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1A and Figs. 5A, B.

DCA confirmed the clinical utility of the developed models across all cohorts. In the training set, the net benefit of applying any prediction model exceeded the simplistic strategies of “treating all” or “treating none” across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 1B). More importantly, this superior net benefit was consistently observed in the independent validation sets: the DLR models provided a greater clinical net benefit than the conventional Rad model in most decision-making scenarios within both the internal validation set (Fig. 6A) and the external test set (Fig. 6B). The distribution of individualized prediction scores for all patients across the four models is further visualized in Supplementary Fig. 3. The ResNet34-based model demonstrated good calibration (Supplementary Fig. 4), with its confusion matrix illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 5. To visualize model interpretability, SHAP analysis was performed (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Based on the Youden index calculated from the training cohort ROC curve, the optimal risk threshold for clinical decision-making was determined to be 0.324 (Supplementary Fig. 7A). At this threshold, the ResNet34-based DLR model achieved a sensitivity of 70.7% and a specificity of 77.0% in the training cohort.

Discussion

Accurately discriminating between N0/N1 and N2/N3 stages in TSCC preoperatively is critical for guiding the extent of neck dissection and the use of adjuvant therapy [15]. In this multicenter study, we developed and validated DLR models for this precise clinical task. Our findings demonstrate that the ResNet34-based DLR model achieved superior and consistent performance across training, internal validation, and external test sets, with AUCs of 0.811, 0.806, and 0.807, respectively. Notably, this model significantly outperformed the conventional radiomics model in both the training (p = 0.015) and internal validation cohorts (p = 0.034), underscoring the added value of DL features. This is the first study to establish and validate a predictive model for detailed N-stage classification in TSCC using a large, multicenter cohort.

To provide robust clinical context, we first performed an independent survival analysis using the public TCGA-HNSCC cohort. This analysis externally validated the established prognostic difference between early (N0/N1) and advanced (N2/N3) nodal stages in TSCC, highlighting the clinical need for accurate preoperative discrimination. Importantly, no TCGA data were used in model development, the analysis serves solely to reinforce the clinical rationale for such a tool. Our DLR models were built and validated exclusively on multimodal MRI data from our multicenter clinical cohorts. This clear separation ensures that the utility of our model is based on routinely available preoperative imaging, while the TCGA analysis reinforces the clinical rationale for such a tool.

The motivation for this work stems from the well-documented limitations of current methods for preoperative LNM assessment [31, 32], which remains a key challenge in head and neck oncology [33]. Clinical palpation is highly subjective and operator-dependent [34], while FNAB, though useful for suspicious nodes, is not feasible for evaluating every non-palpable lymph node. Cross-sectional imaging techniques such as CT and MRI, though widely used, rely predominantly on size and morphological criteria (e.g., the 10-mm threshold) [17, 18, 35]. These criteria are imperfect, as approximately 20% of nodes exceeding this size show no pathological involvement, and up to 23% of patients with sub-centimeter nodes present with extranodal extension [34]. This inaccuracy increases the risks of both overtreatment and undertreatment. Although tumor depth of invasion (DOI) is used as a supplementary indicator for END in early-stage TSCC [36], with a threshold greater than 4 mm being strongly recommended [37], its discriminative power remains suboptimal. As histopathology is postoperative, our ResNet34-based DLR model shows promising potential for non-invasive, preoperative N-stage prediction.

A critical comparison with recent AI-based nodal staging studies helps contextualize the contribution of our work. While a recent MRI-based study using ResNet50 achieved high accuracy (AUC = 0.928) for OLNM in oral/oropharyngeal cancer [20], it was limited to basic metastasis detection (i.e., N0 vs. N+) and a two-center design. In contrast, our model focuses on the more granular discrimination between N0/N1 and N2/N3 stages, a task that requires subtler phenotypic differentiation, and benefits from multicenter validation. Similarly, a CT-based study in laryngeal cancer demonstrated the superiority of decision-level fusion for predicting OLNM [38], underscoring the value of integrating complementary information. Although we employed MRI with a different fusion approach, the principle of integrating multimodal data aligns with their strategy. However, their focus on OLNM differs from our emphasis on detailed N-stage classification in TSCC, which is intended to inform treatment planning. Furthermore, an MRI-based fusion model for TSCC achieved AUCs ranging from 0.81 to 0.85 for OLNM [39], supporting integration strategies while remaining within a conventional positive/negative detection framework. Our work extends this approach to advanced N-stage stratification, potentially enabling finer prognostic stratification. Finally, a three-stage DLR model for oral cancer LNM diagnosis demonstrated strong external validation [40]. While both studies emphasize multicenter validation and multimodal MRI, our model specifically targets pre-operative N0/N1 versus N2/N3 discrimination, a distinction relevant for tailoring treatment intensity, and is validated in a TSCC-specific cohort. In summary, although AI models have demonstrated advantages over conventional methods, most existing studies have focused on basic binary nodal detection (e.g., N0 vs. N+). Our study represents an initial exploration of detailed N-stage classification in TSCC, which may contribute to bridging the gap between metastatic detection and prognostic stratification through multicenter validation.

Radiomics quantifies imaging features to characterize disease phenotypes [41], with the hypothesis that genomic heterogeneity manifests as intra-tumoral heterogeneity assessable through imaging [42, 43]. A radiogenomic study linked a prognostic radiomic signature to underlying gene expression patterns [44]. Multi-feature-based radiomic models can predict CLNM and prognosis in early-stage oral cancers [20], and have been widely applied for preoperative lymph node assessment [8, 45–47]. Recent studies indicate that MRI-based Rad models from primary tumors can predict LNM with higher accuracy than human assessment [48–50]. For instance, machine learning models for predicting occult CLNM in early-stage TSCC achieved an accuracy of 74.1% and an AUC of 0.802 [11]. Another study reported excellent performance using a radiomic model incorporating a 10-mm peritumoral region [47]. However, current radiomic approaches rely on manual segmentation, and their handcrafted features may be too shallow to fully capture heterogeneity, limiting performance and generalizability [51, 52].

DL has emerged as a powerful methodology in head and neck cancer imaging, capable of identifying complex patterns in medical images and achieving diagnostic performance comparable to or surpassing human experts [53–56]. It facilitates personalized planning by automatically extracting high-dimensional features beyond visual assessment [53, 57–59]. When combined with radiomic features, DL has shown significantly improved performance in tumor segmentation, lesion detection, and classification [20, 25]. Wang et al. [60] demonstrated that an integrated model combining both feature types outperformed models using either alone. Nevertheless, most existing models are limited to the binary classification of CLNM (i.e., presence versus absence). More precise models for N-stage differentiation are imperative to optimize treatment.

In our study, we employed three pretrained neural networks (ResNet34, ResNet101, and DenseNet121) for DL feature extraction. ResNet enhances performance through considerable depth and residual modules [61], while DenseNet121 improves feature reuse and parameter efficiency via dense connectivity, mitigating gradient disappearance [62]. Interestingly, ResNet34 demonstrated superior predictive performance in our task. A plausible explanation is that while increasing network depth can improve accuracy, it may also lead to performance saturation and overfitting [62]. The shallower architecture of ResNet34 likely offered better generalization given our dataset size and complexity, effectively minimizing overfitting and proving more suitable for this specific clinical application. To optimize the models, we implemented a full fine-tuning strategy, updating all layers of the ImageNet-pretrained networks. This approach leverages transferable low- and mid-level visual features from natural images as a powerful initialization, subsequently adapting them to the medical imaging domain through fine-tuning—a paradigm supported by established research in the field [63]. The training utilized Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with momentum (0.9), weight decay (1 × 10⁻⁴), and a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler (initial LR = 0.01), with early stopping employed to prevent overfitting. Regarding the model input, our DLR model utilized features extracted from the largest axial cross-sectional slice of each tumor. This 2D approach was a deliberate choice to balance clinical feasibility and reproducibility across heterogeneous multicenter datasets, to ensure computational efficiency allowing for robust transfer learning, and to facilitate the capture of biologically representative information, as this maximal slice often encompasses critical tumor characteristics such as the invasive front. Furthermore, data augmentation was intentionally conservative, employing only random horizontal and vertical flips. This choice prioritized anatomical plausibility given the standardized orientation of axial MRI slices, acknowledging that exploring medically constrained advanced augmentations is a valuable direction for future work to enhance model robustness.

Multimodal MRI combining CET1 and T2WI improves diagnostic performance in soft tissue tumors [64, 65]. By integrating radiomics from both sequences, we captured complementary intratumoral heterogeneity, and direct comparison with single-sequence models confirmed that all multimodal variants consistently outperformed single-sequence models, validating the added value of fusion. A previous study [27] reported that adding sequences did not consistently improve CLNM prediction in TSCC, citing feature redundancy and instability in multiphase fusion [66]. Our use of DL-based radiomics may better extract complementary, non-redundant features, explaining this discrepancy.

To facilitate clinical adoption, we explicitly considered the practical implications of model predictions. Based on the Youden index calculated from the training cohort, we identified an optimal risk threshold of 0.324 (Supplementary Fig. 7). At this threshold, the ResNet34 DLR model achieved a sensitivity of 70.7% and a specificity of 77.0% in the training cohort, with consistent performance observed across the validation cohorts. Importantly, this threshold can be adjusted according to clinical priorities. A lower threshold (e.g., 0.3) may be preferred for screening to maximize sensitivity, whereas a higher threshold (e.g., 0.6) may be used to prioritize specificity when considering aggressive surgery. This flexibility enhances the model’s adaptability to diverse clinical scenarios. False negatives could lead to undertreatment by withholding necessary neck dissection in patients with advanced nodal disease, potentially increasing the risk of regional recurrence. Conversely, false positives could result in unnecessary neck dissection and avoidable morbidity, including shoulder dysfunction and prolonged operative time. Explicit consideration of these trade-offs is essential for informed clinical decision-making.

Regarding comparison with existing clinical tools, current clinical nomograms (e.g., based on tumor appearance, ulceration) and imaging-based morphological staging (CT/MRI) largely rely on the subjective experience of clinicians. In contrast, our model provides objective, quantitative, and reproducible risk stratification, which can reduce inter-observer variability and offer more consistent predictions. This represents a key advantage over subjective clinical assessments.

Beyond predictive performance, our model offers interpretability advantages for clinical adoption. SHAP analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6) links predictions to both deep learning representations and quantifiable imaging phenotypes such as intratumoral heterogeneity, enhancing transparency and physician confidence. This feature-level interpretability also enables comparison with radiological knowledge and supports hypothesis generation for future research. Future efforts should focus on prospective multicenter validation and explore optimal combinations of imaging modalities and tumor subregions to further enhance model performance and clinical utility.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective analysis, it carries the potential for selection bias, and prospective validation is therefore essential to confirm the clinical utility of our models. Second, the sample size of the independent external validation cohort is relatively modest, which may affect the precision of the performance estimates and represents a limitation for broader statistical generalization. On the methodological front, several deliberate simplifications were made to balance feasibility with performance. Our DLR model analyzed 2D axial slices rather than full 3D volumes, which may not capture the complete spatial heterogeneity of tumors. Future studies with larger, prospectively collected cohorts should explore 3D volumetric DL approaches to determine whether they offer incremental predictive value. Similarly, radiomic features were extracted solely from the primary tumor, omitting potential signal from the lymph nodes themselves. Incorporating perinodal or nodal imaging features could further improve model precision. The data augmentation strategy was also conservative, employing only flips to maintain anatomical plausibility. Exploring more advanced techniques may improve robustness. Additionally, the models were developed using conventional MRI sequences (T2WI and CET1), and future integration of advanced imaging or genomic data could enhance predictive power. Finally, while our use of a strict training-test split (with feature selection confined to the training partition) provides a robust and unbiased estimate of model generalizability, future studies with larger cohorts could employ nested cross-validation designs to further optimize feature selection stability and model hyperparameters, potentially enhancing reproducibility.

Conclusion

The proposed DLR models based on multimodal MRI radiomics features from T2WI and CET1 sequences provide a non-invasive and practical method for preoperative classification of N0/N1 and N2/N3 stages in patients with TSCC, with diagnostic performance superior to radiomics models based solely on handcrafted features. Although further validation and refinement are required, this approach represents a meaningful step toward personalized and precision medicine in TSCC management.
