Persona-based stratification and XGBoost modeling for multi-dimensional vehicle sound quality evaluation
Authors: Wendi Wang, Mingsheng Wang, Tao Chen, Xiangwei Lu, Feitan Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the automotive industry transitions toward electrification and intelligent mobility, conventional NVH development approaches struggle to meet users’ personalized sound quality (SQ) expectations. This study systematically investigates the impact of individual differences on multi-dimensional SQ evaluation and develops a robust explanatory reconstruction framework. Using a bipolar 7-point scale, 50 participants provided ratings along the Comfort and Sportiness dimensions, and a threshold-based preference split was applied to construct detailed user personas. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) was employed to model the nonlinear mapping between psychoacoustic features and subjective ratings, with SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis applied to quantify the relative contributions of individual features to each perceptual dimension. The analysis focuses on high-fidelity full-sample models for feature decoupling. SHAP analysis further demonstrates that the derived feature contributions are robust and interpretable across perceptual dimensions. These findings confirm that persona-based stratification allows investigation of population heterogeneity in a mechanistic perspective, providing a physically interpretable basis for precise vehicle SQ tuning.

Introduction

With the automotive industry transitioning toward lightweight, electrified, and intelligent vehicles, consumer expectations for interior noise have shifted from mere quietness to acoustic texture and personalized sound characteristics1,2. Traditional NVH (Noise, Vibration, Harshness) development methods typically focus on reducing sound pressure levels; however, they often fail to meet the refined requirements of high-end users for brand-specific tonal qualities, such as sportiness or luxury3. SQ evaluation is a complex process involving subjective perception and cognitive interaction. Conventional acoustic assessment methods primarily rely on single physical metrics, such as sound pressure level or loudness, but studies indicate that these parameters alone cannot fully capture the human auditory perception and subjective preference for noise, thus failing to satisfy the multidimensional sound quality demands of modern automotive users4,5.

In recent years, an increasing number of psychoacoustic metrics have been proposed to more accurately quantify subjective sound perception6–9. Concurrently, multidimensional sound quality evaluation methods have received growing attention10. For instance, dividing SQ into dimensions such as Comfort, Sportiness, Luxury, and Timbre Pleasantness allows a more comprehensive characterization of occupants’ subjective experience. Nevertheless, most existing studies assume homogeneous perception across all evaluators, overlooking the potential impact of individual differences among drivers. Such differences may arise from factors including gender, age, driving experience, driving style, or psychological preferences, resulting in systematic scoring deviations for the same noise sample across different evaluators, and consequently affecting the accuracy and practical reliability of sound quality assessment models.

Recent studies have demonstrated that individual differences among participants markedly influence SQ evaluations. For instance, in research on HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning) noise, Italian and Japanese participants exhibited distinct preferences under identical noise stimuli. Under high airflow conditions, Japanese participants rated comfort significantly lower than Italian participants, while their annoyance scores were significantly higher11. In vehicle interior SQ perception studies, Noumura et al.12 conducted large-scale subjective evaluations in the United States, Germany, and Japan, revealing that passenger perception primarily aligns along two dimensions: Luxury and Sportiness. Significant cross-national differences were observed: approximately 43% of U.S. participants preferred Sportiness and 57% preferred Luxury; in Japan, 59% favored Sportiness and 41% favored Luxury; German participants overwhelmingly preferred Luxury. A large-scale online survey by Wagner et al.13 further indicated that German consumers favor softer, quieter, and brighter vehicle sounds, with lower emphasis on roughness, sharpness, loudness, and timbre richness compared to American consumers. Kim et al.14 evaluated driving, operational, and warning sounds of a luxury sedan through in-vehicle testing, revealing substantial differences in preferences for rapid acceleration and warning sounds across countries: German and Korean participants preferred refined, low-resonance engine sounds, whereas American participants favored more pronounced engine harmonic components; German participants preferred traditional relay-type turn signal tones, while Korean participants were more receptive to electronically synthesized signals.

Moreover, cluster analyses indicate notable differences in perception models for the same acoustic sources among individuals. Park et al.15 applied k-means clustering based on auditory preferences, proposing a sportiness index model to reflect individual differences. Altinsoy16 highlighted that customer expectations and personal preferences significantly influence evaluations of pleasantness and stimulation. Kim et al.17 empirically demonstrated perceptual differences between drivers and passengers regarding acceleration sounds, showing that role-based differences affect annoyance, Sportiness, acceleration sensation, and overall preference ratings.

To address these challenges, this study introduces the User Persona approach from psychometrics to systematically investigate the impact of individual differences on multidimensional SQ perception. The study uses a sport utility vehicle (SUV) prototype and constructs a dual-dimension evaluation framework encompassing Comfort and Sportiness. First, interior acoustic signals were recorded under steady-state cruising and transient acceleration conditions. Next, statistical and sensory preference features of the evaluators were extracted via multidimensional preference questionnaires, and user personas were constructed using a threshold on preference scores. Based on these personas, a nonlinear regression model employing XGBoost was developed as a high-fidelity reconstruction of the full sample to investigate feature contributions. Finally, the SHAP framework was applied to quantify the contribution of psychoacoustic parameters to subjective evaluations, thereby elucidating the contribution patterns of objective acoustic features across different perceptual dimensions.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and characterization of interior acoustic signals

Interior noise signals were acquired in strict accordance with the acoustic measurement requirements specified in ISO 5128:1980. A SQuadriga 4-channel portable data acquisition front-end (44.1 kHz) captured binaural signals. Measurements were performed on urban and suburban asphalt roads with closed windows and minimal ambient noise. Signals were high-pass filtered at 20 Hz to remove sub-audible components and segmented into 5-second intervals. Playback for subjective evaluation was conducted with Sennheiser HD 4.50 BT professional headphones (18 Hz–22 kHz, 113 dB/W, 18 Ω, < 0.5% distortion) calibrated to reference levels. Measurement conditions included steady-state cruising (20–80 km/h) and transient acceleration (idle to 100 km/h), producing 20 samples per condition. Signal acquisition, layout, and preprocessing followed established validated protocols18–22.

To provide a multidimensional representation of the acoustic environment, a hybrid feature set combining psychoacoustic and physical/operational parameters was constructed. Psychoacoustic metrics included Loudness, Sharpness, Roughness, Fluctuation Strength, Tonalness, and Articulation Index, while the physical/operational parameters included A-weighted sound pressure level (A-weighted SPL) and operating condition (OC).

Jury constitution and persona-based stratification

This study involved a low-risk, non-invasive auditory perception experiment. All experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Academic Committee of Dazhou Vocational and Technical College. We confirm that all methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and the research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all adult participants prior to the commencement of the subjective evaluation experiments.

A jury of 50 participants (32 males and 18 females), all with normal hearing and over three years of driving experience, was recruited to conduct subjective sound quality evaluations. The evaluations encompassed two primary perceptual dimensions: Comfort and Sportiness. Based on the User Persona framework, a nine-item preference questionnaire was developed and assessed using a 7-point Likert scale (see Fig. 1). Each question presented two opposing anchors corresponding to comfort-oriented and sportiness-oriented scenarios, and participants rated their preference on a scale from 1 to 7.

To validate the internal consistency and structural adequacy of the nine‑item preference questionnaire (which was aggregated into a single composite score by arithmetic mean), a psychometric analysis was performed on the 50 × 9 raw response matrix. The analysis yielded a Cronbach‘s α of 0.953 (95% CI [0.940, 0.963]), a Kaiser‑Meyer‑Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy of 0.942, and a single‑factor variance explanation of 64.58%. These statistics demonstrate excellent internal consistency and strong unidimensionality, supporting the aggregation of the nine items into a single preference score for subsequent persona‑based grouping.

Statistical analysis was conducted on the survey results, as shown in Fig. 2. Pronounced gender differences in sound quality preferences were observed: the female participants predominantly favored comfort-oriented ratings, with 66.7% scoring between 1 and 3. In contrast, male participants exhibited a clear inclination toward sportiness, with nearly 50% of scores being 5 or higher. These findings highlight the necessity of employing a persona-based modeling approach, as the ungrouped model would obscure these opposing user preferences.

To facilitate persona-based modeling, neutral scores (4 points) were further categorized. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) was applied to analyze the probability density of subjective ratings, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The rating distribution exhibited a unimodal pattern, with a broad peak concentrated in the transition region between neutral and comfort, indicating a high degree of perceptual overlap among participants. Participants who did not perceive clear sportiness cues tended to provide neutral or comfort-oriented ratings. Based on this analysis, participants with scores ≤ 4 were classified as the comfort-preference group, whereas those with scores > 4 were assigned to the sportiness-preference group, enabling effective segmentation of distinct perceptual cohorts. resulting in 28 participants in the comfort-preference group and 22 in the sportiness-preference group. No participant was assigned to both groups, as the threshold‑based rule produces mutually exclusive subgroups, enabling effective segmentation of distinct perceptual cohorts.

Participant segmentation and subjective rating calculation

To ensure the scientific rigor and reliability of sound quality evaluations, each participant rated every sample along the Comfort and Sportiness dimensions using a 12-point even-numbered bipolar scale without a neutral midpoint. Specifically, scores 1–6 represent the comfort-oriented side (1 = Extremely Comfortable, 6 = Slightly Comfortable, i.e., the weakest comfort leaning), and scores 7–12 represent the sportiness-oriented side (7 = Slightly Sporty, i.e., the weakest sportiness leaning, to 12 = Extremely Sporty). This forced-choice design eliminates central tendency bias by removing the midpoint as a “hiding place” for ambivalent responses23, and the high-resolution scale aligns with standard automotive NVH subjective evaluation practices24,25. Upon completion, the data were subjected to consistency checks in SPSS. In accordance with common psychometric practice, a correlation coefficient below 0.7 between an individual rater’s scores and the group average was considered indicative of poor inter‑rater consistency; accordingly, the ratings of 5 participants whose correlation coefficients fell below this threshold were excluded from subsequent analyses. To assess the necessity of persona-based stratification, two types of subjective metrics were computed based on the remaining 45 valid datasets: ungrouped metrics and persona-based grouped metrics.

High-fidelity modeling based on XGBoost

To address the complex nonlinear mapping between physical features and subjective perception in automotive SQ evaluation, this study employs XGBoost to construct high-fidelity descriptive models. XGBoost, as an efficient ensemble learning algorithm, implements an additive model that transforms the modeling of subjective ratings into an ensemble of base learners26. In the context of vehicle NVH studies, XGBoost has demonstrated superior robustness and generalization capability compared to conventional linear regression or standard feedforward neural networks27.

Results

The global reconstruction performance of the XGBoost-based sound quality models was evaluated using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}$$\end{document} and relative MSE (Table 1). Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of individual differences in multi-dimensional vehicle sound quality evaluation. Participants were assessed using a 9-item preference questionnaire and stratified via KDE, providing a descriptive basis for persona characterization. XGBoost models, built on a hybrid set of psychoacoustic and physical features, were trained on the full sample as a high-fidelity reconstruction tool (full-sample R² > 0.95) rather than for out-of-sample prediction. To provide an empirical indication of predictive generalization, leave-one-out cross-validation was performed, yielding LOO-CV R² of 0.752 for Comfort and 0.776 for Sportiness. SHAP analysis revealed the key feature contributions for each dimension. The standard deviations of SHAP values under LOO-CV were reported to transparently indicate feature-importance stability, without implying that grouping improved predictive performance. These results demonstrate that considering individual differences is essential for constructing robust and interpretable SQ models, providing an engineering reference for targeted NVH development for specific user groups. Nevertheless, this exploratory study is limited by its compact sample size (N = 40) and single algorithm (XGBoost); future work should include larger sample sets and multi‑algorithm validation to enhance generalizability.
