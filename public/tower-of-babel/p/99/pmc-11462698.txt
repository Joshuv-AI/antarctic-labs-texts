Zinc-based Polyoxometalate Nanozyme Functionalized Hydrogels for optimizing the Hyperglycemic-Immune Microenvironment to Promote Diabetic Wound Regeneration
Authors: Chaoyu Pu, Yong Wang, Honglin Xiang, Jiangtao He, Qiyuan Sun, Yuan Yong, Lu Chen, Ke Jiang, Hanfeng Yang, Yuling Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn diabetic wounds, hyperglycemia-induced cytotoxicity and impaired immune microenvironment plasticity directly hinder the wound healing process. Regulation of the hyperglycemic microenvironment and remodeling of the immune microenvironment are crucial.ResultsHere, we developed a nanozymatic functionalized regenerative microenvironmental regulator (AHAMA/CS-GOx@Zn-POM) for the effective repair of diabetic wounds. This novel construct integrated an aldehyde and methacrylic anhydride-modified hyaluronic acid hydrogel (AHAMA) and chitosan nanoparticles (CS NPs) encapsulating zinc-based polymetallic oxonate nanozyme (Zn-POM) and glucose oxidase (GOx), facilitating a sustained release of release of both enzymes. The GOx catalyzed glucose to gluconic acid and (H₂O₂), thereby alleviating the effects of the hyperglycemic microenvironment on wound healing. Zn-POM exhibited catalase and superoxide dismutase activities to scavenge reactive oxygen species and H₂O₂, a by-product of glucose degradation. Additionally, Zn-POM induced M1 macrophage reprogramming to the M2 phenotype by inhibiting the MAPK/IL-17 signaling diminishing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and upregulating the expression of anti-inflammatory mediators, thus remodeling the immune microenvironment and enhancing angiogenesis and collagen regeneration within wounds. In a rat diabetic wound model, the application of AHAMA/CS-GOx@Zn-POM enhanced neovascularization and collagen deposition, accelerating the wound healing process.ConclusionsTherefore, the regenerative microenvironment regulator AHAMA/CS-GOx@Zn-POM can achieve the effective conversion of a pathological microenvironment to regenerative microenvironment through integrated control of the hyperglycemic-immune microenvironment, offering a novel strategy for the treatment of diabetic wounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02840-7.Keywords: Diabetic wounds, Hyperglycaemic microenvironment, Immune microenvironment, Nanozymes, Hydrogels

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major public health crisis, with the number of people suffering from the disease globally predicted to increase to 629 million by 2045 [1, 2]. Diabetic wounds are the most common complication of diabetes mellitus, with a prevalence of 19–34%. Patients with diabetic wounds are often at risk of delayed healing, infection, and even amputation [3]. The main mechanisms underlying impaired wound healing of diabetic wounds include cytotoxicity due to the hyperglycemic microenvironment, the toxic effects of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and a persistent and excessive immune-inflammatory response [4]. Excessive blood glucose concentration induces abnormal activation of the intracellular MAPK pathway, which in turn triggers an inflammatory response and apoptosis [5–7]. In diabetic wounds, hyperglycemia results in an increase electron donors in the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) and impaires the activity of intracellular antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and catalase (CAT), which leads to the generation of excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which activates the NF-κB signaling pathway in macrophages and induces their polarization toward the M1 phenotype, leading to disruption of the immune microenvironment [8–12].

Normal wound healing involves four overlapping cascades of hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [13]. Macrophages are an important part of the innate immune system and a key regulator of wound healing [14]. During the inflammatory phase, M1 macrophages are dominant, exhibiting phagocytic activity and clearing pathogens and damaged tissues. During the proliferative phase of wound repair, M2 macrophages take the lead, limiting local inflammation and promoting cell proliferation, angiogenesis, granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition, and re-epithelialization [15–17]. During the remodeling phase, macrophages regulate the degradation and remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). During this phase, MMPs promote the degradation of type III collagen secreted by fibroblasts in the proliferative phase of inflammation. At the same time, platelets and macrophages secrete transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and other cytokines to promote the secretion of type I collagen by fibroblasts, which in turn replaces type III collagen in remodeling ECM, optimizing the stability and resilience of the nascent tissue [18, 19]. Macrophage phenotypic conversion is therefore essential for normal tissue repair to ensure the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative remodeling phase. In the pathological state of diabetes, macrophage plasticity is impaired and the phenotypic shift from M1 to M2 is impeded, leading to a persistent pro-inflammatory state of macrophages, which in turn hinders the pro-healing activity of endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and other functional cells [20, 21]. Existing treatments for diabetic wounds are inadequate owing to the difficulty of achieving accurate intervention in the complex pathological microenvironment [22]. Existing literature shows that Chen et al. developed hydrogen-doped titanium dioxide nanorods for effective visible light (VIS)-responsive photocatalysis for glucose depletion and hydrogen generation for efficacy in diabetic wound healing [7]. Xue et al. developed an ultra-small (6–8 nm) platinum (Pt) nanozyme loaded with mitochondria-targeted liposomes (Pt@MitoLipo) to attenuate hypoxia and eliminate excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) for the effective treatment of hypoxia-induced oxidatively damaging diseases [23]. Zhao et al. developed a peroxidase-mimicking nanozyme, MnCoO@PDA/CPH, capable of scavenging ROS and concurrently modulating the immune response to foster diabetic wound regeneration and repair [24]. Therefore, in order to achieve effective healing of diabetic wounds, it is necessary to develop therapeutic options focusing on the comprehensive regulation of the the integrated regulation of hyperglycemic-immune microenvironment in diabetic wounds, alleviation of hyperglycemia-induced cytotoxicity and oxidative stress in various types of cells in this hyperglycemic microenvironment, and promotion of macrophage remodeling toward the M2 phenotype are expected to be new therapeutic options to improve the healing process of diabetic wounds [25, 26].

Glucose oxidase (GOx) is a biological enzyme that interacts with glucose to produce glucuronic acid and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which improves the hyperglycemic-immune microenvironment. However, the H2O2 produced by this reaction is not favorable to wound repair, thus limiting its widespread use [27, 28]. Therefore, the elimination of by-products of the glucose degradation process as well as the excess ROS in the wound is of particular importance. It is known that nanozymes are a new type of nanomaterials with enzyme-like developed in recent years, which are able to regulate ROS and immune response, etc [29, 30]. This approach is distinguished by its high catalytic efficiency and excellent stability, enabling it to emulate the functions of biological enzymes, including catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and others. Such properties are instrumental in safeguarding normal cells against oxidative damage and in mitigating inflammation [31–33].Molybdenum-based polyoxometalate (POM) nanoclusters are promising for the treatment of ROS-associated diseases owing to their high ROS scavenging efficacy [34, 35]. It has a POM structure that can introduce catalytically active metal ions and be combined with a variety of metal ions to construct a polymetallic oxide system, which not only possesses enzyme-like efficacy but also possesses metal-ion-related biological activities [36]. Extant studies show that zinc ions are associated with angiogenesis and macrophage polarization; therefore, doping zinc ions into the POM structure to mimic ROS scavenging-related enzyme activities while exerting immunomodulatory the efficacy of zinc ions may be a potential therapeutic strategy [37–39]. Hence, the application of GOx in conjunction with zinc ion-doped POM holds promise for diabetic wound repair. This combination is capable of modulating the hyperglycemic microenvironment, neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), and concurrently regulating the immune microenvironment. In order to make GOx and POM have closer spatial effects and better cascade reactions, a nanozyme composite catalytic system was constructed by loading GOx and POM into Chitosan nanoparticles (CS NPs), as an intermediate carrier due to their are widely used nanocarriers in the field of wound repair because of their excellent biocompatibility, high stability, and controlled drug release [40, 41]. To attain the desired therapeutic efficacy of the nanozyme composite catalytic system, further encapsulation within a hydrogel is envisaged, given its status as an optimal medium for wound repair. Hydrogels are extensively utilized in the management of diabetic wounds due to their exceptional hydrophilicity, robust drug-carrying capacity, and their propensity for sustained drug release [42, 43]. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a naturally occurring hydrogel, is identified as a non-sulfated glycosaminoglycan and serves as a principal constituent of the skin’s extracellular matrix (ECM). The modification of HA with aldehyde and methacrylic anhydride endows the hydrogel with light-curable properties and the capacity for soft-tissue adhesion, facilitated by diverse anchoring mechanisms such as Schiff base formation, hydrogen bonding, and physical interpenetration. These modified HA hydrogels are engineered to enable a controlled release of therapeutic agents, encompassing proteins and nanozymes. Their sustained-release characteristic renders them as prime candidates for drug delivery systems in the context of diabetic wound healing.

In this study, to address the cytotoxic effects and impaired plasticity of the immune microenvironment caused by the hyperglycemic-immune microenvironment, we constructed a nanozyme-functionalized hydrogel as a regenerative microenvironmental regulator for modulation of the hyperglycemic microenvironment and remodeling of the immune microenvironment. To achieve this, we synthesize Zn ion-doped molybdenum-based polyoxometalate (Zn-POM) with CAT and SOD enzyme activities through a one-step reduction method. The Zn-POM and GOx were co-loaded onto CS NPs by electrostatic adsorption (CS-GOx@Zn-POM), and a cascade catalytic reaction was constructed on the carrier surface of CS NPs using the spatial effect to achieve rapid and harmless degradation of glucose. CS-GOx@Zn-POM was then encapsulated in AHAMA hydrogel to construct a microenvironmental regulator for diabetic wound regeneration, namely, the AHAMA/CS-GOX@Zn-POM, which ameliorated the cytotoxic effect induced by hyperglycemia in diabetic wounds, alleviated oxidative stress, and enhanced the pro-healing function of endothelial cells and fibroblasts. Zn-POM was shown to scavenge excessive ROS in the wounds and induced the reprogramming of M1 macrophages to the M2 type by inhibiting the MAPK/IL-17 signaling pathway, thus inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory factors, such as CD86, IL-6, and TNF-α, as well as promoting the expression of anti-inflammatory factors, such as IL-4. Via these effects, effective remodeling of the immune microenvironment is achieved. M2 macrophages also improve the function of vascular endothelial cells and fibroblasts, thus promoting angiogenesis and type I collagen regeneration by releasing cytokines such as IGF-1, VEGF, and TGIF2 through paracrine effects. This results in the conversion of the microenvironment of diabetic wounds from a pathological to a regenerative state, and enhances the healing of diabetic wounds (Scheme 1). The integrated strategy based on modulation of the hyperglycemic-immune microenvironment in this study is expected to be an effective means to precisely regulate the healing of diabetic wounds.

Synthesis and characterization of Zn-POM

Zn-POM was synthesized via a one-step reduction method. Briefly, 2 mmol of ammonium molybdate (NH4)6Mo7O24⋅4H2O was first dissolved ultrasonically in 10 mL of ultrapure water, followed by the addition of 5 mL of ZnCl2 (0.108 mM) solution. Then, 4 mL of saturated vitamin C solution was added dropwise with stirring. Subsequently, the mixture was stirred for an additional 2 h to ensure a thorough reaction. Then, the reaction mixture was dialyzed for 6 h, lyophilized, and stored at 4 ℃.

In this study, the morphological features of Zn-POM under different pH conditions were observed using TEM (Talos F200i, America), and the elemental composition was analyzed by mapping. Meanwhile, the elemental composition and changes in the valence of Zn-POM were measured using XPS (ESCALAB Qxi, America). In addition, the changes in functional groups and oxygen vacancies of Zn-POM were analyzed by FTIR (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5, America) and ESR (Bruker A300, Germany), respectively.

AHAMA synthesis and characterization

First, 1 g of hyaluronic acid (HA) was completely dissolved in 100 mL of ultrapure water. Then, 5 mL of sodium periodate (0.5 M) solution was slowly introduced dropwise with continuous stirring. The solution was stirred at 250 r/min for 2 h at room temperature and protected from light; next 1 mL of ethylene glycol solution was added to deactivate the unreacted sodium periodate. The reacted solution was loaded into a dialysis bag with a molecular weight of 8000 Da and dialyzed for 3 days (with fluid changes every 8 h), and then lyophilized in a lyophilizer to obtain AHA. Next, 1 g of AHA or HA was completely dissolved in 100 mL of ultrapure water, and the double-bond modification was carried out via the addition of 1 mL of methacrylic acid in an ice bath, maintaining the pH between 8 and 8.5, and the reaction was carried out continuously for 12 h. Dialysis was carried out under ice bath conditions for 2 days (with fluid changes every 8 h) and lyophilized in a lyophilizer, dried, and stored at 4 °C to obtain AHAMA or HAMA synthesis. FTIR, and 1H NMR (Bruker 400 MHz, Germany) were used to analyze HA, AHA, HAMA, and AHAMA functional groups and chemical bond compositions for analysis.

Synthesis and characterization of CS NPs

CS NPs were prepared by the ionic cross-linking method. First, 100 mg of chitosan was dissolved in acetic acid solution at pH 5.5 to formulate a 0.7 mg/mL CS solution, and the configured solution was fully filtered through a filter (0.45 μm). Then sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) was dissolved in ultrapure water to configure a 0.5 mg/mL TPP solution, and the solution was fully filtered through a filter (0.22 μm). Next, 8 mL of CS solution was measured out for the subsequent steps in the nanoparticle formation process. The solution was stirred at 600 r/min under an ice bath; then, 2 mL of TPP was added slowly dropwise, and stirring was continued for 2 h. The obtained solution was centrifuged for 15 min (13,000 r/min), and the precipitate was dialyzed in PBS solution for 3 d to remove impurities. The precipitate was freeze-dried to obtain the CS NPs, which were stored at 4 ℃. The morphology of the CS NPs was observed by TEM.

Characterization of GOx, and Zn-POM enzyme activities in vitro

During the oxidation of glucose by GOx, hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid are generated. First, a 20 mM glucose solution was prepared, which was reacted with different concentrations of GOx (0, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL) at 37 ℃. Then the glucose concentration was measured using a glucometer at 0 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, and 24 h, and the pH of the solution was measured at the same time. The concentration of H2O2 was determined using the potassium iodide iodine-blue spectrophotometric method, in which a sample of standard H2O2 (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 mmol/L) was firstly reacted with potassium iodide for 10 min. The absorbance of the reaction product at 580 nm was measured by UV spectrometry and a standard curve was established. When glucose was reacted with glucose oxidase for 24 h, the reaction solution was reacted with potassium iodide, and the concentration of H2O2 was converted by the standard curve. The experiment was repeated three times.

CCK-8 evaluation of the cytotoxicity of Zn-POM

The biosafety of Zn-POM was evaluated by the CCK-8 method. In detail, 5103 human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were inoculated in 96-well plates. DMEM medium containing different concentrations of Zn-POM (0, 25, 50, 100, 200, 300 µg/mL) was added to the cells for 24 h. The medium was aspirated and CCK-8 (100 µl) working solution was added to each well and co-incubated with the cells at 37 ℃ for 0.5 h. Finally, cell viability was calculated by detecting the absorbance value at 450 nm using an enzyme marker. Similarly, this method was used for RAW264.7 and L929. The experiment was repeated three times.

ROS scavenging experiments with different ratios of (GOx: Zn-POM) nanozyme

Negatively charged GOx and Zn-POM were loaded onto positively charged CS NPs by electrostatic adsorption. The solution of CS NPs and Zn-POM (200 µg/mL) was stirred by magnetic stirring for 3 h, followed by centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 15 min). The precipitates were added to GOx (5, 10, 15, 20, 25 µg/mL) solutions of different concentrations, and stirring was continued for 12 h. Centrifugal separation (13000 rpm, 15 min) was performed again to obtain chitosan in different ratios of GOx: Zn-POM (5:200, 10:200, 15:200, 20:200, 25:200) nanozyme complexes. Then, 104 HUVECs were inoculated into confocal dishes and left to attach to the wall. Next, incubation with DMEM medium (different ratios of chitosan nanozyme complexes) was continued for 24 h. The extract was aspirated and the DCFH-DA working solution was added, mixed well, and then reacted for 30 min at 37 ℃ with protection from light. The nuclei of the cells were stained with DAPI for 10 min and then observed and photographed using a confocal microscope. The experiment was repeated three times.

Synthesis and characterization of CS NPs loaded with GOx or Zn-POM, and AHAMA/CS-GOx@Zn-POM

The results of the previous experiments showed that the optimum ratio of GOx to Zn-POM was 1:10. The prepared nanozyme complexes were lyophilized, and the functional groups and elemental compositions of the target products were analyzed using FTIR and XPS, respectively. The prepared target products were encapsulated in AHAMA solution and irradiated under UV light for 30 s. The morphological changes before and after hydrogel formation were observed and recorded. The prepared hydrogel was adhered to the fingertips on one side and to the soft tissues of the rat heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys on the other side to assess the adhesive properties of the material. Hydrogel adhesion was evaluated by visual observation and photographic recording. The prepared hydrogels were further lyophilized and the micro-morphological changes of the AHAMA hydrogels before and after drug loading were observed using scanning electron microscopy (Zeiss Gemini 300, Germany).

Antioxidant and macrophage phenotype modulation by nanozyme functionalized hydrogels

For this experiment, 1 × 106 RAW264.7 cells were inoculated into confocal dishes. The cells were incubated with DMEM medium containing hydrogen peroxide (100 µM) for 24 h to induce an inflammatory microenvironment. Incubation was then continued with the extracts from the PBS group, A-C group, A-C-G group, A-C-Z group, or A-C-G-Z group, for 24 h. The extracts were aspirated, and the DCFH-DA working solution was added, mixed, and then reacted for 30 min at 37 ℃ in the dark. The nuclei of the cells were stained with DAPI for 10 min and then observed and photographed by using a confocal microscope. ROS expression was analyzed by fluorescence quantification using Image J software.

As described above, 1 × 106 RAW 264.7 cells were inoculated into confocal dishes and treated with 200 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 24 h to induce macrophage polarization to the M1 phenotype and induce an imbalance in the immune microenvironment. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with hydrogel extracts of different systems for 24 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. Then, the membranes were disrupted with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min and closed with rapid closure solution for 20 min. CD206/CD86 primary antibodies were added to the cells for overnight incubation at 4 ℃, followed by the addition of fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. The nuclei of the cells were then stained with DAPI for 10 min, observed with a confocal microscope, and photographed. CD206/CD86 fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image J software. The experiment was repeated three times.

Nanozyme functionalized hydrogels enhance the endothelial, and fibroblast functions

After polarization to the M1 phenotype, macrophages were treated with PBS group, A-C group, A-C-G group, A-C-Z group, or A-C-G-Z group extracts, and supernatant was extracted. Then, 104 HUVECs were inoculated into confocal dishes and left to attach to the wall. The HUVECs were incubated with macrophage supernatant for 48 h. After aspirating the extract, the HUVECs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min. The membranes were disrupted with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min and then closed with rapid closure solution for 20 min. CD31 primary antibody was added and incubated at 4 ℃ overnight, and fluorescent secondary antibody was added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Next, the cells were incubated with FITC phalloidin. The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min. Confocal microscopy was used to observe and photograph the cells, and the fluorescence intensity of CD31 was quantified by Image J. The same method was used for fibroblasts. The same method was used for CoL I generation in fibroblasts. The experiment was repeated three times.

Macrophages in each group were lysed using triazole to extract mRNA. The experiment was repeated three times.

Animal studies

All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Animal Experimentation Centre of North Sichuan Medical College (approval number: 2023097). The glucose tolerance model was induced by feeding rats (200–250 g) with high-fat and high-sugar chow for 4 weeks. The type 2 diabetic rat model was induced by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 35 mg/kg). Modeling was considered successful when fasting glucose was ≥ 16.7 mmol/L 3 days after the STZ injection. The diabetic rats were randomly divided into five groups and anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (20 mg/kg). The operative area was prepared and disinfected, and holes were punched on their backs using a circular perforator with a diameter of 10 mm. Then, the whole skin was excised with a scalpel. The wounds were treated with the PBS group, A-C group, A-C-G group, A-C-Z group, or A-C-G-Z group respectively. Wounds were observed and photographed on days 0 d, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d after treatment, and wound healing was quantitatively analyzed with image J. At day 14, the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys of diabetic rats were collected and stained with H&E to assess in vivo biocompatibility. The experiment was repeated three times.

Results

Preparation and characterization of Zn-POM

In this study, Zn-POM was synthesized via a one-step reduction method. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results demonstrated that in PBS (pH 7.4) solution, the nanomaterial was uniformly distributed with a particle size of approximately 5 nm. When the pH was decreased to 6.0 and 4.0, the nanozyme self-assembled and aggregated into larger nanoparticles (Fig. 1a). Elemental mapping revealed that Zn-POM was composed of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, molybdenum, and zinc (Fig. 1b). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further confirmed the presence of these elements (Fig. 1c). The XPS valence spectrum showed peaks for Mo 3d at 230.9 eV and 234.1 eV, corresponding to Mo5+, and at 232.2 eV and 235.3 eV, corresponding to Mo6+ (Fig. 1d) [37]. Zn 2p peaks at 1021.9 eV and 1021.0 eV corresponded to Zn2+ and Zn0, respectively (Fig. 1e) [38]. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) indicated a new absorption peak at 569 nm, representing the Zn-O bonding functional group, which appeared in Zn-POM nanoparticles compared to POM, serving as a characteristic absorption peak of Zn-POM (Fig. 1f) [39, 40]. Electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements of oxygen vacancies in Zn-POM showed a large number of oxygen vacancies at a g value of 2.004, compared to POM (Fig. 1g) [41]. Measurement of the concentration of Zn2+ in Zn-POM, using a zinc ion detection kit, revealed a positive correlation between Zn ion concentration and Zn-POM concentration (Fig. 1h and Figure S1).

The enzyme-catalyzed activity of Zn-POM

After reacting various concentrations of Zn-POM with H2O2 for 10 min, UV spectral analysis revealed a decrease in the UV absorption peak of Zn-POM, indicating its reaction with H2O2 (Fig. 2e and f). The post-reaction H2O2 concentration, measured by potassium iodide iodine blue spectrophotometry, demonstrated that Zn-POM’s efficiency in scavenging H2O2 was positively correlated with its concentration and negatively correlated with pH. At pH 4.0, Zn-POM at 800 µg/mL achieved a H2O2 scavenging rate of 76.2% (Fig. 2g). Measurement of oxygen generation during the reaction between Zn-POM and H2O2, using a dissolved oxygen meter, showed that oxygen production was positively correlated with Zn-POM concentration and negatively correlated with pH. At pH 7.4, 200 µg/mL of Zn-POM produced an oxygen concentration of 5.14 mg/mL at the 10th minute (Fig. 2h and i). The reactive oxygen species (ROS) content, including ABTS radicals, hydroxyl radicals, and superoxide radicals, was measured by UV spectroscopy after Zn-POM treatment. Results indicated that Zn-POM’s ROS scavenging efficiency was positively correlated with its concentration (Fig. 2j and k, and 2l). The enzymatic activity of Zn-POM was evaluated using commercially available catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kits. The outcomes demonstrated a direct correlation between the concentration of Zn-POM and its enzymatic activity. Notably, at a concentration of 400 µg/mL, Zn-POM exhibited CAT and SOD activities of 27.9 U/mL and 46.4 U/mL, respectively (Figure S4 and Figure S5). By assessing the electrochemical properties, absorbance, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity of Zn-POM at specific time points—namely the 0th, 1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, and 14th days—the study revealed that, on the 14th day, the nanozyme exhibited a relatively stable state in terms of their potential, absorbance, and ROS clearance capabilities (Figures S6, S7, and S8).

In vitro biocompatibility of AHAMA/CS-GOx@Zn-POM

The CCK-8 assay results indicated that Zn-POM concentrations ranging from 0 to 200 µg/mL did not exert significant toxicity against HUVECs. However, cell viability decreased significantly at concentrations exceeding 200 µg/mL. Consequently, a Zn-POM concentration of 200 µg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments (Figure S17). Under hyperglycemic conditions, cellular oxidative stress increased, leading to elevated ROS levels. GOx also produced ROS during glucose metabolism. Therefore, regulating the GOx: Zn-POM ratio is crucial for balancing ROS production and clearance. Chitosan nanozyme complexes with varying GOx: Zn-POM ratios were applied to DMEM-cultured HUVECs. The DCFH-DA probe results indicated that when the GOx: Zn-POM ratio exceeded 1:10, ROS production during glucose metabolism surpassed ROS scavenging by Zn-POM. Thus, a GOx: Zn-POM ratio of 1:10 was chosen for further experiments (Figure S18). The biocompatibility of nanozyme functionalized hydrogels was assessed using live-dead staining. In the HG group, HUVECs cultured under 50 mmol/L hyperglycemic conditions showed inhibited cell proliferation and increased cell death. In contrast, the other groups exhibited rapid cell proliferation and low cell death rates, indicating excellent biocompatibility of the nanozyme hydrogels (Fig. 4a and Figure S19).

Verification of the ability of AHAMA/CS-GOx@Zn-POM to promote cell migration and tube formation

The cell migration experiments demonstrated that hyperglycemic conditions significantly inhibited the migration ability of HUVECs. Amelioration of the hyperglycemic microenvironment with GOx did not result in a significant improvement in the cell migration rate, potentially due to H2O2 production during glucose metabolism, which induced oxidative stress and cellular dysfunction. However, the A-C-Z group, which cleared ROS, showed a 1.8-fold increase in cell migration rate compared with the HG group. The A-C-G-Z group, which elicited improvements hyperglycemic microenvironment as well as oxidative stress, exhibited a 2.4-fold increase in cell migration rate compared with the HG group (Fig. 4b, d).

Tube formation assays using Matrigel matrix gel to simulate the extracellular matrix revealed that HUVECs in the HG group had difficulty forming vascular tubes, with significantly fewer nodes and shorter tube lengths than the PBS group. In contrast, the A-C-G-Z group, which improved the hyperglycemic microenvironment and scavenged ROS, significantly promoted vascular tube formation, with the number of nodes and total tube length being markedly higher than in other treatment groups (Fig. 4c, e, f).

These findings indicated that the A-C-G-Z group significantly enhanced HUVEC migration and vascular tube formation by ameliorating the hyperglycemic microenvironment and reducing oxidative stress. This group demonstrated superior pro-migration and angiogenic effects than the other groups.

Discussion

Diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disease characterized by chronic hyperglycemia due to endogenous insulin resistance or deficiency [44]. Diabetes mellitus can lead to multiple organ and tissue impairments and complications. Diabetic wounds are one of the most serious complications among people with diabetes, and more than 50% of non-traumatic amputations of the lower limbs are often caused by diabetic wounds [45–47]. Diabetic wounds are exposed to a complex pathological microenvironment characterized by hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and immune imbalance. The hyperglycemic microenvironment leads to ROS overproduction, which in turn causes cellular oxidative stress damage, resulting in cellular dysfunction and apoptosis [48, 49]. Macrophage-dominated immune microenvironment disorders are another key pathogenic mechanism in diabetic wounds, and impaired macrophage plasticity leads to M1 overaccumulation and obstructs M2 reprogramming, which causes the persistence of a pro-inflammatory state in diabetes, thus further impeding the process of wound repair [50]. Therefore, integrated regulation of the hyperglycemic microenvironment and immune-disordered pathological microenvironment is a key mechanism to achieve diabetic wound regeneration. In this study, we used GOx to ameliorate the hyperglycemic microenvironment and exploited the properties of Zn-POM to scavenge ROS as well as to remodel the immune microenvironment, and constructed a diabetic wound regeneration system by co-loading GOx and Zn-POM onto CS NPs via electrostatic adsorption and encapsulating them in AHAMA hydrogels. The system was shown to be capable of catalyzing the degradation of glucose in a cascade, ameliorating oxidative stress, and enhancing the pro-healing function of endothelial cells and fibroblasts. Simultaneously, it could promote the M2 polarization of macrophages, synergistically accelerating the process of regeneration and remodeling of diabetic wounds. Ultimately, the system was demonstrated to regulate the hyperglycemic-immune microenvironment, remodel the regenerative diabetic wound microenvironment, and promote the repair of diabetic wounds.

In nanomaterials-based applications, it is important to validate the synthesis of nanomaterials and their efficacy for the construction of cascade catalytic systems. In this study, we synthesized Zn-POM via a one-step reduction method and verified that Zn2+ was successfully doped into POM vacancies by TEM, FTIR, and ESR. In Zn-POM, a multi-elemental and multivalent polymetallic oxide, the abundant valence transitions between metal ions played an important role in ROS scavenging. When ROS binds to the Zn-POM enzyme center and undergoes a redox reaction (Zn0, Mo5+ lost electrons exhibiting oxidative properties; meanwhile Zn2+, Mo6+ gained electrons exhibiting reductive properties), metal ions achieve ROS scavenging through electron transfer between valence states. In addition, the nanozyme exhibited a self-assembly effect under acidic conditions, which was caused by acid-induced protonation to form hydrogen bonds. This self-assembly effect of the nanozyme significantly improved ROS scavenging efficacy [51]. Enzymatic activity and stability of nanozymes are important characteristics for their use as catalysts. In this study, the Zn-POM enzyme activity units were measured, which were able to mimic higher levels of SOD and CAT enzyme activity, significantly improving the efficacy of oxidative stress in restoring cellular function. The potential, absorbance, and clearance of Zn-POM were further measured over a period of 14 days, and the nanozyme results and properties remained in a stable state over that period of time, realizing the long-term maintenance of the effectiveness of ROS scavenging. Furthermore, the positively charged CS NPs synthesized by ionic cross-linking could adsorb negatively charged GOx and Zn-POM by electrostatic interaction and form a cascade catalytic system on their surface, that exhibited superior efficacy in the removal of glucose metabolic byproducts owing to its spatial effect.

Hydrogels are an important biomaterial with a variety of medical applications, including wound repair, tissue engineering, and drug release [52]. HA-prepared hydrogels have inherent advantages as they mimic the structure and function of natural ECMs [53]. In this study, AHAMA was obtained by modifying the aldehyde group and methacrylic anhydride on HA to endow it with light-curing ability as well as soft tissue adhesion. The light-cured AHAMA showed a three-dimensional reticular structure under scanning electron microscopy with a large pore structure and surface area, and the nanozyme complex was physically adsorbed on its surface or inside the pores to achieve slow release of the drug. AHAMA could also achieve adhesion to soft tissues through various anchoring mechanisms. The aldehyde group in AHAMA formed a stable Schiff base bond with the amino group in the surrounding soft tissues, facilitating bonding between the hydrogel and the surrounding tissues. Furthermore, water molecules in the hydrogel could form hydrogen bonds with water molecules on the wound surface, which enhanced contact between the hydrogel and the wound. In addition, the water molecules also entered the wounded tissue by physical penetration and formed a water layer, which further consolidated the connection between the hydrogel and the wound surface. All of these modes of adhesion contributed to the wound healing and repair process.

Biocompatibility validation aims to assess the toxicity and safety of biomaterials to cells to ensure that therapeutic efficacy is achieved while minimizing potential side effects. CCK-8 assay results showed that, at concentrations below 200 µg/mL Zn-POM did not elicit significant toxic responses in cells; however excessive concentrations of the nanozyme might exert toxic against cells through a variety of mechanisms, including disruption of cell membranes, intracellular accumulation leading to metabolic disorders, and dysregulation of intracellular ion homeostasis. During the degradation of glucose by GOx, its degradation byproduct H2O2 exerted toxic effects against cells; therefore selection of an appropriate GOx: Zn-POM ratio was crucial. In this study, the DCFH-DA probe results indicated that when the ratio of GOx: Zn-POM was 1:10, the nanozymatic complex achieved an equilibrium state of ROS production and scavenging, which led to efficient degradation of glucose and buffered the toxic effects of glucose degradation by-products against cells.

Cell migration is critical in the process of wound healing because it helps to repair the wound and promote tissue regeneration [54]. When tissue is damaged, surrounding cells move towards the wound by means of cell migration, filling the wound and forming new tissue. This process of cell migration is essential for wound closure and healing [55]. Neovascularization provides an adequate blood supply and oxygen to the wound to support cell survival and tissue regeneration [56]. Under the pathological conditions of diabetes, cell migration, and angiogenesis are impaired, and improvement of vascular endothelial cell function is extremely important for wound healing. In the present in vitro cellular experiments, nanozyme functionalized hydrogels were found to significantly promote vascular endothelial cell migration and tube formation by ameliorating the hyperglycemic microenvironment and scavenging ROS.

In the diabetic pathological state, immune imbalance in macrophages leads to impaired M1 to M2 conversion, which manifests as a state of persistent inflammation and impedes the wound healing process [57]. M1 macrophages accumulate in wounds, generating a pathological microenvironment characterized by oxidative stress, enhanced protein hydrolysis, and cellular damage. Therefore, inducing reprogramming of M1-type macrophages to the M2 phenotype, which in turn alleviates oxidative stress and modulates the inflammatory microenvironment, is important for achieving wound repair. Zinc ions regulate macrophage polarization through multiple signaling pathways, e.g., by inhibiting the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway to regulate macrophage phenotypic transition from M1 to M2, thus improving the inflammatory microenvironment, and accelerating wound healing [58]. In addition, zinc ions promote macrophage polarization towards M2 via the PI3K/AKT and Wnt signaling pathways [59]. Overexpression of ROS is an important factor leading to the blockade of macrophage M1 to M2 reprogramming. Removal of ROS from diabetic wounds ameliorates the inflammatory microenvironment by inhibiting the NF-κB, MAPK, and cGAS-STING signaling pathways; promotes macrophage M1 to M2 phenotypic polarization, and increases the level of anti-inflammatory factors, thus promoting diabetic wound healing [60–63]. In this study, DCFH-DA probe analysis as well as immunofluorescence showed that Zn-POM could significantly reduce the level of ROS in M1-type macrophages. Furthermore, Zn-POM up-regulated the expression of CD206 as well as down-regulated the expression of CD86, which promoted macrophage polarization towards M2, thus ameliorating the inflammatory microenvironment. Regarding the mechanism by which Zn-POM promoted the reprogramming of M1-type macrophages, genetic assay results showed that Zn-POM inhibited the MAPK/IL-17 signaling pathway, down-regulated the expression of inflammatory factors (such as IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, COX-2, and MMP-9), and up-regulated anti-inflammatory factors (such as IL-4), thus achieving the reprogramming of macrophages from M1 to M2.

Dynamic changes in pH during wound healing play a crucial role in the progression of the healing process, affecting cellular life activities and biochemical reactions. Intact skin maintains a naturally acidic environment with a pH between 4 and 6. After a skin injury, during the hemostatic phase, the pH temporarily rises to approximately 7.4 due to microvascular leakage, which results from the influx of blood and plasma components. As healing progresses, the inflammatory phase develops a slightly acidic environment (pH approximately 4.5-6) due to increased production of metabolites. During the proliferation and remodeling phase, the inflammation subsides and the pH approaches a weakly alkaline level, which promotes proliferation and migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts to facilitate wound repair . Diabetic wounds present a chronic inflammatory state, and the repair process is stagnated in the inflammatory phase, which is mainly manifested by excessive oxidative stress as well as increased metabolic wastes leading to an acidic state of the wounds. In our constructed system, acidic conditions promoted GOx (optimal pH 5–6) to rapidly improve the hyperglycemic microenvironment, while Zn-POM underwent a self-assembly effect to enhance its efficacy in scavenging ROS, which in turn significantly improved the pathologic microenvironment. Thus, the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative remodeling phase was achieved, and the normal healing process of wound repair was recovered. In the proliferative remodeling phase of wound healing, the inflammation subsided, its pH became alkaline, the degradation by-products of GOx decreased, and the ROS scavenging activity of Zn-POM stabilized, achieving the effective maintenance of the regenerative microenvironment of the wound. Thus, the evolution of pH during wound healing echoed the nanozyme activity.

Diabetic wounds are characterized by hyperglycemia-induced vascular endothelial cell dysfunction, which leads to impaired vascularization of the wounds, resulting in hypoxia and activation of the hypoxia-induced inflammatory cascade. This ultimately impedes the wound healing process [64]. The hyperglycemic microenvironment also impairs the proliferation, migration, and anti-apoptotic capacity of fibroblasts, as well as inhibiting ECM production, thus hindering wound repair [65]. In diabetic wounds, restricted macrophage conversion from a pro-inflammatory phenotype (M1) to an anti-inflammatory phenotype (M2) disrupts the tissue regenerative microenvironment. In the in vivo experiments, H&E staining results demonstrated that the shortest wound margin spacing as well as visible newly regenerative hair and sebaceous glands were present in the A-C-G-Z group compared with the PBS group, suggesting that the former group effectively promoted the regeneration of diabetic wounds. In addition, the immunohistochemical staining results indicated that compared with the other group, GOx and Zn-POM in the A-C-G-Z group catalyzed the harmless degradation of glucose in a synergistic cascade, ameliorating the cytotoxicity induced by hyperglycemia. Furthermore, the group was able to up-regulate the expression of CD206 and down-regulate the expression of CD86, and promote the reprogramming of macrophages from M1 to M2. The polarized M2 macrophages were able to secrete anti-inflammatory factors and reduce the level of ROS, which ameliorated the deleterious effects of hyperglycemia and immune microenvironmental disorders. Immunohistochemical staining results for CD31 and type I collagen revealed that the expression of CD31 was significantly up-regulated in the A-C-G-Z group compared with the other group, significantly promoting vascularization, which is the major pathway for transporting nutrients and removing metabolic wastes in diabetic wounds. Meanwhile, the expression of type I collagen was up-regulated, with more regularly and densely arranged type I collagen in the A-C-G-Z group, which further enhanced the structure and function of the newly regenerative tissue. Thus, angiogenesis and collagen regeneration synergize with each other to promote the healing of diabetic wounds.

In conclusion, the nanozymatic functionalized hydrogels constructed in this study could effectively regulate the hyperglycemic-immune microenvironment in diabetic wounds, as well as accelerate vascular neogenesis and collagen deposition to alleviate the oxidative stress and restore the normal polarization process of macrophages, remodel the microenvironment of diabetic wounds from pathological to regenerative, and ultimately achieve accelerated repair of diabetic wounds.

Conclusion

In this study, we aimed to target the hyperglycemic microenvironment and impaired plasticity of the immune microenvironment in diabetic wounds, which represented key factors underlying tissue regeneration disorders. To this end, we constructed a comprehensive therapeutic system (AHAMA/CS-GOx@Zn-POM) that integrally regulated the hyperglycemic microenvironment and remodeled the immune microenvironment in diabetic wounds. This system was shown to be capable of effectively eliminating the toxic effect of the hyperglycemic microenvironment in diabetic wounds and catalyzing the harmless degradation of glucose via a cascade reaction to alleviate oxidative stress and improve cellular function. Zn-POM not only ameliorated the inflammatory microenvironment by clearing ROS, but also polarized macrophages towards M2 through the MAPK/IL-17 signaling pathway, and improved the physiological functions of vascular endothelial cells and fibroblasts through the paracrine effect of M2 macrophages. These effects collectively led to the remodeling of the diabetic wound microenvironment to promote tissue regeneration. This comprehensive regulatory strategy based on modulation of the hyperglycemic-immune microenvironment is expected to provide a promising therapeutic approach for diabetic wound repair.
