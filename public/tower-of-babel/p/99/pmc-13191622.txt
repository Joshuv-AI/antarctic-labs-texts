Educational Case: Breast neoplasms: Phyllodes tumor of the breast

These are divided into three basic competencies: Disease Mechanisms and Processes, Organ System Pathology, and Diagnostic Medicine and Therapeutic Pathology. Primary objective

Objective BR2.1: Fibroadenoma and Phyllodes Tumor. Compare and contrast fibroadenoma and phyllodes tumor in terms of clinical features, morphologic findings, and prognosis.

Competency 2: Organ System Pathology; Topic: Breast (BR); Learning Goal 2: Breast Neoplasms.

Patient presentation

A 54-year-old woman presents to the clinic with a large lump in the right breast which she first noticed a week ago while showering. The lump is not painful, and she has not noticed any discharge from the nipple. She is otherwise healthy with well-controlled mild hypertension. Her family history is significant for an aunt who died of breast cancer at the age of 67 (she is unsure of what type) and a younger sister who had a fibroadenoma at the age of 27. Her mother, age 78, has ovarian cancer and is on a clinical trial. She is a math and science teacher in the local high school and is very active. Her review of systems is non-contributory, and she does not report any weight loss. She is post-menopausal, and her last mammogram was 5 years ago and was unremarkable.

Diagnostic finding, Part 1

Physical examination demonstrates a woman in no acute distress. Vital signs are blood pressure of 130/68 mmHg, heart rate of 73/min, respiratory rate of 16 breaths per minute, temperature of 98.8 °F, oxygen saturation 97%, and body mass index of 27.3 kg/m2. A 3 cm, non-tender, round, rubbery, mobile mass is palpated in the right upper outer quadrant of the breast. There is no skin thickening, erythema, retraction, dimpling, or nipple drainage. The left breast is unremarkable. No enlarged supraclavicular or axillary lymph nodes are palpated. Her physical examination is otherwise unremarkable.

Questions/discussion points, Part 1

What is the differential of a breast lump?

A palpable lump in the breast can represent benign or malignant neoplasms, as well as proliferative and non-proliferative fibrocystic changes due to hormone imbalance, hamartomas, hyperplasia, lactational changes, fat necrosis, lipomas, vascular or stromal tumors, inflammatory conditions, or infection with granuloma or abscess formation.

While the differential of a breast mass is extensive, a detailed history and physical exam can help focus the differential diagnosis. For example, if the patient is or was recently pregnant, lactational adenoma may be higher on the differential. If she reports recent trauma to the breast, one might place hematoma or fat necrosis higher in the differential. A travel history, breast-feeding status, or current fever, breast pain, warmth, or erythema might point to infectious causes such as tuberculosis or mastitis with abscess formation. If the patient has a history of diabetes, an inflammatory condition such as diabetic mastopathy should be considered.

While most breast masses are benign, breast carcinoma is very common affecting 1 in 8 women. A thorough breast exam should be performed to help begin to differentiate the probability of benign verses malignant lesions.

What features are suggestive of a benign verse malignant breast lesion?

Benign breast lesions tend to be small, round to lobular, rubbery, mobile, and well-circumscribed with well-defined borders. Whereas if the mass is firm, fixed, non-tender, irregularly shaped, and has indistinct borders, it is more likely to represent an invasive malignant process. Additionally, associated skin changes such as thickening, edema, dimpling, or nipple retraction, and the presence of regional lymphadenopathy (axillary or cervical) should also raise concern for a more aggressive process.

What additional workup would be helpful in determining if the lesion is benign or malignant?

While a thorough family history and physical examination are helpful and have a specificity for identifying malignant breast lesions of 86–99% alone, the sensitivity is only 49–69% and has a poor positive predictive value.2 Radiographic imaging such as mammography, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may add additional information. When radiographic imaging is added to the family history and physical examination, the sensitivity of identifying malignancies improves up to 82%.2, 3, 4

For patients who present with a palpable lesion, both a focused mammogram and ultrasound are typically performed, utilizing the strengths of each to best characterize the lesion. When screening for breast cancer (the patient has no palpable lesions), a mammogram is the initial, and often only, imaging modality in women age 40 and older with less dense breast tissue. Any concerning lesions may be followed up with ultrasound evaluation to further characterize the lesion. In women with dense breast tissue, ultrasound may be a better initial screening test as mammograms are less sensitive in dense breast tissue.3 However, full breast ultrasounds are very labor and time intensive and the quality is dependent on the technologist's skill level. MRI tends to be preferred for patients with dense breast tissue because it has a higher sensitivity than other imaging modalities. However, MRI has a higher false positive rate compared to ultrasound, is time consuming, carries an increased risk of contrast reactions, and is more expensive.2,3

Questions/discussion points, Part 2

Describe what is seen on the mammogram and ultrasound?

On mammogram, there is a well-circumscribed mass with micro-lobulated margins in the right upper outer quadrant. The mass appears isodense to hyperdense compared to the surrounding breast parenchyma and is lacking calcifications. The ultrasound shows an irregularly shaped parallel solid mass measuring over 3 cm. The mass has micro-lobulated margins and is inhomogeneously hypoechoic (there are bright and dark portions).

What are some imaging characteristics of breast carcinoma?

Overarching radiographic findings concerning for breast carcinoma include irregular shape, micro-lobulated or spiculated margins, obscure poorly defined borders, skin thickening, and presence of lymphadenopathy. Since each radiographic modality has its strengths and weaknesses, performing multiple modalities helps to give a more complete evaluation. Mammography's strength is its ability to distinguish suspicious calcifications that are more difficult to appreciate with ultrasound. For example, pleomorphic calcifications are a concerning feature, characterized by close clustering, varying shapes and sizes with jagged edges, and linear and branching patterns which are best visualized on mammogram. However, ultrasound is the preferred modality for detecting other suspicious features, such as posterior acoustic shadowing (an increased darkness immediately below the lesion) and heterogeneous internal echogenicity (areas of differing brightness within the lesion). On MRI, worrisome features for any type of malignancy include spiculated morphology and mass enhancement.

How does the patient's clinical and radiographic findings focus our differential?

The combined clinical and radiographic findings of a round, well-circumscribed, and mobile mass without characteristics of invasion suggest that this is most likely a benign lesion such as fibroadenoma, phyllodes tumor, or hamartoma.

Differentiating phyllodes tumor from fibroadenoma radiographically can be diagnostically very challenging because they display nearly identical features. While a lobular configuration may be a clue to a phyllodes tumor, most phyllodes tumors show a rounded appearance like a fibroadenoma. Despite clinical and radiologic findings favoring a benign process, the patient is usually referred for tissue biopsy, especially in the setting of a solid mass. Furthermore, the presence of micro-lobulated margins and internal heterogenicity in the current case should prompt further workup, specifically, histologic evaluation for diagnostic confirmation.

What is the diagnosis?

Phyllodes tumor.

How does a phyllodes tumor differ from a fibroadenoma clinically, histologically, and prognostically?

Fibroadenomas are benign neoplasms that most commonly present as painless, firm to rubbery, slow growing, mobile, and well-defined nodules. They are typically smaller than 3 cm and tend to occur in postpubescent women younger than 35 years old. However, they can occur at any age as well as in men who have gynecomastia. Fibroadenomas are sensitive to hormones, and some patients describe cyclic growth and tenderness associated with their menstrual cycle and with pregnancy.5

Histologically, fibroadenomas demonstrate a well-circumscribed proliferation of both benign appearing breast glands and stroma. The stroma tends to either proliferate around open ducts, separating the normally clustered glands or compress the glands into elongated cords (Figs. 5 and 6). The stroma tends to have a normal to low cellularity; however, highly cellular fibroadenomas do occur. Prognostically, fibroadenomas are benign and rarely recur after surgical excision; however, synchronous and metasynchronous lesions can occur.5

Phyllodes tumors are usually benign and present similarly to fibroadenomas as painless, firm to rubbery, mobile, and well-defined nodules. However, phyllodes tumors tend to be lobulated, grow more quickly, and be larger at presentation (4–5cm). They can be greater than 10 cm and cause skin ulceration due to compression ischemia.5 They can also present with reactive lymphadenopathy secondary to tumor necrosis,5 which is concerning for malignancy. For these reasons, complete excision is recommended.

Histologically, phyllodes tumors are also well-circumscribed glandular and stromal proliferations. However, they tend to be more cellular than fibroadenomas with greater stromal expansion that stretches the glandular epithelium into “leaf-like” arcs and clefts (Figs. 3 and 4).6 The name phyllodes comes from the Greek words for leaf, “phyllon,” and form, “eidos.” Prognostically, phyllodes tumors are most commonly benign but are more likely to recur than fibroadenomas with a 10–17% recurrence rate.5

A search for what other histologic features should be made in a phyllodes tumor and why?

While most phyllodes tumors are benign, approximately 10% of tumors are malignant, underscoring the need for careful histologic evaluation for atypical features.7 Of malignant phyllodes tumors, 30% recur and around 23% metastasize.5,8,9 Metastatic spread of malignant phyllodes tumors most often consists only of stromal elements and follow a hematogenous route.5 The lungs and bone marrow are the most common metastatic sites, but metastases can arise anywhere.5

To diagnose a phyllodes tumor as malignant, it must either contain malignant heterologous sarcomatous elements (such as chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, etc.) or all of the following five features: increased stromal cellularity, marked stromal nuclear pleomorphism, stromal overgrowth, increased mitotic figures (>10 per 10 high power fields), and infiltrative borders (Fig. 7).5 A diagnosis of borderline phyllodes tumor is rendered when only some of these features are identified. Borderline phyllodes tumors tend to behave more commonly as benign phyllodes tumors and rarely metastasize, but do have a greater rate of recurrence (14–25%).5

The tumor has increased cellularity with condensation under the epithelium (arrows) (H&E, 10x). C. Low power field showing stromal overgrowth without an epithelial component (H&E, 4x). D. What is the pathogenesis of fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumors?

Most neoplasms develop secondary to genetic mutations in either oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes. Oncogenes code for proteins that drive cell growth and proliferation and when mutated cause the protein to be continually active leading to tumor development. Tumor suppressor genes code for proteins that inhibit cell growth and proliferation and when mutated cause the protein to lose its function, allowing cell growth and proliferation to occur uninhibited. Most carcinomas develop mutations in multiple oncogenes and/or tumor suppressor genes.

The majority of fibroadenomas carry a mediator complex subunit 12 (MED12) mutation which codes for one protein of a multiprotein complex called the Mediator.5 The Mediator functions to regulate the transcription of numerous different genes.9, 10, 11 In this way the Mediator behaves in some situations as an oncogene and in others as a tumor suppressor. The MED12 mutation in fibroadenomas works as an oncogene driving stromal and epithelial proliferation.

Phyllodes tumors also tend to harbor MED12 mutations suggesting they share a similar pathogenesis with fibroadenomas. Additionally, approximately one-third of phyllodes tumors demonstrate histologic similarities to fibroadenomas, raising the possibility that fibroadenomas may represent a precursor tumor.3 Phyllodes tumors contain additional mutations, such as oncogenes RARA and TERT, which are thought to play a role in tumorigenesis. Mutations in these genes are not typically seen in fibroadenomas.5 MED12 mutations are less common in malignant phyllodes tumors. However, mutations in well-known oncogenes, such as PIK3CA, BRAF and EGFR as well as tumor suppressor genes, such as RB1, TP53, NF1, and PTEN, are seen in borderline and malignant phyllodes tumors. As expected, higher-grade tumors display an increased number of chromosomal abnormalities.5 A comparison of tumor characteristics between fibroadenoma, phyllodes tumor, and malignant phyllodes tumor is summarized in Table 1.

In the current case, the tumor was excised and histologic evaluation was confirmed to be a phyllodes tumor. Given that malignant features were not identified, no additional follow up was recommended beyond routine mammogram screening.

Teaching points

•The clinical features of both phyllodes tumors and fibroadenomas are those of a benign mass, including round shape with well-defined borders and rubbery consistency. Additionally, they are usually mobile and can be tender, but typically do not cause skin changes. However, phyllodes tumors can be large and cause ischemic ulcerations. Classically, fibroadenomas occur in patients younger than 35, and phyllodes tumors occur in patients between 40 and 50 years old.
