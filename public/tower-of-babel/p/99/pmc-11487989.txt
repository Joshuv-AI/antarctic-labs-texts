The directors of resident support program: a program evaluation of a resident well-being and support innovation
Authors: Shannon M Ruzycki, Amanda Roze des Ordons, Melinda Davis, J MacGregor Steele, Kannin Osei Tutu, Lisa Welikovitch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical training can be a challenging time for residents both professionally and personally. Resident support programs must be able to address a range of potential experiences, be accessible and easy to navigate, and consider the unique context of residency. Rigorous evaluation of resident support programs is needed to determine whether these programs are meeting these goals.MethodsThe Directors of Resident Support (DRS) program, launched in January 2021 at the Cumming School of Medicine at the University of Calgary, is a near-peer support model consisting of three faculty physicians, trained in peer support, who receive contacts from residents needing support for any issue. DRS physicians provide empathetic listening, referral to existing resources, and peer support for residents. The most common topic for contact was to discuss feedback or evaluation (n = 10, 24.4%) and the most common response was listening and support (n = 29, 70.7%). Residents also contacted DRS to discuss experiences of racism, physical assault, sexual harassment, and mental health crises. Residents (n = 13) rated a median score of 74 out of possible 100 for usefulness (interquartile range [IQR] 1-100, with higher scores suggesting greater usefulness). Postgraduate Medical Education offices seeking to create resident support programs may anticipate that about 3% of residents may use a similar program per year and that the typical interaction would last 2 h, with a wide range. Background

Residency is a notoriously challenging time professionally and personally [1–3]. At work, residents may encounter novel, high-stress situations that can be difficult to navigate. For example, adverse events [4], death of a patient [5], and experiences of harassment, discrimination, sexual assault, and racism during medical training affect resident well-being [6–8]. Residency training can also have impacts on a trainee’s personal relationships [9], finances [10], mental health [11], and physical health [12]. Further, residency training may coincide with external stressors, such as parenthood [13], personal illness [14], or the death of a loved one. Because these stressors may be caused or worsened by residency training, resident physicians often require specialized support programs that consider their unique position in the healthcare workforce [15]. However, identifying or accessing the appropriate supports can be a barrier for medical trainees seeking assistance in part due to the breadth of concerns that a resident may encounter [16].

To streamline the process of navigating available supports for residents, the Postgraduate Medical Education Office (PGME) at the University of Calgary’s Cumming School of Medicine (CSM) developed an innovative, near-peer support program for resident physicians. The Directors of Resident Support (DRS) program consists of a team of faculty physicians who are trained in empathetic listening and familiar with available resources for residents. Residents seeking assistance for any issue can contact the DRS program to schedule a meeting with a DRS physician, who will then assist the resident physician through a combination of possible actions. The aim of this report is to describe a rigorous, theory-driven evaluation of the DRS program to inform development of similar programs in other settings. The use of an evaluation framework builds on existing descriptive studies in the literature about effective resident support and will be of particular interest to PGME offices looking to develop or expand support services.

Methods

Setting & initiative

The CSM PGME Office administrates and oversees 65 residency training programs for about 830 residents [17]. A series of internal reports [18] described an important gap for residents in navigating existing supports, policies and procedures; in response, the PGME launched the Directors of Resident Support (DRS) program in January 2021 with the aim of providing empathetic listening and guidance to existing supports for residents with any number of issues that effects their well-being.

The DRS program is a novel, ‘no wrong door approach’ to resident concerns of all types, in contrast to existing programs that focus solely on one topic, such as learner well-being or submission of formal complaints. Residents may approach the DRS program with any issue or concern, whether related to residency training or personal life. The DRS program is led by CSM-affiliated faculty physicians who are trained to help residents navigate the complex network of available supports, policies, and processes pertaining to medical training, careers, illness, remediation, or personal conflicts (Appendix 1). Faculty physicians within ten years of completing their training were thought to be most suitable for this role as a near-peer support approach, where physicians may more clearly understand the nuances of residency training compared to other professionals, such as counsellors, but are not technically peers as they are in a more senior role than the residents. The faculty physicians were from a range of departments (critical care, family medicine, internal medicine, pediatrics) to ensure that residents could be matched with a faculty who was not involved in their training or assessment. Residents seeking support can contact the DRS program either through a secure intake form on the PGME website or through direct e-mail to a confidential mailbox. A PGME administrator monitors for requests daily and directs concerns to DRS physicians on a rotating schedule or by resident request. The DRS physicians respond directly to the resident, with the aim of setting a meeting time within 72 h of contact. Residency programs can also request the DRS meet with a group of residents for support around program-level issues.

Information about the DRS program is presented at the yearly PGME resident orientation for incoming residents, and residency program directors are asked each year to send an e-mail reminder about the DRS program to all residents in their program. The DRS program has also been invited to present on its aims and other available residency supports at academic half-days, town halls, and resident retreats. Further, the DRS program is suggested as a resource for residents undergoing remediation, for residents who require a leave of absence, and when residency training programs have experienced a challenging event.

Evaluation

Evaluation of the DRS program was guided by Donabedian’s framework [19], which considers structure, process, outcomes, and balancing measures, and has been commonly used to guide program evaluation outside of its original conceptualization within quality assurance work (Table 1) [20]. The aim of the evaluation was to describe utilization by residents, resident satisfaction with their DRS interaction, necessary resourcing for scale and spread, and to identify opportunities for program improvement. This program evaluation study was approved by the University of Calgary’s institutional ethics review board.

Directors of Resident Support program evaluation questions, measures, and data sources

Structure measures are the capacity and resources available in the system in which the program is situated [21]. In this evaluation, structure measures include the number of DRS physicians, the training provided to DRS physicians, the amount of administrative support, and the amount of time spent per DRS contact. Process measures, which describe how the program works to achieve its outcome [21], included the number of unique and total contacts to the DRS program, the time between initial contact to first meeting with the resident, and the nature of the concern. Outcome measures were selected to reflect the desired impact of the DRS program [21]. These comprised resident satisfaction with the DRS interaction and the types of resolutions or outcome of the DRS contact.

Data were collected through field notes, standardized data collection forms, and resident surveys (Table 1). Field notes about program function and structure were obtained from DRS meeting agendas. After each DRS program contact, the DRS administrator completed a standardized tracking sheet with the date, training program, contact information, assigned DRS team member, and method of contact. After the first contact, the assigned DRS completed a standardized intake form to record the date and method of initial program contact, the date of initial meeting, the total duration of interaction, a high-level classification of the concern (e.g., remediation/performance, career decision making, workplace conflict, among others), how the interaction was resolved, and space for free text notes (Appendix 2).

Residents who contacted the DRS program through the DRS e-mail or webform received a two-question survey (Appendix 3; Qualtrics, Provo, UT) 6 months to one year after their first contact only. The survey was developed based on the evaluation objectives. A reminder to complete the survey was sent 6 months later. Residents who contacted a DRS physician directly were not included so that the resident information would not have to be shared with the program administrator and to avoid a request for feedback coming directly from the person who provided support. The survey asked residents to first rate the degree to which they perceived the interaction with the DRS program to be helpful (0 indicating completely unhelpful and 100 indicating completely helpful) and an open text box for comments. Demographic data were not collected in order to preserve resident anonymity. Incomplete surveys were included in the analysis. Open text responses were de-identified and themes were identified and summarized. Respondents provided informed consent for their data to be collected and published and were not compensated for their participation.

Results

Resourcing - structure measures

The DRS program recruited three staff physicians with diverse lived and professional experiences and an interest in medical trainee well-being. One physician resigned from the program to take another opportunity in summer 2023 and a new DRS was recruited. Each DRS was allocated 0.2 full-time-equivalents (FTE) of protected time (7 h per week) and an administrative assistant was allocated 0.05 FTE for coordination and administration of the program. DRS physicians underwent training in empathetic listening [22], harassment reporting, accepting sensitive disclosures [23], and local supports available to medical trainees.

Utilization - process measures

There were 62 total contacts to the DRS program between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023 (Table 2). Most of these contacts were initiated by residents (n = 58, 93.5%) with the remaining contacts initiated by program directors and residency training programs (n = 4, 6.4%). Contacts were most commonly from surgical and medical specialty programs (n = 20, 32.3% each). The number of contacts increased each year of the program and contacts peaked in January to March of each year and were lowest in July to September (Fig. 1).

Data collection forms were completed for 41 of the 62 contacts (66.1%). Data collection forms were not completed when a resident did not respond to DRS to schedule a meeting (n = 5), and for unknown reasons/the DRS did not submit a form (n = 16). The median time spent per contact was 2 h (IQR 2–5 h) with a range from 5 min to over 40 h (Table 3). There was no clear relationship between trainee residency program, reason for contact, or outcome and time spent per contact. The most common reason for contact was related to resident evaluation (n = 10, 24.4%) and listening and support were the most common outcomes (n = 27, 65.9%). Other reasons for contact included racism, physical assault, sexual harassment, and mental health crises.

Efficacy - outcomes measures

There were 33 residents who were eligible to complete a follow-up survey, of whom 13 submitted a response to either question (39.4% response rate). The median rating of usefulness of the program was 74 (interquartile range [IQR] 1-100, with higher scores suggesting greater usefulness) (Fig. 2). Ten participants contributed free text comments about their experience with the DRS program. Of the four generally positive comments, three mentioned that the DRS physicians had “validated” or “took seriously” their concerns. Respondents appreciated how the DRS program provided guidance as to “potential further actions” and assisted them in “finding a resolution I was happy with.” Among the six constructive comments, three consisted of concerns about “no follow-up whatsoever” after an initial meeting with a DRS physician. Constructive comments were generally related to concerns “not being taken seriously” or that “I would not be sufficiently support in any further actions.” One respondent was not satisfied with the resolution suggested by the DRS physician and disengaged from the DRS program and two respondents felt that their assigned DRS physician was not a good fit based on lived experience (e.g., shared caregiving responsibilities and race).

Discussion

This evaluation of the DRS program suggests that the service has been well-utilized by residents and has been supportive to residents at CSM across a variety of issues. Our report provides PGME offices seeking to create similar resident support programs with a sense of the resources required for such a program. For example, based on our findings, programs could anticipate that about 3% of their trainees may access their service per year and that contacts may be more frequent in the middle of the training year. The amount of time required per contact is highly variable, though is typically about 2 h. Due to this variability, a greater number of faculty with less allocated FTE may be considered for future programs, such that faculty who receive time-intense issues do not become overburdened with additional contacts. Having a greater number of faculty may also address resident requests for greater diversity of available support people with respect to caregiving, race, and gender identity. If feasible, programs could consider offering the option for residents to specify preferences to be linked to a support person with specific lived experiences.

Physicians in a resident support role should receive training in empathetic listening and providing near-peer support, as these were the most common forms of support. Skills in receiving and managing disclosures of discrimination and harassment are also essential, as these issues were not uncommon among residents who contacted our program. Further, resident support programs should have a formal process to ensure that residents feel that their issues have been sufficiently addressed, as this was a common critique of our program and an area in which we aim to improve upon.

Conclusion

Our evaluation process provides helpful data to guide continual program improvement and to inform the development of support programs in other settings. Limitations to our program evaluation include a low survey response rates from residents. This may be in part due to the delay between DRS contact and the survey request (6 to 12 months); this time delay was intended to protect participant anonymity, though a shorter interval between DRS contact and survey invitation while aggregating multiple responses may increase the response rate while preserving anonymity. Further, there was a lack of resident involvement in program design, which may have contributed to lower ratings by some residents. We also did not seek feedback on program efficacy from DRS supporters, administrators, and PGME leadership, which may have provided additional insights on ways in which the program might be improved. At minimum, we suggest that PGME offices that create similar programs track program usage and resource needs and consider soliciting resident feedback to iteratively improve their services.

Altogether, effective approaches to resident support likely need to be multifaceted. A near-peer support model involving physicians as sources of confidential support and guidance for residents across a range of issues has demonstrated promise in our local context.
