Assessing the consistency of mass spectrometry, a clinical-laboratory model, and immunohistochemistry in amyloid subtyping: a Brazilian experience
Authors: Roberta Shcolnik Szor, Jussara Bianchi Castelli, Rodrigo Andrade Schuch, Valdemir Melechco Carvalho, Vanderson Rocha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic amyloidosis is a potentially fatal protein misfolding disorder usually underdiagnosed in low- and middle-income countries, where limited awareness and restricted access to diagnostic tools contribute to prolonged diagnostic journeys and delayed diagnoses. Accurate identification of the precursor protein is essential but remains a challenge, particularly in resource-limited settings. This study aimed to perform mass spectrometry (MS) for amyloid subtyping and to use it as the reference method to evaluate the consistency of a clinical-laboratory model (CLM) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) in determining the amyloid subtype.MethodsIn this retrospective, observational, single-center study, MS was performed on tissue biopsies from patients diagnosed with systemic amyloidosis between 2009 and 2018 at a public university hospital in Brazil. An IHC panel of four antibodies (anti-kappa, -lambda, -serum amyloid A, -transthyretin) was performed on samples with sufficient material. Review of medical records assessed the amyloid subtype determined by the clinical-laboratory model (CLM), which was based on clinical presentation, laboratory and imaging data, genetic testing, and pathological findings available at the time of the initial diagnosis.ResultsFrom 127 patients, 48 were excluded due to unavailable biopsies or insufficient material for MS analysis. The final cohort consisted of 79 patients, 61% male, with a median age of 61 years. Biopsies from 13 different tissues were analyzed by MS, revealing the following amyloid subtypes: AL (56%), ATTR (25%), AA (6%), AFib (3%), AH (1%). Seven cases (9%) remained inconclusive. IHC correctly subtyped amyloid in 28% of cases but failed in 66%. In 80% of patients the CLM correctly identified the amyloid subtype. However, it generated incorrect typing leading to inappropriate treatments.ConclusionThe consistency analysis between the CLM, IHC and MS demonstrated the superiority of MS in amyloid subtyping from tissue biopsies. While the CLM failed in 20% of cases and resulted in inappropriate treatments due to false-positive results, IHC showed very limited diagnostic performance, contrasting with results from reference centers, with less than one-third of cases correctly classified. These findings reinforce the role of MS as a more accurate and cost-competitive method for amyloid subtyping in middle-income countries.

Introduction

Systemic amyloidosis arises from protein misfolding disorders as a result of the deposition of structured and well organized insoluble amyloid fibrils in the extracellular space of tissues. Clinically, it manifests as progressive organ dysfunction, with the potential for fatal outcomes [1, 2].

To date, there are 42 proteins described as amyloidogenic, and at least 18 are associated with systemic disease. Subtyping amyloid is an essential step in the diagnostic process of amyloidosis, as the precise characterization of the precursor protein is mandatory to correctly establish target-treatment [3].

Given the lack of awareness of amyloidosis and its non-specific manifestations, the disease is often underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed as other conditions. Patients usually experience a long journey from the onset of symptoms, including numerous visits to different specialties and diagnostic procedures. These factors lead to delayed diagnoses occurring in advanced stages of the disease, with poor outcomes. Especially in patients with cardiac involvement [1, 2, 4].

In low and middle-income countries, this scenario is further aggravated by the scarcity of referral centers involved in the care of patients with amyloidosis and the limited access to specific diagnostic modalities for subtyping amyloid. Together, these factors may negatively impact the patient’s journey and further challenge early diagnosis and treatment, contributing to poor outcomes [5].

Besides the combination of clinical presentation, imaging, genetics and laboratory data, various laboratory methods aim to determine the amyloid subtype, but numerous technical limitations hinder their diagnostic performance outside reference centers. As the accurate identification of the amyloid subtype directly impacts treatment planning, it remains a critical aspect of patient care and a challenge in real-world clinical practicing [6–9]. Recent advances in protein research, including proteomic analysis and bioinformatics, have enabled the integration of mass spectrometry (MS) as a more precise method for characterizing amyloid fibrils. Due to its high diagnostic accuracy in determining amyloid subtypes, MS is considered the gold standard by some institutions, whereas other reference centers rely on immune-based techniques such as immunohistochemistry (IHC) or immunogold immunoelectron microscopy [10–15, 24, 25].

In recent years, there has been a global effort to establish amyloidosis referral centers with the goal of increasing disease awareness and shortening the time to definitive diagnosis. In Brazil, a continental country marked by significant regional and social disparities, several amyloidosis centers have emerged, particularly in São Paulo. However, there is still a lack of standardization in diagnostic methods for identifying the precursor protein. In many cases, clinicians rely on a combination of clinical, laboratory, and pathological data, often supported by incomplete immunohistochemical panels composed of commercial antibodies, to define the amyloid subtype. Since 2019, MS has become available at a private laboratory in Brazil. With support from a pharmaceutical company, access to this technique has expanded, improving diagnostic accuracy across the country [16, 17]. Considering the ongoing challenges in accurately subtyping amyloid in Brazil, it is essential to assess the consistency of the available diagnostic methods in comparison with MS. This study aimed to perform MS for amyloid subtyping and to use it as the reference method to evaluate the consistency of a clinical-laboratory model (CLM) and IHC in determining the amyloid subtype.

Methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a single-center, observational, retrospective cohort study at the Hospital das Clínicas da Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de São Paulo, a public university health service in Brazil. The study was approved by our local Institutional Research Ethics Committee. Biopsies of patients with histological-proven systemic amyloidosis diagnosed between 2009 and 2018 were analyzed by MS. A certified pathologist reviewed the diagnoses of amyloidosis by light microscopy and polarization after Congo red staining. The proteomic composition of amyloid deposits was assessed utilizing the methodologies outlined below. An IHC panel of four antibodies was also performed on samples with sufficient material. Previous diagnoses and medical records were reviewed to determine the amyloid subtype using the CLM, which was based on clinical presentation, presence of monoclonal protein, pyrophosphate scintigraphy, genetic mutations and previous pathological findings.

Diagnostic methods

Sections of 10-µm-thick were obtained from paraffin-embedded tissue samples and stained with Congo red. Amyloid deposit areas were extracted by laser microdissection, with an area of 5,000,000 µm2 for subcutaneous fat and 750,000 µm2 for other tissues. The resulting tissue fragments underwent solubilization and protein denaturation by heat and sonication, followed by trypsin digestion. Liquid chromatography separation followed by tandem MS detection of the resulting peptides was performed on a NanoUltimate coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X hybrid quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo-Fisher) operating at positive nanospray ionization and at data dependent acquisition mode. Protein identification was conducted through bioinformatics pipeline combining the MaxQuant (Version 4.2.1) and FragPipe (Version 13.0) processing platforms against UniProt/SwissProt human reviewed canonical database. Proteins were considered for clinical interpretation if they had at least one confidently identified unique peptide (probability > 0.9) and more than five spectral counts.

In samples with a sufficient amount of material, an immunohistochemical panel was performed using four commercial antibodies targeting the following proteins: kappa and lambda light chains, transthyretin, and serum amyloid A. Immunohistochemical results were classified as positive (positive reaction to a single antibody), negative (no positive reaction) or inconclusive (reaction to two or more antibodies).

The CLM determined the amyloid subtype based on the combination of the following parameters available at the time of diagnosis: clinical presentation and organ involvement according to established criteria by amyloidosis experts; screening for monoclonal protein through serum and urine immunofixation and serum free light chain assays; pathology techniques available at the time of diagnosis, including immunohistochemistry or indirect immunofluorescence; identification of a pathogenic genetic mutation associated with hereditary amyloidosis in conjunction with a compatible clinical presentation; and, in cases of cardiac involvement, diagnosis of transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis based on grade 2 or 3 uptake on technetium-pyrophosphate scintigraphy in the absence of a monoclonal gammopathy, according to the non-invasive algorithm. In cases that did not meet these criteria, the subtype was determined based on available tests, clinical presentation, and medical judgement.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Jupyter Notebook, TableOne, and Lifelines packages for Python version 3.9.13. Categorical variables were described using relative and absolute frequencies, and continuous variables using measures of central tendency or interquartile ranges for continuous variables, with a 95% confidence interval. Sensitivity and specificity of the CLM and IHC were calculated using mass spectrometry as the reference method.

Results

Of the 127 patients diagnosed with systemic amyloidosis in our database from 2009 to 2018, 48 cases were excluded: 17 due to biopsies performed at external centers before referral to our service, 8 due to unavailable samples at laboratories, and 7 because the patients were identified after the study had been planned. Among the remaining 95 cases, 14 had insufficient paraffin-embedded tissue samples for laser microdissection, and 2 did not meet the minimum required area for MS analysis. Finally, 79 biopsies underwent MS analysis, and IHC was performed on 47.

MS identified five subtypes of amyloidosis in our cohort. The most common was light chain (AL) amyloidosis (56%, n = 44), followed by ATTR amyloidosis (25%, n = 20), serum amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis (6%, n = 5), fibrinogen (AFib) amyloidosis (3%, n = 2), and heavy chain (AH) amyloidosis (1%, n = 1). In 7 cases (9%), the subtype could not be determined: 4 cases in which amyloid-signature proteins were not detected, 1 case in which amyloid-signature proteins were identified but no known precursor proteins were observed, 1 case with fibrinogen detection in a scattered pattern, and 1 case where both fibrinogen and transthyretin were simultaneously detected.

Additionally, 68% had a performance status of ≥ 2 according to the ECOG scale. Advanced disease, defined as the involvement of ≥ 3 organs, was present in 37% of patients. Using proteomic analysis as the reference method, the CLM accurately identified the amyloid subtype in 80% of the patients. In AL amyloidosis cases, the CLM demonstrated a sensitivity of 93% and a specificity of 71%. However, three cases remained without a definitive diagnosis. Within the ATTR amyloidosis subgroup, the CLM showed a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 100%. Notably, three patients were misdiagnosed with AL amyloidosis, leading to treatment with anti-plasma cell agents in one case; the other three cases remained inconclusive. The patient with AH amyloidosis was misclassified as having the AL subtype by the CLM. Conversely, all patients with AA and AFib subtypes were correctly identified, with both sensitivity and specificity reaching 100%.

Of the 47 cases analyzed by IHC, a specific amyloidosis subtype was suggested in 16 cases. Among these, the precursor protein was correctly classified in 13 cases, representing 28% of all cases analyzed with this method. For the 30 AL amyloidosis cases, IHC demonstrated a sensitivity of 10% and a specificity of 90%, with 14 (47%) false-negative results, 10 (33%) inconclusive, and 3 (10%) false-positive results, where AL was misclassified as AA (n = 1) or ATTR (n = 2). Among the 11 ATTR amyloidosis cases, IHC showed a sensitivity of 73% and a specificity of 94%. However, 3 cases (27%) remained inconclusive. For AA amyloidosis, IHC had a sensitivity of 67% and a specificity of 98%. The single AFib case was not identified due to the absence of a targeted antibody, although no reaction was observed with any of the four antibodies in the panel. Discussion

Subtyping amyloid is a critical step in diagnosing patients with amyloidosis, as the precise identification of the precursor protein is essential for determining the appropriate treatment. Mass spectrometry, by directly analyzing proteins in tissue samples, improves the accuracy of amyloid fibril characterization and is considered by some reference centers as the most reliable and the gold standard method for identifying the precursor amyloidogenic protein. Additionally, the detection of amyloid-associated proteins, which stabilize fibrils and are consistently present across all amyloid types, serves as a quality control measure, supporting the diagnosis of amyloidosis, particularly in challenging cases [10–15]. However, MS is not widely available worldwide, especially in low and middle-income countries, primarily due to its complexity, the requirement for advanced technological infrastructure, and the need of highly specialized professionals. In addition to technical challenges, financial constraints also limit the widespread clinical adoption of MS as a routine tool for amyloidosis subtyping [6, 23].

In Brazil, since the implementation of MS in 2019, the test has not been covered by health insurance. Access has been made possible through a collaborative program supported by a pharmaceutical company, thereby benefiting a larger number of patients. This is the first study to report the use of MS for amyloid typing in a Latin American country and to compares its performance with other diagnostic methods for amyloid typing.

In our study, MS proved to be a viable method for analyzing paraffin-embedded biopsy samples stored for varying periods and subjected to laser microdissection between 2.1 and 14.5 years after collection. Moreover, the technique was successfully applied to thirteen different tissue types, demonstrating its versatility. These findings reinforce the feasibility of performing proteomic analysis in non-fresh samples from various anatomical sites, allowing laboratories without local access to MS to send specimens to reference centers for amyloidosis diagnosis. Similarly, Dasari et al. reported that, among 16,175 Congo red-positive tissue samples analyzed by MS, 15,308 were formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded biopsies [15].

Although the clinical-laboratory model correctly identified 80% of the amyloidosis subtypes, its diagnostic performance is not sufficient to justify its use in clinical practice. As an example, three cases of ATTR were initially misdiagnosed as AL, leading to one patient receiving inappropriate treatment with chemotherapeutic agents. This underscores the critical role of proteomic analysis in accurately subtyping amyloid and highlights the importance of establishing at least one reference center per region worldwide equipped with this technique.

In our study, IHC demonstrated poor diagnostic performance, correctly identifying the amyloidosis subtype in less than one-third of cases. The low positivity rate was particularly evident among patients with AL amyloidosis: the method correctly detected only 10% of cases, yielded false-negative results in nearly half, remained inconclusive in one-third, and produced false-positive results in 10%. IHC also failed to determine the subtype in approximately one-third of ATTR and AA cases. In the AFib case, the precursor protein could not be identified due to the absence of a specific antibody for this subtype in the panel.

The limited diagnostic performance of IHC observed in our cohort markedly differs from the results reported by reference centers. In a collaborative study by the Mayo Clinic and the United Kingdom’s National Amyloidosis Centre, Gilbertson et al. assessed the performance of IHC compared to MS for amyloid subtyping. By analyzing 142 biopsy specimens from 38 different tissue types, IHC correctly identified the amyloid subtype in 76% of cases, whereas proteomic analysis increased diagnostic accuracy to 94%, proving especially useful in cases with inconclusive immunohistochemical results [22]. In another real-world study, Rezk et al. demonstrated that MS identified the amyloid subtype in 80% of 320 biopsies that were inconclusive by IHC. In a subgroup with previously defined subtypes, IHC showed an accuracy of 91%. Overall, MS correctly determined the amyloid subtype in 85% of the 640 samples analyzed, underscoring its value as a complementary method when the immunological approach is inconclusive [29].

The poor performance of IHC in our study may be attributed to its known susceptibility to false-positive and false-negative results in antigen-antibody-based reactions, particularly when performed outside of specialized centers. Factors such as nonspecific background staining due to cross-reactivity with tissue proteins, or the masking of epitopes within amyloid fibrils, may lead to erroneous results. Additionally, immunohistochemical techniques rely on the availability of specific antibodies for each known amyloid subtype and do not allow for the identification of novel or rare amyloidogenic proteins. In reference laboratories, these limitations are often mitigated by the use of extended antibody panels, including both commercial and in-house antibodies, and by advanced methods such as immunogold labeling combined with immunoelectron microscopy, which enhance diagnostic precision. Unfortunately, such sophisticated techniques are not feasible in non-specialized settings. In our study, the exclusive use of commercial antibodies on long-stored tissue specimens likely contributed to the limited diagnostic performance of IHC [6, 8, 15, 19–22, 24, 25].

In middle-income countries, another important factor affecting the implementation of the most appropriate diagnostic method is cost. Considering that IHC requires a panel of multiple antibodies to cover the most common amyloid subtypes, and that MS has an average cost of USD 300, both methods may be considered cost-competitive in these settings.

In our cohort, there was a significant delay in the diagnosis of amyloidosis, highlighting the diagnostic challenges in Brazil. This is evidenced by a late diagnosis occurring approximately one year after symptom onset, often at an advanced stage of the disease and following an average of two biopsies per patient. Our findings also indicate that target-organ biopsies remain commonly used in clinical practice.

AL amyloidosis was the most frequent subtype, followed by ATTR, AA, and rarer hereditary forms such as AFib. The distribution of subtypes aligns with international data, including Ravichandran et al.‘s study of over 11,000 patients in the UK, where AL was most common (55%), followed by ATTR (21%) and AA (8%). Their data showed a decline in AA and a rise in ATTRwt over time. These changes reflect advances in treatment, greater awareness, and non-invasive diagnostic tools. In contrast, our study found fewer ATTR cases, likely due to biopsy-based inclusion criteria and evolving diagnostic practices [26].

The distribution of amyloidosis subtypes reflected the organ tropism of their precursor proteins. AL amyloidosis was found in nearly all tissues, predominantly in kidneys and heart, and also in peripheral sites like fat and bone marrow. ATTR was most common in cardiac and nerve biopsies but also detected in fat, salivary gland, and respiratory tract. These findings support the use of peripheral biopsies in selected cases [1, 2, 27, 28].

As limitations of our study, the retrospective design led to the exclusion of cases due to unavailable or poor-quality paraffin-fixed biopsies, preventing MS analysis in 38% of initially identified cases. Additionally, missing clinical and laboratory data may have impacted some analyses. Another limitation was the inability of MS to determine the amyloid subtype in 9% of cases, though this did not appear to be related to sample storage duration, as it occurred in samples stored from 3.6 to 10.6 years.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated the low diagnostic performance of the CLM and IHC in determining the amyloid subtype in Brazilian patients with systemic amyloidosis, as well as the superiority of MS over both methods. Our findings highlight the limitations of IHC outside reference laboratories and the challenges in replicating the diagnostic accuracy reported by specialized centers, reinforcing the role of MS as a more accurate and potentially cost-competitive method for amyloid subtyping in middle-income countries. Nevertheless, our results also underscore the limitations of MS, as the subtype could not be determined in 9% of cases, emphasizing the importance of precise precursor protein identification to reduce misdiagnosis and prevent patient harm from inappropriate therapies.
