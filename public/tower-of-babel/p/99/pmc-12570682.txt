Role of transforming growth factorβ in neurovascular unit during cerebral small vessel disease
Authors: Yangjie Li, Ying Cai, Kangling Xie, Fan Hu, Jiaohao Li, Cui Li, Runjie Zhang, Zhengwei Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) encompasses diffuse brain lesions arising from structural injury to small vessels, and is closely associated with chronic hypoperfusion and blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction. Its insidious onset and heterogeneous clinical manifestations render elucidation of its pathogenesis and development of targeted interventions of paramount clinical importance. Transforming growth factorβ (TGFβ), a pivotal regulator of vascular homeostasis, exerts bidirectional effects within the neurovascular unit (NVU) during CSVD: under physiological conditions, TGFβ maintains barrier integrity by modulating endothelial tight junction proteins and pericyte adhesion; under pathological stress, dysregulated TGFβ signaling induces endothelial dysfunction, pericyte degeneration and neuroinflammation, thereby promoting white-matter injury. Precise, spatiotemporal modulation of TGFβ pathways therefore represents a promising avenue for stage-specific, molecularly targeted therapy in CSVD.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) represents a progressive, pervasive vascular pathology affecting arterioles, capillaries, and venules throughout the brain [1], and underlies a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations that evolve over time. While early CSVD may be clinically silent or present as mild symptoms [2], including transient ischemic attacks (TIA), subtle gait disturbances [3], or minor cognitive dysfunction [4]—its progression frequently culminates in overt ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke [5], as well as significant neuropsychiatric features such as apathy and executive dysfunction [6]. Importantly, CSVD constitutes one of the principal vascular contributors to cognitive decline and dementia [7], and accounts for a substantial proportion of vascular cognitive impairment in older populations [8], highlighting its clinical and public health significance.

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is essential for preserving microvascular integrity and normal brain function. It, together with pericytes, astrocytes, neurons and microglia, comprises the neurovascular unit (NVU) [9, 10]. White-matter hyperintensities on MRI, the diagnostic hallmark of CSVD, reflect NVU disruption. Any insult compromising NVU structure or function can precipitate CSVD [11–13]. Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, endothelial dysfunction, BBB breakdown and genetic factors constitute principal pathogenic drivers [14, 15], with hypertension and hyperglycemia further accelerating disease progression [16]. Persistent low perfusion correlates strongly with BBB disruption and white-matter lesions [17], heightening the risk of post-stroke dementia and disability [18].

Although the role of Transforming growth factorβ (TGFβ) in vascular development and maturation is well-recognized, its specific functions in CSVD remain incompletely characterized [19, 20]. Emerging evidence reveals a temporal dichotomy: pre-pathologically, TGFβ reinforces BBB integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins and modulating glial phenotypes [21–23]. Under pathological conditions, aberrant TGFβ signaling downregulates junctional proteins, induces pericyte loss and endothelial apoptosis, and triggers neuroinflammation and edema [17]. Progressive microvascular damage promotes glial scarring [24, 25], underpinned by extracellular matrix remodeling and basement membrane thickening [26], which form the substrate for white-matter hyperintensities [23, 27]. This biphasic regulatory behavior of TGFβ not only provides a theoretical basis for targeted intervention but also highlights the necessity of distinguishing disease stages, cellular subtypes, and signaling pathways. In this review, we dissect the molecular mechanisms by which TGFβ orchestrates protective versus deleterious processes within the NVU, with the aim of identifying novel TGFβ–related drug targets for CSVD therapy.

TGFβ as a potential molecular target in cerebral small vessel disease

TGFβ orchestrates vascular development, homeostasis, and angiogenesis via both SMADdependent and independent pathways, modulating endothelial permeability, proliferation, migration, extracellular matrix synthesis, and vessel remodeling [28]. In CSVD, TGFβ assumes a paradoxical, bidirectional role serving as a “physiological guardian” that preserves vascular equilibrium, yet acting as a “silent driver” of pathology when dysregulated [19]. Upon ligand binding to type II TGFβ receptors, receptor phosphorylation activates type I receptors (ALK5 or ALK1), which relay signals through SMAD proteins: ALK5 preferentially phosphorylates SMAD2/3, whereas ALK1 targets SMAD1/5.

Under physiological conditions, endogenous TGFβ1 promotes angiogenesis and vascular maturation while maintaining glial morphology and function [29, 30]. By contrast, pathological overexpression of TGFβ ligands and aberrant activation of downstream effectors provoke oxidative stress and maladaptive responses, leading to tight junction disruption, increased BBB permeability, white matter hyperintensities (WMH), and cognitive decline in CSVD [31–33]. Epigenetic regulators such as NOTCH3 and HtrA1 further modulate TGFβ signaling at multiple disease stages [34–36].

Transgenic mouse models overexpressing TGFβ1 provide compelling evidence for its central role in CSVD: these animals develop key disease features, including WMH, extracellular matrix or amyloid deposition, microvascular fibrosis, impaired cerebrovascular reactivity, and cognitive deficits [37, 38].

Intriguingly, although TGFβ drives endothelial proliferation and neovascularization, it can also transiently increase BBB permeability. This apparent paradox reflects the requirement for brief permeability “windows” during angiogenic sprouting, followed by rapid barrier restoration, an effect for which TGFβ signaling is uniquely suited [39]. This context‑dependent modulation underscores the “double‑edged sword” nature of TGFβ in CSVD and provides a molecular basis for its complex, stage‑specific contributions to disease pathogenesis.

Effects of TGFβ on intercellular junction proteins in endothelial cells during CSVD progression

Tight junctions

Tight junctions (TJs), chiefly composed of occludin and claudins, are anchored to the cytoskeleton via zonula occludens (ZO) scaffolding proteins [40]. Claudin‑5 and occludin constitute the principal barrier against solute and ion flux across the BBB in CSVD [10].

TGFβ signaling exerts bidirectional, context‑dependent modulation of TJ integrity. Under physiological conditions, ALK1/SMAD1/5 activation upregulates ZO‑1, occludin, and claudin‑5 in endothelial cells while concurrently inhibiting RhoA/ROCK–mediated cytoskeletal tension to preserve BBB integrity [41, 42].

In contrast, during inflammation or hypoxia, TGFβ1 engages ALK5 to phosphorylate SMAD3, driving matrix metalloproteinase‑9 (MMP‑9) expression and claudin‑5 downregulation [33, 43]. This shift precipitates extensive NVU remodeling tight junction protein degradation, release of proinflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules, and endothelial apoptosis—thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation in CSVD [43–46].

Moreover, SMAD2/3 activation promotes ubiquitin‑dependent occludin degradation [47], and AXL receptor engagement accelerates proteasomal clearance of claudin‑5 [48]. Excessive TGFβ also antagonizes Wnt/β‑catenin signaling, inhibiting β‑catenin nuclear translocation and further suppressing claudin‑5 expression. This destructive process is amplified by oxidative stress–induced Nrf2 pathway inhibition [41, 48].

Although direct evidence for MMP‑3 in CSVD is limited, emerging studies implicate inflammatory upregulation of MMP‑3 in basement membrane collagen degradation, TJ disruption, and increased BBB permeability. The interplay between TGFβ1/SMAD2/7 signaling and MMP‑3 expression merits further investigation [49–51].

Adherens junctions and gap junctions

Cells within the NVU form a dynamic signaling network through adherens junctions (AJs) and gap junctions (GJs), continuously monitoring neuronal metabolic demands. TGFβ regulates both AJ and GJ function via multiple pathways, with vascular endothelial (VE)‑cadherin and connexin‑43 (Cx43) as principal effectors.

VE-Cadherin

During early CSVD, TGFβ signals through ALK5/Smad2/3 to upregulate VE‑cadherin expression, while BMP9‑activated ALK1/Smad1/5 enhances its membrane stability, together forming a “protective network” that limits endothelial migration and curtails BBB leakage [52, 53]. In later, inflammation‑driven stages, reactive oxygen species (ROS) induce VE‑cadherin phosphorylation at Tyr685, triggering its endocytosis and degradation and shifting the balance toward barrier disruption [54]. VE‑cadherin is also mechanosensitive: TGFβ–mediated modulation of ZO‑1 tension indirectly alters VE‑cadherin stability [55]. Intriguingly, in vitro BBB models treated with TGFβ inhibitors exhibit increased VE‑cadherin levels and tighter junction formation. This appears paradoxical to the early-stage enhancing effects of TGFβ on VE-cadherin likely due to concurrent MMP‑9 downregulation and enhanced collagen scaffold preservation underscoring a complex, stage‑dependent “TGFβ–MMP‑9–VE‑cadherin” axis whose temporal impact on barrier integrity warrants further study [56, 57].

Moreover, TGFβ2 can enhance the expression and signaling of glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) receptors in neurons. GDNF, in turn, reinforces BBB integrity by upregulating claudin-5 and VE-cadherin through activation of the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways [58, 59]. Dysregulation of this co-regulatory system may contribute to BBB disruption in CSVD pathogenesis.

Connexin-43

Cx43‑containing GJs, enriched in astrocytic endfeet [60], mediate intercellular ion and small‑molecule exchange and serve as biomarkers of cerebrovascular injury and cognitive decline in CSVD [61, 62]. C×43and TGFβ engage in bidirectional regulation, jointly modulating vascular stability and fibrosis during CSVD. In the early stages, open C×43channels between endothelial cells permit calcium ion flux, activating Smad signaling and triggering TGFβ secretion, which promotes pericyte differentiation into mural cells and enhances vascular structural stability [63]. TGFβ reciprocally upregulates C×43via ALK5/Smad2/3 in response to mechanical stress, establishing a positive feedback loop that sustains endothelial–mesenchymal communication [64]. However, in the late inflammatory phase, TGFβ–induced MMP‑9 degrades basement membrane collagen, disrupts C×43anchoring, and exacerbates BBB leakage [64]. Moreover, C×43gene polymorphisms (GJA1 SNPs) correlate with CSVD lesion distribution, implicating dysregulated C×43expression in disease progression via TGFβ pathway perturbation [65].

Together, these findings highlight a bidirectional, context‑dependent interplay between TGFβ signaling and NVU junctional complexes, offering multiple targets for therapeutic intervention in CSVD. TGFβ signaling network in endothelial cells

Structural alterations in cerebral vascular endothelial cells are recognized as one of the earliest events leading to BBB leakage in CSVD. Endothelial injury not only compromises NVU integrity but also contributes to sustained cerebral dysfunction, manifesting as cognitive impairment, behavioral disturbances, and gait instability in CSVD patients [15]. TGFβ signaling lies at the heart of endothelial regulation, orchestrating angiogenesis, endothelial maturation, and inflammatory responses that drive CSVD pathogenesis.

In endothelial cells, TGFβ mediates both neovascularization and vessel stabilization through heteromeric receptor complexes involving ALK1/Smad1/5 and ALK5/Smad2/3 pathways [66]. Activation of the ALK1/Smad1/5 axis via Id1‑induced Smad1/5 phosphorylation promotes endothelial migration and proliferation to facilitate angiogenesis. In contrast, the ALK5/Smad2/3 pathway induces plasminogen activator inhibitor‑1 (PAI‑1) expression, fostering endothelial maturation, and upregulates Gadd45b transcription, enhancing endothelial plasticity and supporting neuronal repair [66, 67]. Endoglin, an endothelial co‑receptor, fine‑tunes this balance by inhibiting ALK1/Smad1 signaling while augmenting ALK5/Smad2/3 activation, thereby maintaining the equilibrium between endothelial proliferation and differentiation [68]. During CSVD, Endoglin deficiency disrupts this equilibrium, resulting in aberrant Smad1/5 activation, pathological microvascular proliferation, and BBB breakdown. This imbalance in receptor subtype activity correlates closely with NVU destabilization and highlights selective modulation of TGFβ receptors as a promising strategy to restore cerebrovascular architecture and function.

Additional evidence underscores the context‑dependent duality of TGFβ1 in endothelial biology. In the acute phase of cerebral ischemia, downregulation of TGFβR2 and Smad2/3 in murine micro-vessels exacerbates microvascular hemorrhage; conversely, exogenous monocyte chemoattractant protein‑1 (MCP‑1) can leverage TGFβ signaling to enhance pericyte‑to‑endothelial migration and stabilize nascent vessels [30, 69]. In later stages, excessive TGFβ1 activation drives endothelial apoptosis and aberrant endothelial‑to‑mesenchymal transition (EndMT), and may impair vascular repair through JAK kinase degradation [56, 70]. Especially, TGFβ mediated EndMT induces endothelial phenotypic changes, leading to extracellular matrix deposition, inflammatory activation, and loss of BBB integrity [71]. In the context of CSVD, EndMT contributes to white matter injury and cognitive decline by modulating endothelial junctional proteins and increasing vascular stiffness [72]. Importantly, EndMT is not a uniform process: it may involve not only downregulation but also upregulation of junctional proteins in specific contexts [73, 74]. Sustained activation further amplifies neuroinflammation and vascular rarefaction, thereby driving disease progression [75]. These findings underscore the necessity for precise, spatiotemporal control of TGFβ expression when designing therapeutic interventions for CSVD.

Endothelial inflammation represents a key driver of BBB disruption in CSVD, with TGFβ signaling emerging as a central mediator of vascular inflammation [76]. TGFβ1 enhances oxidative stress and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering the release of IL-1β, IL-18, and MMP-9, which collectively impair NVU function [77, 78]. Under homeostatic conditions, TGFβ attenuates IL-6–induced alkaline phosphatase (ALP) expression to stabilize the BBB. However, in the inflammatory milieu of CSVD, excessive IL-6 signaling perturbs TGFβ activity, elevating ALP, driving pathological angiogenesis, increasing BBB permeability, and exacerbating perivascular gliosis and fibrosis [79]. In brain microvascular endothelial cells, β-1,3-galactosyltransferase 2 (B3galt2) has been shown to counteract pro-inflammatory mediators (NF-κB, IL-6, TNF-α) via TGFβ1–dependent pathways and to inhibit NLRP3 activation, thereby conferring neurovascular protection after ischemic injury [80, 81].

Genetic perturbations in endothelial cells further contribute to CSVD. Mutations in CADASIL-related genes (NOTCH3, HtrA1) and type IV collagen genes (COL4A1, COL4A2) disrupt TGFβ signaling, leading to endothelial apoptosis, vascular fibrosis, inflammation, and BBB transport failure molecular underpinnings of CSVDs genetic heterogeneity [35, 82, 83]. NOTCH3 mutations, for example, sequester latent TGFβ binding protein-1 (LTBP-1) into pathogenic aggregates, altering TGFβ bioavailability and aberrantly activating Smad2/3 signaling [36]. Conversely, reducing TGFβ expression ameliorates disease severity in Col4a1-mutant mouse models of CSVD [84, 85]. Importantly, mutations in ALK1 or Endoglin cause hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) [86], which predisposes to brain arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) and subsequent intracerebral hemorrhage [87]. Although AVMs are rare and not classified as CSVD, their association with TGFβrelated signaling [88], highlighting the broader cerebrovascular relevance of ALK1/Endoglin pathways. Including such examples underscores how endothelial TGFβ dysregulation may predispose to diverse vascular pathologies, from small vessel degeneration to hemorrhagic malformations.

Collectively, TGFβ signaling exerts a dualistic influence in CSVD: it orchestrates endothelial proliferation, differentiation, and maturation to support vascular repair, yet under inflammatory conditions drives endothelial dysfunction and NVU breakdown. Moreover, crosstalk between genetic susceptibility factors and TGFβ pathways reveals a mechanistic basis for CSVDs heterogeneity. Elucidating these context-dependent roles of TGFβ will be critical for designing precise, stage-specific therapies to improve outcomes in CSVD patients.

TGFβ signaling network in pericytes

Pericytes, the mural cells embedded within microvascular basement membranes, are indispensable for cerebral blood flow regulation and BBB integrity within the NVU [89, 90]. In CSVD, chronic hypoperfusion resulting from pericyte loss perpetuates a vicious cycle: reduced blood flow induces further pericyte dropout, exacerbating microvascular damage and white matter injury [17, 91]. A principal driver of pericyte depletion is excessive TGFβ1 signaling [92]. TGFβ1–mediated Smad2/3 phosphorylation diminishes pericyte and vascular smooth muscle cell coverage, often accompanied by luminal dilation and microvascular hemorrhage [38, 93]. Pharmacological blockade of TGFβ receptors restores the myogenic response and promotes smooth muscle regeneration in Col4a1‑mutant mice, underscoring its therapeutic potential [94].

Remarkably, individual pericyte loss does not immediately compromise BBB integrity, thanks to pleiotrophin (PTN) secretion by remaining pericytes [95]. PTN enhances endothelial proliferation via TGFβ1–dependent pathways and upregulates tight junction proteins claudin‑1 and occludin, thereby stabilizing the barrier [96]. Mimicking PTNs effects may thus preserve compensatory BBB function during early pericyte loss, opening a therapeutic window before irreversible barrier breakdown.

As CSVD advances and pericyte depletion intensifies, serum constituents cross the endothelium via transcellular transport, activating the TGFβ/Smad2 axis. This activation triggers microglial recruitment and the release of IL‑6, MMP‑2, and NOX4, while pericyte phagocytic function declines, amplifying inflammation and white matter damage [17, 97].

Recent work has identified Foxf2, a pericyte‑specific transcription factor, as a pivotal upstream regulator of TGFβ signaling in CSVD. Foxf2 enhances Pdgfrβ expression and suppresses Smad1/5/p38 phosphorylation, thereby biasing signaling toward the TGFβ/Smad2/3 pathway. This shift upregulates TGFβ2, ALK5, and integrin αVβ8, reinforcing BBB integrity [98, 99]. Targeting the Foxf2–TGFβ2–integrin αVβ8 axis may reverse early microvascular leakage by modulating pericyte secretory activity.

Effects of TGFβ on endothelial–pericyte interactions

The crosstalk between endothelial cells and pericytes is pivotal in preserving microvascular stability, maintaining capillary tone, and sustaining the functional integrity of the NVU [100]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) encompasses several isoforms with distinct biological roles. VEGFA, often regarded as a marker of endothelial injury in CSVD [101], dynamically regulates vascular homeostasis through multidimensional signaling networks in concert with TGFβ during pericyte–endothelial cell interactions. By contrast, VEGFB is primarily linked to neuronal metabolism and survival [102], whereas VEGFC/D govern lymphangiogenesis [103]. Understanding these isoform-specific functions is essential to elucidate how dysregulated VEGF signaling leads to blood–brain barrier disruption and maladaptive vascular remodeling in CSVD.

Under physiological conditions, TGFβ enhances the expression of contractile markers such as α-SMA in pericytes via the Smad3 signaling pathway [104, 105], thereby supporting vascular tone regulation and suppressing aberrant endothelial proliferation through the upregulation of connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [106, 107]. Meanwhile, VEGFA secreted by pericytes activates VEGFR‑2 and the PI3K/Akt pathway in endothelial cells, promoting cell survival and vessel stabilization [108]. This bidirectional communication is reinforced by the TGFR2–ALK5–Smad3 axis and integrin α2β1 signaling, which together maintain angiogenic quiescence and enable dynamic vascular remodeling as needed [106, 109, 110]. However, studies in Col4a1 mutant mouse models have demonstrated that sustained elevation of TGFβ signaling leads to abnormal expression of contractile proteins in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and age-related loss of arteriolar VSMCs [84]. In CSVD, excessive or dysregulated TGFβ signaling is closely associated with pericyte dropout and impaired vascular stability. This suggests that pericyte contractile plasticity and VSMC mediated pathological changes should be regarded as related but mechanistically distinct processes.

However, in CSVD, this finely tuned balance becomes dysregulated. Loss of integrin αVβ8 impairs the local activation of TGFβ and downregulates VE-cadherin, directly contributing to increased BBB permeability [39, 111–113]. At the same time, unrestrained TGFβ signaling shifts toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, elevating IL‑6 and TNF‑α levels [114]. Excessive VEGFA activation under these conditions further degrades the BBB, enhances leukocyte adhesion, and fosters microvascular damage. Dysfunctional pericyte contraction aggravates cerebral hypoperfusion, fueling a cascade of white matter injury and microvascular rarefaction [39, 104, 106].

Additionally, consistent with observations in astrocytes, pathologically activated endothelial cells upregulate angiopoietin-2 (ANGPT2), inducing integrin-dependent pericyte migration. This maladaptive response alters blood flow dynamics and promotes perivascular fibrosis and vascular remodeling [114]. The disrupted balance between TGFβ, VEGFA, and integrin signaling thus serves as a central mechanism underlying CSVD progression. Restoring homeostasis across this axis offers a promising therapeutic approach to stabilize endothelial–pericyte interactions and rebuild the compromised vascular barrier.

Beyond this core triad, TGFβ exerts additional regulatory effects that reinforce its role in endothelial–pericyte communication. In pericytes, TGFβ/ALK5 signaling recruits Smad3/4, TGIF1, and HDAC5 to form a transcriptional co-repressor complex at the ANGPT2 promoter, suppressing its expression [115]. This mechanism prevents abnormal endothelial proliferation and preserves vascular architecture, underscoring the context-dependent nature of TGFβ signaling. Although this function appears contradictory to TGFβ’s role in pericyte loss, it reflects the context-dependent nature of TGFβ signaling across different molecular pathways. TGFβ also modulates the ANGPT1/Tie2 pathway, a major contributor to vascular stability and BBB protection in CSVD [116, 117]. Following vascular injury, TGFβ/ALK1 signaling in endothelial cells upregulates platelet-derived growth factor-B (PDGF‑B), which binds to PDGFR‑β on pericytes and facilitates their targeted recruitment to damaged micro-vessels [118]. This coordinated response supports extracellular matrix remodeling, suppresses degradation, and promotes capillary repair [119].

In summary, TGFβ orchestrates a complex, temporally and spatially regulated signaling network between endothelial cells and pericytes that dictates vascular fate in CSVD. Future therapies must consider the spatiotemporal signaling dynamics associated with acute and chronic injury phases to optimize intervention windows. Therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring signaling balance especially across the integrin–TGFβ–VEGF axis hold strong potential to reverse microvascular injury, suppress inflammation, and restore BBB integrity.

TGFβ signaling network in Microglia

Microglia, the CNSs resident immune cells, adopt two primary activation states: the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype characterized by cytokine release, astrocytic endfoot phagocytosis, and BBB disruption and the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which preserves barrier integrity via tight junction protein expression (e.g. claudin5), physical interactions with endothelial cells, and secretion of anti-inflammatory mediators [120, 121].

TGFβ1 confers neuroprotection against acute inflammation by engaging TβRI and activating downstream p38 MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways in microglia [122]. In CSVD, TGFβ signaling critically governs microglial polarization dynamics [123]. Early in disease, TGFβ1/ALK5–Smad2/3 signaling drives M2 differentiation, while SMAD4mediated chromatin remodeling stabilizes the M2 state [124, 125]. These M2 microglia clear debris, suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNFα, IL1β, IL6), and amplify TGFβ release to reshape the inflammatory milieu [124, 126]. TGFβ also attenuates tPAinduced MMP expression, reducing hemorrhagic risk post-thrombolysis [127]. Neuronal inhibition of miR21 restores TGFβ activity, enhances M2 polarization, and dampens CCL2driven microglial infiltration through increased anti-inflammatory cytokine secretion [125].

In late-stage CSVD, TGFR3 activation augments TGFβ secretion and, in concert with CCL2/CCR2–STAT6/JAK3 signaling, recruits M1 microglia to perivascular sites [128, 129]. Although TGFR3 does not directly activate astrocytes, CCL2induced IL6 and TNFα from microglia promote astrocytic activation, compromise the BBB, and accelerate fibrotic scarring [130]. M1 microglia undergo morphological transformation [131] and secrete mediators that disrupt tight junctions, increase paracellular permeability, and facilitate leukocyte infiltration, driving a harmful “inflammation–repair–fibrosis” cycle that culminates in vascular stiffening and cognitive decline [132–134].

These mechanistic insights highlight novel therapeutic strategies: antagonizing CCL2 or selectively activating TGFR3 may restore microglial homeostasis and mitigate vascular cognitive impairment in CSVD.

TGFβ signaling network in astrocytes

Astrocytes, a principal glial cell population in the central nervous system, are acutely sensitive to microvascular injury in CSVD [135]. Their perivascular endfeet anchored by aquaporin4 (AQP4) are integral to NVU architecture and BBB maintenance [136–139]. These endfeet mediate neuron–vessel communication, support CSF–interstitial fluid exchange, and facilitate metabolic waste clearance via the glymphatic pathway [140]. Loss of endfeet disrupts glymphatic flow, downregulates AQP4 and IL10, upregulates TNFα, and exacerbates microvascular injury in CSVD [141, 142].

AQP4 dynamically redistributes and interacts with potassium channels and water transporters to regulate neuroglial metabolic coupling [143]. It also sustains TGFβ1 signaling, attenuating acute inflammation. AQP4 deficiency reduces cerebral TGFβ1 levels, triggers microglial activation, impairs the astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle (ANLS), and contributes to cognitive decline [144, 145]. Conversely, chronic TGFβ1 activation while damping acute inflammation can promote demyelination and lowgrade neuroinflammation, as demonstrated in multiple sclerosis models, and may similarly influence CSVD pathogenesis [146, 147]. These effects, though derived from models of multiple sclerosis, bear relevance to CSVD due to overlapping neuroinflammatory mechanisms and may inform therapeutic strategies. BBB breakdown and reactive gliosis in CSVD implicate AQP4/TGFβ1 axis dysregulation in perivascular inflammation and oligodendrocyte dysfunction. Targeting upstream modulators such as angiotensin II or directly modulating TGFβ1 activity may restore AQP4 function [148], enhance glymphatic clearance, and reestablish physiological blood–brain communication, offering a promising therapeutic avenue in CSVD.

Following CSVD onset, dysregulated astrocytic signaling and phenotypic shifts undermine the NVUs control over cerebrovascular tone and synaptic function [149]. Neuroinflammation drives astrocytes toward the neurotoxic A1 phenotype, which amplifies inflammatory cascades and promotes oligodendrocyte death and demyelination [150, 151]. Conversely, in early injury phases, A2 astrocytes are rapidly mobilized; these cells secrete TGFβ, upregulate neurotrophic and angiogenic factors, and support synaptic integrity [152]. TGFβ in turn reinforces A2 polarization, forming a positive feedback loop that enhances IL‑10 and IL‑13 secretion to facilitate BBB repair [153, 154]. In addition, astrocytic αvβ8 acts as a central regulator of cerebrovascular homeostasis by modulating TGFβ activation and promoting vascular differentiation, thereby stabilizing the expression of TGFβ responsive genes, most notably plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 and thrombospondin-1 [155].

Following small‑vessel ischemia, TGFβ signals to astrocytes, neurons, and microglia at lesion borders, and thrombospondin‑1–mediated TGFβ activation boosts neuronal Akt phosphorylation via PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways, conferring neuroprotection [156, 157]. Intriguingly, TGFβ also induces thrombospondin‑1 transcription in astrocytes, creating a self‑reinforcing circuit that preserves cerebrovascular integrity [158]. Brain‑specific miR‑21 further modulates astrocyte proliferation and secretory profiles by targeting TGFβ and activating PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling; its divergent effects in microglia likely reflect cell‑type–specific sensitivities [125, 159].

In mid-to-late CSVD, TGFβ shifts astrocytes toward the pro‑inflammatory A1 state. In vivo imaging shows that A1 activation drives glial scar formation [135], with repulsive guidance molecule A (RGMa) complexing with ALK5 and Smad2/3 to amplify TGFβ1/Smad2/3 signaling, inhibit neuronal recovery, and promote astrocyte proliferation, thus exacerbating parenchymal injury and cognitive decline [160]. Fibroblast growth factors (FGFs), especially FGF2, normally sustain non‑reactive astrocytes; FGF dysregulation facilitates A1 polarization and inflammatory cytokine release [161, 162]. TGFβ signaling network in astrocyte–microglia interactions

TGFβ orchestrates a complex, bidirectional regulatory network between astrocytes and microglia that is central to CSVD pathophysiology. Astrocytederived TGFβ, IL2, and CXCL10 induce IL10 production, which in turn upregulates astrocytic TGFβ receptors, forming a selfreinforcing antiinflammatory loop [164, 165]. Concurrently, TGFβ activates microglial PI3K/Akt signaling to drive M2 polarization [153], promoting synaptic remodeling and releasing TGFβ in a paracrine manner that further induces astrocytic A2 neuroprotective differentiation via the same pathway [166, 167].

Together, TGFβ and IL10 stabilize the neurovascular microenvironment by: Amplifying AntiInflammatory Feedback: IL10 enhances A2 astrocytic TGFβ secretion, which suppresses M1 microglial activation [168]. Suppressing Perivascular Inflammation: Both cytokines synergize to activate Smad3 and antagonize TNFα/NFκB signaling, preserving endothelial quiescence and barrier integrity [169, 170].

Under pathological conditions, loss of astrocytic Mlc1 expression leads to aberrant TGFβ activation, resulting in endfoot disorganization, adherens junction breakdown, and BBB compromise [171, 172]. These structural defects skew microglia toward the M1 phenotype, whose TNFα and C1q release drives neurons and oligodendrocytes into a neurotoxic A1 state, perpetuating an “M1–injury–A1” cycle of inflammation and glial scarring [173]. Moreover, dysregulated TGFβ/Smad3 signaling impairs ZEB1mediated repression of CXCL1, sustaining its overexpression and exacerbating NVU damage across the microvasculature [167, 174].

Current status of TGFβ-targeted therapeutic strategies in CSVD

Endothelial cells

Therapeutic strategies aimed at endothelial cells in CSVD primarily seek to restore tight junction integrity and reinforce the BBB. Although an intact BBB is vital for cerebral homeostasis, it also impedes drug delivery to the brain. Cilostazol, acting through JNK and p38 MAPK pathways, inhibits Smad3 transactivation at the PAI‑1 promoter to downregulate PAI‑1 expression [175]. Moreover, cilostazol enhances tight junction protein levels and attenuates TGFβ1 signaling, thereby mitigating ischemia–reperfusion injury induced by advanced glycation end products [176]. In clinical settings, the combination of isosorbide mononitrate and cilostazol has demonstrated efficacy in reducing recurrence and cognitive decline after lacunar stroke, as well as in preventing additional CSVD‑related complications, both agents are currently undergoing Phase III trials [177, 178]. The research also reveals that cilostazol may be more effective than aspirin in slowing down the progression of white matter lesion volume [179].

The BCR‑ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor imatinib has also shown promise in CSVD models, where it preserves BBB integrity by suppressing aberrant endothelial transcytosis, reducing plasma protein extravasation, and normalizing chronic hypoperfusion‑driven overactivation of TGFβ1/Smad2 signaling [17]. Imatinib treatment not only strengthens the BBB but also attenuates oligodendrocyte precursor cell activation and microglial overactivation, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting TGFβ/Smad pathways in early CSVD intervention [180]. Additionally, genetic ablation of the prostaglandin E receptor EP3 ameliorates hypertension‑induced vascular remodeling and cognitive deficits by downregulating TGFβ1/Smad signaling and modulating extracellular matrix (ECM) composition [181], suggesting that EP3 antagonists could offer another avenue for therapy, albeit currently at the preclinical stage.

Together, these and related compounds including SMAD3 inhibitors and aspirin [182] may converge on the TGFβ1/Smad, JNK/p38, MMP‑9, and claudin‑5 axes to rebuild endothelial tight junctions and restore BBB function in CSVD.

Pericyte

Pericyte dysfunction is a pivotal contributor to CSVD, as pericytes transdifferentiate into myofibroblast‑like cells that deposit collagen and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components, driving vascular fibrosis and luminal narrowing under chronic hypoperfusion [183]. In a chronic hypoperfusion mouse model, the compound edaravone dexborneol developed by Chinese researchers ameliorated ischemic injury by inhibiting TGFβ1/IL‑11 signaling, reducing pericyte fibrosis and ECM protein expression [183]. Simultaneously, it can maintain vascular homeostasis in the context of ischemia-reperfusion and indirectly modulate TGFβ-related inflammatory responses [184, 185]. These results indicate that the therapeutic effects of edaravone-dexborneol are highly dependent on the correction of abnormal TGFβ activation. A Phase III trial in CSVD patients demonstrated that intravenous infusion of 37.5 mg edaravone dexborneol within 48 hours of symptom onset significantly improved cognitive and behavioral outcomes at three months, attenuated cortical neuronal damage and white‑matter lesions, and exhibited a favorable safety profile [186, 187]. This suggests that Edaravone-Dexborneol is not merely an antioxidant drug but also a potential TGFβ-targeted modulator, which holds unique advantages in the treatment of CSVD.

Melatonin has also shown promise in stabilizing the blood–brain barrier by suppressing MMP‑9 activity and expression, restoring tight and adherens junction proteins, and is widely used as an adjuvant therapy in neurological and oncological settings [188]. Similarly, an angiotensin II type-1 receptor blocker, exerts benefits in CSVD beyond blood pressure control, partly through modulation of TGFβ–related pathways. In hereditary CSVD models, candesartan reduced fibronectin and latent TGFβ binding protein-4 accumulation, restored vascular function, and downregulated ECM remodeling genes [189, 190]. It also alleviates BBB dysfunction and abnormal TGFβ signaling in other models [191]. In mild cognitive impairment patients, improvements in microvascular function under candesartan treatment further support its role in modulating small vessel integrity, potentially through TGFβ/ECM effects [189]. These agents converge on TGFβ1/IL‑11, MMP‑9, and NF‑κB pathways within pericytes to reinforce vascular stability and microcirculatory flow.

Astrocyte and microglial

Modulation of astrocyte and microglial polarization represents a promising approach to quell neuroinflammation and foster repair in CSVD. Combined lamotrigine (LTG) and topiramate (TPM) therapy upregulates astrocytic connexin‑43 and amplifies TGFβ1 secretion from both astrocytes and microglia, thereby attenuating neuroinflammatory signaling [192]. Adjudin exerts neuroprotective effects by suppressing MMP‑9, TNF‑α, and IL‑1β production while enhancing tight junction integrity and AQP4 expression, although CSVD‑specific clinical validation remains pending [193, 194]. Salidroside has also been shown to increase microvessel density, reduce infarct volume and improve neurological outcomes in ischemic stroke models via the Shh signaling pathway [195]. Meanwhile, it fosters microglial M2 polarization and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines, contributing to neuroprotection [196]. These findings suggest salidroside’s multi-modal actions angiogenic, anti-inflammatory, and barrier-preserving, could make it a potential therapeutic candidate for CSVD, although direct human trials remain lacking. Another type of natural medicine, quercetin, a natural flavonoid, protects endothelial cells and the BBB. in vitro experiments using human brain microvascular endothelial cells under hypoxia/reoxygenation, it enhances endothelial survival and preserves junction proteins under hypoxic stress [197]. In vivo, it reduces BBB leakage and brain edema by upregulating tight junction proteins and suppressing MMP-9 via Wnt/β-catenin signaling [198]. These effects suggest quercetin may help maintain microvascular integrity in CSVD.

Multi-path integration

Several anti-inflammatory agents have been investigated in the context of CSVD. For instance, minocycline has been shown to reduce levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, and to inhibit MMP-9 activity in ischemic [199] and small vessel disease models [200]. Multiple animal studies further demonstrated that minocycline promotes microglial polarization toward the M2 phenotype [201], a process often accompanied by elevated or restored TGFβ expression [202], thereby contributing to the maintenance of microvascular integrity and improvement of cognitive performance [203–205]. However, in a phase II clinical trial involving patients with moderate-to-severe CSVD, short-term minocycline treatment did not significantly reduce microglial activation or improve BBB permeability [206]. This discrepancy suggests that while minocycline shows promise in animal models, its efficacy in CSVD patients requires further investigation, and glial-targeted strategies may necessitate longer treatment courses or more potent immunomodulatory approaches. Emerging compounds, such as CB2 receptor agonists and gossypol modulate astrocyte and microglial function by restoring C×43expression, balancing TGFβ1 secretion, adjusting M1/M2 and A1/A2 polarization, and enhancing AQP4‑mediated glymphatic clearance, offering multifaceted avenues for therapeutic development.

Multitarget and multi-pathway interventions promise a more holistic strategy to address the multifactorial pathophysiology of CSVD. In hereditary forms such as CARASIL, driven by HTRA1 mutations, excessive TGFβ activation underlies arteriolar fibrosis and white matter damage; preclinical studies demonstrate that losartan, by antagonizing the angiotensin II/AT1 receptor axis, indirectly attenuates TGFβ1 overexpression and ameliorates cerebrovascular remodeling and leukoaraiosis [38]. Similarly, Imatinib, a PDGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor, protects the BBB by reducing endothelial transcytosis, pericyte loss, and aberrant TGFβ/Smad2 signaling in chronic cerebral hypoperfusion models [17, 180], which mimic CSVD. It also suppresses MMP-9 activity and PDGFR-mediated pathways in hemorrhagic models [207, 208], alleviating edema and improving neurological outcomes. These findings suggest imatinib as a potential NVU modulator in CSVD, though current evidence remains limited to preclinical studies. At present, an increasing number of classical Traditional Chinese formulations, have demonstrated comprehensive therapeutic effects on CSVD. Hu Zhang Qing Mai Yin protects vascular endothelial cells from damage caused by CSVD by suppressing inflammation [209]. Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction, has shown neurovascular protective effects by suppressing NF-κB–mediated inflammation and promoting autophagy-dependent clearance of abnormal proteins [210]. It has been approved by the former CFDA in China and included in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, widely used for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions [211, 212]. Tongxinluo Capsules, and Buyang Huanwu Decoction has demonstrated neurovascular benefits in CSVD models by promoting angiogenesis (mainly through VEGFA upregulation alongside Angiopoietin-1 signaling), enhancing neurogenesis, preserving BBB integrity, and advancing neural repair after ischemic injury [213]. More mechanistic studies reveal that BHD also facilitates neural regeneration via caveolin-1 activation of Wnt signaling pathways [214]. Despite its long-standing clinical use and evidence base in China for ischemic stroke and CSVD, both of them have no FDA approval, underscoring the need for rigorous translational research in global regulatory contexts.

Aerobics

Beyond pharmacotherapy, aerobic exercise has emerged as a noninvasive modality that suppresses cerebrospinal TGFβ1, fosters microglial M2 polarization, and establishes a positive feedback loop of anti‑inflammatory TGFβ signaling [215]. Exercise also enhances endothelial shear stress and NO release, improves microcirculatory perfusion, elevates neurotrophic factor levels, and preserves white matter integrity and synaptic plasticity [216–219]. Notably, some studies report that aerobic exercise reduces plasma TGFβ and IL‑6 in patients with motor disorders, thereby ameliorating inflammation‑mediated motor deficits [220, 221]. These apparent contradictions underscore the bidirectional nature of TGFβ signaling and highlight the challenge of precisely modulating inflammatory mediators within the CNS. Future perspectives and challenges

The multifaceted and contextdependent roles of TGFβ signaling in CSVD underscore both its therapeutic promise and the considerable challenges ahead. As this review illustrates, TGFβ exerts stagespecific effects across endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, and microglia, forming an intricate network of crosstalk that cannot be adequately addressed by interventions targeting a single molecule or cell type. Yet, most existing experimental paradigms focus on isolated cell populations and lack the dynamic, multicellular modeling necessary to capture NVU interactions. Compounding these limitations, inconsistencies in CSVD animal model standardization hinder the reliability and translational potential of preclinical findings.

Moreover, key downstream mediators of TGFβ, such as GDF11, matrix metalloproteinases, and various epigenetic regulators remain incompletely characterized, leaving gaps in our understanding of the signaling cascade’s breadth and plasticity. To bridge this knowledge gap, future investigations should leverage integrated multiomics platforms that map the evolving TGFβ driven molecular landscape at each CSVD stage, from early microvascular dysfunction to latestage gliosis and fibrosis. Such comprehensive profiling will pave the way for nanocarrierbased, stagespecific delivery of TGFβ modulators, as well as rational combination therapies that pair neuroprotective agents with proangiogenic compounds to simultaneously repair the microvasculature and support neuronal resilience. TGFβ targeted therapeutics in CSVD remain scarce, with only a handful of candidates advancing into phase II/III clinical trials, often limited by singlecenter designs, small cohorts, and brief followup periods.

Achieving precise modulation of TGFβ signaling in CSVD demands the development of highly selective interventions that account for the pathway’s pleiotropic functions across different cell types and disease stages. Central to this effort is the identification of molecular “nodes” within the TGFβ cascade receptors, coreceptors, downstream SMADs, or nonSMAD effectors, whose activity is both stagespecific and cellspecific. Such targets might include, for example, ALK1 versus ALK5 in endothelial cells, or distinct SMAD complexes in pericytes and glial cells, where contextdependent inhibition or activation could tilt the balance toward repair rather than pathology. Realizing this level of precision will require interdisciplinary collaboration and the integration of precisionmedicine tools, largescale, multicenter clinical trials combined with comprehensive genetic diagnostics and highthroughput drug screening. Genetic profiling of CSVD patients may uncover polymorphisms or mutations in TGFβ pathway components, enabling stratification of individuals most likely to benefit from tailored therapies.

Early clinical intervention emerges as a particularly compelling strategy, since aberrant TGFβ activity often precedes overt microvascular damage. Prompt detection of TGFβ dysregulation via cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers or advanced neuroimaging could inform the timely administration of pathway modulators, potentially delaying or even preventing progression to irreversible whitematter injury. Looking beyond small molecules, emerging platforms such as stem cell–based vascular repair and CRISPR/Casmediated gene editing offer transformative possibilities: transplanted pericyte progenitors or engineered endothelial cells might restore NVU integrity, whereas precise editing of TGFβ related genes could normalize pathway activity in situ. Together, these approaches rooted in an intricate understanding of TGFβ’s cellspecific roles, hold the promise of truly personalized, stageadapted therapies that can overcome the complexities of CSVD.
