Community-based interventions for non-communicable diseases in Africa: a scoping review of scope, effectiveness, and implementation factors
Authors: Pascal Mathew Okorobe, John Onama, Omosola Lydia Bolarin, Saheed Adekunle Akinola, Shadrach Tetteh Boyetey, Josephine Ampong, Yesiru Adeyemi Kareem, Pascal D Sedor, Sarah Marion Nakachwa, Javirah Ahomugisha, Prince Owusu Adoma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increase of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) across Africa has become a major public health concern, contributing to significant morbidity and mortality which exerts significant strain on the already burdened health systems. Community-based interventions (CBIs) offer a strategy to address these diseases but evidence remains scattered on their effectiveness, contextual factors that enable or hinder their implementation. This review aimed to map existing community-based interventions, assess their outcomes, identify key facilitators and barriers, and highlight gaps in the literature across diseases, populations, and regions.MethodsThe review followed Arksey and O’Malley’s scoping review framework and the reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). We searched PubMed, Scopus Web of Science, Google Scholar, and additional grey literature sources to identify studies. Results were synthesized thematically and reported using a narrative approach.ResultsThis review included 100 studies from across 16 African countries, with most conducted in South Africa, Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria, focusing on community-based interventions for NCDs. The interventions addressed screening, health promotion, integrated care, and Health, targeting mainly hypertension, diabetes, cancers, and mental and neurological disorders. Overall, CBIs enhanced screening, health literacy, and selected clinical outcomes, especially blood pressure control, although continuity and linkage to formal care were inconsistent. Key facilitators included trusted community health workers (CHWs) and culturally tailored approaches, task-shifting, proximity to people, and use of digital technologies. Barriers included equipment/medication shortages, inadequate CHW supervision and incentives, poverty, transport costs, stigma, cultural beliefs, low health literacy, and weak referral and health systems.ConclusionCommunity-based interventions for NCDs in Africa demonstrate huge potential to counter the rising NCD burden but encounter significant challenges; successful implementation requires resource sensitive and cultural tailored strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28483-x.

Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory illnesses are rapidly rising in prevalence across Africa, contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality in both urban and rural settings [1, 2]. Globally, Cardiovascular diseases account for most NCD deaths, that is − 17.9 million people annually, followed by cancers (9.3 million), respiratory diseases (4.1 million), and diabetes (1.5 million) [3]. NCDs kill 41 million people each year globally, and 77% of all NCD deaths are in low- and middle-income countries [3].

The African continent has traditionally grappled with infectious diseases, maternal and child health, nutritional related diseases [4]; but over the years, the morbidity and mortality from NCDs has increased, which the World Health organization (WHO) states a rise from 24% in 2000 to 37% in 2019 deaths caused by NCDs in Africa [3]. This places significant pressure on already strained healthcare systems, increasing demand for both acute and chronic care services, especially in urban and poor populations [5].

Community-based interventions (CBIs) are strategies implemented within specific geographic communities such as neighborhood, schools, churches, or workplaces with the aim of improving health outcomes [6]. While long-term clinical health status is the distal goal, CBIs more directly target a spectrum of proximal outcomes, including enhancing health literacy, boosting screening uptake, shaping health-related behaviors, and improving treatment adherence [7]. These interventions often employ educational, organizational, and policy-level approaches that engage individuals, families, social networks, and institutions [6]. While they may involve community input through advisory groups or coalitions, their operational focus is on influencing these proximal individual and social behaviour changes, with broader population-level health impacts seen as the collective, cumulative result of these shifts [6]. They are defined by their emphasis on community participation, the involvement of local health workers, and the adaptation of programs to fit cultural and social contexts [8]. Community based health interventions have shown many positive health outcomes including early detection of NCDs, increased adherence to treatment and modification of lifestyles for prevention [8–10].

There is growing interest in community-based health interventions for the prevention and control of NCDs in Africa, reflecting the urgent need to address the rising burden of these conditions across the continent [11–14]. While some studies highlight the potential of CBIs such as those involving community health workers (CHWs), integrated care models, and participatory approaches to improve access to care and support disease management, there is limited understanding of the specific contextual enablers and barriers that influence their success. For example, challenges such as insufficient training, weak inter-sectoral collaboration, inadequate resources, and unclear roles for CHWs have been identified as obstacles to effective implementation [15, 16]. Additionally, the adaptation of interventions to local cultural, social, and economic contexts is often insufficiently addressed, which can limit both uptake and impact [17].

While several reviews have explored dimensions of NCD management on the continent, they remain distinct in scope, geography, or methodology from this study. For instance, a systematic review by Kassa and Grace (2022) focused exclusively on macro-level national NCD prevention policies and governance equity in African countries [18]. Similarly, a scoping review protocol outlined a strategy to synthesize evidence on the integration of NCD services within existing, formal communicable disease platforms, such as established HIV and TB clinics [19]. Mogo and colleagues (2022) focus strictly on multisectoral and intersectoral interventions targeting behavioural risk factors within urban environments, omitting rural landscapes where health access constraints differ drastically [20]. Prior to initiating this study, an exhaustive search of PubMed, Web of Science, PROSPERO, and the Open Science Framework confirmed that no existing or ongoing review provides a comprehensive, continent-wide map of CBIs spanning the complete spectrum of NCD clinical categories both rural and urban Africa.

Given the heterogeneity of CBIs and the unique socio-cultural and economic contexts in Africa this scoping review is warranted to consolidate these insights into a singular framework. With this review, we aim to: (1) identify the types of community-based interventions that have been implemented to prevent or manage NCDs among populations in African countries; (2) assess the reported outcomes and effectiveness of these interventions; (3) explore the key facilitators and barriers influencing their implementation; and (4) highlight gaps in the existing literature, particularly in relation to understudied populations, specific NCDs, and geographic regions.

Methods

This scoping review followed the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley, 2005, enhanced by Levac et al., 2010 [21], and JBI [22]. It consisted of the following stages: (1) identifying the research question(s); (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) study selection; (4) charting the data; (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting results; and (6) a consultation with stakeholders. The review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [23]. This review protocol was registered on Open Science Framework and is available at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/VEDR2.

This was guided by questions developed using the PCC framework (Population, Concept, Context). The main question was:What is the scope of community-based interventions for the prevention and management of NCDs in Africa, including their progress, effectiveness, and gaps?

Step 2: identification of studies

With the help of a librarian, a comprehensive search strategy was developed following the JBI three step framework [24]. First, a preliminary search was initiated using selected keywords. The titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles were analysed to uncover relevant synonyms, related terms, and indexing language. To ensure comprehensive coverage, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and other controlled vocabularies were explored. The developed search strategy was subsequently be refined and validated.

Secondly, a comprehensive search was conducted across three primary databases — PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. An initial search was performed in June 2025; however, given the time elapsed during the review process, an updated search was conducted on June 20th, 2026, at which point Google Scholar and additional grey literature sources were incorporated to ensure completeness. Grey literature was searched from Google Scholar, WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS), the Africa CDC website, and ProQuest. The latter restricted by source type to dissertations and theses, reports, government and official publications, newspapers, magazines, speeches and presentations, blogs and podcasts, and conference papers and proceedings. Sources that did not generate exportable bibliographic records were manually catalogued, with their full texts attached directly for screening; database records, by contrast, were exported in standard bibliographic format. Given the large volume of results returned by Google Scholar, screening was limited to the first 200 records ranked by relevance, consistent with established practice for grey literature searching using Google Scholar [25]. Next, records from all sources were combined and screened for duplicates in Rayyan web platform. The complete search strategy for each database is provided in Additional File 1.

After full-text screening, snowballing of included studies was done by looking at the references (backward citation chasing) and citing papers (forward citation chasing). The open-source web application citationchaser was used to automate and aid this process by querying the Lens.org meta database [26].

Step 3: selection of studies

Articles were selected against inclusion and exclusion criteria using Rayyan software [27]. Two reviewers PMO and JO independently screen all titles and abstracts to evaluate their relevance according to the predefined inclusion criteria. Studies potentially eligible underwent full-text review by the same reviewers, who assessed them thoroughly against the same criteria. Disagreements at both the initial screening and full-text eligibility assessment stages were resolved through discussion between the primary reviewers, with persistent conflicts adjudicated by a third reviewer (YAK). The full search and selection process, including the number of records at each stage was summarized using a flowchart (Fig. 1).

Quality appraisal of all included studies was conducted and is reported in Additional File 2. The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool was used due to heterogeneity of designs of the included studies [28]. Consistent with scoping review methodology, no studies were excluded on the basis of quality scores.

Step 4: data extraction and charting

A customized data extraction form (see Additional File 3) was developed to capture information relevant to the specific research questions and PCC framework of this review, as no existing standardized extraction tool adequately covered the breadth of variables required, including intervention type, setting, target population, NCD focus, facilitators, barriers, outcomes, and identified research gaps. The form was developed collaboratively by the review team and piloted on 5 randomly selected included studies to assess its clarity, comprehensiveness, and consistency of application. Refinements were made iteratively following the piloting process before the form was applied to all remaining included studies. This form captured key details such as authorship, year, country, study design, sample size, setting, and population characteristics. It also included information on the type and delivery of community-based interventions, duration, stakeholders involved, and reported effectiveness and outcomes. Additionally, data on implementation factors such as facilitators and barriers was collected. All included articles were subjected to double data extraction by two reviewers independently, and a third reviewer compared both extractions for any discrepancies and completeness which were resolved. The data final data extracted from included studies and used in synthesis is provided in Additional File 4.

Step 5: data synthesis, collating and reporting the results

The data synthesis and reporting process followed a descriptive analytical framework to provide a structured map of the existing literature. Quantitative data, including study characteristics (e.g., publication year, geographical distribution, target non-communicable diseases, and study designs), were analysed using descriptive statistics, with frequencies and percentages calculated to summarize distributions. Spatial visualization mapping the country-level distribution of the included literature was generated using RStudio Version 2025.05.1 + 513 (Fig. 2).

For the qualitative and contextual data, specifically relating to intervention components, implementation outcomes, facilitators, and barriers, a descriptive narrative synthesis was employed. This synthesis was guided by a thematic analysis approach, wherein extracted data were iteratively coded and categorized into themes and sub-themes. These themes were mapped against the primary and secondary research questions derived from the PCC framework (Table 1).

Step 6: consulting stakeholders

The consultation stage was operationalized internally through an iterative consensus-building process with our multidisciplinary team of co-authors, who represent public health experts, clinicians, and researchers across multiple African regions (Uganda, Nigeria, Rwanda, and Ghana).

Results

Characteristics and distribution of studies

A total of 5974 studies were retrieved from searching databases and grey literature sources. Following title/abstract and full-text screening, 97 studies were included, with an additional 3 identified through citation chasing, bringing the final total to 100 studies (Fig. 1).

Sixteen countries were represented with South Africa (n = 21) [11, 14, 15, 29–46], Kenya (n = 20) [12, 47–65], Uganda (n = 18) [49, 66–81], Nigeria (n = 10) [82–91], and Ethiopia (n = 9) [92–100], accounting for most of the studies (Fig. 2). Among the 100 included studies, two were multi-country. One covered Kenya and Uganda [49], bringing the total country-level frequency count to 101. The other spanned the DRC, the Philippines, and Cambodia [101]; because this review focuses strictly on Africa, only the DRC-specific data was considered.

Study designs and methodologies

Qualitative approaches were most common (n = 25), including focus group discussions, in-depth interviews, phenomenological, and naturalistic designs, used primarily to explore feasibility, acceptability, and implementation context [13, 15, 31–39, 50–52, 68–76, 84, 94–98, 102–108], followed by mixed-methods studies (n = 20) [29, 33, 40–43, 47, 51, 53, 66, 67, 77, 78, 85–87, 99, 109–111]. The rest were quantitative studies, utilizing the following designs: Quasi-experimental designs (n = 14), largely were pre-post and before-and-after studies [37, 44, 54–59, 72, 79–82, 88, 89, 112, 113] ; RCTs and cluster RCTs (n = 11) [45, 48, 49, 58, 60–62, 92, 96, 100, 114, 115]; cross-sectional studies, including surveys and diagnostic accuracy studies (n = 11) [11, 32, 63, 83, 88, 90, 93, 97, 116–118]; prospective cohort and longitudinal studies (n = 6) [12, 34, 38, 71, 101, 119]; descriptive or programmatic studies (n = 3) [46, 120, 121]; standalone pilot or feasibility studies (n = 8) [55–57, 64, 65, 75, 91, 100, 122, 123]; and cost-effectiveness analyses (n = 2) comprised the remainder [14, 30].

Target NCDs

The community-based interventions included in this review addressed a wide, though uneven, spectrum of NCDs. As some studies targeted more than one condition, the figures presented here reflect condition-level counts and exceed the number of included studies. Cardiovascular Diseases (CVDs) were the most studied group, with Hypertension targeted 39 times (including combinations with other conditions) [11–14, 31–35, 35, 36, 40, 43, 45, 47–49, 57, 59, 61, 67, 69, 71, 72, 77–79, 84–86, 90, 91, 96–98, 102, 103, 107, 108, 113, 114, 116, 119], Stroke 2 times [38, 46], and general CVD risk factors (n = 4) [15, 41, 64, 74]. Cancers were addressed in a total of 27 studies, dominated by Cervical Cancer (n = 23), including 3 combined with breast cancer [37–39, 55–58, 60, 62, 75, 76, 78, 80, 81, 83, 88, 89, 95, 100, 105, 110, 112, 117, 118, 120–122]; fewer studies targeted breast cancer alone (n = 2) [65, 92], prostate cancer (n = 1) [54], and general cancer awareness in 1 instance [111]. Diabetes was addressed in 18 studies: diabetes and hypertension combined 11 times [11, 35, 36, 40, 57, 67, 69, 77, 78, 84, 114], and diabetes mellitus was featured exclusively 7 times [29, 30, 33, 39, 63, 73, 101]. Mental and neurological disorders were targeted 16 times: Dementia (n = 4) [51, 53, 66, 70]; psychotic disorders (n = 2) [99, 106]; postpartum mental disorders (n = 1) [34]; autism/ developmental disorders (n = 2) [52, 93]; schizophrenia (n = 1) [99]; general mental disorders (n = 5) [32, 44, 76, 82, 123]; and mental health promotion (n = 1) [109]. Other conditions included chronic respiratory diseases (Asthma and COPD, n = 2) [67, 68]; Pregnancy-related hypertensive disorders (n = 2) [87, 115], and sickle cell disease (n = 1) [104]. Four studies focused on general NCDs [15, 41, 42, 94].

Types of community-based interventions

We identified a wide array of community-based interventions (CBIs) ranging from targeted screening campaigns to comprehensive, multi-component disease management programs.

Screening and early detection strategies

Screening was the most predominant intervention type, implemented through two primary delivery models: door-to-door household visits and mass community campaigns.

Door-to-door screening

CHWs and Health Extension Workers (HEWs) were deployed to households to measure vital signs and carry out simple tests. This approach was widely used for hypertension and diabetes, utilizing portable electronic blood pressure monitors and glucometers [11, 12, 32–35, 48, 87, 97, 98]. Specialized home-based screening was also employed for dementia and cognitive impairment [51, 66] as well as for COPD using lung function questionnaires and peak flow meters [68]. Prenatal screening for Post-natal depression integrated to routine community-based prenatal outreach was also noted [34].

Campaigns and health fairs

Other interventions utilized high-throughput screening and campaigns at central locations such as markets, schools, and places of worship [70, 113, 118]. These included multi-disease community health campaigns [71], and comparative studies evaluating home-based and community strategies [68].

Cancer screening strategies

Interventions for cervical cancer heavily utilized task-shifting. Strategies included training CHWs to conduct Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) [88, 105, 118] and the distribution of self-sampling kits for HPV to increase privacy and uptake [58, 62, 75, 95, 100, 110, 122]. A targeted school HPV vaccination against cervical cancer [120], as well as door-to-door and community mobilization for Cervical Cancer Screening [60, 80, 100].

Health education and health promotion

Educational interventions aimed to improve health literacy and modify risk behaviors using culturally adapted delivery methods.

Group and peer-based learning

Structured group sessions were common, such as the “Lifestyle Africa” diabetes prevention program [30], community health clubs [42, 73, 116], and supervised community exercise sessions [41]. A community-based psychodrama intervention to promote mental health among adolescents and young adults was also demonstrated [109].

Innovative communication

Novel approaches included the use of applied drama and theatre to share patient stories [39] and the use of a “story-telling cloth” (textile) approach to facilitate community dialogue on health topics [112].

Targeted Counselling

Specific programs targeted deeply ingrained beliefs, such as education to counter fatalistic views on prostate cancer [54], or integrated health messaging into traditional marriage counselling [121]. Counselling was also done for medication and treatment adherence, as well as behavioural change [40, 59, 91].

General awareness

Broader awareness campaigns utilized radio, social media, and traditional community announcements alongside CHW outreaches [37, 59, 65, 103, 113, 114]. Community health education on breast self-examination and awareness about breast cancer was also noted [65, 92].

Integrated management and linkage to care

Beyond screening, several interventions established comprehensive care models to bridge the gap between community detection and clinical treatment.

Task-sharing and home-based care

These programs shifted clinical tasks to lay workers. Examples include CHWs conducted deliveries of medications directly to patients’ homes [15, 29, 40, 45, 49, 76] conducting post-discharge rehabilitation for stroke survivors [38], and managing mental health cases after training in identification and referral [70, 76, 123]. CHWs also screened for hypertension in communities and referred those with elevated blood pressures to the nearest health center [79, 86, 98].

Linkage strategies

To ensure screen-positive individuals reached facilities, interventions employed referral vouchers, SMS reminders for appointments and follow up [12, 71, 103, 119], and in some cases, “screen-and-treat” models for cervical cancer where VIA positive women received same-day thermocoagulation treatment in the village [37, 82].

Self-management support

Interventions empowered patients to manage their conditions through home blood pressure monitoring combined with CHW coaching on diet and adherence [14, 31, 61, 91].

mHealth and technology enablers

Technology was employed in multiple intervention types. Mobile health (mHealth) tools were used for data collection and decision support, such as the AfyaChat app for CVD risk assessment [47], tablet-based dementia screening [51], and smartphone-guided referrals [47, 48, 64, 115]. Additionally, telehealth models enabled CHWs to connect patients in remote villages with clinicians via video for real-time consultations [49]. SMS reminders were employed in health education, reminders for follow-up care, appointments, and for medication adherence [12, 59, 62, 64, 101, 103].

Outcomes and effectiveness of community-based interventions

While CBIs consistently demonstrated high reach in screening and health literacy, their impact on long-term clinical outcomes and linkage to formal care was variable, often influenced by several factors.

Clinical health outcomes

Evidence regarding the impact of CBIs on physiological health outcomes was predominantly positive for hypertension control. Multiple interventions reported statistically significant reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to control groups [45, 49, 59–61, 72, 87, 91, 96, 114, 116]. Correspondingly, several studies demonstrated that community-based care significantly increased hypertension control rates [40, 49, 61, 85, 119], and one cost-effectiveness analysis found that “step-down” medication dosing by CHWs maintained blood pressure control while reducing costs [67]. However, the evidence was not unequivocal; a subset of studies found no statistically significant difference in blood pressure reduction between intervention and control arms [13, 59, 113], or found effects only in specific sub-groups such as younger adults [113].

Results for other conditions were similarly mixed. For diabetes, home delivery of medication was associated with improved glycaemic control [29], yet one study found that patients receiving standard clinic care achieved better outcomes than those in the community arm [40]. Another study found improvement in lifestyle modification, medication adherence with no significant change in glucose control [114].

In the context of stroke rehabilitation, home-based care significantly improved functional independence scores [38, 46], although mortality and residual disability remained high [38]. Furthermore, One community engagement study for preeclampsia found no significant reduction in composite maternal/ newborn mortality despite increased contacts [124]. A community based rehabilitation for Schizophrenia was acceptable, and improved functioning of patients [99].

Screening reach and diagnostic accuracy

A primary strength of CBIs was their ability to expand access to diagnosis. Interventions successfully reached large populations, identifying high rates of undiagnosed conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, dementia, and cervical cancer [11, 12, 35, 47, 51, 64, 72, 118, 122]. Community mobilization strategies, including mass campaigns and an innovative “story-telling” approach, led to substantial increases in screening uptake, with some studies reporting up to a five-fold increase [89, 112]. Self-sampling models for HPV were particularly effective, achieving high participation rates due to increased privacy [75, 81, 105, 110], though participation in some campaigns declined over time due to community rumors [83]. When standardized tools were utilized, task-shifting to lay workers generally yielded acceptable diagnostic accuracy. Health Extension Workers (HEWs) and CHWs achieved high sensitivity and specificity for hypertension [87, 97], and mental health screening [32, 48]. However, accuracy was not uniform; some studies reported low specificity leading to over-referral [66], or poor diagnostic capability when CHWs worked without supervision [86].

Linkage to care and retention

Linkage-to-care rates varied across interventions, ranging from modest rates of 45% [48], to over 80% within six months, with higher rates observed where referrals were made to nearby primary health facilities and where demographic and social factors favoured access [71]. One study reported a linkage rate of 68%, though only 27% were subsequently retained in care [91]. Retention rates were higher in other contexts, with one study reporting 99% retention at one year [67], and a telehealth intervention reporting 95% [49]. A community hypertension screening and linkage program by CHWs lead to increase in cases diagnosed at the nearest health facility [79].

However, despite high screening yields, linkage to formal care remained a critical bottleneck in the care cascade. Numerous studies observed a “leakage” effect, where high screening numbers did not translate into sustained treatment; linkage rates frequently fell way below the screened numbers [47, 53, 74, 9061], and loss to follow-up was also reported [65, 83, 119].

Behavioural, knowledge, and stigma outcomes

Beyond clinical metrics, CBIs were highly effective in modifying health beliefs and increasing literacy. Educational interventions consistently led to statistically significant increases in knowledge regarding cervical cancer symptoms and risk factors [50, 55, 56, 111], diabetes and hypertension [30, 77, 85, 103], dementia [70], COPD [68], and mental health [44]. Furthermore, these programs successfully targeted deep-seated stigma. Interventions significantly reduced fatalistic beliefs regarding prostate cancer [54], and decreased social distance towards marginalized groups, including children with autism [93], and people with dementia [53], or psychosis [82]. While knowledge improved, behavioural changes were more complex; some studies reported reductions in physical inactivity [59], improved lifestyle against NCDs [41, 114], another study found low scores in lifestyle changes (exercise, diet) [63].

Feasibility, acceptability and cost-effectiveness

Feasibility and acceptability were formally evaluated as primary outcomes in a subset of studies. Where assessed, interventions were generally found to be acceptable to communities and CHWs, including those utilizing mHealth tools [47, 53, 54, 61, 64, 104], and home-based care models [43, 46, 78, 103, 107, 108, 123]. However, acceptability did not consistently translate into operational feasibility; several studies reported structural constraints including poor linkage to care, equipment failure, and health system gaps that undermined implementation despite community acceptance [46, 66, 68, 119].

Formal economic evaluation was limited to two studies. Both found CHW-delivered interventions to be cost-effective or cost-saving, one reporting an incremental cost effectiveness ratio of $320 per disability-adjusted life year averted for a hypertension education programme [14], and another finding the intervention arm less costly than standard care due to reduced hospitalizations [30].

Facilitators and barriers to implementation

Health system and workforce factors

Facilitators

The most consistent enabler across studies was the trust and familiarity of the workforce. CHWs were frequently described as trusted “village doctors”, neighbours, or “sons of the soil”, which facilitated entry into households and improved acceptability [11, 31, 98, 102–105, 107, 108]. Task-shifting was feasible when supported by structured training; effective strategies included the use of simple, standardized tools (e.g., pocket guides, aneroid BP machines) and video-based training modules [14, 30, 35, 54, 66]. Integration with existing services was another key success factor; embedding NCD care into HIV platforms, mental health services, or church activities improved feasibility and reduced duplication [57, 123]. Availability of inputs, support supervision and training of CHWs were also reported enablers [61, 74].

Barriers

However, the workforce was often unsupported. A major barrier was resource scarcity, particularly the stockouts of equipment and supplies for use in the community and at referral clinics [11, 31, 40, 67, 73, 87, 107]. Equipment failure was rampant, with reports of broken blood pressure machines and dead batteries halting screening efforts [35, 63, 98, 108, 119]. Where mHealth tools were used, similar infrastructure constraints emerged: system downtime and connectivity failures disrupted program delivery [64, 101, 103]. Device loss, breakage, and handset costs posed challenges [77, 101], and low digital and health literacy limited the utility of SMS-based tools, with illiterate users in one study requiring a family member to relay messages [101].

Systemic issues included staff shortages and inadequate supervision; some studies noted that CHWs received little feedback from facility health professionals [14, 40–42, 84]. Furthermore, CHW workload and lack of financial incentives were critical challenges, with volunteers often overburdened by vast catchment areas and the “opportunity costs” of unpaid work [15, 63, 69, 94, 107, 117, 119]. Lack of confidence in their personal abilities, lack of training and limited health system support were also reported by CHWs [106, 117]. CHWs knowledge gaps were also reported [33, 69].

Community and Socio-cultural factors

Facilitators

Interventions succeeded when they were culturally adapted. The use of local languages [44, 53, 70, 98], and the involvement of community leaders, traditional chiefs, and religious figures [13, 70, 74, 78, 115], were vital for gaining entry and endorsement. Peer support and group dynamics were also beneficial, fostering a sense of shared experience and reducing isolation [41, 53, 73].

Barriers

Deeply ingrained beliefs and stigma posed significant hurdles. Patients frequently attributed symptoms (such as those of dementia, or mental illness) to witchcraft or supernatural causes rather than medical conditions [52, 53, 70]. Stigma was a specific barrier for mental health and smoking-related conditions [51, 68, 106]. Gender dynamics played a restrictive role; studies reported that women often required spousal or male partner approval to access care, with husband disapproval a specific, recurring barrier to cervical cancer screening and HPV self-sampling [37, 80, 81, 95, 100]. Community rumors, including fears of uterus removal and mistrust of outsiders (e.g., fear of spies), as well as broader misconceptions and taboos, also hindered interventions [74, 83]. Low community trust and lack of interest was also an issue [117]. Finally, the asymptomatic nature of NCDs led to complacency, with many patients “feeling well” and thus deprioritizing clinic visits [12, 47, 71].

Logistical and economic factors

Facilitators

Proximity was the strongest logistical enabler. Home-based (door-to-door) screening and “screen-and-treat” models removed the need for travel, significantly boosting uptake [11, 40, 68, 89, 96]. Technology (mHealth) served as a useful tool for standardizing care, improving access, and guiding referrals across a range of conditions, provided devices were functional and charged [12, 36, 47, 49, 51, 64, 77, 100, 101, 103]. Economic incentives, such as transport vouchers, were crucial for converting screening into linkage [71].

Barriers

Conversely, poverty was the overarching structural barrier. The cost of transport to referral facilities was frequently cited as the primary reason for loss to follow-up [37, 48, 51, 53, 57, 67, 71]. CHWs also faced financial constraints while conducting the interventions [68]. Distance itself was a structural barrier particularly in rural settings where referral facilities were several kilometres away, limiting both patients’ ability to attend follow-up and CHWs’ ability to reach their catchment areas [13, 14, 81, 107, 118]. This was compounded by poor road infrastructure and geographic remoteness [72, 100, 122]. Even when screening was free, the cost of treatment or vaccination prevented effective care, with several studies reporting that women were unwilling or unable to pay out-of-pocket fees for HPV vaccination, screening, or medication [67, 88, 89, 112]. Migration and mobility (e.g., participants relocating for work) disrupted continuity of care [12, 34, 52, 54].

Discussion

Our review revealed evidence of CBIs across 16 African countries, with South Africa, Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria accounting for most of the studies. This implies that we found no evidence in about 38 of the 54 countries classified among African countries. This finding suggests geographically concentrated research on community-based NCD interventions in Africa, with South Africa, Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria dominating the literature. Similar geographic concentration has been observed in previous reviews examining the sustainability of health interventions in sub-Saharan Africa, where Kenya and Nigeria had the most representation [124–126]. This severe lack of localized context leaves a vast majority of African nations descriptive data-poor, forcing a reliance on centralized donor priorities rather than grounded community evidence, a systemic gap highlighted in an African policy scoping review [127]. This observation is concerning since NCDs represent an expanding burden across all African countries and require evidence-based community interventions tailored to their diverse contexts.

We also found variations in intervention effectiveness across different NCDs and implementation contexts. Hypertension interventions demonstrated the most consistent positive outcomes, with community-based approaches showing reduction in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, though the evidence base remains inconsistent. Self-sampling models for cervical cancer screening worked well, but the results for mental health and diabetes management were less consistent, depending on how much help CHWs gave and how easy it was to obtain medications at referral facilities. This variation in effectiveness requires contextualized approaches that account for specific disease characteristics, healthcare system capacities, and community preparedness. Previous literature highlighted that priorities for disease management in primary care include the availability of essential diagnostic tools and medications at local primary healthcare clinics, along with standard protocols for diagnosis, treatment, monitoring, and referral to specialist care [128]. Our findings confirm that CBI interventions performed better when these foundational elements were in place.

Key facilitators were rooted in the local context. The trusted status of CHWs was repeatedly cited: community members viewed Health Extension Workers or CHWs as “family” or “neighbours,” enhancing participation and adherence. Programs that leveraged the trust of community health workers viewed as neighbours or ‘one of us’ reported higher participation and adherence in screening activities and other health behaviors [129]. Studies show that CHWs improve health knowledge and community care-seeking, acting as a bridge to formal services. Formal training and supervision also mattered: interventions that gave CHWs clear protocols, simple tools (BP cuffs, flipcharts), and ongoing support reported better performance. Integration into existing structures (e.g. piggybacking on HIV or maternal health platforms, involving local leaders and clinics) further anchored programs in the health system and community networks. On the socio-cultural side, tailoring messages to local languages and engaging traditional authorities or peer groups proved critical to acceptance, as did framing health behaviors in culturally relevant ways. Formal training, ongoing supervision and logistical support (including simple tools) are linked to higher CHW performance and community acceptance [130].

Conversely, barriers frequently undermined impact. Health system constraints loomed large: many programs reported shortages of supplies (e.g. broken BP machines, depleted testing kits) and erratic drug availability, often halting services mid-stream. Workforce issues were also a problem; overworked, unpaid CHWs sometimes lost motivation. These multi-level impediments mirror the structural, organizational, and community-level challenges formally mapped out in other literature regarding CHW deployment for NCDs in Africa [131]. Crucially, our findings regarding high initial screening yields juxtaposed against severe care cascade drop-offs lend empirical weight to these expert warnings: without fortified referral links and sustained state support, community-level outreach cannot bridge the gap to clinical continuity.

These issues echo known challenges in African health systems [130], and imply policy remedies: stable funding and supply chains for NCD programs are needed, and CHW programs must include incentives or career pathways to sustain motivation. Socio-economic hurdles were also critical. Poverty and transport costs consistently impeded care: even when screening was free, many diagnosed individuals could not afford repeated clinic visits or medications [131]. This suggests policy fixes such as travel vouchers or decentralizing treatment (e.g. home drug delivery) can meaningfully improve linkage to care. Furthermore, while CHW frameworks are often deemed cost-effective in isolated theoretical evaluations, expert health economic reviews highlight that true financial affordability and government capacity to absorb recurring implementation costs remain profoundly understudied [132].

Cultural factors too created barriers: in some communities, illnesses (especially mental/cognitive ones) were attributed to witchcraft or spirits engendering stigma. Deep-rooted cultural beliefs and mistrust in health programs can limit engagement, particularly where traditional health systems and explanations of illness differ [131]. Gender norms were relevant in women’s health for instance, cervical cancer outreach faltered when husbands disapproved. Addressing these requires culturally sensitive strategies: involving community leaders, providing couple’s education or male outreach, and sustained community dialogue can help overcome fatalism and mistrust.

Sustainability beyond the initial implementation period emerged as a significant gap across the included literature. Most CBIs were evaluated as short-term pilots or within funded project cycles, with few studies reporting outcomes after donor or research funding ended. Where sustainability was addressed, common threats included CHW attrition due to lack of long-term incentives, reliance on externally supplied equipment and commodities that were not replenished once projects concluded, and limited integration into routine government health system budgets and staffing structures. This pattern echoes broader findings in the sub-Saharan African health systems literature, where CHW programs frequently struggle to transition from donor-funded pilots to nationally owned, government-financed services [133, 134]. Embedding CBIs within existing primary health care structures, securing domestic financing commitments, and building local capacity for program management may be necessary preconditions for sustained impact [135], yet few of the studies in this review were designed or funded to test these pathways.

Implication for Practice

This study included interventions from African countries where most clients with NCDs still present late or remain undiagnosed, thus increasing the morbidity and mortality associated with NCDs in Africa. Screening through door-to-door visits and community campaigns is one way to find undiagnosed NCDs early, but clinical exams and lab tests give more certain diagnoses [11, 136]. This approach assumes that people who are screened will be able to get to health facilities, but the findings here showed that many people who test positive never get treatment. Fundamental drivers of successful CHW programs include structured supervision, patients’ access to care through CHWs, community involvement, remuneration and facilitation, as well as building CHW knowledge and skills through training [137, 138]. Our findings showed that some interventions were able to achieve blood pressure control, improve glycaemic management, and benefit from rehabilitation through consistent CHW support when these elements were present. It implies that comprehensive training on screening, referral, and follow-up will have a substantial impact, especially when CHWs are equipped with functional tools, provided regular supervision, and compensated fairly for their work. When communities are mobilized effectively, individuals receive regular screenings using validated tools, and referral systems provide transport support and same-day treatment options when feasible, early detection can lead to timely care, thereby reducing morbidity and mortality associated with NCDs. Where assessed, most interventions were deemed feasible and acceptable by communities. Therefore, intensive public and institutional education, combined with strengthened referral systems, is required to maximize the impact of community-based NCD interventions, aiming for early detection, successful linkages to care, and sustained treatment adherence.

Implications for Research

Our study highlights geographic concentration in research on community-based interventions for NCDs in Africa, with limited studies from Francophone and Lusophone countries. Most interventions addressed hypertension, diabetes, and cervical cancer, while fewer covered chronic respiratory diseases, mental health, and other conditions. We hope this study spurs research on under-represented NCDs and countries, where NCDs are also rising. We recommend primary studies evaluating the long-term sustainability and cost-effectiveness of CBIs beyond pilot phases, including post-funding follow-up periods, formal sustainability frameworks, and assessment of routine integration into government health systems [139, 140]. We also urge studies on the best training for CHWs, compensation, and supervision to ensure quality and retention. Strong leadership and alignment with government priorities improve success, so research on policy integration and health system readiness is needed.

Given the breadth of this scoping review, quantitative pooling of effect sizes was outside its intended scope. Future researchers should consider targeted systematic reviews and meta-analyses of specific intervention types or NCD categories in African settings, which would provide more precise estimates of effectiveness than a broad mapping review of this nature can offer.

Strengths and Limitations

This scoping review is likely the first to map community-based NCD interventions comprehensively across Africa. Our methods enabled systematic article identification, intervention charting, and outcome analysis across NCDs. We searched 3 primary databases (Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus), google scholar and additional grey literature sources, as well as performed citation tracking of included studies, which ensured comprehensiveness of the search. However, our study is limited by its diverse study designs (observational and experimental), yielding variable evidence quality. While all included studies underwent critical appraisal, none were excluded based on quality scores, aligning with methodological frameworks for scoping reviews. We considered only English publications and only non-English ones with available translations, potentially excluding French, Portuguese, or other-language studies, especially from Francophone and Lusophone Africa. Review articles were excluded and could have identified additional research gaps.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that CBIs successfully expand screening reach, improve health literacy, and improve health outcomes such as enhancing blood pressure control. However, their long-term impact is often limited by poor linkage to formal care and high attrition rates. Success is heavily dependent on trusted CHWs, cultural adaptation, and the use of technology. Conversely, implementation is frequently hindered by resource scarcity, equipment failure, and lack of incentives for the workforce. To maximize effectiveness, future interventions must prioritize sustainable funding, stable supply chains, and better integration with existing health systems.
