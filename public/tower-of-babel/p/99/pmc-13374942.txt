Effectiveness and safety of polyacrylamide hydrogel injection for female stress urinary incontinence: A systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis
Authors: Hendri Tanjaya, Amaditta Jericka, Mutiara Riani, Budi Darmawan, Edihan Mardjuki, Jennifer Uriah, Erik Noverdian Suryadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Polyacrylamide hydrogel (PAHG) injection is a minimally invasive treatment option for female stress incontinence, but reported effectiveness and durability vary across studies. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness, safety, and retreatment rates of PAHG injection for female SUI through a systematic review and meta-analysis.Methods:A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261328968). Literature was retrieved from electronic databases, such as PubMed, ProQuest, EuroPMC, and EBSCO, from database inception through February 2026. Cohort studies assessing PAHG injection for SUI were systematically reviewed. Random-effects models were used, heterogeneity was assessed with I2, and meta-regression examined age and follow-up as moderators. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test.Results:Thirteen studies, including 1797 patients, were analyzed. The pooled responder rate was 72% (95% confidence interval [CI] 64%–78%; I2 = 87%). A greater mean age was significantly associated with higher response rates (P = 0.04). Four studies reporting International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire outcomes demonstrated a pooled mean reduction of −6.65 points (95% CI: −8.52 to −4.78; I2 = 92.8%). The pooled repeat injection rate was 16% (95% CI: 10%–27%; I2 = 94%). Follow-up duration did not significantly influence retreatment rates (P = 0.85). No evidence of publication bias was detected for the primary outcome (P = 0.96).Conclusion:PAHG injection is a minimally invasive treatment associated with clinically meaningful symptom improvement and a favorable safety profile in women with SUI. However, given the single-arm design of the included studies and substantial heterogeneity, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

INTRODUCTION

Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is defined as the involuntary leakage of urine through the urethral meatus that occurs in response to increased intra-abdominal pressure.[1] Although pelvic floor muscle training remains the first-line therapy for SUI, many women require procedural or surgical intervention because of persistent symptoms.[23] Mid-urethral sling procedures are considered the gold standard due to high cure rates and long-term durability, but concerns about mesh-related complications have increased interest in less invasive alternatives.[45] Urethral bulking agent injection, particularly with polyacrylamide hydrogel (PAHG), has emerged as a minimally invasive outpatient option with good tolerability and promising short- to mid-term outcomes, although reported success rates vary and long-term effectiveness is still being evaluated.[6]

Urethral bulking with PAHG is a minimally invasive procedure that can be safely performed in an outpatient setting. Reported subjective success rates at 3-year follow-up are approximately 88.5%, which remain lower than those achieved with mid-urethral sling procedures (100%), and repeat injections are required in up to one-third of patients to maintain clinical benefit.[78] Nevertheless, despite comparatively lower cure rates than mid-urethral sling, PAHG injection demonstrates high patient satisfaction and is associated with a lower incidence of serious complications, supporting its role as a safe and less invasive alternative in appropriately selected women with SUI.[9]

Despite the increasing use of PAHG in clinical practice, the available literature remains heterogeneous in terms of study design, patient selection, outcome definitions, and duration of follow-up.[10] Reported success rates vary considerably across studies, and uncertainty persists regarding long-term durability, retreatment rates, and complication profiles.[11] Furthermore, discrepancies exist between subjective and objective outcome measures, making it challenging to establish a clear estimate of treatment effectiveness. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness and safety of PAHG in the management of female SUI.

METHODS

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and were prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420261328968).[1213] The authors did not use AI-assisted technologies, including large language learning models, in the preparation of the manuscript.

Search strategy

PubMed, ProQuest, EuroPMC, and EBSCO were searched from inception to February 2026. Search terms combined MeSH and free-text words: (“polyacrylamide hydrogel” OR Bulkamid OR “urethral bulking agent”) AND (“stress urinary incontinence” OR “urinary stress incontinence” OR incontinence). Screening and data extraction

All records identified through the literature search were imported into Rayyan, and duplicate entries were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers (H.T. and A.J.) to assess eligibility based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies deemed potentially relevant underwent full-text review to determine final inclusion. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, with consultation of a third reviewer (M.R.) when necessary.

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers using Microsoft Excel Version 16.88 (24081116) (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Extracted information included study characteristics (first author, publication year, country, center, and study design), participant characteristics (sample size, age, and proportion of MUI when reported), details of the intervention, and duration of follow-up. Clinical outcomes extracted comprised responder rate (cured or improved), changes in ICIQ scores, repeat injection rates, and adverse events. Definitions of treatment response (e.g., “cured” and “improved”) and the measurement tools used (e.g., PGI-I and subjective assessment) were extracted for each study. Adverse events were extracted as the number of patients experiencing at least one event and reported as proportions relative to the total study population. Individual adverse events were summarized descriptively as the number and percentage of affected patients across all included studies. When studies reported continuous outcomes as medians with interquartile ranges, these were converted to means and standard deviations using established formula methods.[14]

Quality assessment

Quality assessment was performed using the NIH scale for before–after (pre–post) studies without a control group.[15]

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Meta-analyses of proportions were conducted using generalized linear mixed models with logit transformation to account for the binomial distribution of events. Random-effects models were applied to incorporate between-study variability. Continuous outcomes were calculated using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation.

Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I2 statistic. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered to represent low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Tau-squared (τ2) was calculated to estimate the magnitude of between-study variance.

Meta-regression analyses were performed using mixed-effects models to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The proportion of heterogeneity explained by moderators was quantified using R2 statistics. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using leave-one-out methods to evaluate the influence of individual studies on pooled estimates. Publication bias for the primary outcome was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and formally evaluated using Egger’s linear regression test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

RESULTS

A total of 300 records were initially retrieved, of which 57 duplicates were excluded. The remaining 243 records underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in 216 exclusions. Subsequently, 26 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 13 studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria [Figure 1].

Study characteristics

A total of 13 cohort studies comprising 1797 women with SUI were included in the quantitative synthesis.[16171819202122232425262728] Sample sizes ranged from small single-center cohorts to larger multicenter series. The age of participants varied across studies, generally ranging from 28 to 97 years, with a body mass index (BMI) range from 18 to 47 kg/m2. Follow-up duration varied considerably, with most studies reporting outcomes at least 12 months. While the majority of studies enrolled women with pure SUI, some included patients with stress-predominant MUI [Table 1]. The quality of all before–after studies was rated as good [Supplementary Table 2]. Publication bias of the funnel plot did not demonstrate obvious asymmetry. Egger’s regression test showed no evidence of small-study effects (t = 0.05, P = 0.96) [Supplementary Figure 1].

Responder rate

Eleven studies reporting responder outcomes were included in the primary analysis.[1617182022232425262728] Definitions of responder status varied across studies and are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. The pooled responder rate under a random-effects model was 72% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 65%–78%, I2 = 87%, P < 0.001) [Figure 2]. Meta-regression analysis demonstrated that mean age was associated with responder rate (QM = 4.19, P = 0.041). Increasing mean age was associated with higher odds of treatment response (β = 0.073, 95% CI: 0.003–0.143, I2 = 80.7%, P < 0.001).

Repeat injection rate

Seven studies, including 1224 patients, reported repeat injection rates.[16171820222728] The pooled repeat injection rate was 16% (95% CI: 10%–27%, I2 = 94%, P < 0.001). Exploratory meta-regression analysis did not demonstrate a significant association between follow-up duration and retreatment rate (P = 0.85) [Figure 4].

Adverse effects

Across the 13 included cohort studies comprising 1797 women treated with PAHG, adverse events were generally infrequent and predominantly mild and transient in nature.[16171819202122232425262728] The most commonly reported complication was bladder emptying dysfunction (occurring in 108 of 1797 patients [6.01%]). Urinary tract infection was observed in 62 patients (3.45%), while acute urinary retention occurred in 54 patients (3%). Other reported adverse events included hematuria in 31 patients (1.72%) and nocturia in 28 patients (1.55%). Less frequent complications were urgency or urgency incontinence (0.5%), dysuria (0.44%), and injection-site pain (0.33%). Only one study reported a serious adverse event, namely a seizure occurring in one patient [Table 2].[26]

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first meta-analyses to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of PAHG for the treatment of female urinary incontinence. This analysis synthesizes data from 13 cohort studies comprising 1797 women treated with PAHG (Bulkamid®). Overall, PAHG injection was associated with clinical improvement, with a pooled responder rate of 72% and a clinically meaningful reduction in ICIQ symptom scores. Although this rate remains lower than that reported for mid-urethral sling procedures, the magnitude of symptom reduction (mean ICIQ improvement of −6.65 points) suggests clinically meaningful benefit.[29] Despite the consistent direction of effect across studies, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed in several outcomes, including responder rate (I2 = 87%), ICIQ improvement (I2 = 92.8%), and repeat injection rate (I2 = 94%). The observed heterogeneity is likely multifactorial. First, differences in patient populations may have contributed, as several studies included women with pure SUI, while others included stress-predominant MUI. Second, clinical heterogeneity may arise from variation in disease status, including primary versus recurrent cases. Third, procedural differences such as injection technique, injected volume, and operator experience were not standardized across studies. Although subgroup analyses based on these clinically relevant factors would be valuable to better explain heterogeneity, such analyses were not feasible in the present study. Several included studies reported subgroup data (e.g., pure vs. MUI, primary versus recurrent cases, and first versus repeated injections), but these data were inconsistently reported and limited to a small number of studies for each outcome, precluding robust quantitative subgroup meta-analysis. Meta-regression analysis suggested that increasing mean age was associated with higher response rates; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution, given the borderline statistical significance (P = 0.041). This result indicates a possible association rather than a definitive effect. As meta-regression analyses are based on study-level data, the observed relationship may be subject to ecological fallacy and may not reflect individual-level associations. This finding may be associated with differences in patient expectations, baseline severity, or pathophysiological mechanisms in older women.[3031]

Reported responder rates were derived from studies with heterogeneous follow-up durations, ranging from short-term assessments (3–12 months) to extended follow-up beyond 24 months.[16171819202122232425262728] The pooled estimate represents an aggregate measure of effectiveness across varying time points rather than a standardized long-term outcome. Uleri et al. reported that PAHG injections are an effective and safe treatment option for women with SUI or stress-predominant MUI, demonstrating significant improvement at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months (P < 0.001).[25] Similarly, Lamblin et al. found that urethral bulking with Bulkamid® is a minimally invasive and effective procedure, yielding sustained positive clinical outcomes at 2 years of follow-up.[27] Furthermore, Giammò A et al. confirmed that urethral bulking is a highly safe procedure, reporting a patient satisfaction rate of 69.9%, further supporting its favorable safety and acceptability profile.[23]

Beyond objective efficacy, treatment preference also plays an important role in clinical decision-making. Emerging evidence suggests that women with SUI may favor treatment options that offer a more favorable safety profile, even if associated with comparatively lower efficacy, over more invasive procedures that carry a higher risk of adverse events.[32] Consistent with this, several studies reported that most patients expressed a preference for Bulkamid® compared with mid-urethral sling, autologous fascial sling, and colposuspension procedures.[2833] Brosche et al. evaluated 388 patients treated with PAHG and reported that 65.2% were cured or improved at 7-year follow-up, representing the longest follow-up period currently available in the literature. Notably, 74 patients (19%) subsequently underwent an alternative surgical procedure for SUI.[22]

The pooled repeat injection rate of 16% observed in this meta-analysis should be interpreted in the context of substantial clinical heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 94%). Follow-up duration varied widely, ranging from 3 months to more than 80 months, and retreatment strategies differed considerably between centers.[22] In several studies, repeat injections were performed as part of a staged management approach or in cases of partial response rather than definitive treatment failure.[162022] Consequently, repeat injection does not uniformly indicate loss of efficacy but may instead reflect individualized treatment optimization. Notably, larger contemporary cohorts have reported moderate retreatment rates despite longer follow-up, supporting the durability of PAHG while acknowledging that a subset of patients may require additional injections to achieve or maintain optimal outcomes.[172227]

The procedure demonstrated a generally favorable safety profile, with a modest repeat injection rate (16%). The overall incidence of adverse events was low to moderate, and most were mild and transient in nature. The most frequently reported complications included bladder emptying dysfunction, urinary tract infection, and transient lower urinary tract symptoms. Acute urinary retention was observed in a small proportion of patients and was typically managed conservatively with temporary catheterization. Importantly, serious adverse events were rare, with only one study reporting a seizure in a single patient. Compared with mesh-based sling procedures, which have been associated with mesh exposure, chronic pain, and revision surgery, PAHG injection appears to carry a lower risk of serious long-term complications.[3435] However, the results should be interpreted with caution given the single-arm design of the included studies and the substantial heterogeneity observed across analyses, which likely reflects differences in patient populations, follow-up duration, and adverse event reporting. A recent review by Sikora et al. reported that, although uncommon, certain adverse events associated with urethral bulking agents were serious, including allergic reactions, material migration, tissue erosion, fistula formation, sterile abscesses, and infections, with injection-site rupture identified as the most severe complication.[36]

Clinical implications

These findings support the role of PAHG injection as a valuable treatment option for women seeking a minimally invasive alternative to sling surgery, particularly those with contraindications to mesh procedures or a preference for outpatient treatment. While cure rates may be lower than mid-urethral sling procedures, the favorable safety profile and clinically meaningful symptom improvement make PAHG an appropriate option in carefully selected patients.

Limitations

This analysis has several limitations. First, all included studies were observational cohort designs, and no randomized controlled trials were available. Second, the meta-analysis was based on single-arm studies without a comparator group, limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding relative efficacy or safety compared with other treatment modalities. Third, substantial heterogeneity was observed across multiple outcomes, which limits the interpretability and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Fourth, reporting of adverse events and clinical outcomes was inconsistent across studies, which may have influenced the pooled results. Fifth, follow-up duration varied considerably, limiting conclusions regarding long-term durability. Although clinically relevant subgroup analyses (e.g., pure vs. MUI, disease severity, primary versus recurrent cases, and procedural variations) could have helped explain heterogeneity, they were not feasible due to inconsistent and limited reporting across studies, preventing assessment of differential treatment effects and limiting clinical applicability.

CONCLUSION

PAHG injection provides clinically meaningful symptom improvement with a favorable safety profile in appropriately selected women with SUI, particularly in the context of increasing concerns regarding mesh-related complications. Although a subset of patients may require repeat injections, the overall incidence of serious complications remains low. Despite heterogeneity in follow-up duration and retreatment strategies, the direction of effect was consistent across studies, suggesting a potential beneficial effect, although the robustness of this finding is limited by substantial heterogeneity across studies. Further high-quality comparative studies with standardized outcome reporting, subgroup analyses, and long-term follow-up are warranted to better define the durability, clinical utility, comparative effectiveness, and optimal patient selection of PAHG injection.
