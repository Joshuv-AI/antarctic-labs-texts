Unraveling the pathogenic potential, virulence traits, and antibiotic resistance genes of multidrug-resistant Streptococcus agalactiae strains retrieved from Nile tilapia
Authors: Abdelazeem M Algammal, Mahmoud Mabrok, Bian K Almessiry, Banan Atwah, Amenah S Al-otaibi, Yehia S Mohamed, Sinclair Steele, Mohamed Enany, Geraldine B Dayrit, Fatma M Yousseff, Marwa E Abo Hashem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStreptococcus agalactiae is implicated in severe infections in humans and causes considerable financial losses in the aquaculture industry, particularly in tilapia farming. Given its dual threat to public health and food security, this study was conducted to comprehensively assess the prevalence, molecular identification, multidrug resistance (MDR) patterns, virulence traits, antimicrobial resistance genes, and pathogenic potential of S. agalactiae strains isolated from Oreochromis niloticus. Notably, this study highlights the detection of critical virulence genes (cfb, hylB, lmb, and cylE), which play essential roles in host invasion and immune evasion. In parallel, the presence of resistance determinants such as pbp1A, aac(6’)-aph(2’’), tetM or tetK, and ermB or ermA underscores the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance in aquatic environments.MethodsA total of 180 Oreochromis niloticus samples—comprising 90 apparently healthy and 90 moribund fish—were collected from private fish farms in Ismailia, Egypt. Clinical and postmortem (PM) examinations, along with bacteriological analysis, were subsequently performed. The recovered isolates were then subjected to molecular identification via 16 S rRNA gene amplification, antibiotic susceptibility testing via standard protocols, and PCR-based screening for key virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes.ResultsAll recovered Streptococcus agalactiae isolates were confirmed by the presence of the species-specific 16 S rRNA gene. The overall prevalence of S. agalactiae among the examined O. niloticus was 10% (18/180), with the brain identified as the primary target organ for infection. PCR analysis revealed that the most frequently detected virulence-associated genes among the isolates were cfb (100%), hylB (82.1%), lmb (78.5%), and cylE (57.1%). Additionally, 35.7% of the S. agalactiae isolates exhibited multidrug resistance (MDR) to seven different antimicrobial classes and harbored resistance genes including pbp1A, aac(6’)-aph(2’’), tetM, and ermB. Another 17.8% of the isolates were MDR to six antimicrobial classes and carried the pbp1A, tetM, and ermA genes. Pathogenicity assays demonstrated a strong correlation between the presence of virulence genes and the observed mortality rates in experimentally infected fish. Notably, mortality sharply increased within the first 7 days post-inoculation, reaching approximately 73.3%, underscoring the high virulence potential of the recovered strains.ConclusionBriefly, this study highpoints the development of MDR S. agalactiae in O. niloticus, affirming a public health risk. The emerging MDR S. agalactiae strains in O. niloticus frequently harbor the cfb, hylB, lmb, and cylE virulence genes, and the pbp1A, aac(6’)aph(2’’), tetM or tetK, and ermB or ermA resistance genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04405-6.Keywords: MDR S. agalactiae, Oreochromis niloticus, Virulence genes, Multidrug resistance patterns, Pathogenicity

Background

Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) has become one of the most significant aquatic species worldwide, with production now extending across nearly 100 countries. The rapid commercialization and continued growth of the tilapia industry have propelled O. niloticus to its current position as the second most important farmed fish globally, surpassed only by carp and ranking ahead of the salmonid family [1]. In Egypt, Nile tilapia farming has expanded significantly, accounting for the majority of the total fish yield [2]. Alarmingly, the infectious agents responsible for streptococcosis not only affect aquatic species but can also spread to humans and terrestrial animals, posing a global health risk [3]. Among these pathogens, Streptococcus agalactiae (Lancefield Group B) has evolved as a leading etiological agent, posing serious risks to aquaculture industries worldwide. It is increasingly implicated in large-scale outbreaks characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates in both freshwater and marine fish species, especially under elevated water temperatures [4].

Recent reports have documented catastrophic tilapia mortalities associated with S. agalactiae infections in several regions, including Taiwan [5], Bangladesh [6], India [7], Thailand [8, 9], and Egypt [10–12]. Among farmed Oreochromis species, S. agalactiae is recognized as the predominant bacterial pathogen, accounting for over 50% of isolates recovered from diseased fish [13]. Clinically, infected fish often develop severe systemic infections, presenting with meningoencephalitis and septicaemia. The disease is highly transmissible and is associated with substantial mortality rates, particularly when water temperatures approach or exceed 30 °C [14].

S. agalactiae is a Gram-positive, non-motile, and non-spore-forming bacterium [15]. Morphologically, it typically appears as pairs or chains of cocci. It is classified within Lancefield Group B on the basis of its surface antigens [16]. The severity of streptococcal infections is closely linked to several virulence factors, including specific virulence genes and environmental conditions such as water temperature [17, 18]. Key virulence determinants include genes associated with invasion (hylE), immune evasion and toxin production (cylE) [9, 19, 20]. One particularly important virulence factor is the CAMP factor, encoded by the cfb gene, which promotes GBS pathogenesis by facilitating the formation of distinct pores on host cell membranes and binding to GPI-anchored proteins, ultimately leading to cell disruption [21, 22]. Despite the widespread application of antibiotics to prevent and control streptococcal infections, the indiscriminate use of these agents has accelerated the development of antimicrobial-resistant strains [23–26]. Increasing concerns have been raised over the pervasive and sustained use of antimicrobials in aquaculture, which not only threatens aquatic animal health but also facilitates the potential transmission of resistant “superbugs” across terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems [27–31]. This critical knowledge gap highlights the pressing need for extensive surveillance efforts and detailed molecular investigations to inform more effective control and management strategies [23, 32–34]. In light of the scarce data concerning S. agalactiae outbreaks in cultured tilapia across Egypt, this work assessed the prevalence of infections, characterized the phenotypic resistance profiles of isolated strains, and identified key virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes. By addressing these gaps, research has sought to enhance our understanding of S. agalactiae epidemiology and contribute to improved disease prevention and management strategies in Egyptian aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, clinical, and PM examinations

Approximately 180 Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), weighing 130 ± 5 g, were obtained from private fish farms in Ismailia, Egypt, from June to September 2024. The required sample size was determined using the single-proportion formula: N = Z2p (1 − p)/d2, with a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96). The collected fish (apparently healthy, on the basis of the clinical inspection: n = 90 and moribund fish: n = 90) were transmitted in iceboxes to the laboratory. The fish were euthanized by immersion in an overdose of buffered tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222, 300 mg/L) until opercular movement ceased, following the guidelines established by the American Veterinary Medical Association for the euthanasia of animals [35]. The clinical and PM inspections were performed in accordance with Austin and Austin [36]. Likewise, samples (liver, kidney, brain, and eye) were collected for bacteriological examination.

Isolation and identification of S. agalactiae

A loopful from each sample was streaked onto Edwards medium and 5% sheep blood agar (Oxoid, UK) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Definitive identification was performed depending on the culture characteristics, Gram’s staining, and biochemical characters (catalase test, oxidase test, hemolysis on 5% sheep blood agar, and esculin hydrolysis) [37]. The CAMP reaction was used for the reliable identification of S. agalactiae [38]. Besides, the identification was verified using PCR detection of a species-specific set of primers (Table 1) targeting the 16S rRNA gene of S. agalactiae [39].

Antibiogram of S. agalactiae isolates

The disc diffusion assay was implemented on Mueller‒Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK) supplemented with 5% defibrinated sheep blood (MH‒F agar) and incubated at 35 ± 1 °C with 5% CO₂ according to the CLSI guidelines [50]. Ten antimicrobials were utilized including trimethoprim-sulphamethoxazole (SXT, 25 µg), tetracycline (TE, 30 µg), florfenicol (FLO, 30 µg), enrofloxacin (ENR, 5 µg), gentamycin (CN, 10 µg), erythromycin (E, 15 µg), imipenem (IPM, 10 µg), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (AMC, 30 µg), cefotaxime (CTX, 30 µg), and ampicillin (AMP, 10 µg) (Oxoid, UK). Additionally, the isolated S. agalactiae strains were recognized as multidrug-resistant (MDR) according to Magiorakos et al. [51]. The MAR index was subsequently estimated in accordance with the methods of Krumperman [52].

Investigation of virulence and resistance genes via PCR

PCR was used to examine the occurrence of virulence (cylE, lmb, cfb, and hylB) and antibiotic resistance genes (pbp 1 A, aac(6’) aph(2”), ermA, ermB, tetK, and tetM) in the retrieved S. agalactiae strains. The DNA was extracted via a DNA isolation kit (Norgen Biotek, Ontario, Canada). Moreover, negative controls (DNA-free) and positive control strains, obtained from the Animal Health Research Institute (AHRI), Egypt were included. Subsequently, agarose gel electrophoresis (1.5%) was carried out. The sequences of primers used (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) and their corresponding amplicons are presented in Table 1. Moreover, the PCR cycling conditions are illustrated in the supplementary table (Table S1).

Pathogenicity assay

A total of 90 apparently healthy O. niloticus (average weight: 61 ± 0.4 g) were obtained from a commercial aquaculture facility in Ismailia, Egypt. Prior to the experiment, the fish were confirmed to be free from external lesions and clinical signs of disease. They were acclimated for seven days in a 1000-L fiberglass tank containing filtered, dechlorinated, and aerated freshwater maintained at 28 °C. After acclimation, 60 clinically healthy and behaviorally active fish that exhibited no signs of infection, were randomly allocated into two experimental groups. Each group comprised three replicates, with each replicate maintained in a 100-L glass aquarium with a 10 fish holding capacity. Group 1 (G1, negative control) was intraperitoneally (IP) injected with 100 µl of PBS. Group 2 (G2, the challenged group) was injected with 100 µl of S. agalactiae (strain S9, isolated from the brain), which was isolated in the present study, at a concentration of 5 × 10⁸ CFU/ml. The bacterial suspension was standardized using a McFarland turbidity scale, following the protocol described by Zhang et al. [53]. The strain used was previously identified as highly virulent, carrying the cfb, hylB, lmb, and cylE virulence genes. The fish were observed for 14 days post-inoculation, and mortality was recorded. Dead fish were collected immediately and examined aseptically to fulfill Koch’s postulates and confirm pathogen re-isolation.

Statistical analyses

The data obtained were analyzed with the chi-square test using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute, USA), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. In addition, a correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between the detected antibiotic resistance genes and the antimicrobial agents, utilizing R software (version 4.0.2; https://www.r-project.org/).

Results

Clinical and PM findings

Clinical examination of the affected fish revealed prominent bilateral exophthalmia (Fig. 1a), red eyes, mild abdominal distension, anal prolapse, and noticeable erosion of the caudal fins (Fig. 1b). Additional external signs included multifocal hemorrhages across the body surface, scale detachment, and erythema at the base of the fins (Fig. 1c). Upon necropsy, internal abnormalities, including a friable and discolored liver, a congested kidney (Fig. 1d), hemorrhagic gills, and the accumulation of serous exudate within the body cavity (Fig. 1e), were evident.

O. niloticus naturally infected with S. agalactiae exhibit (a) pronounced bilateral exophthalmia; (b) reddened eyes, mild abdominal distension, anal prolapse, and erosion of the caudal fins; (c) multifocal hemorrhages on the skin, scale loss, and erythema at the fin bases; (d) friable, discolored liver and congested kidneys; and (e) hemorrhagic gills and accumulation of serous exudate within the body cavity

Phenotypic characteristics and the prevalence of S. agalactiae in O. niloticus

The recovered S. agalactiae isolates were Gram-positive non-motile cocci. On Edwards medium, the colonies appeared small and bluish, while on blood agar they exhibited pinpoint β-hemolysis. Biochemically, they tested positive for the CAMP test. Conversely, they tested negative for oxidase, esculin hydrolysis and catalase tests. Additionally, all the retrieved S. agalactiae strains were positive for the 16 S rRNA gene. The prevalence of S. agalactiae in the examined O. niloticus was 10%, as 56 S. agalactiae strains were obtained from only 18 moribund O. niloticus, as illustrated in the supplementary Table S2. With respect to the distribution of S. agalactiae among the examined organs, the prevalence rates were 32.1% in the brain, 26.7% in the liver, 23.2% in the kidney, and 17.8% in the eye, indicating greater affinity of the pathogen for neural tissues (Table 2; Fig. 2). The statistical analysis revealed a nonsignificant difference (p < 0.05) in the distribution of S. agalactiae in the examined organs.

Susceptibility profiles of the recovered S. agalactiae strains from O. niloticus

The obtained S. agalactiae strains were resistant to tetracycline (96.4%), ampicillin (87.5%), cefotaxime and erythromycin (85.7%), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (80.3%), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (80.3%), and gentamycin (78.6%). In contrast, the tested S. agalactiae strains were highly sensitive to florfenicol and imipenem (100%), and enrofloxacin (94.6%) (Table 3; Fig. 3). The resistance of S. agalactiae strains to the tested antibiotics significantly differed (p < 0.05).

Virulence and resistance genes of S. agalactiae

The most prominent virulence genes associated with the retrieved S. agalactiae strains were the cfb (100%), hylB (82.1%), lmb (78.5%), and cylE (57.1%) genes. Besides, the tested S. agalactiae strains inherited the pbp1A, aac(6’)aph(2’’), tetM, ermB, tetK and ermA resistance genes with a percentages of 87.5%, 78.6%, 53.5%, 44.6%, 42.8% and 41.1%, respectively (Table 4; Fig. 4). In this study, a significant variance (p < 0.05) was recorded in the frequency of virulence and resistance genes of S. agalactiae isolates.

Multidrug-resistance profiles of S. agalactiae strains retrieved from O. niloticus

In this study, 35.7% (20/56) of the S. agalactiae strains were MDR to 7 classes and carried the pbp1A, aac(6’)aph(2’’), tetM, and ermB genes. Besides, 17.8% (10/56) of the S. agalactiae strains were MDR to 6 classes and harbor the pbp1A, tetM, and ermA genes. Additionally, 14.3% (8/56) of S. agalactiae strains were MDR to 6 classes and encoded the pbp1A, aac(6’)aph(2’’), and tetK genes. In addition, 14.3% (8/56) of S. agalactiae strains were MDR to 4 classes and had pbp1A, aac(6’)aph(2’’), ermA, and tetK genes. Besides, 8.9% (5/56) of S. agalactiae strains were MDR to 6 classes and encoded the aac(6’)aph(2’’), ermB, and tetK genes (Table 5 and Fig. 5). The MAR index values of the MDR S. agalactiae isolates ranged from 0.40 to 0.70, exceeding the 0.20 threshold, and indicating that these strains originated from environments at high-risk of contamination. Statistically, positive correlations were detected between the pbp1A gene and AMP (r = 1), the aac(6’)aph(2”) gene and CN (r = 1), ermB and E (r = 0.37), ermA and E (r = 0.34), and tetM and TE (r = 0. 21); tetK and TE (r = 0. 17), as described in Fig. 5.

Pathogenicity assay

Over the 14-day post-challenge period, cumulative mortality was censored among the experimentally infected fish. The lesions in the tested group were consistent with those detected in naturally infected O. niloticus, confirming the pathogenic potential of the S. agalactiae strain used. Notably, a sharp increase in mortality, reaching approximately 73.3% occurred within the first seven days post-inoculation, highlighting the rapid disease progression associated with this virulent strain. In contrast, the negative control group (G1) remained clinically normal, with no observed mortality or pathological abnormalities throughout the trial (Fig. 6). Re-isolation of S. agalactiae from internal organs fulfilled Koch’s postulates, confirming the causal role of the pathogen in the observed disease outcomes.

Discussion

Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus, GBS) has gained recognition as a prominent aquatic pathogen, particularly affecting warm-water species such as Oreochromis niloticus (Nile tilapia), where it contributes to substantial economic losses driven by elevated morbidity and mortality rates [54]. Clinical assessments frequently reveal gross pathological lesions indicative of septicemia in agreement with the findings of Preenanka et al. [7]. Likewise, extensive necrosis, degenerative alterations, and internal abnormalities were the predominant lesions identified upon necropsy, primarily affecting the brain, kidney, and liver. Consistent observations have also been documented [55, 56]. The signs observed in this study are attributed primarily to virulence factors and inherited genes expressed by the bacterium [9, 18].

Herein, the recovered isolates presented consistent and uniform traits, allowing their identification as Streptococcus agalactiae. These results are in agreement with those reported in previous studies [10, 57]. Moreover, the biochemical profiles of the isolates were largely comparable to those documented by Barrow and Feltham [37], and Jin et al. [38], further supporting their classification. The prevalence of S. agalactiae was 10%, which nearly agreed with the findings of Duodu et al. [58], who retrieved the pathogen from tilapia at a prevalence of 10.66%. However, this prevalence is notably lower than those reported by Kannika et al. [54] and Kelany et al. [59], who reported that S. agalactiae accounted for 41.6% and 30.9%, respectively. The increased susceptibility of Egyptian tilapia to streptococcosis is attributed to several interrelated factors. The concentration of aquaculture activities in the northern governorates provides ideal conditions for tilapia growth, including abundant water and optimal temperatures (28–32 °C) [60]. However, elevated temperatures during the hot season can increas stress and suppress immune responses, augmenting vulnerability to Streptococcus infections [61, 62]. Additionally, poor management practices such as overcrowding and low-quality feed, further predispose fish to disease [63]. This organotropism is constant with previous reports [64, 65]. S. agalactiae is known to have the capacity to breach the blood-brain barrier, causing meningoencephalitis in teleost fish [66]. Pathological findings typically begin within blood vessels, where bacterial aggregates and exotoxins disseminate, resulting in pronounced lesions in key organs [67, 68]. Furthermore, S. agalactiae demonstrates a marked tropism for the brain, eyes, and heart, with the optic nerve serving as a conduit for bacterial migration between the brain and ocular tissues [69, 70]. These findings highlight the pathogen’s invasive capabilities and the systemic nature of streptococcosis in affected fish.

Relying solely on conventional methods for the identification of Streptococcus species may be inadequate, as these bacteria can display uncommon phenotypic characteristics, and the interpretation of such results is often time-consuming and prone to ambiguity [71]. Therefore, selecting an appropriate molecular assay is crucial for the reliable diagnosis of S. agalactiae. In this context, all the isolates were confirmed using species-specific gene amplification, which produced distinct amplicons of approximately 405 bp. The 16S rRNA gene has been widely employed for the identification and molecular characterization of both S. agalactiae and S. iniae, offering enhanced specificity and reliability over traditional phenotypic assays [72].

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a serious global health risk, significantly undermining the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions and facilitating the persistence and spread of infectious agents. In this study, florfenicol, imipenem, and enrofloxacin exhibited a potent antimicrobial activity against the retrieved S. agalactiae strains, which aligns with the findings of Chu et al. [73]. The observed multidrug resistance patterns are significant, and relating them to local antibiotic use provides valuable context. In major Egyptian tilapia farming areas, antibiotics and disinfectants are occasionally applied through feed or water following disease outbreaks, although fewer than one-third of farms report such practices [74]. Nationally, Egypt uses over 110 tonnes of antibiotics annually in finfish aquaculture—primarily tetracyclines, along with beta-lactams and quinolones—which likely contributes to the development of resistance [75]. Similarly, Muthanna et al. [76] reported the universal susceptibility of S. agalactiae isolates from cage-cultured red hybrid tilapia to a range of antibiotics, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of these agents. Among them, enrofloxacin, a widely used third-generation fluoroquinolone in aquaculture, exerts its antibacterial effect by targeting the DNA gyrase A subunit, thereby disrupting the enzymatic processes essential for bacterial DNA supercoiling, cleavage, and relegation, ultimately arresting DNA replication [77]. Alarm resistance levels were detected among the current isolates against several commonly used antimicrobials, which is consistent with the finding of a previous report [10], highlighting a trend of multidrug resistance in S. agalactiae circulating in aquaculture environments. This escalating resistance underscores the critical need for judicious antibiotic stewardship, continuous resistance surveillance, and the development of alternative disease control strategies to safeguard both aquatic animal health and public health.

In this study, the distribution of the virulence genes among the retrieved isolates was in agreement with that reported by Sudpraseart et al. [5] and Syuhada et al. [78]. These genes encode critical virulence determinants implicated in the pathogenic mechanisms of streptococcal infections [79]. Notably, the cfb gene, which is responsible for encoding the CAMP factor, a pore-forming toxin that induces red blood cell lysis was detected in 100% of the isolates. This consistent presence supports its utility as a phenotypic marker for the identification of S. agalactiae [80], reinforcing its diagnostic and epidemiological importance. The global propagation of MDR S. agalactiae strains has imposed a substantial disease burden on aquaculture production systems. In the present study, a significant proportion of the identified S. agalactiae isolates presented MDR profiles spanning 4–7 antimicrobial classes, frequently harboring resistance determinants such as pbp 1 A, aac(6’)aph(2’’), tetM, ermA, tetK, and ermB. The detection of the tetM gene aligns with previous reports [81, 82], and underscores its global dissemination—largely facilitated by its association with bacterial plasmids [83]. Our initial analysis assessed tetM and tetK separately, which resulted in relatively low r-values (0.21 and 0.17, respectively). When considered together, however, 54 of the 56 resistant isolates carried at least one of these genes, whereas all susceptible or intermediate isolates lacked both. This demonstrates a much stronger, near-perfect association between gene presence and the tetracycline resistance phenotype. Notably, tetracycline resistance genes persist in aquaculture environments long after the cessation of antibiotic use, reflecting their environmental stability and potential for long-term horizontal gene transfer [84]. Tetracycline resistance in Streptococcus species is primarily mediated by ribosomal protection and efflux pump genes, notably tetO, tetM, tetS, and tetK, which contribute to reduced antibiotic efficacy and increased persistence of resistant strains [85].

The challenge trial demonstrated substantial mortality among experimentally infected fish, with clinical signs closely mirroring those observed in natural outbreaks. These outcomes fulfill Koch’s postulates and are consistent with the prior report [54]. The acute mortality of O. niloticus within seven days post-inoculation was likely driven by the action of bacterial toxins, hydrolytic enzymes, and virulence-associated genes, all of which contribute to extensive tissue degeneration and cellular disruption [9, 86, 87]. The extensive destruction of critical blood components, accompanied by perivascular edema and thrombosis of the portal vasculature, likely constitutes a central pathogenic mechanism underlying the observed mortalities associated with this pathogen [88].

Study limitations

More robust confirmatory tools, such as multilocus sequence typing (MLST) or whole-genome sequencing, should be employed to increase confidence in species-level identification. Likewise, the detection of pbp 1 A alone is insufficient to confirm a link with the β-lactam resistance phenotype; therefore, further studies investigating mutations in pbp 1 A, pbp2X, and pbp2B are needed.

Conclusion

This investigation highlighted the occurrence of MDR S. agalactiae strains in O. niloticus. The S. agalactiae strains retrieved from O. niloticus are usually multi-virulent and frequently harbor the cfb, hylB, lmb, and cylE virulence genes. Interestingly, the S. agalactiae strains obtained from O. niloticus exhibited multidrug resistance to 4–7 antibiotic classes and commonly carried the pbp1A, aac(6’)aph(2’’), tetM or tetK, and ermB or ermA resistance genes. Notably, imipenem, florfenicol, and enrofloxacin exhibited promising antimicrobial activities against the obtained S. agalactiae strains. The integration of conventional and molecular assays provides a realistic approach for identifying S. agalactiae in O. niloticus.Unfortunately, the propagation of MDR S. agalactiae strains in O. niloticus highlights a crucial public health concern.
