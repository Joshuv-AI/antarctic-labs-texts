Stereodivergent total synthesis of C5/C6-oxygenated angucyclines: stereochemical reassignment and antitumor activities
Authors: Yuting Cheng, Zihao Dai, Peng Xu, Yingjie Wang, Biao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A unified total synthesis of 16 stereochemically defined 5,6-diol-bearing tetrangomycin-type angucyclines, including PD-116740, TAN-1085, himalaquinone G, and their congeners, was achieved. The synthesis features an acid-catalyzed hydration and subsequent retro-Friedel–Crafts-type ring-opening of a strained cyclobutene precursor to rapidly elaborate the angucycline framework. A completely catalyst-controlled reduction via Noyori asymmetric transfer hydrogenation (ATH) was then implemented to elaborate the C5/C6 diol stereocenters, which overrides inherent substrate biases, precisely transforming a racemic α-hydroxy ketone into all four possible C5/C6 diol stereoisomers. Access to this complete stereoisomeric matrix enabled the stereochemical reassignment of himalaquinone G from the originally assigned cis-(5R,6S)-diol to trans-(5R,6R)-diol. Antitumor activity profiling identified the unnatural enantiomer of PD-116740 as a highly potent agent (IC50 = 0.24 µM against A375 melanoma cells), outperforming its natural counterparts.

Introduction

Angucyclines represent the largest family of type II aromatic polyketides.1,2 These natural products, predominantly produced by Streptomyces species, have attracted extensive research interest in isolation,3,4 biosynthesis,5 and total synthesis6 due to their rich structural diversity and remarkable biological activities. Within this family, the A-ring-aromatized 5,6-diol-bearing tetrangomycin-type angucyclines, such as himalaquinone G (1),7 PD-116740 (2),8 PM070747 (3)9 and TAN-1085 (4)10 (Fig. 1), are chemically robust, due to avoidance of A-ring cleavage common in other subclasses,11,12 and are renowned for their profound antibacterial, antitumor, and anti-angiogenic properties.3 The C5/C6 diols are usually trans-configured;8–10,13 however, himalaquinone G (1), isolated from Streptomyces sp. PU-MM59 collected in the Himalayan region, was assigned as a cis-5,6-diol, with its optical rotation value being opposite to those of its structural analogues.7 Moreover, himalaquinone G (1) exhibited potent cytotoxicity against the PC-3 prostate cancer cell line (IC50 = 0.32 µM) and A549 non-small-cell lung cancer cell line (IC50 = 1.88 µM), which is approximately an order of magnitude higher than that of its structural congeners, such as 2 and 3. Notably, despite lacking glycosidic modifications, its potency reaches the same order of magnitude as landomycin A, one of the most potent and complex angucyclines (IC50 = 0.2 µM against PC-3).7,14,15 This intriguing structural anomaly suggests that the stereochemistry of the C5 and C6 hydroxyl groups might exert a profound impact on biological activities, while the contiguous chiral C5/C6 diol motif presents a unique synthetic challenge.

A-ring-aromatized 5,6-diol-bearing tetrangomycin-type angucyclines.

Indeed, the intriguing chemical structures and potent biological activities exhibited by angucyclines have stimulated extensive synthetic efforts, highlighted by seminal contributions from the groups of Larsen,16 Hauser,17 Suzuki,18–20 Yu,21,22 Shi,23 Gao24 and Mong25 (Fig. 2). Suzuki and co-workers reported the first total syntheses of 2 and 4 by exploiting the axial chirality of biaryldialdehyde 5 to forge trans-diol 6 in up to 96% ee via an SmI2-mediated pinacol coupling (Fig. 2A).26 A related cyclization strategy was adapted by Shi and co-workers for the synthesis of 4 (up to 98% ee).23 Alternatively, Gao and co-workers developed a distinct strategy featuring the asymmetric dihydroxylation of an (E)-diarylalkene 7 to construct the acyclic threo-diol 8, followed by a Cu-promoted oxidative phenol coupling to close the B-ring (Fig. 2B).24 This approach has proven versatile, enabling the syntheses of 2,24 FD-594,27 and calixanthomycin A,28 and was recently employed by the Menche group in the synthesis of the CBS family natural products.29 For the synthesis of CBS72, Menche and co-workers utilized an asymmetric Davis oxidation followed by a chelation-controlled reduction with zinc borohydride to obtain the acyclic erythro-diol, which served as the precursor to the cyclic cis-diol. However, despite rigorous optimization, Davis oxidation only provided up to 84% ee, a limitation inextricably inherited by the final product during the subsequent substrate-directed reduction. To date, synthetic approaches toward these A-ring-aromatized 5,6-diol-bearing angucyclines rely on substrate-controlled stereochemistry and are predominantly restricted to the formation of trans-diols. This lack of modularity precludes the divergent synthesis of all C5/C6 stereoisomers from a unified intermediate, thereby falling short of the rigorous synthetic demands required for comprehensive SAR profiling. Furthermore, while the ketone–aryl cross-coupling employed by the Menche group offers an elegant fragment-assembly strategy, the requisite brominated polycyclic phenols are challenging to access, necessitating tedious oxidation state adjustments.24

These limitations prompted us to envision a tunable, stereodivergent synthetic approach. By employing a relay strategy that couples either achiral or asymmetric Davis oxidation with Noyori asymmetric transfer hydrogenation (ATH),30,31 we hypothesized that C5-ketone intermediate 9 could be converted into any desired C5/C6 diol stereoisomer. This approach would not only ensure precise stereocontrol but also rapidly generate pairs of separable diastereomers, streamlining the assembly of an extensive angucycline analogue library (Fig. 2C). Prioritizing overall synthetic conciseness, our retrosynthetic analysis initially traces the common tetracyclic angucycline scaffold back to the shared uncyclized intermediate 10 (Fig. 2D). We envisioned that 10 would arise from the strained 6/6/4-fused cyclobutene 11via an acid-catalyzed hydration and subsequent retro-Friedel–Crafts-type ring-opening sequence. The tricyclic 11 could be rapidly constructed through a straightforward intramolecular [2 + 2] cycloaddition of yne–allenone, derived from easily accessible 12.32,33

Results and discussion

Assembly of the common intermediate

For the synthesis of C8 O-methyl modified angucyclines, such as 1 and 2, the D-ring fragment 13 was readily prepared from commercially available 2-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde via phenol triflation and a subsequent Sonogashira cross-coupling (see the SI).34 The A-ring fragment 14 was derived from commercial methyl 3-bromo-5-hydroxybenzoate through silyl protection of the phenol. The strategic deployment of a benzoate ester on the A-ring is pivotal for late-stage divergence; this ester can be reduced stepwise to yield various C3 substituents, such as hydroxymethyl or methyl groups, while also providing a versatile handle for potential late-stage derivatization.

Fragments 13 and 14 were united via a Sonogashira cross-coupling (Scheme 1), affording diaryl alkyne 15 in 87% yield. Subsequent Barbier-type propargylation of the aldehyde using propargyl bromide and zinc dust furnished homopropargylic alcohol 12 in 84% yield.35 Notably, oxidation of 12 with Dess–Martin periodinane (DMP) did not yield an isolable homopropargylic or allenic ketone.36–40 Instead, the initially generated homopropargylic ketone underwent in situ isomerization to the transient allenone 16,41–43 which was spontaneously intercepted by the tethered diaryl alkyne via an intramolecular yne–allenone [2 + 2] cycloaddition.32,33 Remarkably, this rapid cascade was proved to be highly scalable, directly providing the stable, isolable 6/6/4-fused tricyclic dienone 11 in 87% yield on a 20-gram scale.

Rapid assembly of the common intermediate ketone 9.

The critical hydration and ring-opening sequence of the strained cyclobutene moiety in 11 was optimized (see Table S1 in the SI). Thus, the transformation proceeded efficiently utilizing a catalytic amount (5 mol%) of hydrochloric acid in acetonitrile containing 1 vol% water, delivering 10 in 93% yield on a 5.0-gram scale. Both the water content and the strength of the protic acid were proved to be critical parameters; precisely 1 vol% water in acetonitrile was optimal, with deviations leading to diminished yields. Interestingly, when a milder protic acid such as trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was employed, the hydrated intermediate, benzocyclobutanol 17, could be observed in the crude 1H NMR spectrum. This intermediate is derived from an acid-catalyzed 1,6-conjugate addition of water to dienone 11.38,39,44 Driven by the thermodynamic release of cyclobutane ring strain, this intermediate underwent a retro-Friedel–Crafts fragmentation to furnish naphthol 10.45,46 This cascade sequence provides a robust method for converting 6/6/4-fused tricyclic dienones into the angucycline scaffold. Subsequent hypervalent iodine-mediated para-dearomatization of the phenol with phenyliodine bis(trifluoroacetate) (PIFA) in the presence of methanol, followed by tert-butyldimethylsilyl (TBS) protection, afforded the desired common intermediate 9 in 82% yield.

Stereodivergent synthesis of the C5/C6 diols

We initially explored chiral Davis oxaziridines for the asymmetric α-hydroxylation of the C5 ketone in 9 (Scheme 2A).30,47 Following extensive optimization (see Table S2 in the SI), we found that complete enolization of 9 with an excess of the sterically hindered base LiHMDS (1.5 equiv.), followed by treatment with (1S)-(+)-(camphorsulfonyl)oxaziridine ((S)-18, 2.0 equiv.), could install the C6 hydroxy group with excellent stereoselectivity, providing (S)-19 in 86% yield and 91.8% ee. To determine the absolute configuration, the corresponding enantiomer (R)-19, synthesized using (R)-18, was subjected to the modified Mosher's method. By preparing both the (S)- and (R)-MTPA esters (22a and 22b) and comparing their 1H chemical shift differences (ΔδS–R), we established the (R)-configuration at C6 for this enantiomer.48,49 Notably, this is opposite to the absolute configuration typically found at C6 in the majority of the tetrangomycin-type natural products. Consequently, the major product from the forward sequence, (S)-19, was confidently assigned the natural (S)-C6 stereochemistry.

Catalyst-controlled divergent synthesis of the C5/C6 diol stereoisomers.

Then, we investigated the stereoselective reduction of the C5 ketone. Direct reduction of (S)-19 with NaBH4 proceeded under chelation control, directed by the adjacent C6–OH, predominantly affording the erythro-diol (5R,6S)-20 in 85% yield with a diastereomeric ratio (d.r.) of 16 : 1. Conversely, protection of the C6–OH as a bulky TBS ether shifted the reaction to a sterically controlled Felkin–Anh addition, smoothly reversing the stereoselectivity to furnish the threo-isomer (5S,6S)-21 in 83% yield (d.r. = 12 : 1).50,51 Advanced intermediates derived from 21 ultimately matched the spectroscopic and chiroptical data of natural 2, providing definitive corroboration of our initial absolute configuration assignments. While this sequence of asymmetric Davis oxidation and substrate-directed reduction successfully validated our synthetic route, it exposed significant limitations during the broader SAR campaign. Specifically, the asymmetric Davis oxidation proved highly sensitive to subtle variations in reaction conditions, exhibiting a remarkably narrow operational window for maintaining high yields and ee values.47 This resulted in poor reproducibility, with the enantiopurity fluctuating significantly across different batches. More critically, because the subsequent substrate-directed reduction relies entirely on the pre-existing C6 stereocenter, the final C5/C6 diol products inextricably inherited these stereochemical erosions.

A more robust methodology capable of reliably generating all four C5/C6 stereoisomers (i.e., the (5R,6R), (5R,6S), (5S,6R), and (5S,6S) configurations) was imperative for comprehensively mapping the SAR profile of these angucyclines (Scheme 2B). Consequently, we pivoted to a catalyst-controlled, stereodivergent relay strategy. The chiral oxidant was replaced with the achiral Davis reagent 23 (2-(phenylsulfonyl)-3-phenyloxaziridine),52 which cleanly converted 9 into the racemic α-hydroxy ketone rac-19. We then sought to leverage Noyori ATH to reduce and resolve this mixture.53 Although Ru(ii)-TsDPEN-η6-arene catalysts frequently promote dynamic kinetic resolution (DKR) in analogous α-hydroxy ketones,54–56 DKR was not observed in our system. Instead, the transformation proceeded via a completely catalyst-controlled reduction. In this process, the two enantiomers of the racemic substrate were independently and stereoselectively reduced to yield a pair of easily separable diastereomers.

During catalyst screening (see Table S3 in the SI), we discovered that Wills' tethered Ru(ii) catalyst significantly enhanced the reaction performance.57,58 Using (R,R)-Teth-TsDPEN RuCl ((R,R)-24) with a formic acid/triethylamine (1 : 1) hydrogen source in dichloromethane, rac-19 was smoothly converted into an equimolar, readily separable mixture of diastereomers (90% combined yield, d.r. = 1 : 1), comprising (5S,6S)-20 (98.7% ee) and (5S,6R)-20 (92.0% ee). Correspondingly, the enantiomeric catalyst (S,S)-24 afforded (5R,6R)-20 (96.6% ee) and (5R,6S)-20 (92.6% ee) under identical conditions. This complementary process provided highly efficient access to all four possible C5/C6 stereoisomers. We hypothesize that the absence of DKR and the superb stereoselectivity might stem from two primary factors. First, the tethered catalyst restricts the spatial orientation of the η6-arene ligand, creating a highly rigid chiral pocket that maximizes CH/π interactions to dominate the transition state.59 Second, the C6 hydroxy group likely forms an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the adjacent C8 methoxy moiety, effectively dampening its ability to engage in the intermolecular hydrogen bonding typically required for DKR. Furthermore, this relay strategy proved highly modular. Coupling the asymmetric Davis oxidation (using (S)-18) with Noyori ATH (using (S,S)-24) enabled the precise synthesis of a targeted single stereoisomer, affording (5R,6S)-20 in 78% yield and 97.5% ee.

Late-stage cyclization and structural revision of himalaquinone G

With ample quantities of stereochemically defined diol building blocks in hand, we advanced to the cyclization stage (Scheme 3). Starting with (5S,6S)-20, which possesses the prevailing natural configuration, a designed silylation/desilylation sequence selectively unmasked the phenolic hydroxyl groups to afford bisphenol 25.60 Subsequent intramolecular oxidative phenol coupling forged the B-ring. While such transformations often necessitate exhaustive optimization,24,61 our substrate smoothly underwent oxidative coupling in DMF utilizing stoichiometric Cu(OTf)2 and TMEDA (2.0 equiv.) as the ligand. This robust two-step sequence delivered the tetracyclic angucycline core 26 in an excellent 90% yield. To facilitate late-stage divergent modification of the C3 substituent, a stepwise reduction strategy was implemented. The ester was initially reduced with DIBAL-H at −20 °C to the hydroxymethyl derivative 27. Following acetylation of the primary alcohol, the electron-withdrawing nature of the acetyl group effectively differentiated its reactivity from that of the TBS-protected C5 and C6 benzylic alcohols.62 This distinction enabled a highly selective Pd/C-catalyzed hydrogenolysis, yielding the methyl derivative 28 in 79% yield over two steps. Pleasingly, subjection of 26–28 to a unified oxidation and deprotection sequence in 68–79% yield successfully furnished the corresponding congeners, including natural product 2 (11 steps, longest linear sequence, 8% overall yield). The analytical data for synthetic 2 perfectly matched those reported in the literature (see Tables S4 and S5 in the SI), again validating our initial stereochemical assignments.

Total synthesis of PD-116740 (2), himalaquinone G (1), and the stereochemically defined congeners.

Having validated this divergent late-stage modification sequence on the (5S,6S)-scaffold, we rapidly expanded the chemistry to the remaining (5R,6R), (5R,6S), and (5S,6R) stereoisomers. This efficiently assembled a library of 12 angucycline stereoisomers. As planned, this included the targeted synthesis of the purportedly cis-diol-bearing himalaquinone G (5R,6S)-1, achieved in 12 steps for the longest linear sequence with a 5% overall yield. Unexpectedly, the NMR data for the synthetic (5R,6S)-1 did not match the reported spectral data of the natural isolate (see Tables S6–S9 in the SI). Enabled by our complete stereoisomeric matrix, we rapidly screened the remaining library members. The NMR spectra of our synthetic trans-diastereomer were found to be completely superimposable with the natural data. Subsequent correlation of specific rotation values unambiguously revised the absolute configuration of himalaquinone G to trans-(5R,6R). Intriguingly, while the relative configuration is trans, its absolute configuration is antipodean to that of the majority of the documented natural angucyclines.13

We attribute the original literature misassignment to a common conformational pitfall. The isolation report relied solely on the presence of a NOESY correlation between H-5 and H-6 to assign a cis-relationship. However, as revealed by Clardy and co-workers in the X-ray crystallographic analysis of 2,8 the 5,6-dihydrobenz[a]anthraquinone core is not planar. The two trans-hydroxyl groups adopt a diaxial orientation with a dihedral angle of approximately 171°. In this preferred half-chair conformation, the trans-diequatorial H-5 and H-6 protons are forced into close spatial proximity, such as to generate a strong NOESY correlation.

Synthesis of TAN-1085 and biological evaluation

In contrast to the C8 O-methyl-angucyclines, other members of this family, such as TAN-1085 (4), bear a free D-ring phenol and an acid-sensitive O-linked deoxysugar moiety at C6. Recognizing that the harsh conditions required for late-stage demethylation would cleave the glycosidic bond,63 we developed a parallel sequence utilizing benzyl ether for the phenol protection (Scheme 4). By replacing the D-ring building block with its benzyl-protected counterpart 30, a similar five-step sequence afforded the corresponding α-hydroxy ketone 31 in 35% yield and 74% ee. Subsequently, TMSOTf-catalyzed glycosylation with l-rhodinosyl acetate 32 at −20 °C proceeded smoothly to furnish the optically pure 33 in 65% yield.23,64 To rapidly access C5 epimers, direct reduction of the ketone with NaBH4 yielded a nearly equimolar mixture of (5S) and (5R) diastereomers. Capitalizing on our established cyclization protocol, this diastereomeric mixture was advanced without separation. Notably, during the subsequent hydroxyl protection sequence, the benzoyl (Bz) group was tactically selected over silyl ether, which could be cleaved simultaneously with the A-ring ester during a later reductive step. Following desilylation and Cu(ii)-promoted B-ring closure, LiAlH4 reduction of the A-ring ester to a hydroxymethyl group synchronously removed the Bz protecting groups from both the aglycone and the sugar moiety. At this stage, the pronounced polarity difference between the (5S,6S) and (5R,6S) diastereomers allowed for facile chromatographic separation. Finally, naphthol oxidation mediated by phenyliodine diacetate (PIDA) and a subsequent Pd/C-catalyzed hydrogenolysis of the phenolic benzyl ether completed the total synthesis of (5S,6S)-4 (13 steps, longest linear sequence, 2% overall yield). Following this protocol, we generated all four possible C5/C6 epimers bearing the glycosidic modification at C3.

Total synthesis of TAN-1085 ((5S,6S)-4) and its C5/C6 epimers ((5R,6S), (5S,6R) and (5R,6R)-4).

With this stereochemically defined library of 16 angucyclines in hand, we evaluated their in vitro antiproliferative activities against four human cancer cell lines (Panc-1, HeLa, A375, and A549). An initial single-dose viability screening (see Fig. S2 in the SI) revealed that the trans-diols generally exhibit superior cytotoxicity compared to their cis-isomers. This preliminary evaluation also indicated that increasing the oxidation state and bulkiness at C3 significantly weakens the activity of the cis-diols, whereas altering the C3 substituent oxidation state exerts a minimal impact on the potency of the trans-diols. We subsequently determined the IC50 values for the five most potent angucyclines, alongside the natural O-glycoside TAN-1085 ((5S,6S)-4) (Table 1 and Fig. S3). Surprisingly, the C6 O-glycosylated (5S,6S)-4 (IC50 = 5.3 µM against A375) and its congeners did not exhibit enhanced activities compared to the aglycones. Remarkably, the unnatural enantiomer (5R,6R)-2 exhibited superior potency (IC50 = 0.24 µM against A375) compared to both the natural (5S,6S)-2 (IC50 = 0.45 µM) and natural (5R,6R)-1 (IC50 = 1.2 µM).

Structurally related griseusins are reported to exert cytotoxicity via peroxiredoxin 1 (PRDX1) inhibition, inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and the mTORC1/4E-BP1 axis suppression.65 However, despite their structural similarity, (5R,6R)-3 did not appear to inhibit 4E-BP1 phosphorylation or induce PARP cleavage at relevant pharmacological concentrations (2 µM).7 To investigate whether their cytotoxicity might still involve PRDX1, a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was performed.66 Treatment with (5R,6R)-2 increased the thermal stability of PRDX1 relative to the vehicle control (see Fig. S4 in the SI), suggesting a potential interaction with this protein. These preliminary findings propose that the (5R,6R)-2 scaffold might physically associate with PRDX1. This association could contribute to its antiproliferative profile, although the precise downstream signaling mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed a versatile and stereodivergent synthetic approach for C5/C6-oxygenated tetrangomycin-type angucyclines. By integrating the yne–allenone [2 + 2] cycloaddition and subsequent acid-catalyzed hydration and retro-Friedel–Crafts fragmentation cascade with the catalyst-controlled stereoselective reduction via Noyori ATH, we efficiently accessed the full stereoisomeric matrix of the C5/C6 diol motif. This unified approach culminated in the total syntheses of PD-116740 ((5S,6S)-2), TAN-1085 ((5S,6S)-4), himalaquinone G ((5R,6R)-1), and 16 congeners. The synthesis facilitated the stereochemical reassignment of himalaquinone G ((5R,6R)-1), exposing a specific NOESY-based structural misinterpretation within this natural product family. Evaluation of the antitumor activities of the ensuing compounds established the trans-diol geometry as a prerequisite for their potent cytotoxicity and identified (5R,6R)-2 as a highly potent lead compound (IC50 = 0.24 µM against A375). Overall, the present stereodivergent synthetic approach provides a powerful framework for the systematic construction of stereochemically diverse angucycline libraries, enabling future structural elucidation and anticancer drug discovery.
