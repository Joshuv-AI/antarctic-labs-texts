Challenges of community health nursing practice in the Primary Health Care network of Iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Iman Sharifikia, Camelia Rohani, Jamileh Farokhzadian, Azam Hosseinnejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Primary Health Care (PHC) network is the cornerstone of health service delivery in the healthcare system of Iran. However, despite the growing health needs due to population aging and the increasing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCD), the potential of Community Health Nursing Practice (CHNP) has not been adequately integrated into the PHC network. Thus, this qualitative study aimed to explore the challenges of CHNP in the Primary Health Care network of Iran.MethodsThis study has a descriptive qualitative design. It was conducted using a conventional content analysis approach based on Graneheim and Lundman’s method. Data were collected between February 2024 and April 2025 through individual semi-structured interviews with 15 participants. The interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using MAXQDA 2018. Trustworthiness of analyzed data was assessed based on Lincoln and Guba’s evaluative criteria. Data reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) Checklist.ResultsFollowing iterative cycles on analyzed data, 319 initial codes were generated. They were organized into 21 subcategories, three categories, and one main theme. The main theme of the study was “structural and functional challenges in the CHNP” which included three categories of “Unclear professional position,” “Insufficient service integration into the PHC network,” and “Limited responsibilities in core public health.”ConclusionThe findings showed that the absence of defined position and specific framework for delivering healthcare services by community health nurses (CHNs) has hindered the integration of the CHNP into the PHC network of the country. Strengthening structural reforms, clarifying the roles of CHNs roles, and expanding their participation in community-based programs can improve their coordination of care and equity in access to the healthcare services. This study emphasizes the need for educational reforms and research to enhance leadership and health policymaking competencies and support sustainable, community-oriented healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14189-8.Keywords: Community health nursing, Professional practice, Public health, Health services accessibility, Qualitative research, Iran

Introduction

Healthcare systems are fundamentally designed to meet the health needs of populations [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has specified a definition for Primary Health Care (PHC). PHC is a whole-of-society health approach aimed at ensuring the highest possible and most equitable distribution of health and well-being. This is achieved by focusing on people’s needs across the entire care continuum from health promotion and disease prevention to treatment, rehabilitation, and palliative care and by providing care as close as feasible to their everyday environment [2]. PHC is the most effective infrastructure for delivering comprehensive and equitable health services [3]. However, the current state of PHC in many countries struggles to meet the needs of the population, particularly in light of growing challenges such as the rise of non-communicable diseases (NCD), psychosocial crises, and an aging population [4].

Efforts to build a strong PHC network in Iran began in 1974. The PHC network first emerged in 1979 and was fully implemented by 1985. This initiative led to the creation of one of the world’s robust PHC networks, which has since evolved to meet the country’s diverse healthcare needs [5]. Iran’s nationwide PHC network includes rural health houses, urban and rural comprehensive health centers, and district health networks, all coordinated through medical universities. Nurses are not formally recognized as core PHC staff. Instead, community health workers (Behvarz) deliver community-level services, particularly in rural areas. However, nurses often contribute informally through health education, maternal and childcare, and preventive activities, especially within urban comprehensive health centers. The non-structural involvement of community health nurses (CHNs) within the PHC network, combined with the absence of a defined professional framework has resulted in the underestimation of their capacities [6]. The expansion of PHC network means that community health nursing practice (CHNP) is increasingly observed as an integral part of its success [7].

CHNP is recognized as a critical component of healthcare services in the PHC network, delivered by community health nurses (CHNs). This specialized field integrates nursing and public health knowledge to provide preventive, rehabilitative, and community-based care [8]. CHNP consists of a range of interventions, including health education, home-based healthcare services, chronic disease management services, and support for vulnerable groups [9]. CHNP serves as a vital link between the public and the formal healthcare system, reducing health inequalities and improving health outcomes [10].

By adopting a community-based approach, CHNP enables CHNs to identify local health needs and provide healthcare services precisely tailored to the social context for individuals, families, and groups in diverse settings, such as clients’ homes, health centers, schools, factories, workshops, hospitals, and even during the natural events and disasters [11, 12]. CHNs focus on individuals and its environment to promote individuals’ health and well-being as well as their occupational and environmental health through preventive health education, early detection of health risks, and community-based interventions [6].

Global evidence demonstrates that strengthening CHNPs enhances service access, lowers healthcare costs, and improves overall health indicators [13]. Given CHNP’s key role in promoting health equity and universal health coverage (UHC), its development directly impacts key national and regional public health outcomes [8].

Many developed countries have firmly institutionalized CHNP as an integral component of the PHC network [14, 15]. CHNs are actively engaged in public health promotion, home care, health education, and chronic disease management [16]. Countries like Canada, the United Kingdom, France, and Australia present a broad spectrum of CHNP services, ranging from prevention to treatment and patient’s follow-up [16, 17]. CHNs are an integral part of PHC teams, and can significantly influence the traditional, physician-centered approach to care [18, 19]. Earlier studies indicate that the institutionalization of CHNP is a key strategy to strengthen PHC and improve health indicators [16, 20].

The development of CHNP across Asian countries demonstrate significant inconsistencies. In several East Asian countries, the rapid aging of the population has expanded nurses’ roles in community-based and public health programs [21]. Healthcare systems in some Southeast Asian countries have formally integrated CHNP into their PHC networks, where nurses are actively involved in chronic diseases management, health education, and patients’ follow-up [22, 23]. In certain middle-income countries, although nurses are mainly involved in maternal-child, mental, and school health programs, they are predominantly working in hospitals [24]. Conversely, a few healthcare systems in Middle East countries have not still specific health policies or frameworks for CHNP implementation [25]. This varied landscape, highlights the diverse health policy needs and levels of CHNP integration into PHC networks across Asian regions [26].

In Iran, CHNP has not yet found its place in the healthcare system of the country [27]. Its development can assist to improvement of healthcare services in different levels of the PHC network. Furthermore, successful models have not been effectively implemented or localized in the area of CHNP [28, 29]. Although nurses possess the capacity for community-based care, this potential remains largely unutilized in enhancing public health in Iran [30]. It can hinder the achievement of PHC goals and global standards outlined in Iran’s scientific roadmap [29].

Given the critical role of CHNP in addressing population health needs and the complexities of health policymaking in Iran, there is an urgent need to gain a deeper understanding of the current challenges in this field. Previous studies have mainly focused on educational or attitudinal dimensions, with limited attention to the professional and structural aspects of CHNP [31, 32]. CHNP contributes substantially to promoting equity and achieving universal health coverage by extending preventive and community-based services beyond hospital settings. Although many developed and several Asian countries have successfully institutionalized this practice through a well-defined scope and integration into PHC services, Iran still lacks a fully adapted and operational model. In this context, a qualitative study was considered most appropriate to explore the lived experiences of CHNs in Iran’s PHC network, enabling an in-depth understanding of contextual and organizational dynamics [33, 34]. This study design is essential in public health research for identifying context-specific challenges and providing health policy-relevant insights to enhance PHC service quality and responsiveness [35]. Therefore, this study was conducted to explore the challenges of CHNP in the PHC network of Iran.

Material and methods

Study design

This is a descriptive qualitative study which was conducted between February 2024 and April 2025. A conventional content analysis approach was used to explore participants’ experiences and perspectives on CHNP challenges in the current PHC network of Iran.

Research question

Based on the participants’ experiences and perspectives, what are the challenges for the CHNP in the current PHC network of Iran?

Participants

Data were collected through 15 semi-structured, individual, in-depth interviews from 15 participants. The interview guide’s clarity and appropriateness were confirmed by pilot testing with two eligible participants and minor revisions were made before final data collection. Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants representing diverse job positions and experiences within the country’s healthcare system and nursing education sectors. Data were collected in two settings—university-based CHN departments and comprehensive health centers—located in five selected provinces of Iran, including Tehran, Isfahan, Fars, Mazandaran, and Khorasan Razavi, to ensure geographical and institutional diversity. The inclusion criteria were being stakeholders, including health policymakers, nurse managers, public health nurses, deans, and CHN faculty members with at least five years of experience for CHNPs in the healthcare system of the country. The exclusion criteria included individuals with limited communication ability, insufficient familiarity with the PHC network, current job position with personal conflicts of interest that might influence their perspectives, and less than five years of relevant professional experience.

Data collection

The data collection was done by a series of semi-structured interviews from 15 participants through an interview guide (Supplementary File-1). Each interview started with an introduction, a brief explanation of the study objectives, and several warm-up questions to build up a good environment for conducting interviews. To obtain more in-depth information, subsequent probing questions were asked such as “Can you explain more?”, “What do you mean?” and so on. Example questions are presented in Table 1.

Guiding questions used in the semi-structured interviews

This semi-structured approach allowed our participants to express their views and experiences freely, aligning with the inductive nature of qualitative research.

Interviews, lasting between 40 and 120 minutes, and they were conducted both in-person and online. Informed consent form was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. For in-person interviews, participants provided written consent by signing a physical form, while for online interviews, electronic consent was secured through a digitally signed consent form submitted via email before participation. All interviews were digitally recorded, transcribed verbatim, and systematically coded. Concurrently, field notes were written to capture contextual details and improve analytical accuracy. Data were collected by two authors (I.SH. and A.H.), both trained nurse researchers with academic backgrounds in community health nursing and qualitative research methods. Supervision was provided by senior faculty members (J.F. and C.R.) specializing in community health nursing and qualitative study. Theoretical data saturation was obtained when new interviews did not deliver the novel codes [36]. This data collection approach provided rich and diverse information pertinent to the study question.

Data analysis

Data analysis, was performed concurrently with data collection, adhered to Graneheim and Lundman’s (2017) conventional content analysis method [37]. The inductive analysis process began with repeated careful readings of the interview transcripts to extract relevant meaning units and initiate coding. Similar codes were then grouped into subcategories and categories based on shared concepts. These categories underwent continuous revision to ensure internal coherence and external distinction. Finally, the main theme was extracted through an interpretive analysis of the categories, focusing on their implicit meanings. MAXQDA version 2018, was used for data analysis [38]. The reporting of this qualitative study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist to ensure comprehensiveness and transparent reporting of the research process and findings (Supplementary File-2) [39].

Trustworthiness

Four-dimension criteria of Lincoln and Guba (2007) were utilized [40] to assess the trustworthiness of the analyzed data: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. These criteria are widely recognized and applied in qualitative studies.

To enhance credibility, the authors engaged with the data and research environment for several months, recording interviews, observations, and field notes. Member checking was used to validate the data and increase credibility. Two participants received their transcripts and the extracted codes to review, thereby ensuring the accuracy of their original input. Dependability was strengthened through peer review sessions, ensuring comprehensive discussions about new emerging data among the research team members. Furthermore, two external reviewers with doctoral degree in nursing audited the results. Confirmability was addressed by considering the background and interests of the authors in relevant topic, alongside rigorous document retention practices. The research team members with three experts in qualitative research analyzed interview transcripts, and extracted codes, categories and subcategories. The authors applied maximum variation sampling, which facilitated the collection of a wide range of experiences, perspectives, observations, and interpretations, and assisted to transferability of the results.

Main theme: structural and functional challenges in CHNP in the PHC network

This study identified the main theme of “Structural and functional challenges in CHNP.” Data analysis showed that this main theme included three categories: Unclear professional position, Insufficient Service Integration into the PHC network, Limited Responsibilities in Core Public Health. Each of these categories included subcategories that are explained using participant quotations.

Category one: unclear professional position

Despite the general definition of job description for CHNs, our findings showed significant ambiguity in practice. Participants emphasized the lack of clear guidelines and contradictory interpretations of responsibilities at different levels of the health care system. The position of CHNPs in the health care system hierarchy is ill defined and poorly understood by health managers. Furthermore, role overlap limits their contribution to coordinated and responsive care delivery.

These challenges undermine the autonomy and professional identity of CHNs in the PHC network.

Subcategory 1: lack of clearly defined job descriptions

The lack of clear job descriptions for CHNs leading to scattered and unspecialized work was a significant challenge within the healthcare system.

When I first joined the center, I did not know what my exact responsibilities were. They assigned me everything from training to injections, even environmental reports. Since there was no clear job description, they just piled all the tasks onto the nurse’s shoulders (P10)

Subcategory 2: uncertainty in the health care system hierarchy

In many health care centers, CHNs lack a defined position within the formal health system. This absence from organizational charts leads to their role being overlooked at decision-making and management levels. Such neglect not only diminishes professional motivation but also prevents their active involvement in broader health programs. Nurses are perceived solely as implementers of top-down policies, rather than as contributors to their design. This ultimately weakens their professional identity and fosters a sense of professional disempowerment within the healthcare system.

When there is no specific place defined for us in the organizational chart, it implies a fundamental disbelief in your role from the outset. They never acknowledge us as effective members of the system. We are merely executors; the decisions are always made elsewhere (P5)

Subcategory 3: overlapping functions with other healthcare professionals

Without clearly defined specialized roles, the activities of CHNs frequently overlap with those of other healthcare team members, including healthcare assistants, healthcare providers, and even general practitioners. This overlap leads to confusion in service delivery, reduced team efficiency, and a weakened professional identity for CHNPs within the PHC structure. The lack of role differentiation ultimately prevents the optimal utilization of nurses’ specialized capacities in public health programs.

We often do not know whose job it truly is. For instance, with a chronic patient who needs follow-up, it is unclear whether I should be following up or the CHN. Sometimes we both do, sometimes neither. This leads to duplicated effort and confusion for the patients (P7)

Category two: insufficient service integration into the PHC network

This category is one of the major barriers to effectively utilizing the capacity of the CHNs. Findings indicated that CHNPs currently lack a clear, stable, and operational role across most services provision areas, including Comprehensive Health Centers, family medicine teams, home-based health care services, Elderly Care Homes, Adult Day Care Centers, Day Centers for Disabled Individuals, Behavioral Health Centers, palliative care services, and tele-nursing. The absence of a supportive structure and a coherent operational algorithm have prevented active contribution of CHNs in these areas. Consequently, this situation has disrupted continuity of care and service provision, and reduced the efficiency of PHC at the community level.

Subcategory 1: provision of care in comprehensive health centers

According to all participants, CHNs should be active in both urban and rural Comprehensive Health Centers, given their community-centered role and direct public engagement. Unlike hospital-based nurses, CHNs operate within the community, delivering effective care with physicians and other healthcare team members.

We train CHNs, but there is no defined position for their specialized work in health centers; all of them have to work in hospitals. While they could potentially fill roles currently held by others, such as midwives in public units in urban and rural centers, there is no supportive structure in place (P14)

Subcategory 2: provision of care in home settings

Participants emphasized the crucial role of CHNs in post-discharge patient follow-up, home visits, and the ongoing care needs of chronic patients, including those with cancer and spinal cord injuries. This is particularly vital as these patients and their families require continuous care and education after leaving the hospital.

After discharge, many patients are left without proper follow-up or guidance. If CHNs were assigned post-discharge care, they could provide illness-specific follow-up, from short-term recovery to ongoing support for chronic and cancer patients (P13)

Subcategory 3: provision of care in family physician teams

The family physician program in Iran presents a significant opportunity for CHNs. Given their specialized skills and community-oriented approach, CHNs can outperform healthcare providers within this program.

The philosophy behind the family physician is to guide patients away from direct specialist visits, instead opting for an initial assessment. Nurses can manage numerous simple ailments themselves, referring only complex cases to specialists. This approach simultaneously reduces costs and the physician’s workload (P3)

Subcategory 4: provision of care for vulnerable populations

Despite their significant interdisciplinary capabilities in providing effective care to vulnerable populations- including patients with special needs, individuals receiving welfare support, prisoners, and other clients- the crucial role of CHNs is largely ignored within the Iranian healthcare system. This is a serious challenge to achieving PHC goals and improving public health outcomes.

Many organizations work with vulnerable groups, but their focus is enforcement or protection, not health. CHNs could fill this gap by providing the community-based care these populations truly need (P10)

Subcategory 5: provision of care in Behavioral health clinics

CHNs can play an effective role in specialized behavioral health clinics, such as AIDS counseling centers, addiction treatment facilities, and mental health and psychiatric clinics. Participants in this study highlighted the nurses’ capacity to provide training, counseling, mental health monitoring, and foster interdisciplinary collaboration to prevent relapse and promote community-based care.

A major challenge in these settings is the lack of a consistent specialized team; CHNs can provide essential coordination, establishing effective communication between patients, families, and the healthcare team to ensure continuous care (P6)

Subcategory 6: provision of care in palliative care settings

In palliative care settings, CHNs are vital for patients with chronic and advanced diseases, offering symptom control, family education, and psychological support. Their presence in the home is critical for reducing unnecessary hospitalizations and enhancing patients’ quality of life.

Patients in the final stages of life often require care that extends beyond medical interventions. CHNs, with their training in home care and family communication, are ideal for filling this gap, though formal licensing for them in these settings is currently lacking (P13)

Subcategory 7: provision of care via follow-up and tele-nursing

Follow-up nursing and tele-nursing services are effective in ensuring continuity of patient care, minimizing unnecessary visits to healthcare centers, and improving patient quality of life. CHNs can use communication technologies to remotely monitor patients’ conditions and provide necessary consultations. This approach offers significant advantages, including continuous follow-up for chronic patients, improved access to services, and reduced treatment costs.

Patients often leave the hospital with unanswered questions. Involving CHNs from discharge could ensure education, follow-up, and coordination with physicians, using approaches such astele-nursing to support families and continuity of care (P1)

Category three: limited responsibilities in core public health

CHNs currently play a limited role in key areas of public health and PHC. This is largely due to a lack of clearly defined job descriptions, leading to confusion and overlap with other healthcare team members. Essential activities such as health education, communicable (CD) and NCD prevention and control, older people care, school health, family health, and occupational health all suffer from ambiguous role boundaries for nurses. This ambiguity severely restricts their professional contribution and the utilization of their expertise.

Subcategory 1: health education and health promotion

Despite their crucial position in health-oriented education, CHNs’ educational role within the PHC system is often ignored or severely limited. Health education, particularly at the primary prevention level—including health promotion, nutritional counseling, disease screening, and prevention—requires the active involvement of CHNs. However, the absence of a clear structure, insufficient attention from management, and a lack of organizational integration into other education-focused departments pose significant challenges to effectively implementing this vital role.

I’ve taught CHN for years, but during internships, students are often told their presence isn’t needed. Many managers still don’t recognize community education as a core nursing responsibility (P2)

Subcategory 2: engagement in regional health policy and decision-making

CHNs can actively contribute to regional policy-making by identifying the health needs of various groups, such as schools and families. At the micro-level, CHNs can design health-oriented programs tailored to local needs. Their macro-level analyses can provide broader frameworks for public health promotion, aligning local and macro-level decision-making within the health system.

CHNs are not represented in regional decision-making. Assigning them clear population health planning roles for defined communities would highlight their expertise and impact (P12)

Subcategory 3: program management and intersectoral coordination

Participants highlighted that CHNs could effectively coordinate healthcare providers, healthcare assistants, and other team members by managing health facilities and centers. This role is crucial for the integrated pursuit of preventive and curative interventions at the regional level, enhancing both service quality and community health access.

Senior CHNs, with their holistic and community-oriented approach, are well qualified to manage comprehensive health centers. Yet, these positions are often given to temporary physicians, while no formal role exists for nurses despite their competence and interest (P3)

Subcategory 4: involvement in health systems research

Participants consistently highlighted the crucial role CHNs can play in health system research, particularly in population-based studies. They emphasized that CHNs should actively engage in conducting and developing health-oriented research. The ultimate goal of this involvement is to use research findings to enhance the quality of healthcare services.

CHNs are trained both as keen observers and as researchers. Establishing a defined research role for them would allow CHNs to identify community health needs, conduct relevant studies, and apply findings directly to practice (P9)

Subcategory 5: responsibility in communicable disease units

Based on participants’ experiences, CHNs can play a highly effective role in communicable disease units. Their strong understanding of physiology and pathophysiology allows them to analyze disease transmission chains and reservoirs accurately. While other healthcare providers often rely solely on published guidelines, CHNs, with their deep understanding of clinical conditions, can control and prevent diseases more effectively. Diseases like tuberculosis, influenza, dengue fever, viral hepatitis, and measles all require the scientific and continuous monitoring by specialized personnel, such as CHNs.

Even when other professionals are knowledgeable about communicable diseases, the absence of CHNs can disrupt care. In a CCHF case, the nurse’s noninvolvement in the primary care team led to delayed symptom detection and stress for both the family and staff (P15)

Subcategory 6: responsibility in non-communicable disease units

Given the high prevalence and significant treatment costs associated with chronic diseases, participants underscored the key role of CHNs in managing these conditions. CHNs can prevent repeated hospitalizations and reduce overall healthcare costs by providing effective, ongoing care. Their involvement is also crucial for the global control of NCD.

Healthcare providers now deliver care at the individual level, but we need CHNs who can be deployed in comprehensive health centers. In the old diabetes clinics, the presence of nurses and teamwork in managing diabetic patients was a successful experience (P10)

Subcategory 7: activity in geriatric care units

Given the increasing aging trend in Iranian society and the lack of comprehensive services for older adults, participants highlighted the critical need for CHNs to provide diverse care. With their in-depth knowledge of the physical, psychological, and social needs of the older adults, CHNs can provide effective preventive and care services. Therefore, geriatric health units are a crucial area where CHNs should play a significant role. Unfortunately, there is no defined position for CHNs in this field.

In one health center, the geriatric unit was managed by a non-nurse who lacked clinical skills to assess older adults. Many disabled elders received no services. Having CHNs in PHC centers could bridge this critical care gap (P11)

Subcategory 8: activities in family health, school health, and mental health units

CHNs play a vital role in promoting well-being across various settings, including family health, school health, and mental health services. Their expertise lies in providing essential preventive and educational services that directly impact the quality of life for individuals and communities. These nurses specialize in assessing family health, educating about mental health, preventing diseases, and following up on maternal-child health.

CHNs could greatly contribute to family, school, and mental health units by providing education and follow-up care. However, since their roles are undefined, these services remain fragmented and inconsistent (P8)

Subcategory 9: activity in occupational health unit

CHNs are crucial in occupational health units. They significantly enhance the quality of occupational health services by performing initial examinations, occupational health assessments, and the early detection of work-related diseases across various environments, including industries. This specialized involvement is highly effective in both preventing and managing occupational hazards.

CHNs can competently perform initial examinations such as spirometry, audiometry, and blood pressure checks, and conduct periodic follow-ups. They could also contribute effectively to primary care services in industrial HSE centers (P15)

Subcategory 10: participation in disaster risk management teams

CHNs can play a critical and active role in disaster risk management teams. Their expertise in health assessment, primary care, and self-care education makes them invaluable assets for resource distribution, family preparedness, and direct service provision in affected areas during crises. However, a significant gap exists in Iran: Currently, nurses are confined to hospital crisis committees, neglecting their crucial role in pre- and post-crisis stages. This oversight means we lose out on their significant potential in crisis management, ultimately making our health system less efficient in responding to public health needs.

CHNs are rarely included in disaster management teams. We only appear in hospitals, while we could play key roles in community preparedness, prevention, service delivery, and follow-up during crises (P4)

Subcategory 11: legal permission for nurse prescribing

Participants emphasized that the lack of legal authorization for nurses to prescribe medications within the PHC system, along with their limited responsibilities and authorities in key public health activities, restricts the effective role of CHNs in health promotion and disease management.

I remember when nurses first began prescribing medications at health centers, particularly for chronic wounds. Their success demonstrated that legal authorization would ease doctors’ workloads and enhanced patient care (P14)

Discussion

This study explored challenges in the CHNP within the PHC network of Iran. We found that the CHNP currently lacks a defined organizational structure and clear responsibilities. Key obstacles include unclear professional position, insufficient integration into PHC settings, and a limited role in core public health and PHC activities. This diminished role is largely due to the absence of supportive policies and frameworks [41]. Our findings provide crucial insights for policy reform to enhance health service accessibility and promote equity.

Unclear professional position

A significant structural challenge for CHNP in Iran is the absence of a clear, structured definition for the professional responsibilities and roles of CHNs. This functional ambiguity blurs their roles, leading to confusion in task implementation and limiting independent decision-making [42]. Consequently, the quality and coordination of services as well as the cohesion of the health team within the PHC are severely reduced. Furthermore, CHNs lack a clear formal position in the healthcare system’s organizational structure, often being excluded from decision-making and public health policy processes [27]. This not only reduces professional motivation but also prevents CHNs from actively participating in macro-health planning. Additionally, overlapping tasks with other healthcare professionals, such as general practitioners or healthcare workers, result in role overlap, decreased team productivity, and wasted human resources [43]. Similar challenges have been reported in countries with comparable health structures, identifying this as a major obstacle to effectively implementing the PHC model and developing community-based services [25].

Insufficient service integration into the PHC network

A key challenge for CHNP in Iran is the lack of structured integration of this role into the PHC system. This is evident in comprehensive health centers, where CHNs largely perform basic services like vaccinations and health education. While CHNs are capable of providing higher-level care, such as post-discharge care, home care, and chronic patient follow-up, the absence of clear guidelines and appropriate organizational position prevents the full utilization of their expertise [44]. This structural weakness extends to other care settings. For instance, home care for discharged patients often occurs informally, without a written plan or official CHNP framework. However, evidence shows that structured home care planning by CHNs can prevent unnecessary hospitalizations and significantly improve care quality [45]. Achieving CHN-led coordination for NCD patients in the home-based care requires the establishment of a robust transitional care protocol to standardize the transfer of care from hospital to home, identify the CHN as the care coordinator, and integrate tele-monitoring and virtual visits to support follow-up. This model can reduce unnecessary hospital readmissions and improve patient outcomes [46].

The same role ambiguity and reduction is seen in the referral system and family physician program, where CHNs’ specialized capabilities are underutilized, often limited to administrative activities. Laurant et al. (2018) highlighted that effectively integrating nurses into these programs can reduce financial burdens on the health system and improve patient access to services [47]. Furthermore, inadequate integration has prevented CHNs’ capacity to care for vulnerable groups (e.g., prisoners, welfare clients, frail older adults). Despite their abilities in education, monitoring, and counseling, a lack of formal structure prevents their effective participation [44, 48].

Similarly, in palliative care, CHN involvement in home-based services has shown promise in reducing readmissions and improving Quality of Life [49]. Integrating community health nursing into early palliative care services is a key public health priority that improves patient care quality and healthcare outcomes. It is recommended that PHC centers adopt a CHN-led palliative care plan. The CHN’s responsibilities include identifying eligible patients, delivering home-based symptom relief, providing psychosocial support to patients and caregivers, and managing necessary referrals to specialist care. Sustainable implementation requires including palliative care competencies in CHN training and formal job-plans [50], However, Iran currently lacks an operational structure and legal framework to support this role. In contrast, leading countries such as the United Kingdom have successfully institutionalized CHNs in chronic and palliative care, utilizing home-based and telenursing approaches to enhance quality of life and reduce unnecessary referrals [51],

Overall, evidence shows that the inadequate integration of CHNP into Iran’s PHC structure not only underutilizes this professional group’s capabilities but also obstructs public health goals and the system’s responsiveness. Addressing this challenge requires developing executive protocols, establishing a clear legal framework, and reforming macro-health policies.

Limited role in core public health

A fundamental challenge to the effective establishment of CHNP in Iran is the limited involvement of CHNs in key public health and PHC activities. Our study findings reveal that while CHNs work across various health domains, their participation often lacks a clear organizational framework, standard job description, and a formal position within the national health care system. This results in scattered, reactive, and incoherent service delivery, significantly reducing the productivity of this specialized role [8].

In the areas of health education and health promotion, where CHNP plays a crucial role in improving public health literacy, Iran’s healthcare system has an opportunity to strengthen its foundation by developing written guidelines to support this important work. This leads to CHNs’ educational activities being informal, sporadic, and unsupported by health policy [28]. Evidence shows that targeted educational programs, such as specialist certificates or master’s programs in community and primary health care nursing, can clarify role boundaries, strengthen professional identity, and improve patient outcomes [52]. In contrast, successful health care systems demonstrate that the structured involvement of CHNs in health education is central to health promotion and prevention [53]. Furthermore, CHNs have very limited participation in regional health policymaking. Despite their intimate knowledge of social contexts and local needs, their formal presence in population-level decision-making and planning remains noninstitutionalized [54]. International experiences; however, show that integrating CHNs’ expertise into health policymaking can significantly enhance the quality and impact of population health interventions [4]. Challenges also impede CHNs’ participation in health care system research. With the growth of higher nursing education in Iran, there is a great potential to better align research training with real-world healthcare needs. This would increase nurses’ involvement in designing and implementing research projects [28]. While other healthcare professionals, like health workers and midwives are currently engaged in these services, involving CHNs in a similar capacity to their counterparts in developed countries could lead to significant improvements and better outcomes for the healthcare system. Unlike countries where community nurses actively contribute to applied research in PHC [16], Iran lacks a clear structure for utilizing CHNP capacity in public health protocols for combating CD and NCD. Research evidence has shown that assigning roles like monitoring, screening, and follow-up to CHNs can reduce the health care system’s burden and improve health outcomes [27, 47].

In disaster risk management, the lack of an organized presence of CHNs in crisis response teams in Iran has led to slower response times, poor inter-sectoral coordination, and reduced quality and impact of health interventions [42]. International evidence confirms CHNPs’ crucial role in the rehabilitation and recovery of affected communities after disasters. Similarly, Iran’s growing older population demands increased assessment, care, and clinical decision-making by CHNs within the PHC system. However, these tasks are often delegated to informal caregivers, while CHNs in Scandinavian countries effectively improve the quality of life for older adults [48, 49].

Furthermore, CHNPs possess significant capabilities in areas like family health, mental health, and school care, but a lack of clear organizational structure and policy in Iran prevents their proper utilization. In contrast, international studies show that CHN presence in these areas correlates with improvements in vaccination safety, child mental health screening, and parent education [53, 55].

Even in occupational health, a well-established field in CHNP in many developed countries, Iranian CHNs still lack a clear role within the occupational health system [46]. This structural oversight means Iran is not fully utilizing CHNs’ specialized skills to prevent occupational diseases and promote workforce health. Although this study primarily identified barriers, participants also highlighted CHNs’ untapped capacities in community engagement, health education, chronic diseases management, and palliative care. Recognizing and investing in these strengths offers the potential to transform CHNs from a marginal role to a central element in PHC delivery services. This change aligns with global evidence emphasizing nurses’ potential to strengthen equity, accessibility, and continuity of care [56, 57].

Overall, the lack of a cohesive structural framework defining the roles of CHNs means that their potential remains underutilized in critical public health areas and within the PHC network. Our results are congruent with Beadle’s Role Theory [38] and Open Systems Theory [39], thereby enhancing the rigor of our findings. According to Biddle’s Role Theory (2024), the absence of clear role definitions results in role conflict, performance ambiguity, and reduced professional performance, which limits CHN impact [58]. Overlapping responsibilities among PHC professionals should not be regarded merely as a challenge, but rather as an opportunity to strengthen teamwork. Evidence shows that integrating nurses into primary care teams enhances service comprehensiveness and coordination. In the country’s PHC network, redefining and streamlining these shared functions through clear interdisciplinary protocols and pilot team-based models would optimize outcomes, workflow, and cost-benefit [50]. From the perspective of open systems theory [59], the lack of feedback and adaptation mechanisms creates a serious obstacle to the dynamic role of CHNs within the health care system [60]. Developing specific service packages for CHNs, particularly through health promotion clinics in Comprehensive Health Centers and hospitals, could be a strategic step toward fully operationalizing their role in health care system of Iran.

Strengths and limitations of the study

By including different participants from various regions across the country, diverse experiences and perspectives on the CHNP challenges were gathered through qualitative, in-depth interviews. The professional and geographical diversity of the participants significantly enriched the data and increased the content validity of the results. However, the study had certain limitations. A significant limitation was the very small number of nurses working in Health Centers across the country, which limited access to their field perspectives and experiences. Additionally, geographical distance and participants’ busy schedules made face-to-face interviews difficult for some participants, although this problem was largely alleviated by the use of virtual networks such as Skype and WhatsApp.

Implications of the study

The findings of this study underscore the urgent need for a clear structural framework that defines the role and scope of CHNs within the PHC network. This framework should be supported by the development of tailored nursing service packages. Reducing role ambiguity and expanding CHNs’ participation in key public health domains through well-designed service packages could substantially enhance both health outcomes and service delivery. Policymakers should focus on clarifying CHNs’ responsibilities, effectively integrating them into PHC, and leveraging their full potential to address emerging health challenges in Iran.

Conclusion

The findings revealed that the lack of a clearly defined position, professional roles, and service framework for CHNP has limited its integration into the PHC network of Iran. Removing these structural and functional challenges requires strategic reforms to establish formal organizational positions and providing healthcare service packages aligned with public health needs. Defining clear roles for CHNs and integrating their services into the healthcare teams, participation with family physicians, and providing home care, and community-based programs can enhance continuity, coordination, and equity in care delivery. The study strongly emphasizes the need for further research using mixed-method and action-oriented designs to evaluate service integration outcomes. These results can be useful for revising nursing education to strengthen CHNs’ competencies in leadership, health policymaking, and intersectoral collaboration. These insights align with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [61], reinforcing CHNs’ pivotal role in promoting community health, achieving equity, and advancing sustainable PHC networks.
