Effectiveness of echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance for screening and monitoring cardiomyopathy in people living with HIV: a mixed-methods systematic review protocol
Authors: Flávia Figueira, Matheus de Sá Freitas Tavares, Carla Betina Andreucci, Bernardino Geraldo Alves Souto, Nuno Miguel S. Osório

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople living with HIV (PLWH) have a markedly increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), including cardiomyopathy, due to a complex interplay of chronic inflammation, viral persistence, and antiretroviral therapy (ART) effects. By consolidating evidence on the applicability of these resources for the timely diagnosis and monitoring of cardiomyopathies in PLWH, it will provide elements for establishing individual care pathways and for improving the efficiency and coverage of respective healthcare. Background

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has turned human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection into a chronic condition; however, as the survival of people living with HIV (PLWH) has increased, cardiovascular disease (CVD) has emerged as a major cause of morbidity and mortality in this population [1]. Current evidence indicates that PLWH have a 61% higher risk of CVD compared to uninfected individuals, as well as an approximately twofold increased risk of acute myocardial infarction [1–6]. This problem is particularly relevant in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where structural limitations of health systems often lead to underdiagnosis and undertreatment of cardiovascular pathology [1, 7, 8].

The high burden of CVD associated with HIV results from multifactorial pathophysiological mechanisms, which involve a complex interaction between persistent chronic inflammation, immune activation, and direct viral injury to the myocardium and vascular endothelium [3, 9, 10]. While ART is critical for viral suppression and has been shown to reduce inflammatory markers and endothelial adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1, its impact on the recovery of endothelial function remains controversial [11]. Some evidence suggests that ART may not completely restore flow-mediated dilation. At the same time, the clinical presentation of HIV-associated cardiomyopathy has changed significantly in the post-ART era [12]. While overt dilated cardiomyopathy predominated in the pre-ART period, more recent data point to a transition to more subtle phenotypes, characterized by subclinical diastolic dysfunction and myocardial fibrosis, which contribute to the development of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), even in individuals with sustained virologic suppression [3, 13, 14].

In this context of structural and functional alterations that are often asymptomatic, cardiac imaging techniques play a central role. Echocardiography stands out for its wide availability, low relative cost, and ability to identify early hemodynamic changes, while cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) offers a more detailed tissue characterization, allowing the detection of fibrosis, inflammation, and myocardial edema [15–20]. Despite the high technical capacity of these modalities to identify subclinical lesions, there is still a critical gap in the literature regarding their clinical utility. In particular, it remains uncertain how or whether early imaging identification of myocardial changes in asymptomatic individuals translates into changes in therapeutic approach or improvements in long-term clinical outcomes, such as reduced hospitalizations or mortality [2, 9, 21, 22].

In addition, comparative evidence between echocardiography and CMR for the early detection and longitudinal monitoring of HIV-associated cardiomyopathy remains limited [22]. The available meta-analyses tend to focus on isolated modalities and are often conditioned by small samples and high methodological heterogeneity. In addition, few studies address fundamental aspects such as the feasibility of implementation, the cost-effectiveness of diagnosis, and the integration of these imaging techniques into structured clinical protocols, particularly critical issues in contexts with limited resources [12, 17, 23].

These limitations are aggravated by profound geographical inequalities in the production of scientific evidence. Most of the available data on HIV-associated CVD come from high-income countries, where ischemic phenotypes of the disease predominate. This contrasts with the global picture, as approximately 70% of the HIV epidemic is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and about 80% of the global burden of CVD occurs in LMICs [11, 12, 23]. Consequently, populations from Africa, South America, and Asia remain underrepresented in research using advanced cardiac imaging techniques, which may hide distinct regional phenotypes of the disease [8, 16, 23, 24].

To mitigate this bias, this systematic review will adopt an inclusive approach, extending the search to regional databases, such as the African Index Medicus and LILACS, and explicitly stratifying the results according to the geographical and socioeconomic context. This strategy aims not only to compare the diagnostic accuracy of different imaging modalities, but also to identify monitoring strategies that are clinically relevant, feasible, and adapted to resource-limited contexts, where the need for optimal cardiovascular care is particularly urgent.

Justification

Although echocardiography and CMR are widely recognized for the diagnosis and evaluation of cardiomyopathy [24], their applicability in PLWH has not yet been systematically assessed. The available evidence remains fragmented and lacks consistent conclusions. In this sense, a systematic review that integrates existing data on the effectiveness of these techniques in the detection and monitoring of cardiomyopathy in PLWH, considering geographical, clinical, virological, and therapeutic aspects, may provide a solid basis for guiding management strategies and improving the management of cardiovascular care in different contexts.

Methods and analysis

This systematic review will be conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) presented in the Additional file (Annex I), and the protocol is registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251023451).

We restricted the systematic review to articles published between 2004 and 2024. This choice is driven by the need for methodological consistency following major advances in cardiovascular imaging, such as perfusion CMR and three-dimensional flowmetric echocardiography. Since 2004, CMR has become the standard for evaluating structural abnormalities like fibrosis and inflammation [14, 25]. Consequently, pre-2004 studies were excluded as they predate current standardization (e.g., LGE, T1/T2 mapping) and rely on outdated guidelines, making them clinically and technically incomparable to modern research. Although this limitation excludes historical work from the early ART era, it is essential to avoid bias introduced by obsolete diagnostic criteria and imaging artifacts. Based on preliminary scoping, we expect to screen 250–300 records, yielding approximately 40–60 eligible studies.

Search strategy

Electronic searches will be performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, and regional databases: LILACS and African Index Medicus (AIM) to ensure representation of studies from LMICs. The search strategies will use a combination of controlled vocabulary (MeSH, Emtree, CINAHL Headings) and free text keywords. The final strategy will integrate Boolean operators and proximity terms, adapted to each database. The strategy will be formulated using Boolean operators and combinations of terms, such as: (HIV OR AIDS) AND (Cardiomyopathy OR Myocardial Dysfunction) AND (Echocardiography OR Cardiac Magnetic Resonance) NOT (Case Reports OR Animals). An appendix detailing the search strategy is presented (Appendix I). These strategies will be updated before the final data extraction phase to capture newly published studies. Any amendments to search terms, Boolean operators, or database coverage will be documented in the updated appendix, with version control and date of each update clearly indicated. This approach aligns with PRISMA 2020 recommendations for living or evolving systematic reviews.

In addition, reference lists from all included studies will be manually selected to identify additional eligible publications. This process will be reported in the PRISMA flowchart, with all records (including those from supplementary research) clearly documented. The search will be updated every three months from protocol registration until manuscript submission to ensure that newly published evidence is captured. All search iterations will be logged with timestamps and archived.

Study selection

Two reviewers (FF and MT) will independently display all titles and abstracts using Rayyan. After this phase, these investigators will screen the titles and abstracts of the manuscripts, excluding those that do not fit the objectives of this review. Any discrepancies will be evaluated by two other investigators (CBA and BS) and discussed among the five researchers for inclusion or exclusion of the manuscript. To maximize the identification of all relevant results and ensure quality control, BS and the NO will analyze a proportion of manuscripts to verify whether they have been included or not, until the perfect match in the selection has been obtained. After the initial screening, the selected articles will be retrieved in full-text form and evaluated according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Grounds for exclusion at the full-text stage will be systematically recorded. The entire process will be documented and summarized following the PRISMA 2020 flowchart.

Inter-reviewer agreement

To ensure methodological rigor and consistency during study selection, inter-reviewer agreement will be assessed quantitatively using Cohen’s kappa (κ). Before each screening phase, reviewers will complete a calibration exercise using a random sample of 50–100 citations. Any discrepancies will be discussed, and interpretations of the eligibility criteria refined until substantial agreement is reached (κ ≥ 0.61). Kappa values will be interpreted according to the widely used Landis and Koch (1977) benchmarks: < 0.20 indicates poor agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and > 0.80, almost perfect agreement. Final κ statistics will be reported in the Results section of the review to document the reliability of the selection process. These benchmarks were selected because they provide a conservative and widely accepted standard for evaluating agreement in clinical systematic reviews, ensuring that “substantial” agreement reflects true consistency between reviewers.

Linking imaging and clinical outcomes

When available, clinical outcomes (e.g., hospitalization, heart failure, mortality) will be analyzed in relation to corresponding imaging biomarkers. Associations between imaging parameters and clinical endpoints will be expressed as correlation coefficients, odds ratios, or hazard ratios, depending on study design and data availability.

For cohort or longitudinal studies, we will extract adjusted effect measures (e.g., HR or OR) describing the prognostic value of imaging findings (e.g., abnormal LVEF, increased ECV) for adverse clinical events.

In cases where imaging and clinical data are reported separately within the same study, we will explore their relationship through subgroup analyses or meta-regression, when feasible. If data are too heterogeneous, the relationship will be synthesized narratively.

PRISMA emphasizes the importance of prespecifying and transparently reporting methods for detecting publication and selective-reporting bias in systematic reviews and meta-analyses, as selective disclosure of favorable results can distort conclusions [10]. Empirical audits show that up to 19% of reviews that do not report any bias assessment still present evidence of bias, potentially inflating pooled estimates by approximately 50% [6]. Analyses of Cochrane reviews indicate that efficacy results favoring the intervention were 27% more likely to be published, while adverse effects were omitted in 78% of cases [10]. Interdisciplinary meta-research further suggests that bias can reduce standardized effect sizes in medicine from d = 0.24 to d = 0.13, whereas selective reporting in randomized controlled trials can inflate effect estimates by up to 4.7 times [10].

Systematic reviews adhering to PRISMA guidelines are more likely to assess these biases. These findings support the explicit inclusion, in our protocol, of methods for detecting and evaluating publication bias, thereby ensuring transparency and the validity of the synthesized evidence.

Heterogeneity analysis

Heterogeneity in meta-analyses stems not only from variations in results, but also from clinical and methodological differences between studies [22]. To ensure comparability, we will consider homogeneous data for pooling purposes when the following conditions are met: (i) populations restricted to adults (≥ 18 years) with confirmed HIV infection, in comparable disease stages (e.g., ART-treated vs untreated patients, suppressed versus unsuppressed viral load), and with similar indices of baseline comorbidities. Studies restricted to extreme scenarios, such as advanced heart failure or severe opportunistic infections, will not be pooled; (ii) interventions restricted to the same imaging modality (echocardiography or CMR) with equivalent technical protocols, including standardized definitions of diastolic dysfunction, uniform cut-off points for ejection fraction, and validated values for T1/T2 mapping. Studies using substantially different criteria for the same marker will be analyzed separately or excluded; (iii) results that address the same domain (e.g., prevalence of diastolic dysfunction, mean left ventricular mass, or proportion of myocardial fibrosis), assessed at comparable time points, either in cross-sectional studies or in longitudinal follow-ups of similar duration. (iv) separate grouping for cohort studies and randomized controlled trials. In observational studies, minimum adjustment for age and sex will be required, with preference for models that include additional HIV-related covariates.

Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed by Cochran’s Q test (chi-square) and the I2 index. For less than 10 studies, we will use p < 0.10 in Q, increasing the sensitivity in view of the low power of the test [31]. Discussion and implications

CVD in PLWH represents a fundamental paradigm shift in HIV care, moving from the acute management of opportunistic infections to the mitigation of chronic, systemic inflammation and end-organ injury. Among these complications, HIV-associated cardiomyopathy has emerged as a leading contributor to morbidity and mortality, yet it remains significantly underrecognized in routine clinical practice [3, 14, 25]. Consequently, early detection and systematic monitoring are critical to reducing the burden of heart failure and its associated complications in this aging population.

Noninvasive cardiac imaging constitutes an essential diagnostic domain for evaluating this cardiovascular involvement. Echocardiography, due to its accessibility and reproducibility, serves as the first-line tool for assessing structural, functional, and hemodynamic alterations. Conversely, CMR offers high-resolution tissue characterization, capable of uncovering diffuse fibrosis and inflammatory processes that remain undetectable by conventional echocardiography. Despite their complementary strengths, the comparative effectiveness and optimal integration of these two modalities within HIV care pathways have yet to be systematically investigated [19, 20, 31].

Recent evidence (2023–2025) has deepened the understanding of HIV-related myocardial pathology. Large multicenter studies and meta-analyses demonstrate a persistent prevalence of subclinical myocardial fibrosis and strain abnormalities even in virologically suppressed individuals, highlighting the silent but ongoing cardiac injury associated with chronic infection [2, 25]. These data underscore the urgent need for evidence-based strategies to determine which modality or combination thereof provides the greatest diagnostic yield across varying healthcare settings. Notably, resource disparities between high- and LMICs further necessitate the evaluation of feasibility alongside diagnostic accuracy [8, 34].

To address these challenges, this systematic review utilizes a mixed-methods framework, integrating quantitative evidence on imaging performance with qualitative data on implementation, feasibility, and contextual variation. This approach is designed to capture not only the diagnostic precision of echocardiography and CMR but also their real-world applicability in diverse clinical environments. The anticipated outcomes include improved cardiovascular risk stratification through validated imaging biomarkers, earlier detection of subclinical myocardial alterations before symptom onset, and the development of individualized management strategies that optimize clinical outcomes.

Ultimately, the findings of this review are positioned to drive significant translational impact across two distinct timelines. In the short term, the results will facilitate the optimization of screening algorithms and the improvement of referral strategies, allowing clinicians to detect pathology earlier in the disease course. In the long term, the review aims to inform health system-level implementation, specifically guiding informed resource allocation for imaging infrastructure in LMICs and supporting the integration of CMR-based tissue characterization into longitudinal HIV care. By emphasizing equity, this study seeks to define how imaging modalities can be prioritized in settings with budgetary constraints, ensuring maximum clinical benefit under limited resources and informing the development of international guidelines, including WHO-endorsed HIV care pathways [8, 19, 25].

Conclusion

Cardiac imaging is useful for the timely evaluation and management of cardiomyopathy in PLWH, although its practical application in this condition lacks in-depth studies. This review will provide a consolidated evidence base to guide rational clinical and management decisions in different socioeconomic and healthcare contexts. Therefore, it will offer elements that can benefit both individuals and health systems regarding HIV-related cardiomyopathies.
