ROS-Responsive MnO2 nanozyme co-delivering cGAMP and TGF-β inhibitor synergistically activates STING signaling and remodels the immunosuppressive thyroid cancer microenvironment
Authors: Guangming Liu, Rui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) of thyroid cancer (TC) limits the efficacy of current immunotherapies due to insufficient STING pathway activation and TGF-β–driven immune tolerance. Here, we developed a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive G5-PAMAM dendrimer-encapsulated MnO2 nanozyme (GM) co-loaded with the STING agonist cGAMP and TGF-β inhibitor SB505124 (GM@cGAMP + SB) to achieve dual-pathway immune modulation. The nanozyme exhibited precise ROS-triggered release, high stability, and selective tumor activation. In vitro, GM@cGAMP + SB elevated intracellular ROS, induced mitochondrial depolarization, and promoted immunogenic cell death (ICD), characterized by CRT exposure, HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion. It effectively enhanced STING signaling and suppressed TGF-β activity in TC cells. In vivo, treatment of humanized TC xenograft mice with GM@cGAMP + SB significantly inhibited tumor growth, elevated serum TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ, and promoted dendritic cell maturation and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, while reducing CD25⁺Foxp3⁺ regulatory T cells. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed remodeled immune–stromal communication networks and activation of antitumor immunity within the TME. Importantly, GM@cGAMP + SB exhibited excellent biocompatibility and negligible systemic toxicity. In conclusion, the dual-pathway modulation achieved by the ROS-responsive MnO2 nanozyme co-loaded with a STING agonist and TGF-β inhibitor significantly boosts antitumor immunity, presenting a viable synergistic strategy for TC treatment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04346-w.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) represents the most common malignancy of the endocrine system, with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) accounting for more than 80% of all cases [1]. Although most patients achieve favorable outcomes following surgical resection combined with radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy [2], a subset of patients harboring the BRAFV600E mutation, presenting with extensive lymph node metastasis, or exhibiting RAI resistance frequently experience tumor recurrence [3, 4]. For these patients, effective systemic therapies remain limited, posing significant clinical challenges [5, 6]. In recent years, immunotherapy has achieved significant success in the treatment of multiple solid tumors. However, TC is generally considered an “immune-cold” tumor, characterized by sparse T-cell infiltration, impaired antigen presentation, and elevated expression of immunosuppressive factors within the tumor microenvironment (TME). These features contribute to the limited therapeutic response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in PTC [7, 8]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the cellular composition of the PTC TME, together with identification of key immune evasion mechanisms and development of strategies to modulate antitumor immunity, is essential for improving immunotherapeutic efficacy in PTC.

The STING signaling pathway is a central DNA-sensing mechanism in innate immunity [9]. Cytosolic DNA is recognized by cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS), which catalyzes the production of cyclic GMP–AMP (cGAMP). cGAMP subsequently binds to STING and activates the downstream TBK1–IRF3 signaling cascade, leading to the production of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines [10, 11]. This pathway enhances antigen presentation, promotes dendritic cell (DC) maturation, and activates CD8+ T cells and NK cells, thereby playing an essential role in antitumor immune responses [12]. In recent years, cGAMP-based STING agonists have been widely explored for developing novel cancer immunotherapeutic strategies [13]. However, their clinical translation remains limited due to several challenges, including poor aqueous solubility, insufficient membrane permeability, low tumor-targeting efficiency, and potential systemic toxicity [14]. In addition, the immunosuppressive TME can attenuate STING pathway activation, resulting in limited antitumor efficacy when STING agonists are used alone [15]. Consequently, strategies that simultaneously enhance immune activation while alleviating immunosuppressive signaling may represent an effective approach to maximize the therapeutic potential of STING-targeted immunotherapy.

Among numerous immunosuppressive factors, TGF-β has attracted extensive attention for its pivotal role in tumor immune regulation [16, 17]. TGF-β not only directly suppresses antitumor immunity by promoting regulatory T cell (Treg) expansion, inhibiting effector T cell function, and inducing immune tolerance but also interferes with downstream signaling of the cGAS-STING pathway in multiple immune cell types [18–20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated TGF-β expression in diverse tumor types significantly reduces the capacity of STING agonists to induce type I interferon production and antigen presentation, thereby compromising their immunotherapeutic efficacy [21, 22]. These findings suggest that the synergistic co-delivery of STING agonists and TGF-β signaling inhibitors may represent a promising strategy to overcome the immune resistance of “immune-cold” tumors. Nevertheless, achieving precise, controllable, and coordinated intratumoral delivery of these two agents remains a major technical challenge for clinical translation.

The rapid advancement of intelligent nanodrug delivery systems offers new solutions for multi-target combination therapy [23, 24]. Among these systems, MnO2 nanozymes have attracted increasing attention because of their reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsiveness, controllable degradability, and favorable biocompatibility, making them suitable platforms for stimulus-responsive drug delivery [25, 26]. The elevated ROS levels typically present in tumor tissues can trigger the collapse of MnO2 nanostructures, leading to drug release while generating Mn2+ ions that further enhance cGAS activity and synergistically amplify STING pathway activation [27, 28]. MnO2 nanozymes also possess the potential to neutralize the acidic TME and improve immune cell function, exhibiting robust immune activation in various tumor models [29, 30]. Notably, systematic investigations of ROS-responsive MnO₂ platforms for the co-delivery of STING agonists and inhibitors of immunosuppressive signaling pathways remain limited. Moreover, their application in TC, a typical “immune-cold” tumor, has not yet been explored. Consequently, the development of an efficient, safe, and programmable co-delivery system is of considerable significance for precisely regulating tumor immune pathways.

Recently, the emergence of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has provided a powerful approach for investigating the TME [31, 32]. This technique enables precise characterization of the cellular composition, functional states, and interaction networks of immune cells within tumor tissues, thereby offering important insights for the development of personalized immunotherapeutic strategies [32]. Previous studies have shown that TC tissues display a marked reduction in immune effector populations such as T cells and NK cells, accompanied by an increased proportion of immunosuppressive cells including macrophages and Tregs, as well as high expression levels of inhibitory factors such as TGF-β and IL-10, indicating an active immune evasion mechanism [31, 33, 34]. Analysis of scRNA-seq datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database further revealed significant remodeling of the immune microenvironment in PTC. Although STING pathway genes are inherently expressed, their activity is suppressed by TGF-β-mediated immunosuppression, providing a crucial rationale for the dual-regulation strategy proposed in this study.

Based on these findings, the present study developed a reactive oxygen species-responsive G5-PAMAM dendrimer-encapsulated MnO2 nanozyme (GM) loaded with STING agonist cGAMP and TGF-β pathway inhibitor SB505124 (SB) (GM@cGAMP + SB) to achieve precise modulation of the TME. In vitro models were employed to evaluate its effects on ROS levels, immunogenic cell death (ICD), and immune activation. Combined with scRNA-seq analyses from public databases, the study elucidated the immune landscape and regulatory targets within the PTC TME. Furthermore, in a humanized immune system (HIS) mouse model of TC, its antitumor efficacy, regulation of STING/TGF-β signaling, and systemic toxicity were validated. This work explores a ROS-responsive MnO2 nanozyme platform for the synergistic regulation of immune activation and suppression pathways, systematically validating its role in TME remodeling. These findings provide a promising strategy for TC immunotherapy and offer a conceptual basis for precision nanomedicine targeting immune-cold tumors.

Materials and methods

Public data acquisition and scRNA-seq analysis

The scRNA-seq dataset GSE193581 was obtained from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). From this dataset, scRNA-seq data of six adjacent normal thyroid tissues and seven TC tissues were selected for analysis. Data preprocessing and analysis were performed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) in R. Quality control filtering was applied with the criteria 200 < nFeature_RNA < 5000 and percent.mt < 20. The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified based on expression variance. Batch effects were corrected using the SCTransform (SCT) method. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for dimensionality reduction, and the top 30 principal components were used for UMAP visualization (dims = 1:30). Cells were clustered using the FindClusters function with a resolution parameter of 0.8, resulting in 29 transcriptionally distinct clusters. Cell types were annotated using the CellMarker 2.0 database, supported by published references and validated through canonical marker gene expression patterns.

The prop.table function in Seurat was used to estimate the relative abundance of each cell type across samples. Cell–cell communication networks were inferred using the CellChat package (v1.6.1) based on ligand–receptor interactions to compare signaling activity between normal thyroid and TC tissues. Interaction patterns were visualized using chord diagrams and information flow heatmaps, with a threshold of interaction.score > 0.05.

TC cell culture and grouping

Human PTC cell lines (BCPAP and TPC-1) and the human normal thyroid follicular epithelial cell line (Nthy-ori 3 − 1) were used in this study. BCPAP and TPC-1 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), while Nthy-ori 3 − 1 cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). All cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (31800-022, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10099141 C, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For experimental treatments, cells were divided into four groups: Control (untreated), cGAMP (4 µM 2′3′-cGAMP; HY-100564 A, MCE, USA), SB505124 (4 µM SB505124; HY-13521, MCE, USA), and Combination (cGAMP + SB505124 at the aforementioned concentrations). All treatments were administered for 24 h.

Cells were assigned to five treatment groups: phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), GM, GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB. Nanoparticles were administered at a final concentration of 100 µg/mL for 24 h.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 micro-UV spectrophotometer (1011U, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR047A, Takara, Japan). Reverse transcription was performed at 42 °C for 30–50 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 s. Quantitative PCR was conducted using the Fast SYBR Green PCR Kit (RR820A, Takara, Japan) on an ABI PRISM 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 57 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Each reaction was performed in triplicate, with β-actin used as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All experiments were repeated three times. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted from each treatment group to evaluate the expression of proteins associated with the STING and TGF-β signaling pathways. Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China), and protein concentrations were determined with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample) were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes, followed by blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-STING (1:1000, 50907), STING (1:1000, 13647), p-TBK1 (1:1000, 5483), TBK1 (1:1000, 3013), p-IRF3 (1:1000, 4947), IRF3 (1:1000, 4302), SMAD2/3 (1:1000, 8685), and β-actin (1:1000, 7252) (all from Cell Signaling Technology, USA); p-SMAD2/3 (1:1000, ab63399), IFN-β1 (1:1000, ab275880), and PD-L1 (1:1000, ab213524) (all from Abcam, UK). After washing with TBST three times (10 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG, 1:10000, 7074, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (32106, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and visualized with a Gel Doc XR+ imaging system (Bio-Rad, 1708195, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, with β-actin serving as the loading control.

CCK-8 cell viability assay

TPC-1, BCPAP, and Nthy-ori 3 − 1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated for 24 h to allow attachment. Subsequently, the cells were treated with PBS, GM, GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, or GM@cGAMP + SB formulations at varying gradient concentrations (0, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL). After 24 h of treatment, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (96992, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to each well. Following incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher, USA), and the relative cell viability percentage was calculated.

Evaluation of synergistic effects

The pharmacological interaction between cGAMP and SB505124 was assessed using the Chou–Talalay method, a widely accepted approach for drug combination analysis [35]. BCPAP and TPC-1 cells were exposed to varying concentration gradients of cGAMP, SB505124, or their combination at a fixed 1:1 ratio. After 24 h of treatment, the fraction affected (Fa), representing the inhibition rate of cell viability, was determined using the CCK-8 assay. The Combination Index (CI) was calculated using CompuSyn software (ComboSyn Inc., USA). Drug interactions were interpreted as synergistic (CI < 1), additive (CI = 1), or antagonistic (CI > 1).

Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI double-staining kit (556547, BD Biosciences, USA). Following treatment, cells were harvested and resuspended in 500 µL of 1× binding buffer, then stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI. After incubation for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, apoptotic cells were quantified by flow cytometry (FACS, BD Biosciences, USA).

ROS level detection

Intracellular ROS generation was detected using DCFH-DA (S0033S, Beyotime, China). After 24 h treatment, cells were stained with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 30 min, washed with PBS, and observed using a laser confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany). ROS-associated fluorescence was further quantified by flow cytometry.

Mitochondrial membrane potential detection

Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) were evaluated using the JC-1 staining kit (C2006, Beyotime, China). After 24 h of treatment, cells were trypsinized, collected, and washed twice with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in 500 µL JC-1 working solution and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Following staining, cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 500 µL JC-1 buffer. Fluorescence signals were detected by flow cytometry. Red fluorescence (PE channel, JC-1 aggregates) indicated high ΔΨm, whereas green fluorescence (FITC channel, JC-1 monomers) represented mitochondrial depolarization. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1, BD Biosciences, USA).

Live/Dead cell staining assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Live/Dead Cell Double Staining Kit (L3224, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 24 h treatment, cells were stained with Calcein-AM (2 µM) and PI (4 µM) for 20 min, washed with PBS, and imaged by confocal microscopy. Green and red fluorescence represented live and dead cells, respectively.

Induction and activation of DCs

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (PCS-800-011, ATCC, USA) were used as the source of DCs. PBMCs were seeded in serum-free culture flasks and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. Non-adherent cells were removed, and adherent monocytes were collected and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, recombinant human IL-4 (10 ng/mL, 200-04, PeproTech, USA), and recombinant human GM-CSF (50 ng/mL, 300-03, PeproTech, USA). The culture was maintained for 5–6 days to generate immature DCs, with fresh cytokine-containing medium replaced on day 3.

For co-culture, BCPAP tumor cells from each treatment group were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in the upper chamber of 24-well Transwell inserts (0.4 μm, 3413, Corning, USA) and treated for 24 h. The pretreated tumor cells, along with the Transwell inserts, were then transferred to the lower chambers containing CD11c+ DCs (5 × 104 cells per well) and co-cultured for an additional 24 h.

CD8+ T cell isolation, expansion, and activation assay

CD8+ T cells were isolated from PBMCs using the EasySep™ Human CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (19053, STEMCELL Technologies, Canada) based on a negative selection protocol. The purified CD8+ T cells were seeded into 24-well plates pre-coated with anti-CD3 antibody (2 µg/mL, 16-0037-81, eBioscience, USA) and cultured with anti-CD28 antibody (2 µg/mL, 16-0289-85, eBioscience, USA) and recombinant human IL-2 (100 IU/mL, 200-02, PeproTech, USA) for activation. Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, 1× non-essential amino acids (NEAA), and 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, maintaining a cell density of ≥ 1 × 106 cells/mL. Fresh complete medium containing IL-2 was replenished every 48 h, and the culture was maintained for 14 days.

Activated CD8+ T cells were labeled with CFSE dye (2.5 µM, 65-0850-84, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by termination with complete medium and three washes. CFSE-labeled CD8+ T cells were then co-cultured with previously generated DCs at a 5:1 ratio for 48 h. After co-culture, non-adherent cells were collected and stained with antibodies against CD69 and CD107a to evaluate T cell activation.

Flow cytometry analysis of immune cell function

DCs were stained with flow cytometry staining buffer (420201, BioLegend, USA) for 30 min using the following antibodies: FITC-CD80 (375405, BioLegend, USA, 1:100), APC-CD86 (305412, BioLegend, USA, 1:100), PE-MHC-II (327007, BioLegend, USA, 1:100), and PE-MHC-I (343305, BioLegend, USA, 1:100).

After 48 h of co-culture, CD8+ T cells were surface-stained with FITC-CD69 (310904, BioLegend, USA, 1:100) and APC-CD107a (328620, BioLegend, USA, 1:100).

Tumor tissues were collected, minced, and digested at 37 °C for 30 min with 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (17104019, Gibco, USA) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (11284932001, Roche, Switzerland) under gentle agitation. The digested suspension was filtered to obtain single cells, and red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (R1010, Solarbio, China). Following Fc receptor blocking (553142, BD Biosciences, USA), cells were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 30 min in the dark. For DC identification, antibodies included CD11c-FITC (301603, BioLegend, USA) and MHC-II-PE (307606, BioLegend, USA). For M1 macrophage characterization, antibodies included CD11b-FITC (101205, BioLegend, USA) and CD68-APC/Cy7 (137023, BioLegend, USA), while CD80-APC and APC-CD86 were used as described above. For T cell profiling, antibodies included CD3-PE (300308, BioLegend, USA), CD4-PerCP/Cy5.5 (100434, BioLegend, USA), CD8-PE (300908, BioLegend, USA), CD25-FITC (302604, BioLegend, USA), and Foxp3-PE (320007, BioLegend, USA), with the latter requiring intracellular staining. Stained samples were analyzed on a BD flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.8, Tree Star Inc., USA).

ELISA

After collecting the cell culture supernatant, the concentrations of IFN-γ (88-7316-88, Invitrogen, USA) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (A22066, Invitrogen, USA) were determined. The release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein was measured using an HMGB1 ELISA detection kit (E-EL-H1685c, Elabscience, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Colorimetric detection was performed, and absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, with protein concentrations determined according to the standard curve.

On the second day after the final administration, blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus of mice and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min. Serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 min. The levels of TNF-α (PT512, Beyotime, China), IL-6 (PI326, Beyotime, China), and IFN-γ (PI508, Beyotime, China) were quantified using mouse-specific ELISA kits. Absorbance was read at 450 nm, and cytokine concentrations were calculated accordingly.

Immunofluorescence and TUNEL staining

BCPAP cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime, China) for 15 min at room temperature without permeabilization. Paraffin-embedded tumor sections were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked with 1% BSA (SW3015, Solarbio, China) for 60 min. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-calreticulin (CRT) (ab2907, Abcam, UK, 1:200) and anti-Ki67 (ab15580, Abcam, UK, 1:500). After washing, sections were incubated for 60 min in the dark with Cy3-conjugated (A0516, Beyotime, China, 1:500) or Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated (A-11008, Invitrogen, USA, 1:500) goat anti-rabbit IgG. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime, China, 1 µg/mL) for 10 min, and images were acquired using a confocal microscope (63× oil objective).

Apoptosis was further evaluated using a TUNEL kit (C1088, Beyotime, China). After DAPI counterstaining, fluorescence images were obtained by confocal microscopy.

Synthesis of GM@cGAMP + SB

To prepare the GM@cGAMP + SB, G5.NH2 dendrimers (50 mg, dissolved in 10 mL deionized water; Q-0275313, Xi’an Qiyue Bio, China) were reacted with mPEG-NHS (48.1 mg, dissolved in 10 mL deionized water; HY-W591424, MCE, USA) under magnetic stirring at 30 °C for 12 h in a weakly basic buffer. The reaction mixture was dialyzed in a 10,000 Da molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) dialysis bag (132724, Spectrum Labs, USA) against 2 L of deionized water for 3 days (water replaced three times daily) and lyophilized (FreeZone 6 L, Labconco, USA) to obtain G5.NH2-mPEG powder. To enhance the loading capacity (LC) of SB, β-CD-COOH (C4767, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in MES buffer (50 mM, pH 5.5-6.0) and activated using EDC (1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide, Lot No. 03449, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and NHS (N-hydroxysuccinimide, Lot No. 130672, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a molar ratio of β-CD-COOH: EDC: NHS = 1:1.2:1.2. The activated β-CD-COOH was then reacted with residual primary amine groups of G5.NH2-mPEG to form amide bonds, introducing β-cyclodextrin moieties that provide host-guest binding sites. The reaction mixture was dialyzed (MWCO 10 kDa) to remove small molecules and lyophilized to yield G5-PEG-CD.

KMnO4 (223468, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to the β-CD-grafted G5.NH2-mPEG solution (1 mg/mL, deionized water). The primary amines on the dendrimer surface reduced KMnO4 to MnO2, leading to the in situ formation of MnO2 nanocores within the dendrimer framework during the reaction. After stirring at room temperature for 1 h, the resulting GM nanoparticles were collected by ultrafiltration (MWCO 10 kDa) and resuspended in deionized water.

Subsequently, 10 mg cGAMP (HY-100564 A, MCE, USA) was dissolved in 10 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.2) and mixed with the positively charged GM nanoparticles. After incubation at room temperature for 2 h, electrostatic interactions facilitated cGAMP loading. Free cGAMP was removed by ultrafiltration (MWCO 10,000 Da) to obtain GM@cGAMP. Next, 10 mg SB (HY-13521, MCE, USA) was dissolved in a minimal amount of DMSO (≤ 2% v/v) and slowly added under stirring to the β-CD-grafted GM suspension. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 4 h. Free drug and residual solvent were removed by ultrafiltration (Amicon Ultra-15, MWCO 100 kDa, Millipore, USA) to yield GM@SB or GM@cGAMP + SB nanoparticles.

Characterization of GM@cGAMP + SB

GM nanoparticles and their drug-loaded complexes (1 mg/mL) were deposited (5 µL) onto carbon-coated copper grids (CF400-CU, Electron Microscopy Sciences, USA) and air-dried. Morphology and particle size were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-2100 F, JEOL, Japan) operating at 200 kV. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were determined using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyzer (Nano ZS90, Malvern Panalytical, UK). Prior to measurement, samples were diluted to 0.2 mg/mL in deionized water. The chemical valence state of Mn was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using an Al Kα radiation source with a pass energy of 50 eV. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was performed to identify characteristic functional groups. The crystalline structure of the nanoparticles was further analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with a Cu Kα radiation source (40 kV, 30 mA) over a 2θ range of 3–90°.

Evaluation of hydroxyl radical (•OH) generation ability

Methylene blue (MB; 341939, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used as a reactive probe to assess •OH generation by monitoring absorbance changes under different conditions. GM or MnCl2 (M1254, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 100 µg/mL was incubated with MB (10 µM) in PBS containing varying concentrations of glutathione (GSH) (0, 1, 2, 5, and 10 mM; G4251, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min.

Elemental analysis and manganese quantification

Elemental mapping was performed using energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), while the Mn content was quantitatively measured using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Prodigy, Leeman Labs, USA). The Mn2+ release profile was recorded under conditions of pH 7.4, pH 6.5, and pH 6.5 + H2O2 (100 µM; 88597, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) with or without GSH (10 mM).

The absorption peak of cGAMP was determined using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Japan) at 256 nm. The concentration of SB was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (LC-20 A, Shimadzu, Japan) using a C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm) with a methanol/water mobile phase (70:30) and detection at 270 nm. The effect of pH and redox conditions on drug release was evaluated using the dialysis method. GM@cGAMP + SB samples were placed in dialysis bags (10 kDa, Thermo Fisher, USA) and incubated at 37 °C in shaking baths containing solutions of pH 7.4, pH 6.5, pH 6.5 + H2O2 (200 µM), pH 6.5 + GSH (5 mM), or pH 6.5 + H2O2 + GSH. At predetermined time points, aliquots were withdrawn, and drug concentrations were determined by UV spectrophotometry and HPLC analysis.

The colloidal stability of the nanoparticles was evaluated by DLS. Samples (100 µg/mL) were stored in PBS at 25 °C, and particle size variations were monitored over 7 days.

Cellular uptake of nanoparticles

To visualize cellular internalization, the visible-light fluorophore FITC (46950, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was conjugated to the surface of GM@cGAMP + SB nanoparticles. FITC was selected for its high quantum yield and excellent spatial resolution, which perfectly match standard detection channels in confocal microscopy and flow cytometry, allowing for precise analysis of in vitro uptake behavior. FITC-labeled nanoparticles (100 µg/mL) were added to BCPAP cell cultures, and uptake was monitored at 1, 2, 4, and 8 h. The intracellular distribution of FITC fluorescence was observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), while the percentage of fluorescence-positive cells was quantified by flow cytometry.

Additionally, to evaluate uptake in different cell lines, Cy5-NHS ester (PA15101, Biorigin, China) was reacted with GM@cGAMP + SB to label the nanoparticles with red fluorescence. Cy5-labeled nanoparticles (100 µg/mL) were incubated with Nthy-ori 3 − 1, TPC-1, and BCPAP cells for 4 h. The cellular uptake of the red fluorescent particles was subsequently visualized using CLSM.

In vivo biodistribution (in vivo imaging system [IVIS] fluorescence imaging)

A subcutaneous BCPAP xenograft model was established in BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old, 16–18 g, n = 3 per group). Once tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were intravenously injected via the tail vein with GM@cGAMP + SB labeled with the near-infrared (NIR) fluorescent probe Cy5.5 (200 µL, dose: 10 mg/kg). Given that visible light suffers from limited tissue penetration depth and significant autofluorescence interference in biological tissues, the NIR probe Cy5.5—characterized by strong tissue penetration and minimal background noise—was selected to ensure reliable deep-tissue distribution data for in vivo optical imaging. Fluorescence signals were monitored at 1, 12, and 24 h using an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA). At 24 h post-injection, the mice were euthanized, and tumors along with major organs were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. All images were analyzed semi-quantitatively using Living Image software.

Establishment of a humanized mouse BCPAP xenograft model and treatment protocol

Female NOG SCID immunodeficient mice (4–5 weeks old; Aniphe Biolaboratory, China) were used for all experiments with approval from the institutional ethics committee. Animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 25 ± 2 °C, 40–60% relative humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. After one week of acclimatization, subsequent procedures were performed.

To generate a HIS mouse model, 5 × 104 CD34+ human hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs; 70012, STEMCELL Technologies, Canada) were intravenously injected via the tail vein. After 1–2 weeks of differentiation, peripheral blood was analyzed to assess human immune cell reconstitution. Mice with an hCD45+ cell proportion exceeding 45% were considered successfully humanized. Tumor implantation was then carried out [36].

To establish the xenograft model, BCPAP cells (5 × 106 cells suspended in 100 µL PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the right dorsal flank. When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6): PBS, GM, GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB. Treatments were administered by tail vein injection twice per week for four weeks. Tumor size was measured weekly using a vernier caliper (500-159-30, Mitutoyo, Japan) and calculated as: volume = length × width2 × 0.5.

At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were harvested. Each tumor was divided into two portions: one fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099-3 L, Beyotime, China) for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), immunohistochemical, and immunofluorescence staining, and the other snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent experimental analyses.

H&E staining of tumor tissues

After euthanasia, tumor tissues and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. After deparaffinization with xylene and graded ethanol dehydration, sections were stained with hematoxylin (H8070, Solarbio, China) for 5 min, differentiated with 0.5% acid alcohol, and counterstained with eosin (E607318, Sangon Biotech, China) for 3 min. Following dehydration and mounting, stained sections were observed and photographed using an upright optical microscope (Leica DM2500, Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Serum biochemical analysis

Blood samples were collected on the second day after the final administration, and serum was isolated and sent to a third-party testing facility (Servicebio, China) for automated biochemical analysis of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total bilirubin (TBIL), and uric acid (UA). The assays were performed using an automatic biochemical analyzer (7600 − 210, Hitachi, Japan) to evaluate hepatic and renal function as well as systemic toxicity.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Differences between two groups were evaluated using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; ns, not significant). Each in vivo experimental group contained six mice (n = 6), and in vitro experiments were performed at least three times (n = 3). Statistical analyses were based on biological replicates rather than technical replicates.

Results

cGAMP combined with SB inhibits TC cell proliferation and activates the STING pathway

To elucidate the biological effects of the STING agonist cGAMP and the TGF-β pathway inhibitor SB on TC cells, TPC-1 and BCPAP cells were used as models. Four groups were established: Control, cGAMP, SB, and the combination group (cGAMP + SB), followed by cell function and signaling pathway analyses (Fig. 1A). WB analysis showed that cGAMP treatment markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 in both TPC-1 and BCPAP cells (Fig. 1B), indicating activation of the STING signaling pathway. In contrast, SB treatment significantly reduced p-SMAD2/3 expression, suggesting effective inhibition of TGF-β signaling (Fig. 1C).

CCK-8 assay results showed that, relative to the Control group, cell viability in both the cGAMP and SB groups was significantly reduced in TPC-1 and BCPAP cells, while the combination group exhibited a further decrease in viability, indicating a synergistic inhibitory effect on cell proliferation (Fig. 1D). Consistently, Annexin V/PI flow cytometry revealed that each monotherapy induced apoptosis, while the cGAMP + SB group exhibited a markedly higher apoptotic rate than either single-agent group (Fig. 1E). To quantitatively evaluate the synergistic effect of the combined treatment, we calculated the CI using data from various co-administration concentrations. As shown in Fig. 1F, the CI values for both BCPAP and TPC-1 cells were consistently less than 1 across the tested range of Fa. Specifically, at the representative concentration of 4 µM, the CI values for BCPAP and TPC-1 cells were 0.71 and 0.65, respectively. These results confirm that the STING agonist cGAMP and the TGF-β inhibitor SB505124 exhibit a significant synergistic effect in inhibiting TC cell growth.

Collectively, these findings indicate that cGAMP and SB significantly suppress TC cell proliferation and promote apoptosis, acting through activation of the STING pathway and inhibition of the TGF-β signaling pathway, respectively.

GM@cGAMP + SB nanoparticles enable efficient dual-drug co-loading

In this study, the GM was designed and constructed to achieve the co-delivery of the STING agonist cGAMP and the TGF-β pathway inhibitor SB. To develop a nanozyme system with ROS responsiveness and dual-drug delivery capability, MnO2 was used as a carrier template to co-load the immune agonist cGAMP and the TGF-β pathway inhibitor SB, yielding GM@cGAMP + SB nanoparticles. A series of physicochemical characterizations and structural confirmation experiments were subsequently performed (Fig. 2A). Four formulations were prepared: GM (blank), GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB. In the compositional analysis of GM, XPS results revealed that the binding energies of Mn 2p₃/2 and Mn 2p₁/2 were centered at 641.95 eV and 653.65 eV, respectively. The characteristic spin-orbit splitting peaks indicated that the predominant oxidation state of manganese was + 4. This finding suggests that the synthesized nanoparticles can participate in redox reactions with H2O2 in the TME, catalyzing its conversion into cytotoxic •OH (Figure S1A). XRD analysis was conducted to further assess the crystalline structure of GM and its drug-loaded complexes (Figure S1B). The results revealed prominent diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 19.2°, which are fully consistent with the crystalline characteristics of the mPEG segments within the carrier system. The broad diffraction humps observed at approximately 37° and 66° indicate a typical low-crystallinity or amorphous state, aligning well with the standard characteristics of δ-MnO2 (JCPDS No. 80-1098). Furthermore, the XRD patterns before and after drug loading remained highly consistent, with no new impurity peaks or phase transitions detected, confirming the excellent structural integrity of the GM nanosystem during the drug encapsulation process.

TEM images revealed that the constructed GM nanomaterials exhibited a spherical morphology with an intact structure. Partial aggregation was observed in the GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB groups, further confirming the successful loading of cGAMP and/or SB (Fig. 2B). DLS analysis showed that, compared with the blank GM group, the particle sizes of the drug-loaded nanoparticles increased significantly. The average diameters of GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB were 118.2, 102.4, and 145.7 nm, respectively. Meanwhile, the zeta potential slightly decreased, with values of 6.7, 8.4, and 7.2 mV, respectively (Fig. 2C-D). Regarding drug EE, ultraviolet spectrophotometry and HPLC analyses demonstrated that the cGAMP EE and LC of the GM@cGAMP group were approximately 72.6% and 14.8%, respectively, while those of SB in the GM@SB group were 68.4% and 13.5%, respectively. In the dual-drug-loaded GM@cGAMP + SB group, the EE values of cGAMP and SB reached approximately 70.1% and 66.9%, and the LC values were about 14.2% and 12.9%, respectively, showing no significant difference compared with their single-drug counterparts (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that dual-drug co-loading does not compromise individual drug EE, suggesting that the GM nanocarrier possesses excellent multi-drug LC and structural stability.

Since GM is capable of consuming GSH, its •OH generation ability was evaluated under varying GSH concentrations. The results showed that at GSH concentrations of 5 mM and 10 mM, the MB degradation efficiencies of GM were 46.8% and 18.7%, respectively—both markedly higher than those of equimolar MnCl2 (6.1% and 0%). This finding suggests stronger reactivity of GM in GSH-mediated chemodynamic therapy. In the absence of GSH, GM could not effectively convert to Mn2+; the solution appeared yellow due to MnO2, and when mixed with blue MB, turned blue-green with high absorbance in the 400–500 nm range (Figure S1C-D).

Drug release analysis revealed that the release rates of cGAMP and SB505124 from GM@cGAMP + SB nanoparticles were significantly upregulated under acidic conditions mimicking the TME (pH 6.5) compared to physiological conditions (pH 7.4). The introduction of H2O2 further enhanced the drug release rate, indicating that an acidic and ROS-rich environment accelerates payload release (Fig. 2F).

To deeply evaluate the responsiveness and stability of the carrier, we further monitored Mn²⁺ release behavior using ICP-OES. The results showed that under simulated normal physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the cumulative release of Mn2+ from GM was only 20.1% over 48 h. This relatively low level represents baseline release under physiological conditions, a common phenomenon in MnO₂-based delivery systems typically attributed to the dissociation of surface-adsorbed ions or trace dissolution equilibrium. In sharp contrast, Mn²⁺ release was significantly accelerated under simulated TME trigger conditions. Specifically, at pH 6.5 alone, pH 6.5 + H2O2, and pH 6.5 + GSH, the 48-h release rates reached 41.2%, 80.6%, and 90.8%, respectively. Notably, under acidic conditions containing both high concentrations of H2O2 and GSH (pH 6.5), GM exhibited a rapid “on-off” switching responsiveness, releasing 81.1% of Mn2+ within just 2 h and reaching a cumulative release of 93.1% by 4 h (Fig. 2G). This stark contrast between the release rates under trigger conditions (> 90%) versus physiological conditions (~ 20%) strongly demonstrates that the GM nanosystem possesses excellent TME-stimuli responsiveness and relatively good stability in physiological environments.

Furthermore, our DLS monitoring data confirmed this stability: the hydrodynamic diameters of the GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB complexes remained stable in aqueous solution over one week, with no significant changes or obvious aggregation/precipitation observed (Fig. 2H). This structurally validates the excellent colloidal stability of the carrier. Collectively, these results indicate that the nanosystem maintains relative stability under physiological conditions while achieving efficient responsive degradation and drug release upon reaching the TME.

Collectively, these results confirm the successful construction of the nanoplatform, which exhibits robust structural stability and efficient dual-drug co-loading capability.

In vitro evaluation of tumor selectivity and cytotoxicity of GM@cGAMP + SB

To evaluate the biocompatibility, dose-dependent effects, and potential cytotoxicity of GM@cGAMP + SB in normal and tumor cells, in vitro toxicological assessments were performed in the normal human thyroid epithelial cell line (Nthy-ori 3 − 1) and TC cell lines (TPC-1 and BCPAP) using a gradient concentration range of 0–400 µg/mL (Fig. 3A). The CCK-8 assay demonstrated a clear dose-dependent inhibitory effect on TPC-1 and BCPAP cells across all treatment groups. At 100 µg/mL, the GM carrier significantly reduced tumor cell viability compared with PBS. Drug-loaded formulations further enhanced this effect, with GM@cGAMP and GM@SB producing greater inhibition, while the combination treatment (GM@cGAMP + SB) exhibited the strongest suppression of cell viability (Fig. 3B and Figure S2A). When the concentration increased to 400 µg/mL, the antitumor activity of the combination group was further amplified and remained significantly stronger than that of the single-drug formulations (p < 0.01).

In contrast, all formulations showed excellent biocompatibility toward normal thyroid epithelial cells, with Nthy-ori 3 − 1 viability consistently exceeding 90% across the entire concentration range (Figure S2B), demonstrating the tumor-selective cytotoxicity of the nanosystem.

Intracellular ROS were measured using the DCFH-DA probe. Both confocal microscopy and flow cytometry revealed that GM treatment markedly increased ROS levels in TPC-1 and BCPAP cells. Drug-loaded nanoparticles further enhanced ROS generation, with GM@cGAMP + SB producing the highest ROS accumulation (Fig. 3C–D and Figure S3A–B).

Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using JC-1 staining. GM treatment significantly reduced the red-to-green fluorescence ratio, indicating mitochondrial depolarization. This effect was further intensified by GM@cGAMP and GM@SB, while the combination group showed the most pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 3E & Figure S3C).

Live/Dead double staining further validated the above findings. Compared with the PBS group, the GM-treated TPC-1 and BCPAP cells exhibited a marked reduction in green fluorescent live cells and a pronounced increase in red fluorescent dead cells. Relative to the blank GM group, the GM@cGAMP and GM@SB groups showed further decreases in green fluorescence and increases in red fluorescence, while the GM@cGAMP + SB group demonstrated an even greater extent of cell death (Fig. 3F & Figure S2C). Consistently, Annexin V/PI flow cytometry demonstrated a progressive increase in apoptosis across treatment groups. GM treatment significantly elevated the proportion of apoptotic cells, whereas the GM@cGAMP and GM@SB groups exhibited higher apoptotic rates, and the combination treatment resulted in the greatest level of apoptosis (Fig. 3G & Figure S2D).

Collectively, these results indicate that GM@cGAMP + SB effectively induces oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis in tumor cells, while exerting negligible cytotoxicity on normal cells, thereby demonstrating excellent tumor selectivity and biosafety.

Cellular uptake and in vivo biodistribution dynamics of GM@cGAMP + SB

To clarify the cellular uptake capability and in vivo distribution characteristics of GM@cGAMP + SB, FITC- or Cy5.5-labeled GM@cGAMP + SB systems were constructed according to the different optical requirements for in vitro and in vivo imaging, respectively. Dynamic tracking was subsequently performed through both cellular uptake assays and in vivo imaging experiments (Fig. 4A). In BCPAP cells, confocal microscopy revealed a pronounced increase in cytoplasmic fluorescence intensity of FITC-GM@cGAMP + SB within 1–8 h, with most particles localized in the cytoplasmic region, indicating efficient cellular internalization and successful intracellular delivery of FITC-GM@cGAMP + SB (Fig. 4B). Quantitative flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that the fluorescence-positive cell rate in the FITC-GM@cGAMP + SB group increased significantly in a time-dependent manner, suggesting high cellular uptake efficiency of the nanoparticles (Fig. 4C). Moreover, to assess uptake selectivity among different cell types, Cy5-labeled GM@cGAMP + SB nanoparticles were evaluated in BCPAP, TPC-1, and Nthy-ori 3 − 1 cell lines. Confocal imaging showed that Nthy-ori 3 − 1 cells exhibited minimal nanoparticle uptake, whereas uptake efficiency was markedly enhanced in TPC-1 and BCPAP cells compared with Nthy-ori 3 − 1 cells. Notably, BCPAP cells displayed significantly higher uptake efficiency than TPC-1 cells (Figure S4). These findings indicate that MnO2 nanozymes exhibit cell line-specific uptake efficiency among different TC cell types, with BCPAP cells demonstrating a superior internalization capacity.

Next, in the established BCPAP subcutaneous xenograft mouse model, the in vivo distribution of near-infrared fluorescence-labeled GM@cGAMP + SB (Cy5.5-GM@cGAMP + SB) was dynamically monitored using an IVIS in vivo imaging system. As shown in Fig. 4D, compared with the free Cy5.5 group, the Cy5.5-GM@cGAMP + SB group exhibited a highly consistent tumor-specific accumulation pattern in all tested mice (n = 3). The fluorescence signal in the tumor region gradually increased over time and reached a peak at 24 h post-injection.

Strong fluorescence signals were consistently observed in tumor regions, confirming the robust tumor-targeting capability of this nanosystem. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs and tumor tissues further supported these findings (Fig. 4E). Tumors from the Cy5.5-GM@cGAMP + SB group displayed markedly higher fluorescence signals than those from the free Cy5.5 group. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 4F) showed good consistency among biological replicates.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that GM@cGAMP + SB exhibits efficient tumor-targeted biodistribution in vivo, providing a strong basis for subsequent immune activation and therapeutic efficacy.

scRNA-seq reveals cellular heterogeneity and immune network remodeling in the tC TME

To further elucidate the potential impact of GM@cGAMP + SB treatment on the immune landscape of the PTC TME, we first obtained scRNA-seq data of normal and tumor thyroid tissues from public databases, followed by a comprehensive analysis pipeline involving UMAP clustering, cell annotation, and cell-cell communication analysis (Fig. 5A). UMAP-based dimensionality reduction identified several major cellular subpopulations, including T cells, tumor cells, macrophages, DCs, NK cells, B cells, normal thyroid follicular cells (TFCs), fibroblasts, and endothelial cells (Fig. 5B-C). Feature gene expression profiling further confirmed the characteristic marker signatures of these cell subsets (Fig. 5D). Comparative analysis of cell proportions revealed a reduction in T cells, NK cells, and B cells, accompanied by an increase in tumor cells and macrophages within tumor tissues relative to normal tissues (Fig. 5E, Figure S5). Cell-cell interaction heatmap analysis demonstrated that both the number and intensity of intercellular interactions among multiple subpopulations were substantially remodeled in tumor tissues, suggesting that immune regulatory mechanisms were likely disrupted (Fig. 5F). Further gene expression analysis revealed that the immunosuppressive factor TGFB1 was markedly upregulated across several cell types within the tumor tissue, particularly in DCs, macrophages, and tumor cells, implying its crucial role in mediating immune evasion in PTC (Fig. 5G).

To verify the expression level of the STING pathway in tumor tissues, we focused on analyzing the distribution of its key molecules, cGAS and STING1, across different cell types. UMAP visualization showed that cGAS and STING1 exhibited a broader expression range in tumor tissues, predominantly localized within macrophages, tumor cells, and T cells, among other immune-related populations (Figure S6A-B). Although the cGAS-STING pathway displayed a detectable expression baseline in tumor tissues, its functional activity might still be compromised by immunosuppressive factors within the TME, such as TGF-β.

Taken together, these findings indicate that the immune cell composition and intercellular interaction patterns in the PTC TME are markedly altered, and that the immunosuppressive factor TGFB1 and the cGAS-STING pathway may play critical roles in modulating the TME.

GM@cGAMP + SB enhances sting signaling and suppresses TGF-β Activity

To elucidate the mechanism by which GM@cGAMP + SB activates the STING signaling pathway and inhibits TGF-β signaling, BCPAP cells were divided into five treatment groups (PBS, GM, GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB), and a series of mechanistic validation experiments were conducted (Fig. 6A). WB showed that GM treatment significantly increased the phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3, accompanied by elevated expression of the STING-induced type I interferon IFN-β1, compared with PBS. Incorporation of cGAMP further enhanced the levels of p-STING, p-TBK1, p-IRF3, and IFN-β1, whereas GM@SB alone produced no obvious change. Notably, the GM@cGAMP + SB combination further amplified the phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 relative to GM@cGAMP, suggesting that inhibition of TGF-β signaling relieves its suppressive constraint on the cGAS–STING pathway (Fig. 6B). These results support the hypothesis that active TGF-β signaling in tumor cells suppresses the cGAS–STING axis through molecular crosstalk, acting as a regulatory “brake.” Inhibition of this pathway by SB505124 therefore relieves this suppression and facilitates the phosphorylation cascade of downstream STING signaling components. Consistent with this mechanism, RT–qPCR analysis showed that the GM group exhibited significantly increased IFN-β1 and CXCL10 mRNA levels compared with PBS. The GM@cGAMP group displayed a further elevation of both transcripts, whereas GM@SB alone did not produce significant changes. Notably, the combination treatment (GM@cGAMP + SB) resulted in the highest expression levels of IFN-β1 and CXCL10, indicating enhanced activation of STING-mediated immune signaling (Fig. 6C).

Meanwhile, the inhibitory effect on the TGF-β signaling pathway was further examined. WB analysis showed that GM treatment did not significantly alter p-SMAD2/3 expression compared with PBS. In contrast, both GM@SB and GM@cGAMP + SB markedly reduced p-SMAD2/3 levels, whereas GM@cGAMP alone produced no significant change, indicating that SB-mediated blockade effectively suppresses TGF-β signaling (Fig. 6D). Consistently, RT-qPCR results demonstrated that TGF-β1 mRNA expression remained unchanged in the GM group compared with PBS, but was significantly downregulated in both the GM@SB and GM@cGAMP + SB groups, with no apparent change in the GM@cGAMP group (Fig. 6E). Similar results were obtained in TPC-1 cells (Figure S7A-D).

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the nanosystem coordinately modulates both the STING and TGF-β signaling pathways, achieving a bidirectional remodeling of the TME at the mechanistic level.

GM@cGAMP + SB synergistically activates STING and inhibits TGF-β to regulate immune activation and ICD response

To evaluate the effect of the GM@cGAMP + SB delivery system on the immunogenicity of tumor cells and the functional activation of immune cells, a Transwell co-culture system was established using DCs and BCPAP tumor cells. Five groups were included for comparison: PBS, GM, GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB (Fig. 7A). Flow cytometry showed that GM treatment significantly increased the expression of DC surface costimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 compared with PBS. This effect was further enhanced in the GM@cGAMP group, indicating that cGAMP promotes DC maturation through STING activation. The GM@SB group also exhibited increased CD80 and CD86 expression, suggesting that TGF-β pathway inhibition alleviates the suppressive constraints on DC maturation. Notably, the GM@cGAMP + SB group displayed the highest levels of CD80 and CD86, indicating a synergistic enhancement of DC maturation (Fig. 7B). We further evaluated the effect of GM@cGAMP + SB on DC functional activation. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that the GM@cGAMP + SB group exhibited the highest expression levels of MHC-II and MHC-I among all groups, significantly exceeding those observed in the GM@cGAMP and GM@SB groups (Fig. 7C-D). These results indicate that the combined delivery strategy synergistically enhances the antigen-presenting capacity and immune factor secretion of DCs, thereby eliciting a stronger antitumor immunogenic activation.

In the DC-CD8+ T cell co-culture system, flow cytometry demonstrated that GM treatment significantly increased the expression of the T cell activation markers CD69 and CD107a relative to PBS. This effect was further enhanced in the GM@cGAMP group, while GM@SB also promoted T cell activation, suggesting that blockade of the TGF-β pathway relieves immunosuppressive constraints. Notably, the GM@cGAMP + SB group exhibited the highest levels of CD69 and CD107a expression, indicating a synergistic enhancement of T cell activation (Fig. 7E-F). ELISA analysis showed progressive increases in IFN-γ secretion from GM to GM@cGAMP and GM@SB treatments, reaching the highest level in the GM@cGAMP + SB group (Fig. 7G).

Tumor cell immune responses were further examined. RT–qPCR and WB analyses revealed that PD-L1 expression was elevated following GM treatment and increased further in both GM@cGAMP and GM@SB groups, suggesting that enhanced antigen presentation triggered feedback immune regulation. The GM@cGAMP + SB group displayed the strongest PD-L1 upregulation (Figure S8A-B), indicating that the combined strategy enhanced immune activation while reinforcing feedback regulation.

To verify the occurrence of ICD, three classical ICD markers—CRT surface exposure, HMGB1 extracellular release, and ATP secretion—were analyzed. GM treatment significantly increased all three markers, while GM@cGAMP and GM@SB further amplified these responses, indicating enhanced tumor cell immunogenicity. The combination treatment (GM@cGAMP + SB) produced the most pronounced increases in all ICD indicators, demonstrating a superior capacity to induce ICD (Figure S9A-C).

Taken together, the GM@cGAMP + SB nanosystem effectively induces a robust ICD response, promotes DC maturation and antigen presentation, and markedly enhances CD8+ T cell activation. Collectively, these effects synergistically strengthen the in vitro antitumor immune response and provide strong mechanistic evidence supporting its potential in cancer immunotherapy.

GM@cGAMP + SB combination therapy synergistically activates the STING pathway and enhances antitumor immunity in vivo

To verify the synergistic activation of the STING pathway and the antitumor efficacy of the GM@cGAMP + SB delivery system in vivo, a HIS mouse model bearing subcutaneous BCPAP xenografts was established. The mice were divided into five treatment groups: PBS, GM, GM@cGAMP, GM@SB, and GM@cGAMP + SB (Fig. 8A). Tumor growth monitoring showed that GM treatment significantly suppressed tumor progression compared with PBS. Both GM@cGAMP and GM@SB further enhanced tumor inhibition, whereas the GM@cGAMP + SB combination group exhibited the slowest tumor growth rate, indicating a strong synergistic antitumor effect (Fig. 8B). Consistent with these findings, final tumor weights followed a similar trend. GM treatment reduced tumor burden relative to PBS, while GM@cGAMP and GM@SB produced additional reductions. The lowest tumor weight was observed in the GM@cGAMP + SB group, confirming the superior therapeutic efficacy of the combined treatment (Fig. 8C).

H&E staining revealed markedly enlarged necrotic regions in tumors from the GM-treated group compared with PBS. Necrotic areas were further expanded following GM@cGAMP and GM@SB treatments, with the GM@cGAMP + SB group displaying the most extensive tumor necrosis (Figure S10A). Consistently, Ki67, CRT, and TUNEL staining indicated significant suppression of tumor proliferation and enhanced apoptosis. GM treatment reduced the number of Ki67-positive proliferating cells, accompanied by increased CRT expression and TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells. These effects were further amplified in the GM@cGAMP and GM@SB groups, while the GM@cGAMP + SB treatment exhibited the lowest Ki67 positivity and the highest CRT expression and TUNEL staining intensity (Figure S10B-D).

Flow cytometric analysis showed that GM treatment significantly increased the proportions of mature DCs (CD11c+MHC-II+CD80+CD86+), M1 macrophages (CD11b+CD68+CD80+CD86+), and CD8+/CD4+ T cells, while decreasing the proportion of CD25+Foxp3+ Tregs compared with PBS. Relative to the GM group, both GM@cGAMP and GM@SB further enhanced the infiltration of effector immune cells and reduced immunosuppressive cell populations. Notably, compared with the GM@cGAMP group, the GM@cGAMP + SB group showed a more pronounced increase in effector immune cell infiltration and a further reduction in immunosuppressive cell frequency, indicating a synergistic enhancement of immune activation signaling (Fig. 8D-G).

To further assess STING pathway activation, WB analysis showed that GM treatment significantly increased the expression of p-STING, p-TBK1, p-IRF3, and IFN-β1 compared with PBS. These proteins were further elevated in the GM@cGAMP group, whereas GM@SB alone produced no significant change. Notably, the GM@cGAMP + SB combination induced the highest levels of these signaling molecules, indicating enhanced activation of the STING pathway (Figure S11A). In the evaluation of the TGF-β signaling pathway, WB results demonstrated that the GM group had no significant effect on p-SMAD2/3 expression, while both the GM@SB and GM@cGAMP + SB groups showed significant downregulation, and the GM@cGAMP group exhibited no noticeable change (Figure S11B). These findings confirm that the system effectively enhanced STING activation while simultaneously suppressing TGF-β signaling in vivo.

Regarding peripheral immune responses, ELISA results revealed that, compared with the PBS group, the GM group significantly increased serum levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ. The GM@cGAMP group further elevated these cytokines, while the GM@SB group showed a modest but non-significant increase. Notably, cytokine levels in the GM@cGAMP + SB group reached the highest values among all treatments (Fig. 8H).

For biosafety evaluation, H&E staining revealed no observable histopathological abnormalities or tissue damage in major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, across all five groups (Figure S12). Consistently, serum biochemical analyses indicated that the levels of ALT, AST, CREA, BUN, TBIL, and UA remained within normal physiological ranges, with no significant differences among groups, demonstrating the favorable systemic biocompatibility of the delivery system (Figure S13).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that GM@cGAMP + SB combination therapy effectively synergizes STING pathway activation and TGF-β pathway inhibition in vivo, thereby remodeling the TME, amplifying antitumor immune responses, and markedly suppressing tumor progression while maintaining excellent safety and tolerability.

Discussion

As a pivotal bridge between innate and adaptive immunity, the STING signaling pathway has recently garnered significant attention for the development of innovative cancer immunotherapy strategies [37, 38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that STING agonists, such as cGAMP, effectively induce the production of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines, thereby activating DCs and enhancing effector T-cell immune responses [39]. However, the immunosuppressive TME, particularly elevated TGF-β signaling, often dampens STING activation and limits its therapeutic efficacy [40, 41]. This challenge is especially evident in so-called “immune-cold” tumors, such as PTC, where the clinical outcomes of STING agonist-based therapies remain unsatisfactory. Therefore, developing synergistic strategies that enhance STING activation while alleviating TME-mediated immunosuppression holds substantial therapeutic promise.

The TGF-β signaling pathway represents one of the dominant immunosuppressive mechanisms within the TME. It suppresses the function of CD8+ T cells and DCs through SMAD-dependent signaling, while promoting the expansion of immunotolerant Tregs [19, 42]. Increasing evidence further indicates that TGF-β can directly attenuate cGAS–STING signaling by reducing the phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3, thereby weakening downstream immune activation [43]. A key finding of this study is that blockade of TGF-β signaling produces a pronounced de-repression effect, markedly enhancing cGAMP-induced phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3. This molecular synergy likely arises from several mechanisms. First, TGF-β1 can reduce basal STING and cGAS expression through both SMAD-dependent and SMAD-independent pathways [40]. Second, TGF-β may recruit protein phosphatases such as PPM1A to dephosphorylate p-STING or p-TBK1, thereby terminating signal transduction [44]. In addition, TGF-β has been reported to suppress the transcriptional activity of IRF3 after its nuclear translocation [41]. By removing these molecular “brakes” through SB505124-mediated inhibition of TGF-β signaling, the cGAMP and Mn2+ released from the nanozyme platform can more effectively drive type I interferon production, ultimately generating a synergistic immunostimulatory effect.

In this study, an intelligent nanodelivery platform, GM@cGAMP + SB, was developed to target this critical mechanistic intersection. The system achieves dual regulatory effects by simultaneously enhancing antitumor immune activation and alleviating TME-mediated immunosuppression. To our knowledge, this represents the first functional coupling strategy integrating STING activation with TGF-β blockade, thereby providing a promising translational framework for improving the clinical efficacy of STING-based immunotherapy.

Compared with conventional delivery systems, the MnO2 nanozyme platform developed in this study exhibits distinct multifunctional advantages. As a representative nanozyme material [29, 30], MnO2 possesses excellent drug-LC and can spontaneously degrade in the ROS-enriched TME, thereby releasing cGAMP and SB for on-demand responsive drug delivery. More importantly, the Mn2+ ions generated during MnO2 degradation act as cofactors to activate cGAS enzymatic activity, further amplifying STING pathway signaling. This dual enhancement mechanism fundamentally differs from previously reported cGAMP liposome or polymer-encapsulation strategies, providing both theoretical and experimental support for improved efficacy with reduced dosage. Notably, XRD characterization confirmed that the MnO₂ constructed in this study exhibits a low-crystallinity nanostructure, likely due to its in situ formation under mild conditions and encapsulation within dendrimers. This amorphous or microcrystalline state is advantageous for enhancing its chemical reactivity and ROS-responsive degradation efficiency within the TME [45].

This study further demonstrates that the MnO2 nanozyme functions not only as a drug carrier but also as an active regulator of intracellular stress responses. By enhancing oxidative stress, inducing mitochondrial damage, and reducing mitochondrial membrane potential, the nanozyme triggers ICD characterized by ATP release, HMGB1 secretion, and CRT surface exposure. These events promote the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby facilitating antigen recognition and activation of antigen-presenting cells. Compared with biologically inert delivery carriers, this stress-inducing capability provides a clear advantage in enhancing immunotherapeutic efficacy. Consistent with these findings, in vitro coculture assays confirmed that treatment with GM@cGAMP + SB markedly promoted DC maturation and proinflammatory cytokine expression, accompanied by enhanced CD8+ T-cell activation, indicating that the platform successfully initiates and amplifies the antitumor immune cascade. Interestingly, in the Transwell co-culture system, the GM blank carrier group also induced a moderate degree of DC maturation, highlighting the intrinsic immunoadjuvant activity of the MnO2 nanozyme platform. This effect likely arises from two complementary mechanisms. First, Mn2+ ions released during MnO₂ degradation act as essential cofactors for cGAS, enhancing the sensitivity of antigen-presenting cells to cytosolic DNA and amplifying innate immune signaling [46, 47]. Second, GM-induced ICD in tumor cells promotes the release of DAMPs, including CRT, HMGB1, and ATP, which function as danger signals that directly stimulate DC maturation [48]. Together, these mechanisms indicate that the MnO2 nanozyme platform itself possesses the capacity to remodel the tumor immune microenvironment, even in the absence of additional immunostimulatory agents.

To systematically characterize the cellular composition and signaling regulatory networks within the TME, this study, for the first time, integrated publicly available scRNA-seq data to perform a single-cell-resolution transcriptomic analysis of normal thyroid tissues and PTC tissues. A comprehensive cellular annotation map was constructed, identifying major subpopulations including T cells, B cells, NK cells, tumor cells, macrophages, and DCs. A heatmap of intercellular interactions further revealed the extensive remodeling of immune communication networks within tumor tissues. Notably, TGFB1 was markedly upregulated in DCs, macrophages, and tumor cells of PTC tissues, suggesting its pivotal role in shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Meanwhile, both cGAS and STING1 were found to be broadly expressed within tumor tissues, indicating that this pathway remains transcriptionally active in situ and supporting the feasibility of a “stimulation plus suppression-relief” synergistic therapeutic strategy.

In the in vivo experiments, a human immune system mouse model reconstituted with human PBMCs was employed, which more closely recapitulates human immune responses compared with conventional BALB/c or C57 immunodeficient mouse models. Therapeutic evaluation demonstrated that the GM@cGAMP + SB group exhibited the most pronounced tumor growth inhibition, accompanied by a significant increase in immune-activating populations within tumor tissues, including DCs, M1-like macrophages, and CD8+ T cells, while Tregs were markedly reduced. These findings indicate that this strategy effectively remodeled the TME and promoted the infiltration of effector immune cells. Regarding the observed significant reduction of CD25+Foxp3+ Tregs, we propose that this effect arises from the combined action of direct blockade by SB505124 and indirect antagonism mediated by a proinflammatory cytokine milieu. First, the direct effect stems from SB505124 acting as a highly selective ALK4/5/7 inhibitor [49], which directly blocks TGF-β signaling, a key driver of Foxp3 expression and the differentiation of inducible Tregs (iTregs), thereby interrupting the Treg differentiation pathway at its source. Second, the cascading immune activation triggered by the GM@cGAMP + SB system leads to the production of abundant proinflammatory cytokines, which exert critical indirect antagonistic effects. Previous studies have shown that IL-6 can completely inhibit the generation of TGF-β–induced Foxp3⁺ Tregs [50], TNF-α significantly impairs the differentiation efficiency and stability of iTregs [51], and type I interferons (e.g., IFN-β) can directly suppress both the functional activity and accumulation of Tregs within tumors [52]. The interplay between direct removal of the differentiation-driving signal and indirect environmental antagonism highlights the unique advantage of this system in precisely modulating the immunosuppressive TME. In addition, elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ in peripheral blood further corroborate the activation of systemic immune responses. Consistently, protein expression analyses also verified the functional regulation of both the STING signaling pathway and the TGF-β pathway under this therapeutic regimen, establishing a coherent mechanistic framework for the observed antitumor immune effects.

Compared with previous studies, the multi-target synergistic strategy proposed in this work provides important insights for the treatment of immune-cold tumors. PTC and other thyroid malignancies are typically characterized by low immune infiltration, limited immune activation, and strong immunosuppressive signaling, which restricts the therapeutic efficacy of conventional ICI monotherapy. By simultaneously activating the cGAS–STING pathway and alleviating TGF-β-mediated immunosuppression, the present study effectively remodeled the immunologically “cold” TME. These findings highlight the broad applicability and translational potential of nano-immunoengineering strategies for improving immunotherapeutic outcomes in refractory solid tumors.

In terms of safety, this study systematically evaluated multi-organ histopathology and serum biochemical indicators, and no obvious organ damage or inflammatory responses were detected, indicating that the MnO2-based platform exhibits favorable biocompatibility under controllable dosing conditions. Notably, the GM@cGAMP + SB system was assessed across a broad concentration range from 0 to 400 µg/mL, and this dose-dependent evaluation strategy is highly consistent with previously reported studies on MnO₂-based nanoplatforms [27]. In addition, regarding the tracer evaluation strategy, this study employed a dual-modal fluorescence labeling approach, combining visible-light fluorescence (FITC) for in vitro imaging and near-infrared fluorescence (Cy5.5) for in vivo imaging. This design, which optimizes probe performance according to different imaging scenarios, not only improves the spatial resolution of subcellular localization but also ensures sufficient tissue penetration depth and signal-to-noise ratio for in vivo tumor-targeting monitoring [53]. Such an imaging strategy provides valuable methodological reference for future translation toward large-animal studies and potential clinical applications. Compared with the immune-related adverse events reported for some STING agonists in clinical trials, the controlled-release and tumor-targeting properties of this nanoplatform may help improve therapeutic tolerability and safety profiles.

In summary, this study introduces an innovative immunotherapeutic strategy, GM@cGAMP + SB. Mechanistically, the system exerts dual modulation of tumor immune activation and suppression pathways, significantly induces ICD, and remodels the TME, ultimately enhancing antitumor efficacy. The system demonstrates robust therapeutic performance and favorable biosafety both in vitro and in vivo, offering a promising strategy for treating immune-cold tumors such as TC. Future studies should validate its efficacy in organoid models and patient-derived tumor tissues, while further optimizing drug ratios, release kinetics, and delivery strategies to facilitate safer and more efficient clinical translation. Overall, this work establishes a new framework for nanotechnology-enabled combinational immunotherapy and provides important insights for the development of precision immunotherapeutic approaches.
