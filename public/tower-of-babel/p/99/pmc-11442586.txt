The location of cationic substitutions in carbonated biomimetic apatites significantly affects crystal nanomechanics
Authors: Stephanie Wong, Abigail Eaton, Christina Krywka, Arun Nair, Alix Deymier
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Bone and teeth are comprised of carbonate-substituted apatites with cationic substitutions, like sodium and potassium. Cations substitute for calcium in the apatite lattice but it is unclear whether they substitute for Ca(1) or Ca(2). Additionally, although we know that anionic substitutions affect the mineral mechanics, it is unclear how cationic substitutions affect mineral stiffness. Here, a combined experimental and theoretical approach using in situ fluid-mediated hydrostatic loading with synchrotron Wide Angle X-ray Scattering (WAXS) and Density Functional Theory (DFT) is used to elucidate the role of CO32− and Na+ or K+ co-substitutions on the atomic structure and mechanics of biomimetic apatites. Comparison of WAXS and DFT results showed that preferential substitutions at the Ca(1) and Ca(2) sites depended on cationic type and concentration, with a preference for Ca(1) at higher levels of co-substitution. Substitution levels and location of the cationic substitution both significantly affected the modulus of the minerals. This presents a new paradigm for the development of biomimetic apatites with multi-property tunability by considering composition and atomic organization.

Introduction

Mineralized tissues, like bone and teeth, are primarily composed of apatite, a type of calcium phosphate (Fig. 1)1. This inorganic mineral phase is responsible for the providing tissue strength and stiffness2,3. Although often reported as hydroxylapatite, biological apatite is highly substituted resulting in distinctly modified properties4. Understanding the effect of these substitutions on mineral composition, structure, and mechanics is critical for understanding their effect on bone health in homeostatic and pathological situations and for the development of tunable biomimetic mineralized tissue engineering.Figure 1The effects of concomitant Na+/K+ and CO32− substitutions in aqueously precipitated biomimetic apatites were investigated using a combined theoretical and experimental approach. The effects of concomitant Na+/K+ and CO32− substitutions in aqueously precipitated biomimetic apatites were investigated using a combined theoretical and experimental approach. Density functional theory and high-energy X-ray Diffraction approaches were utilized to measure the atomic and lattice changes as well as modifications to the mechanical properties of the crystals.

Bone mineral has been shown to contain between 2 and 6 wt% carbonate (CO32−)5. This CO32− can either exchange for PO43− (referred to as B-type) or for OH− groups (A-type)6. An increase in CO32− content has been shown to decrease crystal size and atomic order while increasing the mineral solubility7–10. The presence of CO32− also significantly reduces the stiffness of apatite which could have significant consequences on biological tissue health and function7,11. However, CO32− substitutions do not occur independently of the surrounding apatite structure. Due to the charge imbalances induced by both B- and A-type CO32− substitutions, such compositional changes are generally accompanied by a substitutional exchange of calcium (Ca2+) for monovalent cations like sodium (Na+) or potassium (K+)12–16. The simultaneous uptake of these cations alongside CO32− has recently been shown to affect the preferential location of the CO32− within the apatite structure as well as induce significant modifications to the lattice structure14.

Despite understanding the effect of cationic uptake on CO32− substitutions, the distribution of cations within the apatite lattice remains unclear. The ability for Na+ to incorporate easily into the lattice is mainly due to its similar ionic size to Ca2+ 17, including other factors18, allowing for a deeper understanding of this ion’s effect on the lattice19–21. In Na-carbonated apatites with compositions similar to biological mineral, it has been suggested that Na+ preferentially substitutes in the Ca(2) location where the metal ions form equilateral triangles along the c-axis, creating what is known as the anion channel, Fig. 122,23. For K+, it is unknown where it substitutes within the lattice. The larger ionic radius is a primary reason for its inability to incorporate as readily and, thus, less information is known about this ion within biological apatites. Strontium, which has a larger ionic radius similar to K+17, has been shown to substitute in both the Ca(1) and Ca(2) locations depending on concentration24,25, suggesting that K+ may have a similar mechanism. While previous studies have given great insight on apatite substitutions primarily for Na+ 26, these apatites have been synthesized at temperatures and pressures significantly higher than what occurs in vivo and does not provide the distinctive structural properties of biogenic apatite, such as the amorphous hydrated layer27. Ultimately, it is not known how either Na+ or K+ substitutes within apatites of biological relevance. Therefore, to truly understand the effect of substitutions on bone mineral, it becomes necessary to examine the location of cationic substitutions both as a function of cationic type and concentration.

Due to the complexity of bone mineral and the difficulty in controlling its composition and mechanical behavior, there has been a need to develop biomimetic apatites with controlled compositional, structural, and mechanical properties28. These apatites are generally formed via aqueous precipitation techniques and mimic the nanocrystalline size and hydrated structure of biological apatites8,29; however, they can also be compositionally and structurally modified to exhibit specific functional properties. For example, it has previously been shown that increased CO32− content in biomimetic apatites significantly reduced the mineral Young’s modulus by up to 50%7. These studies posited that this was primarily caused by the formation of calcium vacancies in the network and increased atomic disorder; however, it failed to account for the possible simultaneous substitutions of cations. Therefore, in this study, biomimetic bone-like apatites exhibiting concurrent CO32− and cationic substitutions with either Na+ or K+ will be investigated for several physiologically relevant levels of CO32−. This will be done by combining state of the art Synchrotron Wide Angle X-ray Scattering (WAXS) with in situ fluid-mediated hydrostatic loading in parallel with Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations.

Results and discussion

Substitutional location of Na+ and K+ is concentration dependent

A unique suite of apatites was created using aqueous precipitation to obtain not only variable levels of carbonate substitutions (2, 4, and 6 wt%) but increasing levels of sodium and potassium substitutions as well. The amount of CO32− was controlled to represent the levels of CO32− found in mineralized tissues while the amount of sodium and potassium was allowed to freely substitute. These apatites were fully characterized in14 showing that the addition of cations into the lattice significantly affected the carbonate environment as well as the atomic structure. Here, we used synchrotron X-ray diffraction with in situ hydrostatic loading and density functional theory to elucidate the location of the substitutions as well as their consequences on the nanoscale mechanical properties of the apatites.

The substitution of cations (Na+, K+) and anions (CO32−) into the apatite lattice has been shown to significantly affect the lattice structure30,31. The substitution of Na+ has been associated with an increase in the c-axis spacing and a decrease in the a-axis22 while K+ caused an increase in spacing along both axes14,16. The c-axis and a-axis lattice spacings of the Na- and K-containing carbonated apatites made here were calculated from the location of the 002 and 310 X-ray diffraction peaks in the unloaded samples. Both the Na- and K-substituted apatites exhibited an expansion of the c-axis lattice spacing and a contraction of the a-axis lattice spacing with increasing CO32− as expected for apatites with primarily B-type CO32− substitutions (Fig. 2A)8,32. At low CO32−, the a- and c-axis lattice spacings were identical between the Na- and K-containing apatites. However, the change in both the a and c-axis with increasing CO32− was significantly larger in the Na-containing apatites (− 0.8% and 0.4% for a and c respectively) than the K-containing apatites (− 0.5% and 0.3% for a and c). As a result, the c-axis spacing shifts from being larger in the K-containing sample at 4% to being larger in the Na-containing apatite at 6%. The opposite is true for the a-axis. Computational predictions using DFT of the K- and Na-apatite d-spacings using the assumption that the cations substituted in the Ca(2) positions (Fig. 2B) and that all CO32− substitutions were B-type did not completely replicate the trends between Na+ and K+ seen in the experimental d-spacing. This is particularly true for the c-axis for K-apatite and Na-apatite at 6 wt% CO32− as well as the a-axis K-apatite at 4 wt% CO32− (Fig. 2B). To recapitulate the experimental trends between Na- and K-containing apatites in the model, we developed four additional computational models with the K+ (4%) and Na+ (6%) ion in the Ca(1) position and K+ (6%) and Na+ (4%) in the Ca(2) position (Fig. 2C). This shift at specific amounts of CO32− and cationic substitutions indicate that the location of K+ and Na+ cations in particular calcium sites within the apatite lattice structure was highly dependent on the cationic concentrations. This agrees with previous work suggesting cations like Na+ and Mg+ preferentially substitute for calcium in the Ca(2) location in substituted apatites, but other ions like Mn2+ and Sr2+ can also substitute in the Ca(1) position depending on concentration22,23,33. We also compared the ionic radius of Na+ and K+ to determine how their substitutions may affect the volume of the apatite cells. Though K+ has a larger ionic radius than Ca2+, K+ will only result in a 0.4% increase in total volume of the cell as CO32− increases from 0 to 6 wt%. However, while Ca2+ and Na+ are much closer in size, Na+ substitution will result in a 1.3% decrease in the unit cell volume of the apatite at 6 wt% CO32− (Fig. 2D). These ionic radii are represented in Fig. 2E for 6% wt CO32− substitution. The variance in volume indicates that, with the presence of more Na+ substitutions, the a- and c-axis spacing of these apatites deviate more than those with K+ substitutions despite the similarity of size of Na+ and Ca2+ ionic radii. This is specifically reflected in the decrease of a-axis spacing for the experimental results of Fig. 2A. In the context of mineralized tissues, this suggests that bone and teeth may have significant structural changes when surrounded by high Na+ concentrations in the fluid environment. It also suggests that shifts from the very different chemical environments of Ca(1) and Ca(2) could significantly impact the mineral hydration34, solubility, and mechanics. This provides a new avenue for developing biomimetic apatites with multi-property tunability.

Apatite mechanical properties are highly dependent on cationic substitutions

Due to the nanometer size of apatite crystals as well as their hydrated state, it has been extremely difficult to measure their mechanical properties. However, to understand the effect of variable CO32−, Na+, and K+ concentrations as well as variations in the locations of the substitutions on the mechanical properties of apatites, we adapted a relatively novel technique35,36 in which the crystals were mechanically loaded using fluid mediated hydrostatic loading with in situ synchrotron X-ray diffraction7,11,37 (Fig. 3). The apatites were suspended in ethanol and pressurized step-wise up to 500 MPa. The lattice strain for both the K- and Na-containing apatites—calculated from the change in d-spacing—increased linearly as a function of applied hydrostatic load (Fig. 4). This suggests that all experiments remained within the elastic loading zone. The bulk modulus, κ, of the Na-containing apatites exhibited a clear 11% decrease with increasing CO32− content from 2 to 6 wt% in agreement with previous measures of CO32− substituted apatites with low Na+ content7 (Fig. 5B). In the K-containing apatites, there was an initial 6% decrease in bulk modulus from 2 to 4 wt% CO32− which was recovered at 6 wt% CO32−. Bulk moduli were similar between Na+ and K+ groups, except at 6 wt% CO32− where the K-containing apatites exhibited a significantly higher κ than the Na-containing apatites (84.9 GPa for K-apatites and 78.9 GPa for Na-apatites). The general behavior of the bulk modulus as a function of Na/K and CO32− content is recapitulated in the DFT model within error (Fig. 5A). The DFT elastic constant data were used to provide initial guesses for the elastic constants of the Na- and K-containing apatites. These were used alongside established optimization procedures to calculate the experimental elastic constants of the apatites7. There were discrepancies between the predicted DFT values and the experimental values (Fig. 6). For the Na-containing apatites, the experimental elastic constants, except for C33, were generally larger than the initial guesses and exhibited a steeper decrease with increasing CO32− content. For the K-containing apatites, the elastic constants were generally larger than the initial DFT guesses except for C33 as well. In addition, the optimized experimental values showed different trends with a steeper significant decrease in C11, C13, and C12 at 4 wt% CO32−, which was recovered in part at 6 wt% CO32−. This may be caused by the shift in substitutional locations. Discrepancies between the experimental and computational compliance values are likely due to the nano-scale nature of the apatite crystals which causes significant surface effects and reduced crystallinity, whereas in DFT calculations a bulk apatite crystal (periodic in all directions) is studied. Although the probability distribution of the elastic constants broadened with increasing standard deviation of the initial guess, the mean values of all of the elastic constants remained the same within approximately ± 1.5 GPa.Figure 6Plot of the DFT and experimentally determined elastic constants for Na- and K-containing apatites as a function of carbonate content.

Plot of the DFT and experimentally determined elastic constants for Na- and K-containing apatites as a function of carbonate content.

These optimized elastic constants were then used to determine the Young’s moduli of the apatites. The Young’s moduli of the Na-containing apatites stayed relatively constant along the a-axis and increased along the c-axis with increasing CO32− content (Fig. 7). This trend was well recapitulated by the DFT model for E11 but not for E33. These results are in partial agreement with the previously recorded decrease in E11 and E33 measured for CO32− substituted apatites with low Na+ levels7. In the low Na+ apatites, it was determined that the decrease in modulus may result from the formation of Ca2+ vacancies leading to replacement of covalent Ca-O bonding with weaker hydrogen bonding38,39. These vacancies may have predominantly existed in the Ca(2) sites as Ca(1) has been suggested to destabilize the apatite structure40. However, with the addition of Na+, multiple Na–O bonds can be formed. Although these bonds are weaker than the initial Ca-O bonds present in hydroxylapatite, they may increase bond strength compared to Ca2+ vacancies, significantly affecting the apatite mechanics. Of course, the substitution of Na+ into the lattice structure does not eliminate the possible presence of vacancies, which may continue to contribute to the reduced crystal modulus. Similarly, the K-containing apatites exhibited a decrease in E11 with increasing CO32− which was well captured by the DFT model. As for the E33, K-containing apatites showed an initial increase in the elastic moduli between 2 and 4 wt% CO32− followed by a decrease at 6 wt%, likely due to the change in the location of the K+. K–O has the lowest bond energy of the three cations, but its shift from a smaller space of the Ca(2) site to a larger location of the Ca(1) site at higher concentrations may be responsible for stabilizing the apatite40, the increased coordination number, and overall apatite mechanics. The discrepancy between the DFT models and the experimental results for E33, especially at 4%, may result from the initial a-axis spacing difference between experimental and computational methods. The 4 wt% samples, for both K- and Na-containing apatites, have similar a-axis spacings that differ by 0.02%. There is a more notable difference between the experimental Na and K 4 wt% samples’ a-axis spacing. This effect on the a-axis spacing may also be responsible for the difference noted for the C11, C12, and C13 elastic constants at 4 wt% Na, as these constants are all determined in DFT by straining the a-axis. These elastic constant variances, which are then utilized for the approximation of E11 and E33, show the propagation of the difference between a-axis spacings of the experimental and DFT models for the E33 modulus. The 6 wt% data displays a more consistent pattern for E11 and E33, as there is more consistency in the a-axis spacing comparison across methods for the higher composition of both Na and K substitutions. There may also be more variability in the experimental values that are not shown here because synchrotron experiments require us to test only a small number of samples.Figure 7(A,B) Experimentally and (C,D) Computationally determined Young’s moduli for the Na- and K-containing apatites as a function of carbonate content.

Overall, combined in silico and state-of-the-art experimental techniques were used to determine the effects of cationic/anionic co-substitutions on biomimetic apatites. Comparison of XRD determined d-spacing with DFT predictions indicated that both Na+ and K+ undergo concentration dependent shifts in substitutional location from Ca(2) to Ca(1) with increasing substitutions. This change in location results in increased bulk moduli and reduced Young’s modulus especially in the case of K+ substitutions. This work points to a new paradigm in the development of tunable biomimetic apatites that uses compositional changes to promote atomic lattice variations and induce further controllable chemical and mechanical properties.

Methods

Synthesis

Nanocrystalline apatite samples were precipitated in either Na- or K-rich environments according to and characterized in14. In short, for the Na-rich apatites, 0.295 M of calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca(NO3)2∙4H2O, Sigma Life Science) was added to a solution with a 1:1 CO32−/PO43− molar ratio of 0.91 M of diammonium hydrogen phosphate ((NH4)2HPO4, Carlo Erba reagents) and 1.43 M sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, VWR Normapur Prolabo). For the K-rich apatites, the level of carbonate was varied by changing the CO32−/PO43− molar ratio to 1:1 or 2:1 using potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3, Rectapur Prolabo). The precipitates were allowed to mature for either 20 min, 1 day, or 3 days at room temperature or 60 °C to respectively attain CO32− levels of 2, 4, and 6 wt% for each cationic condition to mimic bone mineral compositions. Powders were then filtered, rinsed with deionized water, and lyophilized.

DFT

The hydroxylapatite crystal has a structure with 44 atoms in the hexagonal primitive cell39,41–45 (space group P63/m and a 50% partial occupancy of the OH sites) (Supplementary Materials). The crystal consists of tightly bonded PO4 tetrahedral units with two types of Ca ions and the OH groups. The OH is aligned along the crystalline c-axis. There are two Ca sites, Ca I and Ca II, that are usually labeled as columnar Ca and axial Ca, respectively, and which play the role of ionically bonding the PO4 units together. Hydroxyapatite crystallizes in a hexagonal lattice (a, b and c axes) with the OH group oriented along the c-axis. For studying mechanical properties, it is more practical to transform it into an orthorhombic cell (x, y and z axes) with the z-axis parallel to the c-axis as illustrated in Fig. 1. However, it is necessary to double the b-axis direction to maintain the periodicity of the lattice in the orthorhombic description. We use Quantum ESPRESSO46,47 with ultrasoft pseudopotentials48–50, PBE exchange–correlation functional type51, a kinetic energy cutoff of 1225 eV (90 Ry), and a 2 × 2 × 2 K-point mesh. This K-point mesh was selected based on energy convergence studies with different mesh sizes. Grimme’s DFT-D2 van der Waals scheme was also used for the correction of dispersion forces, as this has been noted to impact the properties of the hydroxyapatite and is necessary for accurate calculations52–54. Lattice parameters are determined using a variable-cell relaxation prior to elastic constant calculations.

Elastic constants of a material determine its material stiffness and resistance to deformation. Predicting the elastic constants41,42 using DFT has been well established within the scientific community and has been applied to a wide range of material systems. Recent studies of hydroxyapatite elastic constants using DFT have been validated by experiments, thus giving us the confidence to use the method in predicting the elastic properties of hydroxyapatite substituted with Na and K ions8,39. Hydroxyapatite has also been successfully modelled using ab initio methods for both A- and B-type carbonate substitutions54–56.

The elastic constants are computed by fitting Hooke’s law for energy-strain relations for infinitesimal deformation57. Mechanical testing

Apatite crystal powders were packed into a 7 mm3 cavity of sample holders with two flexible, x-ray-transparent polyimide (Kapton) windows (Fig. 3A). 100% ethanol (EtOH) was then added to the cavity to ensure that the powders were suspended in fluid and the sample carrier was sealed via an O-ring screw. The sample holders were placed in a custom-made pressurization chamber with X-ray transparent entry and exit diamond windows encasing the sample7,11,37,58,59 (Fig. 3B). The chamber was then filled with the pressurizing fluid: tap water, that never interacted with the powders. Water and ethanol are slightly compressible (K = 2.15 and 0.9 GPa, respectively); however, they remain an excellent means to apply pressure to the apatite nanoparticles. The fluids were manually pressurized via a screw pump such that the powders were gradually compressed in increments of ~ 100 MPa up to ~ 500 MPa (Fig. 3C) and finally returning the starting pressure to ensure reversibility of the detected changes.

X-ray diffraction measurements

The pressure chamber was mounted at the P07 High Energy Materials Science beamline of the PETRA III synchrotron radiation source (DESY, Hamburg, DE) in the experimental hutch EH3 operated by Helmholtz-Zentrum Hereon60. The pressure chamber was oriented such that the 1 × 1 mm2 73.8 keV X-ray beam passed through the diamond and Kapton windows, interacting with the pressurized powder and thereafter exiting towards the detector (Fig. 3). The lanthanum hexaboride (LaB6) calibrated sample to detector distance, z, was 1.53 m and was selected to guarantee that both the apatite (002) and (310) peaks were recorded. Five diffraction patterns were captured at each load increment on a 2048 × 2048 pixel Si detector (Perkin Elmer) with an acquisition time of 10 s (Fig. 3D). Holders containing just Ethanol were also run at each load to obtain background diffraction images.

X-ray diffraction patterns were analyzed as previously described in11,35,61,62 and summarized here. Background patterns taken for the EtOH at each load were subtracted from the powder spectra. The corrected patterns were then converted from polar to Cartesian coordinates. The apatite (002), (310), and (004) peaks were fit using a pseudo-Voigt function to find the average peak center (R) and full-width-half-max (FWHM) at each angular position (φ). As a result of the hydrostatic stress as well as the randomly dispersed powder, R remained constant across all φ; therefore, the average of R(φ) was used for all diffraction patterns. The peak center, Rave, was then converted to d-spacing according to the equation: d = λ/2sin(R/z), assuming the diffraction angle is small. The lattice strain of each set of planes was calculated from ε = (do − d)/do. (002) peak strains represent deformation along the crystal c-axis and are referred to as ε33. (310) strains represent deformation along the a-axis and are referred to as ε11.

Optimization

To optimize the values of C11, C12, C13, and C33 from the hydrostatic data, two assumptions were made: (1) the optimized values must result in K and η equal to the experimental values and (2) the residual sum of square differences between the optimized values and DFT-determined initial guesses must be minimized7,37. Initial guesses of C11, C12, C13, and C33 were taken from the DFT models described above. Guess variability in the form of a standard deviation from the mean compliance values was added to the optimization to account for possible discrepancies between then guesses and experimental values. These were run with standard deviations of 1, 5, and 10 GPa to confirm that the results converged to the similar values and that the optimization was sensitive and accurate. Using the mean and standard deviations of the calculated C11, C12, C13, and C33 at each composition from the DFT model, the elastic constants were linearly interpolated between 0 and 6 wt% carbonate. At each value of carbonate, the elastic constants were randomly generated from a normal distribution with the mean parameter being calculated from the mean and standard deviation values determined from DFT. With established guesses, the elastic constants were optimized using the previously discussed assumptions. Since elastic constants were optimized each of the 6 measured stresses for each sample, sample internal variability was accounted for in addition to guess variability.
