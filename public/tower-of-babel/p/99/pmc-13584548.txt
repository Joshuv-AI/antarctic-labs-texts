Malpractice fear and career decision regret among resident physicians: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Arif Garbioğlu, Erdi Kutlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalpractice litigation is increasingly prevalent in contemporary healthcare and may influence both clinical decision-making and physicians’ psychological well-being. The present study aims to evaluate the fear of malpractice and career decision regret among resident physicians and to identify factors associated with these constructs.MethodsA total of 308 resident physicians (87.7% of the eligible population) from surgical and non-surgical clinical specialties at a university hospital in Turkey were included. Fear of malpractice was assessed via the Malpractice Fear Scale, and career decision regret was assessed via the Decision Regret Scale. We collected data on sociodemographic characteristics, specialty preferences, and exposure to malpractice complaints. Multivariate linear regression identified independent predictors of fear of malpractice and career decision regret. ResultsHigh levels of fear of malpractice were reported by 73.1% of the residents, and 73.4% experienced varying degrees of career decision regret, predominantly mild regret (44.2%). Fear of malpractice and career decision regret were modestly (rs = 0.228, p < 0.001) but significantly associated. Higher fear of malpractice scores (β = 0.178, p = 0.001) and prior personal exposure to malpractice complaints (β = 0.132, p = 0.017) independently predict increased career decision regret. Awareness of colleagues accused of malpractice was strongly associated with greater fear of malpractice (β = 0.219, p < 0.001). Intrinsic motivations, including interest in the chosen specialty (β = −0.176, p = 0.001) and professional satisfaction (β = −0.147, p = 0.007), were associated with lower career decision regret. No significant differences in fear of malpractice were observed between residents in surgical and non-surgical clinical specialties (p = 0.227).ConclusionsFear of malpractice is a prevalent stressor among resident physicians and is significantly associated with career decision regret. While malpractice-related experiences may increase regret, intrinsic motivations for specialty choice—particularly personal interest and professional satisfaction—appear to buffer against career decision regret. These findings suggest that strengthening both medicolegal support and factors that promote professional fulfillment may help improve residents’ career well-being. Integrating medicolegal education, psychological support, and institutional risk-management strategies into residency programs may help mitigate these adverse effects.Keywords: Resident physician, Fear of malpractice, Career decision regret, Health law

Background

Malpractice is harmful to a patient because of a physician’s failure to apply standard treatment, a lack of professional competence, or failure to deliver treatment [1]. The increasing number of medical malpractice claims in Turkey and globally has intensified debates within the field of medical law [2]. A study focusing on the misdiagnosis of major diseases across all care settings estimated that 2.59 million diagnostic errors occur in the United States each year, resulting in approximately 371,000 deaths and 424,000 permanent disabilities due to misdiagnosis [3]. Additionally, a systematic review revealed that 28% of autopsied intensive care unit patients reported at least one misdiagnosis [4]. These findings underscore that medical error remains an inherent challenge across healthcare systems worldwide. As global awareness of diagnostic errors and patient safety increases, physicians in many countries, including Turkey, face growing medicolegal scrutiny, which may contribute to concerns about malpractice and its professional consequences.

Medicolegal concerns have become increasingly prominent in Turkey. Although comprehensive national data on medical malpractice claims remain limited, available evidence suggests a substantial increase in medicolegal activity over recent decades. Data from the Council of Forensic Medicine indicate that the number of medical malpractice files referred for expert evaluation increased from 295 in 2004 to 3,006 in 2013, representing a more than ten-fold increase [2]. Furthermore, according to data presented to the Turkish Grand National Assembly, 5,933 medical malpractice lawsuits were filed between 2012 and 2022 [5]. This growing medicolegal burden may contribute to heightened malpractice-related concerns among physicians, including resident physicians.

In contemporary healthcare systems, malpractice constitutes not only a legal concern but also a salient psychosocial factor that influences physicians’ decision-making processes and professional satisfaction. Physicians may adopt ‘defensive medicine’ practices in an effort to protect themselves against possible litigation risks. Defensive medicine refers to deviations from sound clinical practice that are primarily motivated by concerns about potential malpractice claims. This attitude among physicians leads to their medical decisions being shaped by legal concerns rather than by clinical necessity [6]. Physicians may adopt defensive medicine practices, such as ordering medically unnecessary tests and consultations or avoiding high-risk cases. This situation not only imposes additional financial burdens on the healthcare system but also negatively impacts the quality of patient-centered care [7]. The diagnostic error rate, which varies by clinical context, is estimated to account for up to 15% of diagnoses [8].

The fear of malpractice (hereafter called FM) creates both systemic and individual strain on physicians. As physicians’ FM increases, job satisfaction decreases [9]. FM significantly contributes to the burnout of healthcare workers [10]. All these adverse factors may contribute to occupational stress and lead physicians to question their career choices. Young physicians primarily choose medicine out of a desire for respectability and to help others. However, these negative factors can gradually erode this idealistic perspective. This situation can lead to burnout, loss of motivation, and regret about career choices among physicians [11, 12]. Physicians ask themselves questions such as “Was choosing this profession the right decision?” or “Should I have chosen a different specialty?” during their residency or fellowship. Lu et al. [13] reported that increased feelings of mistreatment in the workplace among emergency medicine residents were associated with burnout and career regret.

Several theoretical perspectives provide a conceptual basis for the potential relationship between FM and career decision regret (hereafter called DR) during residency. According to Conservation of Resources theory, individuals experience psychological stress when valued personal, professional, or social resources are threatened or depleted [14]. Within residency training, persistent concerns about malpractice and its medicolegal consequences may be perceived as threats to professional confidence and emotional well-being, thereby increasing psychological distress. Such stress may contribute to residents questioning the suitability of their specialty choice and experiencing greater career DR. Conversely, intrinsic motivation may serve as a personal psychological resource that helps buffer the adverse effects of occupational stress and workplace challenges [15]. In parallel, theories of professional identity formation propose that residency is a critical period during which physicians integrate personal values, professional roles, and career aspirations into a coherent professional identity [16, 17]. Medicolegal concerns may challenge this developmental process by undermining confidence in one’s professional role, whereas strong intrinsic motivation may facilitate adaptation to these challenges. These theoretical perspectives provide a conceptual framework for examining the relationships among FM, intrinsic motivation, and career DR in resident physicians.

The present study aimed to determine the levels of FM and career DR among resident physicians from different specialties, as well as the factors influencing their attitudes toward these issues.

Methods

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional descriptive design.

Sample size and participants

The population of this study consisted of resident physicians at Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University Faculty of Medicine Hospital. At the beginning of the study, the total number of resident physicians at the university hospital was 351. The study was conducted with 308 participants who voluntarily agreed to participate, and 87.7% of the population was included in the study. Cohen’s criteria were used for sample size estimation [18]. On the basis of an a priori power analysis (effect size d = 0.40, α = 0.05, 1 – β = 0.85), using the Mann–Whitney U test, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 228 participants. This calculation was based on the planned comparison between residents in surgical and non-surgical clinical specialties during the study design phase. Participants were therefore stratified according to specialty group (surgical specialties vs. non-surgical clinical specialties). Because multivariate linear regression analyses were subsequently performed, we additionally evaluated the adequacy of the final sample size for regression modeling. Assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.177), α = 0.05, and 1 – β = 0.95, the minimum required sample size for the regression model was estimated to be 124 participants. Therefore, the final sample of 308 participants exceeded both the minimum sample size estimated by the a priori analysis and commonly recommended sample-size requirements for multivariate regression analyses. G*Power 3.1.9.2 was used to analyze the sample size.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criterion was resident physicians working in surgical specialties (e.g., General Surgery, Orthopedic Surgery, Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery) or non-surgical clinical specialties (e.g., Internal Medicine, Emergency Medicine, Pediatrics) at a university hospital who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study.

The exclusion criteria included specialist physicians, faculty members, nurses, pharmacists, and other healthcare personnel. Residents in basic medical sciences were excluded because their small sample size did not allow meaningful statistical comparison with the study groups. Additionally, records with inconsistencies and incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection procedure

The questionnaire was developed by the authors and administered in paper-based format. Resident physicians working in hospital wards and outpatient clinics were approached on designated days and times. The objectives of the study, the intended use of the collected data, and the plan to disseminate findings in an anonymized format were explained to all eligible participants. Participation was entirely voluntary. Those who agreed to participate were provided with paper-based questionnaires to complete individually in a private setting to safeguard confidentiality and reduce the potential for response bias. Approximately 48 h later, the same residents were revisited, and the completed questionnaires were collected. If residents were unavailable due to on-call duties or post-call leave, their departments were revisited on alternate days to maximize participation. This approach resulted in a participation rate of 87.7%. Upon collection, all the questionnaires were systematically checked for completeness before analysis.

Measurements

The survey form was prepared by researchers through a literature review [9, 19, 20]. It consists of three sections. The first section includes items that inquire about the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants (such as sex, age, marital status, etc.), their field of specialization choices, and whether they have faced a malpractice complaint against themselves or their colleagues.

The second section contains items from the DR scale. The DR scale is a measurement tool designed to assess regret experienced following a specific decision developed by Brehaut et al. [21]. A study of the validity and reliability of the scale was conducted by Erdurcan and Kırdök [22]. During the adaptation of the scale into Turkish, attention was given to minimizing potential misinterpretations in research settings, maintaining objectivity in both administration and scoring, and ensuring ease of score calculation. Therefore, in the Turkish version, the scale is arranged on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Items 1, 3, and 5 are reverse-coded, and the summed item scores are multiplied by five to yield a total score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores on this scale reflect greater levels of DR. A total score of 0–24 indicates ‘no decision regret’, 25–49 reflects ‘mild decision regret’, 50–74 denotes ‘moderate decision regret’, and 75–100 corresponds to ‘severe decision regret’ [22].

The third section of the survey includes the FM scale developed by Katz et al. [23] to measure physicians’ levels of FM. It consists of 6 items measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree”). Total scores range from 6 to 30, with higher scores reflecting greater levels of FM. The instrument further categorizes responses into three levels: low (< 15), medium (≥ 15 to < 20), and high (≥ 20) FM. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Uğrak and Işık [24].

The internal consistency of the scales in the present sample was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.888 for the DR scale and 0.820 for the FM scale.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) statistical software package. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are reported as the means, standard deviations, medians, minimums, and maximum values, whereas categorical variables are summarized using frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test used for test of normality. According to the normality results, Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two independent groups.

Multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of the FM and DR scale scores. Regression assumptions were assessed by examining multicollinearity using variance inflation factor (VIF) values, independence of errors using the Durbin–Watson statistic, normality of residuals using histograms of standardized residuals and normal probability (P–P) plots, homoscedasticity using standardized residual scatterplots, and influential observations using Cook’s distance values and casewise diagnostics. The diagnostic evaluation demonstrated no substantial violations of the assumptions of linear regression in either model. VIF values indicated no evidence of problematic multicollinearity, and the Durbin–Watson statistics were 1.834 and 2.003 for the DR and FM regression models, respectively, indicating no substantial autocorrelation in the residuals. Histograms and normal probability (P–P) plots showed that residuals were approximately normally distributed, whereas standardized residual scatterplots showed no evidence of substantial heteroscedasticity. Cook’s distance values remained well below the conventional threshold of 1 in both models (maximum values: 0.095 and 0.116), indicating that the identified outlying observations were not influential. A backward elimination approach was used to derive the final models.

Associations between categorical variables were examined using the Pearson chi-square test. Correlations between continuous variables were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in compliance with ethical principles according to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinic Research Ethics Committee of Zonguldak Bulent Ecevit University (No. 2025/07, Date April 09, 2025). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All the data were anonymized and handled with strict confidentiality.

Results

A total of 308 resident physicians were included in the analysis. The majority were female (51.0%), with a mean age of 29.15 ± 3.38 years (range: 24–45) and a median age of 28.5 years. Among the participants, 65.3% were single, and 57.5% were resident physicians in non-surgical clinical specialties (Table 1). The mean duration of professional experience in the field of expertise was 22.48 ± 15.22 (range: 1–66) months (Table 1). Table 2 summarizes the reasons reported by resident physicians for choosing their specialty. Regarding malpractice exposure, 18.2% reported having personally been the subject of a malpractice complaint, 70.1% indicated having colleagues who had been accused of malpractice, and 22.1% reported being aware of physicians in their professional environment who had received penalties due to malpractice (Table 3).

Scale scores

The mean score on the DR scale among the resident physicians was 35.9 ± 23.1, whereas the mean score on the FM scale was 21.8 ± 4.5. According to the DR scale categorization, 26.6% of the participants reported no DR, 44.2% reported mild DR, 22.4% reported moderate DR, and 6.8% reported severe DR. According to the FM scale, 6.8% of the participants reported low-level fear, 20.1% reported moderate-level fear, and 73.1% reported high-level FM. A statistically significant association was found between the categories of the DR and FM scales (p = 0.0013).

Correlation analysis revealed that FM scores were positively associated with both DR scores and duration of practice in the specialty (rs = 0.228, p < 0.001; rs = 0.132, p = 0.020). No significant correlation was observed between DR scores and duration of practice in the specialty (rs = − 0.024, p = 0.677) (Table 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined FM and career DR among resident physicians from the surgical and non-surgical clinical specialties and explored the factors associated with these constructs.

Our findings indicate that both FM and DR were highly prevalent in this cohort of resident physicians and were modestly yet significantly associated. Nearly three-quarters (73.1%) of the resident physicians reported high levels of FM, and a comparable proportion (73.4%) experienced varying degrees of career DR, with the majority falling into the ‘mild’ category (44.2%). The prevalence of FM observed in this study is consistent with, and in some contexts exceeds, rates reported in prior research across diverse healthcare systems. Previous studies have documented substantial variability in FM, with prevalence estimates ranging from approximately 40% to over 80%, depending on specialty, clinical setting, and the surrounding medicolegal climate [10, 19, 23]. This finding suggests that the looming threat of litigation extends beyond its influence on clinical decision-making, progressively undermining the core sense of professional satisfaction among the early-career resident physicians in our sample.

Similarly, our findings regarding DR are consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that career DR is a common experience during residency training. For example, Emiral et al. [20] reported that only a small proportion of residents were entirely free of DR, highlighting the pervasiveness of professional ambivalence in this stage of medical careers. This study also revealed that high levels of depression, anxiety, and stress were highly prevalent among physicians and were significantly associated with higher levels of career DR, emphasizing the importance of addressing modifiable psychosocial stressors to mitigate career DR. While both studies were conducted in Turkey, the convergence of these findings suggests that FM and DR may be driven less by country-specific legal frameworks and more by broader structural and psychosocial stressors inherent to residency training.

In contrast to the traditional assumption that surgical disciplines—characterized by high-risk interventions—harbor significantly higher levels of legal anxiety [25, 26], our study revealed no significant difference in FM levels between residents in surgical and non-surgical clinical specialties. This observation is consistent with recent findings reported by Kaya et al. [19] and points toward a broader shift in the perception of malpractice risk among physicians. While surgeons are primarily exposed to malpractice risk through invasive procedures, residents in emergency medicine, pediatrics, and internal medicine increasingly bear the burden of malpractice concerns related to diagnostic errors, delays in treatment, and the management of complex, multimorbid patients. This finding may suggest that malpractice-related concerns are not limited to traditionally high-risk specialties but are experienced across a range of specialties, potentially reflecting broader medicolegal pressures within the healthcare environment. Consequently, the diagnostic complexity inherent in non-surgical clinical specialties is now perceived as legally high-risk as surgical procedures, bridging the traditional gap in malpractice fear between these fields.

FM scores tended to be higher among married residents and those reporting lower income levels. This observation is consistent with the findings of Liang et al. [10] and suggests that increasing financial and familial obligations may be associated with heightened concern regarding the potential personal and professional consequences of malpractice litigation. Age was not significantly associated with FM in our cohort, whereas a longer duration of residency was positively associated with higher FM scores, differing from prior reports suggesting that greater clinical experience may attenuate FM [19, 27]. In this context, increasing clinical responsibility and prolonged exposure to the medicolegal environment may be associated with heightened awareness of potential risks and greater anxiety over the course of residency training. These findings may also be interpreted through the lens of Conservation of Resources theory, which posits that stress may arise when valued personal, social, or economic resources are threatened [14].

An apparently paradoxical finding was the positive association between selecting a specialty because of its perceived lower malpractice risk and higher FM scores. One possible explanation is that residents who are particularly concerned about malpractice may preferentially choose specialties they perceive as legally safer. In this context, the preference for a lower-risk specialty may reflect pre-existing sensitivity to malpractice risk rather than protection against such concerns.

Specialty training in medicine involves a delicate balance between the demands of safe and accurate patient care and the requirements of professional learning within a high-pressure environment. During this period, exposure to patient complaints or medico-legal processes may adversely affect residents’ professional development and career trajectories. Moreover, residents have described complaints as events that prompt them to reinterpret their experiences, professional identities, and roles [28]. An important insight emerging from our multivariate regression analysis is the distinction between direct and indirect exposure to malpractice claims. While direct personal exposure to a malpractice complaint was a significant predictor of career DR, the awareness of a colleague accused of malpractice emerged as a strong predictor of FM. The present findings suggest that residents may experience elevated fear even without direct personal involvement in a malpractice complaint. Awareness of the professional or psychological consequences experienced by a colleague following litigation may be sufficient to increase FM. In contrast, career DR appears to be more closely associated with direct personal exposure to a malpractice complaint. This finding may reflect a form of social contagion, in which malpractice-related concerns are transmitted through shared experiences, observations, and informal discussions within the clinical environment.

The observed association between FM and DR may also be interpreted within broader frameworks of occupational stress and professional identity formation. Residency training represents a formative period during which physicians gradually develop professional confidence, autonomy, and commitment to their chosen specialty while simultaneously facing increasing clinical responsibility and uncertainty. Occupational stress models suggest that persistent work-related stressors may influence both emotional responses and career-related attitudes, whereas professional identity formation theories emphasize the ongoing integration of personal values, professional roles, and career aspirations [16, 17, 27]. From this perspective, FM and DR may represent related manifestations of the broader professional challenges encountered during residency training.

The study identified a significant association between FM and DR. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship and should instead be understood as reflecting the co-occurrence of FM and DR. One possible interpretation of this association is that FM and career DR may coexist as interrelated manifestations of broader occupational stress experienced during residency. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal ordering and directionality of this association. However, the regression analysis also revealed several protective factors. Intrinsic motivations—particularly interest in the chosen specialty and perceived professional satisfaction—were independently associated with lower levels of career DR. These findings suggest that intrinsic motivation may attenuate the adverse psychological impact of the medicolegal environment. Residents who selected their specialty primarily on the basis of personal interest, rather than predominantly extrinsic considerations such as income, appeared to be more resilient to career-related doubts in the context of malpractice-related concerns.

Our findings regarding specialty selection reveal a nuanced pattern. Although personal interest in the specialty appeared to play a greater role than malpractice risk in specialty selection, malpractice-related concerns may nevertheless be associated with subsequent career-related decisions. The observed positive association between FM and the availability of fellowship training may indicate that residents with higher levels of fear are more likely to consider additional training opportunities. This tendency may reflect the perception that advanced training in a more narrowly defined clinical area offers greater professional control or a more manageable risk profile. Taken together, these findings may warrant further investigation into whether medicolegal concerns are associated with preferences regarding future career pathways and postgraduate training choices. Although these findings may be relevant to residency training in comparable settings, differences in malpractice legislation, residency structures, institutional culture, and healthcare systems may limit their transferability to other residency training environments.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings:

First, this study was limited to 308 resident physicians who participated in the survey. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all resident physicians in Turkey.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the associations between medicolegal concerns and career-related attitudes among resident physicians. Importantly, the findings suggest that malpractice-related experiences and perceptions, together with individual motivational factors, may contribute to residents’ professional attitudes during residency rather than operating as isolated influences.

Conclusions

This study suggests that FM is a common stressor among the resident physicians included in this study and is associated with career DR. Intrinsic motivations in specialty choice may serve as protective factors against career DR. However, exposure to malpractice-related experiences may represent an external factor associated with higher levels of career DR.

The global increase in malpractice litigation has placed substantial pressure on healthcare, judicial, and insurance systems. These pressures have important time-related, financial, and emotional consequences for both physicians and patients. Taken together, our results highlight the importance of integrating medicolegal education, psychological support, and institutional risk-management strategies into residency training programs to reduce FM and its downstream effects on career DR. In particular, we recommend that residency directors implement practical tools—such as legal case simulations and regular joint meetings with corporate risk management and hospital law teams—to make legal processes more understandable.
