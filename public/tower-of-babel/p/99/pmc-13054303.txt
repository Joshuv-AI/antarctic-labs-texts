Reversible cognitive and gait impairment by a giant quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cyst: A case report of marked reversal following neuroendoscopic fenestration
Authors: Toshihide Takahashi, Kiyoyuki Yanaka, Akira Tamaoka, Saaya Maruyama, Hitoshi Aiyama, Aiki Marushima, Eiichi Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cysts are rare in adults, but can cause reversible gait and cognitive impairment secondary to obstructive hydrocephalus. Differentiating these structural lesions from idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) or neurodegenerative diseases is essential because they are surgically treatable.Case Description: A 71-year-old woman presented with progressive gait disturbance and mild cognitive impairment. Neuropsychological tests showed a Montreal Cognitive Assessment–Japanese version (MoCA-J) score of 21/30 and a frontal assessment battery (FAB) score of 13/18, indicating executive dysfunction. Standard cognitive screening such as mini-mental state examination did not detect the subtle deficits identified by MoCA. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a giant arachnoid cyst in the quadrigeminal cistern compressing the midbrain and cerebral aqueduct, resulting in obstructive hydrocephalus. Endoscopic cyst fenestration was performed through a right frontal burr hole. The cyst was opened and communication with the third ventricle was achieved. Gait and cognitive functions markedly improved postoperatively (MoCA-J 26/30, FAB 17/18) and radiographic findings showed that the hydrocephalus had resolved. Ocular symptoms, including difficulty gazing upward, also resolved after surgery.Conclusion: Quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cysts can mimic iNPH or neurodegenerative disorders, but represent a reversible cause of cognitive and gait impairments. Detailed neuroimaging and sensitive cognitive assessments are essential for diagnosis. Neuroendoscopic fenestration provides a treatment, i.e., minimally invasive and effective, even in elderly patients.

INTRODUCTION

Disturbances of gait and cognition are common in elderly patients, but are diagnostically challenging. These symptoms are often attributed to neurodegenerative disorders such as idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH), Parkinson’s disease, or Alzheimer’s disease.[10] However, there is also a need to consider reversible causes related to structural lesions. Our group previously demonstrated that chronic subdural hematoma represents a treatable cause of dementia in elderly patients.[5] This highlights the importance of distinguishing reversible structural abnormalities from degenerative processes such as iNPH and supports the need for comprehensive diagnostic evaluation to identify surgically curable conditions.

Arachnoid cysts are benign, congenital malformations of the arachnoid membrane, which are usually asymptomatic and incidentally detected.[2] When located in critical areas such as the quadrigeminal cistern, they can enlarge and compress adjacent neural structures, leading to obstructive hydrocephalus or focal neurological symptoms.[9,13] Among these, cysts in the quadrigeminal cistern are particularly important because they can potentially result in both hydrocephalus and cognitive impairment by compressing the midbrain and periaqueductal structures.

While shunting procedures have long been the standard treatment for hydrocephalus, advances in neuroendoscopic techniques now allow direct and minimally invasive treatment of both the cyst and the associated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) obstruction.[1] Clinically, a quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cyst in an adult can closely resemble iNPH or other neurodegenerative disorders, particularly in the context of predominant cognitive and gait disturbances.[12]

Here, we report an elderly woman presenting with progressive gait disturbance and mild cognitive impairment, who had a giant quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cyst. Her symptoms, which closely resembled iNPH, were markedly reversed after neuroendoscopic cyst fenestration. This case highlights the importance of detailed neuroimaging and sensitive cognitive assessment in distinguishing structural, treatable lesions from degenerative conditions and demonstrates the efficacy of endoscopic fenestration for resolving symptoms, even in the elderly.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 71-year-old woman with a history of thymic cyst excision at the age of 50 presented with progressive gait disturbance. She reported difficulty in tandem gait and an inability to stop walking suddenly, without limb weakness, urinary incontinence, or constipation. She also complained of difficulty maintaining an upward gaze and often had to raise her chin to look up. Neurological examination revealed poor tandem gait. Cognitive screening tests showed a Montreal Cognitive Assessment Japanese version (MoCA-J) score of 21/30, a frontal assessment battery (FAB) score of 13/18, a Hasegawa Dementia Scale revised (HDS-R) score of 26/30, and a mini-mental state examination (MMSE) score of 27/30, indicating mild cognitive impairment mainly in the executive domain.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a giant arachnoid cyst occupying the quadrigeminal cistern, compressing the midbrain and cerebral aqueduct, and resulting in obstructive hydrocephalus [Figure 1a-d]. The cyst contents showed signal-intensity equivalent to that of CSF on both T1- and T2-weighted images, while there was no contrast enhancement along the cyst wall. Routine laboratory data were normal except for mild hyperlipidemia, and a dopamine transporter (DAT) scan demonstrated symmetrical tracer uptake, ruling out Parkinson’s disease.

(a) Axial T1-weighted image without contrast. (b-d) T2-weighted images demonstrating signal intensity equivalent to that of cerebrospinal fluid within the cyst: (b) axial, (c) sagittal, and (d) coronal views.

Endoscopic fenestration of the arachnoid cyst was performed. A burr hole was made 3 cm to the right of the midline at the level of the coronal suture. After performing a puncture with a Dandy needle from the cortical surface to the lateral ventricle, a transparent sheath was inserted. A flexible neuroendoscope (VISERA ventricular videoscope, OLYMPUS VEF TYPE V; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was introduced into the lateral ventricle and advanced through the foramen of Monro into the third ventricle. Intraoperative neuroendoscopy revealed marked thinning of the posterior wall of the third ventricle, allowing the underlying cyst wall to be visualized [Figure 2a and b]. The thinned posterior third ventricular wall, together with the visible cyst wall behind it, was incised using a monopolar electrocautery device [Figure 2c]. Insertion of the endoscope into the cyst cavity allowed direct visualization of the superior surface of the cerebellum [Figure 2d].

The cerebral aqueduct was identified caudal to this structure (black arrow). (b) External appearance of the thinned posterior wall of the third ventricle, through which the cyst wall is visible. (c) Fenestration of the posterior wall of the third ventricle and the cyst wall (double arrow) using a monopolar electrocautery device. (d) The superior surface of the cerebellum was visualized within the cyst cavity.

Her tandem gait steadily improved with postoperative rehabilitation, and she was discharged home on postoperative day 9. The limited ability to perform an upward gaze and posture involving elevation of the chin to compensate for this disappeared markedly after surgery. At an outpatient follow-up 39 days after surgery, her cognitive function had markedly improved, with MoCA-J 26/30, FAB 17/18, HDS-R 30/30, and MMSE 30/30. Marked resolution of the gait disturbance had also occurred. Follow-up computed tomography imaging demonstrated progressive reduction in cyst size and ventricular dilatation, confirming the improvement of hydrocephalus [Figure 3a-d].

DISCUSSION

Disturbances of gait and cognitive function in older adults are frequently attributed to neurodegenerative diseases such as iNPH, Parkinson’s disease, or progressive supranuclear palsy.[7,10] However, as demonstrated in the case reported here, structural lesions such as arachnoid cysts can mimic these disorders and cause reversible neurological impairment. Our patient exhibited gait freezing, difficulty in tandem walking, and mild executive dysfunction resembling iNPH, but neuroimaging revealed a giant quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cyst causing aqueductal compression and obstructive hydrocephalus. Detailed evaluation by MRI and DAT was essential to rule out neurodegenerative etiologies and identify a lesion that could be treated surgically.[8] The marked recovery of both gait and cognitive function after surgery underscores the importance of meticulous differential diagnosis in elderly patients with such symptoms.

Standard cognitive screening tools such as MMSE and HDS-R are useful for detecting global cognitive decline, but they often fail to capture mild cognitive impairment, particularly in the executive domain.[4,6] MoCA is more sensitive to subtle deficits in executive function, attention, and visuospatial ability, which are frequently affected in frontal lobe dysfunction.[3] In the case reported here, although the patient’s MMSE and HDS-R scores were nearly normal, her MoCA-J and FAB scores revealed executive dysfunction, which was markedly reversed by surgical decompression. The observed pattern of cognitive deficits, particularly the low scores on FAB and MoCA, pointed toward a predominantly frontal-executive dysfunction, consistent with disruption of the fronto-thalamic circuitry by chronic aqueductal compression. This emphasizes the utility of MoCA and FAB in detecting frontal-subcortical dysfunction due to reversible structural compression. This case also demonstrates that mild cognitive impairment can remain undetected by conventional screening tools such as MMSE, underscoring the clinical importance of incorporating more sensitive measures, such as MoCA, in elderly patients with suspected reversible cognitive decline.

The delayed onset of symptoms in this elderly patient raises an important pathophysiological question. The cyst was most likely congenital and had remained asymptomatic for decades. In the elderly, gradual age-related changes, such as reduced arachnoid membrane elasticity, minor adhesions, or partial obstruction of the cyst–cisternal communication, can increase intracystic pressure. In addition, age-associated changes in CSF absorption or brain atrophy may modify local CSF dynamics, leading to secondary aqueductal compression and progressive hydrocephalus. Therefore, this case probably represents a late symptomatic phase of a longstanding quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cyst, rather than a newly formed lesion.

Given the anatomical location of the cyst, compressing the midbrain and periaqueductal region, it likely disrupted the frontal-subcortical circuits responsible for regulating gait and executive processing.[11] Neuroendoscopic cyst fenestration effectively relieved this mechanical compression, resulting in rapid and sustained cognitive and motor recovery. Unlike shunting procedures, which only address CSF diversion, endoscopic fenestration directly targets the underlying pathology – both the cyst and the associated CSF obstruction – while avoiding shunt-related complications. The patient’s rapid postoperative improvement, confirmed by imaging demonstrating cyst reduction and ventricular normalization, supports the effectiveness of this approach.

Several endoscopic approaches have been reported for the treatment of quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cysts, including endoscopic cyst fenestration using either rigid or flexible neuroendoscopes, with or without endoscopic third ventriculostomy. The choice of endoscope is an important technical consideration and should be tailored to the anatomical characteristics of the lesion.

Rigid endoscopes offer superior image quality and larger working channels, allowing the use of a wider range of surgical instruments, including bipolar coagulation devices, and providing excellent stability for precise manipulation. However, their straight trajectory limits access to posteriorly located lesions, such as quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cysts. In these situations, achieving an adequate surgical corridor may require increased brain retraction, potentially imposing additional mechanical stress on the surrounding brain parenchyma.

In contrast, flexible neuroendoscopes can navigate curved anatomical pathways, enabling access to deep and posterior lesions through a single burr hole while minimizing brain retraction. This advantage allows both endoscopic third ventriculostomy and cyst fenestration to be performed through the same approach when indicated, thereby reducing overall invasiveness. For lesions located deep within the ventricular system and extending posteriorly, such as in the present case, flexible endoscopes provide a particularly favorable surgical option. Their limitations include relatively lower image resolution and a restricted selection of compatible instruments compared with rigid endoscopes.

In the present case, a flexible neuroendoscope was selected to safely access the markedly thinned posterior wall of the third ventricle and the underlying quadrigeminal cistern cyst while minimizing brain manipulation. This strategy enabled effective cyst fenestration and resulted in marked clinical and radiological improvement without surgical complications.

In addition to improvements in gait and cognitive function, our patient exhibited marked resolution of the preoperative findings of difficulty maintaining an upward gaze and adopting a posture with the chin elevated to compensate for this. These ocular findings likely reflected dorsal midbrain compression and improved after cyst decompression. The coexistence of gait, cognitive, and ocular recovery supports the notion that both frontal-subcortical and midbrain circuits were functionally impaired but structurally preserved, allowing full recovery upon relieving the mechanical compression.

In conclusion, this case illustrates that gait and cognitive disturbances in the elderly can have reversible, structural causes. Detailed neuroimaging and sensitive cognitive evaluation are crucial to distinguish these conditions from neurodegenerative diseases. Minimally invasive neuroendoscopic fenestration provides an excellent therapeutic option that can achieve full neurological recovery, even in older patients. Clinicians should include structural lesions such as quadrigeminal cistern arachnoid cysts in the differential diagnosis when evaluating elderly patients with symptoms of gait and cognitive disturbances suggestive of iNPH.

CONCLUSION

This case demonstrates that disturbances of gait and cognitive function in elderly patients, although frequently attributed to neurodegenerative diseases or iNPH, can occasionally result from reversible structural lesions such as arachnoid cysts. Detailed neuroimaging combined with sensitive cognitive evaluation, including the Montreal Cognitive Assessment and FAB, is essential for detecting such treatable conditions. Furthermore, associated ocular symptoms such as limited ability to direct the gaze upward due to dorsal midbrain compression can also be markedly reversed after cyst fenestration. Neuroendoscopic cyst fenestration offers a minimally invasive and effective treatment that can achieve full neurological recovery, even in elderly patients with symptoms mimicking degenerative disorders.
