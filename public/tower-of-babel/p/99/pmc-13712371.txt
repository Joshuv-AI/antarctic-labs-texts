Discovery of a small molecule RIOK1 inhibitor CINS-1 against colorectal cancer via disrupting ribosomal biosynthesis
Authors: Ying Jiang, Chuan Huang, Fang Wu, Guolong Liu, Shanhe Wan, Yongxin Chen, Ruohong Yan, Zichao Yang, Yuanxin Tian, Tingting Zhang, Xiaoyun Wu, Le Yu, Shaoyu Wu, Shuwen Liu, Jianjun Chen, Wei Xu, Jiajie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the most prevalent malignancies globally. Despite significant advancements in targeted therapies, drug resistance and therapeutic target mutations continue to pose challenges. Consequently, identifying novel therapeutic targets and developing related compounds are essential for discovering new CRC treatment strategies. The RIO Kinase 1 (RIOK1) protein has emerged as a potential anti-tumor target, playing a critical role in eukaryotic ribosomal biosynthesis and modulating cellular proliferation. However, few compounds have been designed to target RIOK1, and the antitumor activity of RIOK1 inhibitors has yet to be thoroughly investigated. In this study, we report the optimization of pyridine caffeic acid benzyl amide derivatives as RIOK1 inhibitors and identified CINS-1 as a potent compound with markedly enhanced binding affinity for AfRIOK1 and RIOK1. Notably, we elucidated the crystal structure of AfRIOK1 in complex with CINS-1. Our findings further confirm the inhibitory effects of CINS-1 on CRC cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, CINS-1 was shown to disrupt ribosomal biosynthesis by hindering 18S maturation and triggering a P53-mediated nucleolar stress response. Overall, these results provide a theoretical and empirical basis for considering the RIOK1 inhibitor CINS-1 as a promising candidate for anti-CRC therapy.Key words: RIOK1, Colorectal cancer, CINS-1, Pyridine caffeic acid benzyl amide, Crystal structure, Ribosomal biosynthesis, 18S maturation, P53

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most prevalent malignancies globally, posing significant health risks1. The pathogenesis of CRC involves intricate genetic mutations in CRC cells, including alterations in RAS, P53, and PTEN genes2,3. These mutations render current therapeutic targets susceptible to resistance, necessitating the identification and development of inhibitors targeting novel pathways for innovative treatment approaches. Previous studies have reported that classical chemotherapeutic agents like oxaliplatin and 5-FU, exert their effects through diverse mechanisms involving modulation of rRNA biogenesis and ribosome biogenesis stress4,5.

Ribosome biogenesis is hyperactive in tumor cells, and disruptions in this process are associated with the pathogenesis of most spontaneous cancers6,7. Moreover, disruptions in ribosome biogenesis, including impaired Pol I transcription, rRNA processing, and ribosome assembly and transport, trigger nucleolar stress that activates the P53 pathway, which leads to cell cycle arrest, senescence, autophagy, and apoptosis8,9. Inhibiting oncogene-induced upregulation of ribosome biogenesis offers a targeted therapeutic approach for selectively targeting cancer cells while sparing normal cells10.

Studies have focused on atypical protein kinases such as RIO Kinase (RIOK1, RIOK2, and RIOK3), which possess ATP-dependent catalytic activity11. RIOK1 is essential for ribosome synthesis, particularly in the final stage of cytoplasmic maturation of the precursor 40S ribosomal small subunit12. This process involves converting pre-rRNA into mature 18S, 5.8S, and 28S rRNAs through a series of snoRNAs13,14. RIOK1 facilitates ATP binding and hydrolysis to aid in the cleavage of 18S rRNA by endonuclease NOB1 and its associated protein PNO115,16. Accumulating evidence indicates that RIOK1 plays a critical role in tumor regulation by suppressing cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion upon silencing17, 18, 19. Elevated expression of RIOK1 has been observed in CRC, with its suppression inhibiting CRC cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo20,21, suggesting potential therapeutic targets for hindering CRC growth. In summary, manipulating RIOK1 to target ribosome biogenesis shows promise as a therapeutic strategy for CRC.

Despite the growing interest in RIOK1 inhibitors, their development remains limited, with only a few compounds specifically designed for RIOK122. Known compounds such as Toyocamycin, TIBI, and Levosimendan have shown capacity to inhibit or interact with RIOK123, 24, 25(Supporting Information Fig. S1). However, there is limited literature on the anti-tumor efficacy and mechanisms of action of these compounds in CRC. Our research group analyzed the structures of RIOK1 and AfRIOK1 proteins, revealing high similarity in their ATP binding pockets. Leveraging the crystal structure of AfRIOK1 protein and the inhibitor WP1086 reported in the literature26, we synthesized a series of compounds by modifying the polarity of the pyridine ring and benzene ring of WP1086.

Here, we report the rational optimization of pyridine caffeic acid benzyl amide derivatives as selective RIOK1 inhibitors, leading to the development of CINS-1, a novel small molecule inhibitor capable of binding to RIOK1 with higher affinity. This study utilized CRISPR-Cas9 technology to validate the role of RIOK1 in colorectal cancer cell proliferation. Subsequently, the anti-CRC efficacy of the RIOK1 inhibitor CINS-1 was evaluated both in vitro and in vivo, revealing a mechanism involving ribosome synthesis.

Results

Knockout RIOK1 inhibits CRC cell growth and proliferation

To investigate the role of RIOK1 in CRC, mRNA expression levels of RIOK1 were analyzed levels in various cancer tissues using the DepMap Portal database. The analysis revealed elevated RIOK1 mRNA levels in 29 primary tumors, including CRC, compared to healthy tissue (Fig. 1A). Additionally, high RIOK1 expression was observed in 73 CRC cell lines, suggesting a correlation between RIOK1 expression and tumor cell proliferation.

Analysis of RIOK1 expression in colorectal cancer by DepMap Portal database and UALCAN database. (A) Expression level of RIOK1 in solid tumor cell lines (DepMap Portal Database). (B) Differential expression of RIOK1 in colorectal cancer and normal colorectal tissues (UALCAN Database).

Furthermore, the mRNA expression levels of RIOK1 in clinical tumor patients were analyzed using the UALCAN database. The results showed that the mRNA expression levels of RIOK1 were significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues, with a similar trend observed across different stages of tumor progression (Fig. 1B). Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that RIOK1 is overexpressed in CRC patient tissues and that high RIOK1 expression correlates with poor prognosis21. These protein-level findings provide direct clinical support for the pathological relevance of RIOK1 in CRC and are consistent with our observations from public mRNA datasets.

To evaluate the prognostic value, we calculated a risk score based on RIOK1 expression and stratified patients into low-risk (n = 233) and high-risk (n = 34) groups. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the high-risk group had significantly shorter overall survival (OS) compared with the low-risk group (P = 0.00015, Supporting Information Fig. S2). Multivariate Cox regression, adjusting for age, tumor stage, MSI status, RAS/BRAF mutation, and treatment, identified high RIOK1 expression as an independent unfavorable prognostic factor for OS (HR > 1, P < 0.05; Supporting Information Table S1).

To assess the impact of RIOK1 on CRC cell growth and proliferation, the RIOK1 gene was knocked out using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Western blot analysis confirmed the successful knockout effects of both gRNAs on RIOK1 protein levels (Fig. 2A). The knockout of RIOK1 resulted in a significant inhibition of CRC cell growth (Fig. 2B). The colony formation experiment was also conducted, which revealed a substantial reduction in the number of CRC cell clones compared to the control group (Fig. 2C and D). To further verify that RIOK1 affects cell proliferation, EdU incorporation assays were performed. The gRNA 2 (g2) was selected for RIOK1 knockout in CRC cells. After treatment with g2, the percentage of EdU-positive cells (green fluorescence) obviously decreased in HCT116, HCT15, HCT-8, and SW620 cell lines (Fig. 2E and F).

Design optimization of compound 1a–5g based on WP1086

The X-ray crystal structure of compound WP1086 in complex with AfRIOK1 revealed that WP1086 is embedded within the ATP-binding pocket of AfRIOK1, with its pyridine ring positioned beneath the p-loop and its benzene ring extending into the adenine pocket. To gain further insight into the binding model of WP1086 and RIOK1, molecular docking simulations were performed by positioning compound WP1086 within the crystal structure of RIOK1. The binding modes observed in both proteins were highly similar: the pyridine ring was found to interact with the solvent region, while the benzene ring extended into the hinge region. This suggests that structural optimization of the benzene ring may produce new interactions and potentially yield target small molecules with improved activity.

Using compound WP1086 as a lead structure, the polarity of WP1086 was modulated by introducing –Cl or –Br groups into the pyridine ring, while hydrophobicity was reduced through the addition of hydroxyl groups. Additionally, different aromatic rings were used to replace the pyridine ring to explore their effects on activity. For the benzene ring in the hinge region, substituents of varying sizes and polarities were incorporated with the aim of generating additional interactions or enhancing stabilization within the adenine-binding pocket (Fig. 3). Based on these modifications, a series of derivatives (compounds 1a–5g) were synthesized and subjected to evaluation for inhibitory effects against CRC cells.

Chemistry

According to the study by Nicole et al.23, a linker of 5-atom length between aromatic rings is crucial for maintaining inhibitor activity. Therefore, this 5-atom linker was preserved, while structural modifications were introduced into the aromatic ring moieties on both sides. Compounds 1a–5g were synthesized following the synthetic route illustrated in Scheme 1.

Synthesis of derivatives of WP1086. Reagents and conditions: (a) NaBH3, NH4OH, MeOH, rt, 12–15 h, 60%–70% yield; (b) methyl cyanoacetate or ethyl cyanoacetate, DIPEA, MeOH, rt, 6–8 h, 80%–95% yield; (c) pyridine or piperidine, acetonitrile, 85 °C, overnight, 30%–50% yield.

Benzaldehyde compounds substituted with various functional groups were treated with sodium borohydride in NH4OH/MeOH to afford intermediates 7a–7e in yields ranging from 35% to 60%. Subsequently, intermediates 8a–8e were obtained from 7a–7e through amide condensation reactions. Finally, the active methylene group on intermediates 8a–8e underwent a Knoevenagel reaction with an aldehyde group to yield compounds 1a–5g.

Antiproliferative activity of 1a–5g on CRC cells

The antiproliferative activity of compounds 1a–5g against CRC cell lines HCT116 and SW620 was evaluated using the MTT assay. Halogen atoms, hydroxyl groups, phenyl groups, etc., were introduced to the benzene ring at position R1, while the pyridine ring of R2 was replaced with various aromatic heterocycles based on the concept of bioisosterism.

As shown in Table 1, replacing the R2 position with different aromatic heterocycles did not significantly enhance the inhibitory effect on cell proliferation. Additionally, when the benzene ring at the R1 position contained small substituents, there was no significant improvement in tumor inhibition. Surprisingly, when R1 was a biphenyl structure, the antitumor activity was markedly enhanced. Notably, compound 5e (CINS-1) exhibited significantly better inhibitory activity (IC50 = 7.81 μmol/L) than WP1086 (IC50 = 27.55 μmol/L) in HCT116 cells.

Crystal structure of AfRIOK1 in a complex with CINS-1

In order to explore the binding mode of CINS-1 to RIOK1 protein, we resolved the crystal structure of the complex of CINS-1 and Archaeoglobusfulgidus RIOK1 (AfRIOK1) protein. In the overall structure of the complex at a resolution of 2.76 Å (Supporting Information Table S2), it could be observed that the inhibitor CINS-1 was located near the ATP activity pocket (Fig. 7A), the overall surface electrostatic potential map showed that CINS-1 was completely encapsulated by the cavity formed by AfRIOK1 (Fig. 7B) and the electron density maps showed that the electron cloud density signal of the inhibitor CINS-1 was relatively intact and that most of the structure of CINS-1 could be captured (Fig. 7D). Further analysis of the forces between CINS-1 and RIOK1 protein showed that CINS-1 was mainly bound in RIOK1 protein through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic forces, in which the glutamic acid at position 148 in AfRIOK1 protein formed an intermolecular hydrogen bond with the oxygen atom at position one in CINS-1, and the hydrophobic interaction involved 15 nearby residues in the binding interface between AfRIOK1 and CINS-1 (Fig. 7C). Although we resolved the structure of the AfRIOK1 protein, we found similarities by comparing the structure of AfRIOK1 with that of RIOK1 (PDB ID: 4OTP), as well as key active site residues. In particular, the configuration and ATP activity pocket volume of RIOK1 in the kinase region maintains high similarity, and the corresponding amino acids were also highly conserved. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) value for both was 0.865 (Fig. 7E). In addition, we aligned the complexes of RIOK1 (HsRIOK1) and CINS-1 obtained by molecular docking with the structures of our resolved complexes, and the results showed that the active pockets occupied by CINS-1 largely overlapped, and only the molecular orientation of CINS-1 was different (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, we performed a residue-by-residue comparison of the binding pocket (Table 3). The key residues responsible for anchoring CINS-1 (e.g., Lys59, Glu60, Glu148, Phe149, Ile150) were identical between species. The hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding landscape was largely preserved.

CINS-1 targets RIOK1 protein and occupies the binding pocket of ATP

Further analysis showed that CINS-1 had a similar interaction with RIOK1 (HsRIOK1) and AfRIOK1. CINS-1 formed hydrogen bond with Ser187 of RIOK1 (corresponding to Ser56 of AfRIOK1) and engaged in hydrophobic interactions with multiple hydrophobic amino acids in the ATP pockets of both proteins. The difference was that in the RIOK1 pocket, CINS-1 formed a hydrogen bond with Asp341 and Ser343, respectively (Fig. 8A and B). In the AfRIOK1 pocket, CINS-1 formed a hydrogen bond with Lys59, as well as a π-cation interaction and a salt bridge with Lys80. In summary, compound CINS-1 binds stably to RIOK1 and AfRIOK1, and the binding modes of the two proteins to CINS-1 are highly similar.

To validate the interaction between CINS-1 and RIOK1, binding affinity was examined using ITC. The expression and purification of AfRIOK1 and RIOK1 were described in Supporting Information Fig. S3. The results showed that WP1086 bound to AfRIOK1 with weak binding affinity (Kd = 50.6 μmol/L) (Fig. 8C), whereas CINS-1 exhibited much stronger binding affinity (Kd = 0.097 μmol/L) under the same conditions (Fig. 8D). Notably, CINS-1 bound to human RIOK1 with a Kd of 0.176 μmol/L, as shown in Fig. 8E.

To further explore the targeting effect of CINS-1 on RIOK1 protein, a thermal shift assay was performed. The results showed that the degradation of RIOK1 protein began at 52 °C compared to the control group (Fig. 8F). At 54 °C, the thermal stability of RIOK1 protein was significantly reduced compared to the control group, with a statistically significant difference.

Furthermore, comparative analysis of the ATP-binding pockets across various kinases revealed that both RIOK1 (PDB ID: 4OTP) and AfRIOK1 (PDB ID: 4JIN) exhibited a smaller ATP-binding pocket volume relative to other kinases (Supporting Information Table S3). To explore kinase selectivity profile of CINS-1, other protein kinases were assessed (Supporting Information Table S4). The results showed that CINS-1 exhibited poor inhibitory activity against VEGFR, EGFR, HER2, c-ABL, and AXL. These findings indicate that CINS-1 binds to RIOK1 and alters its thermal stability, further verifying RIOK1 as a direct target.

Deletion RIOK1 or CINS-1 affects processing of 18S-E to 18S rRNA

The ATPase activity of RIOK1 is crucial for the processing of 18S-E pre-rRNA into mature 18S rRNA in the cytoplasm. As shown in Fig. 9A, the conversion of 20S rRNA to 18S-E rRNA retains the 5′ region of ITS1 within the nucleus. Following nuclear export of pre-40S particles, 18S-E rRNA is further processed into mature 18S rRNA in the cytoplasm.

To demonstrate that RIOK1 plays a critical role in this maturation process, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis was conducted using a Cy3-labeled oligonucleotide complementary to the 5′ region of ITS1 as a probe. The FISH signal (red fluorescence) was observed primarily in the nucleolus, indicating early nucleolar precursors of 18S rRNA. A stronger FISH signal was detected in the cytoplasm compared to control cells (Lenti), suggesting that RIOK1 was essential for the final stages of 18S pre-rRNA processing (Fig. 9B and C).

Previous studies have shown that CINS-1 can occupy the ATP binding pocket of RIOK1, thereby inhibiting its ATPase activity. To further investigate the impact of CINS-1 on 18S rRNA maturation, CRC cells were treated with CINS-1, which produced a pattern consistent with that observed following RIOK1 knockout (Fig. 9D). In control cells, only weak signals were detected in the cytoplasm, whereas treatment with CINS-1 resulted in an increased signal, indicating impaired processing. Taken together, these results indicate that RIOK1 is essential for the final stages of 18S pre-rRNA processing and that CINS-1 hinders the formation of the ribosomal 18S subunit.

CINS-1 activates P53-mediated nucleolar stress response

To further investigate the signaling pathways affected by CINS-1, RNA-seq analysis was performed on HCT116 cells treated with CINS-1 and compared with untreated controls. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses indicated that CINS-1 predominantly affected the cell cycle signaling pathway. However, assessment of Cyclin A, Cyclin B1, and Cyclin D1 expression in HCT116 and HCT15 cells revealed no consistent trends across different concentrations and time points (Supporting Information Fig. S4).

Next, the expression of P53 protein following CINS-1 treatment was examined. As shown in Fig. 10A, P53 protein levels increased in a concentration-dependent manner, along with an upregulation of its downstream target P21 in HCT116 cells. Similar results were observed in HCT15 cells, where CINS-1 treatment at various concentrations and time points led to elevated levels of P53 protein (Fig. 10B). To further validate the activation of P53 by RIOK1 inhibition, RIOK1 knockout was performed in both HCT116 and HCT15 cells. The results showed that the expression of P53 was significantly increased in both cell lines (Fig. 10C and D).

It has been reported that P53-mediated nucleolar stress response is associated with ribosome biogenesis, particularly through the binding of ribosomal proteins to MDM2, leading to P53 stabilization27. To explore this mechanism further, the expression levels of ribosomal protein RPS26 and MDM2 were examined. Our results showed that knockout of RIOK1 increased mRNA levels of RPS26 and promoted MDM2 expression (Fig. 10E and F). Consistently, CINS-1 treatment had similar effects on both mRNA and protein levels of RPS26 (Fig. 10G).

CINS-1 inhibits the growth of CRC cells in vivo

Given the significant anti-tumor activity observed for CINS-1 in CRC cells in vitro, its inhibitory effect on CRC cells was further evaluated in vivo. A subcutaneous xenograft model was established using human HCT116 cells, in which CINS-1 treatment (80 mg/kg) suppressed tumor (Fig. 11A) and progressively reduced tumor size (Fig. 11B and C) with a tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rate of 67%. During the 20-day treatment, CINS-1 had no impact on mouse bodyweight (Fig. 11D), and hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining revealed no obvious damage to the main organs of mice following CINS-1 treatment (Fig. 11E). Histological analysis revealed tumor cell atrophy and enlarged intercellular spaces following CINS-1 administration (Fig. 11F). Immunohistochemical (IHC) assays further demonstrated that CINS-1 decreased the protein level of Ki-67, a marker for cellular proliferation, while simultaneously increasing the protein level of P53 (Fig. 11G). Specifically, we established an SW620 subcutaneous xenograft model and evaluated the antitumor activity of CINS-1 in parallel with capecitabine, an oral fluoropyrimidine that is widely used as a standard-of-care treatment for colorectal cancer. As shown in Supporting Information Fig. S5, treatment with CINS-1 at 80 mg/kg significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to the vehicle control group. Notably, CINS-1 demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy relative to capecitabine at the tested doses, with a more pronounced reduction in both tumor volume and tumor weight at the end of the treatment period. Collectively, our results suggest that CINS-1 inhibits CRC growth in vivo.

Pharmacokinetic studies of CINS-1

To better comprehend the druggability of CINS-1, male SD rats were employed to assess the PK properties. The dose of intravenous injection (i.v.) was 5 mg/kg, and the dose of oral administration (p.o.) was 25 mg/kg. The pharmacokinetic parameters are summarized in Fig. 12 and Table 4. CINS-1 exhibited an elimination half-life (t1/2) of 2.205 h via p.o. administration. As shown in Table 4, CINS-1 attained the AUC0‒t of 996.868 h⸱mg/L with Cmax of 3374.401 mg/L via i.v. administration. Also, CINS-1 was distributed into tissue, with a V value of 0.145 L/kg, and showed a CL of 0.005 L/h/kg following i.v. administration in rats. Besides, the absolute bioavailability of CINS-1 was 16.6% and the mean residence time (MRT0‒t) was 5.641 h (Table 4). All these data demonstrated that the structure of compound CINS-1 required further optimization to improve its pharmacokinetic properties, thereby enhancing its efficacy.

Discussion

In eukaryotic cells, RIOK1 plays an essential role in processing 18S rRNA28. It assists endonuclease NOB1 and its binding factor PNO1 to complete the cleavage of 20S rRNA by hydrolyzing ATP16,29. ATP-bound RIOK1 cooperates with ribosomal protein RPS26 to displace DIM2 from the 3′ end of 18S rRNA, thereby enabling NOB1 to complete the cleavage of 20S rRNA30. The subsequent release of ADP leads to the dissociation of RIOK1 from the 40S subunit, eventually forming 18S rRNA. This process highlights the critical role of RIOK1 in ribosomal biogenesis. Building upon the essential role of RIOK1 in ribosome biogenesis, recent studies have established a strong link between RIOK1 dysregulation and tumor progression31. Downregulation of RIOK1 has been shown to suppress the growth of hepatocellular carcinoma and ovarian cancer, underscoring its oncogenic potential32,33. Despite these findings, no RIOK1 inhibitor has yet entered clinical development, and to date, no co-crystal structure of human RIOK1 in complex with a small-molecule inhibitor has been reported.

In this study, computational analysis revealed that the overall fold of AfRIOK1 is highly homologous to human RIOK1 (RMSD 0.829 Å), with the ATP-binding pocket being evolutionarily conserved. Based on the previously reported co-crystal structure of AfRIOK1 with WP1086, we performed structure-guided optimization to obtain CINS-1. We subsequently determined the co-crystal structure of AfRIOK1 in complex with CINS-1, which revealed the binding mode at atomic resolution. The interaction between CINS-1 and RIOK1 was further corroborated by ITC, which yielded a Kd of 0.176 μmol/L, and by CETSA, confirming target engagement in living cells. Collectively, these findings provide a structural and mechanistic foundation for the development of RIOK1-targeted therapies.

Despite these advances, we were unable to obtain crystals of sufficient diffraction quality to resolve the human RIOK1–CINS-1 complex, likely due to the intrinsic flexibility or instability of the human protein variant under crystallization conditions. Future efforts will focus on optimizing crystallization strategies, exploring alternative constructs of human RIOK1, and leveraging these structural insights to develop next-generation inhibitors with improved potency and drug-like properties.

The current standard clinical treatment for CRC involves surgical resection combined with chemotherapy34. However, this approach has significant adverse effects. Targeted therapy has emerged as a promising alternative, particularly for CRC cases attributed to rat sarcoma (RAS), where approximately 50% of CRC cases are involved35. Kirsten rat sarcoma (KRAS) is one of the most frequently mutated oncogenes in CRC, affecting about 40% of patients. Initial outcomes of direct KRASG12C inhibitors have shown promise but require further validation35. The continued challenge posed by RAS as a treatment target necessitates the development of specific therapeutic options.

In this study, the inhibition of RIOK1 by CINS-1 or genetic knockout significantly suppressed cell proliferation, arrested the cell cycle at G2/M phase, and promoted apoptosis in KRAS-mutant tumor cells. This suggests that targeting RIOK1 may hinder the growth of RAS-mutant tumors. Consistently, we demonstrated that CINS-1 effectively suppressed the growth of KRAS-mutant CRC xenografts (HCT116 and SW620) in vivo, with no obvious toxicity observed in major organs of treated mice. Future studies will further evaluate the safety profiles of CINS-1 analogs using serum biochemical parameters, thereby providing a foundation for the development of RIOK1 inhibitors. Nevertheless, while a comprehensive FFPE IHC cohort with standardized scoring and predefined cut-offs would be valuable for establishing RIOK1 as an independent prognostic and predictive biomarker, such clinical validation lies beyond the scope of this exploratory study and represents a key direction for future investigation.

Further studies found that CINS-1 affects IκBα expression in the cytoplasm, thereby inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway (Supporting Information Fig. S6). After high concentrations of CINS-1 treatment, phosphorylation levels of JAK2, BRAF, and AKT proteins were reduced, and KRAS protein levels were affected in HCT116 cells (Fig. S6). In conclusion, CINS-1 can affect the NF-κB/JAK–STAT/RAF–RAS–ERK/PI3K–AKT signaling pathway. Combined with related studies, inhibiting RIOK1 protein can regulate downstream KRAS signaling pathways20. Therefore, a novel inhibitor like CINS-1 holds promise for providing new perspectives and treatments for KRAS-mutant CRC patients.

Studies have identified six key driver genes in CRC, including APC, KRAS, and p5336,37. The p53 gene, located on chromosome 17p, is a tumor suppressor that regulates cell cycle, DNA repair, and apoptosis. MDM2 protein binds to P53 to form a stable complex, promoting P53 degradation through ubiquitination38,39. Interestingly, the expression of P53 and MDM2 was up-regulated after RIOK1 deletion and its inhibitor treatment. The nucleolar stress response triggered by ribosomal defects is primarily responsible for stabilizing and activating P538. Since RIOK1 mainly affects 40S subunit processing, further detection of ribosomal small subunit proteins revealed that the expression level of RPS26 was affected by RIOK1 and its inhibitors. It has been reported that RPS26 binds to MDM2 and prevents MDM2-mediated P53 ubiquitination, resulting in P53 stabilization40. In general, the absence of RIOK1 or the effect of its inhibitor CIINS-1 can lead to up-regulation of P53 expression, indicating that RIOK1 protein also regulates P53 protein related to blocked ribosomal biosynthesis.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. First, the current evidence linking RIOK1 inhibition to nucleolar stress and translational suppression remains largely correlative rather than causal. In addition, although our findings demonstrate that CINS-1 promotes P53 expression, the effects of CINS-1 under P53-null or pharmacologically inhibited conditions have not been thoroughly investigated. Finally, comprehensive characterization of nucleolar stress markers, particularly the RPL/RPS–MDM2 axis, remains to be fully addressed. Elucidating the causal pathway from RIOK1 inhibition to 40S ribosomal maturation arrest and subsequent translational suppression represents a valuable direction for future research.

Conclusions

This study reported the discovery of CINS-1 through structural optimization based on WP1086. The crystal structure of AfRIOK1–CINS-1 confirmed a specific binding interaction between RIOK1 and CINS-1. Our findings demonstrate that CINS-1 exhibits potent proliferation inhibition activity against CRC cells both in vitro and in vivo. Specifically, CINS-1 blocks the cell cycle at G2/M phase and promotes apoptosis in CRC cells.

It is proposed that CINS-1 inhibits the formation of 18S rRNA by affecting RIOK1 function, thereby inducing a P53-mediated nucleolar stress response (Fig. 13). Further investigation is required to confirm this hypothesis by testing the ubiquitination levels of RPS26 and P53. Additionally, elucidating the specific regulatory mechanism between ribosome synthesis and KRAS-driven tumors remains an important area for future study.

The anti-CRC potential mechanism of RIOK1 inhibitor CINS-1 in disrupting ribosomal biosynthesis.

In conclusion, RIOK1 protein is implicated in the occurrence and development of CRC tumors, suggesting promising application prospects for RIOK1 as a novel therapeutic target. Importantly, this study provides a theoretical and experimental basis for the potential use of RIOK1 inhibitors as antitumor drugs.

Experimental

Cell line and reagents

All reagents used in this study are commercially available. 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a BRUKER AVIII 400 and 101 MHz spectrometer (Bruker Co., Switzerland) in dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d6). Chemical shifts (δ) are given relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS) or the solvent peak, and coupling constants (J) are given in Hz. Low resolution mass spectra were performed on WATERS ZQ4000 (Waters Co., Milford, USA) electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS). Analytical thin-layer chromatography was conducted using silica gel plate 60 F254, and flash column chromatography was carried out with silica gel 60 (200–300 mesh), both from Qingdao Ocean Chemical Co., Ltd. Compounds CINS-1 and WP1086 (purity ≥95%) were synthesized at Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of New Drug Screening, School of Pharmaceutical Science, Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). Detailed synthetic procedures, NMR spectroscopic data, and HPLC chromatograms for all compounds are provided in the Supporting Information.

Human CRC cell lines HCT116, HCT15, SW620, HT29, HCT-8, Colo201 and DLD-1 were purchased from Cellcook (Guangzhou, China). NCM460 and FHC cells were donated by the research group of the Department of Gastroenterology, Nanfang Hospital. HEK-293T cell line was donated by Prof. Le Yu's group41 from the School of Pharmacy, Southern Medical University. The CRC and NCM460 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, CA, USA) supplemented with 100 μg/mL penicillin, 100 U/mL streptomycin, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Procell, Wuhan, China) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C. FHC and HEK-293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Gibco, CA, USA).

Generation of knockout cells

The CRISPR-Cas9 system was utilized to disrupt the RIOK1 gene in HCT116, HCT15, HCT-8, and SW620 cells. The plasmid lenti-CRISPR v2 and non-targeting control gRNA were obtained from Prof. Le Yu41. Gene-specific sgRNAs were designed using the CRISPR design tool available at http://crispr.mit.edu. Two guide sequences were used in the study (g1: AGGAGTTGGAAAACTCGCCA, g2: CTGGCCCATAGGTACCACGG). Cells were transfected with the lenti-CRISPR v2 plasmid containing the sgRNA constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). After transfection, cells were selected with puromycin for 3 d to eliminate non-transfected cells. The concentrations of puromycin used were as follows: HCT116 (0.5 μg/mL), HCT15 (3 μg/mL), HCT-8 (3 μg/mL), and SW620 (0.5 μg/mL). Surviving cells were collected, and the knockout efficiency was validated by Western blot analysis.

MTT assay

Human CRC cell lines and normal cell lines were seeded into 96-well plates and exposed to various concentrations of compounds (0, 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 μmol/L). After 72 h of treatment with compounds, cells were incubated with 10 μL of MTT solution for 4 h. The absorbance was then recorded at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Colony formation experiment

Human CRC cell lines (800 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates, and treated with different concentrations of CINS-1 (0, 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 μmol/L). After 10–14 days of cultivation, cells were stained with 0.4% trypan blue to visualize formed colony and count the number of colonies using an Enzyme-linked Immunospot Analyzer (CTL, S6 Versa, USA).

EdU staining

Cell proliferation was assessed using the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). CRC cell lines were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with CINS-1 for 48 h. The cells were then incubated with 20 μmol/L EdU for 2 h at 37 °C and processed following the manufacturer's guidelines. Images were acquired using a fluorescence inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Axio Observer A1, Germany).

Flow cytometric assays of cell cycle and apoptosis

Human CRC cell lines were seeded into 6-well plates and exposed to various concentrations of CINS-1 (0, 2.5, 5, 10 and 15 μmol/L) for 48 h. Following treatment, the cells were collected, washed twice with cold PBS, fixed in 70% ethanol overnight at 4 °C, and subsequently stained with propidium iodide (PI) solution to assess cell cycle distribution using flow cytometry. The analysis of results was performed using the Modfit software. Apoptosis analysis was conducted using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Following washing with PBS, the cells were resuspended in 195 μL of Annexin V-FITC binding solution, then 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 μL of PI were added. The cell apoptosis rates were subsequently assessed by flow cytometry after a 20-min incubation at room temperature in the dark. The data were analyzed using Flowjo software, and each assay was performed in triplicate.

Western blot assay

The protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific CA, USA). An equivalent amount of protein samples was subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred to NC membranes (PALL, NY, USA). Antibodies against RIOK1 (17222-1-AP, 1:1000), RPS26 (14909-1-AP, 1:1000) and MDM2 (27883-1-AP, 1:1000) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China); the antibody against P21 (ab109520, 1:1000) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); the antibody against P53 (#2527, 1:1000) from CST (Boston, USA); and the antibody against GAPDH (FD0063, 1:5000) from Fude Biotech Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). The specific manipulations were conducted according to previous studies42. Quantification of the Western blot was conducted using ImageJ software.

Real-time qPCR (RT-qPCR)

Cultured cells were extracted with TRIzol (Life Technologies, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. With oligo (dT) and random hexamers, cDNAs were reverse transcribed, followed by RT-qPCR and RT-PCR analyses. Different gene sets were quantified based on their relative expression in GAPDH mRNA. The sequences for primers are listed in the Supporting Information Table S5.

Molecular docking

All the molecular docking analyses were performed using the Schrodinger software package (Maestro 11.1). The atomic coordinates of human RIOK1 (RIOK1) (PDB ID: 4OTP) and Archeaoglobusfulgidus Rio1 (AfRIOK1) (PDB ID: 4JIN) were retrieved from Protein Data Bank (www.rcsb.org) and chosen as receptors. The structure of CINS-1 was constructed in Maestro and further processed and optimized with the LigPrep panel. The Induced Fit Docking (IFD) module was employed for flexible docking, as described43.

Examination of physical interaction between RIOK1 and CINS-1

RIOK1 protein (residues 143–494) and AfRIOK1 protein were expressed and purified at Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of New Drug Screening, School of Pharmaceutical Science, Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China) (Supporting Information Fig. S2). The interaction between AfRIOK1 or RIOK1 and CINS-1/WP1086 was measured by MicroCal PEAQ-ITC (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., UK). The test compounds were diluted to a final concentration of 10 μmol/L in Tris–HCl buffer, and protein was diluted to 100 μmol/L, followed by measurement according to the manufacturer's instructions. The binding data were fitted using MicroCal PEIQ-ITC Analysis Software.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

The CETSA assay was carried out as reported previously44,45. The HCT116 cell line was incubated with 10 μmol/L CINS-1 or DMSO for 3 h. Subsequently, the harvested cells were resuspended in PBS supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, divided into eight aliquots, and exposed to various temperatures (50, 52, 54, 56, 58, 60, 62, and 64 °C) using a PCR instrument. Then cells were lysed by subjecting them to three cycles of freezing and thawing in liquid nitrogen, followed by centrifugation at 16,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were transferred to new microtubes and analyzed by Western blot.

Crystal structure of AfRIOK1 in a complex with CINS-1

Purified AfRIOK1 was concentrated to approximately 30 mg/mL. Prior to crystallization, AfRIOK1 protein was incubated with CINS-1 at a 1:5 M ratio for 1 h on ice. The initial crystal was obtained using a commercial screening SaltRx kit (Hampton Research, USA) with the vapor diffusion method. The protein and the reservoir solution were mixed in a 1:1 volumetric ratio. The best crystals were produced in a reservoir solution containing 1.5 mol/L lithium sulfate monohydrate, 0.1 mol/L sodium acetate trihydrate pH 4.6, and crystals appeared within 12 days. Selected crystals were immersed in a protective solution containing all components of the storage solution supplemented with 20% glycerol for 5 min and then the crystals were immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Data collection was performed at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF, Shanghai, China), and data processing was carried out with the program XDS. Molecular replacement was performed with Phaser using the known structure of AfRIOK1 (PDB ID: 4JIN) as a search model. By further building the model manually with Coot and refining with Refmac5, the complex structure of the AfRIOK1 and CINS-1 at a resolution of 2.76 Å was finally obtained. The complex structure has been deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with ID 9X8Y.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

According to the manufacturer's instructions, cells cultured in confocal dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized in 0.5% Triton-X-100 for 5 min, and subsequently blocked in pre-hybridization solution for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with hybridization solution containing the 5′-ITS1 probe (5′-CCT∗CGCCCTCCGGGCTCCGTTAATTGATC-3′)46, synthesized by Guangzhou Ribo Bio Co. Ltd. After three washes with 4 × , 2 × , and 1 × SSC at 42 °C, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, DAPI was added for incubation, followed by PBS washing, and the cells were immediately imaged using an immunofluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, LSM880, Oberkochen, Germany).

Xenografts

Male athymic BALB/c nude mice, aged 6 weeks, were obtained from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China). The animal study protocol was approved by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University (Ethical Approval No.: SMUL2021089). HCT116 cells (3 × 106) or SW620 cells (3 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of the mice.

Random assignment was used to divide the tumor-bearing mice with HCT116 into three groups. The mice were orally administered CINS-1 (80 and 40 mg/kg) or a vehicle (2.5% DMSO + 5% Tween 80 + 40% PEG 300 in saline solution) for 20 days. Body weight was recorded and xenograft tumor growth was measured every two days. On Day 20, the tumors were weighed. Tumor tissues and organ (heart, liver, lung, kidney) tissues were excised and subjected to analysis.

For the tumor-bearing mice with SW620, the mice were randomly assigned to 4 groups and were administered CINS-1 (80 and 40 mg/kg) or capecitabine (100 mg/kg) or a vehicle (2.5% DMSO + 5% Tween 80 + 40% PEG 300 in saline solution) via oral gavage daily for 14 days. Body weight and xenograft tumor growth was measured every two days. On Day 14, the tumors were weighed, and tumor tissues were excised.

H&E staining and IHC

Tumor tissues fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and prepared into paraffin sections were used for H&E and IHC analysis. H&E staining was performed according to the standard histopathological protocol. Paraffin sections were incubated with primary antibodies, Ki-67 antibody and P53 antibody, respectively. The stained sections were observed and photographed under a light microscope.

Pharmacokinetics study

Male SD rats were provided by the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). A total of 6 rats were used in the experiment and divided into two groups according to their average body weight, with 3 rats in each group, to receive intravenous (5 mg/kg) or orally administered (25 mg/kg) compound CINS-1. Blood samples (0.5 mL) were collected from the orbital vein into heparinized tubes at 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24 h after administration. Samples were processed as previously described47. 10 μL aliquot of the resulting solution was injected into the LC–MS/MS system for analysis. The pharmacokinetic parameters determined included the elimination half-life (t1/2), time of peak plasma concentration (tmax), maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), area under the concentration-time curve (AUC), mean residence time (MRT) and bioavailability (F). The relevant pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using DAS 2.0 software.

Data analysis

Statistical values represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. GraphPad Prism 7 was used to analyze the data. The prior level of significance was set at 5% or P < 0.05.
