MiR-3653 blocks autophagy to inhibit epithelial–mesenchymal transition in breast cancer cells by targeting the autophagy-regulatory genes ATG12 and AMBRA1
Authors: Huachen Song, Zitong Zhao, Liying Ma, Bailin Zhang, Yongmei Song, Peifang Wei
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Metastasis is the main cause of tumor-associated death and mainly responsible for treatment failure of breast cancer. Autophagy accelerates tumor metastasis. In our work, we aimed to investigate the possibility of microRNAs (miRNAs) which participate in the regulation of autophagy to inhibit tumor metastasis.Methods:MiRNA array and comprehensive analysis were performed to identify miRNAs which participated in the regulation of autophagy to inhibit tumor metastasis. The expression levels of miR-3653 in breast cancer tissues and cells were detected by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction. In vivo and in vitro assays were conducted to determine the function of miR-3653. The target genes of miR-3653 were detected by a dual luciferase reporter activity assay and Western blot. The relationship between miR-3653 and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) was assessed by Western blot. Student's t-test was used to analyze the difference between any two groups, and the difference among multiple groups was analyzed with one-way analysis of variance and a Bonferroni post hoc test.Results:miR-3653 was downregulated in breast cancer cells with high metastatic ability, and high expression of miR-3653 blocked autophagic flux in breast cancer cells. Clinically, low expression of miR-3653 in breast cancer tissues (0.054 ± 0.013 vs. 0.131 ± 0.028, t = 2.475, P = 0.014) was positively correlated with lymph node metastasis (0.015 ± 0.004 vs. 0.078 ± 0.020, t = 2.319, P = 0.023) and poor prognosis (P < 0.001). miR-3653 ameliorated the malignant phenotypes of breast cancer cells, including proliferation, migration (MDA-MB-231: 0.353 ± 0.013 vs. 1.000 ± 0.038, t = 16.290, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.200 ± 0.014 vs. 1.000 ± 0.043, t = 17.530, P < 0.001), invasion (MDA-MB-231: 0.723 ± 0.056 vs. 1.000 ± 0.035, t = 4.223, P = 0.013; MDA-MB-468: 0.222 ± 0.016 vs. 1.000 ± 0.019, t = 31.050, P < 0.001), and colony formation (MDA-MB-231: 0.472 ± 0.022 vs. 1.000 ± 0.022, t = 16.620, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.650 ± 0.040 vs. 1.000 ± 0.098, t = 3.297, P = 0.030). The autophagy-associated genes autophagy-related gene 12 (ATG12) and activating molecule in beclin 1-regulated autophagy protein 1 (AMBRA1) are target genes of miR-3653. Further studies showed that miR-3653 inhibited EMT by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1.Conclusions:Our findings suggested that miR-3653 inhibits the autophagy process by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1, thereby inhibiting EMT, and provided a new idea and target for the metastasis of breast cancer.Keywords: Breast cancer, miR-3653, Autophagy, Autophagy-related gene 12, Activating molecule in beclin 1-regulated autophagy protein 1, Epithelial-mesenchymal transition

Introduction

Breast cancer is the main cause of cancer death in females.[1] Breast cancer exhibits heterogeneous metastatic propensity and tendencies toward metastasis to different organs, including bone, lung, liver, and brain, leading to different treatment responses and prognoses for patients.[2] It has been reported that the likelihood of metastasis in patients with breast cancer after primary tumor diagnosis and therapy is 20% to 30%, and approximately 90% of cancer-related deaths are attributed to metastasis.[3] Metastasis is mainly responsible for treatment failure of breast cancer. Although there are some treatments, more effective therapeutic drugs are being explored.

Autophagy is a highly conserved process that degrades intracellular components through lysosomal mechanisms in the presence of nutritional deficiencies or other adverse environments.[4] There is growing evidence that autophagy disorders in cells are associated with cancer.[5] Autophagy accelerates metastasis by promoting local adhesion, anti-anoikis and tumor metabolism.[6] Autophagy-related gene 12 (ATG12) and activating molecule in beclin 1 (BECN1)-regulated autophagy protein 1 (AMBRA1) promote tumor occurrence and development by promoting autophagy.[4] Therefore, autophagy-related intervention is a promising new strategy for tumor treatment.

microRNAs (miRNAs) are a group of highly conserved and endogenous non-coding RNA molecules that are ∼22 nt in length.[7] By binding to the messenger RNA (mRNA) 3′ untranslated region (UTR), miRNAs suppress mRNA translation or promote the degradation of mRNA and play a role as negative gene expression regulators.[8,9] Many studies have reported that miRNAs play a significant role in regulating tumor initiation and development,[10] and miRNAs are also involved in the regulation of the autophagy process.[11] miRNAs secreted by cells exist in a variety of body fluids and are easily quantified. Therefore, miRNAs can be used as ideal candidate biomarkers for almost all human diseases, including tumors.[12]

Several studies indicated that miR-3653 inhibited growth and metastasis in colon cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma,[13,14] and could serve as a valuable diagnostic indicator in glioma and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors.[15,16] However, the relationship between miR-3653 and autophagy in breast cancer remained to be elucidated. In our work, we aimed to investigate whether miRNAs participate in the regulation of autophagy to inhibit tumor metastasis. An miRNA array was used to screen differentially expressed miRNAs between breast cancer cells with high metastasis ability and low metastasis ability. Comprehensive analysis, functional assays and molecular mechanism studies were used to investigate the role of miRNAs in the autophagy and metastasis.

Methods

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) analysis

The RNA sequencing data of miR-3653 expression in various tumors and the miRNA expression data of normal tissue samples paired with tumor samples were downloaded from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). miRNA-seq data were obtained from TCGA, and the data in RPM (reads per million mapped reads) format were log2 converted. The above data were all analyzed by Student's t test. The figures were created with the R software package ggplot2 (Lucent Technologies, Murray Hill, NJ, USA).

Clinical tissue samples

Human breast cancer tissues (100 pairs of tumor and adjacent tissues) were obtained from Cancer Hospital, the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and Peking Union Medical College. All tissues were collected and quickly stored at −80°C. The clinical information of breast cancer patients in our study is shown in Supplementary Table 1. This work was approved by the Ethics Committee of Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences/National Cancer Center (No. NCC2018–043). All patients participating in this study provided informed consent.

Cell culture

Human breast cancer cell lines MCF-7, ZR-75-1, CAL-51, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, HCC1937, T47D, and SK-BR-3 (gifted by Prof. Chen Lin, Cancer Hospital, the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and Peking Union Medical College) were cultivated in DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The human normal breast epithelial cell line MCF10A was cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 5% horse serum, insulin, epidermal growth factor, cholera toxin, and hydrocortisone. All cells were cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. We confirmed the authentication of all cell lines used.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

The collected cells and fresh frozen tissues were lysed to obtain total RNA using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). According to the manufacturer's instructions, we synthesized complementary DNA from RNA using Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). The mRNA expression of hsa-miR-3653, ATG12, and AMBRA1 was measured by qPCR using the Blastaq™ 2X qPCR MasterMix (ABM, Jiangsu, China) and CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). The internal references for miRNA and mRNA were U6 and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), respectively. The results were analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method, with Ct representing the threshold cycle. The qPCR primers used in our study are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Cell transfection

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting ATG12 or AMBRA1, a miR-3653 mimic and corresponding negative control (miR-NC), and a miR-3653 inhibitor and its negative control (miR-NC inhibitor) were purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). All sequences in our study are shown in Supplementary Table 3. The ATG12 and AMBRA1 overexpression plasmids were purchased from GeneCopoeia (Rockville, Maryland, USA). miRNA and siRNA transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), and we transfected plasmids into cells with Neofect™ DNA transfection reagent (NEOFECT, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Lentiviral infection

A green fluorescent protein (GFP) vector overexpressing miR-3653 (LV-miR-3653) and lentivirus expressing GFP empty vector (LV-control) were constructed by JTS Scientific Inc (Wuhan, China). MDA-MB-231 cells were infected with the LV-miR-3653 and LV-control lentiviruses at a multiplicity of infection of 10 with 6.0 μg/mL polybrene. Seventy-two hours after transduction, the infected cells were selected with puromycin (1 μg/m). Subsequently, qPCR was used to determine cell overexpression efficiency.

Western blot

Total protein extraction, protein concentration quantification, and sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis experiments were performed as previously described.[17] Primary antibodies against GAPDH (1:1000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech, Rosemont, Illinois, USA), ATG12 (1:1000, 11264-1-AP, Proteintech), AMBRA1 (1:1000, 13762-1-AP, Proteintech), light chain 3 (LC3) (1:1000, L8918, Sigma-Aldrich, Louis, MO, USA), Beclin-1 (1:1000, 3738S, CST, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA), p62 (1:1000, 5114S, CST), E-cadherin (1:1000, 3195S, CST), vimentin (1:1000, sc-32322, Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), slug (1:1000, sc-15391, Santa Cruz), and twist (1:1000, ab49254, Abcam, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) were used overnight at 4°C. The protein level was detected by chemiluminescence (General Electric Company, Boston, Massachusetts, USA).

Cell proliferation assay

The xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analyzer-MP system (ACEA Biosciences/Roche Applied Science, San Diego, California, USA) was used to determine the proliferative capacity of breast cancer cells. We first added 50 μL medium to E-Plate 96-well plates (Roche Applied Science, Indiana, USA) for baseline detection. A total of 1.5 × 103 transfected cells/well were resuspended into E-Plate 96-well plates, which were placed in the system after standing for 30 min at room temperature.

Transwell migration/invasion assays

Migration and invasion assays were performed as described.[18] The ability of breast cancer cells to migrate and invade was detected by Transwell chambers (Corning, New York, New York, USA). For the migration experiment, 100 μL serum-free medium was used to resuspend 1 × 105 transfected cells in the top chamber. Then, we filled the lower chamber with 600 μL medium containing 30% FBS. Fifteen to 24 h later, we fixed the upper chambers with methanol for 5 min and then stained them with 2% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich) solution at room temperature for 8 min. We rinsed the chambers with water, carefully wiped off the medial membrane with cotton, and dried them. The chamber membrane was cut off and fixed on the slide. We used a phase-contrast microscope to observe images and photographs. Nine fields were randomly selected from each chamber to count cells using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA), and the results were counted by GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Invasion assays were performed similarly to the migration assay, but 100 μL 2% Matrigel was added to the upper chamber in advance.

Colony forming cell assay

Colony forming cell assay was performed as described.[18] The transfected cells were seeded at 1.5 × 103 cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured for approximately 10 to 14 days. Then, we washed the cells twice with phosphate buffered solution (PBS). One milliliter of methanol was added for 5 min of fixation, followed by 2% crystal violet solution staining for 8 min, and the cells were counted.

Reporter construction and luciferase reporter assay

The pmirGLO dual-luciferase plasmid with wild-type (WT) or mutated (MUT) ATG12 and AMBRA1 3′ UTRs inserted downstream of the firefly luciferase gene was constructed by Generay (Shanghai, China). The luciferase reporter assay was performed according to the instructions of the Dual Luciferase Reporting and Detection System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Detection of autophagic flux using GFP-mCherry-LC3B cells

The GFP-mCherry-LC3B lentivirus construct was obtained from Hanbio (Shanghai, China). The changes in LC3II and autophagic flux can be labeled and tracked by the GFP-mCherry-LC3B dual fluorescent system. When autophagosomes fuse with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, the acidic lysosome environment quenches acid-sensitive GFP fluorescence, whereas mCherry is not affected.[19] Therefore, the ratio of GFP to mCherry fluorescence indicates autolysosome formation. We imaged the cells by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Olympus, Miyazaki, Japan).

Animal experiments

We purchased 5-week-old BALB/c female nude mice from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China). There were six experimental mice in each group. To further substantiate the role of miR-3653 in breast cancer metastasis, we injected MDA-MB-231 cells (1 × 106 cells) stably expressing miR-3653 or negative control into BALB/c nude mice via the tail vein. Eight weeks later, the mice were euthanized, and the livers were isolated and analyzed. Animal experiments in our study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of National Cancer Center/National Clinical Research Center for Cancer/Cancer Hospital, the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and Peking Union Medical College (No. NCC2018A014). The protocols of the animal experiments were strictly followed in accordance with the guidelines of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health.

Statistical analysis

In our study, GraphPad Prism, IBM SPSS 20 (St. Louis, MO, USA), and ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) were used for the statistical analysis of images and data. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used for clinical data analysis, Student's t test was applied for difference analysis between any two groups, one-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni post hoc test was used for difference analysis among multiple groups, and the survival data were analyzed by Kaplan–Meier analysis and the log-rank test. Pearson correlation was used to calculate r and P values. For clinical data, the chi-square test and Fisher's exact test were used for classification variables. We performed all experiments at least thrice. We presented the data with normal distribution as the mean ± standard error of mean. A P value <0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

miR-3653 is lowly expressed in breast cancer

To explore the miRNAs related to metastasis in breast cancer, an miRNA array was performed using breast cancer cells with high metastatic ability and low metastasis ability (using a four-fold cutoff, GEO accession number: GSE110204, Figure 1A).[20] Among the top 10 candidate miRNAs, seven of them were upregulated except miR-1275, miR-3653, and miR-423 in breast cancer according to the TCGA database [Figure 1B]. Next, we analyzed the target genes of these three miRNAs through the TargetScan website and found that they are closely related to autophagy-associated genes [Figure 1C]. Studies have reported that miR-1275 and miR-423 participate in the regulation of autophagy in tumors.[21,22] However, the correlation between miR-3653 and autophagy remains unclear. Therefore, we aimed to focus on miR-3653 in our subsequent research. The TCGA data showed that miR-3653 is upregulated in prostate cancer (P < 0.001), non-small cell lung cancer (P < 0.001), and gastric cancer (P = 0.044) and is expressed at low levels in cholangiocarcinoma (P = 0.008) and colon cancer (P < 0.001) [Figure 1D–H]. To explore the effect of miR-3653 on breast cancer development, we performed qPCR to detect miR-3653 levels in 100 pairs of tumor and adjacent tissue samples. The expression of miR-3653 in tumor tissues was lower than that in paracancerous tissues (0.054 ± 0.013 vs. 0.131 ± 0.028, t = 2.475, P = 0.014, Figure 1I). Compared with non-metastatic tissues, miR-3653 expression was downregulated in tissues with lymph node metastasis (0.015 ± 0.004 vs. 0.078 ± 0.020, t = 2.319, P = 0.023, Figure 1J). However, we found no significant difference in miR-3653 expression in different subtypes of breast cancer tissues by qPCR [Supplementary Figure 1A]. We found no correlation between the prognostic value of miR-3653 distribution and breast cancer subtypes [Supplementary Figure 1B-D]. Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that patients with low miR-3653 expression had reduced survival (P < 0.001, Figure 1K). TCGA database analysis demonstrated that the copy number of the region where miR-3653 is located was lower in breast cancer tissues compared with paracancerous tissues [Figure 1L].

miR-3653 inhibits the malignant phenotypes of breast cancer cells

We measured miR-3653 levels in MCF10A and breast cancer cells. miR-3653 levels were lower in the eight breast cancer cell lines compared with MCF10A cells (P < 0.001, Figure 2A). To elucidate the role of miR-3653 in the phenotypes of breast cancer cells, we transfected miR-3653 mimics into MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells and miR-3653 inhibitors into MCF-7 and CAL-51 cells. The results showed that miR-3653 was increased by mimic transfection (53.248 ± 3.572 vs. 1.046 ± 0.206, t = 14.590, P < 0.001; 33.186 ± 4.045 vs. 1.013 ± 0.120, t = 7.950, P = 0.001), whereas miR-3653 expression was decreased by transfection of inhibitors (0.211 ± 0.085 vs. 1.001 ± 0.036, t = 8.605, P = 0.001; 0.127 ± 0.004 vs. 1.003 ± 0.052, t = 16.700, P < 0.001) [Figure 2B, C]. Our results suggested that upregulation of miR-3653 significantly abrogated malignant phenotypes of breast cancer cells, including proliferation, migration (MDA-MB-231: 0.353 ± 0.013 vs. 1.000 ± 0.038, t = 16.290, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.200 ± 0.014 vs. 1.000 ± 0.043, t = 17.530, P < 0.001), invasion (MDA-MB-231: 0.723 ± 0.056 vs. 1.000 ± 0.035, t = 4.223, P = 0.013; MDA-MB-468: 0.222 ± 0.016 vs. 1.000 ± 0.019, t = 31.050, P < 0.001), and colony formation (MDA-MB-231: 0.472 ± 0.022 vs. 1.000 ± 0.022, t = 16.620, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.650 ± 0.040 vs. 1.000 ± 0.098, t = 3.297, P = 0.030). Conversely, inhibition of miR-3653 expression markedly promoted the malignant phenotypes in breast cancer cells (migration [MCF-7: 1.300 ± 0.026 vs. 1.000 ± 0.055, t = 4.937, P = 0.008; CAL-51: 2.200 ± 0.024 vs. 1.000 ± 0.038, t = 26.930, P < 0.001], invasion [MCF-7: 1.600 ± 0.033 vs. 1.000 ± 0.031, t = 13.230, P < 0.001; CAL-51: 2.000 ± 0.028 vs. 1.000 ± 0.046, t = 18.430, P < 0.001], and colony formation [MCF-7: 7.000 ± 0.808 vs. 1.000 ± 0.110, t = 7.356, P = 0.002; CAL-51: 10.300 ± 0.058 vs. 1.000 ± 0.017, t = 154.800, P < 0.001]) [Figure 2D, E].

ATG12 and AMBRA1 are direct targets of miR-3653

To reveal how miR-3653 regulates the phenotypes of breast cancer cells, two miRNA target prediction programs (TargetScan and miRanda) were used for computational analysis to look for miR-3653-related direct target genes. The results showed 16 potential genes in the intersection of the prediction results from the two databases. Among these candidate genes, six are associated with autophagy [Figure 3A]. The effect of overexpressed miR-3653 on the expression of these six autophagy-related genes was measured by qPCR. The results showed that the expression of ATG12 (0.118 ± 0.010 vs. 1.000 ± 0.009, t = 63.750, P < 0.001) and AMBRA1 (0.222 ± 0.042 vs. 1.014 ± 0.101, t = 7.220, P < 0.001) was reduced most significantly [Figure 3A]. Several studies have found that ATG12 and AMBRA1 can promote pathological progression by regulating autophagy levels in tumor cells. Therefore, we considered ATG12 and AMBRA1 as candidate target genes for miR-3653 in our subsequent research.

The expression of ATG12 and AMBRA1 mRNA in cells overexpressing miR-3653 was measured by qPCR. miR-3653 mimics decreased the mRNA levels of ATG12 (MDA-MB-231: 0.384 ± 0.003 vs. 1.000 ± 0.001, t = 176.900, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.377 ± 0.038 vs. 1.000 ± 0.001, t = 19.450, P < 0.001) and AMBRA1 (0.349 ± 0.015 vs. 1.000 ± 0.001, t = 38.090, P < 0.001; 0.396 ± 0.028 vs. 1.000 ± 0.001, t = 25.500, P < 0.001) [Figure 3B]. Western blot results revealed that overexpression of miR-3653 suppressed the protein levels of ATG12 and AMBRA1 [Figure 3C]. Moreover, an approximately inverse correlation was observed between ATG12 or AMBRA1 expression and miR-3653 in breast cancer cells [Figure 3D]. Based on miRNA prediction programs, we assessed the binding of miR-3653 to ATG12 and the AMBRA1 mRNA 3′UTRs using a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Consistent with our prediction, overexpressed miR-3653 remarkably reduced luciferase activity in pmiR-ATG12/AMBRA1-WT cells, whereas the luciferase activity in pmiR-ATG12/AMBRA1-MUT cells did not significantly change, indicating that miR-3653 can directly bind to ATG12 (MDA-MB-231: 0.714 ± 0.021 vs. 1.005 ± 0.021, t = 9.792, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.554 ± 0.007 vs. 1.076 ± 0.073, t = 7.140, P < 0.001) and AMBRA1 (MDA-MB-231: 0.461 ± 0.012 vs. 0.922 ± 0.017, t = 21.550, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.421 ± 0.052 vs. 0.717 ± 0.026, t = 5.098, P < 0.001) mRNA 3′ UTRs [Figure 3E].

We conducted qPCR to detect ATG12 and AMBRA1 expression in 41 pairs of breast cancer and adjacent tissue samples. Compared with adjacent tissues, ATG12 (1.661 ± 0.358 vs. 0.008 ± 0.001, t = 4.606, P < 0.001) and AMBRA1 (1.872 ± 0.695 vs. 0.373 ± 0.095, t = 2.136, P = 0.036) expression levels were perceptibly higher in breast cancer tissues [Figure 4A, B]. The qPCR results showed that high expression of ATG12 (P < 0.001) and AMBRA1 (P = 0.012) was associated with a poor survival rate for breast cancer patients [Figure 4C]. The results were consistent with the results from the UALCAN database (P = 0.003, http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) and Kaplan–Meier plotter (P = 0.088, https://kmplot.com/analysis/) [Figure 4D]. Moreover, miR-3653 expression was inversely correlated with ATG12 (P < 0.001) and AMBRA1 (P = 0.013) expression in breast cancer patients [Figure 4E].

miR-3653 inhibits autophagy of breast cancer cells

Our previous results indicated that the direct target genes of miR-3653 are ATG12 and AMBRA1, and we inferred that miR-3653 might participate in autophagy regulation. LC3-II is commonly used as a marker for autophagosome identification.[23] Sequestosome 1 (also known as p62) acts as a connexin by binding ubiquitinated proteins to aggregate and deliver them to the autophagosome. In the process of lysosomal degradation, p62 bound to substrates is degraded by proteolytic enzymes. Therefore, elevated p62 levels are generally considered indicators of autophagy inhibition. In MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells, overexpression of miR-3653 reduced BECN-1 and LC3 II expression and enhanced p62 expression [Figure 5A]. In addition, miR-3653 knockdown elevated BECN1 and LC3-II expression and suppressed p62 expression in MCF-7 and CAL-51 cells [Figure 5A]. MDA-MB-231 cells stably transfected with GFP-mCherry-LC3B were transfected with mimics to overexpress miR-3653, and autophagic flux was detected by immunofluorescence. Our results showed that acid-sensitive GFP fluorescence was quenched to a greater extent in cells with low miR-3653 expression. Compared with control cells, miR-3653 overexpression impeded the flux from autophagosomes to autolysosomes in GFP-mCherry-LC3B cells (GFP, 28.667 ± 1.764 vs. 5.333 ± 0.882, t = 11.830, P < 0.001; mCherry, 29.333 ± 1.202 vs. 6.667 ± 1.202, t = 13.340, P < 0.001) [Figure 5B]. The above data showed that miR-3653 inhibits autophagy of breast cancer cells.

miR-3653 inhibits autophagic activity and malignant phenotypes of breast cancer cells by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1

To further investigate whether ATG12 and AMBRA1 are involved in autophagy inhibition by miR-3653, we assessed autophagy-associated protein levels and autophagic flux in cancer cells. We observed that miR-3653 mimics, si-ATG12, and si-AMBRA1 decreased BECN1 and LC3-II levels and increased p62 levels, whereas overexpression of ATG12 or AMBRA1 restored the expression levels of these proteins [Figure 5C, D]. Similarly, miR-3653 mimics, si-ATG12, and si-AMBRA1 decreased autophagic flux (GFP: 19.333 ± 0.882 vs. 6.667 ± 0.667, t = 11.460, P < 0.001; 19.000 ± 0.577 vs. 6.667 ± 0.667, t = 13.980, P < 0.001; 19.667 ± 1.764 vs. 6.667 ± 0.667, t = 6.894, P = 0.002; mCherry: 17.333 ± 1.453 vs. 6.000 ± 0.577, t = 7.249, P = 0.002; 15.000 ± 1.000 vs. 6.000 ± 0.577, t = 7.794, P = 0.002; 18.000 ± 0.577 vs. 6.000 ± 0.577, t = 14.700, P < 0.001). Overexpression of ATG12 or AMBRA1 rescued the reduction in autophagic flux caused by miR-3653 mimics (GFP: 8.000 ± 0.577 vs. 19.333 ± 0.882, t = 10.750, P < 0.001; 7.667 ± 0.882 vs. 19.333 ± 0.882, t = 9.354, P < 0.001; mCherry: 7.667 ± 0.882 vs. 17.333 ± 1.453, t = 5.687, P = 0.005; 8.333 ± 0.882 vs. 17.333 ± 1.453, t = 5.295, P = 0.006) [Figure 5E].

Next, we investigated whether miR-3653 inhibited malignant phenotypes by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1 in breast cancer cells. The results revealed that si-ATG12 and si-AMBRA1 inhibited proliferation, migration (MDA-MB-231: 0.495 ± 0.005 vs. 1.000 ± 0.018, t = 26.760, P = 0.004; 0.534 ± 0.018 vs. 1.000 ± 0.018, t = 18.290, P = 0.009; MDA-MB-468: 0.450 ± 0.036 vs. 1.000 ± 0.051, t = 8.823, P < 0.001; 0.481 ± 0.037 vs. 1.000 ± 0.051, t = 8.274, P = 0.001), invasion (MDA-MB-231: 0.312 ± 0.017 vs. 1.000 ± 0.114, t = 5.998, P < 0.001; 0.455 ± 0.007 vs. 1.000 ± 0.114, t = 4.793, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.354 ± 0.052 vs. 1.000 ± 0.030, t = 10.770, P < 0.001; 0.417 ± 0.046 vs. 1.000 ± 0.030, t = 10.710, P < 0.001), and colony formation (MDA-MB-231: 0.268 ± 0.021 vs. 1.000 ± 0.041, t = 15.880, P < 0.001; 0.313 ± 0.031 vs. 1.000 ± 0.041, t = 13.340, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.630 ± 0.040 vs. 1.000 ± 0.071, t = 4.542, P = 0.011, 0.685 ± 0.055 vs. 1.000 ± 0.071, t = 3.494, P = 0.025) of MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells [Figure 6A–D]. Moreover, ATG12 (migration, MDA-MB-231: 0.948 ± 0.041 vs. 0.511 ± 0.013, t = 10.160, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.851 ± 0.025 vs. 0.412 ± 0.040, t = 9.340, P < 0.001; invasion, MDA-MB-231: 0.986 ± 0.029 vs. 0.309 ± 0.017, t = 20.170, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.923 ± 0.030 vs. 0.345 ± 0.015, t = 17.380, P < 0.001; colony formation, MDA-MB-231: 0.881 ± 0.017 vs. 0.525 ± 0.051, t = 6.640, P = 0.003; MDA-MB-468: 1.082 ± 0.055 vs. 0.507 ± 0.047, t = 7.890, P < 0.001) or AMBRA1 (migration, MDA-MB-231: 0.733 ± 0.029 vs. 0.511 ± 0.013, t = 6.945, P < 0.001; MDA-MB-468: 0.873 ± 0.014 vs. 0.412 ± 0.040, t = 10.900, P < 0.001; invasion, MDA-MB-231: 0.930 ± 0.049 vs. 0.309 ± 0.017, t = 11.990, P = 0.002; MDA-MB-468: 0.762 ± 0.033 vs. 0.345 ± 0.015, t = 11.660, P < 0.001; colony formation, MDA-MB-231: 0.884 ± 0.014 vs. 0.525 ± 0.051, t = 6.806, P = 0.003; MDA-MB-468: 1.096 ± 0.047 vs. 0.507 ± 0.047, t = 8.777, P < 0.001) overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of miR-3653 mimics on malignant phenotypes in breast cancer cells [Figure 6A–D]. In general, these data indicate that miR-3653 inhibits autophagic activity and malignant phenotypes by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1.

miR-3653 inhibits epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1

With the knowledge that miR-3653 plays a regulatory role in autophagy and considering the interaction between autophagy and metastasis,[6] we investigated whether miR-3653 participated in metastasis through ATG12 and AMBRA1. EMT is considered a significant factor in tumor metastasis.[24] We assessed the expression of ATG12, AMBRA1, and EMT-related proteins, including E-cadherin, vimentin, slug, and twist, in breast cancer cells. Downregulation of ATG12 and AMBRA1 by siRNAs inhibited the expression of vimentin, slug, and twist and increased E-cadherin expression [Figure 7A]. miR-3653 mimics had a similar effect on the expression of these proteins, and this effect was prevented in MDA-MB-231 or MDA-MB-468 cells cotransfected with ATG12 or AMBRA1 plasmids [Figure 7A]. In contrast, miR-3653 inhibitors promoted the expression of vimentin, slug, and twist in MCF-7 and CAL-51 cells, and the upregulation of these proteins was prevented in cells cotransfected with si-ATG12 or si-AMBRA1 [Figure 7B]. The above data indicated that miR-3653 inhibited the EMT process in breast cancer cells by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1.

Given the previous finding of a close relationship between miR-3653 and the migration and invasion of breast cancer cells, we aimed to confirm the effect of miR-3653 on breast cancer metastasis in vivo. We stably infected MDA-MB-231 cells with miR-3653 lentiviral vectors (LV-miR-3653) or negative control (LV-control). As expected, miR-3653 expression in LV-miR-3653 cells was higher than that in LV-control cells (3.938 ± 0.280 vs. 1.000 ± 0.007, t = 10.500, P < 0.001, Figure 8A), and LV-miR-3653 cells showed alleviated malignant phenotypes (migration: 0.400 ± 0.020 vs. 1.000 ± 0.038, t = 14.020, P < 0.001; invasion: 0.333 ± 0.027 vs. 1.000 ± 0.048, t = 12.090, P < 0.001; colony formation: 0.240 ± 0.017 vs. 1.000 ± 0.052, t = 13.880, P < 0.001; Figure 8B–D). We established an in vivo metastasis model by injecting two groups of cells into nude mice via the tail vein. After 8 weeks, the area and number of liver metastases were analyzed. The hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) results revealed that the area of metastasis in LV-miR-3653 was diminished compared with that in LV-control mice [Figure 8E]. Discussion

Autophagy is a known intracellular evolutionarily conserved catabolic degradation process that helps prevent damaged organelle and protein accumulation, recycles cytoplasmic components, and maintains the homeostasis of cells.[25] There is some debate about the relationship between autophagy and cancer. Several studies have demonstrated that autophagy plays a double-edged role in tumors, inhibiting the growth of benign tumors and stimulating the development of advanced tumors.[25,26] Studies have shown that autophagy has many benefits for tumor cell proliferation and metastasis.[27] Tumor cells have high metabolic characteristics and require higher nutrition and energy than normal cells, and the tumor microenvironment often fails to satisfy the malignant growth of the tumor. However, increasing autophagy activity helps tumor cells to survive this crisis.[28] Tumor cells produce a great number of harmful components, such as damaged organelles and damaged proteins, after chemotherapy and radiotherapy treatment. In this scenario, increasing autophagy activity removes these deleterious substances promptly and provides emergency substrates and energy to repair damaged DNA of tumor cells.[29] Autophagy is generally believed to promote the survival and expansion of metastatic cells.[26] It has been reported that the autophagy process promotes the aggressiveness of many kinds of tumors by facilitating metastasis; these tumor types include breast cancer,[30,31] melanoma,[32] hepatocellular carcinoma,[33] and glioblastoma.[34]

ATG12 and AMBRA1 promote tumor initiation and development by promoting autophagy.[35] Evidence has demonstrated that upregulated ATG12 induces autophagy and promotes hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation,[36] which promotes chemotherapy resistance in gastric cancer,[37,38] and high expression of ATG12 is associated with poor survival in clear cell renal cell carcinoma patients.[39] AMBRA1 is an activated factor of BECN1, which mainly regulates the lipid kinase activity of the BECN1/vacuolar protein sorting 34 complex by binding to BECN1 and promoting the formation of autophagosomes.[40] Studies have found that AMBRA1 is a biomarker of primary melanoma.[41] Our study found that both ATG12 and AMBRA1 are upregulated in breast cancer patients and are inversely correlated to prognosis, suggesting that ATG12 and AMBRA1 might influence the acceleration of pathological progression in breast cancer through the autophagy pathway. Our study found that ATG12 and AMBRA1 are target genes of miR-3653, and miR-3653 inhibits the expression level of ATG12 and AMBRA1. We found that miR-3653 inhibits autophagy flux of breast cancer cells, revealing that miR-3653 inhibits autophagy flux of breast cancer cells by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1, thereby inhibiting the malignant phenotype of breast cancer cells.

EMT is a process in which tumor cells are reprogramed from epithelial phenotypes to mobile and invasive mesenchymal-like phenotypes through cytoskeletal rearrangement, loss of cellular junctions, resistance to anoikis, and extracellular matrix remodeling.[42] EMT is a dynamic process influenced by transition states and the microenvironment, and there is no single signaling pathway governing this transition.[43] EMT increases the metastatic characteristics of breast cancer cells. Once the low motility epithelial cells become mesenchymal phenotype, the migration of breast tumor cells will occur. The activation of EMT leads to the transition from epithelial phenotype to mesenchymal phenotype. At molecular level, E-cadherin is downregulated and vimentin and N-cadherin are upregulated during EMT in breast cancer.[44] Some studies have found that induction of EMT not only enhances the ability of cell metastasis, but also increases the level of proliferation, leading to drug resistance of breast cancer, including chemotherapy resistance and radio-resistance.[45] Therefore, EMT inhibition can be considered as a synergistic therapy with radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

Several lines of evidence suggest a complicated association between EMT and autophagy. Previous evidence has found that EMT-related signaling pathways can trigger or suppress autophagy. Notably, autophagy can also participate in the induction or inhibition of the EMT process.[46] The relationship between autophagy and EMT is controversial. Autophagy plays a role as a tumor suppressor and tends to selectively downregulate key transcription factors of EMT in the early stage to prevent metastasis. Additionally, the autophagy process provides essential nutrients and energy for EMT, which assists tumor cells in survival under stressful conditions. Therefore, blocking the EMT process through autophagy inhibitors or activators may become an effective strategy for cancer treatment.[46] Evidence has shown that autophagy is regulated by the BECN1, Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription, p53, and phosphatidylinositide 3-kinases/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin signaling pathways, which can significantly affect the EMT process. BECN1-induced autophagy promoted the EMT process by increasing vimentin and twist expression and inhibiting E-cadherin expression. BECN1 has been identified as an independent biomarker for the prognosis of overall survival and progression-free survival of gastric cancer and liver cancer patients.[46] It has been suggested that cells with EMT-like phenotypes have high autophagy levels, and autophagy inhibitors may be more beneficial for renal cell carcinoma when used in combination with current treatment regimens.[47] Our work found that miR-3653 was downregulated in breast cancer cells with high metastatic ability, and miR-3653 inhibited autophagy flux. Further study substantiated that miR-3653 could block the EMT process of breast cancer cells by inhibiting autophagy related genes ATG12 and AMBRA1. Therefore, miR-3653 is expected to become a novel target for the treatment of breast cancer.

In conclusion, our work demonstrated that downregulation of miR-3653 was correlated with lymph node metastasis and poor prognosis in breast cancer, suggesting that miR-3653 might be used as a potential prognostic indicator in clinical breast cancer. miR-3653 reduced autophagy levels to inhibit EMT of breast cancer cells by targeting ATG12 and AMBRA1. Our work provided a new idea and target for the metastasis of breast cancer.
