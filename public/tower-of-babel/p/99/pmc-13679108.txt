A bladder cancer–associated gene signature for exploratory qPCR-based survival stratification in NAC-Treated muscle-invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Xingyu Chen, Toru Sakatani, Sunao Tanaka, Alberto Contreras-Sanz, Peter Black, Charles J Rosser, Hideki Furuya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMuscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) is an aggressive and heterogeneous malignancy with limited biomarkers to guide patient stratification and response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). We sought to define the biological basis and clinical utility of a bladder cancer–associated ten-gene signature for predicting prognosis and therapeutic response.MethodsBulk transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNASeq) of primary MIBC tumors were integrated to characterize the cellular context of the ten-gene bladder cancer (BLCA) signature. Machine-learning approaches were applied to qPCR-derived expression data to develop an exploratory model for survival stratification.ResultsAn expression-based direction-weighted BLCA signature score was associated with worse OS, DSS, and PFI in TCGA-BLCA and marked a tumor state characterized by coordinated activation of oncogenic programs including EMT, cell-cycle, and MAPK/PI3K signaling. snRNASeq revealed enrichment of signature activity within the stromal compartment, marked by expansion of FAP+ /PDPN+ fibroblasts and MHCII+ macrophages with endothelial remodeling. High-signature tumors exhibited an immune-dysregulated microenvironment characterized by increased immune checkpoint expression (CD274, HAVCR2, TIGIT) and reduced antigen-presentation machinery. This stromal-dominant phenotype was particularly associated with Luminal MIBC. Functionally, high expression of key signature components (e.g. SDC1, VEGFA, CXCL8) predicts a distinct, targetable vulnerability to MEK inhibitors in drug sensitivity databases.ConclusionsTranslating these findings, qPCR-derived expression of the ten-gene panel was used to build an exploratory machine-learning model for survival stratification in the NAC-treated MIBC cohort. Background

Bladder cancer (BLCA) is the second most common genitourinary malignancy, ranking as the sixth most common cancer in men and the seventeenth in women [1], which is a highly heterogeneous disease with variable clinical outcomes and treatment responses [2, 3]. Though muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) represents approximately 20% of newly diagnosed bladder cancer cases, it is the most aggressive form and thus is associated with a poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options. Radical cystectomy (RC) remains the most effective treatment for MIBC; however, about half of the patients treated with RC eventually experience recurrence with distant metastases, often resulting in fatal outcomes [4]. Recent studies indicate that neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) can increase cancer-specific survival by 5–10% in MIBC patients [5]. Despite these benefits, NAC presents potential risks, including non-responsiveness in some patients, treatment toxicity, and critical surgical delays [6]. In addition, reliable biomarkers for patient stratification remain an unmet clinical need. Identifying molecular determinants of therapeutic response and resistance, particularly to NAC, is essential for advancing precision oncology in MIBC.

To address this gap, we previously developed and analytically validated a urine-based diagnostic assay (Oncuria) comprising ten biomarkers—ANG, APOE, CA9, CXCL8, MMP9, MMP10, A1AT, PAI-1, SDC1, and VEGFA [7, 8]. The BLCA–associated signature captures diverse aspects of tumor biology, including tumorigenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune modulation, and angiogenesis [9–14]. While initially established for non-invasive detection of BCa, their strong biological relevance suggests that this BLCA gene signature (ANG, APOE, CA9, CXCL8, MMP9, MMP10, SERPINA1, SERPINE1, SDC1 and VEGFA) may also inform tumor progression and therapeutic response [15].

In this study, we performed a comprehensive reanalysis of our previously generated single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNASeq) dataset [16] from our 25 treatment-naïve MIBC samples to investigate the cellular and molecular mechanisms associated with the BLCA–associated gene signature. By integrating single-cell transcriptomics and machine learning (ML)-based predictive modelling, we aimed to (i) characterize the expression patterns of these biomarkers across distinct tumor-associated cell types, (ii) assess their relationship with molecular subtypes of BLCA, (iii) determine their role in immune modulation and tumor-stroma interactions, and (iv) develop an exploratory qPCR-based model for survival stratification in a NAC-treated MIBC cohort.

Methods

Patient populations

Our cohort included 82 MIBC patients treated with NAC followed by RC at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (n = 7), University of British Columbia (n = 47) [17], and Kyoto University Hospital (n = 28). All patients provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Cedars-Sinai Institutional Review Board (IRB#00000451) and University of British Columbia (UBC)’s Research Ethics Board (H09-01628, H16-01490, and H20-02726). The patients with variant subtype histology types other than glandular and squamous differentiation were excluded. FFPE samples were collected via transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) at diagnosis. The clinical annotation cohort included 82 patients. Among these, 81 had matched qPCR data and were included in qPCR-based modeling. Survival modeling was performed in 79 patients with complete qPCR and OS data. An additional testing cohort of 46 patients was used for split-sample/internal evaluation.

NAC cohort and response definition

The qPCR-based NAC cohort included patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical cystectomy. Clinical annotation included institution, pathological T stage at radical cystectomy, NAC response category, overall survival, and recurrence-free survival.

NAC response was defined according to pathological response annotation at radical cystectomy. Complete response (CR) corresponded to ypT0/T0 disease, partial response (PR) corresponded to non–muscle-invasive residual disease or pathological downstaging, including ypTa, ypTis/Tis, or ypT1, and non-response corresponded to residual muscle-invasive disease or clinically annotated non-response. For binary NAC response modeling, CR and PR cases were combined as responders, whereas cases annotated as “non” were classified as non-responders.

Overall survival and recurrence-free survival were measured in months from radical cystectomy to death, recurrence, or last follow-up. In the source clinical file, censoring was coded as 1. For Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses, event status was recoded using the standard survival-analysis convention, with event = 1 indicating death or recurrence and event = 0 indicating censoring.

Patients with missing qPCR data or missing NAC response annotation were excluded from NAC response modeling. Patients with missing survival time or survival status were excluded from survival-specific analyses.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from 4 FFPE curls of 10 μm thickness using AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE kit (QIAGEN) as described previously. cDNA synthesis was performed with 5 μL of RNA solution using the Prelude One-Step PreAmp master mix as described previously. For the UBC’s cohort, samples were extracted with the Promega Maxwell RSC RNA FFPE kit following the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified with a Qubit fluorometer. Total RNA was assessed for fragment size using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Psomagen) as previously described [15]

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis

qPCR was conducted using the QuantStudio Real-Time PCR System and PowerUP SYBR Green master mix for qPCR (Applied Biosystems). Primers were specific to the target genes including 10 biomarkers genes (ANG, APOE, CA9, CXCL8, MMP9, MMP10, SERPINA1, SERPINE1, SDC1 and VEGFA) and the housekeeping genes (TBP, ATP5E and CLTC). Gene expression levels were normalized using the 2^-Δ Ct method with housekeeping genes, as previously described [18].

Public data Acquisition

TCGA-BLCA Cohort: Publicly available data for The Cancer Genome Atlas Bladder Cancer (TCGA-BLCA) cohort, including clinical data, RNA-sequencing (RSEM normalized), somatic mutation (MAF), and copy-number variation (GISTIC2 processed) data, were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) data portal. snRNASeq Cohort: We performed a reanalysis of a previously published snRNASeq dataset [16] comprising 25 treatment-naïve, MIBC samples.

TCGA-BLCA data analysis

Gene-set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using the GSVA R package (v1.48.3) to calculate a composite enrichment score for the 10-gene BLCA gene signature. This score was compared between groups using appropriate statistical tests (see below). Correlations between gene-specific CNV and mRNA expression were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Somatic alteration landscapes were characterized using the maftools R package (v2.16.0). Gene–pathway interaction modeling was performed using the GSCALite [19] web server. The tumor-immune microenvironment was assessed by correlating the GSVA score with the abundance of 24 immune-cell populations inferred using ImmuCellAI.

Expression-based BLCA-associated signature survival analysis

To directly evaluate the prognostic association of the expression-based BLCA-associated gene signature in TCGA-BLCA, transcriptomic and clinical data were obtained from the UCSC Xena data portal. Survival outcomes, including overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI), were retrieved from the curated TCGA Pan-Cancer Clinical Resource. Only primary tumor specimens (TCGA sample type 01) were included. Samples with missing expression data or incomplete endpoint-specific survival data were excluded from the corresponding analysis.

Log2-transformed RSEM expression data were used for the ten BLCA-associated signature genes: ANG, APOE, CA9, CXCL8, MMP9, MMP10, SERPINA1, SERPINE1, SDC1, and VEGFA. Gene expression values were z-score normalized across primary tumor samples. Because individual genes showed heterogeneous associations with OS, we constructed a direction-weighted expression score in which each gene was oriented according to the sign of its univariate Cox coefficient for OS, the pre-specified primary survival endpoint. The direction-weighted BLCA signature score was calculated as the mean of the oriented z-scores across the ten genes. Higher scores therefore indicate higher predicted risk. The OS-derived gene directions were fixed and were not re-estimated for DSS or PFI.

Patients were dichotomized into high- and low-signature groups using maximally selected rank statistics with a minimum group proportion of 0.30. Kaplan–Meier curves were compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed for both the continuous signature score and the dichotomized high-versus-low signature group, with the low-signature group as the reference. Hazard ratios greater than 1 indicate worse outcome in the high-signature group. These analyses were performed in R using the survival and survminer packages, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

snRnaseq data processing and Re-analysis

The raw count matrix from the 25-sample MIBC cohort [16] was re-processed using the scanpy package (v1.9.3) in Python (v3.11). Briefly, cells were filtered based on standard quality control metrics (e.g., n_genes_by_counts, total_counts, and pct_counts_mt). Filtered data was normalized using sc.pp.normalize_total, log-transformed using sc.pp.log1p, and scaled. Highly variable genes were identified for principal component analysis (PCA).

Downstream analysis, including Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for dimensionality reduction and visualization, was performed. Major cell lineages were identified and annotated based on canonical marker gene expression. A composite signature score was calculated per cell using sc.tl.score_genes. Tumors were stratified into ‘low’, ‘medium’, and ‘high’ signature groups based on their sample-level mean score. Cellular composition shifts were quantified using a Ratio of Observed to Expected (Ro/e) analysis (χ2 test). Differential expression analysis (DEA) between high and low signature groups was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test within scanpy’s sc.tl.rank_genes_groups function.

snRnaseq pathway and gene ontology (GO) analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify enriched pathways in specific lineages. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment network mapping was used to visualize functional relationships between terms upregulated in high-signature tumors. These analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.8.3) and GSEA (MSigDB Hallmark & GO, FDR < 0.25). Functional scores for specific gene sets (e.g., immune pathways) were calculated on a single-cell basis using AUCell (v1.22.1).

Drug sensitivity correlation

To explore therapeutic vulnerabilities, biomarker gene expression from the TCGA cohort was correlated (Spearman) with drug sensitivity data (IC₅₀ values) from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) databases.

ML model development and validation

Using the qPCR-derived gene expression data from the NAC-treated MIBC cohort, we developed exploratory ML models for survival stratification. The cohort was divided into training and testing subsets for initial model screening. For survival stratification, model performance was evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), and StepCox (forward) + random survival forest (RSF) was selected as the top-performing algorithm in the apparent train/test model screen. For prognostic modeling, the endpoint was overall survival, measured in months from radical cystectomy to death or last follow-up. Event status was coded as 1 for death and 0 for censoring. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to compare survival between ML-derived high- and low-risk groups, and log-rank tests were used to assess group differences. Univariate Cox regression was used to estimate the association between the ML-derived risk score and survival.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1), Python (v3.11), and JMP Pro (Version 16.1.0). For comparisons of continuous variables between two groups, the two-sided Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for multi-group comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test. Correlations were assessed using Pearson r or Spearman ρ. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the Benjamini–Hochberg method was used to control the false discovery rate (FDR) where noted. The significance threshold was uniformly set at p < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05. A fixed random seed (seed = 12345) was set for all computational analyses to ensure reproducibility.

Results

The BLCA gene signature is elevated in tumors, linked to somatic alterations, and associates with outcome in TCGA-BLCA

We first calculated a GSVA score for the BLCA gene signature (ANG, SDC1, MMP9, MMP10, CA9, VEGFA, CXCL8, SERPINE1, SERPINA1, and APOE) in TCGA-BLCA. The score was significantly higher in tumors than in matched normal urothelium (Wilcoxon, p = 0.02; Fig. 1A) and modestly higher in non-papillary than papillary disease (p = 0.04; Fig. 1B). Across AJCC stages, scores increased overall (Kruskal–Wallis; Fig. 1C).

To define genomic correlates of this signature, we analyzed the relationship between copy-number variation (CNV) and mRNA abundance. Several genes—including VEGFA, MMP9 and SDC1—showed positive CNV–expression correlations (Spearman, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 1D), and both amplifications and deletions were frequent across the panel (Fig. 1E). Somatic alterations were mostly missense substitutions with fewer truncating events (Fig. 1F), predominantly driven by single-nucleotide variants (SNVs; Fig. 1G, H). VEGFA exhibited the highest mutation frequency (~17%, Fig. 1I), largely driven by shallow deletions and low-allele-fraction SNVs. Clinically, patients with ≥1 nonsynonymous SNV showed unexpectedly more favorable PFS, OS, and DSS, whereas DFI was not significantly different (log-rank tests; Fig. 1J–M). Together, these data show that the BLCA-associated gene signature is tumor-enriched, and associated with recurrent genomic events. Because mutation status and expression-based signature activity represent distinct molecular features, we next directly assessed the prognostic relevance of the expression-based signature score.

Expression-based BLCA signature score is associated with adverse survival in TCGA-BLCA

Because the mutation-based analysis does not directly test the prognostic relevance of the expression-based BLCA-associated signature score, we next evaluated a direction-weighted expression score in TCGA-BLCA. The ten signature genes showed heterogeneous individual associations with OS; therefore, each gene was oriented according to the sign of its univariate Cox coefficient for OS, and a unified direction-weighted BLCA signature score was calculated from the mean of the oriented z-scores.

Using this expression-based score, high-signature patients showed significantly worse outcomes across multiple survival endpoints. For OS, high-signature tumors were associated with shorter survival compared with low-signature tumors (n = 406; 178 events; high n = 284, low n = 122; log-rank p = 0.009; Cox HR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.12–2.31, p = 0.010). The continuous score was also associated with OS (HR = 1.89, p = 0.003). Similar associations were observed for DSS (n = 394; 123 events; high n = 228, low n = 166; log-rank p = 0.006; HR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.16–2.53, p = 0.007) and PFI (n = 186; 32 events; high n = 117, low n = 69; log-rank p = 0.035; HR = 2.27, 95% CI 1.04–4.95, p = 0.040). These results support the prognostic relevance of the expression-based BLCA-associated signature score in TCGA-BLCA.

In contrast, the mutation-based analysis of the same ten genes showed an unexpected direction: patients harboring ≥ 1 nonsynonymous SNV in any signature gene displayed more favorable PFS, OS, and DSS compared with WT cases, whereas DFI was not significantly different. We therefore interpret the mutation-based analysis as a distinct genomic observation and do not use it to infer the prognostic direction of the expression-based signature score.

The BLCA gene signature tracks oncogenic pathway activity and reshapes the immune contexture

Pathway analyses revealed that the BLCA gene signature score correlated positively with apoptosis and EMT activity (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.33 and 0.38, FDR < 0.05) and negatively with DNA-damage response and androgen-receptor signaling (ρ ≈ −0.34 and −0.45, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2A). In a broader panel of canonical pathways, higher scores aligned with increased Apoptosis, EMT, and Hormone ER and with reduced DNA-damage and Hormone AR signaling (Fig. 2B). Gene–pathway modelling indicated that SERPINA1, SERPINE1, CXCL8, APOE and MMP9 contribute most consistently to pathway activation across tumors (Fig. 2C).

Immune deconvolution linked higher scores to a reconfigured tumor immune microenvironment. Here, ‘reconfigured’ refers to systematic shifts in immune cell composition and functional state in high-signature tumors relative to low-signature tumors within the same TCGA cohort, rather than comparison to normal urothelium. Across 24 immune populations, the BLCA gene score associated positively with exhausted and effector T-cell states, selected myeloid subsets, and NK/NKT compartments (FDR-significant tiles; Fig. 2D). Tumors with BLCA gene signature panel-gene mutations showed corresponding shifts—reduced NKT cells and increased exhausted T-cell states (Wilcoxon, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2E). CNV events also carried distinct immune signatures: amplifications were associated with decreases in several innate/naïve populations, whereas deletions were associated with increases in Th17 and central-memory T cells and decreases in NK, NKT, Cytotoxic and Th2 (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2F).

Collectively, these results indicate that the BLCA gene signature aligns with proliferative and EMT signaling, and an immune milieu skewed toward chronic activation and dysfunction—features consistent with aggressive, therapy-refractory disease.

High-BLCA gene signature tumors are characterized by fibroblast–myeloid enrichment, endothelial remodeling, and broad transcriptional reprogramming toward immune dysregulation

To investigate how the BLCA gene signature relates to the cellular ecosystems of BLCA, we reanalyzed our previous snRNASeq dataset comprising 25 treatment-naïve, high-grade MIBC tumors [16]. UMAP embedding resolved major tumor-associated populations, including epithelial, fibroblast, endothelial, lymphocyte, and myeloid lineages (Fig. 3A). Feature plots showed cell-type-specific expression of the ten individual biomarkers (Fig. 3B). A composite score derived from this signature was highest in fibroblasts, followed by endothelial cells, epithelial, myeloid, and lymphocyte (Fig. 3C–D). Notably, the individual signature components showed distinct lineage distributions: VEGFA and SERPINE1 contributed strongly to the stromal/angiogenic component, whereas SDC1 was more prominent at the epithelial–stromal interface. The composite score should therefore be interpreted as a coordinated multicellular program rather than a marker of a single cell lineage. At the subtype level, FAP+ fibroblasts, inflammatory macrophages, and KRT13+ epithelial cells showed the highest mean scores (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Averaging the single-cell score per tumor revealed marked inter-tumor heterogeneity, allowing classification of the 25 samples into ‘low’, ‘medium’, and ‘high’ signature groups based on their BLCA gene score ranking (Fig. 3E). When these group labels were mapped back to the UMAP, cells from high-score tumors clearly concentrated within the epithelial and stromal territories (Fig. 3F). Ratio of Observed to Expected (Ro/e) analysis formalized these shifts, showing significant enrichment of fibroblast, myeloid, and endothelial lineage in high-signature tumors (Fig. 3G).

A detailed subtype-level Ro/e analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1b) confirmed these findings, showing significant enrichment of FAP+ and PDPN+ fibroblasts, as well as inflammatory and MHCII+ macrophages in tumors with high-BLCA gene signature. Further analysis confirmed that tumors with high-BLCA gene signature exhibited a distinct cellular landscape characterized by an increased fibroblast and myeloid presentation, together with altered endothelial composition (Fig. 3H, top-left). Finer-grained analysis of cell subtypes underscored these differences. Tumors with high-BLCA–associated gene signature were specifically enriched for FAP+ and PDPN+ fibroblasts, as well as inflammatory and MHCII+ macrophages. Within the epithelial compartment, KRT13+ cells increased in the high-signature group, while CDH12+ cells were reduced (Fig. 3H). We next investigated the transcriptional programs associated with these compositional shifts. Pathway analysis of immune signatures (Fig. 3I) revealed a clear immune-evasive phenotype in high-signature tumors. Both MHCI and MHCII signatures were significantly downregulated in the high gene signature group compared to the low and medium gene signature groups, suggesting impaired antigen presentation. This was further supported by a decrease in the Th1 signature and a concurrent, significant increase in the Th2 signature, indicating a shift away from cytotoxic T-cell responses. Moreover, TCR signaling was progressively upregulated from the low gene signature to high gene signature group. Signatures related to NK cells and NK HLA genes were also significantly downregulated in the high gene signature and medium gene signature groups, pointing to a reduction in innate immune surveillance (Fig. 3I).

To pinpoint the molecular correlates of this phenotype, we performed unbiased differential expression analysis comparing the high vs. low gene signature groups across cell types (Fig. 3J). In tumors with high-BLCA–associated gene signature, T cells (CD8+ and Treg) upregulated immune checkpoint and exhaustion markers, including LAG3, TNFRSF18, TIGIT, ICOS, and HAVCR2. Concurrently, myeloid cells (e.g., inflammatory macrophages) and fibroblasts overexpressed co-inhibitory ligands such as CD274 (PD-L1), CD276, and PDCD1LG2. Notably, this was consistent with the pathway analysis, showing a broad downregulation of MHC class I (HLA-B, HLA-C) and class II (HLA-DPA1, HLA-DRA, HLA-DPB1) molecules across multiple cell types (Fig. 3J). Collectively, these transcriptional data demonstrate that tumors with high-BLCA gene signature co-opt a fibro-myeloid stroma, accumulate dysfunctional T cells, and establish an immune-evasive microenvironment.

Tumors with high BLCA gene signature exhibit lineage-specific transcriptional reprogramming driving malignant progression

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the pro-fibrotic and immunosuppressive TME observed in tumors with high BLCA gene signature, we performed a transcriptomic comparison using snRNASeq data. The analysis revealed profound and widespread transcriptional reprogramming across endothelial, epithelial, fibroblast, myeloid, and lymphoid lineages in tumors with high BLCA gene signature (Fig. 4A, B).

GSEA revealed two key axes of this reprogramming. Although pathway enrichment is detected across multiple lineages, the biological interpretation arises from the cell-type–specific predominance of these programs. Stromal lineages (fibroblasts and endothelial cells) primarily drive angiogenic and extracellular matrix–remodeling pathways, whereas immune lineages co-activate inflammatory and immunoregulatory programs. Together, these lineage-restricted patterns define a coordinated tumor microenvironment state rather than uniform pathway activation across all cell types. First, stromal and endothelial lineages exhibited strong features of pro-tumoral microenvironment activation (Fig. 4C). Fibroblasts were significantly enriched for pathways related to ‘Extracellular matrix organization’, ‘Angiogenesis’, and ‘Epithelial to mesenchymal transition’. Endothelial cells similarly showed enrichment for ‘Angiogenesis’ and ‘Vasculature development’ pathways. These data confirm that the molecular signature of the high BLCA gene signature state is a fibroblast-dominated, highly reactive, pro-angiogenic stroma, providing physical and signaling support for tumor invasion and metastasis.

In sharp contrast to the stromal cells, immune lineages (myeloid and lymphoid) tumors with high BLCA gene signature displayed strong signatures of immune activation, including ‘Leukocyte activation’, ‘T cell activation’, and ‘Positive regulation of cytokine production’ (Fig. 4C, D).

Crucially, GO enrichment network mapping (Fig. 4E) uncovered the dysfunctional nature of this immune activation. Within the myeloid and lymphoid gene clusters upregulated in the groups with high BLCA gene signature, immune-activating pathways (e.g., ‘Antigen receptor-mediated signaling pathway’) were tightly linked with immunoregulatory and inhibitory pathways (e.g., ‘Immune response-regulating signaling pathway’, ‘Negative regulation of leukocyte activation’). Indeed, we observed significant contributions from multiple key immune checkpoint genes within these networks, including CD274 (PD-L1), HAVCR2 (TIM-3), and LGALS9 (Galectin-9) (Fig. 4E, Myeloid & Lymphocyte maps).

In summary, the transcriptomic analysis in Fig. 4 reveals the malignant molecular features of tumors with high BLCA gene signature: a pro-angiogenic and fibrotic physical microenvironment driven by stromal cells (fibroblasts, endothelial cells) coexists with a dysfunctional and highly suppressed state of ‘pseudo-inflammation’ mediated by immune cells. This dual pathological mechanism collectively constitutes the immune-evasive phenotype and aggressive biological behavior of tumors with high BLCA gene signature.

BLCA gene signature defines a luminal-predominant, proliferation-driven subtype of MIBC with MEK inhibitor vulnerability

Functionally, tumors with high BLCA gene signature activity displayed a distinct transcriptional state characterized by coordinated activation of proliferative and oncogenic programs. Pathway analysis revealed significant upregulation of homologous recombination, cell-cycle, and glycolytic metabolism pathways in the tumors with high BLCA gene signature (Fig. 5A), alongside major oncogenic cascades including MAPK, RAS, WNT, and TGF-β signaling (Fig. 5B). In contrast, tumor-suppressor and checkpoint pathways such as DNA-damage response (DDR), TP53 activation, and senescence were markedly downregulated in the tumors with high BLCA–gene signature (Fig. 5A–B). Together, these data indicate that tumors with high BLCA gene signature adopt a hyperproliferative and metabolically rewired phenotype driven by mitogenic and stromal signaling.

To determine whether this program aligns with established molecular subtypes, we classified our 25-sample snRNASeq cohort into 17 luminal, 3 basal, and 5 neuroendocrine-like (NE-like) tumors. Compositional analysis confirmed this association (Fig. 5C): all tumors with high BLCA gene signature was luminal, whereas all five NE-like tumors and most basal cases were confined to the low BLCA gene signature group. At single-cell resolution, UMAP density and feature plots revealed that luminal tumors contained cells expressing the highest density of BLCA genes, concentrated in epithelial and fibroblast subsets (Fig. 5D–E), with detailed quantification showing the highest scores in luminal epithelial and fibroblast subsets (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Notably, the luminal panel showed two spatially separated regions with high BLCA-associated signature density. Comparison with the original cell-state annotation indicated that these regions corresponded to distinct epithelial-state compartments, suggesting that high BLCA-associated signature activity spans multiple luminal epithelial programs rather than a single homogeneous luminal cell state. The BLCA gene signature score was overwhelmingly highest in luminal tumors (Fig. 5F). This localization underscores the fibro-epithelial axis that typifies luminal MIBC. At the gene level, cells expressing high levels of BLCA genes group was enriched for luminal markers (GATA3, KRT20), whereas the low group was enriched for basal markers (KRT5, KRT14) (Fig. 5G). Consistently, luminal signature scores were maximal, and basal and NE-like scores minimal, in the high gene signature group (Fig. 5H).

We next examined clinical and developmental transcriptional programs (Fig. 5I). The carcinoma in situ (CIS) signature was significantly enriched in low biomarker tumors, consistent with an undifferentiated basal-like state. Conversely, Sonic Hedgehog signaling was lowest in low and highest in high biomarker tumors, suggesting that pathway activation accompanies luminal differentiation and stromal engagement.

Finally, to explore potential therapeutic implications, we correlated biomarker gene expression with drug-response data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) dataset (Fig. 5J). High expression of SDC1, VEGFA, and CXCL8 was negatively correlated (FDR < 0.05) with IC₅₀ values for multiple MEK inhibitors—including trametinib, selumetinib, and RDEA119—indicating increased sensitivity. Conversely, the same genes correlated positively with resistance to select PI3K/AKT inhibitors. A similar pattern was observed in the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) database, which similarly showed significant correlations between biomarkers like SERPINE1, SDC1, and CXCL8 and sensitivity to multiple agents (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Collectively, these findings establish the BLCA gene signature as a hallmark of luminal MIBC, integrating epithelial–stromal activation and oncogenic signaling with a distinct, targetable vulnerability to MEK-pathway inhibition.

Exploratory qPCR-based ML model internally evaluates survival stratification in BLCA

To explore whether the BLCA-associated gene signature could be translated into a qPCR-based risk-stratification framework, we developed exploratory ML models using qPCR-derived expression of the ten biomarker genes in the NAC-treated MIBC cohort.

In the initial apparent train/test model screen, StepCox (forward) + RSF showed the highest survival-model performance, with C-index values of 0.87 and 0.88 in the training and testing cohorts, respectively (Fig. 6A).

Because the cohort was modest in size and multiple algorithms were screened, we performed bootstrap optimism correction as the primary internal validation analysis for the selected StepCox (forward) + RSF model. The apparent full-cohort C-index was 0.865; after 500 bootstrap resamples, the optimism-corrected C-index was 0.695 (95% CI 0.628–0.792), indicating moderate internal discrimination after correction for optimism (Fig. 6B).

The ML-derived risk score stratified patients into low- and high-risk groups with significantly different survival in both the training and testing cohorts, with survival time measured in months from radical cystectomy (Fig. 6C–D). In univariate Cox meta-analysis, a higher risk score was associated with worse survival across cohorts (random-effects model HR = 2.42, 95% CI 1.47–3.97, p < 0.001; Fig. 6E). Together, these findings support the feasibility of qPCR-based exploratory survival stratification but indicate that external validation is required before clinical application.

Discussion

This study integrates bulk and single-cell transcriptomics with machine-learning analysis to define the biological and clinical significance of a BLCA gene signature in MIBC. Across independent datasets, the signature consistently marked a luminal-predominant, hyperproliferative tumor state characterized by fibroblast–myeloid expansion, epithelial–stromal crosstalk, and dysfunctional immune activation. These convergent findings outline a coherent tumor ecosystem associated with aggressive disease behavior and actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities.

In TCGA-BLCA, the signature was tumor-enriched, increased with pathological stage, and aligned with CNV-driven expression changes in several components. High-signature tumors activated EMT, cell-cycle, and MAPK/PI3K pathways while suppressing DNA-damage programs [20]. The positive association between the signature and apoptosis reflects stress-induced signaling commonly seen in high-grade cancers. Immune correlates showed enrichment of macrophages, Th17/Th2 cells, cytotoxic T cells, and exhausted phenotypes, together suggesting a microenvironment that is inflamed but functionally ineffective [21, 22]. Single-cell profiling provided cellular granularity to these features. Fibroblasts exhibited the highest signature activity, followed by inflammatory macrophages, endothelial cells, and KRT13+ epithelial cells. High-signature tumors displayed marked expansion of FAP+ /PDPN+ fibroblasts and MHCII+ macrophages, together with altered endothelial presentation and changes in immune-lineage composition. These tumors clustered within fibroblast-rich territories, accompanied by reduced MHC I/II, Th1, NK, and TCR signaling. Together, these patterns define a stromal-dominated, pseudo-inflamed niche in which immune activation is tightly coupled to immunoregulatory circuits and checkpoint expression, resulting in ineffective antitumor immunity [22, 23].

Lineage-specific transcriptional analysis reinforced this framework. Fibroblasts and endothelial cells upregulated angiogenic and matrix-remodeling programs, whereas epithelial cells exhibited enhanced migratory and EMT-related pathways. Myeloid and lymphoid cells co-activated leukocyte-activation and immunoregulatory pathways, consistent with an activated yet suppressed immune state. These features explain the stromal remodeling, epithelial plasticity, and immune dysfunction observed in high-signature tumors. The inverse association between BLCA-associated signature activity and AR signaling should be interpreted in a context-dependent manner. Although AR signaling has been linked to bladder cancer proliferation in some settings, high-signature tumors in our analysis were characterized by luminal features, stromal remodeling, EMT-related programs, and activation of RTK, MAPK, and PI3K/AKT signaling. Thus, high BLCA-associated signature activity may define a luminal but stromal- and growth-factor-driven tumor state rather than a strictly AR-dependent proliferative phenotype.

Subtype analyses further demonstrated that the signature is tightly coupled to luminal MIBC [3, 24]. High-signature tumors were exclusively luminal and displayed elevated luminal marker expression, whereas basal and NE-like tumors were confined to the low-signature group. High-signature tumors also exhibited increased Sonic Hedgehog signaling, while low-signature tumors showed stronger CIS-associated programs, consistent with their basal origin. Drug-response correlations revealed that expression of markers such as SDC1, VEGFA, and CXCL8 predicted increased sensitivity to multiple MEK inhibitors, implicating MAPK-pathway dependence as a potential therapeutic vulnerability [4, 25, 26].

Finally, the qPCR-based machine-learning analysis should be interpreted as exploratory. Although the selected StepCox (forward) + RSF model showed high apparent C-index values in the initial train/test split, bootstrap optimism correction reduced the estimated C-index to 0.695, indicating that the original performance estimate was optimistic. This corrected estimate supports moderate internal discrimination but does not establish clinical readiness. Thus, the qPCR-plus-ML framework provides a candidate approach for future biomarker-driven survival stratification, but it is not yet clinically deployable and requires validation in larger independent cohorts.

This study has several limitations inherent to its design. First, while we leveraged snRNASeq to gain high-resolution mechanistic insights into the fibroblast-driven TME, the observed cellular states and signaling axes are based on correlative transcriptomics and require rigorous functional validation (both in vitro and in vivo) to establish causality. Second, the qPCR-based ML model was internally evaluated in a retrospective NAC-treated cohort and lacks independent external validation. Although bootstrap optimism correction reduced overestimation of survival-model performance, the corrected estimate remains an internal validation result. Detailed NAC regimen, clinical T/N stage, pathological N stage, and some clinicopathological covariates were not uniformly available, limiting multivariable clinical adjustment. Larger independent NAC-treated MIBC cohorts are required before clinical implementation.

Conclusion

The BLCA gene signature captures a luminal, fibroblast–myeloid–dominated tumor state characterized by proliferative and immune-dysregulated biology. Expression-based signature activity was associated with adverse outcomes in TCGA-BLCA and with candidate therapeutic vulnerabilities. qPCR-based machine-learning modeling provides an exploratory framework for survival, but larger independent validation cohorts are required before clinical implementation.
