Soil Microbial Diversity in Páramos Wetland of the Colombian Andes Reveals Novel and Unique Features Within a Global Wetland Database
Authors: Estelle Couradeau, Joan Vanegas, Daniela Betancurt-Anzola, Sarah Glass, Kara Eckert, Emily K Bechtold, Jared B Ellenbogen, Luisa Robles Zaragoza, Kelly Wrighton, Johanna Santamaria Vanegas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTropical wetlands are the largest natural source of methane on Earth, yet they remain the least studied, particularly high-altitude wetlands like those in the Páramo of Chingaza, Colombia. These ecosystems are crucial for water provisioning, carbon sequestration, and biodiversity conservation but are threatened by rapid climate change. While the páramo biome supports thousands of endemic plant species and plays a vital role in balancing carbon inputs and greenhouse gas outputs (CO₂ and CH₄), its soil microbial diversity and functional roles in soil processes are largely unexplored. To fill this knowledge gap, we conducted amplicon sequencing of the ITS, 16S rRNA, and 18S rRNA genes to examine microbial diversity across three distinct ecosites at Laguna Seca, Chingaza, characterized by different macrotopographies, water-table levels, and vegetation assemblages. Our findings revealed significant variations in microbial community structure, with the peatland ecosite showing the highest diversity across all amplicons. Comparative analysis with global wetland datasets indicated that microbial communities at Laguna Seca share similarities with subarctic Stordalen Mire fen and other peat-forming wetlands. Notably, our targeted assessment identified a diversity of potential methanogens and methanotrophs exclusively within the peatland ecosite, at low but comparable abundance to other wetlands. This suggests that methane cycling in the other ecosites of this wetland may either be less prominent than expected or involve organisms not previously associated with known methane processes. These findings establish a baseline for understanding microbial diversity in tropical high-montane wetlands and underscore the unique ecological significance of páramo peatlands amid climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02738-x.Keywords: Páramo, Soil, Microbiome, Wetland

Introduction

Peatlands cover just 3% of Earth’s land area yet store approximately 21% of global soil organic carbon (~644 Gt), making them critical carbon sinks [29, 74]. Tropical peatlands, making up 12.7% of global peatlands, are predicted to be disproportionately affected by climate change, as shifting rainfall patterns and rising temperatures could transform wetland landscapes and the peatlands that form in their lowpoint, eventually converting these carbon sinks into carbon sources and exacerbating greenhouse gas emissions [29]. It is estimated that their degradation will lead to an additional 40-58 t CO2 eq ha-1 yr-1 largely through the release of methane [7] – an order of magnitude more than high-latitude peatlands. Despite their disproportionate contribution to future greenhouse emissions, they remain the least studied wetland types on Earth [56].

Wetlands, encompassing peatlands, marshes, swamps, bogs, and fens, store more carbon per unit area than any other ecosystem [52, 72]. The constant presence or excess of water in wetlands creates anoxic conditions that inhibit microbial decomposition of organic matter through biochemical inhibition of enzymes directly and through the amplification of phenol accumulation that inhibits biodegradation [20, 67]. Wetland microorganisms are widely recognized as essential to the net balance of carbon storage and release [72] as they directly consume and produce atmospheric gases (such as carbon dioxide and methane) [21, 69] and play key roles in processes that affect soil organic matter: formation, mineralization, and stabilization [26, 37, 70].

Amongst tropical wetlands, páramos ecosystems are particularly vulnerable to degradation and are projected to shrink by 39–52% within 50 years under current climate-change scenarios [14]. Páramos are high-altitude Andean tundra ecosystems nested between 2800 and 4700 m — known as “the fastest-evolving and coolest biodiversity hotspot” [31] where an estimated 60% of vascular plant species are endemic [15]. These high altitude island-like ecosystems are vital as the primary headwaters for tropical lowlands and urban centers in the Andean and Amazon regions [14, 22, 28]. Additionally, the combination of low temperatures, high humidity, frequent flooding conditions and high water tables, results in low and uneven weathering of the parent material along the soil profile, as well as slow microbial metabolic activity and accumulation of organic matter in páramo peatlands [6, 23]. While the above ground biodiversity of Páramos has been extensively studied and documented [48], the soil microbial diversity within these unique landscapes remains largely understudied.

Because atmosphere-plant-soil feedbacks are unique in páramo ecosystems, we aim to explore the microbial diversity in a representative páramo landscape and compare it to wetlands and peatlands characterized at higher latitudes. Assessing microbial community composition along the landscape is a critical first step toward establishing a baseline for the microbial diversity of páramo soils and understanding whether these wetlands function and respond to disturbances, including climate change, in ways similar to other wetland ecosystems. This is vital for making predictions about the ultimate fate of carbon stored in páramo soil. Here, we characterized the soils and microbial (Bacteria, Archaea, Fungi, and Protists) and plant diversity across three different ecosites at a seasonal lagoon, Laguna Seca, located within the Páramo Chingaza (Colombia) [42]. These ecosites, which we termed “convex”, “concave”, and “peatland”, are characterized by variations in water table levels as well as by distinct plant assemblages. Convex sites were situated higher on the landscape and therefore experience greater fluctuation in water level, while concave sites represent an intermediate position on the landscape between the convex sites and the peatland ecosite, which was the lowest, with the water table near or above the soil surface. Additionally, we examined the relative abundance of taxa potentially associated with methane cycling to provide a qualitative indication of their presence in these soils. Finally, we compared the bacterial and archaeal diversity at this site with the global peatland database MUCC 2.0 [5], to assess similarity to that of other highly organic (peatforming) wetland soils. We predicted that microbial composition in páramo soil would exhibit a high degree of diversity as well as endemism compared to other wetlands, mirroring patterns of found in páramo plant communities. Additionally, we predicted that patterns of microbially community assembly would be strongly associated with ecosite, with the peatland ecosite having a greater proportion of organisms responsible for methane cycling.

Materials and Methods

Sampling site and Experimental Design

This work was carried out under the auspices of memoranda 20,212,000,003,583 and 20,212,000,004,343 from Parques Naturales Nacionales de Colombia and under the Scientific Research in Biodiversity Permit No. 00213, issued on 28 January 2021 by the National Environmental Licensing Authority (ANLA) in Colombia. The study site is located within Páramo Chingaza (Colombia) (Fig. 1A). The climate is cold and humid, with an average annual temperature ranging from 6.7 to 7.9 °C. Average maximum temperatures range from 12 to 14 °C, while average minimum temperatures can reach − 2 °C, with significant variations between day and night. The average annual rainfall is 2,700 mm, with relative humidity ranging between 85% and 90%. The rainy season begins in April, peaks in July, and gradually becomes less intense from November onwards.

The páramo terrain in this study consisted of moraine landforms with undulating topography where alternating ridges (convex or CV) and depressions (concave or CC) were interspersed with lower-lying water-logged or inundated peatlands (Supplementary Material 1). Six sampling sites were located to the west of Laguna Seca and distributed over an area of approximately 29 hectares (Table 1; Fig. 1B and C). Sampling pits (50 cm x 50 cm) were dug at three convex sites (CV1, CV2, CV3) (Fig. 1E) and three on concave sites (CC4, CC5, CC6) (Fig. 1F). Representative samples of the A and B horizons (when present) were collected during pit excavation. For undisturbed samples, a soil block (20 × 20 × 15 cm) was extracted 5 cm below the start of the horizon for volumetric measurements, moisture retention, and particle size analysis. The block was carefully carved to minimize disturbance, wrapped in cling film, and placed in a rigid container to preserve structure. In the laboratory, Kopecky rings were used to subsample this block for physical property determination. Bulk soil samples were collected from the same depth by cutting a V-shaped notch and removing a vertical slice (10 × 10 cm), which was stored in sealed plastic bags for analysis. All pits were sampled using the same procedure and dimensions to ensure comparable sample sizes. Physicochemical variables and their methodologies, are found in Supplementary Material 4. After exposing the peat profile, the depth and characteristics of each horizon (color, texture, structure) were recorded. Microbial samples were collected directly from the pit wall within identified horizons (A and B). To avoid contamination from upper layers, a 5 cm diameter area was cleaned using a sterile knife. A sterile soil core cylinder (2.5 cm diameter × 10 cm length) was then inserted perpendicularly into the cleaned spot to extract the sample. Three replicates were taken from separate positions within the same horizon, spaced at least 10 cm apart. Transition zones between horizons were excluded. Samples were placed in sterile bags, stored at 4 °C during transport, and then kept at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. The seventh and final sampling site was located in a peatland area near Laguna Seca in the bajo region of the postglacial U-shaped valley (Fig. 1G). Using an auger, three seven centimeters samples of an intermediate decomposing organic matter layer were collected from below the floating aquatic plants in a submerged zone of the peatland (see Fig. 1J). These three samples were collected 3 m apart from each other to ensure sampling independence. All soil replicates collected from both horizons were processed for analysis separately, as were all peat replicate samples. Soil and peat samples were collected on January 22 and 23, 2022.

Plant Survey

A single, 100ft transect (30.48 m) was established at each of two ecosites – convex (site CV1) and concave (site CC5) (Fig. 1D, E and F, Supplementary Material 5). Within the site, each transect began near the collection sites for Licor gas measurements, ran parallel to any slopes, and did not represent any significant elevation gain to encompass similar soil moisture conditions and vegetative micro-communities. CC1 transect was established on June 18, 2024 with an azimuth of 242° SW and ran on top of a small ridge. CV5 transect was established on June 19, 2024 with an azimuth of 208° SW and consisted of wetter, bog-like conditions. Line-point intercept (LPI) method was used to measure vegetative cover and we examined 100 points of vegetative readings evenly spaced across each transect. At each point on the transect, a perpendicular line was visualized, and plant species were recorded from the top layers to the lowest layers. Vascular plants were identified to the species level and bryophytes were estimated at the genus level. Relative abundance barplots were made in R to compare community composition between the two microsites. Raw survey data can be found in Supplementary Material 5.

Soil Biogeochemistry

Physicochemical parameters were determined from three soil replicates taken from horizons A and B (when present) at each sampling site (Supplementary Material 4). Physical characteristics included bulk density, real density, total porosity, texture, volumetric water content (VWCf) measured in the field, and volumetric water content (VWC) at field capacity, wilting point, and saturation. The chemical characteristics included pH, electrical conductivity (EC), cation exchange capacity (CEC), effective cation exchange capacity (CICe), organic carbon (OC), organic matter (OM), total nitrogen (N), total phosphorus (P), ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺), nitrate nitrogen (NO₃⁻), mineral nitrogen (N-min), available phosphorus (Pav), exchangeable bases (K, Ca, Mg, Na) and interchangeable acidity (Al). Only one replicate sample was processed for chemical analysis in the peatland. Physical soil analyses were carried out by the soil laboratory at the Center for Tropical Agriculture in Palmira, Colombia, and chemical analyses were performed by the Bio Systems Center at the University of Jorge Tadeo Lozano in Bogotá, Colombia. The methodology used for these measurements is indicated in Supplementary Material 4. Statistical tests (t test followed by multiple testing adjustment using the False Discovery Rate method) were performed to compared Convex sites (CV1, CV2, CV3) to Concave sites (CC4, CC5, CC6) and showed no significant differences, see Supplementary Material 4. Note that the biogeochemistry data from the peatland ecosite were not replicated, preventing us from testing for differences from the other ecosites.

DNA Extraction and Sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from 3 g of each soil replicate collected from the A and B horizons as well as from the 3 g of each peat replicate sample using the DNA Elution set from the RNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer’s instructions. See Supplementary Material 6 for a complete list of samples and associated extraction metadata. DNA isolations from soil and organic matter samples were repeated with the DNA Ultra Clean DNA kit from (MoBio Laboratories Inc.). Due to the high concentration of organic matter in the samples, the instructions of this kit were modified to optimize DNA clean-up and the amount of DNA isolated. 500 µL of phenol was added to remove the excess organic matter present in the samples previously treated with the protein precipitation reagent. After separating the phenol/aqueous phases by centrifugation, the aqueous phase was mixed with the kit´s DNA-binding salt solution. The following steps were as indicated in the instructions.

The 16S rRNA gene V4 variable region PCR primers 515F GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA [40]/ 806R GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT [3], the ITS1f CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA / ITS2 GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC [10], and the 18S rRNA gene Euk1391f GTACACACCGCCCGTC / EukBr TGATCCTTCTGCAGGTTCACCTAC [63] primers were used in a PCR using the HotStarTaq Plus Master Mix Kit (Qiagen, USA) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 53 °C for 40 s and 72 °C for 1 min, after which a final elongation step at 72 °C for 10 min was performed. Samples were multiplexed using unique dual indices and are pooled together (e.g., 100 samples) in equal proportions based on their molecular weight and DNA concentrations. Pooled samples are purified using calibrated Ampure XP beads. Then the pooled and purified PCR product is used to prepare an Illumina DNA library. Sequencing was performed at MR DNA (www.mrdnalab.com, Shallowater, TX, USA) on a MiSeq (Illumina) following the manufacturer’s guideline and aiming at a minimum of 20k paired end reads (250 bp x 250 bp) per sample.

Data Analysis

The 16S rRNA gene, ITS, and 18 S rRNA gene were processed independently. Raw demultiplexed unpaired reads were checked using FastQC to assess the quality of the forward and reverse reads and determine the trimming length of the sequences per dataset. Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME 2 version 2024.10) pipeline [11] was used to process the reads. After being imported into Qiime2 the reads were trimmed, denoised and chimera were removed using DADA2 [13]. The parameters used for the DADA2 pipeline for each dataset can be found in the associated GitHub repository (https://github.com/couradeaulab/Couradeau_Paramos_2025). DADA2 denoising statistics for all data are presented in Supplementary Material 6. The classify-sklearn qiime feature classifier was further used to assign reads taxonomy. The Greengene 2 2024.9 [9, 32] and the GTDB r220 [41, 51] database were used to classify the 16 S rRNA gene representative sequences. The Unite vre10 99% [1] was used to classify ITS representative sequences. The Silva 138 [46] 99% was used to classify 18 S rRNA gene representative sequences. The feature table (ASV tables), representative sequences and taxonomy were imported into a Phyloseq object using the qiime2R package. The three ASV tables were filtered to remove every ASV with less than 5 reads total and/or present in less than 2 samples. The 16S rRNA gene was further filtered to remove sequences from Chloroplast (class) and Mitochondria (Family). The 18S rRNA gene was further filtered to remove sequences from multicellular organisms and bacteria (using the exclusion: terms vertebrata, bacteria, unassigned, embryophyta, annelida, arthropoda, nematozoa, gastrotricha, platyhelminthes, tardigrada, chlorophyta). In general, the quality of the 18S rRNA gene dataset was lower and only a small fraction of the reads were conserved through the filtering process (see Supplementary Material 6).

The barplots at the phylum level were made in R and the vegan package was used to generate diversity metrics R (R Core Team [47]) including the Observed_Species and Shannon (Alphadiversity) and Bray Curtis dissimilarity metric (Betadiversity). To test for differences in alpha diversity a Shapiro test (normality of residuals) followed by a Levene test (homogeneity of variance) were performed on each dataset. The Observed_Species dataset (one per amplicon type) met these requirements and were further analysed using an ANOVA that were significant and followed by a post-hoc pairwise comparison using Tukey HSD. The Shannon datasets failed the Levene test and were further analysed by a Welch Anova that does not assume homogeneity of variance. Pairwise posthoc comparison following the significant Welch Anova were performed using the emmeans function and a bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. The differences in betadiversity were tested using a PERMANOVA (Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance) on the Bray-Curtis diagonale distance matrix. We used the adonis2 function in R to test for differences per Ecosite with 999 permutations.

To contextualize microbial community profiles in the páramos relative to other wetlands globally, we compared 16 S rRNA gene composition to data from the Multiomics for Understanding Climate Change (MUCC) v2.0.0 database [5]. MUCC is a publicly available resource containing metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, and amplicon data from a wide range of freshwater wetlands. For this comparison, we selected 16S rRNA gene amplicon data from various wetland types, including marshes (Prairie Potholes Region Permanent Wetlands North Dakota (P7 & P8), AmeriFlux site ID US-OWC Ohio (OWC), AmeriFlux site ID US-LA2 Louisiana (LA2), AmeriFlux site ID US-TWI California (TWI), bogs ( Spruce and Peatland Responses Under Changing Environments Minnesota (SPRUCE) and Stordalen Mire Bog Sweden (STM-bog), a swamp (Jean Lafitte National Historical Park and Preserve Louisiana (JLA), and a fen (Stordalen Mire-Fen Sweden (STM-fen). TWI samples were excluded due to the use of different sequencing primers. All retained samples were originally amplified with the Earth Microbiome Project primers 515 F/806R (Parada, Needham and Fuhrman 2016b; [62]) and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Sequences were processed in QIIME2 using the DADA2 plugin [11, 13], following a standardized pipeline. Taxonomic assignment was performed against the SILVA v138 database. Most datasets, including the páramo samples, were sequenced with 250 bp paired-end reads, which were trimmed to a uniform length and merged at the amplicon sequence variant (ASV) level. The STM dataset, generated with 150 bp paired-end reads, was processed separately and merged with the rest of the dataset at the genus level to account for differences in read length. After filtering out chloroplast and mitochondria sequences, the combined ASV table was rarefied at 12,000 reads per sample to compute the Bray-Curtis pairwise distance that was further ordinated using NMDS in R using the packages phyloseq [33], tidyverse [68], and vegan [39].The differences in beta diversity between the 2 sample groups (Sweden, Minnesota, Colombia (SMC-bog/fen) and Louisiana, Ohio, North Dakota (LON-marsh/swamp) were tested using a PERMANOVA (Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance) on the Bray-Curtis diagonale distance matrix using the adonis2 function in R with 999 permutations.

LefSe (Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size) [54] was used to determine which groups at the phylum levels were differently abundant between the SMC-bog/fen and LON-marsh/swamp. The data set was filtered to select 33 samples per geographic location (those with the highest number of reads) and 3 locations per group and further rarefied to the lowest sample read count (10302) prior to running LefSe. Only phyla with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and a LDA score > 2 were retained in the plot.

Targeted Analysis of Potential Methanotrophs and Methanogens

First, methanogenic and methanotrophic taxa were identified via manual inspection of the list of unique GTDB-produced taxonomy of the ASVs (see Supplementary Data 5 from the MUCC article for a complete list with references [5]. This taxa list was used to subset the data for methane-cycling community analysis in R.

Next, 16S data were linked to MAGs in the MUCC v2.0.0 database to allow more physiologically-informed conclusions from the ASV data. Briefly, a full length Escherichia coli 16 S gene sequence was BLASTed against the MUCC v2.0.0 database to produce a list of 16 S sequences present in the database MAGs. Then, a BLAST database was generated of the 16 S gene sequences from this study, and the MUCC 16 S sequences were BLASTed against this database, to compare similarity between the páramo ASVs and the MUCC MAGs. The BLAST results were filtered to only retain BLAST with > 97% sequence over > 250 bp, and dereplicated to only retain the best hit per ASV. The final dataset contained 729 unique MAGs with a positive BLAST hit to the current páramo dataset.

Results and Discussion

The Plant Assemblages Define Three Distinct Ecosites in the Laguna Seca Landscape

This study focuses on the Páramo Chingaza, a wetland landscape located in Chingaza National Natural Park (CNNP) in Colombia’s eastern Andes (Parque Natural Nacional Chingaza [42]). The area, shaped by Pleistocene glacial activity [35], experiences high humidity and low temperatures, leading to limited weathering and microbial activity, resulting in soils with a developed A horizon, an absent or incipient B horizon, and a diverse C horizon [24, 34, 35, 45, 57]. Unlike many other páramos, the bedrock at the Laguna Seca field site is of post-glacial origin rather than volcanic [64].

The Laguna Seca site, located within the high-altitude Chuza River Basin, is characterized by mountainous terrain and lateral moraine landforms [65] (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Material 1). The landscape features three distinct ecosites defined by their topographical positions and associated plant assemblages: convex slopes, concave depressions, and peatlands (Supplementary Material 1).

Soil analyses of the A and B (when present) horizons at the convex and concave sites revealed no significant differences in biogeochemistry (Supplementary Data 1). The soils are shallow, acidic (pH = 4.36 ± 0.12), with high exchangeable acidity and low base saturation (Table 1). Convex and concave sites exhibited slightly evolved Inceptisol with claystones and siltstones as the predominant parent materials (Vargas et al. 2003). The soil texture was similar in both convex and concave sites, with a loamy clay texture predominant in the A horizon and a silty clay or silty sand texture predominant in the B horizon. The soils were shallow and ranged from humid to very humid. The color of the A horizon was black with an organic matter content of 50% and 58% at the convex and concave sites, respectively. The B horizons were dark grey in color with an organic matter content of 17% at both types of sites. On average, the soils exhibited a bulk density of 0.46 g cm⁻³. Saturated volumetric moisture content (%VWC) exceeded 87% for both convex and concave sites. The field capacity and the permanent wilting point decreased from 64.4% to 60.2% and 59% to 55%, respectively from convex to concave sites. In contrast, the peatland soils, composed of semi-decomposed organic matter, exhibited higher %VWC (96%), higher pH (5.6), greater base saturation, higher cation exchange capacity, and elevated levels of organic carbon and nitrogen compared to convex and concave soils (Supplementary Material 4). At the time of sampling, water table depth differed between ecosites. Excavated pits at concave sites flooded with surface water at approximately 25–50 cm depth, whereas convex sites remained dry at the maximum pit depth (50 cm), indicating a deeper water table. Despite the shallow water level at concave sites, A horizons showed no evidence of gleying, suggesting these soils experience periodic saturation rather than permanent inundation.

The main feature differentiating the ecosites is the vegetation assemblage as revealed by the plant survey results (Fig. 1D). The convex slopes host Cortaderia columbiana (Poaceae), Espeletia grandiflora (Asteraceae), and abundant Calamagrostis effusa (Poaceae) (Fig. 1E and H) while concave slopes are dominated by Chusquea tessellata (Poaceae), Herbertus sp. (Marchantiophyta) (Fig. 1F and I). The peatland features ~ 5–7 cm of organic matter in intermediate decomposition (Fig. 1G and J). While no significant differences in soil biogeochemistry were observed between convex and concave sites, the differences in plant communities could be driven by abiotic factors such as microtopographic variation or by biotic factors, including plant reproductive biology, dispersal capacity, and thermal niches [55].

Páramos are known for their exceptional plant biodiversity and high levels of plant endemism [15]. These plants occupy some of the smallest thermal niches among tropical Andean systems [16], a phenomenon attributed to the unique bioclimatic patterns and high fragmentation of páramo ecosystems, which act as “sky islands” [44]. Previous studies have documented vegetation shifts with elevation at broader spatial scales in páramos [8, 30, 58]. However, our results indicate that vegetation assemblages also vary at smaller spatial scales within the Laguna Seca landscape. This fine-scale variation is ecologically significant, as páramo plants indirectly shape soil microenvironments by influencing local soil temperature and moisture [36] and directly control the quality of organic matter accessible to microbes through plant litter production and root exudation [72].

The differences in vegetation assemblages at Laguna Seca are closely tied to their positions in the landscape (Supplementary Material 1). Convex slopes likely experience greater fluctuations in the water table due to their higher position in the landscape, along with increased solar radiation exposure while concave depressions likely retain more moisture and experience different microclimatic conditions. These abiotic differences drive the selection of plant species, as plants exhibit distinct thermal niches, traits, and life strategies that influence their distribution. The resulting plant assemblages, in turn, shape the soil microenvironment by altering temperature, moisture, and organic matter inputs. This is especially important in peatlands and wetlands, where organic matter quality controls soil pH, a key driver of microbial community structure and organic matter turnover rates.

These distinct vegetation assemblages and their associated soil characteristics highlight the intricate feedbacks between plants, soils, and landscape position in the Laguna Seca ecosystem. By shaping the quality and availability of organic matter, as well as local soil pH and moisture, these plant-soil interactions create microenvironments that likely influence the structure and function of the microbial communities. To better understand these dynamics, we next explored the soil microbial diversity across the three ecosites, focusing on the bacterial and fungal communities that drive key biogeochemical processes within this high-altitude páramo landscape.

Ecosites Assemble Different Microbial Communities, with the Peatland Being more Diverse

Microbial diversity across the three ecosites at the Laguna Seca Páramo site was assessed using 16 S rRNA gene, ITS, and 18 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (Fig. 2). Consistent trends emerged across all sequencing datasets, revealing distinct community structures for each ecosite. Notably, the peatland exhibited systematically higher alpha diversity, as measured by observed species counts and Shannon diversity indices (Fig. 2).

The bacterial and archaeal diversity (16 S rRNA gene) was dominated by members of Acidobacteriales, Pseudomonadota, and Chloroflexota (Fig. 2A). This composition aligns with previous findings from Páramo soils at Las Dominguez (3800 m.a.s.l.) and Páramo del Duende (3200 m.a.s.l.) [49, 66]. In addition to the higher diversity observed in the peatland (Fig. 2B), 16 S rRNA gene analyses revealed notable differences between the concave (CC) and convex (CV) sites (Fig. 2B/2 C). Concave sites exhibited higher alpha diversity compared to convex sites, and the ecosites were clearly separated on beta diversity plots, with non-overlapping ellipses. The genus Acidoferrum (Acidobacteriales) was the most abundant taxon in both the concave (8.3% ± 1.8) and convex sites (8.5% ± 2.6), contrasting with previous studies at other Páramo sites, where Acidibacter and Acinetobacter were the dominant genera of the Acidobacteriales [49, 66]. Bacterial diversity in the convex sites was further characterized by a high abundance of Ktedonobacteria (Chloroflexota), particularly in CV2, where they accounted for 37% ± 3.4 of the community. This class is noteworthy for its diverse carbohydrate-active enzymes, which suggests a high cellulolytic potential and a key role in carbon cycling [75]. We suggest that their genome features in the Chingaza Páramo and level of activity should be targeted by future research. Interestingly, the two most dominant ASVs in the peatland were assigned to the genus Bradyrhizobium, showing 100% sequence similarity to nitrogen-fixing symbionts associated with legumes (Supplementary Material 2). The abundance of this genus was also reported in the Páramo del Duende site [49], which could suggest an important role of these organisms in biological nitrogen fixation in páramo ecosystems if confirmed through genomic and functional analyses. Understanding the role of soil microorganisms in the nitrogen cycle is of the utmost importance in peatlands because plant litter tends to be of high C: N ratio and the source of nitrogen fueling organic matter decomposition remains uncertain. Biological nitrogen fixation could play a larger role than N deposition in northern peatland but this has never been studied in tropical peatlands [73]. Beyond nitrogen cycling, Bradyrhizobium species are also known to degrade lignin-derived aromatic compounds (e.g., phenolics) [59], which are abundant in peat-forming vegetation. This metabolic capability could make them key players in the breakdown of recalcitrant organic matter and, consequently, in carbon cycling within these ecosystems.

Fungal diversity, as assessed by ITS sequencing (Fig. 2D), appeared one order of magnitude lower than bacterial/archeal diversity based on 16 S rRNA gene data (Fig. 2E). However, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously, as ITS and 16 S rRNA gene provide different taxonomic resolution and suffer different amplification biases. The alpha diversity in the peatland was higher than that of CC and CV sites and marked by the abundance of the Rozellomycota at this ecosite as compared to the others (Supplementary Material 2). Rozellomycota have been reported in other Colombian forest soils and páramo soils [66], however in lower abundance. Rozellomycota are a sister group to other fungi, characterized by the absence of a chitinous cell wall. While their biology remains poorly understood, they exhibit remarkable diversity. The group that we recovered in the Laguna Seca peatland is dominated by 2 ASVs that both belong to the clade GS11 (Supplementary Material 2). This clade is distributed worldwide and is especially abundant in tropical lowland but has not been identified in high-altitude tundra ecosystems [61]. The CC and CV sites are more similar to each others and dominated by Ascomycota and Basidiomycota similarly to previous studies [49, 66]. Interestingly, the Enthorrizhomycota are more abundant in the CV than in the other sites indicating a possible importance of plant-fungi interactions at this ecosite [4].

The 18S rRNA gene followed the same trend as the ITS, with some fungi being found overlapping in both dataset. The peatland had a higher alpha diversity (Fig. 2H) than the other ecosite as well as a clearly distinct community as shown on the beta diversity ordination (Fig. 2I) plot while the CV and CC sites were not distinct. While the 18S rRNA gene dataset is sparser due to its focus on protists, it is notable that 51% of the 945 ASVs lacked taxonomic assignment at the phylum level. While this makes the interpretation of this data difficult it also highlights the important potential for discovery of new protist lineages in this understudied ecosystem. Beyond fungi, a diverse array of protists was retrieved including Ciliophora, Cercozoa, Euglenozoa and marine stramenopiles MAST-12 also known to colonize soil and freshwater [38].

Laguna Seca Páramo Ecosites Holds low Abundance but Diverse Methanogens and Methanotrophs with peak Abundance in the Peatland

Methane metabolism is a key process mediated by soil microorganisms in wetland that will impact the fate of the global carbon cycle under the rapidly changing climate conditions and other anthropogenic disturbance [50]. For this reason, we conducted a taxonomy based derived analysis of the potential for the Laguna Seca páramo soil microbiome to contribute to methanotrophy or methanogenesis. This analysis reveals a diverse assemblage of both methanogenic and methanotrophic microorganisms, although their relative abundance across all samples is low while consistent with previous reports [27]. This indicates that despite their limited abundance, these microbial groups could play important roles in carbon cycling, and their spatial distribution appears to be influenced by the local topography.

Methanogenic archaea are almost exclusively concentrated in the peatland environment, with their abundance markedly reduced in samples from both concave and convex ecosites (Fig. 3A). The most prominent methanogenic microorganisms belong to the genera Methanoregula and Methanocella A (Fig. 3A), both of which are known for their roles in hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis under anaerobic conditions (Sakai et al., 2011, [2, 18, 71]).

The heatmaps display the presence/absence data at the genus level and the histograms show their average relative abundance in the three ecosites. (B) NMDS ordination of the betadiversity Bray Curtis distance metric combining 16S rRNA gene dataset from páramo (this study) and the MUCC 2.0 database allowing the comparison with dataset collected from other wetland ecosites. Only phyla with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and a LDA score > 2 were retained in the plot

Methanotrophic bacteria, in contrast, exhibit greater taxonomic diversity and are generally more abundant than methanogens across the ecosites. Like the methanogens, methanotrophs were at highest relative abundance in the peatland. However, their presence is not limited to this environment. Notably, convex areas harbor a significant population of Methylocella, a facultative methanotroph capable of oxidizing both methane and multi-carbon substrates [17] (Fig. 3A).

This distribution pattern suggests that topographic variation, likely through its effects on moisture, organic matter availability, and oxygen gradients, shapes the ecological niches available for methane-cycling microorganisms in the páramo landscape. Their detection in concave and convex zones, environments with more variable moisture and oxygenation suggests a capacity to exploit transient methane availability and adapt to fluctuating environmental gradients. Collectively, these findings improve our predictive understanding of methane-cycling microbes in high-altitude Andean peatlands, an ecosystem that remains largely ignored from global methane models.

To place these findings in a broader ecological and biogeochemical context, we next compared the the bacterial and archaeal diversity of the Laguna Seca ecosites with other peatlands around the world, exploring how tropical high-altitude systems differ from temperate and boreal peatlands in microbial composition and function.

Laguna Seca Páramo Microbiome is Distinct from Other Wetlands

To contextualize the páramo dataset, we compared it with the published MUCC 2.0 database [5] that comprises a collection of microbiome data from diverse wetlands including marsh, swamp, and peatlands. We first looked for similarities between our dataset and the MAGs collection from MUCC 2.0. Among the 729 dereplicated MAGs from the MUCC 2.0 database that had a BLAST hit in the páramo dataset (Supplementary Material 3), the dominant taxa included Desulfobacterota, Verrucomicrobiota, Pseudomonadota, and Acidobacteriota. These groups are widely recognized for their roles in biogeochemical processes such as carbon cycling, methane metabolism, and sulfate reduction, which are critical in wetland ecosystems [19, 25]. However, while the presence of these MAG hits highlights potential functional similarities between the páramo and other wetlands, further studies using MAG-resolved metagenomics will be necessary to confirm specific metabolic pathways and ecological roles. This additional functional resolution is essential to robustly draw parallels between the microbial communities of the páramo and those of other organic-rich wetlands.

To directly compare our datasets, we reanalyzed the MUCC 16 S rRNA gene amplicon along with the páramo dataset. When ordinating the beta diversity of the combined ASV table using the Bray-Curtis distance metric, we found that the sample’s original location explained 44% of the variance (PERMANOVA p < 0.001) and the samples separated based on this parameter, including the páramo samples (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, the first axis of the NMDS separated the samples in two groups (Sweden, Minnesota, Colombia (SMC-bog/fen) and Louisiana, Ohio, North Dakota (LON-marsh/swamp), the páramo samples grouped with other peatland type wetlands (permafrost peatland and peat forming fen and bog), while the less organic carbon accumulating wetlands separate away, including the swamp and marsh samples (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.23 p < 0.001). Swamps and marshes generally exhibit higher pH levels compared to bogs and fens, primarily due to their minerotrophic water sources and higher nutrient availability [12]. This pH difference could be an important factor driving the observed patterns of microbial diversity as pH is one of the main factors structuring microbial communities in soils [21]. To further explore the difference between these two groups, we ran LefSe and found 19 phyla enriched in the SMC-bog/fen group while 34 were enriched in the LON-marsh/swamp group. Acidobacteria, Verrucomicrobia and Actinobacteria are the most differentially abundant in the SMC-bog/fen group, with phylum WPS-2 (Candidatus Eremiobacterota) having the lowest p-value. Desulfobacteria, Sva085 (Candidatus Acidulodesulfobacterales) are the most differentially abundant in LON-marsh/swamp. (Fig. 3C). Although physiology studies will be needed to confirm their function, based on their taxonomy, it is likely that these last two groups may be able to perform sulfate reduction [60]. Interestingly, sulfate reduction coupled to organic matter oxidation is more thermodynamically favorable than methanogenesis [43]. Our results suggest that the role of sulfate reduction in mitigating the carbon dioxide/methane emissions balance could be different in the LON-marsh/swamp group and in the SMC-bog/fen group, motivating a broader focus on the sulfur cycle in future functional analysis.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive examination of the Laguna Seca páramo ecosites, highlighting the intricate relationships between vegetation assemblages, landscape position, and microbial diversity. Our findings reveal that these ecosites reflect critical spatial scales for investigating the rich microbial communities associated with páramo soils and peatlands. Notably, the peatland site exhibited significantly higher diversity in bacterial, archaeal, fungal, and protist communities compared to the convex and concave sites. This trend was particularly pronounced in the targeted analysis of methane-cycling organisms, which, despite their low overall abundance, demonstrated remarkable metabolic diversity in the peatland.

By comparing the microbial diversity of the páramo with the MUCC dataset, we found that while the páramo microbiome is distinct from other wetland types, it shares closer affinities with organic soil and peat-forming wetlands such as bogs and fens. This establishes a valuable baseline for monitoring microbial diversity in the rapidly changing Chingaza Páramo, underscoring the need for ongoing research in this unique ecosystem.

Given páramos’ critical role in water provisioning and their vulnerability to climate-driven hydrological changes, understanding the diversity, distribution, and ecological drivers of methanogenic and methanotrophic communities here is essential. Our results and previous reports [67] suggest that changes in ecosites within the páramo area could lead to microbial community shifts with possible consequences for the methane cycle, underscoring the need to incorporate tropical wetlands into global assessments of greenhouse gas emissions and climate feedbacks.

Future investigations should focus on the substantial diversity of unclassified eukaryotic ASVs and further explore the roles of microbial communities in nitrogen and sulfur cycling, particularly in the context of CO₂ and CH₄ production. Such studies will enhance our understanding of ecosystem functions and the resilience of páramo landscapes amid environmental change.
