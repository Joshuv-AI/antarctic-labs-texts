PrPC modulates BACE1-dependent amyloid pathology under chronic cerebral hypoperfusion: Implications for vascular cognitive impairment
Authors: Qingqing Sun, Xiang Yin, Yue Lang, Baizhuo Zhang, Jie Shao, Yanan Zhang, Yaru Wang, Ying Wang, Li Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular cognitive impairment (VCI) currently lacks effective pharmacological interventions, underscoring the need to identify novel therapeutic targets. Increasing evidence suggests that amyloid-β (Aβ) contributes to VCI pathogenesis by promoting neurovascular dysfunction and cognitive decline. The cellular prion protein (PrPC) has been implicated in the regulation of β-secretase (BACE1), a key enzyme involved in Aβ generation. In this study, we examined the relationship between PrPC expression and BACE1-related Aβ pathology using transgenic mouse models with different PrPC levels, including PrPC gene (PRNP) knockout (KO), wild-type (WT), and overexpressing (Tga20) mice. Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH)-induced VCI was established using bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS). We found that altered PrPC expression was associated with changes in BACE1 expression, enzymatic activity, and subcellular distribution, accompanied by corresponding differences in Aβ burden and cognitive performance in BCAS mice. These observations suggest that PrPC may influence BACE1-related Aβ metabolism under CCH conditions. Collectively, our findings provide evidence for a potential link between PrPC and BACE1 regulation in VCI and highlight the PrPC-BACE1 axis as a pathway that may warrant further investigation in the context of VCI pathophysiology.Keywords: Vascular cognitive impairment, Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, Cellular prion protein, Aβ, β-secretase

Introduction

VCI encompasses the contribution of vascular pathology to cognitive decline across a spectrum ranging from subjective cognitive decline and mild cognitive impairment to dementia, it has become a major global public health concern [1,2]. While Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia in Western countries, VCI represents the second most common etiology and may be the predominant cause in East Asia [3]. Vascular pathology is common in elderly individuals with cognitive impairment, and CCH is considered a major contributor to VCI [2]. However, pure vascular dementia is relatively uncommon, as many patients exhibit mixed neuropathology that includes both vascular lesions and Alzheimer-type changes, such as Aβ plaques and tau tangles [1].

Experimental and clinical studies suggest that CCH may promote Aβ accumulation by increasing the expression and activity of BACE1 and γ-secretase, thereby contributing to neurovascular dysfunction and cognitive decline [[4], [5], [6]]. Globally, approximately 50 million people are currently living with dementia, and this number is projected to triple by 2050 [7]. Vascular factors contribute to a substantial proportion of these cases and impose significant health and socioeconomic burdens [8]. Despite this, no disease-modifying therapies specifically targeting VCI have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration [9]. Emerging evidence indicates that Aβ-related pathways may represent potential therapeutic targets, although the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood [10,11].

PrPC is best known for its role in prion diseases, in which its misfolded isoform (PrPSC) induces spongiform neurodegeneration and accumulates extensively in the brain [12]. Under physiological conditions, PrPC is widely expressed, particularly in the central nervous system (CNS) [13]. Although its precise biological functions remain incompletely defined, PrPC is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored protein capable of interacting with multiple intra- and extracellular partners [14]. Notably, several studies have reported interactions between PrPC and components of the amyloidogenic pathway, including amyloid precursor protein (APP), BACE1, and Aβ [[15], [16], [17]]. In experimental models of AD, PrPC has been proposed to participate in both the clearance and the neurotoxic signaling of Aβ, although its exact role remains controversial [[18], [19], [20]]. Given the growing evidence implicating Aβ in the pathogenesis of VCI [4,10,21], the potential involvement of PrPC in this context warrants investigation. To date, however, the role of PrPC in VCI and its possible regulatory effects on Aβ metabolism under conditions of chronic hypoperfusion remain largely unexplored.

In the present study, we investigated the relationship between PrPC expression and cognitive impairment in a mouse model of CCH-induced VCI established by BCAS. Using mice with different levels of PrPC expression, including PrP knockout, wild-type, and PrP-overexpressing lines, we evaluated cognitive performance and pathological changes associated with the APP-BACE1-Aβ pathway. Behavioral testing, histopathological examination, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, ELISA, and Western blot analyses were performed to assess alterations in Aβ-related pathology. We further explored the potential mechanisms by which PrPC may influence BACE1 regulation and Aβ metabolism in vitro. We hypothesized that PrPC may modulate the APP-BACE1-Aβ axis and thereby influence cognitive outcomes in VCI.

Materials and methods

Ethic statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Research Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Jilin University and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines to minimize animal suffering and the number of animals used.

Animals

Male FVB/N mice of three genotypes-PRNP+/+ (WT; Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China), PRNP−/−(KO) and PRNP++/++ (Tga20) mice (Institute of Medical Laboratory Animals, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences)-aged 4–5 months (25–30 g) were used, Pregnant females at embryonic day (E)16–18 were used for primary neuronal culture. Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C; 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water.

Bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS)

CCH was induced using the BCAS model as previously described [22]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane in 30% O2/70% N2O and maintained at 1.5% isoflurane during surgery. Following a midline cervical incision, both common carotid arteries were carefully isolated and stenosed using microcoils (internal diameter: 0.18 mm; Sawane Spring, Shizuoka, Japan) (Fig. 10A).

Schematic overview of experimental design and workflow. A. Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH) was induced via bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) using 0.18 mm microcoils in WT, PRNP−/− (KO), and Tga20 (TG) mice. Cognitive function was evaluated 8 weeks post-BCAS using the Morris water maze (MWM). B. Brains were bisected; one hemisphere was fixed, paraffin-embedded, and processed for histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC), while the other was homogenized for biochemical analyses including Western blotting and ELISA. C. Immature primary cortical neurons were isolated from embryonic day 16–18 WT, KO, and Tga20 mice, cultured for 7 days, and subjected to chronic hypoglycemic-hypoxic (CHH) stress. Neuronal cultures were assessed via immunocytochemistry, immunofluorescence (IF), BACE1 activity assays, Western blotting, and ELISA.

Sham-operated mice underwent identical surgical procedures without microcoil placement and were anesthetized for the same duration. Body temperature was maintained at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C throughout the procedure.

Eight weeks post-surgery, cognitive function was assessed using the Morris water maze. Group sizes were as follows: WT BCAS (n = 10), WT Sham (n = 10), KO BCAS (n = 10), KO Sham (n = 10), Tga20 BCAS (n = 10), and Tga20 Sham (n = 10).

Morris water maze (MWM)

Spatial learning and memory were evaluated eight weeks after BCAS using the Morris water maze (Noldus EthoVision XT 8.5) [23].

The circular pool (diameter 100 cm; height 50 cm) was filled with opaque water maintained at 22 °C. A circular platform (diameter 10 cm) was positioned in the pool. Distinct visual cues were placed around the pool to facilitate spatial orientation.

The experiment consisted of a 5-day acquisition phase followed by a probe test on day 6 [24].

During the acquisition phase, mice underwent four trials per day from different starting quadrants. On day 1, the platform was visible (1 cm above the water surface). On days 2–5, the platform was submerged (1 cm below the surface) at a fixed location.

Each mouse was given 60 s to locate the platform. Escape latency was recorded. Mice that failed to locate the platform were guided to it and allowed to remain for 15 s, and a latency of 60 s was assigned. Inter-trial intervals were at least 20 min. Swimming speed and escape latency were recorded daily.

During the probe trial (day 6), the platform was removed. Mice were released from the quadrant opposite the original platform location and allowed to swim for 60 s. The number of platform crossings, time spent in the target quadrant, percentage of time in the target versus opposite quadrant, and swimming trajectories were recorded to assess spatial memory.

Tissue preparation and histological analysis

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were prepared and processed for histological and immunohistochemical analyses following established protocols [25]. Mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min. Brains were removed and bisected along the midsagittal plane (Fig. 10B). One hemisphere was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, rinsed in distilled water, dehydrated in graded ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Coronal sections (5 μm) were prepared using a microtome (Leica, Germany), mounted on glass slides, and dried at 60 °C for 40 min. Sections were stored at 4 °C until further use. These sections were used for subsequent hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staininng, Nissl staininng, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. The contralateral hemisphere was used for Western blotting and ELISA analyses.

H&E and nissl staining

For H&E staining, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin for 10 min, differentiated briefly in alcohol, blued with ammonia for 1 min, and counterstained with eosin for 2 min.

For Nissl staining, sections were incubated with toluidine blue at 60 °C for 30 min and differentiated briefly in 95% ethanol.

After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted with neutral resin and imaged using light microscopy (Fig. 10B). Histopathological injury was semi-quantitatively scored on a 0–4 scale for H&E sections, and normal neurons in Nissl-stained sections were counted per field under 400 × magnification.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for Aβ

Immunohistochemical staining for Aβ was performed using an anti-Aβ antibody (NBP2-13075, Novus) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 98 °C for 10 min. Sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity and then blocked with 5% BSA and 10% goat serum for 3 h at room temperature.

Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody (1:500) diluted in 1% BSA containing 0.1% Triton X-100. After washing, sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody followed by streptavidin–HRP and visualized using DAB. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Images were captured and analyzed using ImageJ to quantify Aβ-positive staining.

Primary neuronal culture [26] and immunocytochemical characterization [27]

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from embryonic day 16–18 mice of the three genotypes (Fig. 10C). Cells were cultured in Neurobasal medium (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

After 7 days in vitro, neuronal identity was confirmed by immunocytochemistry using a β3-tubulin antibody (1:300). Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for visualization, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Cultures with ≥90% β3-tubulin–positive neurons were considered suitable for subsequent experiments.

Chronic hypoglycemic-hypoxic (CHH) model [28]

After 7 days in culture, immature neurons were subjected to chronic metabolic stress by replacing the culture medium with low-glucose DMEM. Cells were incubated for 24 h in a tri-gas incubator at 37 °C under hypoxic conditions (5% O2, 5% CO2, 90% N2). (Fig. 10C).

Immunofluorescence (IF) for Aβ and BACE1

Immature primary neurons were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked with 10% goat serum. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against Aβ (1:500, NBP2-13075, Novus, USA) and BACE1 (1:500, NBP2-37261, Novus, USA). Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (ab150113, Abcam, UK) were applied for 1 h at room temperature.

Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescent images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ. (Fig. 10C).

BACE1 colocalization with TGN38 or EEA1

To examine BACE1 subcellular localization, double immunofluorescence staining was performed. Cells were first incubated with BACE1 primary antibody followed by Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody. After re-blocking, cells were incubated with antibodies against EEA1 (1:100, ab206860, Abcam, UK) or TGN38 (1:100, CQA6182, Cohesion Biosciences, UK) and visualized using Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (ab150132 or ab150080, Abcam, UK).

Images were captured using fluorescence microscopy and analyzed with ImageJ. (Fig. 10C).

ELISA measurement of Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42

Hippocampal and cortical tissues were homogenized in ice-cold PBS (1:9, w/v), sonicated, and centrifuged at 5000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C. (Fig. 9B).

PrPC confers neuroprotection in CCH-induced VCI by modulating BACE1 expression, enzymatic activity, and subcellular localization, thereby limiting Aβ1-42 accumulation. A. In PRNP-knockout (KO) mice, CCH induces marked BACE1 upregulation, enhanced enzymatic activity, and increased BACE1–early endosome (EEA1) colocalization, resulting in excessive Aβ1-42 accumulation and severe neuronal injury. B. In wild-type (WT) mice, CCH moderately elevates BACE1 expression and activity, leading to partial Aβ1-42 accumulation and intermediate neuronal damage. C. In PrPC-overexpressing (Tga20) mice, PrPC suppresses BACE1 expression and activity, promotes BACE1 redistribution toward the trans-Golgi network (TGN), and limits endosomal Aβ1-42 production, thereby preserving neuronal integrity and cognitive function.

Cell culture media were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C before storage.

Levels of Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions (Fig. 9C). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated using standard curves. (Fig. 10B–C).

Quantification of BACE1 by ELISA

Hippocampal and cortical tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer (30 mg/mL), sonicated, and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected for analysis. (Fig. 10B).

BACE1 levels were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Cloud-Clone Corp., Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and concentrations were determined based on standard curves. (Fig. 10C).

BACE1 enzymatic activity assay

BACE1 activity was measured using a fluorescence-based activity assay kit (23603W, China). Neuronal lysates were prepared using extraction buffer and centrifuged to remove debris. Proteins were precipitated with saturated ammonium sulfate and resuspended in assay buffer.

Samples were incubated with a fluorogenic substrate at 37 °C for 30 min. Fluorescence was measured at Ex/Em = 345/500 nm using a microplate reader, and enzymatic activity was calculated based on EDANS fluorescence standards. (Fig. 10C).

Western blot analysis of APP and BACE1

Cortical and hippocampal tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer containing PMSF. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay.

Equal amounts of protein were separated on 4–20% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with TBST containing BSA and skim milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies GAPDH (1:2000, Bioss, China), BACE1 (1:1000, Novus, USA), and APP (1:1000, CST, USA).

After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence and imaged using a Western blot detection system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ. (Fig. 10B).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 26.0), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 9). Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was examined prior to statistical testing. For normally distributed data with equal variances, comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA, followed by appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison tests. For data that did not meet these assumptions, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used. All tests were two-tailed. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MWM testing reveals genotype-dependent cognitive impairment following BCAS-induced CCH

To evaluate the effects of PrPC expression on cognitive performance after CCH. Morris water maze (MWM) testing was conducted eight weeks post-BCAS in WT, KO, and Tga20 mice. The assessment included a five-day acquisition phase (Days 1–5) and a probe trial on Day 6 (Fig. 1A). Mice were assigned to nine experimental groups (three genotypes × BCAS, Sham, Control). As no significant behavioral differences were observed between Sham and Control groups across key MWM parameters (P > 0.05; Fig. 1B), subsequent analyses focused on BCAS versus Sham comparisons within each genotype.

During the visible platform test on day 1, swimming speed did not differ among groups (P > 0.05; Fig. 1C), indicating intact motor ability and motivation. Latency to reach the platform, however, varied significantly among groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D). WT Sham (WTS) mice reached the platform faster than WT BCAS (WTB; ∗P < 0.05) and KO Sham (KOS; ∗∗P < 0.01). Tga20 Sham (TgS) mice performed better than KOS (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and Tga20 BCAS (TgB) mice reached the platform faster than WTB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001). No significant differences were observed between KOS and KO BCAS (KOB), TgS and TgB, or WTS and TgS (P > 0.05).

Across the five-day acquisition phase, escape latency decreased over time in all groups, with the magnitude of improvement differing by genotype (P < 0.05; Fig. 1E). WTS, TgS, and TgB mice exhibited the greatest learning, whereas KO mice (KOS, KOB) showed minimal improvement. These results indicate that PrPC deficiency impairs spatial learning following BCAS, while overexpression may mitigate learning deficits.

In the probe trial (Day 6), significant genotype-dependent differences were observed across multiple measures of spatial memory, including target quadrant retention, quadrant preference, platform crossings, and search path efficiency (P < 0.05; Fig. 1F and G).

•Target Quadrant Retention (Fig. 1Fa): KOB mice spent less time in the target quadrant than TgB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and KOS mice spent less time than both WTS (∗∗P < 0.01) and TgS (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001).

Target Quadrant Retention (Fig. 1Fa): KOB mice spent less time in the target quadrant than TgB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and KOS mice spent less time than both WTS (∗∗P < 0.01) and TgS (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001).

•Quadrant Preference (Fig. 1Fb): WTS mice showed greater preference than WTB (∗P < 0.05). KOB had reduced preference compared with TgB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and KOS scored lower than WTS (∗∗P < 0.01) and TgS (∗∗∗P < 0.001).

Quadrant Preference (Fig. 1Fb): WTS mice showed greater preference than WTB (∗P < 0.05). KOB had reduced preference compared with TgB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and KOS scored lower than WTS (∗∗P < 0.01) and TgS (∗∗∗P < 0.001).

•Platform Crossings (Fig. 1Fc; Fig. 1G): WTS crossed the former platform location more frequently than WTB (∗P < 0.05). KOB crossed less frequently than TgB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and KOS crossed fewer times than WTS (∗∗∗P < 0.001) and TgS (∗∗P < 0.01).

Platform Crossings (Fig. 1Fc; Fig. 1G): WTS crossed the former platform location more frequently than WTB (∗P < 0.05). KOB crossed less frequently than TgB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and KOS crossed fewer times than WTS (∗∗∗P < 0.001) and TgS (∗∗P < 0.01).

•Search Path Efficiency (Fig. 1Fd): WTS exhibited more efficient search strategies than WTB (∗∗P < 0.01). KOB displayed impaired efficiency relative to WTB (∗P < 0.05) and TgB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and KOS required longer to locate the target than WTS and TgS (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001).

Search Path Efficiency (Fig. 1Fd): WTS exhibited more efficient search strategies than WTB (∗∗P < 0.01). KOB displayed impaired efficiency relative to WTB (∗P < 0.05) and TgB (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), and KOS required longer to locate the target than WTS and TgS (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001).

Overall, these findings demonstrate that PrPC expression modulates susceptibility to BCAS-induced cognitive deficits, with KO mice exhibiting pronounced impairments and Tga20 mice showing relative preservation of spatial learning and memory.

PrPC expression modulates CCH-induced neuropathology in mice

To evaluate neuropathological changes associated with CCH, cortical and hippocampal tissues were collected 48 h after MWM testing and analyzed using hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Nissl staining analysis (Fig. 2A).

H&E staining revealed genotype-dependent neuronal alterations in both the cortex and hippocampal CA1 region.

In the cortex, WT BCAS mice (WBC) displayed reduced neuronal density, cellular shrinkage, nuclear condensation, and loss of nucleolar structure compared with WT Sham mice (WSC) (∗P < 0.05; Fig. 2B c-d; Fig. 2C), consistent with neuronal injury associated with chronic hypoperfusion. In contrast, Tga20 mice showed relatively preserved neuronal morphology and higher neuronal density compared with KO mice (Fig. 2B a-b, e-f; Fig. 2C). No significant differences were observed between Tga20 BCAS (TBC) and Tga20 Sham (TSC) groups (Fig. 2B e-f; Fig. 2C).

In the hippocampal CA1 region, similar alterations were observed. WT BCAS mice (WBH) exhibited decreased neuronal density, disorganized cellular arrangement, and indistinct nucleoli compared with WT Sham mice (WSH) (∗P < 0.05; Fig. 2D c-d; Fig. 2E). Compared with KO mice, Tga20 mice displayed more intact neuronal architecture and greater neuronal density (Fig. 2D a-b, e-f; Fig. 2E). No significant differences were detected between Tga20 BCAS (TBH) and Tga20 Sham (TSH) mice (Fig. 2D e-f; Fig. 2E).

•Nissl Staining

Nissl Staining

Nissl staining further supported these observations by revealing genotype-dependent differences in neuronal integrity.

In the cortex, WBC mice showed a significant reduction in the number and size of Nissl bodies compared with WSC mice (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001; Fig. 2F c-d; Fig. 2G). Tga20 mice exhibited significantly greater Nissl staining intensity and larger Nissl bodies compared with KO mice (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001; Fig. 2F a-b, e-f; Fig. 2G). No significant differences were detected betweenTBC and TSC groups (Fig. 2F e-f; Fig. 2G).

In the hippocampal CA1 region, Nissl staining demonstrated a marked reduction in Nissl bodies in WBH mice relative to WSH Sham mice (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001; Fig. 2H c-d; Fig. 2I). Tga20 mice showed greater Nissl body preservation compared with KO mice (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001; Fig. 2H a-b, e-f; Fig. 2I), while no significant differences were detected between TBH and TSH mice (Fig. 2H e-f; Fig. 2I).

Together, these histological findings indicate genotype-dependent differences in neuronal morphology following CCH. KO mice exhibited more pronounced neuronal alterations, whereas Tga20 mice showed relatively preserved neuronal structure in both cortical and hippocampal regions.

PrPC expression is associated with genotype-dependent differences in Aβ accumulation following CCH

To evaluate Aβ pathology after chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH), Aβ levels were assessed in the cortex and hippocampal CA1 region using immunohistochemistry and ELISA (Fig. 3).

In the cortex, immunohistochemical staining revealed increased Aβ-positive neurons and amyloid deposition in WBC mice compared with WSC mice (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001; Fig. 3B c-d; Fig. 3C), indicating enhanced Aβ accumulation following CCH. ELISA Quantification

Quantitative ELISA analysis further supported these findings. In the cortex, Aβ1-42 levels were significantly elevated in WBC compared with WSC (∗P < 0.05; Fig. 3F) and were higher than those observed in TBC (∗∗∗P < 0.001; Fig. 3F). Immature primary cortical neurons exhibited genotype-dependent differences in neurite outgrowth, vulnerability to hypoxic stress, Aβ1–42 accumulation, BACE1 expression/activity, and BACE1 subcellular trafficking. KO neurons showed the most pronounced susceptibility, whereas Tga20 neurons demonstrated relative resilience. These results support a regulatory role for PrPC in modulating amyloidogenic processing at multiple cellular levels under pathophysiological conditions.

Discussion

VCI rarely arises from isolated vascular pathology, instead, mixed dementia-particularly in late-onset cases-predominates and is characterized by the convergence of vascular injury and neurodegenerative processes, most notably those associated with AD [1,29]. Cerebrovascular pathology occurs more frequently in AD than in other neurodegenerative disorders [30], suggesting shared pathogenic mechanisms. CCH has been proposed as a common contributor to both conditions, as it promotes Aβ accumulation and impairs Aβ clearance [31]. Progressive Aβ deposition may in turn exacerbate vascular dysfunction and neurodegeneration. Therefore, identifying mechanisms that regulate Aβ production under CCH conditions may provide potential therapeutic strategies for VCI.

In this study, we established a CCH model using BCAS with 0.18-mm microcoils. This model reproduces the moderate reduction in cerebral blood flow observed in VCI and has been shown to induce white matter damage and cognitive impairment in mice [22,32,33]. In FVB/N wild-type (WT) mice, BCAS induced cognitive deficits consistent with VCI. In contrast, FVB/N PrPC-deficient (KO) mice displayed persistent cognitive impairment regardless of surgical intervention, whereas FVB/N PrPC-overexpressing (Tga20) mice consistently demonstrated superior performance in the MWM compared with other genotypes. These findings suggest that PrPC expression levels are associated with differential susceptibility to CCH-induced cognitive impairment.

Histological analysis further supported these observations. H&E and Nissl staining revealed pronounced neuronal loss and morphological abnormalities in the cortex and hippocampal CA1 region of KO mice, including nuclear pyknosis and nucleolar loss, with little difference between sham and CCH conditions. WT mice exhibited moderate neuronal injury following CCH compared with sham controls, whereas neuronal architecture remained largely preserved in Tga20 mice. Together, these results indicate that PrPC expression is associated with protection against neuronal damage under chronic hypoperfusion.

PrPC is a GPI-anchored membrane protein that interacts with multiple molecules involved in synaptic function and amyloid metabolism, including APP, BACE1, and Aβ [14,34]. Because CCH has been shown to promote Aβ accumulation [31], we examined Aβ deposition in cortical and hippocampal tissues. WT mice subjected to BCAS exhibited increased Aβ immunoreactivity compared with sham controls, indicating enhanced Aβ accumulation in the VCI model. Aβ levels were highest in KO mice and lowest in Tga20 mice. Isoform-specific ELISA further demonstrated significant differences in Aβ1-42 levels across genotypes, particularly in cortical tissue, whereas Aβ1-40 levels remained relatively unchanged. Given that Aβ1-42 is more aggregation-prone and neurotoxic than Aβ1-40 [[35], [36], [37], [38]], these findings suggest that PrPC expression may influence Aβ pathology under CCH conditions.

Previous studies have shown that CCH promotes Aβ accumulation through activation of BACE1 [4,5,39], the rate-limiting enzyme in amyloidogenic APP processing. Consistent with this mechanism, CCH increased BACE1 expression in WT and KO mice. Notably, PrPC deficiency exerted a stronger influence on BACE1 expression than CCH itself, whereas PrPC overexpression suppressed BACE1 levels despite CCH exposure. These results suggest that PrPC may function as an important regulator of BACE1 expression in the context of chronic hypoperfusion.

BACE1 catalyzes the initial cleavage of APP in the amyloidogenic pathway and is therefore a major determinant of Aβ production [[40], [41], [42]]. Although pharmacological inhibition of BACE1 has been widely explored as a therapeutic strategy [[43], [44], [45]], complete suppression of BACE1 activity may disrupt its physiological roles in neuronal maintenance and synaptic function [44,46]. Consequently, mechanisms that modulate BACE1 activity without abolishing its essential functions may represent a more balanced therapeutic approach. Our findings suggest that PrPC may act as a physiological modulator of BACE1, restraining amyloidogenic processing while preserving neuronal integrity.

Most previous studies investigating interactions between PrPC and BACE1 have focused on AD models, and their relevance to VCI has remained unclear. To address this, we examined immature primary cortical neurons derived from KO, WT, and Tga20 mice. In vitro results paralleled the in vivo findings, with genotype-dependent differences in Aβ production and BACE1 expression. In addition, BACE1 enzymatic activity was significantly reduced in Tga20 neurons compared with WT and KO neurons, indicating that PrPC influences both BACE1 abundance and enzymatic function.

BACE1 undergoes complex intracellular trafficking that is critical for its activity. The enzyme is synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum as an immature pro-enzyme and subsequently matures in the trans-Golgi network (TGN) through removal of its pro-domain and post-translational modifications [47,48]. Mature BACE1 is then transported to the plasma membrane and internalized into endosomes together with APP, where the acidic environment favors amyloidogenic cleavage [[49], [50], [51]]. Because APP and BACE1 must converge within the same subcellular compartment for Aβ generation, their trafficking dynamics play an essential role in regulating amyloid production.

Previous studies have reported that PrPC modulates BACE1 activity by altering its intracellular localization rather than by directly affecting its expression, although the regulatory effects appear to vary across experimental models and mouse strains [16,[52], [53], [54]]. APP is a type I transmembrane protein that traffics between the plasma membrane and acidic intracellular compartments [55]. While non-amyloidogenic cleavage occurs at the plasma membrane, amyloidogenic cleavage by BACE1 preferentially takes place within acidic intracellular compartments such as endosomes, where BACE1 exhibits maximal enzymatic activity [[56], [57], [58], [59]]. After trafficking from the TGN to the plasma membrane, BACE1 and APP are rapidly internalized into early endosomes [60,61],which are considered a major site of amyloidogenic processing [62].

PrPC interacts with multiple ligands, through its intrinsically disordered N-terminal domain, including BACE1 [14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that BACE1 co-immunoprecipitates with PrPC in both mouse and human brain tissue. PrPC binds to the immature pro-domain of BACE1 within the Golgi apparatus, thereby limiting its trafficking to the cell surface and endosomal compartments where APP cleavage occurs [16].

Consistent with these observations, our colocalization analyses revealed increased BACE1-EEA1 colocalization in KO neurons under hypoxic conditions, indicating enhanced targeting of BACE1 to early endosomes. In contrast, Tga20 neurons showed a modest increase in BACE1 retention within the TGN. These findings suggest that PrPC regulates BACE1 intracellular trafficking in the FVB/N background, potentially limiting its localization to compartments that favor amyloidogenic processing.

Although the physiological functions of PrPC remain incompletely understood, our results provide evidence that PrPC modulates BACE1 expression, activity, and intracellular trafficking. Through these mechanisms, PrPC may reduce amyloidogenic processing and mitigate neuronal damage under conditions of chronic cerebral hypoperfusion. These observations suggest that PrPC may represent a potential therapeutic target in VCI and related mixed dementias.

In summary, this study demonstrates that PrPC plays an important neuroprotective role in CCH-induced vascular cognitive impairment. PrPC overexpression in Tga20 mice was associated with reduced neuronal injury, lower Aβ1-42 accumulation, and improved cognitive performance, whereas PrPC deficiency resulted in more severe neuropathological and behavioral impairments. Mechanistically (Fig. 9A-C), PrPC appears to modulate the APP-BACE1-Aβ pathway by reducing BACE1 expression, decreasing its enzymatic activity, and altering its intracellular trafficking, particularly by limiting localization to early endosomes where amyloidogenic processing occurs.

Together, these findings highlight PrPC as a potential regulator of amyloidogenic processing under chronic hypoperfusion and suggest that targeting PrPC-mediated pathways may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for VCI and mixed dementia.

Limitations and future prospects

Safety considerations remain important, as misfolded PrPSc can cause fatal prion diseases. Its formation may be influenced by factors such as pH, temperature, ionic strength, and oxidative stress, although interspecies transmission is partially constrained by the species barrier. In addition, the reproducibility of PrPC-mediated effects should be validated across different genetic backgrounds and species.

Furthermore, the in vitro hypoglycemia–hypoxia experiments were performed in immature primary cortical neurons at 7 days in vitro (DIV7), which do not represent fully functional mature neurons. Future studies using more mature neuronal cultures are warranted to further validate these findings. Clinical translation therefore remains a long-term objective. Given the absence of FDA-approved therapies for VCI, identifying novel regulatory pathways remains a priority. Because PrPC interacts with key components of Aβ metabolism, it may represent a potential therapeutic target for VCI and related mixed dementias.
