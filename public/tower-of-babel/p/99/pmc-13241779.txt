Seasonal dynamic and individual determinants of Amphibiocystidium sp. infection in the palmate newt during the aquatic breeding season
Authors: Léa Lorrain-Soligon, François Brischoux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amphibian populations are undergoing global declines, with infectious diseases playing a major role in these losses. While chytridiomycosis has been widely studied, other pathogens, such as mesomycetozoean parasites, are increasingly recognized as potential contributors to amphibian health deterioration. In this study, we investigated the seasonal dynamics of Amphibiocystidium sp. infection in the palmate newt (Lissotriton helveticus) during its aquatic breeding period in western France. From January to July 2023, across 12 sampling sessions and 585 captured individuals, we monitored host morphology, body condition, and infection status. We found strong seasonal variation in host traits, with body mass and condition declining over the breeding season, particularly in females. Infection prevalence and cyst intensity peaked at the end of March, and were not observed after the end of May. Smaller individuals were more likely to be infected, and infected females exhibited higher parasite loads than males. Additionally, infection probability increased with body condition in males only. The temporal restriction of infection suggests that environmental factors and host aggregation during breeding may play a key role in transmission.

Introduction

Amphibian populations are declining worldwide at unprecedented rates, and infectious diseases are recognized as major drivers of these losses (Shelly and Singh, 2024). Among emerging infectious diseases, chytridiomycosis caused by the fungal pathogen Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd) has received considerable attention due to its devastating effects on amphibian biodiversity (Fisher and Garner, 2020; Kilpatrick et al., 2010). However, comprehensive assessments have revealed that additional infectious agents also contribute to amphibian declines, with substantial ecological consequences (DiRenzo and Campbell Grant, 2019; Karwacki et al., 2021).

In recent decades, increasing attention has been given to fungal-like protists belonging to the class Mesomycetozoea, a group positioned at the boundary between animals and fungi (Mendoza et al., 2002). Parasites of this group have the potential to drive hosts to extinction, due to high virulence, low host specificity, and free-living infectious stage (Rowley et al., 2013). Within this group, members of the order Dermocystida, particularly the genus Amphibiocystidium, sister group of Dermocystidium and closely related to Rhinosporidium (Pereira et al., 2005), infect amphibians. This parasitic protozoan has been previously identified in both frogs (Fagotti et al., 2019; Pascolini et al., 2003) and newts (Courtois et al., 2013; Lorrain-Soligon et al., 2023; Raffel et al., 2008). This parasite is known to form cysts mainly apparent in the skin of infected amphibians (Densmore and Green, 2007), although internal organs such as the liver may also be affected (González-Hernández et al., 2010).

Infections by Amphibiocystidium were originally thought to have low consequences on individuals and populations (Densmore and Green, 2007). However, they have since been shown to impact host health and population dynamics, due to interferences with gas and water exchanges through the skin (González-Hernández et al., 2010), increased secondary infections by other diseases and microorganisms (Raffel et al., 2008), debilitation and emaciation (Densmore and Green, 2007), as well as shifts to new host species (Pascolini et al., 2003). Still, infection can persist within populations for several years without necessarily causing rapid or catastrophic declines (Fagotti et al., 2019), suggesting a dynamic that may be chronic and seasonally structured rather than acutely epidemic.

In temperate amphibians, seasonal life-history dynamics may strongly influence host-parasite interactions. Many, otherwise terrestrial, species rely on aquatic sites as breeding habitats, where individuals congregate during a limited reproductive season. Such aggregations are expected to increase contact rates among individuals and can therefore facilitate pathogen transmission (Ritchie et al., 2021). Reproductive investment can be particularly costly, including courtship and competitive behaviors in males, and vitellogenesis and egg production in females (Wells, 2007; Woodley, 2018), as illustrated by substantial mass loss during this period, particularly in females due to oviposition (Ryser, 1989; Strickland et al., 2015). Such seasonal physiological changes, combined with increased host aggregation, may alter susceptibility to infection and influence parasite development and transmission dynamics, which, to our knowledge, has not been investigated in Amphibiocystidium sp.

In this study, we investigated the seasonal dynamics of Amphibiocystidium sp. infection in the palmate newt, Lissotriton helveticus (Razoumowsky, 1789), during its aquatic breeding period in western France. Specifically, we first aimed to characterize (i) temporal variation in host body size, mass, and body condition throughout the breeding season. Secondly, we focused on Amphibiocystidium sp. dynamic, by characterizing (ii) the phenology and intensity of cyst occurrence, and (iii) the link between these infections and host traits.

Methods

Study species

We studied Lissotriton helveticus (palmate newt), which is commonly found in various freshwater habitats across Europe, including ponds, lakes, and slow-moving streams (Speybroeck et al., 2018). This species has been reported to host infections by Amphibiocystidium sp., with cysts typically developing in the skin of infected individuals (Lorrain-Soligon et al., 2023). In temperate regions, L. helveticus is characterized by a relatively prolonged breeding period, during which individuals remain in aquatic habitats for several months (Harrison et al., 1983; Speybroeck et al., 2018). This extended aquatic phase leads to extended aggregation of individuals and prolonged exposure to aquatic pathogens.

Sampling method

Between 12 January 2023 and 21 July 2023, we conducted several successive newt capture sessions (authorization ‘Arrêté préféctoral n° 006-2023/DBEC’) starting at the onset of arrival of the first reproductive individuals at the pond. These sessions occurred once a month until indices of Amphibiocystidium sp. infections were detected (N = 2 sessions) and their frequency increased every two weeks afterward (N = 10 sessions). No captures occurred between 5 February and 17 March 2023, as a cold spell prevented newt activity and captures (see Appendix A). Newt captures occurred at night, around 10:00 p.m., on one small pond (6.5 × 5.5 m) situated in the Deux-Sèvres department (France; 0.4254°W, 46.1469°N, 70 m elevation), where the presence of Amphibiocystidium sp. infecting palmate newts has already been reported (Lorrain-Soligon et al., 2023). A total of 585 newts (324 females and 261 males) were captured by dip netting over the course of the 12 sessions. These individuals were not individually marked at capture, so that recaptures of the same individual across different sessions was likely. Indeed, our goal was to comprehensively describe the local population at each sampling session. Importantly, sampling of successive different cohorts of individuals across the breeding season was unlikely as this species is characterized by two waves of breeding migration (pre-breeding and post-breeding) in-between which individuals stay on the reproductive site (Harrison et al., 1983).

Once captured, the newts were carefully transferred to transparent plastic containers (14*16*9 cm) containing water from the pond, and transported to the laboratory, located less than 100 m from the pond. Individuals were measured for snout-vent length (SVL) using a ruler and weighed to the nearest milligram. A detailed examination was conducted to assess the presence of skin lesions (cysts), characteristic of infection by Amphibiocystidium sp., and which facilitates its detection (Courtois et al., 2013; González-Hernández et al., 2010). We assessed the presence of cysts and for each infected individual, the number of cysts, ranging from single cutaneous lesion to multiple coalescing skin ulcers (see Lorrain-Soligon et al., 2023 for pictures).

The subcutaneous cysts, specific of infection by Amphibiocystidium sp., are readily identifiable (Courtois et al., 2013; González-Hernández et al., 2010; Lorrain-Soligon et al., 2023; Raffel et al., 2008). In contrast, other parasites, such as metacercaria, are typically found in the body cavity and internal organs (digestive tract, lungs, kidneys and bladder, Sinsch et al., 2019, 2018). In the infrequent instances when metacercaria produce subcutaneous cysts (e.g. Caffara et al., 2014), these cysts strongly differ from those linked to Amphibiocystidium sp. First, these cysts are characterized by visible yellow grub (see pictures in Caffara et al., 2014 compared to pictures in Lorrain-Soligon et al., 2023). Second, they are mainly located on the cephalic area of the hosts, unlike Amphibiocystidium sp. cysts which can be widespread on the entire body surface (see Caffara et al., 2014 compared to Lorrain-Soligon et al., 2023).

A Body Condition Index (BCI) for each individual was calculated using the linear regression between log10 (SVL) and log10 (body mass), providing an estimate of the health and nutritional status of the newts. After the examination, all individuals were immediately released back into the pond from which they were captured.

We first characterized temporal variation in host body traits. We tested for the effect of date, the quadratic effect of date (date2), sex, and their interactions (date*sex and date2*sex) on newt size (SVL), mass, and body condition index (BCI). The quadratic term was included to account for potential non-linear temporal patterns across the breeding season, such as increases followed by decreases in the measured variables.

Body mass was influenced by the quadratic form of date, as well as by sex, but not by their interaction (Fig. 1B–Table 1). Indeed, body mass was overall larger in females than in males, and body mass was larger at the beginning of our study period, continuously decreased throughout the reproductive season (Fig. 1B–Table 1).

BCI was influenced by date and its quadratic form, sex, and the interaction between date and sex, but not by the interaction between the quadratic form of date and sex (Fig. 1C–Table 1). Indeed, BCI was overall higher in females than in males, and BCI was higher at the beginning of our study period and continuously decreased throughout the reproductive season, a trend that was more pronounced in females than in males (Fig. 1C–Table 2).

Amphibiocystidium sp. infection dynamic

Out of the 585 newts, 34 individuals (15 females and 22 males) were found to be infected with Amphibiocystidium sp. The number of cysts in parasitized individuals ranged from 1 to 42, with an average of 5.32 cysts (±1.45).

Cyst presence and number were only influenced by date and its quadratic form, and not by sex, or by interactions (Fig. 2AB, Table 1). Influence of individual traits on Amphibiocystidium sp. infection

When restricting our analyses to the period where cysts were observed (from March 17th to May 25th), we found that cyst presence was influenced by the interaction between body condition and sex (Fig. 2D–Table 1), increasing with increased body condition in males only (Fig. 2D–Table 2). Cyst number was influenced by SVL only, decreasing in larger individuals in both sex (Fig. 2E–Table 1).

Restricting our analyses to infected individuals only, cyst number was influenced by SVL, by sex and their interaction (Fig. 2F–Table 1). Overall, cyst number in infected individuals decreased with increasing SVL, but this relation was only found in females (Fig. 2F–Table 2), and was higher in females as compared to males (Fig. 2F–Table 1).

Discussion

We found marked seasonal variation in both Lissotriton helveticus host traits and Amphibiocystidium sp. parasite occurrence. Females newts were overall larger and in better condition than males, but experienced a sharper seasonal decline in body condition. Amphibiocystidium sp. dynamic was influenced by seasonality and by host characteristics. Parasite presence and cyst number peaked around the core of the breeding period. There was no influence of sex on infection probability, but infected females harbored more cysts than males. Cyst numbers decreased with increasing body size, and infection probability increased with increasing body condition but only in males.

Host traits dynamic during the breeding period

Temporal variation in SVL, body mass, and body condition were consistent with the marked seasonality of reproduction in temperate amphibians. The quadratic relationship between date and size likely reflects the protracted wave of pre-breeding migration (Harrison et al., 1983). Females were overall larger and heavier than males, in line with sexual size dimorphism commonly reported in newts (Monnet and Cherry, 2002; Shine, 1979). Body condition loss during the course of the breeding season is consistent with the substantial energetic costs of reproduction documented in amphibians, particularly in females (Ryser, 1989; Strickland et al., 2015). The seasonal decline in body condition observed here likely reflects cumulative reproductive effort and reduced feeding during the aquatic phase.

Parasite dynamic during the breeding season

Cyst presence and cyst number peaked around the end of March, suggesting a strong seasonal component to infection dynamics. This timing coincides with the core of the breeding season, when host density in the pond is presumably the highest. As the Mesomycetozoea group mainly includes aquatic parasites producing infective stages that can disperse in water (Rowley et al., 2013), increased host aggregation likely enhances transmission opportunities.

Influence of host traits on infection risk

We found that infection probability and cyst number decreased with increasing SVL, consistent with results found in Bd (Cheatsazan et al., 2013). Larger individuals are probably older and in better condition and may invest more in the immune response to infection (Meurling et al., 2024; Rollins-Smith et al., 2011). Alternatively, this result might be linked to selective mortality in smaller and weaker individuals (Bonneaud et al., 2017), which might be confirmed by the small increase of cysts presence in males with increasing body condition.

In infected individuals, females exhibited higher cyst loads than males, a pattern that seems repeatable across years (Lorrain-Soligon et al., 2023). This sex bias in infection intensity may reflect differential allocation of resources during reproduction. This might be linked to energetic costs of reproduction experienced by females during vitellogenesis (Aranzábal, 2011; Silla et al., 2021), as suggested by the stronger decrease in body condition we detected in females. Indeed, females experience high energetic trade-offs between reproduction and immune defense (Cox et al., 2010; Lochmiller and Deerenberg, 2000). As a result, once infected, they may experience higher parasite proliferation.

Interestingly, cyst presence was restricted to a relatively short temporal window, occurring in only six sampling sessions during the middle of the study period. This concur with studies showing that Amphibiocystidium sp. prevalence is higher in early spring while not being observed in late spring (Pérez, 1907; Raffel et al., 2008). This pattern could be linked to a higher pathogenicity of parasites at lower temperatures (Berger et al., 2004), and/or to better immune responses at higher temperature in the ectothermic host (Wright and Cooper, 1981). Nonetheless, as already found with Amphibiocystidium sp. (Raffel et al., 2008), we did not detect the progressive increase over time that might have been expected under a simple accumulation scenario, suggesting that infection dynamic is not only a function of host density, decreasing body condition, and cumulative reproductive costs. Such pattern raises important questions about Amphibiocystidium sp. seasonal ecology and the mechanisms allowing persistence when hosts are absent from the water. For instance, Amphibiocystidium sp. may persist outside the host in the aquatic environment in a dormant or low-detectable stage, or alternatively rely on reservoirs or environmental conditions for transmission. Given the growing recognition of mesomycetozoean parasites as emerging pathogens, documenting their prevalence, ecological impact, and geographic distribution remains an urgent priority for amphibian conservation.
