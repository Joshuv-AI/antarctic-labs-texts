Bereavement Interventions for Children and Adolescents: An Evidence and Gap Map of Primary Studies and Systematic Reviews
Authors: Leonor Rodriguez, James Lyons, Amy Maloy, Ailsa O’Brien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExperiencing the death of a loved one can be very distressing for children and adolescents. If the bereavement is not managed, it can lead to lifelong consequences such as poor mental health outcomes. Despite this being a universal experience, there is a lack of agreement in the theoretical definitions of fundamental concepts of grief, loss and bereavement. Research has suggested child grief has specific characteristics determined by their age and developmental stage and needs, as well as the circumstances surrounding the death. A significant challenge in the field is identifying age appropriate and validated interventions that are specific for children and adolescents. Evidence and Gap Maps (EGMs) can contribute to addressing this gap by providing a comprehensive and visual synthesis of the existing evidence.ObjectivesThis EGM aimed to identify, synthesise and map existing published and unpublished (grey literature) evidence of primary studies and systematic reviews of bereavement interventions for children and adolescents, targeted at improving psychosocial outcomes.Search MethodsThe searches for this EGM were carried out using electronic databases (n = 6), web search engines, repositories and grey literature. The search terms were developed based on the participants, bereavement terminology and types of interventions. Searches were conducted over May and June 2024.Selection CriteriaAll primary studies and systematic reviews of bereavement interventions for children and adolescents between 0 and 24 years of age, in English or Spanish were included in this EGM.Data Collection and AnalysisSearch results were imported into EndNote, where results were collated and duplicates deleted. Results were then imported into EPPI-reviewer where additional duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened in duplicate. Potential studies were then divided and full text was screened in duplicate, a third reviewer resolved any conflicts. Data was extracted from sources that fit the inclusion criteria in duplicate. The information extracted was participant age, country, study design, intervention type, intervention name, duration, form of delivery, provider, target death and type of death, evaluation and outcomes.ResultsA total of 99 sources are included in this EGM. Interventions were carried out in different parts of the world but most evidence was identified in the USA, Australia, Canada and The Netherlands. The age that most evidence concentrated on is the age group between 10 to 14 years with underpinning theories, cognitive behavioural therapy and expressive therapies the most reported. The majority of interventions were delivered face to face and included different types (interventions, programmes, support groups and camps). Most reported a duration of 6–10 sessions and targeted parental death. Outcomes reported were various (n = 49), mostly focused on grief, coping, emotional expression, adjustment to death and emotional wellbeing. The majority of systematic reviews were rated as low quality and the majority of domains classified for randomised control trials were unclear.ConclusionsThis EGM provides a systematic, searchable and visual tool for policy makers, practitioners, researchers and members of the public to identify the existing evidence on bereavement interventions for children and adolescents. The EGM identified the areas where most evidence is currently concentrated, as well as areas where further research is warranted for specific age groups and types of death experienced. The EGM also identified the need for higher quality evidence as well as longitudinal evaluations.

Background

Experiencing the death of a loved one is one of the most distressing and impactful experiences for children and adolescents (Melhem, 2011; Stephen & Wimpenny, 2006). These experiences, if not managed, may lead to lifelong poor mental health outcomes and somatic symptoms (Stephen & Wimpenny, 2006).

One of the challenges associated with understanding death and bereavement in children and adolescents is the lack of agreement on conceptual and theoretical definitions of bereavement, loss and grief (Zisook & Shear, 2009). Bereavement, grief and loss are often used interchangeably in the literature (Duncan, 2020; McLaughlin et al., 2019). These are interrelated concepts but not equal. Bereavement refers to an event of death and grief corresponds to the cognitive, affective, and behavioural responses to a bereavement (Glorioso et al., 2020; Revet et al., 2020). Due to this lack of conceptual specificity and in the interest of proving a comprehensive search this EGM includes the terminology bereavement, grief and loss to determine the eligibility of included studies.

Grief reactions in children and adolescents are specific to their age and developmental stage at the time they experience the bereavement (Alvis et al., 2022; Kaplow et al., 2010). Child grief is often a non-linear process, for example it may be masked or supressed and emerge again months or years later, increasing the risk of experiencing psychological disorders and functional impairment (Boelen et al., 2019; Duncan, 2020). Children’s bereavement is determined by many factors such as social supports, attachment, social upbring, cognitive abilities and previous experiences (Santos et al., 2021). Effective interventions should be tailored to the characteristics and needs of child and adolescent grief due to bereavement; as well as consider the individuality in the experience of grief and the need for child-centred approaches (Lohnes & Kalter, 1994). Research, however, has identified that many bereavement interventions are designed in response to the grief reactions of adults and are rarely adapted to recognize the grief reactions of children and adolescents (Zhang et al., 2023).

Bereavement can lead to negative and lifelong responses (Feigelman et al., 2017). Children can experience depression, anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder (Kaplow et al., 2010; Spuij et al., 2013). Recently, prolonged grief disorder (PGD) has been recognised and included in the diagnostic manual ICD-11; WHO, 2019) and the DSM- V (DSM-5-TR; APA, 2013), acknowledging the possibility of pathology as a result of unresolved grief. These symptoms have distinctive characteristics and, therefore, require different types of treatment and interventions (Shear et al., 2005).

Another challenge in the field of child and adolescent bereavement is identifying validated outcome measures suitable to capture the developmental trajectories and characteristics of child grief, which could also help to identify risk factors for the development of lifelong mental health issues (Hill, 2015; Zhang et al., 2023). Many studies, informing the design of interventions use adult bereavement measures that lack developmentally appropriate language and do not assess the full range of grief reactions most often exhibited by children and adolescents (Alvis et al., 2022).

The EGM is live, searchable and publicly available providing a visual presentation of bereavement interventions for children and adolescents, including evidence from primary studies and systematic reviews. EGMs are used to graphically present the concentration of coverage and areas of deficiency where evidence exists, as well as to evaluate the quality of that existing evidence (Bakrania, 2020). This facilitates (1) funders to identify knowledge saturated areas and redirect resources to areas requiring more research (2) policy makers and practitioners can use the EGM to inform policy and practice (3) minimise research duplication (4) the public can advocate for services using evidence-based information that has a user-friendly format accessible for a variety of stakeholders (McConnell et al., 2024).

Objectives

This EGM aimed to identify, synthesise and map existing published and unpublished (grey literature) evidence of primary studies and systematic reviews of bereavement interventions for children and adolescent targeted at improving psychosocial outcomes in a user-friendly format.

Additionally, this EGM map and present in a systematic way the existing theoretical context in the field of child and adolescent bereavement. The map will inform the co-creation of a bereavement peer intervention for adolescents experiencing death from parental cancer (Bereavement Mentoring Programme for Adolescents, BMPA). The EGM will help identify existing interventions and the characteristics that make these interventions suitable, safe or age appropriate. The EGM will inform the co-design process by comparing the existing evidence with the lived experience of the co-creation team.

Methods

This EGM is based on a published protocol (Rodriguez et al., 2025) which was preregistered with the Campbell Collaboration (ID: cl2.20240071).

Framework Development and Scope

The standard EGM framework was used as a matrix to inform the eligibility criteria of the map (White et al., 2020). The EGM complied with the standard EGM framework as a matrix and followed practice guidelines. The rows of the matrix represent the types of interventions (e.g., group intervention, camp, individual psychotherapy, family therapy) and the columns represent the psychosocial outcomes identified (mood, wellbeing, stress, improving relationships etc.).

This EGM also aimed to inform the co-creation of a peer mentoring intervention for adolescents who experience parental death from cancer (BMPA). Therefore, the options to filter the EGM were purposefully chosen to provide a comprehensive understanding of the existing body of evidence around this topic. Options to filter the map included: age, region, delivery and other study characteristics were also created. Recognizing the EGM framework as a ‘living’ document enabled reviewers to add additional subcategories during the data extraction process, based on the data sourced within the articles. The EPPI-Mapper tool was used to develop the interactive online EGM.

Dimensions

The EGM framework informed the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this EGM. Psychosocial outcomes (e.g., mood, wellbeing, social support) and types of interventions (e.g., group intervention, camp, individual psychotherapy, family therapy) were the key dimensions of the EGM.

The inclusion criteria for EGMs follows the PICOS approach of population, intervention, relevant comparison groups, outcomes and study design (Saran & White, 2018). Therefore, the study inclusion criteria for this EGM also used this approach.

Characteristics and Frequency of Included Studies

Study designs included RCTs, pre-test, post-test design, qualitative studies, longitudinal studies, experiments and systematic reviews (literature reviews, rapid reviews, scoping reviews, meta-analysis). These are further described in Table 1.

Types of Intervention/Problem

Interventions types included in this EGM are listed in Table 1. These interventions had to be targeted at bereaved children and adolescents. Due to the comprehensive nature of this EGM, intervention types included are: camps, counselling sessions, interventions, peer support programmes, programmes, support groups, therapy and treatment.

Types of Participants

Participants were children and adolescents are defined as any person from 0 to 24 years of age and classified as follows: children (0–9 years), early adolescence (10–14 years), late adolescence (15–19 years) and young adulthood (20–24 years) (Patton et al., 2016).

Outcomes

This EGM include studies if these were focused on improving psychosocial outcomes e.g., mental health, functional impairment, stress and behaviour (full list of outcomes included in Table 1).

Studies were excluded if they did not report psychosocial outcomes in children and adolescents impacted by bereavement.

Other Eligibility Criteria

Studies were included if these were published or ongoing in English or Spanish languages. There were no date limits added to the searches.

Settings and modes of delivery were also not restricted.

Additional Dimensions

Additional dimensions to further understand the types and qualities of bereavement interventions were included in this EGM: age, country, study design, type of intervention, name of the intervention, duration, form of delivery, intervention provided by, targe death, type of death, evaluation and comparison condition.

Search Methods and Sources

Literature retrieval methods and search strategies in this EGM were carried out following Campbell Collaboration Guidelines for EGMs (White et al., 2020).

The search strategy was developed around the population and intervention of interest. This was carried out in collaboration with a subject expect librarian and piloted to determine if it was comprehensive and sensitive to identifying major and relevant interventions identified in the developing of the background literature of this EGM. This included for example the Family Bereavement Programme and child and adolescent bereavement camps. After this pilot, the word camp* was added to the search terms. Search Terms

Manual searches for relevant EGMs, systematic reviews and meta-analyses via The Campbell Library, The Evidence for Policy and Practice Information and Co-ordinating Centre (EPPI- Centre), and The Social Care Institute for Excellence (SCIE) databases were conducted. Protocols relevant to child and adolescent bereavement were searched via the PROSPERO database. Reference lists of all relevant systematic reviews were also hand searched to identify any eligible studies.

Electronic Searches

To identify all relevant studies, multiphase research searches were conducted between May and June 2024. The selection of these databases was based on the researcher’s university database subscriptions and the relevance to the topic. The search included four overarching academic databases and platforms:• Applied Social Sciences Index and Abstracts (ASSIA), ERIC, Social Services Abstracts and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global (ProQuest platform).• APA PsycInfo, Embase, and MEDLINE All (Ovid platform).

Other Sources

A grey literature search was undertaken via Overton, Policy Commons and Google Scholar which yielded a total of n = 2155 additional results. Only the first 1,000 records from Google Scholar and Policy Commons, sorted by relevance, were exported, as this is deemed an acceptable number to capture the most relevant results (Miller et al., 2023).

Analysis and Presentation

Following data extraction, the team exported coded data from EPPI-Reviewer 4.0 (Thomas et al., 2010) into a JSON file. The file was imported into EPPI-Mapper software to generate the EGM.

Data Collection and Analysis

Screening and Study Selection

Reviewers completed the searches and stored the results in a RIS file. These files were uploaded to the reference software managing tool EndNote. Following this, references were uploaded to EPPI Reviewer 4.0 and duplicate studies identified (n = 39,434) were removed on the threshold range of 0.98 to 1.00 (Thomas et al., 2010).

A total of n = 63,538 studies identified by EPPI reviewer remained, ahead of initial tittle and abstract screening. A pilot test was carried out with 100 studies. All reviewers screened the batch independently and met as a group to discuss any discrepancies to ensure consistency in the understanding and use of the inclusion criteria. The interrater reliability between reviewer pairs was over 90% and therefore, adequate to continue the screening process.

In the first phase of screening, titles and abstracts were examined by two researchers independently, to identify eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. This was followed by a second stage of more thorough screening of full texts in duplicate. Studies that met the eligibility criteria were then selected for data extraction, coding, and bias appraisal. Discrepancies at all analysis stages were resolved by a third reviewer. Any interventions where multiple publications were identified were grouped under the code “Different publication from same study”. The software used (EPPI Reviewer) does not allow merging studies. This is important to understand why multiple studies may have repeated or multiple codes. Additionally, due mainly to the inclusion of systematic reviews, a single study could be categorised into several categories, therefore a single study can be included in the map in multiple locations, making the evidence seem larger than it is.

Data Extraction and Management

A data extraction form was created in EPPI. Due to the exploratory nature of this EGM and its objective to inform the subsequent design of a peer-support mentoring intervention (BMPA), the data extraction form could be updated and more categories were added based on the data included and extracted from the studies. The categories and subcategories of extraction are included in Table 1.

Reviewers met regularly during data extraction. In these meetings, the team also agreed on the changes made to the data extraction form, for example, the concepts of prolonged grief disorder and complicated grief were merged in a single category as these are synonyms. Day-to-day contact was maintained with the reviewers by using a Team Channel.

Quality Appraisal

The methodological rigour of systematic reviews included was evaluated using the revised edition of the critical appraisal tool ‘A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews’ (AMSTAR-2) (Shea et al., 2017). AMSTAR 2 is designed to evaluate quantitative studies of healthcare interventions, whether they have randomized or non-randomized designs. This tool is formed of a domain-based rating system (seven critical and nine non-critical domains) and aims to generate an assessment of confidence (high, moderate, low or critically low) in the results of a systematic review.

Additionally, the ‘Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Controlled Trials’ (Higgins, 2011) was also used to assess the risk of bias within randomised control trials (RCTs) included in the EGM. This instrument evaluated the methodological strength and reporting quality of RCTs across six key domains: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias and other biases. Quality appraisal was also carried out in duplicate.

It is acknowledged that any studies that were not systematic reviews and/or RCTs were not included for quality assessment, as this is not a mandatory procedure of EGMs (White et al., 2020) and the number of studies included would have made the process too resource intensive.

Synthesis of Included Studies

A total of 99 studies were identified as being eligible for inclusion in this EGM. It should be noted that the map contains more entries than there are studies. A single primary study or systematic review can assess multiple outcome domains and subdomains. Likewise, a study or systematic review may focus on more than one type of intervention or targeted psychosocial outcome, depending on the nature of the intervention. As a result, some records are counted multiple times to ensure all intervention and outcome categories, including subcategories, are coded and mapped across all included studies. Recognizing this is important for gaining a full understanding of the results of the EGM, the data extracted from of included studies is described in Table 1.

Where Were the Studies Conducted?

Interventions included in the EGM were carried out in different parts of the world. Due to the included systematic reviews, a category including several countries (various) had to be created and it represents the highest number (n = 54, 54%), however these are not individual studies. East Africa is also a region of African countries (n = 18, 18%) so the second most frequent number of studies were reported in the group, however the interpretation has to be done carefully for this reason. Regarding individual countries, United States reported 8 interventions (87%), followed by Australia, Canada and The Netherlands with 3 each (3%).

Study Participants

The age range for this EGM was 0 to 24 years of age. Theoretically informed age groups were created to group the interventions; however, some interventions have been included in more than one age category if the target age range was wider. Most interventions included early adolescents between 10 and 14 years (n = 75, 35%). An equal number reported children 0–9 years (n = 59) and late adolescence 15–19 (n = 59, 27.3%). Some studies did not specify an age range and only reported children (n = 10) or adolescents (n = 5).

Study Interventions

The EGM was interested in identifying the foundation of existing bereavement interventions. Data on the theoretical foundation of the interventions was extracted. The most predominant theoretical foundations were cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) (n = 21, 21.2%) and expressive therapy (i.e., art/music/drama/literature) (n = 27, 27.3%). Nearly half were founded on grief therapy, trauma-focused therapy or group psychotherapy (n = 45, 45.6%). Some lacked this foundation or did not report it (n = 17, 17.2%).

Included interventions were delivered in different ways. The majority were delivered face to face (n = 76, 76.8%), online (n = 4, 4.0%), or through other forms of technology (n = 1, 1.0%). The study which noted the intervention being delivered through other forms of technology described the implementation of a technology whereby young people speak to a camera behind a two-way mirror (McGurl et al., 2015). A total of 18 sources did not clearly provide this information (n = 18, 18.2%).

Intervention types were also varied. Therapy (n = 24, 24.2%), interventions (n = 21, 21.2%) and programmes (n = 18, 18.2%) were the modalities most reported followed by support groups (n = 13, 13.1%) and camp settings (n = 12, 12.1%). Intervention duration also varied, the majority were in the range between 6 and 10 sessions (n = 25, 25.3%), followed closely by 11–15 sessions (n = 13, 13.1%). This was not relevant or not reported on some papers, including systematic reviews (n = 18, 18.2%). It was also challenging to compare duration, as this information was not reported in a systematic manner. Some studies reported number of sessions, others the frequency per week or month or total duration in hours.

Interventions were also targeted at a specific type of death experienced by the child or young person, most interventions targeted the impact of parental death (n = 58, 58.6%). The deaths of a sibling (n = 32, 32.3%) “any close other” (n = 26, 26.3%) and any other family members (n = 26, 26.3%). The majority of interventions did not specify the nature of the death experienced (n = 42, 42.4%). However, several studies honed their research to focus on deaths by suicide (n = 21, 21.2%), cancer (n = 15, 15.2%) or an unspecified illness (n = 15, 15.2%).

Types of Outcomes

Due to the exploratory nature of this EGM to inform the co-creation of a peer mentoring bereavement intervention (BMPA), restrictions on outcomes were not included a priori. All outcomes included are under the umbrella of psychosocial outcomes. For this reason, The EGM framework consists of 49 measured outcomes, each looking at a targeted input, skill or behaviour (see Table 1). Most studies investigated grief or coping (n = 54, 54.5%), followed by the child’s ability or capacity to express emotions (n = 24, 24.2%), their adjustment to the death (n = 22, 22.2%) and emotional wellbeing (n = 21, 21,2%).

Study Types

When measuring the impact of interventions on outcomes, most included studies did not have a comparison condition in their study design (n = 41, 41.4%). Of those that reported a comparison condition, a selection of studies utilised a control group as their comparison condition (n = 14, 14.1%). Some reviews compared waiting lists (n = 6, 6.1%), other experimental conditions (n = 5, 5.1%), treatment as usual (n = 5, 5.1%) or no treatment (n = 1, 1.0% %).

Discussion

Summary of Results

This evidence and gap map (EGM) was designed to identify, categorize, and synthesize existing primary and systematic research on interventions focused on bereavement for children and adolescents, targeted at improving psychosocial outcomes. The EGM maps the current evidence and identified areas where additional evidence is required. The visual format is a user-friendly format accessible for different stakeholders such as children and families themselves, practitioners, researchers and policy makers.

The EGM included 99 studies; the USA is the country presenting the most individual evidence. Most interventions are targeted at early adolescents between 10 and 14 years. The EGM includes a total of 16 RCTs. Types of interventions varied but therapy was the most common, therefore the most common provider was a counsellor, therapist or clinician. The Family Bereavement Programme is the intervention with more publications (n = 6). Duration varied; 6–12 sessions was the most common. The majority of interventions are delivered face to face. Most interventions were targeted at parental death; however, the cause of death was mostly not reported, but suicide was the second most common. Most studies consisted of a pre-post intervention with no comparison group. Outcomes identified were 40 in total. The outcomes reported the most were behaviour, depression, post-traumatic stress and anxiety.

Areas of Gap in the Evidence

Bereavement interventions in this EGM are mostly focused on the adolescent population, with a greater focus on early adolescents compared to those investigating children and late adolescents. According to Brent et al. (2012), there may be differential psychological and functional effects that are conditional on the age that a young person loses a parent. In addition to psychological reactions, bereavement in adolescents can have an adverse impact on their relationships with friends and their family system, especially on the quality of their relationships with a remaining parent. As a result, the impact of the loss with regards to their psychosocial reactions may be accelerated and lead to psychopathological dysfunction (Ross et al. (2021)). This finding means that less evidence exists regarding other age groups, particularly under 14 and over 20 years of age. Although the impact of bereavement for those in their adolescent period are significant, research into the efficacy and availability of interventions targeted at younger or older age ranges would maximise the knowledge that exists, congruent with the needs and characteristics of various developmental stages.

Research has found that the nature of the death experienced and the relationship with the deceased (Karabulut et al., 2021; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2013) will determine the impact this has on the child or adolescent. It was significant that not all interventions included in the EGM reported on or examined the associations between the nature of death and post-death psychological functioning in youth, a greater number of studies have called attention to ways in which traumatogenic factors embedded in the circumstances of the death can interfere with adaptive grieving processes (McClatchey & Vonk, 2005). Although some studies examined the effects of death by suicide, there were still several sudden deaths (e.g., violence, accident, war or natural disasters) that were not considered, and this could be a vital area where more evidence is needed.

Heterogeneity in the duration of interventions was another pattern identified in the EGM. The majority of evidence is concentrated in those interventions which had a duration between 6 and 10 sessions, followed closely by 11–15 sessions and only 3 studies noted that the delivery duration of an intervention was dependent on the individual. This finding is important as the notion that grief is experienced as a universal and linear process is no longer plausible, as many circumstances can affect the type of grief experienced and the way it manifests or is expressed (Montano et al., 2015; Santos et al., 2021). Often the delivery of interventions is restricted by limited financial and human resources and, therefore, a set number of sessions for an individual is deemed appropriate, having more known interventions that support a child or adolescent based on their individual and developmental needs (Lohnes & Kalter, 1994) is fundamental but evidence is lacking. This is in line with research on child and adolescent bereavement which has identified that child bereavement has specific trajectories and characteristics (Zhang et al., 2023) which are different to adult bereavement.

Another important gap identified was the limited evidence on longitudinal evaluations included in the EGM. Only a small number of studies (7 out of 99) had longitudinal evaluations, 12 months or over. In general, longitudinal studies often focus mainly on the first two years post-parental death, which means there is an opaquer understanding of how this loss affects children and adolescents over a longer period or how it connects to their subsequent emotional and social well-being (Pham et al., 2018).

Another interesting finding was the lack of theoretical foundation underpinning interventions, or a lack in reporting. This is important as the theoretical basis of a study guides the formation of a hypothesis and choice of research methods as well as connecting the researcher to existing evidence (Sacred Heart University, 2022). In this EGM, the theoretical foundation of interventions described in studies was predominantly CBT or expressive therapy (i.e., art/music/drama/literature). Nearly half were founded on grief therapy, trauma-focused therapy or group psychotherapy. Although the range of theoretical underpinnings of studies included is promising, there are arguably aspects of the experience of bereavement that may not be targeted. The lack of clarity or reporting regarding the theoretical underpinning of the interventions may be contributing towards the significant number of outcome measure reported across all included interventions, making potential comparisons such as systematic reviews and meta-analysis a challenge. Another important aspect to consider regarding heterogeneity in outcomes is the lack of theoretical clarity in relevant concepts; loss, grief and bereavement (Zisook & Shear, 2009).

Quality of Evidence

The current EGM highlights the presence of common methodological shortcomings in the included studies. For example, RCTs lacked transparency and specificity in the reporting, particularly in domain 1 and 1b (randomisation process). There is a concentration of ‘unclear or no information’ across several domains in the quality assessment. This lack of clarity questions the methodological rigour of the RCTs. The academic consensus is that in efficacy research, RCTs are traditionally considered the “gold standard” as usually RCTs can offer a consistently unbiased estimate of treatment effects, as randomization controls for unknown and unmeasured differences between comparison groups in research (Grossman & Mackenzie, 2005; Hariton & Locascio, 2018). Yet, with so many RCTs in this EGM revealing domains to be listed as ‘‘unclear”, the capacity and quality of the evidence used to inform future work could arguably be hindered. Regarding the risk of bias of systematic reviews, a similar pattern could be identified of lack of clarity and transparency in the reporting. Domains of study design, the justification for excluded studies, funding sources, the risk of bias in individual studies and publication bias are not reported. Additionally, only 4 of these systematic review methods included a meta-analysis, which would have provided the highest level of evidence to determine intervention effects. This is an area where more work is needed.

Potential Biases in the Mapping Process

To reduce potential biases in the mapping process, this EGM followed the Campbell Collaboration Guidelines for EGMs (White et al., 2020) and followed the protocol. Searches, screening, data extraction and risk of bias were carried out in duplicate, reducing the risk of bias in the mapping process and increasing eligibility of studies (Stoll et al., 2019). Furthermore, the PICO protocol (Methley et al., 2014) was followed to guide the inclusion of studies in the EGM. Adhering to these guidelines in the creation of an EGM is recognised as increasing reliability and validity of the research (Gholizadeh et al., 2020).

A systematic approach, supported by a subject specialist librarian also informed the searches. Searches also included source of potentially ongoing research such as trial registries and repositories to ensure the most recent evidence was also captured.

Strengths and Limitations of the EGM

The main strength of this EGM is that it is the first evidence and gap map of its kind which visually synthesises the current evidence of bereavement interventions for children and adolescents targeted at improving their psychosocial outcomes.

This is a comprehensive EGM which did not have a date limit, meaning that it captures a significant, if not all, the published and grey literature available on this topic up to the date the different searches were carried out on May 9, 2024. This, however, also presented some challenges as different sources and databases may have inaccurate publication dates across different databases. The inclusion of grey literature presented a further challenge. According to Aali and Shokraneh (2021) records may have several data types, which may not align with the actual publication date and can be problematic when understanding where a piece of research is truly at in its development e.g., published, unpublished, or ongoing research.

Due to the volume of studies screened in the EGM and the subsequent number of studies included, risk of bias assessment was conducted only for randomised control trials and systematic reviews. Additionally, this is not mandatory according to the EGM guidelines (White et al., 2020).

Due to the exploratory nature of this EGM and its subsequent use to underpin the co-creation of a peer mentoring bereavement intervention (BMPA), stakeholders were not involved in the creation of this EGM but will subsequently have a very significant role in the co-creation process of BMPA. This is acknowledged as a limitation of the EGM as the lived experience of participants could have helped to validate and interpret the findings.

A limitation in the visualisation of the map itself is the vast number of outcomes finally included in the map, maybe saturating the visual more than recommended. This was due to the exploratory nature of this map and to provide a clear understanding of the outcomes currently included in bereavement interventions. Additionally, broadening definitions to group outcomes to fit into the framework, the EGM risked oversimplifying the range of psychosocial responses of a bereavement that are displayed by children and adolescents across the lifespan. It should also be considered that, due to the lack of a specific and universal definition of child bereavement, loss and grief; there is a significant variation in the range of constructs that exist surrounding this topic (Glorioso et al., 2020; Revet et al., 2020). Therefore, a decision was made to prioritise the capacity of the EGM to inform policy and practice through identifying the gaps and areas of concentration in evidence, rather than the overall presentation of the map, which is still useful.

Authors Conclusions

Implications for Research

This EGM provides a clear and comprehensive visual summary of the existing evidence on bereavement interventions for children and adolescents. The EGM identified areas where most research is available and importantly identifies areas that could benefit from further investment in research. More research is needed in younger children and those between 20 and 24 years of age. More longitudinal research is needed to evaluate the long-term impact of outcomes. RCTs in this field require more transparency in their reporting and rigour in their methods to reduce the risk of bias and increase the quality of the evidence.

Further and alternative methods, such as meta-analyses can be used to determine the efficacy of these interventions, as the EGM is a description of the evidence only. The inherent limitation of this method is that it cannot determine efficacy. It would also be beneficial to include the voices of children and adolescents regarding their experiences of being part of these interventions, as part of intervention evaluation. Qualitative and participatory methods can be appropriate to capture these experiences.

The quality and transparency of published systematic reviews can be improved. There should be clarity and transparency in the reporting. All domains of the reviews need to be included particularly the justification for excluded studies, funding sources, the risk of bias in individual studies and publication bias of included studies should be reported.

This EGM highlighted an ample variety of outcomes associated with grief and bereavement in adolescents. This reflects the complexity of the experience of bereavement; however, it represents a clear challenge when comparing the efficacy of interventions. Further research in the field of bereavement in children and adolescents may be useful to potentially identify more relevant outcomes across cultures and across experiences of grief, even if the authors acknowledge bereavement is a universal yet personal experience.

Implications for Policy

This EGM highlights the need for investment in evidence-informed interventions for children and adolescents that have a developmental understanding and approach, that considers and responds to the characteristics of child and adolescent bereavement and how this may change and manifest over time (Lohnes & Kalter, 1994; Zhang et al., 2023). The EGM identified that the current concentration of interventions is only in four countries, despite the fact that bereavement is an issue that impacts children and adolescents globally. Children under ten and over 14 have fewer interventions designed for them specifically, highlighting a gap in the research and subsequently in policy and practice for these groups.

Implications for Practice

This EGM identified a variety of bereavement interventions available for children and adolescents. Children between 10 and 14 have targeted interventions, however less evidence is shown for other age groups which can be equally impacted by bereavement short and long term. It could also be that interventions with this groups are happening in practice settings but not shared through commercial or non -commercial publications. Examples of good practice with these age groups can be shared to inform practice and ensure children and adolescents are accessing and receiving adequate supports. Although efficacy cannot be determined in this EGM by statistical analyses, as this was not the purpose, the EGM adds significant information about the characteristics and quality based on the interventions available. For example, interventions should consider the developmental age and non-linear nature of bereavement in children and adolescents as well as the theory underlying intervention creation or study evaluations. Additionally, further information about effectiveness can be obtained by carrying out targeted systematic reviews in this topic as the EGM provided an initial mapping of where the majority of the evidence is concentrated and where more evidence is required.
