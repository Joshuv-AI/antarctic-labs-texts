Association between parental firefighting status and adverse birth outcomes in Arizona: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Paulina Luna Ramirez, Michelle Valenti, Caitlin Clarkson Pereira, Maia Brown, Stuart Sherman, John Gulotta, Melissa A Furlong, Jefferey L Burgess, Leslie V Farland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFirefighters are continuously exposed to hazardous conditions including smoke, chemicals, stress, long work shifts and exhaustion, which may lead to a higher risk for chronic conditions and reproductive health issues. However, there is limited research looking at the association between parental firefighting with adverse birth outcomes. This cross-sectional study aimed to determine whether female or male firefighting was associated with adverse birth outcomes.MethodsArizona birth certificates for the years 2006–2013 were used to compare firefighters with teachers, law enforcement officers and all other occupations while stratifying by sex. Logistic regression models estimated ORs and 95% CIs.ResultsWe included 720 686 births, 5490 paternal firefighters and 376 maternal firefighters. Maternal firefighting was associated with increased odds of preterm birth compared with teachers (OR: 1.46, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.97) and all other occupations (OR: 1.41, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.88). Higher odds for neonatal intensive care unit admission were observed when compared with teachers (OR: 1.77, 95% CI 1.22 to 2.48), law enforcement officers (OR: 1.74, 95% CI 1.07 to 2.81) and all other occupations (OR: 1.84, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.56). Paternal firefighting was statistically significantly associated with decreased caesarean section and low birth weight.ConclusionsFemale firefighters may have higher odds of adverse birth outcomes. Further research should aim on understanding the biological mechanism behind these associations and ways to mitigate the risk in this population.

Introduction

Firefighting is a hazardous occupation due to numerous occupational exposures that can influence health such as chemical exposures, stress, shift work, heat, radiation and post-traumatic stress disorder.1 Some chemicals, such as per-fluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and volatile organic compounds, are associated with reproductive toxicity. Much of the existing literature on firefighter occupational health outcomes has focused on chronic conditions,25 while only a few have investigated the relationship with reproductive health.6 Prior research on firefighter mothers has suggested that their occupational exposures may increase the risk of stillbirth, preterm birth and miscarriage.6 7 Birth defects from paternal firefighting reported include total anomalous pulmonary venous return, cleft palate, cleft lip and transverse limb deficiency.8

Recent studies have investigated the association between firefighting occupation and reproductive outcomes in female firefighters. One survey study of 934 female firefighters found that women firefighters had 1.41 times the risk of preterm birth compared with women in all other occupations.7 Another study, which included 1821 firefighter women, concluded that female firefighters have a higher incidence of miscarriage and preterm birth compared with the general population.6 These studies highlight the importance of examining occupational exposures in females. However, a limitation of these studies is their small sample size, which may limit statistical power, and reliance on self-reported data. Using self-reported data has drawbacks including recall bias, social desirability bias and selective recall.9

To overcome limitations in previous research and address this current gap in knowledge, we used a large dataset of birth certificates from Arizona to investigate the association between parental firefighting status and adverse birth outcomes including low birth weight, preterm birth, caesarean-section, low Apgar score and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission. Firefighting involves several aspects of physical fitness including cardiovascular endurance, power and flexibility,9 which may contribute to differences in health characteristics compared with the general population. To address this healthy worker effect, firefighters were compared with teachers, law enforcement officers and all other occupations. These occupational groups have similar work demands that allow a more appropriate assessment of risks specific to firefighting.

Methods

Study population

Birth certificate data were acquired from the Bureau of Vital Statistics at the Arizona Department of Health Services and gathered between 2006 and 2013. The study period was selected based on the availability of parental occupation information on Arizona birth certificates. The initial sample included the birth records of 741 571 newborns and was reduced to 721 244 after removing births from mothers younger than 18 or older than 50. Additionally, birth certificate records with missing data for birth weight or gestational age were removed (N=558). The final sample size consisted of 720 686 individuals. A flowchart of the exclusion criteria is displayed in figure 1. The medical portion of the birth certificate was completed by a clinician, while demographic information was provided by the mother or a representative.

Flowchart of exclusion criteria for Arizona birth certificates (2006–2013). Flowchart displaying the exclusion of births with missing gestational age, birth weight or maternal age <18 or >50, resulting in a sample size of 720 686.

Exposure

The primary exposure of interest was firefighting occupation based on self-reported occupational data from the birth certificate collected for both parents. Possible responses for firefighting occupation included firefighter, fire marshal, fire captain, fire lieutenant, fire and rescue, among other terms (online supplemental material 1). Teachers were selected for the comparison group if they worked in the K-12 setting. Inclusion words included teacher, elementary school teacher, middle school teacher and high school teacher. They were included because they share a similar socioeconomic status, commitment to public service and level of work stress but have less exposure to chemicals, heat and shift work.10 While teachers experience a low exposure to chemicals such as pesticides, formaldehyde and toxic reagents,11 firefighters are exposed to agents with higher intensity including combustion byproducts like carbon monoxide, hydrogen cyanide and fine particulate matter.1

Finally, law enforcement officers were included if they reported their occupation as police officer, law enforcement officer, sheriff, deputy, sergeant and detective. They were included as a comparison group because they share shift work, violence but have less fire exposure.12 Occupational terms for firefighters, teachers and law enforcement officers are available on online supplemental material 1. In addition, a final comparison group called ‘all other occupations’ was created for all individuals who did not list their occupation as firefighters to ensure we had a representation of the general working population. This group includes teachers and law enforcement officers, who were also examined separately as individual comparison groups to observe the occupation-specific risks. Occupational information was missing for 30 mothers and 265 fathers. However, these records were included in the ‘all other occupations’ group for the analyses. All of the comparison groups were stratified by sex of the parent.

Birth outcomes

Birth weight was reported continuously in grams and gestational age at pregnancy was reported continuously in weeks. We categorised birth weight as low birth weight if weight was less than 2500 g.13 Preterm was defined as gestational age before 37 weeks of gestation.14 Caesarean section was included as a binary variable. Apgar score is a newborn measurement taken 5 min after delivery to assess their clinical condition. A score lower than or equal to 7 indicates a need for immediate medical attention,15 therefore we dichotomised Apgar score at ≤7. The final outcome examined was admission to the NICU.16

Covariates

Covariates for the analysis were selected based on previous knowledge of their potential association with our exposure and outcome. Information on maternal age at the time of delivery, maternal education (greater than high school, high school or less and unknown), marital status (married or single), race (White or non-White), ethnicity (Hispanic or non-Hispanic) and birth year (2006–2013) were self-reported in the birth certificates. Both race and ethnicity information were recorded separately and treated as different variables.

Statistical methods

Descriptive summary statistics for firefighters compared with teachers, law enforcement officers and all other occupations were stratified by parental sex. Logistic regression models were used to estimate the ORs and 95% CI of the association between parental occupation and adverse birth outcomes. Individual regression models were performed for each comparison group, with occupation coded as a binary exposure (firefighter vs comparison group) and firefighters serving as the reference group.

Multivariable logistic regression models were adjusted for mother’s education (high school or less, greater than high school and unknown), mother’s age (<20, 21–34, 35+) and birth year (2006–2013) (model 1). Low birth weight was additionally adjusted for gestational age to account for the time of fetal development.17

In sensitivity analyses, we additionally adjusted the multivariable models for mother’s race, ethnicity and marital status (model 2) to account for sociodemographic factors, which may influence birth outcomes such as preterm birth and low birth weight.18 A third model adjusted for county (Apache, Cochise, Coconino, Gila, Graham, Greenlee, La Paz, Maricopa, Mohave, Navajo, Pima, Pinal, Santa Cruz, Yavapai and Yuma) to account for health disparities among the counties in the state of Arizona.19 A fourth sensitivity analysis included the adjustments of model 1 and an additional adjustment in the outcome of NICU admission for gestational age (continuous variable represented in weeks), for newborn conditions (sepsis, jaundice, hypoglycaemia, respiratory problems or any other newborn health conditions) and maternal diabetes (yes or no). These variables were included to account for known NICU risk factors.20 Multiple gestation pregnancies are associated with a higher risk for adverse birth outcomes,21 so a sensitivity analysis restricting to singleton births was conducted (model 5). A sensitivity analysis was conducted with both maternal and paternal occupation to determine if father’s coexposure affected birth outcomes (model 6). Finally, we performed a birth weight z-score to account for population average for age and sex. All analyses were performed with RStudio V.2024.04.2.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Firefighter mothers and fathers were slightly younger than teachers and law enforcement officers, while youngest ages were observed among individuals in all other occupations (table 1). Compared with individuals in all other occupations, firefighter mothers were more likely to have an education beyond high school (77% vs 47%) and be married (67% vs 56%). Firefighter fathers were more likely than individuals in all other occupations to have completed high school (76% vs 46%) (table 2).

Preterm birth

Maternal firefighting was associated with higher odds of preterm birth compared with teachers and all other occupations (table 3). When compared with law enforcement officers, there were non-statistically significant higher odds of preterm birth.

Caesarean section

Maternal firefighting only showed a non-statistically significant weak association compared with individuals in all other occupations.

Low Apgar score

Firefighter mothers had a pattern suggesting a non-statistically significant increased odds of low Apgar score in their newborns compared with teachers and all other occupations.

NICU admission

A statistically significant association between maternal firefighting and higher odds of NICU admission was observed across all comparison groups (table 3). In the fourth sensitivity analysis, firefighter mothers still had higher odds of NICU admission when compared with teachers (online supplemental material 2). A statistically significant association was observed when compared with law enforcement officers and all other occupations.

Paternal occupation and birth outcomes

Low birth weight

There was no association between paternal firefighting and low birth weight when compared with paternal teachers, law enforcement officers and all other occupations (table 4).

Paternal firefighter status had a suggestion of greater odds for preterm birth in comparison to paternal teachers, but this association was not statistically significant. There was no association observed compared with law enforcement officers and all other occupations (table 4).

Caesarean section

Among paternal firefighters, there was no statistically significant association observed for caesarean delivery compared with teachers. Compared with law enforcement officers, there was a statistically significant 11% lower odds of caesarean section and 8% lower odds when compared with all other occupations (table 4).

Low Apgar score

Paternal firefighters had a non-statistically significant higher odds for low Apgar score compared with paternal teachers and law enforcement officers (table 4).

NICU admission

No association between paternal firefighting and NICU admission was observed when compared with all the occupational groups (table 4).

Sensitivity analysis

After additionally adjusting for race, ethnicity, marital status and county in maternal and paternal occupation (onlinesupplemental materials 3 4), there was no meaningful change in our effect estimates. Exclusion of 20 664 observations with multiple gestations did not change the effect estimates of the outcomes of interest (online supplemental material 5). However, the association between maternal firefighting and preterm birth was no longer statistically significant. After adding both occupations into the models, maternal occupation was the main factor associated with birth outcomes while paternal occupation showed inconsistent effect estimates with wide CIs (online supplemental material 6). The frequency of maternal and paternal combinations is shown in online supplemental material 7. Several of the paternal and maternal combinations were rare, leading to low frequencies. The final sensitivity analysis, using birth weight Z-scores standardised for infant sex and gestational age, did not show an association between standardised birth weight and parental occupation (online supplemental material 8).

Discussion

In this study, we observed that firefighter mothers had higher odds of preterm birth and NICU admission compared with the different occupational groups. Paternal firefighting was not associated with statistically significant increased odds of adverse birth outcomes, however there was a suggestion of increased odds of preterm birth when compared with teachers as well as low Apgar score when compared with teachers and law enforcement officers. Lower odds of caesarean section were observed among female partners of male firefighters when compared with law enforcement officers and all other occupations.

Characteristics of the study population

For mothers, the mean age ranged from 27 to 31 among all groups, which was similarly observed in fathers. For both mothers and fathers, the education achieved was lower among firefighters compared with teachers and law enforcement officers. Although we controlled for maternal education, residual confounding may exist from father’s education. Educational attainment may not be a good representation of socioeconomic status in firefighters because even though it does not require high education, it can be well paid. However, education remains as an important covariate due to its association with access to healthcare and health outcomes22 including preterm birth23 and low birth weight.24 Marital status also varied between groups and has also been suggested to be associated with risk of low birth weight, preterm birth, small for gestational age and neonatal mortality.25

Maternal occupation and neonatal health

Our results suggest an association between maternal firefighting and higher odds for some outcomes but not caesarean section. For low birth weight, when compared with all other occupations, we did not observe an association between firefighters and odds of low birth weight after controlling for gestational age. This finding agrees with prior research among 1821 female firefighters that observed no difference in low birth weight for firefighter mothers when compared with expected rates for non-Hispanic White women in the USA.6 We did observe that the odds of having a newborn with low birth weight was 33% higher among firefighter mothers compared with law enforcement officers. Even though both professions share similar exposures such as stress, heavy workload as well as physical and chemical hazards,26 firefighters experience higher exposure to combustion byproducts27 and other chemicals, including per-fluoroalkyl and PFAS.28 These chemicals are used in firefighter protective clothing and equipment to repel water and oil.29 PFAS exposure during pregnancy has been associated with a higher risk of low birth weight.30

We observed an increase in the odds of preterm birth in firefighter mothers compared with all occupational groups. This observation is supported by a previous cross-sectional survey study from the USA among 934 women that observed when compared with non-firefighters, firefighters had ~40% increased risk of preterm birth (OR: 1.41).7 Although PFAS exposures are hypothesised to influence risk of preterm birth by hormone disruption and placental dysfunction,31 32 other exposures associated with preterm birth such as shift work33 may also be operational. The smaller effect size between firefighters and law enforcement officers could suggest that both groups have similar stressors and/or exposure, such as shift work.

Firefighter mothers had a 62% higher likelihood of having newborns with a low Apgar score compared with teachers and 65% compared with all other occupations. None of the results reached the threshold of statistical significance. While our study had a large sample size, low Apgar score is a rare outcome with a low number of events, which likely limited statistical power.

The most substantial finding was observed for NICU admission. Compared with all groups, firefighter mothers had a higher likelihood of having a newborn that requires intensive care. The most common indications for NICU admission in the US are sepsis, meconium aspiration syndrome, respiratory distress syndrome, low birth weight, seizures, jaundice, hypoglycaemia, intrauterine growth restriction and lack of oxygen.20 A previous study of 1821 female firefighters observed that newborns from firefighter mothers were more likely to have jaundice,6 which may partially explain the association with increased NICU admission observed in our study. However, in the fourth sensitivity analyses, which was adjusted for several newborn conditions including jaundice, our results did not appreciably change.

Paternal occupation and neonatal health

The findings suggested possible patterns of association between paternal firefighting and adverse birth outcomes, however no observed associations were statistically significant. For low birth weight, we did not observe an association between paternal firefighting and odds of low birth weight suggesting that low birth weight may be more likely to be influenced by the mother’s exposure. A modest and non-statistically significant association between parental firefighting and preterm birth was only observed when compared with teachers. Even though male firefighting has been associated with fetal development34 and birth defects,8 there are no current studies on father firefighters and risk for preterm birth. A statistically significant lower odds of caesarean section among female partners of male firefighters were observed in comparison to female partners of male law enforcement officers and all other occupations. A non-statistically significant higher odds for low Apgar score among paternal firefighting was observed when compared with teachers and law enforcement officers. There was no association observed between paternal firefighting and NICU admission compared with the other groups, leading to the conclusion that being a firefighter father did not increase the odds of having a newborn admitted to the NICU.

Overall, we observed that paternal firefighting had less of an influence on birth outcomes compared with maternal firefighting, even after including paternal occupation in the same model as maternal occupation. The effect of paternal occupation may be better explained by having larger sample sizes among groups, measuring exposure in a more detailed way and incorporating genetics. However, paternal firefighting has been suggested to influence birth outcomes in previous research. An Australian exploratory analysis found that female partners of male firefighters had a miscarriage rate of 24%, which is similar to the rate of miscarriage for women firefighters (22%).35 There are several studies that have found an association between male firefighters and increased risk for heart defects in their offspring such as ventricular septal defects, atrial septal defects and anomalous pulmonary venous return.8 36 37 Cleft lip has also been associated with paternal firefighting.38 A California study that used birth certificates found an increased risk for circulatory and respiratory defects among children of male firefighters. This study demonstrated the risks of male firefighting and the benefits of using birth certificates to capture outcomes that happen after birth, which is similar to the approach used in the present study.34

Sensitivity analyses

The analyses that were restricted to singleton births yielded similar results to the main model, indicating that the observed associations were not driven by multiple gestations.

Although paternal occupation could cause maternal stress or introduce harmful exposures to the household, models that included both maternal and paternal occupation did not change the effect estimates suggesting that paternal occupation was not a coexposure. However, several of the maternal and paternal combinations were infrequent leading to wide CIs. This may have contributed to unstable estimates. Additionally, it is possible that both exposures were highly correlated, leading to multicollinearity, changing the effect estimates. Finally, birth weight Z-scores did not show an association between standardised birth weight and parental occupation, suggesting that the results obtained in raw birth weight were not influenced by infant sex or gestational age.

Strengths

The primary strength of this study is the use of birth certificate data that included parental occupation and neonatal outcomes as well as important demographic covariates. It also allowed for a large data set (n=720 686); therefore, increasing statistical power. Stratification by sex was performed to distinguish between the direct effect of maternal occupation and indirect effect of paternal occupation, rather than focusing on sex as a primary exposure. Finally, our comparison groups allowed a more accurate representation of different exposures and work-related demands.

Limitations

There was a potential for misclassification in both the exposure and the outcome. Occupation was self-reported, which could have been affected if workers inaccurately reported a job. Additionally, birth certificates did not include years of firefighting or firefighter type, which could have been useful to have a better assessment of exposure effects. Even though there was a possibility for misclassification on clinical outcome data, prior research comparing birth certificate data with medical records has found it to be valid for several birth outcomes.39 We were unable to account for multiple pregnancies, and therefore, the outcomes from these pregnancies may be correlated and not independent. Logistic regression requires independence of observations, otherwise, there is an underestimation of SEs. For this reason, we may be overestimating our statistical precision. The study period was restricted to 2006–2013, due to the availability of parental occupation in Arizona birth certificates. For this reason, more recent births could not be included, limiting the ability to determine whether associations remain after new workplace policies and firefighting practices. The lack of diversity among participants in this study may decrease the generalisability. The sample size of comparison groups may have affected the power to observe statistical significance of the results. An additional limitation is related to the coexposure analysis that includes both maternal and paternal exposure. Since some maternal and paternal combinations were rare, smaller sample size reduced the precision of effect estimates. Future research should focus on the effect of the paternal occupation as a coexposure in birth outcomes.

Conclusion

This study is one of the few studies that has been able to investigate the impact of occupation on both mothers and fathers on adverse birth outcomes. It also investigated birth outcomes that have not been studied in relation to firefighting such as caesarean section, low Apgar score and NICU admission. Additional research is needed to better understand if there are interventions, which may reduce risk of adverse outcomes among firefighters.
