Durability and Safety of Imeglimin as an Add‐On to DPP‐4 Inhibitors in Japanese Type 2 Diabetes: The 104‐Week FAMILIAR Trial
Authors: Masashi Shimoda, Takeshi Osonoi, Masahiro Iwamoto, Hideaki Kaneto, Kohei Kaku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo evaluate the long‐term efficacy and safety of imeglimin added to dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP‐4) inhibitors in Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes, focusing on glycemic durability and safety in elderly patients over 104 weeks.Materials and MethodsThis multicenter, randomized, placebo‐controlled trial comprised a 24‐week double‐blind phase (imeglimin 1000 mg or placebo twice daily) followed by an 80‐week open‐label extension in which all patients received imeglimin. Eligible patients had inadequate glycemic control despite DPP‐4 inhibitor monotherapy. The main assessment measured HbA1c changes from baseline to week 104. Secondary assessments included meal tolerance tests (MTT) for evaluating physiological changes in β‐cell function and insulin resistance and safety monitoring.ResultsOf 117 randomized patients, 81 completed 104 weeks. In the early‐start group that received imeglimin from week 0, the significant HbA1c reduction observed at week 24 (−0.65%) was maintained through week 104 (−0.55%; p < 0.001 vs. baseline). The delayed‐start group that switched to imeglimin at week 24 achieved similar glycemic control thereafter. Elderly patients (≥ 65 years) in the early‐start group maintained stable HbA1c reduction (−0.58%) without hypoglycemic events over 2 years. MTT analysis in the early‐start group showed sustained improvements in glucose AUC and insulin sensitivity without unnecessary insulin secretion over time.ConclusionsImeglimin added to DPP‐4 inhibitors appeared to improve glycemic control for 104 weeks, without clear attenuation. The combination was well‐tolerated with a low risk of hypoglycemia even in elderly patients. The long‐term effect may be associated with improvements in insulin sensitivity. Trial Registration: jRCTs061210082.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a progressive metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and a gradual decline in pancreatic β‐cell function [1, 2, 3]. The management of elderly patients with T2D poses a critical clinical challenge, particularly in Japan, which has a super‐aging society [4, 5]. According to the 2024 National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan, the prevalence of diabetes or suspected diabetes was 18.1% among individuals aged 65–74 years and 22.1% among those aged ≥ 75 years [6]. Given the increasing burden of diabetes in the elderly population, treatment strategies that ensure both efficacy and safety are critically important. Therapeutic strategies for this population must be aimed at balancing robust glycemic control with the minimization of adverse events (AEs), especially hypoglycemia, which is strongly associated with an increased risk of falls, fractures, and cognitive decline in elderly adults [7, 8, 9].

In Japan, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP‐4) inhibitors are widely prescribed at various stages of treatment due to their safety profile and efficacy [10, 11, 12]. However, progressive β‐cell exhaustion often leads to the attenuation of glycemic control over time, a phenomenon known as secondary failure, which necessitates combination therapy involving agents that have complementary mechanisms of action [13].

Imeglimin is a first‐in‐class oral agent containing a tetrahydrotriazine ring that targets mitochondrial bioenergetics. It has a unique dual mechanism of action that amplifies glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) and ameliorates insulin resistance, with potential protective effects on β‐cell mass and function, as demonstrated in preclinical and clinical studies [2, 14, 15, 16]. These properties suggest that imeglimin can be an ideal therapeutic partner to DPP‐4 inhibitors [17], potentially offering durable glycemic efficacy by preserving β‐cell function.

The FAMILIAR trial (eFficacy and sAfety of iMeglimin add‐on therapy to DPP‐4 Inhibitor in patients with type 2 diabetes melLltus: A Randomized, double‐blind, clinical trial) was a 104‐week study conducted at 21 sites in Japan to evaluate the long‐term glycemic durability and safety of imeglimin added to DPP‐4 inhibitors. This trial comprised a 24‐week randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, parallel‐group period and a subsequent 80‐week open‐label extension. We previously reported the interim results (week 24) of the FAMILIAR trial, which revealed the significant efficacy and favorable safety profile of imeglimin add‐on therapy in Japanese patients with T2D that were inadequately controlled with DPP‐4 inhibitors [18]. However, a 24‐week period is insufficient to evaluate the long‐term durability of glycemic control in chronic progressive disease or to fully assess the safety risks in vulnerable elderly populations.

In this study, we aimed to report on the results of the FAMILIAR trial through a period of 104 weeks, including an 80‐week open‐label extension. The main objective was to verify the long‐term durability of glycemic control and safety of imeglimin in combination with DPP‐4 inhibitors. Furthermore, given the specific needs of the aging population, we aimed to clarify the safety profile, particularly in terms of hypoglycemia, and efficacy in elderly patients (≥ 65 years). Additionally, we explored the longitudinal changes in β‐cell function and insulin resistance to elucidate the physiological mechanisms underlying the sustained efficacy of this combination.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This study reports the results of the FAMILIAR trial over a 104‐week period. The detailed study design, eligibility criteria, and results of the 24‐week interim analysis were published previously [18]. Briefly, the trial involved a 24‐week double‐blind treatment period followed by an 80‐week open‐label extension period (Figure 1A). Eligible participants were Japanese adults (≥ 20 years) with T2D who had inadequate glycemic control (glycated haemoglobin [HbA1c] level, 7.0%–9.0%) despite being treated with DPP‐4 inhibitor monotherapy for at least 12 weeks. One exclusion criterion was impaired renal function, defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 at screening. Patients receiving oral glucose‐lowering agents other than DPP‐4 inhibitors at the time of informed consent were required to discontinue these medications and undergo a washout period of at least 12 weeks before initiation of study treatment. During the study, concomitant use of other oral glucose‐lowering agents, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, and insulin (except for temporary perioperative use) was prohibited. In addition, medications with the potential to affect glycemic control or body weight were not permitted to be newly initiated during the study; however, medications already being used before study entry were allowed, provided that the dosage remained unchanged throughout the study period.

Patients were randomized at a 1:1 ratio to receive either imeglimin 1000 mg or a placebo twice daily, which was added to their baseline DPP‐4 inhibitor treatment during the double‐blind treatment period. In the open‐label extension period (weeks 24–104), all patients received open‐label imeglimin 1000 mg twice daily in addition to the DPP‐4 inhibitor. Consequently, the “early‐start group” received imeglimin for 104 weeks, while the “delayed‐start group” received placebo for 24 weeks followed by imeglimin for 80 weeks. After week 24, patients with HbA1c levels > 9.0% at two consecutive visits were considered eligible for rescue therapy. In such cases, additional oral glucose‐lowering agents (other than DPP‐4 inhibitors) could be initiated at the investigator's discretion.

Data were collected and managed using an electronic data capture system with built‐in logical checks to minimize missing data and data entry errors. To reduce inter‐site variability, HbA1c, the primary endpoint, was measured centrally at a certified laboratory (SRL Inc., Tokyo, Japan).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and registered with the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCTs061210082). The study protocol was approved by the Kawasaki Medical School Clinical Research Review Board, Japan (CRB6200004; reference number 21002‐01). Written informed consent was obtained from all enrolled patients.

Endpoints

Key assessments included HbA1c levels, fasting plasma glucose levels, and safety parameters measured at regular intervals throughout the 104‐week period. The main interest of this long‐term analysis was the change in HbA1c levels from baseline to week 104 in both the early‐start and delayed‐start groups, which was measured to evaluate the durability of glycemic control. Secondary efficacy endpoints included the evaluation of β‐cell function and insulin resistance using a meal tolerance test (MTT) at weeks 0, 24, 52, and 104. To elucidate the long‐term physiological effects of imeglimin and its contribution to glycemic durability, MTT analyses were focused on the early‐start group, in which continuous clinical and physiological data were obtained throughout the entire 104‐week observation period. Parameters calculated using MTT analyses included the area under the curve (AUC) for glucose and insulin levels (0–3 h), Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA‐IR), HOMA‐β, and the Matsuda Index. Safety endpoints included the incidence of AEs over 104 weeks, physical examination findings, and vital signs.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was based on the TIMES 1 study [19], assuming a between‐group difference of 0.50% in HbA1c change at week 24 and a common standard deviation (SD) of 1.00%. Under these assumptions, 86 patients per group were required to achieve 90% power at a two‐sided significance level of 0.05. To account for potential dropout, the target sample size was set at 100 patients per group.

The primary efficacy analysis at week 24 was conducted using a mixed‐effects model for repeated measures. Sensitivity analyses of the primary endpoint were performed using alternative analysis populations and model specifications, including analyses based on the per‐protocol set. For the 104‐week evaluation, observed‐case data without imputation were summarized as secondary analyses, and changes from baseline were analysed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, without using MMRM.

Efficacy and safety analyses were performed in the modified intention‐to‐treat (mITT) population and safety population, respectively. The mITT population comprised all randomized patients who received at least one dose of the study drug and had at least one post‐baseline efficacy value. The safety population included all patients who were administered the study treatment at least once after randomization.

Data are presented as means ± SDs or least squares mean with 95% confidence intervals, as appropriate. Changes in HbA1c levels over time were analysed using summary statistics for the overall population and groups stratified by age (< 65 and ≥ 65 years).

For the MTT parameters in the early‐start group, longitudinal changes from week 0 to weeks 24, 52, and 104 were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and a two‐sided p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Disposition and Baseline Characteristics

Of the 147 patients screened, 117 were randomized to the early‐start (n = 58) or delayed‐start (n = 54) groups (Figure 1B). Among patients in the mITT population (n = 111), 81 (73.0%) completed the entire 104‐week study period (early‐start: 42/57 [73.7%], delayed‐start: 39/54 [72.2%]). Reasons for discontinuation are summarized in Table S1.

Baseline characteristics were generally balanced between the two groups (Table 1). The mean age was 66.8 years in the early‐start group and 65.8 years in the delayed‐start group. The mean duration of diabetes at week 0 was 9.8 and 9.4 years, respectively. Elderly patients (≥ 65 years) comprised more than half of the study population (early‐start: 57.9%, delayed‐start: 57.4%), ensuring a sufficient sample size for age‐stratified analyses. The mean body mass index (BMI) in the early‐start group was 23.9 kg/m2, with 71.9% of patients having a BMI < 25 kg/m2 and 28.1% having that ≥ 25 kg/m2. In the delayed‐start group, the mean BMI was 23.5 kg/m2, with 70.4% of patients having a BMI < 25 kg/m2 and 29.6% having that ≥ 25 kg/m2. The mean HbA1c level at week 0 was 7.35% in the early‐start group and 7.38% in the delayed‐start group.

Long‐Term Glycemic Control (Durability)

In the early‐start group, the significant reduction in HbA1c levels observed at week 24 (−0.65% from baseline) was maintained throughout the 104‐week treatment period. The mean change in HbA1c levels from baseline was −0.65% at week 52 and −0.55% at week 104 (p < 0.001 vs. baseline at all time points) (Figure 2A). In the delayed‐start group, HbA1c levels increased from 7.38% at week 0 to 7.76% during the 24‐week placebo period, followed by a decrease after switching to imeglimin. At weeks 52 and 104, HbA1c levels in the delayed‐start group were 6.74% and 7.09%, respectively, which were comparable to those in the early‐start group (6.71% at week 52 and 6.87% at week 104).

Subgroup Analyses by Age and BMI

In the early‐start group, the mean change in HbA1c levels at week 104 was −0.52% (p = 0.009) in non‐elderly patients (< 65 years) and −0.58% (p < 0.001) in elderly patients (≥ 65 years) (Figure 2B,C). Regarding BMI, subgroup analysis showed no significant difference in HbA1c level changes between patients with a BMI < 25 kg/m2 and those with that ≥ 25 kg/m2.

Target Achievement

In the early‐start group, the proportion of patients achieving the target HbA1c level of < 7.0% was 33.3% at week 0, 67.3% at week 52, and 57.1% at week 104 (Table 2). In the delayed‐start group, the corresponding achievement rates were 33.3% at week 24, 65.9% at week 52, and 56.4% at week 104. Among patients in the elderly subgroup, the achievement rates at weeks 0, 52, and 104 were 39.4%, 69.2%, and 66.7%, respectively, in the early‐start group. The achievement rates for the delayed‐start group at weeks 24, 52, and 104 were 34.5%, 72.7%, and 61.9%, respectively.

Pathophysiological Changes (MTT)

To evaluate the long‐term effects of imeglimin on β‐cell function and insulin resistance, MTT data from the early‐start group (n = 42 at week 104) were analysed (Table S2). The AUC0–3h for blood glucose was significantly lower at weeks 24, 52, and 104 than at week 0 (mean [SD] at week 0, 695.46 [126.45] mg·h/dL; change at week 104, −54.26 mg·h/dL, p = 0.009). Regarding insulin metrics, the Matsuda Index, a marker of whole‐body insulin sensitivity, significantly increased from week 0 (mean [SD], 4.88 [2.94]) to week 104 (change: +0.85, p = 0.002). Concurrently, HOMA‐IR significantly decreased from week 0 (mean [SD] at week 0, 2.91 [1.84]; change at week 104, −0.66, p < 0.001). The mean change in insulin AUC0–3h from week 0 (mean [SD], 86.39 [41.33] μIU·h/mL) to week 104 was −3.28 μIU·h/mL (p = 0.492). HOMA‐β increased from 34.13 (SD, 27.52) at week 0 to 42.54 (SD, 27.00) at week 24 (p < 0.001) and was 37.85 (SD, 27.04) at week 104 (change: +1.23, p = 0.309). Furthermore, the insulinogenic index increased from 0.22 (SD, 0.17) at week 0 to 0.31 (SD, 0.30, p = 0.003) at week 24 and was 0.34 (SD, 0.45, change, +0.11, p = 0.123) at week 104.

Safety

During the 104‐week treatment period, the most frequently reported AEs in the overall population (N = 112) were coronavirus disease (18.8%) and nasopharyngitis (14.3%). Gastrointestinal disorders, including abdominal discomfort (6.3%), nausea (6.3%), diarrhoea (4.5%), vomiting (3.6%), and gastroesophageal reflux disease (3.6%), were reported (Table 3). Hypoglycemia occurred in three patients (2.7%) in the overall safety population. In the subgroup analysis of elderly patients within the early‐start group, the incidence of hypoglycemia was 0% throughout the 104‐week study period. No severe hypoglycemic events were observed.

Discussion

This study provides evidence suggesting the long‐term efficacy and safety of imeglimin add‐on therapy to DPP‐4 inhibitors over a 104‐week period in Japanese patients with T2D. The combination appeared to provide sustained glycemic control over the 2‐year study period, with no clear evidence of attenuation of effect. Notably, in elderly patients (≥ 65 years), whose condition poses a significant management challenge in super‐aging societies, such as the Japanese society, the regimen showed robust efficacy and an excellent safety profile, with no hypoglycemic events reported in the early‐start group throughout the study period.

A key finding of this study was the sustained durability of glycemic control. In the early‐start group, the reduction in HbA1c levels achieved at week 24 was maintained through week 104. The observation that the significant reduction in HbA1c was maintained over 104 weeks, despite the typically progressive nature of T2D, suggests that this combination therapy provided durable glycemic efficacy in this cohort. Although a modest imbalance in sex distribution was observed between the groups, no apparent impact on efficacy outcomes was identified, as subgroup analyses demonstrated that significant reductions in HbA1c were consistently maintained through week 104 in both male and female patients. This durable efficacy contrasts with the progressive loss of efficacy often observed when other insulin secretagogues, such as sulfonylureas, are used. Although such insulin secretagogues primarily stimulate insulin secretion without typically improving insulin sensitivity, they can exhaust β‐cells over time [13, 20]. Our longitudinal analysis of MTT data in the early‐start group revealed that while insulin secretion (HOMA‐β and insulinogenic index) initially increased, it did not continue to rise over the long term. Instead, we observed a significant and progressive improvement in insulin resistance at weeks 52 and 104, as evidenced by both the Matsuda Index and HOMA‐IR. HOMA‐IR primarily reflects hepatic insulin resistance, whereas the Matsuda Index serves as a measure of whole‐body insulin sensitivity, encompassing both hepatic and skeletal muscle tissues. Importantly, glycemic control was maintained despite the numerical decrease in the insulin AUC. These findings suggest a potential reduction in β‐cell workload, possibly secondary to the relief of glucose toxicity and direct improvement in insulin resistance brought about by imeglimin. By ameliorating insulin resistance over time, imeglimin improves the metabolic efficiency of glycemic control, allowing for a lower demand on insulin secretion and thereby potentially promoting β‐cell rest and preserving β‐cell function [21, 22, 23]. Similar improvements in insulin sensitivity were observed in elderly patients, as indicated by significant changes in the Matsuda Index and HOMA‐IR at week 104. These findings support a role for improved insulin sensitivity in maintaining glycemic control in this subgroup. Furthermore, indices of insulin secretion, including HOMA‐β and insulin AUC, did not show significant changes over 104 weeks. This may reflect physiological adaptation, whereby improved insulin sensitivity allows glycemic control to be maintained with lower insulin requirements. These findings may explain the lack of statistically significant changes in insulin secretion indices at later time points. However, these results should be interpreted with caution given the exploratory nature of this subgroup analysis and its small sample size. In addition, the limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant changes.

In contrast to the effects of conventional insulin secretagogues, which often lead to secondary failure [13], the temporal dissociation between the initial increase in insulin secretion and delayed improvement in insulin sensitivity supports a “time‐lag” hypothesis. It is plausible that the distinct mitochondrial mechanism of imeglimin first amplifies GSIS [16], and subsequently, the relief from glucose toxicity combined with direct effects on peripheral tissues leads to a gradual improvement in insulin sensitivity. This suggests that long‐term treatment may be important to fully realize the physiological benefits of imeglimin, particularly in terms of its insulin‐sparing and β‐cell protective properties [24]. Beyond glycemic control, imeglimin may also exert vascular protective effects. Preclinical studies have demonstrated its anti‐atherosclerotic potential [25], which is mechanistically supported by the inhibition of macrophage foam cell formation [26]. Importantly, these findings are consistent with clinical observations of improved endothelial function (flow‐mediated dilation) in patients with T2D [27], suggesting that imeglimin offers cardiovascular benefits distinct from the action of other oral antidiabetic agents.

A paramount concern in the management of T2D in elderly patients is ensuring safety—specifically in terms of preventing hypoglycemia [5, 7, 12]. In this study, elderly patients (≥ 65 years) in the early‐start group achieved stable glycemic control comparable to that in non‐elderly patients, yet experienced no hypoglycemic events over the 104‐week period. Our findings are consistent with recent real‐world evidence demonstrating that the efficacy and safety profile of imeglimin are maintained even in patients aged ≥ 75 years [28]. This safety profile, combined with the mechanism of reducing postprandial glucose excursions (glucose AUC), makes the combination of imeglimin and DPP‐4 inhibitors a physiologically rational and safe option for elderly patients, addressing a significant unmet medical need. Regarding other safety findings, all cases of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) and nasopharyngitis were assessed as unrelated to the study drug. Their relatively high incidence likely reflects the overlap of the study period with the COVID‐19 pandemic in Japan rather than any drug‐related effect. All COVID‐19 cases were mild, and no study treatment discontinuations occurred, suggesting that these events did not materially affect the study outcomes.

From a clinical perspective, a substantial proportion of patients, including elderly individuals, achieved HbA1c levels < 7.0% with a low incidence of hypoglycemia, suggesting that this combination may be a reasonable treatment option without increasing the risk of hypoglycemia. Although a previous report [29] demonstrated greater efficacy in patients with lower BMI and reduced efficacy in patients with obesity with high HOMA‐β values reflecting insulin resistance, comparable target achievement rates were observed in our cohort regardless of BMI (< 25 vs. ≥ 25 kg/m2). Given the small number of obese patients included, these findings should be interpreted cautiously; however, they may suggest potential clinical utility in individuals without obesity with declining β‐cell function.

This study had some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the study design lacked a placebo control group after week 24, and there was no active comparator arm. Therefore, we cannot directly compare the durability or weight changes observed in this study with those produced by other agents, such as sulfonylureas, which are known to cause weight gain and secondary treatment failure. Likewise, comparative effectiveness relative to other standard therapies could not be assessed. Additionally, because the extension phase after week 24 was conducted in an open‐label manner, the influence of non‐pharmacological factors cannot be excluded. Accordingly, causal attribution of the long‐term efficacy findings solely to the study drug is limited. Finally, as the primary endpoint was the change in HbA1c at week 24, the findings at week 104 should be interpreted with caution.

Second, the discontinuation rate was approximately 27% over 2 years. While this is not unexpected in a long‐term study in routine clinical settings when compared with rigorous Phase 3 trials, it may introduce some survivor bias. The number of patients who completed the 104‐week MTT was limited (n = 42), which may affect the robustness of the findings and the interpretation of the results. In addition, given the rate of discontinuation and the reasons for discontinuation, the assumption that data were missing at random may not have been fully satisfied, potentially introducing bias into the long‐term analyses. Third, the study was conducted exclusively in Japanese patients, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. To address these limitations, future studies with longer follow‐up periods, larger sample sizes, and more diverse populations are warranted.

Conclusions

Add‐on imeglimin therapy to DPP‐4 inhibitors appeared to provide sustained glycemic control for 104 weeks in Japanese patients with T2D. The combination was well‐tolerated in elderly patients, with no observed increase in severe hypoglycemia. The long‐term durability of efficacy may be driven by an insulin‐sparing mechanism, in which initial improvements in secretion are followed by progressive gains in insulin sensitivity, thereby reducing the secretory burden on β‐cells. These findings suggest that imeglimin may represent a useful long‐term therapeutic partner to DPP‐4 inhibitors, particularly for treating the aging patient population with T2D.
