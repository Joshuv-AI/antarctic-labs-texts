Pulmonary Tumor Thrombotic Microangiopathy With Biventricular Heart Failure
Authors: Yutaka Mihara, Daisuke Miyahara, Norio Suzuki, Yasuhiro Tanabe, Tomoko Norose, Chie Okawa, Naoki Shimada, Ruka Aoki, Yoshihiro J Akashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary tumor thrombotic microangiopathy (PTTM), a rare and often fatal condition characterized by tumor cell embolization within pulmonary arterioles, is frequently diagnosed using pulmonary artery aspiration cytology. PTTM is associated with poor prognosis.Case SummaryA 51-year-old woman with ovarian cancer presented with biventricular heart failure and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. Right heart catheterization showed a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 34 mm Hg, consistent with combined pre- and postcapillary pulmonary hypertension. Despite these findings, pulmonary artery aspiration cytology confirmed the diagnosis of PTTM. The patient showed clinical improvement after heart failure management and chemotherapy.DiscussionThis case highlights the potential for underdiagnosing PTTM in patients with malignancies in whom pulmonary hypertension appears to be attributable to heart failure. The clinical relevance and diagnostic considerations of PTTM in such cases warrant further investigation.Take-Home MessagePTTM with mild or atypical hemodynamic manifestations may be under-recognized in clinical practice.Key Words: ovarian cancer, pulmonary artery aspiration cytology, pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary tumor thrombotic microangiopathy

History of Presentation

A 51-year-old woman with no specific medical history presented to our general internal medicine outpatient clinic with symptoms of exertional dyspnea, bilateral leg edema, and abdominal distention, which had worsened 2 months before her visit. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a huge mass with a diameter of 44 cm in the pelvis. Bilateral pleural effusion and ascites were also observed. The patient was referred to our obstetrics and gynecology department for consideration of surgery. Eight days after the initial visit, left salpingo-oophorectomy was performed. The postoperative course was unremarkable, and the patient was discharged on the 11th day after surgery. The pathology results showed clear cell carcinoma of the ovary; therefore, the patient was scheduled for radical surgery after evaluation of distant metastasis. After discharge, the patient experienced worsening exertional dyspnea and was admitted to our department for further evaluation. On examination, the blood pressure was 123/89 mm Hg and SpO2 was 98% (room air). Regular tachycardia (heart rate, 100 beats/min) was noted. Marked edema was observed in the extremities.

Key Echocardiographic and Cytological Findings During the Clinical Course

At the time of admission, the patient's body weight was 65.5 kg. Echocardiography revealed an LVEF of 33% and a TRPG of 36.7 mm Hg. On hospital day 4, right heart catheterization and pulmonary artery aspiration cytology were performed, leading to a subsequent diagnosis of pulmonary tumor thrombotic microangiopathy. The patient was discharged on hospital day 29 weighing 49.6 kg. Follow-up echocardiography on day 85 showed an improvement in LVEF to 48% and TRPG to 19.2 mm Hg, indicating a favorable outcome. LVEF = left ventricular ejection fraction; PAC = pulmonary artery aspiration cytology; RHC = right heart catheterization; TRPG = tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient.

Past Medical History

The patient had no history of smoking or alcohol use and a medical history of deep vein thrombosis during the perioperative period of ovarian cancer.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included heart failure, malignant peritonitis, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (PH), liver cirrhosis, and malnutrition.

Investigations

Electrocardiography revealed sinus tachycardia and low voltage in the limb leads. Blood tests showed anemia (hemoglobin 9.3 mg/dL), iron deficiency (serum iron 25 μg/dL, total iron-binding capacity 406 μg/dL, and ferritin 17.9 ng/mL), normal liver and kidney function (aspartate aminotransferase 22 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 16 U/L, and creatinine 0.71 mg/dL), normal serum albumin levels and thyroid function (albumin 3.0 g/dL, thyroid-stimulating hormone 6.27 μIU/mL) and elevated N-terminal fragment of brain natriuretic peptide precursor (3,909 pg/mL), D-dimer (7.0 μg/mL), and elevated tumor markers (CA125 341 U/mL). Ascitic fluid cytology revealed no findings suggestive of malignancy. We suspected congestive heart failure, and the echocardiogram (Figure 1) revealed left ventricular (LV) dysfunction (LV ejection fraction 33%), biventricular enlargement (LV end-diastolic volume/end-systolic volume 163/110 mL and mid–right ventricular [RV] diameter 41.7 mm), and PH (tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient 36.7 mm Hg). RV systolic function was assessed using tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (20.5 mm) and RV fractional area change (26%). McConnell's sign was not observed (Videos 1 and 2). The CT scan before left salpingo-oophorectomy had revealed RV enlargement (Figure 2). Therefore, we suspected that the patient had a preoperative condition that caused RV enlargement and that the removal of the ovarian tumor relieved the physical compression of the inferior vena cava and increased venous perfusion. Given that the patient was diagnosed with cancer and had RV enlargement before surgery, the possibility of underlying PH was considered. Previous CT images had no findings consistent with pulmonary embolism. A pulmonary perfusion scan was performed (Figure 1), which showed diffuse hypoperfusion in the peripheral lung fields. Positron emission tomography-CT was also performed to search for metastatic lesions of ovarian cancer. No clear metastasis was observed, and there was no significant uptake in the lungs. To further investigate the circulatory dynamics, we performed right heart catheterization (RHC), which revealed a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) of 34 mm Hg, pulmonary artery wedge pressure of 24 mm Hg, cardiac output of 4.78 L/min, pulmonary vascular resistance of 2.0 Wood units, and pulmonary artery saturation of 71.7%, indicating mixed pre- and postcapillary PH. Based on reports of PH in patients with carcinoma, pulmonary tumor thrombotic microangiopathy (PTTM) was raised as a differential diagnosis. Therefore, we conducted pulmonary artery aspiration cytology (PAC) during the catheterization. Coronary angiography was performed to investigate the cause of LV dysfunction; however, no significant stenosis was found. A subsequent cardiac magnetic resonance with late gadolinium enhancement demonstrated faint, diffuse, midmyocardial enhancement in the LV, indicative of myocardial scar formation (Figure 3). Finally, the PAC revealed cells consistent with adenocarcinomas (Figure 4). Based on the patient's medical history, presentation, and findings, a diagnosis of PTTM secondary to ovarian cancer was established.

Management

Figure 5 shows an overview of the patient's clinical course. Therapeutic interventions included the use of diuretics to control fluid overload and cardioprotective agents to address heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction. The treatment of PTTM requires prompt initiation of chemotherapy; therefore, paclitaxel and carboplatin were administered on the 26th day after admission before the scheduled surgery.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's heart failure was compensated for and her general condition improved until she was able to walk without difficulty. The patient was discharged home on day 29 after chemotherapy. Follow-up echocardiography 85 days after the initial admission showed enhanced LV systolic function (LV ejection fraction 48%), improvement of PH (tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient 19.2 mm Hg), and a reduction in biventricular dimensions (LV end-diastolic volume 115 mL, LV end-systolic volume 60 mL, and mid-RV diameter 35.7 mm). After a 233-day interval following the initial oophorectomy, the patient underwent radical surgery for ovarian clear-cell carcinoma, including total hysterectomy, bilateral adnexectomy, lymph node dissection, and mastectomy. As of the last postoperative follow-up, the patient has been doing well.

Discussion

PTTM, first described in 1990, is a lethal condition characterized by the embolization of tumor cells into the pulmonary arterioles, resulting in subsequent vascular endothelial remodeling and irreversible PH.1 The rapid progression and poor prognosis associated with this disease make antemortem diagnosis highly challenging.2 Since the 1985 report demonstrating the ability of PAC to detect tumor cell invasion into the pulmonary circulation,3 a growing number of case reports have described the diagnosis of PTTM in patients with cancer with PH. These reports describe using PAC during RHC to assess the hemodynamics of patients with severe PH, marked by an mPAP >40 mm Hg.4, 5, 6, 7 Prompt diagnosis, followed by early administration of treatment, with a focus on chemotherapy, is critical for enhancing prognosis.8

Here, we presented a case of PH in a patient with ovarian cancer. Initially, given the presence of LV systolic dysfunction, postcapillary PH was attributed as secondary to increased venous return after massive tumor resection. However, a retrospective review of preoperative imaging revealed preexisting RV dilatation, suggesting that another etiology contributed to RV dilatation before surgery.

Subsequent RHC demonstrated an mPAP of 34 mm Hg, with a pulmonary vascular resistance of 2.0 Wood units, and a pulmonary artery wedge pressure of 24 mm Hg, suggesting predominantly postcapillary PH and only a minor precapillary component, consistent with heart failure as the primary etiology. However, given the patient's underlying malignancy, PTTM was considered, and PAC yielded a definitive diagnosis. The patient showed a favorable response to heart failure therapy, including diuretics and cardioprotective agents. Although PH was considered to be primarily due to left heart failure, the possibility of PTTM-related PH warranted close monitoring and the initiation of chemotherapy.

This case study presents 2 key points for discussion. First, PTTM may be underdiagnosed in patients with cancer with only mild PH. In the present case, the PH severity was lower than that in typical patients with PTTM. Although the precise contribution of PTTM to PH development remains uncertain, heart failure was considered the primary factor. A previous report described the case of a patient who had undergone gastric cancer surgery 3 years previously. A pulmonary nodule led to a lung biopsy and a subsequent diagnosis of PTTM. An RHC was not performed and echocardiography had not revealed PH. The patient achieved favorable outcomes with anticoagulant and anticancer therapies.8 This case, similar to the present case, is noteworthy for the differential diagnosis of PTTM in the absence of severe PH. The present patient's PH improved with standard heart failure treatment. The potential prognostic significance of diagnosing PTTM with subtle hemodynamic effects warrants further investigation. Second, the etiology of the reduced LV systolic function in this case warrants further consideration. The development of biventricular heart failure symptoms after ovarian cancer surgery in the present patient was likely influenced by the preexisting reduced LV systolic function, necessitating further investigation. To our knowledge, no previous reports have described cases of PTTM complicated by reduced LV systolic function. Cardiac contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance revealed mild late gadolinium enhancement in the midmyocardium, raising the possibility of underlying cardiomyopathy. Although the etiology of myocardial damage, specifically whether the damage was preexisting or secondary to PTTM, was the central focus of our study, a comprehensive work-up, including blood tests for secondary cardiomyopathies, did not reveal significant abnormalities. Given the improvement in cardiac function after the administration of cardioprotective agents, a myocardial biopsy was not performed. Therefore, further research is needed to elucidate the interrelationships among malignancies, PH, and reduced LV systolic function.

In conclusion, this case report underscores the potential for PTTM to be in patients with cancer, particularly those with gastric, breast, lung, urothelial, or ovarian cancers. Given that PTTM is a recognized complication in these populations, a finding of even mild PH should prompt a high index of suspicion for the condition. The more proactive use of lung perfusion scintigraphy could enable a more accurate determination of its indication, leading to earlier diagnosis of PTTM. This, in turn, may significantly influence treatment strategies and improve patient prognosis. However, the clinical implications of diagnosing PTTM with subtle hemodynamic effects and optimal management strategy for these patients require further investigation.
