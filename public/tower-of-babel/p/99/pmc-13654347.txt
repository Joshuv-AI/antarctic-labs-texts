Prevalence, Health Care Burden, and Longitudinal Outcomes of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Among People With Heart Failure
Authors: Tauben Averbuch, Brandon Zagorski, Joan Carles Trullàs, Lehana Thabane, Harriette G.C. Van Spall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are common comorbidities. We aimed to assess health care resource use and outcomes associated with COPD in patients hospitalized for HF.MethodsThis was an exploratory cohort analysis of patients hospitalized for HF and enrolled in the Patient-Centered Care Transitions in HF cluster-randomized trial. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included all-cause and HF hospitalization, health care resource use, and direct health care costs. Survival was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for baseline variables.ResultsAmong 4441 patients discharged alive, 1136 (25.6%) had COPD at baseline. All-cause mortality was higher at 5 years among patients with vs without COPD (75.2% vs 60.9%, adjusted hazard ratio 1.29, 95% confidence interval 1.19-1.40). Patients with COPD had more frequent all-cause rehospitalizations (mean [standard deviation] 3.4 [6.6] vs 2.7 [8.6] per patient, P = 0.01), but no significant difference in HF rehospitalizations (0.9 [2.3] vs 0.9 [4.5] per patient, P = 0.74). Patients with COPD had more annual physician visits, multidisciplinary clinic visits, and supportive care (all P < 0.01), as well as greater annual direct health care costs in Canadian dollars (at 5 years: $91,888 [$109,319] vs $76,823 [$116,218]) than those without COPD, primarily driven by rehospitalizations.ConclusionsAmong patients discharged alive after hospitalization for HF, COPD is common and is associated with higher all-cause mortality and all-cause rehospitalizations, greater health care use, and higher direct health care costs. Interventions to reduce the health care burden in HF should include broad approaches that address important comorbidities.

Heart failure (HF) is a leading cause of mortality, morbidity, and health care system costs, affecting 55.4 million people worldwide.1 After hospitalization for HF, patients face a risk of death at 5 years that exceeds several cancers.2 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a relatively common comorbidity in people with HF, but its role in prognosis and health care use among people with HF has not been explored.2,3 COPD is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.4,5 COPD affects approximately 10% of adults ≥ 40 years of age6 and, like HF, is associated with functional impairment from dyspnea, a waxing-and-waning course, and overall decline in health status. Both diagnoses are listed among the top drivers of health care system costs in high-income countries.4,5

The coexistence of COPD and HF has been reported to range from 7% to 42%, possibly due to shared risk factors and overlapping mechanisms.7, 8, 9 The coexistence of COPD in people with HF may portend a worse prognosis than either condition alone.10, 11, 12 Yet, care for each condition is delivered in organ-specific siloes.

There are limited data on the long-term clinical outcomes and the health care system burden of COPD in patients hospitalized for HF. In this exploratory analysis of the pragmatic Patient-Centered Care Transitions in Heart Failure (PACT-HF) trial,13,14 we describe the long-term clinical outcomes, health care use, and direct health care costs in patients after hospitalization for HF.

Methods

The PACT-HF multicenter, stepped-wedge, randomized controlled trial tested the effect of a transitional care bundle on patients who were discharged after hospitalization for HF in 2015-2016 in Canada; the intervention had a neutral effect on the primary clinical outcomes.2,13, 14, 15 In the trial, HF was defined using the validated Boston criteria or natriuretic peptide levels.13 We performed an exploratory cohort analysis of patients enrolled in the trial, with a follow-up period of 5 years. The PACT-HF trial was approved by the research ethics boards of all participating institutions.

Outcomes

The primary outcome for this analysis was all-cause mortality. Secondary clinical outcomes included all-cause rehospitalization, HF rehospitalization, and all-cause emergency department (ED) visits not resulting in hospitalization. We also reported health care resource use, accounting for clinical encounters (both, inpatient and outpatient); cardiac diagnostic investigations; invasive procedures; and supportive care, defined as rehabilitation, long-term care, complex continuing care, and home care. All outcomes were determined relative to date of discharge from the index hospitalization.

Data collection

We identified clinical outcomes and resource use through linkages to administrative databases accessed at the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences (ICES).14 Health care encounters were identified using linkages to administrative databases that capture all publicly funded episodes of care within Ontario over each patient’s lifespan using unique health care numbers without loss to follow-up.

Baseline comorbidities were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 codes from a 5-year lookback of Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) claims and the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) Discharge Abstract Database (DAD) using the ICD and Related Health Problems, 10th revision, Canada (ICD-10-CA). Comorbid COPD was identified based on at least 1 hospitalization for COPD or at least 3 OHIP claims for COPD over a 2-year period.16 Inpatient episodes of care were identified using the DAD and the CIHI National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS) was used to identify ED visits. Deaths were identified using the Ontario Registered Persons Database (RPDB).14 Outpatient and inpatient physician services relating to patient visits or the delivery of diagnostic and invasive care were identified using OHIP reimbursement claims. Outpatient medication dispensations were identified using the Ontario Drug Benefits (ODB) database, which provides coverage to low-income patients and those > 65 years of age. We recorded guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) classes with regulatory approval for HF dispensed during the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics, expressed as numbers and percentages for categorical variables, means with standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, and median with interquartile range (IQR) for non–normally distributed continuous variables, were obtained. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 statistic and continuous variables were compared using the Student t test or Wilcoxon test for normally and non–normally distributed variables, respectively.

Patients were followed in 1-year increments for up to 5 years or death, with observation time censored at death for those who died before the end of the follow-up period. The index hospitalization was not included in our analysis of clinical events. ED visits were limited to unscheduled visits that did not result in hospitalization. We identified total resource use per patient during a given year of follow-up. Resource use was annualized and, for patients who died prior to the end of a given year of follow-up, the number of days alive was used to calculate annual usage.

All-cause mortality was defined as the proportion of those deceased and events per 100 years, with the log-rank test used for comparisons. The total number of years alive among patients discharged alive (mean and SD) was identified. We used Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models, stratified by baseline COPD status, adjusting for relevant baseline characteristics including sex, age at index hospitalization, neighborhood income quintile, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of myocardial infarction (MI), history of gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, liver disease, cancer, peripheral vascular disease, and prior stroke. Estimates are presented as hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI).

We identified the proportion of patients rehospitalized and the total number of rehospitalizations for any cause and for HF. We also obtained data on health resource use, which included rehospitalizations, ED visits not requiring hospitalization, invasive procedures, ambulatory care visits, diagnostic tests, palliative care, home visits, rehabilitation, and long-term care. Finally, we identified the proportion of patients prescribed each class of GDMT at 30 and 365 days, as well as the association between baseline COPD and prescriptions for each class of GDMT using negative binomial models, adjusted for sex, age at index hospitalization, neighborhood income quintile, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of MI, history of GI bleeding, liver disease, cancer, peripheral vascular disease, and prior stroke.

We performed a descriptive analysis of direct health care costs, adjusted for inflation and reported in Canadian dollars. Total and annualized estimates for health outcomes, resource use, and costs were assessed. All statistical analysis were performed using SAS version 9.4 for Unix (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between February 1, 2015, and March 30, 2016, we included a total of 4441 patients (50.7% female) hospitalized for HF across the 10 participating hospitals. Clinical outcomes

Primary clinical outcome

At 5 years, all-cause mortality was high and occurred in 854 of 1136 (75.2%) patients with COPD and in 2012 of 3305 (60.9%) patients without COPD (P < 0.01) (Central Illustration, Fig. 1, and Table 2). Patients with COPD also had a higher proportion of in-hospital deaths vs those without COPD (40.9% vs 34.7%, respectively, P < 0.01). Patients with COPD had significantly lower survival vs patients without COPD (mean [SD] 2.48 [1.78] vs 2.98 [1.93] years, P < 0.01) (Table 2). The proportion of annual deaths was greatest in the first year after index hospitalization (32.6% and 24.2% for patients with and without COPD, respectively) and remained consistently higher in patients with vs without COPD (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). At 5 years, COPD was associated with a 43% greater crude risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.32-1.55, P < 0.01) and a 29% greater adjusted risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.19-1.40, P < 0.01).Central IllustrationClinical outcomes and health care resource use after hospitalization for HF in patients with vs without COPD. Patients with COPD received more home care visits (mean [SD] 74.6 [99.0] vs 57.3 [96.0] visits, P < 0.01) and had longer stays in inpatient rehabilitation (5.0 [25.4] vs 3.0 [17.3] days, P < 0.01). However, there was no significant difference in length of stay in long-term care facilities between patients with and without COPD (mean [SD] 39.2 [99.6] vs 33.9 [92.9] days, P = 0.10).

A smaller proportion of patients with baseline COPD were dispensed beta-blockers at both 30 days (56.2% vs 61.4%, P = 0.01) and 1 year (74.0% vs 81.6%, P < 0.01) than patients without baseline COPD. However, after adjustment for relevant baseline characteristics, there was no significant association between baseline COPD and dispensation of GDMT (including beta-blockers) at 30 days or 1 year (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Health care resource costs

Patients with COPD had significantly higher annual direct health care costs vs those without COPD, both at 1 year (mean [SD] $84,365 [$110,515] vs $72,415 [$117,054]) and 5 years ($91,888 [$109,319] vs $76,823 [$116,218]) after HF hospitalization (Table 5 and Fig. 3). The most substantial cost driver was rehospitalization, which accounted for approximately half the total annual costs in patients with and without COPD; this proportion would be much higher if inpatient physician costs were taken into consideration. Discussion

In this exploratory analysis of the PACT-HF trial, we found that, among patients hospitalized for HF, 25.6% of patients had COPD at baseline. Patients with COPD had a 29% greater adjusted risk of all-cause mortality and a 0.50-year shorter survival than those without COPD. All-cause, but not HF, rehospitalizations were more frequent among patients with baseline COPD; rehospitalizations were largely for causes other than HF, even more so among those with COPD. Patients with COPD had longer lengths of inpatient stay; more physician visits, multidisciplinary clinic visits, and home care visits; and higher annual direct health care costs over a 5-year follow-up period. Our findings are unique in providing a comprehensive view---both from a patient and health care system perspective---on the long-term burden of COPD among those hospitalized for HF. The detailed breakdown of cost drivers, including outpatient services and home care, provides valuable insights for payers and policymakers.

Average survival in patients with COPD was only 2.48 years, 0.50 years shorter than those without COPD (P < 0.01), and, after adjusting for baseline demographics, socioeconomic status, and comorbidities, COPD was associated with a 29% increased risk of death relative to no COPD. The annual risk of death was greatest in the first year after hospitalization. The association between COPD and death in patients with HF has varied across studies, likely due to the underlying characteristics of patients.17, 18, 19, 20 In the explanatory Efficacy of Vasopressin Antagonism in Heart Failure Outcome Study With Tolvaptan (EVEREST) of 4133 patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) of ≤ 40%, only 10% had COPD and there was only a numeric association between COPD and all-cause mortality in the multivariable adjusted analysis (HR 1.17, 95% CI 0.96-1.42).21 The mean follow-up in the EVEREST study was 9.9 months, much shorter than in our study. In addition, noncardiac causes contribute relatively infrequently to all-cause death in HFrEF; our cohort included patients across the LVEF continuum.22 In a meta-analysis of 7121 HF inpatients and outpatients across the continuum of LVEF,23 COPD was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality than no COPD (adjusted HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.21-1.6). In an analysis of the Norwegian Heart Failure Registry that enrolled both ambulatory and hospitalized patients with a new diagnosis of HF across the LVEF continuum, COPD was associated with a 19% higher adjusted risk of death after a mean follow-up of 13.3 months (adjusted HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.02-1.39).24 Our follow-up duration was longer and restricted to a higher risk group of patients hospitalized for HF.

We found that patients with COPD experienced higher rates of all-cause rehospitalizations (3.4 vs 2.7 per patient, P = 0.01), with a greater proportion rehospitalized multiple (≥ 3) times (51.6% vs 39.5%) vs those without COPD. HF accounted for a small proportion of these rehospitalizations and the number of HF rehospitalizations per patient was not different between groups, highlighting the burden of noncardiac comorbidities among patients with baseline COPD. Although select drug trials reported associations between COPD and HF hospitalization, trial participants were predominantly younger ambulatory patients with HFrEF and enriched for HF events, in contrast to our trial population.10,11,19 In machine-learning analyses, COPD was shown to be a distinct phenogroup associated with the highest risk of all-cause death or hospitalization postdischarge than other phenogroups.25 In an analysis of 225,160 patients hospitalized for HF and discharged alive, COPD was associated with a greater risk of 90-day all-cause (HR 2.12, 95% CI 2.07-2.16) and respiratory (HR 3.08, 95% CI 2.90-3.29) rehospitalizations.26

Our study is unique in describing the health care resource use associated with COPD among patients hospitalized for HF. We found that, during the 5-year follow-up, patients with COPD had significantly greater health care use vs those without COPD, with longer cumulative lengths of inpatient stays (hospital and rehabilitation) and more frequent inpatient and ambulatory physician visits. The home care and inpatient rehabilitation costs suggest a greater need for ongoing support and postdischarge recovery in COPD patients, possibly due to higher levels of frailty, functional impairment, or multimorbidity.27,28 These findings emphasize the need for targeted care strategies and improved coordination of health care services to address the high health care demands and resource use in this population.

Our findings demonstrate the significant financial burden associated with COPD in people hospitalized for HF. Direct health care costs were significantly higher in patients with COPD, both at 1 year and 5 years after hospitalization. The costs among patients hospitalized for HF with comorbid COPD have not been described previously; however, among patients with COPD, comorbid cardiovascular disease is a known driver of health care costs. In a cohort of 4594 patients with COPD, the annual direct health care costs were significantly higher among patients with comorbid cardiovascular disease ($22,755 vs $8036).29 The substantially higher readmission costs for COPD highlights a critical opportunity for cost reduction through targeted interventions aimed at preventing rehospitalizations. Addressing comorbidities, optimizing GDMT for both HF and COPD, and ensuring reliable follow-up could potentially reduce readmissions and associated costs by reducing reliance on acute care services.30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35 Infections are known to be a common cause of readmissions, and focused efforts to increase the uptake of influenza and pneumococcal vaccines may be useful in people with COPD given their predilection to pulmonary infections.35

Previous studies have drawn attention to the underuse of GDMT among several demographic populations in observational studies and clinical trials, particularly among older adults, women, and those from racial or ethnic minority groups.36 Although beta-blockers had lower uptake in those with COPD, we found that, after adjusting for baseline comorbidities, there was no difference in the use of any classes of GDMT, including beta-blockers, among those with vs without COPD.

Our study has several strengths, including the pragmatic eligibility criteria (and therefore generalizable data), large sample size, multicenter inclusion, and complete follow-up. Data collection occurred via linkages between clinical trial and administrative data sets using unique identifiers that remain consistent through to death, allowing for complete data collection and follow-up through to death. Historically underrepresented groups were well-represented in this trial, with 50% of women and a high proportion of patients with complex comorbidities.13,14,37,38 There are important limitations to acknowledge. Our data set did not include information on race or ethnicity that can influence outcomes after hospitalization for HF.39 Although we collected patient-reported outcomes in this trial, we did not have access to data on pulmonary symptom burden, pulmonary function tests, or Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) stage, which could have provided a deeper understanding of the relationship between COPD severity and outcomes.40 Furthermore, we did not collect data on tobacco use or frailty, which may partly account for the excess risk associated with COPD.41 Case report forms were limited in size to avoid research burden on trial sites and patients, and data collection was designed around the primary trial hypothesis. As a result, we could not account for differences in pulmonary pressures or right heart function in our analysis. We did not stratify our analysis by ejection fraction (reduced or preserved), because ejection fraction was only available for a subset of patients; the burden of comorbid COPD may have differed between patients with preserved and reduced ejection fraction.42,43 However, COPD is associated with all-cause death or rehospitalization independent of ejection fraction.44 Lastly, given the study period, there was limited use of sacubitril-valsartan and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors. The study was conducted within a single-payer health care system in Canada, and costs may differ in other types of health care systems. Although our analysis has captured direct health care costs based on provincial claims, it did not include indirect costs such as loss of productivity or out-of-pocket expenses.

Conclusions

In this study we have highlighted the substantial clinical and economic burden of COPD in patients hospitalized for HF. Among patients hospitalized for HF, comorbid COPD is associated with higher adjusted risk of mortality, shorter lifespan, more frequent all-cause readmissions (with only a small proportion due to HF), increased health care resource use, and greater direct costs, primarily due to rehospitalization.
