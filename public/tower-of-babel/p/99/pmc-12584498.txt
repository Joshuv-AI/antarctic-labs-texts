Does behaving green influence how we feel? pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being
Authors: Mariana Pinho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe climate crisis is the most pressing crisis of our time, requiring urgent mitigation measures at all levels of society. Among those is the need to increase pro-environmental behaviours.MethodsThe current study examined the role of social-psychological characteristics on pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being, using a nationally representative sample.ResultsResults indicated that environmental identity, climate change perceptions and direct experience of climate change were positively related to pro-environmental behaviours. More specifically, individuals with stronger environmental identity and heightened perceptions of climate change reported more frequent conservation behaviours (e.g., reducing heating, lighting, or energy use), greater engagement in sustainable transportation behaviours (e.g., carpooling, walking, or using public transportation), and higher likelihood of adopting food-related behaviours (e.g., reducing meat consumption and increasing vegetarian meals). Climate anxiety was positively related to all three pro-environmental categories of behaviours examined. Finally, engaging in these pro-environmental behaviours was related to higher levels of positive affect, negatively related to negative affect. Those relations were mediated by climate anxiety.ConclusionOverall, the findings offer insights into how to motivate climate action to mitigate the impacts of climate change.Keywords: Pro-environmental behaviour, Climate change anxiety, Environmental identity, Well-being, Climate change

Introduction

The climate crisis stands as the most urgent challenge of our time, requiring swift and effective strategies for mitigation and adaptation across all societal levels, from government policies to individual lifestyle choices. Among these strategies, encouraging sustainable and pro-environmental behaviours has become increasingly important (e.g. 1; 2), particularly as they remain uncommon in many countries (3). Pro-environmental behaviours refer to intentional actions taken by individuals to reduce or minimise their negative impact on the environment (4; 5). Examples include recycling, reducing water or energy consumption, opting for sustainable food choices, and using alternative modes of transportation instead of personal vehicles. Several theoretical perspectives have been developed to explain why individuals engage in such behaviours. Stern’s Value-Belief-Norm theory highlights the role of personal norms, activated by environmental values and beliefs, as drivers of pro-environmental behaviour [1]. Similarly, environmental identity theory [2, 3] suggests that the extent to which individuals see themselves as interconnected with the natural environment strongly predicts sustainable action. Relatedly, research on connectedness to nature, defined as “an individual’s experiential sense of oneness with the natural world” [4], has consistently found positive associations with pro-environmental behaviour across cultural contexts [5, 6]. This construct provides a psychological foundation for environmental concern, motivation, and stewardship.

Another construct of increasing relevance is climate change anxiety, commonly described as the emotional distress and worry about the anticipated impacts of climate change [3]. According to [7] climate change anxiety should not be seen only as pathological but also as a potentially adaptive response that can motivate engagement in climate action. Supporting this, recent research shows that climate anxiety is often positively associated with pro-environmental behaviour [8]. Thus, considering both connectedness to nature and climate anxiety is crucial for understanding how psychological factors shape environmental action. Despite their potential benefits, pro-environmental behaviours are often portrayed in public opinion and the media as inconvenient or detrimental to personal well-being [9, 10]. The scientific literature on the connection between pro-environmental behaviours and subjective well-being, however, presents mixed findings. While several studies report a positive association between pro-environmental behaviours, subjective well-being, and life satisfaction (e.g [9–12]). , others found a negative or negligible link (e.g [13, 14]). Moreover, engagement in pro-environmental behaviours varies widely across countries and cultures, underscoring the importance of context-specific research (e.g [15, 16]). Given this complexity, it is essential to explore further how social-psychological factors influence both pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being. This study aims to do so by drawing on data from a nationally representative sample of Portugal. It investigates how individuals’ sense of connectedness to nature, along with their knowledge and personal experience of climate change, shape their pro-environmental behaviours. By addressing these, the current study contributes valuable insights to an evolving field and helps inform strategies for encouraging climate action that also enhance well-being.

Pro-environmental behaviour and individual-level factors

Sociodemographic factors

Existing research has highlighted several sociodemographic variables that influence engagement in pro-environmental behaviours. Characteristics such as age, educational attainment, income level, and political orientation have all been linked to differences in environmental behaviour. In particular, individuals with more left-leaning political views are consistently found to be more likely to adopt environmentally friendly practices (e.g [17–20]). , . Similarly, higher levels of education are often associated with increased pro-environmental actions (e.g [18, 21, 22]). , .

However, the relationships between age, household income, and environmental behaviour are less clear-cut. Some studies suggest that younger individuals and those with higher income levels are more likely to engage in sustainable practices (e.g [18, 21–24]). , . In contrast, other research indicates the opposite, associating higher income with reduced environmental behaviour (e.g [25, 26]). , and showing inconsistent findings regarding age (e.g [26–29]). , .

Social-psychological factors

In addition to sociodemographic influences, numerous social-psychological factors have been found to positively correlate with and predict pro-environmental behaviour. One such factor is environmental identity, which refers to the extent of a person’s emotional and cognitive connection to the non-human environment [2, 30]. Environmental identity has been positively related to pro-environmental behaviours and efforts to prevent climate change (e.g [5, 6, 30, 31]). The same is true for a higher connectedness to nature, frequent contact and experiences in nature [6, 32–34]. Several studies further emphasise environmental identity as a significant predictor of pro-environmental behaviour (e.g [35–37]).

Another important factor is climate change perceptions, that is, seeing climate change as real, caused by humans and having negative consequences. These perceptions have been found to correlate with and predict pro-environmental behaviour ( [38–41]). For instance, dietary and transportation choices are often related to environmental attitudes [42, 43]. Building on this, research further shows that when people directly experience climate change, or perceive it as a proximal threat, they are even more likely to engage in pro-environmental behaviour [44–46].

It is therefore hypothesised that environmental identity (H1a), climate change perceptions (H1b) and direct experience of climate change (H1c) will be positively related to pro-environmental behaviours. This hypothesis builds on environmental identity theory [2], which suggests that individuals who see their self-concept as closely tied to the natural environment are more likely to act in ways that protect it. Previous research consistently demonstrates that a stronger environmental identity predicts a higher likelihood of engaging in conservation, sustainable transportation, and pro-environmental dietary choices [5, 6]. Similarly, perceiving climate change as real, human-caused, and harmful is a robust predictor of sustainable behaviour [38, 40]. Finally, prior experiences of climate-related events increase the salience of climate risks, making individuals more likely to engage in mitigation behaviours [45, 46].

Pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being

One psychological consequence of climate change is climate anxiety, which refers to the emotional distress individuals experience in response to the current and anticipated effects of climate change [3, 47]. This form of anxiety has been increasingly recognised in the literature as a significant mental health concern (e.g [48]). Interestingly, some research has found that climate anxiety may lead to pro-environmental behaviour (e.g [8, 49]). Hence, it is hypothesised that climate anxiety is predicted to be positively related to pro-environmental behaviours (H2). Climate change anxiety has emerged as an increasingly recognised emotional response to the climate crisis [48]. While anxiety is often framed negatively, research shows that it can also have adaptive qualities, serving as a motivator for protective action [7, 8]. Recent studies demonstrate that individuals experiencing higher climate-related worry or distress are more likely to adopt pro-environmental behaviours [49]. Thus, climate anxiety is expected to positively predict engagement in sustainable actions.

An expanding body of research has also examined the link between pro-environmental behaviours and subjective well-being, though findings remain mixed. Several studies found pro-environmental behaviour to be associated with greater subjective well-being (e.g [9, 11, 50]). This means that the “greener” behaviours relate to higher self-evaluation of one’s well-being and life, including cognitive understanding and emotional responses to life (e.g [51–53]). Research suggests that including pro-environmental behaviours into people’s daily routines enhances their sense of happiness, meaning in life and well-being (e.g [11, 54]). A possible explanation for this relation is that one feels good about themselves when they do something with a positive impact on the environment (e.g [55, 56]). As some of the behaviours also have a social component (e.g. carpooling), leading people to feel more connected to the community (e.g [11, 57]). Nonetheless, not all research aligns with these positive outcomes. Some studies found divergent results, suggesting that engaging in pro-environmental behaviour may not always translate to improved well-being (e.g [13, 14]).

Based on the rationale detailed above, pro-environmental behaviour is predicted to be related to higher levels of subjective well-being. Specifically, it will positively relate to life satisfaction (H3a) and positive affect (H3b) and negatively relate to negative affect (H3c). Several studies have found that engaging in sustainable behaviours contributes to greater subjective well-being by fostering a sense of purpose, alignment with personal values, and feelings of social connectedness [11, 12]. For example, participating in collective practices like carpooling can enhance community belonging, while adopting conservation behaviours can strengthen self-image as a morally responsible person [55]. Conversely, failure to act in accordance with one’s environmental values may generate dissonance and negative affect. Therefore, pro-environmental behaviour is expected to enhance well-being.

Finally, the relationship between pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being will be mediated by climate change anxiety (H4). This hypothesis is grounded in research showing that emotional responses to climate change can shape how behaviour impacts well-being. While climate anxiety can increase distress in the short term, it may also channel concern into constructive behavioural responses that, in turn, reduce feelings of helplessness and enhance positive emotions [7]. Recent findings suggest that climate anxiety plays a dual role: it intensifies negative affect but can also foster meaning-making and “agency-enhancing” behaviours that promote positive affect and well-being [8]. In this study, it is therefore hypothesised that climate anxiety mediates the link between pro-environmental behaviour and well-being, acting as an emotional mechanism through which sustainable practices influence both positive and negative affect. By examining the link between pro-environmental behaviour and well-being, this study provides new perspectives on the previously inconsistent evidence.

Present study

The present study aims to deepen our understanding of the unique contributions of social-psychological characteristics to pro-environmental behaviour. For this study, individual behaviours that are identified by the literature as highly impactful were selected, namely: reducing energy use, consumption of meat and animal products, and car use [58, 59]. The hypotheses were tested in a large nationally representative Portuguese sample. Portugal continues to be one of the most climate change vulnerable countries in Europe, suffering severe consequences from it (e.g. heat waves, flooding, wildfires, droughts). With its long and densely populated coastline, Portugal is also highly threatened by rising sea level. Given that pro-environmental behaviours differ greatly between countries (e.g [15, 16]). , examining countries that are highly vulnerable to climate change, like Portugal, is extremely necessary.

Method

Sample and procedure

Original data was collected from a nationally representative sample. The sample comprised 3,300 adults, consisting of 1,583 men and 1,717 women. Descriptive statistics for the sample are shown in Table 1. Data were gathered using an online survey administered by Qdata, a research company that maintains a panel of 860,000 individuals. Qdata is one of the leading survey companies in Portugal. Emails were sent to panellists selected at random from the base sample of the required profile. The responding sample was weighted to the profile of the sample definition based on census data to provide a representative reporting sample, with a margin of error of 2% and a confidence level of 95% of the total Portuguese population.

Measures

Climate change anxiety

Climate anxiety as a psychological response to climate change was measured using [48] 13-item instrument. The measure is composed of two subscales: (a) cognitive impairment, 8 items (e.g., “Thinking about climate change makes it difficult for me to sleep”) and (b) functional impairment, 5 items (e.g., “I have problems balancing my concerns about sustainability with the needs of my family”). Responses were indicated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = Never to 5 = Almost always. The respondent’s average score on each dimension was computed. Cronbach’s alphas for these dimensions were 0.91 and 0.89, respectively. The average of all 13 items was also calculated to obtain a total climate change anxiety score. Cronbach’s alpha for the overall climate change anxiety scale was 0.94.

Environmental identity

Participants’ environmental identity was assessed using [60] scale. The measure included 14 items that measure individual differences in a stable sense of interdependence and connectedness with nature. Responses were indicated on a scale from 1 = Not at all true of me to 7 = Completely true of me. The average score was computed to measure participants’ overall environmental identity. Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.93.

Climate change perceptions

Participants’ perceptions of climate change were assessed using [41] scale, which included three types of perceptions: (a) reality (e.g., “I believe that climate change is real”); (b) causes (e.g., “Human activities are a major cause of climate change”); and (c) valence of consequences (e.g., “Climate change will bring about serious negative consequences”). Participants used a 7-point Likert-scale from 1 = Strongly disagree to 7 = Strongly agree. An average of all climate change perception items was also calculated to create a total score. Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.96.

Experience of climate change

Experience of climate change was measured using the three-item scale from [48] (e.g. I have been directly affected by climate change). Responses were indicated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = Never to 5 = Almost always. Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.84.

Subjective well-being

Subjective well-being was assessed using the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS) [61, 62]. The scale contains two dimensions: Positive Affect (PA) reflects the extent to a person feels enthusiastic, active, and alert (e.g. “Interested”); and Negative Affect (NA) reflects subjective distress and displeasure (e.g. “Distressed”). The measure had 20 items, and responses were recorded on a scale from 1 = Very Slightly/Not at All to 5 = Extremely. The items related to each dimension were averaged separately to create the participants’ scores on two scales. On the PA sub-dimension, higher scores represented higher levels of positive affect, while higher scores on the NA sub-dimension represented higher levels of negative affect. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.91 for each of these scales.

Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction was measured with the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), a five-item measure developed by [63] and validated for the Portuguese population by [64] was used to assess satisfaction with the respondent’s life as a whole. Subjective well-being is defined as cognitive and affective evaluations of one’s life [51]. This measure relates to the cognitive evaluative component of subjective well-being. Participants answered on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 7 = Strongly Agree to indicate the extent to which they agreed with each statement (e.g. “In most ways my life is close to my ideal”). The average score for the five items was computed in order to measure participants’ life satisfaction. Higher scores on this measure reflected higher levels of satisfaction. Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.90.

Pro-environmental behaviour

Pro-environmental behaviours were measured using an adapted version of the [65] scale. The scale included six conservation behaviours addressing how often individuals reduce their consumption of heating, air-conditioning, hot water, and lighting. Responses were indicated on a scale from 1 = Never to 5 = Always. The average score was computed to measure participants’ conservation behaviours. Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.69. Two items of environmental citizenship, regarding membership in an environmental, conservation or wildlife organisation, were also included. Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.53. Two items about decreased consumption of meat (beef, pork, and poultry) and increasing consumption of vegetarian meals within the last year were also included. Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.57. Transportation-related behaviours, such as carpooling, taking public transportation, and walking or cycling instead of driving in the past year were also evaluated through three items. Responses were indicated on a scale from 1 = Never to 5 = Frequently. The average score was computed to measure participants’ transportation-related behaviours. Cronbach’s alpha for this measure was 0.60.

Sociodemographic variables

Participants indicated their age, gender, occupation, level of education and marital status. Participants also reported the age of their youngest child, the total number of children, their individual monthly income on a seven-point scale ranging from 1 (less than €590) to 7 (more than €6720). Political ideology was measured on a scale from 0 = Left and 10 = Right, with higher levels meaning more right-leaning political ideology.

Results

Preliminary analysis

Regarding pro-environmental behaviours, participants indicated frequent conservation behaviours (e.g. reducing consumption of heating, air-conditioning) (M = 4.21, SD = 0.57), uncommon use of carpooling or alternative modes of transportation (M = 2.87, SD = 1.02) and did not decrease their meat consumption or increased their consumption of vegetarian meals during the past year (M = 1.63, SD = 0.48).

Means, standard deviations, and Pearson correlations among the sociodemographic and pro-environmental behaviour are presented in Table 2. Age was negatively related to transportation behaviour (r = − .12, p < .001), indicating that the younger people were, the more frequently they carpooled or used alternative modes of transportation. Nevertheless, age was positively related to conservation (r = .16, p < .001) and food behaviour (r = .09, p < .001), demonstrating that the older people were, the more frequently they exhibited conservation behaviours and consumed vegetarian meals over the past year. To explore the role of sociodemographic characteristics on pro-environmental behaviour, a set of multiple regression analyses was conducted (see Table 3). The findings show that age was also a significant predictor of conservation behaviour (β = 0.13, p < .001). The older participants were, the more frequent conservation behaviours they had. Taken together, these results are consistent with previous research that shows younger people tend to use greener transport, while older people were more likely to enact conversation behaviours and reduce meat consumption (e.g [66]).

Social-psychological characteristics and pro-environmental behaviour

The first set of hypotheses suggested that environmental identity (H1a), climate change perceptions (H1b) and direct experience of climate change (H1c) will be positively related to pro-environmental behaviours. To test these hypotheses, means, standard deviations, and Pearson correlations among social-psychological measures and pro-environmental behaviour were analysed (Table 2). As predicted, environmental identity was positively related to pro-environmental behaviour, namely conservation behaviour (r = .37, p < .001), transportation behaviour (r = .21, p < .001) and food behaviour (r = .26, p < .001). Climate change perceptions were also positively related to pro-environmental behaviour, that is conservation behaviour (r = .25, p < .001), transportation behaviour (r = .12, p < .001) and food behaviour (r = .18, p < .001). Additionally, direct experience of climate change was positively related with pro-environmental behaviour, to be specific conservation behaviour (r = .09, p < .001), transportation behaviour (r = .25, p < .001) and food behaviour (r = .18, p < .001).

To explore the role of social-psychological characteristics on pro-environmental behaviour, a set of multiple regression analyses was conducted. As indicated in Table 3, environmental identity and climate change perceptions were significant predictors of conservation behaviour (β = 0.37, p < .001; β = 0.15, p < .001). The more salient environmental identities participants had and the more they believed climate change was real, caused by humans and had negative consequences, the higher levels of conservation behaviour they declared.

Pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being

The second hypothesis suggested that climate anxiety would be positively related to pro-environmental behaviours (H2). In line with hypothesis 2, the results indicated that climate change anxiety was positively related to pro-environmental behaviour (r = .07, p < .001; r = .23, p < .001; r = .21, p < .001, conservation, transportation and food, respectively) (Table 2).

The third and fourth hypotheses suggested that pro-environmental behaviour would be related to higher levels of subjective well-being. Specifically, it would be positively related to life satisfaction (H3a) and positive affect (H3b), negatively related to negative affect (H3c), and those relations would be mediated by climate change anxiety (H4).

To test these hypotheses, the mediating role of climate change anxiety on the relationship between pro-environmental behaviour and well-being was examined, and methods developed by Preacher and Hayes were followed ( [68]). These analyses were conducted using the PROCESS program (Model 4; [69]) with bias-corrected bootstrap estimates and 95% confidence intervals (Table 4). These results indicate that conservation, transportation and food behaviour had indirect effects on positive affect. These effects were mediated by climate change anxiety and were significant, as indicated by bootstrap confidence intervals entirely above zero (95% CI [0.059, 0.022]; 95% CI [0.018, 0.033]; 95% CI [0.042, 0.072], for conservation, transportation and food behaviour, respectively). The results obtained also indicated that transportation and food behaviour had a significant indirect effect on negative affect, mediated by climate anxiety, as indicated by bootstrap confidence intervals entirely above zero (95% CI [0.067, 0.093]; 95% CI [0.127, 0.183], respectively). These results provide support for the mediation process in the effect of pro-environmental behaviours on positive and negative affect, but not life satisfaction.

Discussion

This study examined the role of social-psychological characteristics on pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being, using a nationally representative Portuguese sample. It contributes to the existing literature by advancing knowledge in an evolving field of research, examining how individuals’ connection to nature, knowledge and experience of climate change influence the development of pro-environmental behaviours. By exploring the intricate connection between pro-environmental behaviours and subjective well-being, this study also offers insights into how to motivate climate action to mitigate the negative impacts of climate change.

The findings supported the first set of hypotheses, indicating that environmental identity, climate change perceptions and direct experience of climate change were positively related to pro-environmental behaviours. Furthermore, environmental identity and climate change perceptions were significant predictors of conservation behaviour. The results thus replicated and extended those of previous studies (e.g [30, 39, 40, 43, 45]). by providing evidence for the role of social psychological characteristics in a large Portuguese representative sample. Therefore, contributing to the growing body of research that shows the importance of social psychological characteristics in pro-environmental behaviour (e.g [35, 40, 70]). and how individuals who strongly identify with the natural environment or view climate change as real, human-caused, and threatening are more likely to adopt sustainable practices [2, 6, 40]. They also provide empirical support for theoretical frameworks such as environmental identity theory and the Value-Belief-Norm model [1], reinforcing the centrality of identity and values in environmental action.

An unexpected finding was that personally experiencing climate change did not significantly predict conservation behaviours. While individuals who directly experienced climate change impacts were more likely to adopt sustainable transportation and dietary changes, these experiences did not translate into energy conservation practices. One possible explanation is that conservation behaviours, such as reducing heating or electricity use, are often shaped more by household routines, infrastructure, or economic incentives than by emotional responses to climate threats. Unlike transportation or food choices, which may feel more directly connected to mitigating emissions, conservation actions may be perceived as habitual or less impactful, making them less sensitive to personal climate experiences. This result is consistent with research suggesting that certain behaviours are driven more by structural conditions and social norms than by risk perception alone [40]. It also highlights the need for targeted interventions, such as feedback systems, financial incentives, and awareness campaigns, that make the link between energy conservation and climate change more salient.

Socio-demographic patterns further enrich the interpretation of these findings. Age differences emerged as particularly relevant, with younger participants more frequently engaging in sustainable transportation behaviours, while older participants reported higher levels of conservation practices and greater willingness to adopt dietary changes, such as reducing meat consumption. These results suggest that life stage and daily routines create different opportunities and barriers for environmental action, highlighting the value of age-tailored interventions, for instance, improving public transport and cycling infrastructure to support younger populations, and promoting energy-saving or food-related initiatives for older groups. Household income and political ideology were also significant predictors, as lower-income individuals and those with more left-leaning views reported more frequent engagement in greener transportation and dietary choices. This indicates that both economic constraints and ideological values shape environmental practices, underscoring the importance of designing policies that are both financially accessible and politically inclusive. Interestingly, education showed a comparatively weaker relationship with behaviour, suggesting that while environmental knowledge is important, it is not sufficient on its own to motivate action unless paired with strong environmental identity and perceived urgency of climate change. Taken together, these findings emphasise the need for policy frameworks that are sensitive to socio-demographic differences, ensuring that interventions are context-specific, equitable, and more likely to foster widespread pro-environmental engagement.

The results also fully supported the second hypothesis by showing that climate change anxiety was positively related to the three pro-environmental behaviours examined, echoing those of previous research (e.g [8, 49]). This aligns with recent work positioning climate anxiety not merely as a psychological burden but also as an adaptive motivator for action [7, 8]. The present study adds to this literature by demonstrating, in a large representative sample, that climate anxiety can play a constructive role in fostering sustainable behaviour.

Consistent with previous research (e.g [11, 52]). , the results supported the prediction that pro-environmental behaviour is related to higher levels of positive affect and negatively related to negative affect. Replicating these findings in this context enhances our confidence in their generalisability and underscores the importance of country-specific information. Moreover, those relations were mediated by climate change anxiety. These results provided support for the mediation process in the effect of pro-environmental behaviours on positive and negative affect, but not life satisfaction. This highlights a dual role of climate anxiety: while it can increase distress, it also motivates behavioural engagement that reduces helplessness and fosters positive emotions. This nuanced finding advances theoretical debates on the psychological consequences of climate change, showing that pro-environmental behaviour can serve as both a coping mechanism and a pathway to well-being. Consequently, it highlights the need for further exploration of the mediating role of climate change anxiety in pro-environmental behaviour and the importance of analysing a variety of behaviours and subjective well-being measures. It further demonstrates the adaptive role that climate change anxiety can play in enhancing pro-environmental behaviour (e.g [8, 49]). and its direct effects on well-being. Taken together, the results can contribute to the swift change in public perception and challenge the notion that pro-environmental behaviour is contrary to individual well-being (e.g [71, 72]). It can further persuade people to adopt and increase sustainable and pro-environmental behaviours, as they remain low in most countries [73].

Policy implications

These findings contribute to the ongoing discussion on the relationship between sustainable practices and subjective well-being. While some earlier studies have suggested that green behaviours require personal sacrifice [13], the current study provides evidence to the contrary, showing that sustainable lifestyles can enhance well-being. This not only challenges common public narratives but also offers a compelling argument for promoting pro-environmental policies: they need not be at odds with individual happiness. This research provides valuable guidance for both policy implementation and the promotion of pro-environmental behaviour by showing that acting in environmentally responsible ways is not only beneficial for the planet but also positively linked to people’s well-being. By demonstrating that environmental identity, climate change perceptions, and even climate anxiety encourage individuals to adopt greener behaviours, the study highlights psychological levers that policymakers can use to design effective interventions. Importantly, the findings challenge the widespread belief that sustainable practices require personal sacrifice, instead showing that they can enhance positive emotions and reduce negative affect. This evidence strengthens the case for policies that integrate psychological and social factors, such as awareness campaigns, nature-based initiatives, and community engagement programs, to foster widespread behavioural change. Ultimately, the research supports the idea that promoting sustainability does not conflict with personal happiness but can, in fact, reinforce it, making pro-environmental policies both more appealing and more effective.

Limitations and future research

Limitations must be acknowledged. First, self-report measures could be affected by social desirability and memory/recall biases. This could impact the reliability of the data, as one can be expressing their self-conception more than their actual behaviour ( [74–76]). Despite such criticism, recent evidence shows that social-desirability bias is narrowly associated with reports of pro-environmental behaviour [77]. Nonetheless, future research would benefit from integrating diverse measurement methods, including field or laboratory observations of pro-environmental behaviour. The cross-sectional nature of the study is another limitation, as it does not allow for conclusions in terms of causality with confidence. Future research needs to further establish the causal relationship between social-psychological characteristics, pro-environmental behaviour and well-being. Future studies would benefit from a quasi-experimental, intervention design, making a longitudinal examination of mediating and moderating factors in the relationship between pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being.

Another limitation concerns the measurement of pro-environmental behaviour. Although the study distinguished between conservation, transportation, and food-related behaviours, the internal consistency of some of these subscales was relatively low. This suggests that the items may not have fully captured the breadth or complexity of each behavioural domain. For example, conservation behaviours may vary from simple habit-based actions (e.g., switching off lights) to more effortful changes (e.g., reducing heating), which may not cluster strongly together. Similarly, transportation and dietary behaviours are influenced by diverse structural, cultural, and personal factors, which may explain their lower reliability as unified constructs. As such, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future research would benefit from employing more comprehensive and psychometrically robust measures of pro-environmental behaviour, possibly combining self-report scales with behavioural observations.

Conclusion

Overall, this study makes three key contributions. First, it demonstrates that environmental identity and climate change perceptions, change significantly shape pro-environmental behaviour, offering strong support for identity-based and value-based theories of environmental action. Second, it highlights the adaptive role of climate anxiety, showing that emotional responses to climate change can motivate action and mediate the relationship between behaviour and well-being. Third, by drawing on a large nationally representative sample from Portugal, a country highly vulnerable to climate impacts, this study provides context-specific evidence that extends the generalisability of prior research. The findings have important theoretical implications, advancing our understanding of how identity, values, and emotions jointly influence sustainability. They also carry practical policy implications: interventions should leverage environmental identity, address perceptions of climate change, and acknowledge the motivating role of climate anxiety. Tailored strategies that consider socio-demographic differences will further enhance the effectiveness of policy measures. In conclusion, this study underscores that pro-environmental behaviours not only help mitigate climate change but also promote individual well-being. By reframing sustainable lifestyles as pathways to both planetary and personal health, policymakers and practitioners can foster broader engagement with climate action. In doing so, the false dichotomy between environmental responsibility and happiness can be dismantled, paving the way for more resilient and sustainable societies, shedding light on the role of social-psychological characteristics on pro-environmental behaviour and subjective well-being.
