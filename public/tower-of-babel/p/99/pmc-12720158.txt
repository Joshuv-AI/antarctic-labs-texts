To the Editor:

Asbestosis is a rare, progressive interstitial lung disease caused by inhaling asbestos fibres [1]. Despite asbestos restrictions since the 1980s, the global incidence of asbestosis has risen [2]. Pirfenidone is an antifibrotic treatment for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) patients [3]. Nintedanib, another antifibrotic agent, slows lung function decline in IPF and progressive fibrosing interstitial lung diseases other than IPF [4]. Two studies suggest that pirfenidone may also reduce disease progression in non-IPF progressive pulmonary fibrosis [3, 5]. A prior multicentre prospective study assessed pirfenidone's safety and efficacy in 10 asbestosis patients with progressive lung function decline [6]. The authors found that pirfenidone was safe and well-tolerated but did not detect significant changes in forced vital capacity (FVC) due to a small sample size [6]. In this study, we aimed to retrospectively assess the difference in lung function decline before and after the initiation of pirfenidone in asbestosis patients in the Netherlands who have received treatment for at least 12 weeks.

We performed a multicentre retrospective study at four hospitals in the Netherlands: Antoni van Leeuwenhoek, Amsterdam University Medical Centers, Erasmus Medical Center, and St Antonius Hospital, from January 2014 to January 2024. Inclusion criteria were: adult patients (age 18+) with asbestosis treated with pirfenidone for over 12 weeks, availability of at least one lung function test three months before and after treatment initiation, and a baseline lung function test. The asbestosis diagnosis was confirmed by the treating pulmonologist and the Institute of Asbestos Victims (IAS) team, which verifies whether the Dutch Health Council criteria are met. These criteria include: 1) diffuse lung fibrosis on high-resolution computed tomography or a biopsy showing fibrotic abnormalities, 2) at least five fibre-years of asbestos exposure, 15+ years before symptom onset, and 3) loss of lung function, classified by the American Medical Association criteria. Patients were selected from a national database of asbestosis patients applying for compensation for asbestos-related diseases. If the diagnosis was disputed by the medical team or IAS, the patient was excluded, as were those receiving immunosuppressive treatment. The primary outcome was the reduction in FVC decline rate following pirfenidone treatment, expressed in mL per month. The secondary outcome was the reduction in diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) decline rate. FVC and DLCO data were collected from pulmonary function tests at the treating hospitals.

We collected baseline characteristics of the included asbestosis patients: age, sex, smoking status, histology and treatment dosage, in addition to FVC and DLCO before and after treatment. We analysed the effect of initiating pirfenidone on FVC decline in mL per month using a piecewise linear mixed-effects model. This model described FVC for each patient as a linear function of time before pirfenidone and a separate linear function, potentially with a different slope, after treatment. The pre-pirfenidone FVC decline was modelled with both random (patient-specific) and fixed (group-averaged) components for both intercept and slope. The change in slope between pre- and post-pirfenidone was modelled only as a fixed effect, assumed to be the same across all patients. The magnitude of this fixed effect, representing the average impact of pirfenidone on FVC decline rate while accounting for repeated measurements, was the primary outcome. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Netherlands Cancer Institute (institutional review board number: IRBd19-136).

From 1 January 2014 to 1 January 2024, 1004 patients applied for an asbestosis reimbursement. Of these, 479 met the criteria for asbestosis. We included 22 of 479 (4.6%) asbestosis patients from the national database who were treated with pirfenidone for at least 12 weeks and had pre- and post-treatment lung function tests and a baseline test. Some of the patients included were previously described in a prospective study on asbestosis [6]. The median age was 73.5 years (interquartile range (IQR): 71.0–75.0). Most patients were male (90.9%) and former smokers (90.9%). Only one patient (4.5%) had histology-proven asbestosis. Pirfenidone was titrated to the target dose of 801 mg three times daily in all patients. Seven (31.8%) patients experienced clinically significant drug-related adverse effects, including decreased appetite and diarrhoea, necessitating dose reduction. Treatment was discontinued in two patients (9.1%) due to intolerable toxicity. An additional 15 patients (68.2%) discontinued pirfenidone due to clinical deterioration. Five patients (22.7%) remained on treatment until death.

The baseline median FVC at the start of pirfenidone treatment was 3.11 L (IQR: 2.6–3.7). In the primary analysis, the decrease in FVC, expressed in mL per month, decreased by 7.0 mL (95% CI −5.8–19.7) per month (p=0.22): from an average of 26 mL per month decrease in FVC before pirfenidone to an average of 19 mL per month decrease on pirfenidone, see figure 1a. The baseline median DLCO before pirfenidone was 41.0% (IQR: 34.3–46.8). The decrease in DLCO, expressed in percentage points per month, decreased by 0.11% points per month (95% CI −0.19–0.41) (p=0.30): from an average of 0.48% percentage points per month decrease in DLCO before pirfenidone to an average of 0.37% percentage pointsper month after pirfenidone, see figure 1b.

In our real-world Dutch cohort of asbestosis patients, we observed that FVC decline decreased by 7.0 mL per month (95% CI −5.8–19.7, p=0.22), and DLCO decreased by 0.11% points per month (95% CI −0.19–0.41, p=0.30) after pirfenidone initiation. To our knowledge, this is the largest cohort of asbestosis patients treated with pirfenidone reported. Due to the small sample size and limited pulmonary function measurements, it remains uncertain whether a significant effect could have been detected. Maher et al. [5] and Behr et al. [4] studied larger cohorts with more frequent pulmonary function testing and reported significant reductions in FVC decline after pirfenidone in progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease patients. These studies enrolled heterogeneous populations with various fibrosing interstitial lung diseases but did not focus on asbestosis as a distinct subgroup.

Our study has limitations, primarily the small sample size, which is partly due to asbestosis being rare and the off-label use of pirfenidone in this population being limited. Consequently, only a small number of patients were included, and it remains uncertain whether a statistically significant effect of pirfenidone on FVC and DLCO decline could be detected in this cohort. Another limitation is the retrospective design, which may have introduced selection or information bias. Also, pulmonary function tests were not performed at fixed time intervals and were conducted at various hospitals, introducing the risk of measurement variability. Additionally, due to the retrospective design, self-reported adverse events were not reliably reported. This is an important limitation, as treatment with pirfenidone is associated with side effects, which may lead to treatment discontinuation.

A key strength of our study is its national multicentre design using a large database that includes all asbestosis patients in the Netherlands who applied for compensation for an asbestos-related disease, resulting in the largest known cohort of asbestosis patients treated with pirfenidone with available pre- and post-treatment lung function tests. Another strength is the real-world nature of the data, reflecting routine clinical practice and increasing the generalisability of our results to similar healthcare settings for asbestosis patients.

In conclusion, our study provides real-world evidence on pirfenidone use in asbestosis patients. Although based on a small cohort, our results did not show a significant reduction in FVC or DLCO decline in treated patients, highlighting the need for further research.
