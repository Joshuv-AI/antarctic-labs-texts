Missing in Action: Where Are the Patient-Centered, Telehealth-Ready Quality Measures for Common Mental Health Disorders?
Authors: Benjamin S Metrikin, Ashlynn R Torres, Sharon L Levine
Published: 2023

Demand for mental health services (MHS) has grown steadily over the past 2 decades, with a relative increase of 46.5% in treated prevalence of depression between 1998 and 2015. 1,2 In 2019, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, nearly 1 in 5 adults in the US had a mental health diagnosis, and at the height of the pandemic, 40% of adults reported symptoms of anxiety or mild to moderate depression, 2 of the 3 most common mental health diagnoses. 3

The sustained increase in demand for MHS already outstrips an unevenly distributed capacity. In the US, 150 million people live in federally designated mental health shortage areas, and more than half of US counties lack even a single psychiatrist. Although mental health spending accounts for 6% of all direct health care spending, the 27% of individuals with a mental health diagnosis and another medical condition account for almost 60% of total annual health care costs. 3

Consumers, patients, family members, and payers are seeking mechanisms to ensure that high quality, accessible, and affordable mental health care enables individuals to achieve their personal therapeutic goals, whether that care is delivered by mental health professionals or primary care clinicians and whether the care is delivered in person or via telehealth. Estimates are that primary care clinicians treat 60% of people with depression and prescribe 79% of antidepressants. 4 Regardless of where or how patients receive their mental health care, reliable and validated quality measures are essential.

As the move to value-based payment for health care services gains steam, the need for patient-centered outcome measures for common mental health conditions (mild to moderate depression and anxiety) becomes more urgent. There is no evidence that current metrics measure symptom relief or outcomes important to patients. Without a robust portfolio of such "patient-centered" outcome measures, the push for value-based payment will lack substance and the ability of clinicians and payers to improve quality will flounder. If we want to be able to link payment to the quality of care that patients receive, we must have reliable ways of defining and measuring quality.

There is a need for a consensus definition of patient-centered outcomes in mental health, as distinct from patient-reported outcomes. Patient-report survey modalities like the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) are clinically important but cannot be the primary tool for determining quality outcomes. Engaging patients as full partners in the measure development process is crucial, and understanding the kinds of outcomes that are important to patients and will improve quality of life must guide the measure development process. Measures which are not co-developed “…may simply provide a patient-rated version that nevertheless reflects clinician, not patient priorities.” 5 Engaging individuals with lived experience with common mental health disorders in the co-development, testing, and validation of quality metrics will bridge the distance between patient-reported outcome measures and patient-centered outcome measures and will begin to narrow the distance between clinician and patient assessments of quality and value.

Prior to the pandemic, quality metrics in mental health largely consisted of a mix of process measures (eg, timely access measures or the rate of follow-up after hospitalization) and appropriation of tools designed for screening (eg, PHQ-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) scale) for use as patient-reported outcome assessments. The Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set, a portfolio of quality measures maintained by the National Committee for Quality Assurance, contains 3 measures related to depression in nonpregnant adults, and there are no measures that directly address anxiety. 6 The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Measure Inventory tool includes 1 additional measurement for this population: suicide risk assessment. 7 By comparison, there are 5 Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set measures alone for care of adults with diabetes.

Developing and implementing quality measures for common mental health disorders is not without challenges. The often-subjective nature of mental health creates difficulties in outcome measure development and has led to a reliance on patient-reported responses to screening tools like the PHQ-9. Although these types of measures are widely in use, there is no evidence to suggest that they are patient-centered nor that they are capturing meaningful information about quality. A 2016 Cochrane review found no evidence to support the routine use of these tools in mental health quality measurement. 8,9 A statistically significant decrease in PHQ-9 score does not necessarily directly correlate with a measurable change in a patient’s quality of life. Another barrier to quality metrics is in implementation, as nonintegrated information technology infrastructure, fragmentation of mental health clinicians and service delivery, and inconsistent insurance utilization hamper measurement efforts. 10

These challenges continued to complicate mental health quality measurement during the pandemic as symptom burden increased and virtual care became the norm. Rapidly increasing population-wide psychological distress resulted in increased demand for virtual MHS. 11 Prevalence of depression symptoms more than tripled in the first year of the pandemic, 12 and the greatest increases in service utilization were seen for anxiety, amounting to a 20% increase between March and December of 2020. 10 Most mental health care during this time was delivered virtually, facilitated by policies that expanded reimbursement for telehealth, eliminated geographic restrictions on providing care, and allowed prescribing of controlled substances without an in-person visit. Some of these changes have been extended through December 31, 2024, while others have become permanent changes to Medicare policy, signaling the opportunity for continued use of telehealth for provision of MHS. 13

During the pandemic, multiple processes of care were disrupted due to changes like shelter-in-place orders, which led to a greater reliance on virtual care. Prior to the pandemic, there were no widely used measures for common mental health disorders that specifically assess quality of virtual care, and we continue to use the same quality measures to measure outcomes for virtual care as we do for in-person care. 10 Given that telehealth has not previously been used widely to deliver mental health care, it remains to be seen whether different outcome measures are needed for virtual and in-person care. Although in some ways, a virtual modality of care may facilitate the integration of tools such as a PHQ-9 into the workflow, there are also differences in how care is delivered across modality. This may be exemplified by process measures, such as whether there were technological glitches that interrupted the encounter, whether the virtual setting is able to provide a safe space for a patient to share details about their mental health, or how difficult it was for patients to find a private space with a reliable signal where they could log on to a virtual visit. Technology glitches will impact a patient’s experience of care, and quality outcome measures for virtual care must capture that information.

There is no indication that the growth in demand for MHS experienced prior to and during the COVID-19 pandemic will abate going forward, and mental health care will likely continue to represent an outsized proportion of health care spending. 10 The pandemic, and the policies and practices that resulted, have changed the delivery of MHS and consumer expectations for the foreseeable future. Pandemic-era changes have shown that we need effective measures that are applicable across both in-person and virtual settings. The challenge now is to create a portfolio of quality outcome metrics that will accurately assess care quality while limiting, as much as possible, added administrative burdens on clinicians and health systems. If meaningful quality outcome measures remain elusive, the promise of value-based payment for value-based care will be an empty promise, and the human and economic burdens of common mental health conditions will continue to grow, unabated.
