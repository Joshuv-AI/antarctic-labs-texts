Neuroleptic malignant syndrome precipitated by chlorpromazine: a case report
Authors: Abdirahman Abdikadir Osman, Hussein Mahdi Ahmed, Mustafa Abdulrahman Ali, Abdisalam Ismail Hassan, Mustafa Yusuf Ahmed, Ahmed Muhammad Bashir, Mohamed Farah Osman Hidig, Marian Muse Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a rare but potentially fatal reaction to dopamine-blocking agents. Although most commonly associated with prolonged or high-dose antipsychotic use, it can also occur after a single therapeutic dose.Case presentationA 56-year-old Somali man developed acute NMS within hours of receiving a single intramuscular dose of chlorpromazine for agitation, presenting with hyperthermia, severe rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability, and markedly elevated creatine kinase levels. Alternative diagnoses were excluded, and prompt discontinuation of chlorpromazine with supportive care and bromocriptine resulted in rapid clinical and biochemical recovery.ConclusionThis case highlights that NMS may occur rapidly following a single lowtherapeutic dose of chlorpromazine. Early recognition and timely management are essential for favorable outcomes. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of chlorpromazine-induced NMS from Somalia.

Introduction

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a rare but life-threatening neurological emergency triggered by dopamine-blocking medications and characterized by the hallmark tetrad of hyperthermia, severe muscular rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability [1]. Although infrequent, NMS carries significant morbidity and mortality, making timely recognition and rapid intervention essential. First described by Delay and colleagues in 1960 in association with haloperidol, NMS has since been documented with both typical and atypical antipsychotics, with an incidence estimated between 0.02% and 3% among exposed individuals [2, 3].

Chlorpromazine, which came out in 1952 as one of the first-generation antipsychotics, is a strong dopamine D2 receptor antagonist and is still known to cause NMS [4]. The precise pathophysiology remains incompletely understood; however, current evidence suggests that acute dopaminergic hypoactivity in the hypothalamus, basal ganglia, and brainstem, together with peripheral abnormalities in muscle metabolism, contributes to the characteristic hyperthermia, rigidity, and autonomic dysfunction seen in NMS [5]. Symptoms most commonly appear within 24–72 h of exposure; however, onset may occur within hours or be delayed for days to weeks, particularly with depot formulations [6].

Although improved diagnostic awareness and supportive care have reduced mortality from earlier rates of 20–30% to approximately 5–11.6%, delayed diagnosis or inadequate treatment remains associated with severe complications such as rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury (AKI), disseminated intravascular coagulation, arrhythmias, and death [7, 8]. NMS precipitated by a single low-dose intramuscular administration of chlorpromazine is particularly uncommon and may mimic infectious, metabolic, or neurological emergencies, resulting in diagnostic uncertainty [3, 7, 8].

Given these challenges, reporting such cases is clinically valuable. NMS induced by a solitary low dose of chlorpromazine is uncommon and may be confused with infectious or neurological emergencies, resulting in a delay in suitable treatment. Here, we present a case of chlorpromazine-induced neuroleptic malignant syndrome with a favorable outcome, which, to our knowledge represents the first documented case from Somalia. This report aims to highlight the importance of early recognition and prompt management to prevent life-threatening complications.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old Somali man with a psychiatric history suggestive of schizophrenia and poor adherence to antipsychotic medications was brought to the emergency department with a 24-h history of high fever, progressive generalized muscle stiffness, confusion, and profuse sweating. Formal psychiatric records were unavailable. He was not receiving any regular medications and had no known chronic medical conditions or family history of psychiatric or neuromuscular disorders. He lived with his family, was unemployed, and denied the use of alcohol, tobacco, or illicit substances.

The symptoms developed shortly after he received a single 25 mg intramuscular injection of chlorpromazine for acute agitation on the previous day. Within a few hours of administration, his family observed increasing generalized rigidity, dark-colored urine, reduced responsiveness, and progressive disorientation, prompting presentation to the emergency department.

On arrival, the patient appeared acutely ill, diaphoretic, and confused, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 12/15. His vital signs demonstrated marked hyperthermia, tachycardia, hypertension, and tachypnea. (Table 1) Physical examination revealed severe generalized muscle rigidity, including pronounced masseter spasm causing trismus, though no focal neurological deficits were present. Pupils were equal and reactive, breath sounds were clear bilaterally, and the cardiovascular examination revealed tachycardia with a regular rhythm and strong peripheral pulses. Initial laboratory tests showed significant abnormalities consistent with rhabdomyolysis and systemic stress, including a creatine kinase level of 21,000 U/L, leukocytosis of 17,000/mm3, serum creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL, potassium of 5.8 mmol/L, and positive urine myoglobin (Table 2). These findings were consistent with severe rhabdomyolysis, early renal involvement, and hyperkalemia. Brain MRI was normal, effectively excluding intracranial infection, hemorrhage, or other structural causes for the altered mental state. The combination of acute hyperthermia, severe rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability, and markedly elevated CK in close temporal association with chlorpromazine exposure strongly suggested Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome. Differential considerations initially included tetanus, serotonin syndrome, malignant hyperthermia, sepsis, and central nervous system infections; however, the absence of a wound or toxin exposure, the lack of serotonergic medication use, the absence of anesthetic triggers, normal imaging, and no identifiable infectious source made these diagnoses unlikely. The clinical picture fulfilled Levenson’s diagnostic criteria for Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome, confirming chlorpromazine as the precipitating factor.

The patient met all three major Levenson’s criteria (fever, rigidity, and elevated creatine kinase) as well as multiple minor criteria, including tachycardia, autonomic instability, altered mental status, diaphoresis, leukocytosis, and renal impairment, further confirming the diagnosis of Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome.

Chlorpromazine was immediately discontinued, and the patient received aggressive intravenous hydration with normal saline to maintain a urine output above 200 mL/hour to prevent myoglobin-induced renal injury. Continuous cardiac monitoring was initiated due to the risk of arrhythmias associated with hyperkalemia, and a Foley catheter was placed for strict urine output assessment. Hyperkalemia was managed promptly with intravenous calcium gluconate, insulin with dextrose, and beta-2 agonists. Bromocriptine therapy was started via the oral or nasogastric route to counteract dopamine receptor blockade and address the underlying pathophysiologic mechanism of Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome.

Within 48 h of treatment initiation, the patient showed marked clinical improvement. His temperature normalized, muscle rigidity significantly decreased, mental status returned to baseline, CK levels began to decline sharply, and serum potassium normalized. The dark color of the urine resolved as myoglobinuria cleared, and no progression to acute kidney injury or cardiac arrhythmias occurred. He continued to improve with supportive care and ultimately made a full recovery. At outpatient follow-up two weeks after discharge, the patient remained clinically stable with no recurrence of fever, rigidity, or altered mental status, and was referred to psychiatric services for reassessment and initiation of a structured antipsychotic treatment plan. Discussion

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is one of the most severe adverse reactions associated with antipsychotic therapy, with an estimated incidence of 0.02% to 3% among exposed patients [9]. Although traditionally linked to high-potency antipsychotics and dose escalation, the present case highlights that NMS can also occur rapidly following a single low intramuscular dose of chlorpromazine, underscoring the importance of maintaining clinical vigilance even with modest dopamine antagonist exposure.

The fundamental pathophysiology of NMS is intricate and remains incompletely understood. The prevailing theory centers on acute dopaminergic hypoactivity within the central nervous system, particularly in the hypothalamus and basal ganglia, resulting in impaired thermoregulation, severe muscle rigidity, and autonomic instability [10]. Dopamine D2 receptor blockade disrupts the dopaminergic-cholinergic balance of the basal ganglia, producing extrapyramidal symptoms analogous to severe Parkinsonian rigidity [11]. Peripheral mechanisms have also been suggested, such as improper calcium handling and prolonged muscle contraction, which can lead to rhabdomyolysis and the significant increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels seen in many patients [12]. These mechanisms correlate well with the clinical presentation observed in our patient, who developed profound rigidity and a CK elevation of 21,000 U/L, reflecting significant muscle breakdown.

NMS is clinically defined by a tetrad of hyperthermia, rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic dysregulation, but these features are not pathognomonic and frequently overlap with other life-threatening conditions. The presence of severe trismus in our patient initially raised suspicion for tetanus; however, the absence of a wound or exposure history, the rapid symptom progression, and the extraordinarily elevated CK rendered this diagnosis unlikely. Likewise, meningitis and encephalitis were considered, prompting a brain MRI, which was normal and helped exclude intracranial infection [14]. This highlights the diagnostic complexity of NMS and the crucial role of thorough history-taking, particularly regarding recent exposure to dopamine-blocking medications, in avoiding misdiagnosis. Although no single laboratory test confirms NMS, supportive findings such as leukocytosis, myoglobinuria, hyperkalemia, and elevated CK can reinforce clinical suspicion [15], all of which were present in our case.

Rhabdomyolysis represents a major contributor to morbidity in NMS, arising from sustained muscle contraction and hypermetabolic states [16]. Myoglobin released from damaged muscle can precipitate in renal tubules and lead to acute kidney injury [17]. In our patient, the combination of dark urine, elevated CK, and mildly increased serum creatinine suggested early-stage renal involvement. Importantly, no progression to overt kidney failure occurred, likely due to timely, aggressive intravenous hydration initiated upon diagnosis. Hyperkalemia, another consequence of muscle lysis, poses a high risk for malignant arrhythmias [19]. Prompt correction of potassium levels in this case prevented cardiac complications and contributed to the favorable outcome.

Management of NMS requires immediate discontinuation of the offending antipsychotic agent, aggressive supportive care, and pharmacologic therapy aimed at restoring dopaminergic activity [20]. Our patient received high-volume isotonic saline to maintain adequate urine output and limit myoglobin-mediated renal injury. Electrolyte imbalances were quickly fixed, and bromocriptine, a dopamine agonist, was started to counteract central dopaminergic blockade. The remarkably rapid clinical improvement observed within 48 h, evidenced by normalization of temperature, decreasing rigidity, recovery of mental status, and declining CK levels, aligns with literature demonstrating accelerated recovery in patients who receive dopamine agonist therapy. Dantrolene, a skeletal muscle relaxant frequently reserved for refractory cases, was not required due to the patient’s favorable early response.

Unlike the Australian case, where NMS emerged several days after recovery from a massive mixed overdose of olanzapine, chlorpromazine, and escitalopram and required prolonged ICU support, our patient developed acute, severe NMS within hours following a single therapeutic 25 mg intramuscular dose of chlorpromazine without any co-ingestions—demonstrating a far more rapid onset, a purer pharmacologic trigger, and an unusually favorable and complete clinical recovery [21].

The prognosis of NMS is generally good when diagnosed early, with most patients recovering within 7–14 days (2–4). Modern mortality rates have decreased to 5–11.6%, reflecting improvements in recognition and supportive management. The present case reinforces the importance of early identification, as the patient experienced rapid clinical recovery and avoided severe complications such as acute kidney injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and respiratory failure even in the presence of severe rigidity and significant biochemical derangements.

A final consideration in NMS management is the cautious reintroduction of antipsychotic therapy. Current guidelines recommend waiting at least two weeks after complete symptom resolution, selecting a low potency or atypical antipsychotic, initiating at a low dose, and titrating slowly while monitoring closely for recurrence. Given the estimated recurrence risk of 30–50%, the offending agent, chlorpromazine, in this case, should be avoided in the future. This is particularly relevant for our patient, whose NMS developed after a single low dose, indicating heightened sensitivity to dopamine blockade.

Overall, this case emphasizes the need for clinicians to recognize that NMS may occur even after minimal antipsychotic exposure, to rapidly differentiate it from mimicking conditions, and to initiate early, aggressive management to prevent complications. The patient’s favorable outcome demonstrates the effectiveness of timely identification combined with supportive care and dopamine agonist therapy, and it contributes to the limited documented data on NMS presentations within Somalia.

Conclusion

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome remains a potentially fatal adverse reaction to antipsychotic medications, yet as demonstrated in this case, it can occur even after a single low therapeutic dose of chlorpromazine. Early recognition, rapid exclusion of mimicking conditions, and prompt initiation of supportive care and dopamine agonist therapy were central to the patient’s rapid and complete recovery. In contrast to reported cases associated with antipsychotic overdose or polypharmacy, the unusually rapid onset and favorable outcome in this patient underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for NMS regardless of dose, duration, or drug potency. This case expands the limited body of literature on NMS presentations in Somalia and highlights the need for continued clinical vigilance to prevent life-threatening complications.
