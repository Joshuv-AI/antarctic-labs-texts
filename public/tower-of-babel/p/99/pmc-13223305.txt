Stem thin disc (STD) based somatic embryogenesis and genetic homogeneity assessment in Alhagi maurorum Medik
Authors: Tanvi Agarwal, Nikita Gautam, Amit Kumar Gupta, Harish, Narpat Singh Shekhawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlhagi maurorum (camelthorn) is a woody shrub of arid and semi-arid ecosystems. It contributes to soil stability, land restoration, phytoremediation of heavy metals, carbon sequestration, climate regulation, traditional medicine, and the pharmaceutical industry. To assist such use, an efficient protocol for somatic embryogenesis from Stem Thin Disc (STD) culture technology has been established. Stem thin discs prepared from in vitro regenerated shoots responded to 1 mg l−1 NAA (1-naphthalene acetic acid) and 1 mg l−1 BAP (6-benzyl amino purine) fortified MS media with a maximum 72.22% regeneration frequency/inoculum within 18 days. The regenerated creamy white, granular, and fast-growing embryogenic callus was differentiated into a compact mass of yellowish green embryos on BAP (0.5 mg l−1) + NAA (1.0 mg l−1) + additives fortified MS medium. The mature embryos germinated into complete plantlets with a maximum 66.67 ± 1.73% germination rate in 25–28 days of transfer to germination medium. Among the total germinated embryos, 23.33% of complete plantlets were obtained. A total of six molecular markers, comprising three inter simple sequence repeat (ISSR) and three start codon targeted (SCoT) primers, were employed to assess the genetic homogeneity of the in vitro regenerated plantlets. The ISSR markers revealed 100% monomorphism, while SCoT markers exhibited 93.33% monomorphism, resulting in an overall genetic homogeneity of 96.66% between the regenerated plantlets and the mother plant. This efficient protocol reported here is the first report on Alhagi maurorum that may greatly facilitate the large-scale production of plantlets with genetic stability.Keywords: Genetic fidelity, Morphogenesis, ISSR, Thin cell layer, SCoTSubject terms: Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Somatic embryogenesis (SE) is a fascinating phenomenon of plant tissue culture. It occurs when reprogrammed somatic cells produce bipolar embryoids. It is a core pillar of modern plant biotechnology, which impacts everything from global food security to climate change mitigation and conservation biology1,2. Since the inception of the somatic embryogenesis method for carrot explants3, this technology has been applied to numerous plant species of agricultural, medicinal, horticultural, and ornamental importance. However, SE technology of woody plant species faces several challenges, including recalcitrance, genotype dependency, low embryogenic potential, slower rate of maturation and germination of embryos, high incidence of contamination, etc.1. In view of these limitations, alternative explant preparation strategies, such as the Stem Thin Disc (STD) culture technique, have been explored to improve somatic embryogenesis efficiency in woody plant species. In the past few decades, efficient in vitro SE methods have been successfully established for both woody gymnosperm (Quercus, Pinus, and Pseudotsuga) and angiosperm plant genera, including Populus, Betula, Tectona, and Eucalyptus4,5. To overcome challenges associated with the SE in woody plants, recent developments of the Stem Thin Disc (STD) culture have been introduced, providing enhanced efficiency and consistency. In this technique, a thin section of the stem is utilized as an explant, which increases the surface area exposure of the explants to the nutrient media. Consequently, it enhances the efficiency of nutrient and PGRs (Plant Growth Regulators) absorption, thereby boosting morphogenesis response and accelerating the rate of regeneration. It offers multiple benefits, including minimum plant material requirement, reduced callus phase, enhanced efficiency, consistent somatic embryo development, and suitability for transformation studies6. It has been successfully established for several plant species, like Allium sativum7, Phaseolus vulgaris8, Rubus sanctus and Rubus hirtus9, Dendrobium aqueum10, and Saccharum species hybrids6.

Alhagi maurorum (camelthorn), a woody shrub (Fig. 1), belongs to the Fabaceae family and acts as an integral part of the sustainability of arid and semi-arid ecosystems by facilitating soil stability and land restoration. It acts as a pioneer species in dryland ecosystems, which contributes to addressing global challenges such as phytoremediation of heavy metals, carbon sequestration, and climate regulation11. It holds remarkable market value, particularly in traditional medicine and the pharmaceutical industry. A. maurorum is a potential source of the significant bioactive phytocompound "lupeol," which offers therapeutic benefits for different health ailments12. In the present scenario of increasing global demand for natural products, alongside ecological and agricultural considerations, Alhagi maurorum needs a sustainable, large-scale propagation system. To improve the production, some regeneration protocols for Alhagi maurorum have been published to date, including clonal propagation using nodal explants13,14, leaf and nodal explant15, and seed explants16. These methods are primarily organogenesis-based, with prolonged culture durations. Despite the availability of regeneration protocols for Alhagi maurorum, a reproducible somatic embryogenesis system has not yet been reported. Therefore, the present study was undertaken to (i) establish an efficient somatic embryogenesis protocol using stem thin disc (STD) explants, (ii) optimize plant growth regulator combinations for callus induction, embryo differentiation, maturation, and germination, and (iii) assess the genetic homogeneity of regenerated plantlets using ISSR (Inter Simple Sequence Repeat) and SCoT (Start Codon Targeted) markers. Somatic embryogenesis is often associated with somaclonal variation due to prolonged in vitro culture, exposure to plant growth regulators, and callus-mediated regeneration. The combined use of ISSR and SCoT markers enhances the reliability of genetic homogeneity assessment in regenerated plantlets17,18. ISSR markers target microsatellite-rich regions19, whereas SCoT markers amplify conserved gene-flanking regions20, providing complementary coverage of both coding and non-coding regions of the genome. The developed protocol is expected to provide a reliable and genetically stable propagation system, thereby reducing pressure on wild populations and ensuring a sustainable supply of uniform A. maurorum plantlets to meet societal and medicinal demands.

Materials and methods

Explant preparation and culture establishment

Stem (Internode) thin discs (0.3–0.6 cm) excised from in vitro regenerated shoots (Fig. 2A) of Alhagi maurorum13 were used as explants for culture initiation. Healthy, actively growing in vitro shoots obtained from 20–25-day-old subcultures were used for the explant excision. Stem (Internode) thin discs (0.3–0.6 cm thick with 0.1–0.2 cm diameter) were prepared using a sharp scalpel under LAFB (Laminar air flow bench) and inoculated on MS (Murashige and Skoog)21 medium containing additives (25 mg l−1 each of Adenine sulphate, L-Arginine, Citric acid, and 50 mg l−1 of Ascorbic acid), various concentrations of PGRs, including 2, 4-D/BAP (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid/6-benzyl amino purine) and NAA (1-naphthalene acetic acid) (Table 1), Sucrose (3%) and Agar–Agar as gelling agent (0.8%). The cultures were incubated in a growth chamber at 28 ± 2 °C under a 16 h photoperiod with a light intensity of 50–60 μmol m−2 s−1, following an initial two-day incubation under diffused light at 10–20 μmol m−2 s−1 as per our previous report Agarwal et al. (2015)13. After the establishment of cultures, the callogenesis intensity was recoded before further experiments to optimize the PGR type and concentration.

Callus proliferation and differentiation

After initiation of callus culture, approximately 50–100 mg of callus tissue excised from the explant was transferred to MS medium supplemented with various concentrations and combinations of BAP (0.5–2.0 mg L−1) and NAA (0.5 or 1.0 mg L−1) (Table 1), along with additives, for further maintenance and differentiation of the callus. After 20–25 days, the embryogenic callus was subcultured onto the same medium for multiplication and maturation of somatic embryos. Further, the differentiated callus was observed based on morphology, colour, and texture.

Germination of somatic embryos

7 embryos were transferred to germination media, containing MS salt+additives and various concentrations and combinations of BAP (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, or 1.0 mg l−1)+NAA (1.0 mg l−1) (Table 1) to optimize the concentration of PGRs for embryo germination. Embryos that germinated into only shoots were further transferred to the rooting medium as per our previous protocol13. Embryo germination was evaluated based on the germination rate, cotyledons’ opening, rhyzogenesis, shoot emergence, and complete plant development.

Hardening of plantlets

In vitro regenerated plantlets were carefully excised, gently washed to remove the agar–agar adhered to the roots, and transferred to the culture bottles (135 mm × 70 mm) containing autoclaved soil-rite and moistened with 1/4thMS macro salts. To facilitate the process of hardening and acclimatization, initially the plantlets were kept near the pad section (RH 80% and temperature 28–30 ºC) and gradually shifted towards the fan section (RH 40–50% and 32 ± 2 °C) in the greenhouse. The hardened plantlets were transferred to polybags containing a 1:1:1:1 ratio of sand, farmyard manure (FYM), Soil-Rite, and vermiculite.

Genetic homogeneity assessment

The mother plant and five randomly selected somatic embryo-regenerated plants were fixed in liquid nitrogen and used for assessing genetic homogeneity. Genomic DNA was isolated using the modified CTAB (Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) method22. Purified genomic DNA was further used for amplification using SCoT and ISSR PCR-based DNA markers (sequences included in Table 5). A 15 μl of PCR reaction mixtures for selected primers was prepared using 40 ng of genomic DNA, 1 × PCR buffer [100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 9.0), 500 mM KCl, and 1% Triton X-100 with 15 mM MgCl₂], 0.2 mM of each dNTP (Bangalore Genei, India), an additional 2.5 mM MgCl₂, 1 µM primer (Integrated DNA Technologies, India), and 1.5 U of Taq DNA polymerase (Bangalore Genei, India) for both SCoT and ISSR primers. PCR amplification using SCoT primers was performed with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min, followed by 38 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at the primer-specific temperature (Ta) for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 8 min. In contrast, ISSR primer amplification was carried out with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 40 s, annealing at the primer-specific temperature (Ta) for 60 s, extension at 72 °C for 60 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 8 min. Amplified PCR products were resolved on 1.4% agarose gels prepared in 1 × TBE buffer and stained with 0.25 µg mL−1 ethidium bromide, and fragment sizes were estimated using a 100 bp DNA ladder. Gels were visualized and documented using a gel documentation system13.

Observation and data analysis

The stock cultures of differentiated embryos were regularly sub-cultured for culture maintenance within 25–28 days. The data were recorded every 25–28 days after each experiment. The experiments were repeated three times with 30 replicates per treatment. One replicate means one culture vessel. The results are expressed as mean ± SD of three experiments. The data are subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a P < 0.05 level of significance using SPSS v.17 (SPSS, Chicago, USA). Grouping of means is done using Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT). For homogeneity assessment, each PCR-amplified fragment generated using SCoT and ISSR markers was scored as binary data, with the presence and absence of bands recorded as 1 and 0, respectively. Results and discussion

Initiation of callus culture

The thin-layer stem disc technique proved as an efficient method for in vitro establishment of aseptic culture. However, in vitro somatic embryogenesis is influenced by various factors, including the type of explant, the concentrations of both endogenous and exogenous PGRs, genotype, and the culture conditions23–25. In the present study, some of the thin disc explants began to form an undifferentiated mass of cells (Primary Callus) after 16–18 days of inoculation on nutrient media. Among all inoculated explants, a maximum of 72% explants responded to callogenesis (Fig. 2B, Table 1). Thin disc explants with wider surface area and lower endogenous hormone level played a stimulatory role in nutrient uptake and exogenous hormone-derived callogenesis24. Out of different combinations of PGRs, the maximum 72.22% response (Table 2) was observed on MS medium supplemented with a combination of 1 mg l−1of each auxin (NAA) and cytokinin (BAP). On this combination, creamy white, granular (embryogenic), and fast-growing callus was observed (Fig. 2C). Similar results were obtained from blackberry TCL (Thin Cell Layer) cultures on cytokinin and auxin fortified MS medium9. The maximum intensity of callus was obtained on MS media containing a combination of 1 mg l−1 NAA+2mg l−1 2,4-D auxins (Table 2). However, the callus developed only in the auxin-fortified medium was soft, fragile (non-embryogenic), and slow-growing. Previous reports on the TCL technique by Ahn et al. (1996)26 on Panax ginseng, Cruz De Carvalho et al. (2000)8 on Phaseolus vulgaris, Sabooni & Shekafandeh (2017)9 on Rubus hirtus and Rubus sanctus, and Parthibhan et al. (2018)10 on Dendrobium aqueum observed that cytokinin-containing media, either alone or combined with auxin, were more efficient than 2,4-D for the callus initiation from TCL explant.

Callus proliferation and differentiation

The primary callus was transferred to fresh nutrient medium supplemented with different PGR combinations for further proliferation and differentiation. The use of a suitable PGR combination is a key factor for callus differentiation into somatic embryos27. Here, Alhagi maurorum-derived STDs exhibited higher potential for regeneration and improved callus quality in terms of their regenerative nature on MS media with reduced concentration of BAP (0.5 mg l−1) along with 1.0 mg l−1 of NAA+additives (Table 3). The compact and yellowish-green callus and globular-shaped embryos were observed on this combination of PGRs (Fig. 2D). The synchronized and repetitive formation of morphologically typical globular-to-cotyledonary stage embryos across independent culture cycles further supports their embryogenic nature rather than organogenic regeneration. When cultures were further transferred to the same nutrient media, different stages of embryo maturation were observed (Fig. 2E&F). On higher concentration (1.0 mg l−1) of BAP, differentiation was observed, but response and differentiation potential were less compared to lower concentration of BAP (Table 3). A higher concentration ratio of auxin to cytokinin was also recommended by Li et al. (2025)28 for somatic embryo regeneration of Tilia cordata. Generally, auxin, specifically 2,4-D, alone is well known as the master player of somatic embryogenesis by driving auxin homeostasis in the explant27. In the case of Alhagi maurorum, the 2,4-D augmented medium was found incompetent for further regeneration (non-embryogenic). However, the present study correlates with the previous studies on the tea plants, where 2,4-D was unable to induce somatic embryogenesis29. In contrast, the combined application of cytokinin and auxin was found to be optimal for regulating somatic embryogenesis in A. maurorum. Among auxins, NAA is known for its chemical stability and sustained physiological activity in vitro, which contributes to prolonged embryogenic competence. Several studies have demonstrated that NAA in combination with BAP effectively induces and maintains embryogenic callus and promotes somatic embryo development, supporting its successful application in the present study30.

Germination of somatic embryos

The germination of somatic embryos to complete plantlets is a bottleneck step in the successful development of in vitro methods. It is more wearisome in the case of woody plant species growing in challenging environmental conditions31,32. In the present study, mature embryos were transferred to germination nutrient media devoid of or containing different combinations of PGRs. The MS nutrient medium containing BAP (0.5 mg l−1)+NAA (1.0mg l−1) was found to be optimum for the successful germination of STD derived somatic embryos (Fig. 2G). Cytokinins such as BAP promote cell division, meristem activation, and cotyledon expansion, which are essential for the transition from the cotyledonary stage to active germination, while auxins like NAA support root meristem differentiation and vascular patterning, enabling whole plantlet development30,33. On this PGR’s combination maximum 66.67 ± 1.73% germination rate was observed, with 23.33% of single-step complete plantlet conversion from in vitro-derived somatic embryos (Table 4). A similar observation of BAP+NAA effectiveness on somatic embryo germination was previously reported in STD derived embryos of sugarcane6. In terms of germination to only the shoot was also higher at this concentration of PGRs, with a 36.67% highest shoot germination. These shoots were further transferred to in vitro rooting media as per the previously described method by Agarwal et al. (2015)13. A higher concentration of BAP (1.0 mg l−1) had an inhibitory effect on embryo germination, with the lowest 18.89 ± 2.08% germination rate (Table 4). The prolonged exposure to PGRs may be detrimental to germination due to the formation of abnormal shoot apices32,34. However, the PGRs supplementation to nutrient media is critical for germination; without PGRs, embryos faced difficulty in germination; rather, 50% of embryos remained in a close cotyledonary stage. Although the germinated embryos were restricted to only root development, no complete plant or shoot formation was observed on PGR-free nutrient medium. It may be due to insufficient endogenous hormone levels, which alone may not overcome developmental constraints or dormancy-like responses. Without exogenous PGR support, key developmental pathways such as meristem activation and auxin gradient formation may be too weak to drive embryo emergence, resulting in incomplete germination33. The previous reports on somatic embryogenesis also found exogenous fortification of PGRs and their exposure as a key to somatic embryo germination to complete plant or shoot32,35,36. In addition to qualitative regeneration frequency and based on preliminary observations, approximately 100 mg of embryogenic callus yielded an average of 3–4 regenerated plantlets after ~ 50–55 days of maturation and germination. The successfully developed plantlets were subjected to hardening and acclimatization. About 75% plantlets successfully acclimatized and were transferred to polybags for field transfer (Fig. 2H).

Genetic homogeneity assessment

Dedifferentiation of explant cells, exposure to artificial conditions, and extended culture duration cause the genetic and epigenetic changes and introduce somaclonal variations among developing in vitro plantlets37. Assessment of genetic homogeneity is a prerequisite for the development of the somatic embryogenesis method aimed at cloning the elite genotype10. PCR-based molecular markers can be used as a promising tool to evaluate the genetic homogeneity of in vitro developed plantlets38. Therefore, a combination of ISSR (specific for non-coding region) and SCoT (specific for coding region) markers was used to validate the genetic makeup of somatic embryo developed plantlets in comparison with the mother plant. Both the marker systems are simple, easy for experimentation, reliable, cost-effective, widely used, and proven efficient for Alhagi maurorum genetic homogeneity in our previous report13,37. Here, six markers, three of each ISSR and SCoT, were employed based on clear and reproducible bands in initial screening (Table 5). A total of 31 bands were observed, of which 30 were monomorphic among all 6 plant samples. This shows the high percentage (96.66%) of genetic homogeneity. SCoT-31 produced the maximum number of bands (10) among all the primers studied. SCoT-28 produced 5 bands, of which 4 were monomorphic and 1 was polymorphic. ISSR markers produced fewer bands than SCoT markers (Fig. 3, Table 5). These findings correlate with the findings of Agarwal et al. (2015)13 on Alhagi maurorum, Faroda et al. (2025)37 on Calligonum polygonoides, Shilpha et al. (2021)39 on Solanum trilobatum, and Agarwal et al. (2022)40 on Ziziphus mauritiana, who used SCoT and ISSR markers to assess the genomic integrity and uniformity of in vitro regenerants.

Conclusion

In this study, a reproducible system for in vitro plant regeneration via stem thin disc-based somatic embryogenesis was developed in Alhagi maurorum. MS medium supplemented with 1 mg l−1 NAA and 1 mg l−1 BAP was found optimal for callus induction, while embryo induction, maturation, and plantlet regeneration were effectively achieved on MS medium containing 1 mg l−1 NAA and 0.5 mg l−1 BAP. Genetic homogeneity analysis using ISSR and SCoT markers indicated the genetic stability of the regenerated plantlets. The developed protocol provides an effective approach for overcoming regeneration limitations associated with woody explants and may facilitate large-scale propagation of elite genotypes of Alhagi maurorum. Furthermore, this system may have potential applications in genetic transformation, synthetic seed production, and germplasm conservation.
