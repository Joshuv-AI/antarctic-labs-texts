Impact of changes in sarcopenia status on incident cardiovascular disease and mortality in older adults
Authors: Na-Noom Jeong, Hyun Joung Lim, Chang Oh Kim, Min-Ju Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcopenia is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality. However, the impact of longitudinal changes in sarcopenia remains unclear. We investigated the relationship between changes in sarcopenia status and the risk of CVD and mortality in older Korean adults. This study included 2,997 older adults aged ≥ 65 years from the Korea Urban and Rural Elderly Study. Sarcopenia status was classified as non-sarcopenia, possible sarcopenia, or functional sarcopenia. Changes in sarcopenia status were assessed at baseline and during a follow-up visit 4 years later. Incident CVD was defined as hospitalization for ischemic heart disease or cerebrovascular disease. All-cause mortality was determined using death records provided by Statistics Korea. Associations were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. During the follow-up, a total of 423 incident CVD cases and 438 deaths were identified. At baseline, individuals with functional sarcopenia had a higher risk of incident CVD and mortality than those without, after adjusting for all covariates. Similarly, participants who progressed from non-sarcopenia to sarcopenia or had stable sarcopenia showed increased CVD and mortality risk. Sarcopenia was associated with elevated risks of CVD and mortality. Introduction

Sarcopenia is a multifactorial skeletal muscle disorder characterized by a progressive decline in muscle strength, mass, and function, primarily affecting older adults1–3. It leads to a range of adverse clinical outcomes, including an increased risk of falls and fractures, progression of physical disability, reduced quality of life, and elevated mortality risk4. A meta-analysis reported that the prevalence of sarcopenia was higher in patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) than in the general population, with pooled prevalences of 35% and 13%, respectively, indicating that sarcopenia is approximately twice as common among individuals with CVDs5. In Korea, the pooled prevalence of sarcopenia among adults aged > 65 years was 13.1%, highlighting the considerable health burden of sarcopenia in this population6.

CVD remains the predominant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and substantially contributes to global healthcare costs and disability7,8. The global prevalence of CVD is expected to almost double from 598 million in 2025 to 1.14 billion in 2050, with annual cardiovascular deaths increasing by 73.4% to reach 35.6 million by 20508. CVD is a complex condition influenced by a wide range of both non-modifiable and modifiable risk factors, including advanced age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking, unhealthy diet, inadequate sleep duration, and physical inactivity9–12. In older adults, sarcopenia is recognized as an important geriatric syndrome associated with CVD, potentially reflecting shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction13. Several studies have demonstrated an association between sarcopenia and CVD outcomes.

A recent population-based study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2011–2018) reported that individuals with sarcopenia had a higher risk of CVD among 7,702 participants aged ≥ 20 years14. In a UK Biobank study, patients with sarcopenia and type 2 diabetes had a significantly higher risk of incident CVD15. Another study using data from the China Longitudinal Study demonstrated that individuals who progressed from non-sarcopenia to sarcopenia were significantly associated with the risk of new-onset CVD16.

Furthermore, sarcopenia is an independent risk factor for mortality17. A longitudinal analysis of 17,920 adults from the Korea NHANES (2008–2011) study reported that individuals with sarcopenia had a higher risk of all-cause mortality18. Consistent with these findings, a prospective analysis of 10,778 adults in the NHANES (1999–2006 and 2011–2018) study showed that those with both sarcopenia and metabolic syndrome had a higher risk of all-cause mortality than those without either condition19.

Recently, the Korean Working Group on Sarcopenia (KWGS) established diagnostic and assessment criteria for sarcopenia in older Korean adults using the Delphi method20. In particular, the KWGS introduced a new concept termed “functional sarcopenia,” characterized by reduced muscle strength and physical performance in the absence of low muscle mass20. A subsequent study applying the KWGS guidelines in the Korean population further demonstrated the clinical relevance of functional sarcopenia, showing that this phenotype is associated with greater frailty than sarcopenia21.

Although previous studies have identified an association between sarcopenia, CVD, and mortality, evidence regarding the impact of longitudinal changes in sarcopenia status on cardiovascular outcomes and mortality remains limited, particularly among older adults. This study aimed to examine the impact of changes in sarcopenia status on incident CVD and mortality using the linked Korean Urban Rural Elderly (KURE) study and the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims dataset.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 2,997 participants were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 71.7 years, and 68.0% women. The demographic characteristics of the participants according to their baseline sarcopenia status are shown in Table 1. The prevalence of non-sarcopenia was 70.0% (n = 2,098), whereas 23.0% (n = 689) and 7.0% (n = 210) of participants had possible and functional sarcopenia, respectively. Compared with participants in the non-sarcopenia group, those in the functional sarcopenia group were olde, had lower levels of education, regular physical activity, and HGS, had a smaller social network size, and demonstrated poorer performance on the TUG test. They were more likely to be women and to have lower insurance status, obesity, IADL impairment, cognitive impairment, hypertension, and diabetes. To analyze changes in sarcopenia status, 1,990 participants were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 71.0 years, and 68.5% were women (Table 2). Compared with participants in the stable non-sarcopenia group, those in the stable sarcopenia group were older and had lower levels of education and HGS, lower participation in regular physical activity, a smaller social network size, and poorer performance on the TUG test. In addition, they were more likely to be women and to have cognitive impairment and hypertension.

Regarding CVD, the median follow-up durations were 9.5 years for the baseline sarcopenia status and 5.7 years for the analysis of changes in sarcopenia status. A total of 423 incident CVD cases were identified in the baseline sarcopenia analysis, and 184 cases were identified in the change analysis. For all-cause mortality, the median follow-up durations were 9.6 years for the baseline sarcopenia status analysis and 6.2 years for the analysis of changes in sarcopenia status. A total of 438 deaths were identified in the baseline sarcopenia status group, and 140 deaths were identified in the change analysis.

Risk of incident CVD and mortality according to baseline sarcopenia status

Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that individuals with functional sarcopenia had significantly higher CVD and all-cause mortality rates compared with those with non-sarcopenia (Log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. 1a and b). Table 3 shows the risk of incident CVD and mortality according to baseline sarcopenia status. During the follow-up, participants with functional sarcopenia had a higher incidence rate of CVD (29.0 per 1,000 person-years) compared with those with non-sarcopenia (14.3 per 1,000 person-years). In the fully adjusted model, individuals with functional sarcopenia had a significantly higher risk of incident CVD compared with those with non-sarcopenia (hazard ratio [HR], 1.918; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.365–2.697). For all-cause mortality, the incidence rate was highest among participants with functional sarcopenia (33.5 per 1,000 person-years), followed by those with possible sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia. After adjustment for all covariates, functional sarcopenia was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR, 1.446; 95% CI 1.057–1.976), whereas the significant association for possible sarcopenia was attenuated and no longer statistically significant. In the CVD subtype analysis, functional sarcopenia was associated with an increased risk of cerebrovascular disease in model 2 (HR, 1.988; 95% CI 1.342–2.945), whereas the association with ischemic heart disease was not statistically significant (Supplementary Table S1). The LASSO-selected Cox models showed results consistent with those of the main and subtype analyses (Supplementary Table S3).

Risk of incident CVD and mortality according to change in sarcopenia status

Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that individuals with changes from non-sarcopenia to sarcopenia and stable sarcopenia had significantly higher CVD and all-cause mortality rates compared with those with stable non-sarcopenia (Log-rank P < 0.05) (Fig. 2a and b). Table 4 shows the risk of incident CVD and mortality according to changes in sarcopenia status. With respect to changes in sarcopenia status, compared with those with stable non-sarcopenia, individuals with a change from non-sarcopenia to sarcopenia had a significantly higher risk of incident CVD after adjusting for all covariates (HR, 1.564; 95% CI 1.074–2.277). Similarly, participants with stable sarcopenia showed an increased risk of incident CVD (HR, 1.649; 95% CI 1.097–2.480). For all-cause mortality, compared with individuals with stable non-sarcopenia, those who progressed from non-sarcopenia to sarcopenia had a significantly higher risk (HR, 1.670; 95% CI 1.087–2.567), and this increased risk was observed in those with stable sarcopenia (HR, 1.649; 95% CI 1.037–2.623). In contrast, individuals who changed from sarcopenia to non-sarcopenia did not show a significant association with incident CVD or all-cause mortality. In the CVD subtype analysis, these two groups were significantly associated with an increased risk of cerebrovascular disease (HR, 2.099; 95% CI 1.348–3.267 and HR, 2.075; 95% CI 1.279–3.368, respectively), whereas no significant associations were observed for ischemic heart disease (Supplementary Table S2). Similar results were observed in the LASSO-selected Cox models, which showed increased risks of incident CVD, cerebrovascular disease, and all-cause mortality, but not ischemic heart disease (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study examined the impact of baseline sarcopenia and its longitudinal changes on incident CVD and all-cause mortality in older Korean adults. Our results showed that baseline functional sarcopenia was a significant predictor of CVD and mortality. Furthermore, participants who developed or maintained sarcopenia had a higher risk of incident CVD and mortality than those who remained in the stable non-sarcopenia group. In contrast, a change from sarcopenia to non-sarcopenia was not significantly associated with the incidence of CVD and mortality.

Similar to our baseline findings, a cross-sectional study including 2,432 Chinese participants found that both sarcopenic lean and sarcopenic overweight/obesity groups had substantially higher odds of myocardial infarction than the non-sarcopenic lean group, with adjusted odds ratios of 3.08 (95% CI 1.28–7.45) and 4.07 (95% CI 1.31–12.62), respectively22. Another study, a prospective UK Biobank cohort study including 452,931 participants without a history of CVD, reported that individuals with sarcopenic obesity had a higher risks of incident CVD (HR, 1.42; 95% CI 1.31–1.55), CVD mortality (HR, 1.78; 95% CI 1.45–2.18), and all-cause mortality (HR, 1.31; 95% CI 1.18–1.45) than those without sarcopenia and obesity23. Consistent with these findings, studies applying the KWGS criteria have demonstrated that functional sarcopenia is significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality24. Furthermore, the Sarcopenia Definitions and Outcomes Consortium (SDOC) proposed an evidence-based definition of sarcopenia based on low muscle strength and poor physical performance, which is conceptually consistent with functional sarcopenia as defined by the KWGS25. SDOC-based sarcopenia has also been associated with adverse outcomes, such as falls and fractures26. These results support our findings, demonstrating that functional sarcopenia is an independent risk factor, even after adjusting for all covariates.

Our study extends the baseline analysis by using follow-up data to evaluate the impact of longitudinal changes in sarcopenia status. Although evidence regarding changes in sarcopenia status remains limited, several recent cohort studies have reported associations between changes in sarcopenia status and the risk of CVD and all-cause mortality. In particular, Bing et al. revealed that individuals who exhibited changes in sarcopenia status from sarcopenia to possible sarcopenia/non-sarcopenia had a lower risk of incident CVD (incidence rate ratio [IRR], 0.61; 95% CI 0.43–0.87 in the China health and retirement longitudinal study of 6,608 participants; IRR, 0.20; 95% CI 0.11–0.39 in the Health and Retirement Study of 4,316 participants)27. Similarly, a longitudinal analysis using data from the China health and retirement longitudinal study reported that new-onset and persistent sarcopenia were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, with HR of 1.86 (95% CI 1.39–2.47) and 2.48 (95% CI 1.78–3.46), respectively28. However, our study did not find a significant association in the improvement group defined by a change from sarcopenia to non-sarcopenia status. This finding should be interpreted cautiously for several reasons. First, since CVD and mortality develop through long-term cumulative exposure to aging-related and cardiometabolic risk factors, a transition from sarcopenia to non-sarcopenia during a single follow-up interval may not be sufficient to immediately normalize accumulated cardiovascular risk. Furthermore, sarcopenia and CVD are closely linked through shared cardiometabolic and inflammatory pathways13, suggesting that the consequences of prior sarcopenia status may persist even after apparent improvement in muscle strength or physical activity. Second, because sarcopenia status was determined using only two assessments, the observed improvement may have partly reflected short-term variability or regression to the mean rather than sustained physiological recovery. Third, the improvement group was relatively small (n = 103), and the absolute number of events was very low, with only 11 CVD events and 4 deaths, which may have resulted in unstable estimates and limited statistical power. Similarly, the subtype-specific findings should also be considered exploratory. Although both baseline sarcopenia status and changes in sarcopenia status were significantly associated with cerebrovascular disease, no significant associations were observed for ischemic heart disease. However, the number of ischemic heart disease events was substantially small, which may have limited the statistical power to detect significant associations. Therefore, further studies with repeated assessment of sarcopenia status, larger sample sizes, and sufficient numbers of subtype-specific CVD events are needed to clarify whether sustained improvement in sarcopenia reduces long-term CVD and mortality risk and whether these associations differ across CVD subtypes.

Previous studies using the HGS and TUG tests, which were also employed to define sarcopenia in our study, have reported the following findings. In Korea, a prospective cohort of older adults from the NHIS, including 40,774 participants aged 66 years, showed that individuals with abnormal TUG results had a higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR, 1.23; 95% CI 1.02–1.48) than those with normal TUG results29. In a British community-based cohort comprising 5,300 participants, individuals with low stable HGS had a higher risk of incident CVD than those with high stable HGS (HR, 2.17; 95% CI 1.52–3.09)30. A longitudinal study on Korean older adults further demonstrated that individuals in the stable low HGS trajectory group had a higher risk of all-cause mortality than those in the stable high HGS group (HR, 3.46; 95% CI 2.17–6.69 in men and HR, 2.69; 95% CI 1.39–5.21 in women)31. Another study in the panel on health and aging of Singaporean older adults, including 2,673 participants, also reported that each 1 kg annual increase in HGS was associated with a 13% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR, 0.87; 95% CI 0.82–0.93)32. A population-based Singapore Chinese Healthy Study (age ≥ 63 years) on 13,789 participants reported that individuals with weak HGS and slow TUG test results had higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR, 3.93; 95% CI 3.06–5.05) and CVD mortality (HR, 4.34; 95% CI 2.75–6.86) than those with strong HGS and fast TUG test results33. These findings further support the use of muscle strength and physical performance as clinical indicators for identifying high-risk older adults. In our study, sarcopenia status was defined using functional sarcopenia criteria based on handgrip strength and TUG test. This definition may provide important insight into the role of neuromuscular impairment in the relationship between sarcopenia and CVD. Unlike sarcopenia defined primarily by low muscle mass, functional sarcopenia is characterized by decreased muscle strength and impaired physical performance despite preserved muscle mass, that this phenotype may reflect deterioration in muscle quality and neuromuscular function rather than reduced muscle quantity alone. Previous evidence suggests that age-related declines in muscle strength are not fully explained by reductions in muscle mass; instead, neural and non–mass-dependent mechanisms, including impaired neural activation, loss of motor units, altered denervation–reinnervation processes, and reduced contractile capacity, may substantially contribute to muscle weakness, slow mobility, and poor physical performance in older adults13,34. Because muscle strength and physical performance are closely related to mobility and overall physiological reserve, functional sarcopenia may also capture broader vulnerability in older adults beyond skeletal muscle mass alone. This vulnerability may, in turn, be linked to CVD risk through shared inflammatory, metabolic and vascular pathways13.

Sarcopenia and CVD share a pathophysiological basis, involving inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction35. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, can act on muscle receptors to activate catabolic pathways while suppressing anabolic signaling, particularly the insulin-like growth factor-1/protein kinase B-mammalian target of rapamycin pathway, thereby promoting muscle protein degradation or impairing muscle regeneration36. Elevated inflammatory mediators observed in individuals with sarcopenia may also directly compromise vascular endothelial function, contributing to endothelial dysfunction and accelerating the development of atherosclerosis37. Aging is also associated with increased generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and a decline in antioxidant defense, resulting in elevated oxidative stress38. Excess ROS promotes muscle protein breakdown while impairing protein synthesis, ultimately contributing to muscle mass loss35. In addition, increased ROS induces vasoconstriction and accelerates atherosclerotic plaque formation, which may subsequently lead to various CVDs39.

Beyond cardiovascular outcomes, sarcopenia may increase the risk of all-cause mortality through systemic vulnerability, characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation, malnutrition, and reduced functional decline38,40. These conditions predispose individuals to adverse clinical events, such as falls, fractures, and hospitalization, which are known contributors to mortality in older adults41,42. Chronic inflammation contributes to muscle catabolism and functional decline, which may increase the vulnerability to infections and adverse clinical outcomes43.

This study has several strengths. The linkage of the KURE cohort with NHIS data allowed for a more accurate and complete ascertainment of CVD outcomes, including precise onset dates and long-term follow-up, thereby minimizing recall bias and potential outcome misclassification. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between changes in sarcopenia status and new-onset CVD and mortality in older Korean adults.

However, this study has some limitations. First, sarcopenia was assessed only at two time points, which may not fully capture dynamic changes. In addition, because changes in sarcopenia status were determined based on both the baseline and first follow-up assessment, follow-up for the change analysis began at the first follow-up rather than at baseline. Consequently, the change analysis had a shorter observation period than the baseline analysis, which may have resulted in fewer CVD and mortality events and reduced statistical power. Therefore, further studies with a longer follow-up after changes in sarcopenia status are needed to evaluate the long-term impact of sarcopenia trajectories on CVD and mortality. Second, because muscle mass data were unavailable, the present study evaluated sarcopenia based on muscle strength and physical performance. However, previous studies employing the same assessment tools as those used in our study have reported findings consistent with our results. According to the KWGS and SDOC, functional sarcopenia is characterized by concurrent low muscle strength and poor physical performance without reduced muscle mass. Therefore, further studies incorporating muscle mass measurements are warranted to validate these findings. Third, although we adjusted for a wide range of potential confounders in our dataset, the possibility of residual confounding factors cannot be completely excluded. In conclusion, functional sarcopenia and unfavorable changes in sarcopenia status are associated with an increased risk of CVD and all-cause mortality in older Korean adults. In particular, individuals who developed or maintained sarcopenia over time had a higher risk of adverse outcomes compared with those who remained in the non-sarcopenia group. These findings emphasize the importance of early identification and longitudinal monitoring of functional decline, along with targeted interventions such as resistance exercise, balance training, and gait training for older adults with reduced handgrip strength or abnormal TUG performance.

Methods

Study participants

The KURE study, conducted by the Korea National Institute of Health in 2012, is a community-based prospective longitudinal cohort study conducted in urban and rural areas. The KURE cohort collected data from face-to-face interviews, laboratory tests, and physical examinations of a Korean population aged ≥ 65 years. The detailed cohort design and protocol of the KURE cohort have been previously described44,45. The baseline of the KURE was 2012–2015, and the first follow-up visits were conducted between 2016 and 2019. The KURE cohort was linked to NHIS claims data.

Of the 3,517 participants who provided baseline data, 520 were excluded based on the following criteria: unmatched with the NHIS (n = 12), history of CVD (n = 393), missing sarcopenia index (n = 23), and missing covariate data (n = 92) (Fig. 3a). To analyze changes in sarcopenia status, we excluded 1,007 of the 2,997 eligible baseline participants based on the following criteria: lost to the first follow-up (n = 809), history of CVD (n = 95), and missing sarcopenia index (n = 103) (Fig. 3b). Therefore, complete-case analyses were performed using participants with available data on sarcopenia status, covariates, and outcome information for each corresponding analysis.

Flowchart of study participants. (a) Baseline sarcopenia status. (b) Change in sarcopenia status.

All participants provided informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Korea National Institute of Health (KNIH IRB no. KDCA-2024-06-11). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of sarcopenia status

Sarcopenia was defined according to the modified criteria proposed by the 2023 KWGS, which consists of three components: physical performance, muscle strength, and muscle mass. Based on these criteria, participants were classified as non-sarcopenia, possible sarcopenia, functional sarcopenia, sarcopenia, and severe sarcopenia20: non-sarcopenia, defined as no impairment in muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance; possible sarcopenia, defined as impairment in any single component; functional sarcopenia, defined as low muscle strength and low physical performance without low muscle mass; sarcopenia, defined as low muscle mass accompanied by either low muscle strength or low physical performance; and severe sarcopenia, defined as the presence of all three components. As muscle mass data were unavailable in the KURE dataset, we defined sarcopenia status based solely on muscle strength and physical performance. Accordingly, three categories were used in this study: non-sarcopenia, possible and functional sarcopenia. Non-sarcopenia was defined as high muscle strength and physical performance. Possible sarcopenia was defined as low muscle strength or physical performance, whereas functional sarcopenia was defined as low muscle strength and physical performance. Low muscle strength was defined as a handgrip strength (HGS) < 28 kg in men and < 18 kg in women20. HGS was measured twice for each hand using a digital hand dynamometer (T.K.K 5401, Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and the highest value among all trials was used for the analysis. Low physical performance was defined as a timed up-and-go (TUG) test result of ≥ 12 s20. The TUG test was performed on a flat walking surface according to the standardized protocol.

Changes in sarcopenia status were evaluated based on assessments at baseline and the first follow-up visit conducted 4 years later. Sarcopenia was defined as the presence of possible or functional sarcopenia for the analysis of changes in sarcopenia status. Participants were classified into four groups: stable non-sarcopenia, sarcopenia to non-sarcopenia, non-sarcopenia to sarcopenia, stable sarcopenia. Stable non-sarcopenia was defined as non-sarcopenia at both baseline and the first follow-up visit. Sarcopenia to non-sarcopenia was defined as sarcopenia at baseline and non-sarcopenia at the first follow-up visit. Non-sarcopenia to sarcopenia was defined as non-sarcopenia at baseline and sarcopenia at the first follow-up visit. Stable sarcopenia was defined as sarcopenia at both baseline and the first follow-up visit.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was incident CVD, and the secondary outcome was all-cause mortality. Incident CVD was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for ischemic heart disease (I20–I25) and cerebrovascular disease (I60–I69) based on hospitalization for at least 2 days46. Both primary and secondary diagnoses were used to define incident CVD. In additional subtype analyses, incident CVD was classified into ischemic heart disease and cerebrovascular diseases. Ischemic heart disease was defined using ICD-10 codes I20–I25, and cerebrovascular diseases were defined using ICD-10 codes I60–I69. All-cause mortality was determined using death records provided by Statistics Korea. For the baseline analysis, CVD incidence and mortality records were used from baseline assessment data to December 31, 2023. CVD incidence and mortality records were used from baseline assessment data to December 31, 2023. To analyze changes in sarcopenia status, the follow-up period was calculated from the date of the first follow-up assessment to December 31, 2023. Accordingly, the follow-up duration differed between the baseline and change analyses because of their different starting points.

Other variables

Baseline demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory data were collected. The demographic characteristics used in the analysis included age (continuous in years), sex (men and women), residence (urban and rural), and educational level (middle school and below, and high school and above). Instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) impairment was assessed using the IADL score, which ranged from 0 to 7, with scores ≥ 1 indicating IADL impairment. Insurance status was initially classified into 20 income quantiles and subsequently regrouped into three categories (0–6, 7–13, and 14–20). Social networks were assessed based on the frequency, size, and closeness of social contacts, reflecting the level of social connectedness and support (range, 0–7). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (non-smoker, ex-smoker, and current smoker), drinking status (non-drinker, ex-drinker, and current drinker), obesity (body mass index ≥ 25 kg/m2), and regular physical activity. Anthropometric measurements included systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Laboratory data included fasting glucose, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL cholesterol). Comorbidities included cognitive impairment, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus. Cognitive impairment was defined as a score of < 24 on the Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, use of antihypertensive medications, or ICD-10 codes I10–I13, I15. Dyslipidemia was defined as TC levels ≥ 240 mg/dL, TG levels ≥ 200 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol levels ≤ 40 mg/dL, the use of lipid-lowering medications, or ICD-10 code E78. Diabetes was defined as a fasting blood glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, use of oral antidiabetic agents, insulin, diagnosis of diabetes, or ICD-10 codes E10–E14.

Statistical analyses

The demographic characteristics of the participants were presented according to their baseline sarcopenia status. Continuous variables are described as means with standard deviations. Categorical variables are described as number with percentages. Differences across baseline sarcopenia status groups were examined using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate HR and 95% CI for incident CVD, CVD subtypes, and all-cause mortality according to baseline sarcopenia status. For multivariate analysis, Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex, and Model 2 was further adjusted for residence, educational level, insurance status, IADL impairment, social network, smoking status, drinking status, obesity, physical activity, cognitive impairment, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus. The same models were applied to examine the risk of incident CVD, CVD subtypes, and all-cause mortality according to changes in sarcopenia status. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using the ZPH option in SAS PROC PHREG, based on Schoenfeld residuals. When one or more covariates violated the proportional hazards assumption, stratified Cox proportional hazards models were fitted by including the covariates violating the assumption as stratification variables. Multicollinearity among independent variables included in the fully adjusted models was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Variables with VIF > 5.0 were considered to have substantial multicollinearity and cannot be included in the Cox proportional hazards regression model. To reduce model complexity and evaluate the robustness of the multivariable models, we additionally performed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression for variable selection. Candidate variables included the exposure variable and all covariates considered in the fully adjusted model. The variables selected by LASSO were included in multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to estimate HR and 95% CI. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were estimated, and differences between sarcopenia statuses were evaluated using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using the SAS Enterprise Guide (version 7.15) and R software (version 4.0.3). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
