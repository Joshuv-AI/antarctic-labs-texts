Comparison of treatment outcomes between slow coagulation transscleral cyclophotocoagulation and micropulse transscleral laser treatment
Authors: Young Hoon Hwang, Sharon Lee, Mijin Kim, Jaewan Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study compared treatment outcomes of slow coagulation transscleral cyclophotocoagulation (SC-CPC, 65 eyes) and micropulse transscleral laser treatment (MPL, 134 eyes) in patients with medically uncontrolled glaucoma. Success was defined as achieving an intraocular pressure (IOP) of 6–21 mmHg with a ≥ 20% reduction from baseline, no reoperation for glaucoma, and no loss of light-perception vision. Visual acuity, number of glaucoma medication, corneal endothelial cell count, aqueous flare values, and complications were analyzed. At 12 months, mean IOP decreased from 32.2 ± 13.4 to 17.9 ± 8.3 mmHg in the SC-CPC group and from 26.4 ± 10.8 to 16.5 ± 6.8 mmHg in the MPL group. No significant changes were observed in visual acuity, medication count, or corneal endothelial cell count. Aqueous flare values increased immediately after the procedure and gradually decreased in both groups, with greater changes at 1 week significantly associated with greater IOP reduction (p < 0.05). Pupillary abnormalities were found in 5 eyes (4.5%) of the MPL group, with no severe complications. The 12-month success rates were 50.1% for SC-CPC and 38.2% for MPL (p = 0.131). Both SC-CPC and MPL effectively controlled IOP, with early postoperative aqueous flare values predicting treatment outcomes.Keywords: Glaucoma, Micropulse laser, Cyclophotocoagulation, SurgerySubject terms: Eye diseases, Optic nerve diseases

Introduction

High intraocular pressure (IOP) is the most important risk factor for the development and progression of glaucoma. Various medications, laser, and surgery have been developed to control IOP by inhibiting aqueous production and/or facilitating aqueous outflow. Cyclophotocoagulation (CPC) has been introduced to control IOP by reducing aqueous production from ciliary body1–3. Traditional transscleral CPC uses continuous mode diode laser, with the power increased until an audible explosive, cavitating tissue-derived pop sound is heard1–3. Despite its effective IOP control, the high energy setting of CPC raises the possibility of complications, including phthisis bulbi, chronic hypotony, inflammation, or visual disturbance1–3. Consequently, CPC has mainly been used only when incisional surgeries have a high risk of failure, have already failed, or eyes with no (or minimal) useful vision1–3.

To reduce the complications of traditional CPC, modifications to laser setting or application of new lasers have been suggested. For instance, slow coagulation CPC (SC-CPC) technique uses lower energy and a longer duration of application compared to traditional CPC4–12. After its introduction, several studies reported that SC-CPC provided effective IOP control with fewer complications than traditional CPC4–12. Micropluse laser treatment (MPL) uses a micropulse diode laser, alternating between treatment (pulses: ON cycles) and rest (thermal relaxation times: OFF cycles) peroids13–20. These OFF cycles reduce thermal damage or necrosis which can be caused by traditional CPC13–20.

Given the reduced complications compared to traditional CPC, both SC-CPC and MPL can be considered alternatives to traditional CPC. Several studies have compared the treatment outcomes between traditional CPC and SC-CPC10 or traditional CPC and MPL21–23. However, to date, little has been reported regarding the comparison of clinical outcomes between SC-CPC and MPL.

Laser application to the ciliary body induces intraocular inflammation, acting as a ‘double edged sword’. Inflammation plays a role in the IOP reduction either by suppressing aqueous humour production and/or increasing aqueous humour outflow24,25. Conversely, inadvertent severe, chronic inflammation can cause complications, such as synechia, cystoid macular edema, or corneal decompensation. Therefore, assessing inflammation after SC-CPC and MPL is important. Detecting mild inflammation and measuring its degree quantitatively using a slit-lamp biomicroscope is challenging. Laser flare photometry enables the measurement of the degree of inflammation in the anterior chamber by assessing the protein concentration26. To date, only a few studies have investigated aqueous flare levels after traditional CPC or MPL using a laser flare photometer27–29, and little is known about the aqueous flare change after SC-CPC.

This study was performed to compare the efficacy (IOP, number of IOP-lowering medication) and safety (visual acuity, aqueous flare, corneal endothelial cell, and other complications) of SC-CPC and MPL.

Materials and methods

Participants

This retrospective, observational study protocol was approved with the waiver of informed consent by the Institutional Review Board at Korea National Institute for Bioethics Policy, Republic of Korea (P01-202308-01-034). All the procedures conformed to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients with glaucoma who underwent SC-CPC or MPL at Central Seoul Eye Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea between Jan 2021 and December 2022 were enrolled.

Each participant underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination that included visual acuity assessments, IOP measurements using a Goldmann applanation tonometer, anterior segment examination using slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and optic nerve head and fundus examination. Glaucoma was defined as the presence of glaucomatous changes (i.e., a large cup-to-disc ratio and neuroretinal rim narrowing) in optic nerve head and a retinal nerve fibre layer defect observed in fundus examination. Automated visual field test was performed using a Humphrey Visual Field Analyser (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). The visual field test was initially performed with the 24 − 2 Swedish Interactive Thresholding Algorithm (SITA) standard strategy using a size III stimulus. When useful information was not obtained by 24 − 2 SITA strategy due to severely depressed retinal sensitivity, 10 − 2 visual field tests using either size III or V stimuli were subsequently performed.

The indication for SC-CPC or MPL was medically uncontrolled glaucoma in patients over 18 years of age, regardless of the glaucoma severity and type, visual acuity, and previous history of glaucoma surgery. Eyes with previous cyclodestructive procedures, including continuous wave CPC using traditional setting, SC-CPC, or MPL were excluded from the present report. In our clinical practice, when IOP is not sufficiently controlled by medication or selective laser trabeculoplasty, surgery is usually recommended. However, SC-CPC or MPL was recommended in cases where: (1) patients did not wish to undergo surgery, (2) the condition of the eye was expected to have a poor prognosis with surgery (e.g., severe conjunctival scarring or neovascular glaucoma), or (3) the eye had poor vision (e.g., visual acuity worse than hand motion). In all cases, the pros and cons of the treatment modalities were discussed with the patients, and when SC-CPC or MPL was deemed appropriate, the procedures were performed.

Surgical technique

Under the retrobulbar anesthesia, SC-CPC was performed using the 810 nm continuous wave infrared diode laser generated by G-Probe (Iridex Corp., Mountain View, CA, USA) with a power setting of 900–1250 mW and a duration of 4 s. The G probe footplate was held perpendicular to the sclera, with the curved edge of the footplate placed at the limbus so that the laser beam was directed 1.2 mm posteriorly toward the ciliary processes4–9. Once pop sound was heard, the power was lowered. Laser was applied to 15 to 35 spots in 3 quadrants, sparing the sites of previous glaucoma surgery (filtering bleb, tube), areas of scleral thinning, and 3- and 9-o’clock meridians to avoid injury to the long ciliary nerves and vessels; one quadrant (superior-nasal or superior-temporal) was spared to prevent hypotony. Variations in power and number of spots were made at the surgeon’s discretion. Postoperatively, prednisolone acetate 1% eye drops were used 6 to 8 times a day for the first postoperative 1 or 2 weeks and then tapered until postoperative 1 to 2 months according to the condition of eyes.

MPL was performed under the retrobulbar anesthesia using G-Probe (Iridex Corp., Mountain View, CA, USA) with a power setting of 2000–3000 mW, a duty cycle of 31.3%, and a duration of 100–160 s in 4 quadrants, sparing the sites of previous glaucoma surgery (filtering bleb, tube), areas of scleral thinning, and the 3- and 9-o’clock meridians. Postoperatively, prednisolone acetate 1% eye drops were used 4 times a day for the first postoperative 1 or 2 weeks and then tapered until postoperative 1 to 2 months according to the condition of eyes.

Outcome measures

IOP, number of IOP-lowering medication, visual acuity presented as logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR), and complications were assessed before the procedure (baseline) and 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months thereafter. Corneal endothelial cell count was evaluated by specular microscope (NSP-9900 Konan Noncon Robo Specular Microscope X; Konan medical, Inc., Hyogo, Japan) before and 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after the procedure. Aqueous flare values were measured by laser flare photometry (Kowa FM-600; Kowa Acculas, San Jose, CA, USA) before and 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after the procedure. Total energy of laser treatment was calculated as power in Watts x total treatment duration in seconds (time x number of spots) for SC-CPC and power in Watts x total treatment duration in seconds x ON cycle (31.3%) for MPL.

Statistical analyses

Success was defined as an IOP of 6–21 mmHg and a ≥ 20% reduction from baseline, no reoperation for glaucoma, and no loss of light-perception vision. To achieve robust outcomes, if repeated SC-CPC or MPL was needed, it was considered as a surgical failure. When analyzing visual acuity, count fingers vision was presented by 2 on the logMAR scale and hand motion vision was presented by 330. Visual acuities with light perception and no light perception were not converted to logMAR and excluded from the calculations30. Changes in variables between baseline and follow-up visits were analyzed using paired t-test and difference between the two groups was assessed esing independent t-test. Correlations among variables were evaluated by Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Success rate was calculated by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. A Cox proportional hazed model was used to identify factors affecting survival. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

The most common diagnosis was primary open-angle glaucoma (50.8% in the SC-CPC group and 67.2% in the MPL group). Among the 199 eyes, 33 (50.8%) in the SC-CPC group and 53 (39.6%) in the MPL group had a previous history of glaucoma surgery.

The average mean deviation (MD) of visual field test was − 19.38 ± 9.47 dB (range, -33.48– -1.65).

For the MPL group, mean visual acuity, refractive error, IOP, and number of IOP-lowering medications at the baseline visit were 0.56 ± 0.89 logMAR (range, 0–3.0), -1.87 ± 2.59 diopters (range, -10.87–5.00), 26.4 ± 10.8 mmHg (range, 11–66), and 2.6 ± 0.9 (range, 0–4), respectively. Among the 134 eyes, 7 (5.2%) had count fingers vision, 10 (7.5%) had hand motions vision, and 10 (7.5%) had light perception or no light perception. The average MD of visual field test was − 17.63 ± 9.73 dB (range, -32.9–1.53).

The mean baseline IOP in the MPL group was significantly lower than in the SC-CPC group (p = 0.001). Other variables, including age, diagnosis distribution, previous glaucoma history, visual acuity, refractive error, number of medications, and visual field test results, did not show significant differences between the groups (p > 0.05).

Complications

The most common complication was pupillary abnormality (mydriasis), found in 5 eyes (4.5%) in the MPL group. Other complications, including phthisis bulbi, hypotony, cystoid macular edema, hyphema, and corneal decompensation, were not observed in either group.

Discussion

In the present study, success rate of a single session of SC-CPC and MPL at 12 months postoperative was 50.1% and 38.2%, respectively. Previous studies with adult patients reported that success rate ranged from 60.6 to 72.2% for SC-CPC5–7,9,12] and 26–74% for MPL18. The discrepancy in success rate among studies may be explained by differences in the characteristics of the study population (e.g., type of glaucoma, history of glaucoma surgery, preoperative IOP, ethnicity) and treatment protocol. An important issue is the permission of repeated treatment for the definition of success. In previous studies, repeated treatment was performed in 7.5–26.9% eyes treated with SC-CPC5,7,9,10 and up to 46% of eyes treated with MPL18, and this was not considered as failure. In contrast, we aimed to investigate the outcomes of a single session of SC-CPC and MPL. Therefore, in the present study, when repeated CPC or MPL was needed, it was considered a surgical failure, which may have led to a lower success rate comparted to previous studies. It remains controversial whether repeated treatment should be considered or if another treatment option would be better after failed SC-CPC or MPL17,31. Currently, the authors are analyzing outcomes of SC-CPC and MPL with a longer observation period, including cases with various retreatment combinations (e.g., MPL first and SC-CPC as the next step treatment option). We hope that our subsequent study results may provide useful information for improving treatment algorithm.

When outcomes of SC-CPC and MPL were compared, success rate, IOP level, and amount of IOP reduction at 12 months postoperative did not show significant difference. However, at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperative, the amount of IOP reduction was greater, and number of IOP-lowering medication was lower in the SC-CPC group compared to the MPL group. These findings suggest that SC-CPC shows a greater IOP-lowering effect than MPL at least in early postoperative period. The greater IOP-lowering effect of SC-CPC may be attributed to the greater structural change in ciliary body compared to MPL.

Application of the laser to the ciliary body can cause destruction of blood-aqueous barrier and a subsequent increase in aqueous flare concentration27–29. In the present study, aqueous flare values drastically increased immediate after the procedure and gradually decreased thereafter. Previous studies also reported a transient aqueous flare value elevation after traditional CPC28 or MPL27,29. The amount of elevation was greater in the SC-CPC group compared to the MPL group, and the elevation remained significant until 6 months. These findings imply that SC-CPC induces a greater change in the ciliary body than MPL, and in some cases, this may persist longer than 6 months. Prolonged inflammation induced by the breakdown of the blood-aqueous barrier can cause various complications. However, in the present study, inflammation-related complications were not found. Thus, we think that clinically nonsignificant elevation of aqueous flare values after SC-CPC or MPL per se may not be considered a postoperative complication.

In correlation analysis, a greater change in aqueous flare value was correlated with a greater IOP reduction at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperative in the SC-CPC group. In the MPL group, the change in aqueous flare value showed a significant correlation at 1 week postoperative. Previous studies also reported that degree of aqueous flare value elevation in the early postoperative period was correlated with the amount of IOP reduction in eyes with traditional CPC28 or MPL27,29. These findings imply that the amount of aqueous flare change after CPC, MPL, and SC-CPC may be a prognostic factor for IOP reduction. For instance, if the aqueous flare value does not increase after the treatment, we may expect insufficient laser application to ciliary body. Therefore, in such case, additional laser treatment or other treatment option may be considered.

Corneal decompensation is an important complication after incisional glaucoma surgery32 or traditional CPC18. In the present study, no significant change in number of corneal endothelial cells was found in both the SC-CPC and MPL groups. Previous studies also reported no significant change in number of corneal endothelial cell after a single session MPL33,34. Studies regarding SC-CPC reported that corneal decompensation was not common5–7, and SC-CPC showed safe and efficient outcomes even in eyes with previous keratoplasty12. These findings suggest that SC-CPC and MPL may be useful treatment options in cases with impaired corneal endothelial cell function. To date, little is reported about the effect of SC-CPC on corneal endothelial cell. Further studies investigating the effect of repeated SC-CPC on corneal endothelial cell are necessary.

Previous studies reported that hypotony was found in 0 to 5.6% of eyes after SC-CPC5–7,9,12] and 0 to 9% of eyes after MPL18. In the present study, ocular hypotony or phthisis bulbi was not found after a single session of SC-CPC or MPL. Therefore, SC-CPC and MPL may be considered safer procedures compared to traditional CPC in terms of hypotony or phthisis bulbi18. However, there still remains a possibility of hypotony or phthisis bulbi in specific conditions or after repeated SC-CPC or MPL. Regarding this issue, further studies are needed.

In our study participants, the most common complication was pupillary abnormality (mydriasis), which was found in 5 (4.5%) eyes of the MPL group. Previous studies also reported mydriasis after MPL in 1.8 to 11% of the eyes31. In contrast, after SC-CPC, pupillary abnormality was not found in the present study and previous reports4–12. More anterior laser application areas near the iris root using the original probe of G-probe for MPL compared to the SC-CPC may attribute to this finding;31 in the present study, all of the eyes with pupillary abnormality underwent MPL with original probe, which was used prior to the development of the revised probe. Another possibility is damage to the terminal branches of the short posterior ciliary nerves associated with the continuous “painting” motion during MPL16. A low possibility of pupillary abnormality may be an advantage of SC-CPC.

In conclusion, both SC-CPC and MPL revealed safe and effective IOP control. Aqueous flare change in the early postoperative period predicted treatment outcomes. When SC-CPC and MPL are compared, SC-CPC showed a greater effect and a fewer complications, especially in terms of pupillary abnormality.
