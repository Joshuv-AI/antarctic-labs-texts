Salt-associated angina bullosa haemorrhagica in a child: A case report with literature review
Authors: Khlood A. Alkurdi, Jihad A. Alokaily, Sara D. Alzanbaqi, Hassan H. Alamer, Jawaher M. Alotaibi, Abdullah F. Alshammari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Angina bullosa haemorrhagica is a rare, benign, self-limiting oral mucosal disorder characterised by the sudden appearance of blood-filled bullae in the absence of systemic or haematological abnormalities. Paediatric cases remain exceptionally uncommon. This report describes a 5-year-old Saudi Arabian boy who presented with multiple painless haemorrhagic oral bullae after ingesting natural salt crystals during a visit to a salt lake in Al-Qassim, Saudi Arabia. Clinical examination revealed 18 haemorrhagic bullae involving the buccal mucosa, labial mucosa, and tongue, without systemic symptoms or signs of trauma. Laboratory investigations were unremarkable, and a probable clinical diagnosis of angina bullosa haemorrhagica temporally associated with salt ingestion was established. The lesions ruptured spontaneously within 24 h and healed completely without scarring within 7 days. A literature review identified 40 reported cases from 26 studies, with idiopathic causes and local traumatic or dietary factors being the most commonly associated findings. This report highlights the importance of recognising angina bullosa haemorrhagica in children and considering possible environmental or dietary contributing factors while avoiding unnecessary investigations and interventions.

Introduction

Approximately 40% of parents experience high levels of stress when confronted with the sudden onset of unfamiliar medical conditions in their children. 1 This anxiety can sometimes extend to dental healthcare workers, particularly when faced with rare diseases that lack well-documented management guidelines. Angina bullosa haemorrhagica (ABH) is a rare, benign, and self-limiting mucosal disorder characterised by the sudden appearance of blood-filled bullae in the oral cavity, often without an identifiable systemic cause or underlying haematological disorder. 2 Due to its uncommon presentation, abrupt onset, and alarming clinical appearance, ABH can provoke significant concern among both parents and healthcare providers. Improving awareness of this rare condition may facilitate earlier recognition, reduce unnecessary anxiety, and help identify potential contributing factors.

Despite the increasing number of ABH reports in adults, paediatric presentations remain poorly documented, and limited evidence is available regarding potential environmental or dietary triggers in children. Therefore, this report aims to describe a rare paediatric presentation of ABH temporally associated with salt ingestion and to provide a contextual literature review summarising previously reported cases, clinical characteristics, potential triggering factors, and management approaches.

Case presentation

A 5-year-old Saudi Arabian male patient presented to the emergency department at King Saud Hospital, Unaizah, Al-Qassim, Saudi Arabia, in August 2025, accompanied by his parents. His chief complaint, as described by his parents, was the presence of ‘red bubbles in the mouth’, which had appeared 1 day prior. The patient was immediately referred to the Oral Medicine Department for further evaluation.

The child was otherwise healthy, with no history of allergies or prior medication use. There was no history of similar oral lesions or any history suggestive of haematological disorders, leukaemia, drug reactions, diabetes, prolonged corticosteroid use, hypertension, frequent epistaxis, or gastrointestinal bleeding. His family history was also largely unremarkable, though his grandparents (both maternal and paternal) had diabetes and hypertension, which were well controlled.

The patient’s mother noticed the oral blisters approximately 1 h after the family returned from a trip to Al-Awshaziyah Salt Lake, a well-known natural saltwater attraction near Unaizah, Al-Qassim Province, Saudi Arabia. His father reported that the child had repeatedly consumed salt crystals from the ground, which were mixed with sand, during the outing. There was no associated history of fever, pain, systemic symptoms, or trauma.

Investigations

The patient’s vital signs, including temperature, pulse rate, and blood pressure, were all within normal limits and unremarkable at the time of examination. Intraoral examination revealed a total of 18 raised, blood-filled, painless blisters distributed across various sites within the oral cavity. Right buccal mucosa: six blisters (sizes: 8 × 8, 4 × 3, 5 × 3, 3 × 2, 3 × 6, and 2 × 3 mm). Upper labial mucosa: four blisters (sizes: 2 × 1, 1 × 1, 3 × 2, and 5 × 4 mm). Lower labial mucosa: two blisters (sizes: 1.5 × 2 and 1 × 1 mm). Dorsum of the tongue: three blisters (sizes: 1.5 × 1, 1 × 1, and 2.5 × 1.5 mm). Left buccal mucosa: Three blisters (sizes: 9 × 7, 6 × 8, and 5 × 4.5 mm). The bullae were intact, containing dark-red haemorrhagic fluid, and were non-tender. No associated ulceration or necrosis was observed at the time of presentation (Figure 1(a)–(c)).

Initial presentation. Multiple intact, dark-red haemorrhagic bullae observed on the buccal mucosa, labial mucosa, and tongue upon arrival at the dental emergency department. (a)right buccal mucosa and lips. (b) ventrum of tongue and lips. (c) left buccal mucosa and lips.

A general physical examination revealed no skin abnormalities on the limbs and no purpura or petechiae. Based on the clinical presentation, a working diagnosis of ABH was made. However, several differential diagnoses were considered due to the haemorrhagic and bullous nature of the lesions. These included thrombocytopenia (e.g. immune thrombocytopenic purpura and leukaemia-associated thrombocytopenia), haemorrhagic bullous disorders such as epidermolysis bullosa and mucous membrane pemphigoid, chemical or thermal injury (particularly considering the history of salt ingestion), fixed drug eruption (in case of undisclosed medication or food-related reaction), and herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection, although the lesions in this case were painless and non-ulcerative, making HSV less likely.

Additional differential diagnoses reported in similar cases include pemphigus vulgaris, bullous pemphigoid, acquired epidermolysis bullosa, linear Immunoglobulin A dermatosis, herpetiform dermatitis, oral amyloidosis, and bullous lichen planus. These conditions often share overlapping clinical features with ABH but can typically be differentiated based on patient history, lesion characteristics, histopathological findings, and immunofluorescence results. Accurate clinical evaluation and the exclusion of systemic or autoimmune aetiologies are essential to establish a confident diagnosis of ABH and avoid unnecessary investigations or treatment.

Laboratory tests were performed to rule out systemic conditions (Tables 1 and 2). No biopsy or direct immunofluorescence (DIF) testing was performed, given the self-limiting nature of the condition.

Treatment, outcome, and follow up

At the 24-h follow-up, the blisters had spontaneously ruptured and showed signs of healing (Figure 2(a)–(c)). At the 7-day follow-up, the lesions had completely healed without scarring or recurrence (Figure 3(a)–(c)).

Follow-up at 7 days. Complete healing of the oral mucosa without scarring, with full resolution of lesions and no recurrence observed. (a). right buccal mucosa. (b). ventrum of tongue. (c) left buccal mucosa.

A probable clinical diagnosis of ABH temporally associated with salt ingestion was established based on (1) acute onset of multiple haemorrhagic bullae; (2) absence of systemic illness, haematological abnormalities, or immune-mediated disease; (3) self-limiting nature with spontaneous resolution; and (4) history of salt ingestion as a possible triggering factor.

The parents were reassured regarding the benign and self-limiting nature of the condition, and the lack of need for aggressive treatment was emphasised. They were advised to ensure that the child remained well-hydrated and to provide a soft, non-irritating diet to support oral healing and comfort. Education was provided about avoiding potential triggers, and the family was encouraged to monitor the child for any recurrence, although none was anticipated given the favourable clinical course.

Literature search strategy

A structured electronic literature search was conducted utilising online scientific databases, including Embase, Scopus, and PubMed, to identify relevant case reports published within the last 15 years (2010–2025). Additionally, a manual search was performed by reviewing reference lists to identify supplementary studies. The search strategy incorporated combinations of keywords and indexed terms related to Angina Bullosa Haemorrhagica, including ‘Angina Bullosa Hemorrhagica’, ‘Oral Hemorrhagic Bullous Disorder’, ‘Hemorrhagic Bullous Angina’, ‘Idiopathic Oral Hemorrhagic Bullae’, ‘Recurrent Oral Hemorrhagic Bullae’, ‘Benign Oral Hemorrhagic Bullae’, ‘Idiopathic Angina Bullosa Hemorrhagica’, and ‘Oral Hemorrhagic Vesiculobullous Lesion’, combined with the terms ‘case report’, ‘case series’, ‘clinical details’, ‘manifestation’, and ‘treatment’.

Studies were selected based on predefined eligibility criteria. Case reports or case series involving patients diagnosed with ABH were included irrespective of age, gender, or underlying causes. Only studies published in English within the specified timeframe were considered. Exclusion criteria encompassed publications unrelated to ABH, duplicate reports, systematic reviews, dissertations, letters, personal opinions, book chapters, conference abstracts, and pilot studies. The screening process and exclusion criteria are detailed in Figure 4. The titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies were independently assessed by two researchers (K.A.A. and A.F.A.). Disagreements were resolved by discussion. If a consensus was not achieved, a third investigator (J.A.A.) provided input. The reviewers (K.A.A. and J.M.A.) independently evaluated the full-text articles for final inclusion, with S.D.A. being involved in resolving any remaining discrepancies. The kappa coefficient for the initial screening phase was calculated at 0.75, reflecting substantial agreement, while the full-text selection stage yielded a value of 0.89, demonstrating near-perfect concordance between the reviewers. As this review was intended to provide contextual support for the presented case, formal systematic review procedures such as protocol registration and methodological quality assessment of included studies were not performed. Data from the included studies were narratively synthesised according to patient demographics, clinical presentation, triggering factors, lesion location, treatment, and outcomes.

PRISMA 2020 flowchart illustrating the literature search and screening stages.

A total of 43 papers were retrieved through database searches: PubMed (n = 24), Scopus (n = 7), and Embase (n = 12). Six additional records were identified through manual searching. After the removal of duplicates, 36 unique records remained. The selection process involved two screening stages, namely title/abstract screening and full-text screening, conducted according to the predefined eligibility criteria. During the title and abstract screening phase, three articles were excluded for not being case reports or case series. An additional nine studies were excluded at the full-text screening stage due to not being in English, unavailability of the full-text article, or not involving the oral cavity (Figure 4).

Following the screening process, 26 studies were included in the final analysis (Figure 4). These studies collectively represented a diverse range of international case reports, reflecting the global nature of ABH presentations. The extracted data encompassed variables such as patient age, gender, clinical presentation, location and number of lesions, triggering factors, treatment modalities, and recurrence (Table 3). It is important to note that in some cases, more than one causative factor was reported for a single patient; therefore, the total number of triggering factors illustrated in the PRISMA diagram exceeds the number of cases included.

Characteristics of the retrieved papers.

A total of 40 cases derived from the 26 included studies, some of which were multiple case series, were included in the analysis. Of the patients, 55.0% (n = 22) were female and 45.0% (n = 18) were male, indicating a slight female predominance. The recurrence of lesions was reported in 57.5% (n = 23) of cases, while 10.0% (n = 4) showed no recurrence; recurrence status was not specified for the remaining cases. In some cases, multiple causative factors were reported for a single patient; therefore, the total number of causative factors exceeded the number of cases and did not correspond one-to-one. The most common category was idiopathic, accounting for 42.5% (n = 17) of cases. Thermal and dietary irritants were identified in 32.5% (n = 13) of cases, systemic or medication-related factors in 27.5% (n = 11) of cases, while local mechanical trauma was reported in 5.0% (n = 2). This distribution underscores the multifactorial aetiology of ABH and the frequent lack of a clearly identifiable trigger.

Literature review

Pathogenesis

ABH is a rare, benign oral mucosal disorder characterised by the abrupt formation of blood-filled subepithelial bullae, often in the absence of systemic disease or haematological abnormalities.3 –5 While its precise aetiology remains unclear, its pathogenesis is thought to be multifactorial, involving mechanical, chemical, and possibly vascular factors. Minor trauma to the oral mucosa, such as that caused by mastication, dental procedures, or thermal injury, can lead to the rupture of superficial blood vessels in susceptible individuals. Histological studies have revealed subepithelial clefts filled with erythrocytes, minimal inflammatory infiltrate, and no evidence of vasculitis or immune complex deposition.6 –10

In recent literature, environmental and dietary triggers have also been proposed. Of particular interest in this context is the ingestion of natural salt crystals, which may act as an irritant to the mucosa and induce osmotic or mechanical stress, thereby contributing to bulla formation.11,12 Salt, due to its abrasive texture and hyperosmolar nature, may weaken the epithelial barrier or cause superficial mucosal injury. Furthermore, capillary fragility and reduced elastic fibre content in the mucosa have been noted in some patients, possibly contributing to increased vulnerability to such triggers. DIF findings are typically negative results, helping to distinguish ABH from autoimmune blistering diseases.13 –17

Clinical picture

ABH typically presents as a sudden-onset, painless haemorrhagic blister within the oral cavity. The soft palate is the most frequently affected site, though lesions may also appear on the buccal mucosa, tongue, and lips.11 –15 Blisters range from a few millimetres to several centimetres in size and often rupture spontaneously within 24–48 h, followed by rapid healing without scarring. 18 Predominantly reported in adults, paediatric cases remain exceedingly rare. 19

The condition is generally asymptomatic, but patients may occasionally report a tingling or burning sensation preceding the eruption. In cases extensively involving the oropharynx, the risk of airway obstruction exists and should be monitored.20 –24 Due to their alarming appearance, ABH lesions can lead to misdiagnosis or unnecessary investigations for haematological, infectious, or autoimmune conditions.25,26

Diagnosis

ABH is diagnosed clinically based on its characteristic presentation, patient history, and the exclusion of systemic or haematological conditions. 27 Routine laboratory parameters, including platelet count, coagulation profile, and complete blood count, are typically within normal ranges.17,18 Histopathological examinations reveal a subepithelial haemorrhagic bulla with minimal inflammatory infiltrate and no evidence of vasculitis or immune complex deposition.19 –22

Ordioni et al. 27 proposed nine diagnostic criteria to assist in confirming ABH. The proposed criteria include the presence of oral haemorrhagic bullae, palatal localization, triggering events, recurrent lesions, rapid healing without scarring, painless presentation, normal haematological findings, negative DIF, and exclusion of systemic disorders. 27 These criteria are useful for differentiating ABH from other vascular, nervous and infective lesions as well as serious conditions like mucous membrane pemphigoid, pemphigus vulgaris, epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, and thrombocytopenia-related lesions.25 –28

Treatment

ABH is a benign and self-limiting condition that typically does not require pharmacological intervention. 29 Management is primarily supportive, focussing on symptom relief and the prevention of further trauma. Key measures include reassurance, oral hygiene maintenance, avoiding hot, spicy, or coarse foods, and ensuring adequate hydration.30 –32 In paediatric patients, caregiver education is essential to reduce anxiety and avoid unnecessary intervention.

In most cases, lesions rupture spontaneously and heal without scarring within a few days. However, rare cases involving oropharyngeal bullae that pose a risk of airway obstruction warrant urgent evaluation and management.31,33 Antibiotic therapy may be considered only if there are signs of secondary infection, although this is uncommon. Long-term outcomes are excellent, with recurrence being infrequent and usually linked to identifiable triggers.32,34

Discussion

ABH is a rare, non-infectious oral mucosal disorder predominantly observed in middle-aged and older adults, with only sporadic paediatric cases reported in the literature. The current report describes a unique paediatric case of ABH, possibly associated with the ingestion of natural salt crystals, reinforcing the need to broaden the clinical index of suspicion for ABH among paediatric populations, especially when environmental or dietary irritants are involved.

The case meets all nine diagnostic criteria proposed by Ordioni et al., 27 including the presence of a sudden-onset haemorrhagic blister confined to the oral mucosa, rapid healing without scarring, absence of systemic disease, and normal haematological profiles, alongside the absence of immune-mediated findings such as vasculitis or positive immunofluorescence results. 29 These criteria are particularly valuable in paediatric contexts, where rare conditions often risk being misdiagnosed as serious systemic or autoimmune disorders.

A number of cases have demonstrated that ABH can occur following exposure to salt-containing food additives, as described by Alsunni et al., Alsheerawi et al., Satici et al., and Martins et al.4,8,13,26 However, according to our knowledge, the current study is the first to document ABH following direct ingestion of natural mineral salt crystals, distinguishing it from previously reported cases involving processed or prepared food sources. Al-Awshaziyah Lake, located in the Qassim Province of Saudi Arabia, is a natural hypersaline environment characterised by dense salt deposits formed through evaporation processes. The salt crystals found on the lake’s surface are highly concentrated, unrefined, and possess a coarse, abrasive texture.14,35 ,36 In this case, the direct consumption of these raw salt crystals may have contributed to both mechanical irritation and osmotic imbalance at the epithelial surface of the oral mucosa. The hyperosmolarity and abrasive nature of the salt crystals may have contributed to superficial mucosal irritation and epithelial barrier disruption, potentially acting as a triggering factor for haemorrhagic bulla formation in a susceptible individual. This observation is consistent with previous reports suggesting that chemical irritants or local trauma may contribute to superficial capillary injury in susceptible individuals. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be established from a single case, the present findings align with previously reported associations between ABH and thermal, dietary, or traumatic mucosal triggers.

The clinical picture was notable for the unusually high number and bilateral distribution of lesions, which is atypical for ABH. Most adult cases report fewer lesions, often localised to the soft palate or buccal mucosa.26 –31 Despite the extensive presentation, the condition remained painless and self-limiting and required only supportive care, highlighting the benign nature of ABH, even when multiple sites are affected.

Differentiating ABH from other paediatric conditions is critical to avoid unnecessary investigations and anxiety. The differential diagnosis for oral haemorrhagic lesions in children includes thrombocytopenia, bullous pemphigoid, epidermolysis bullosa, HSV infection, and mucous membrane pemphigoid. Unlike these conditions, ABH presents with negative systemic findings, no underlying haematological disorder, and a consistent pattern of spontaneous resolution, making accurate history-taking and clinical correlation essential for diagnosis.

This case underscores the importance of parental education and clinical reassurance. A review by Stremler et al. revealed that up to 40% of parents experience heightened stress when facing unexplained medical conditions in their children. 28 Regarding ABH, clear communication regarding its benign prognosis can significantly alleviate concerns and prevent unwarranted therapeutic interventions.

Conclusion

This report contributes to the limited literature on paediatric ABH and describes a rare clinical presentation temporally associated with salt crystal ingestion. The findings highlight the importance of recognising ABH in children, considering possible environmental or dietary contributing factors, and differentiating the condition from other haemorrhagic or autoimmune disorders to avoid unnecessary investigations and parental anxiety. Given the observational nature of this report, no causal relationship can be established. Further studies are needed to better understand the potential triggers and clinical characteristics of paediatric ABH.
