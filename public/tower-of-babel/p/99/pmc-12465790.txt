Seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in Ethiopia: findings from the 2022 national population-based survey (second round)
Authors: Geremew Tasew, Saro Abdella, Birra Bejiga, Eshetu Ejeta, Lehageru Gizachew, Leuel Lisanwork, Feyiso Bati, Ebba Abate, Legesse Negash, Zelalem Bonger, Joan Miquel, Festo Mazuguni, Zelalem Mekuria, Kokou Nouwame Alinon, Wang Shu-Hua, Ahmed E Ogwell Ouma, Hiwot Moges, Wondwossen Gebreyes, Getachew Tollera, Masresha Tessema, Desmond E Williams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAssessing COVID-19 burden through clinical surveillance is suboptimal, as individuals with mild or no symptoms rarely seek care. Seroprevalence studies overcome this limitation by capturing both symptomatic and asymptomatic infections, providing accurate estimates of community transmission, immunity gaps, and epidemic progression. This is critical for informing public health strategies, especially in resource-limited settings like Ethiopia post-vaccine rollout.ObjectiveTo estimate the national seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in Ethiopia after widespread vaccine deployments.MethodsA population-based cross-sectional study was conducted using stratified three-stage cluster sampling across 11 regions (excluding Tigray). We enrolled 14,074 participants (99% response rate) adult population aged (≥ 15 years). Data on demographics, comorbidities, vaccination status, and preventive behaviours were collected via structured questionnaires. Serological testing used RightSign™ COVID-19 IgG/IgM Rapid Test Cassettes. The data were entered into Open Data Kit (ODK) Survey-weighted analyses in STATA 17 included summary statistics, bivariate analysis, and multivariate binary logistic regression (reporting Adjusted Odds Ratios, AOR). ResultsAmong 14,074 participants (99% response rate; 58.9% female; mean age 35.3 years), the overall national seropositivity for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies was 48.0% (95% CI: 46.6–49.4%). IgG seroprevalence (45.1%) significantly exceeded IgM (1.2%), indicating predominantly past infections. Significant geographic variation existed, with Addis Ababa showing the highest seropositivity (68.1%; 95% CI: 65.7–70.5%) and Sidama (39.6%; 95% CI: 36.39–42.98%) and Gambella (40.5%; 95% CI: 37.01–44.02%) the lowest. Higher seropositivity was observed in females (50.1% vs. males 45.8%), urban residents (55.6% vs. rural 45.7%), and vaccinated individuals (59.1% vs. unvaccinated 37.2%). Multivariate analysis identified key predictors: vaccinated individuals had 2.64-fold higher odds (AOR = 2.64: 95% CI = 2.34–2.99; p < 0.001); urban residence increased odds by 41% (AOR = 1.41; 95% CI = 1.25–1.59; p < 0.001); diabetes (AOR = 1.87; 95% CI = 1.17–2.97; p = 0.009) and hypertension (AOR = 1.72; 95% CI = 1.23–2.41; p = 0.001) elevated risk; while smoking reduced odds by 24% (AOR = 0.76; 95% CI = 0.60–0.97; p = 0.028). Older age groups showed progressively higher odds (e.g., 55–64 years: AOR = 1.56; 95% CI = 1.19–2.06; p < 0.001 vs. 15–24 years)ConclusionThe study found Nearly half of Ethiopians had SARS-CoV-2 antibodies by mid-2022, with Significant disparities by regions, demographic groups, and vaccination status. Dominance of IgG antibodies (45.1%) over IgM (1.2%) indicates widespread prior infection. An identified rural immunity gap underscores the urgent need for targeted vaccination strategies among high-risk subgroups in underserved areas to advance toward herd immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11551-9.Keywords: Seroprevalence, COVID-19 vaccines, SARS-CoV-2, Antibodies, IgG, IgM, Ethiopia

Introduction

The novel Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) which is caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) emerged at the end of 2019, spread rapidly resulting in a global pandemic [1]. It infected people of all ages and sex, although elderly and individuals with underlying medical conditions were at higher risk of severe COVID-19 disease [2]. The majority of the COVID-19 data available are limited to laboratory-confirmed cases for symptomatic patients and/or suspected cases with an exposure history [1, 3]. However, a significant proportion (5–80%) of SARS-CoV-2 infection can occur in individuals who are asymptomatic or with mild disease [4]. Individuals with asymptomatic and subclinical infections can still transmit the virus and raise concerns for public health due to being undetected in the population [5, 6].

Serological testing for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies provides critical epidemiological insights by distinguishing between acute (IgM) and resolved (IgG) infections [7]. These assays serve three primary public health functions: quantifying cumulative infection burden through IgG detection, including asymptomatic cases, monitoring active transmission via IgM prevalence, and evaluating vaccination impact by tracking IgG seroconversion patterns [8]. Importantly, while natural infection generates antibodies against both spike (S) and nucleocapsid (N) proteins, most vaccines elicit only anti-S responses [9]. This fundamental difference enables differentiation between infection- and vaccine-induced immunity when using N-specific assays [10]. Therefore, detecting anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies along with vaccination history could be a better way to estimate the burden of SARS-CoV-2 infection [11].

Several large-scale nationwide seroprevalence studies have been conducted around the world with wide contextual variations [12]. Most of the studies target blood donors and healthcare personnel, with only a small number targeting the general population. Population-based SARS-CoV-2 infection range in the early 2020 s varied from 0.73% in India during May to June 2020 [13]; 5% in Spain during April to May 2020 [2]; 4.7% in Los Angeles County, California during April 2020 [14],to 6.9% in Wuhan, China during April 2020 [15].

Serological testing during the pandemic allowed Ethiopia to monitor the COVID-19 situation and detect any rise in prevalence. Serum antibody testing, accompanied by epidemiological data on symptoms, risk of exposure and demographics, provided a dataset to model future scenarios of spread and provide the necessary information to policy makers looking to balance necessary social interaction and movement of individuals with the containment of COVID-19.

As of July 2022, Ethiopia had reported 493,000 confirmed cases and 7,572 COVID-19 deaths [16, 17], with underreporting suspected due to testing coverage of < 1% nationally [18]. These limitations underscored the need for Sero surveillance to quantify population immunity during vaccine rollout a critical gap in Africa’s pandemic response [19]. Against this backdrop, we conducted Ethiopia’s second-round national serosurvey to: estimate SARS-CoV-2 antibody prevalence, assess vaccination impact, and identify immunity disparities.

Therefore, this survey aimed to estimate the more recent population level seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies to understand and monitor the disease burden in Ethiopia after availability of vaccines in the country.

Methods

Study setting and period

This study was conducted in Ethiopia from July 10, 2022 to August 20, 2022 in two city administrations, Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa, and all regional states, except Tigray due to conflict in the region. Administratively, Ethiopia is divided into eleven geographical regions and two city administrations. Each region is sub-divided into zones and regional towns, each zone into woredas and towns, then each woreda into woreda towns, and all towns at all levels into kebeles.

Study design and population

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was used to estimate the proportion of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in Ethiopia. This design was selected because it: aligns with Ethiopia’s three-tier administrative structure (region-zone-woreda), enables cost-effective nationwide coverage, and permits comparability with our first-round national to ensure national representativeness across all accessible regions while maintaining comparability with Ethiopia’s first-round serosurvey [20].

Study population

The source population comprised all Ethiopian residents aged 15 years or older. The study population included individuals from 474 randomly selected census enumeration areas, with proportional allocation across 11 regions and 2 city administrations. The sample excluded Tigray region due to security constraints.

Participant selection

Within each selected household, one adult participant was chosen using the WHO-modified Kish grid method [21, 22]. Field teams first enumerated all eligible household members (permanent residents aged ≥ 15 years), then applied standardized selection tables to ensure random sampling. The process was documented electronically using Open Data Kit with GPS verification.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were permanent residents aged 15 years or older who provided written informed consent. We excluded temporary visitors, critically ill individuals, and those unable to consent. The age cutoff aligned with national COVID-19 vaccination policies during the study period.

Sampling procedure

Our study employed a stratified three-stage cluster sampling design derived from the 2019 Ethiopia Population and Housing Census (EPHC) [23]. Regions were stratified into urban-rural domains, with census enumeration areas (EAs) serving as primary clusters. Each EA contained approximately 131 households with documented geographic boundaries and residence types. First stage: 474 EAs (235 urban, 239 rural) were selected as primary sampling units (PSUs) with probability proportional to size (PPS), where “size” referred to the household count per EA. Second stage: 30 households per EA were selected via systematic random sampling from updated EA household lists. Third stage: One eligible adult per household was randomly selected using the Kish method or a random number table.

Due to data privacy protocols, GPS coordinates of sampled EAs are available upon formal request to CSA. The full sampling frame excluding inaccessible regions (notably Tigray) is archived at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute data base.

Non-response handling

If a household refused or was unavailable, it was replaced by the next eligible household in the sampling frame. EAs with > 20% non-response was revisited to minimize bias.

Data collection tools and procedures

The study employed a structured questionnaire (Supplementary File 1) that was developed through a rigorous multi-phase process. Initial items were adapted from WHO COVID-19 behavioral indicators and validated instruments used in similar seroprevalence studies. The questionnaire captured demographic characteristics, medical history, preventive behaviors, and vaccination details through interviewer-administered interviews.

Prior to implementation, the instrument underwent comprehensive validation. Professional translators performed forward- and back-translation between English and five regional languages (Amharic, Oromo, Somali, Tigrinya, and Afar), with reconciliation by a panel of bilingual public health experts. Psychometric evaluation during pilot testing demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.82) and test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.89 at 7-day interval).

Field staff received standardized five-day training covering administration protocols, probing techniques, and ethical considerations. To minimize bias, interviewers were blinded to participants’ vaccination status and antibody results throughout data collection. Quality assurance measures included daily spot-checks of interviews by supervisors and digital recording of randomly selected interviews for independent review.

The questionnaire was administered using secure electronic tablets with built-in skip patterns and range checks to ensure data quality. A standardized field manual containing translated versions of all questions and detailed administration guidelines ensured consistent implementation across all study sites. The complete questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Serological sample collection and testing

The study employed the RightSign™ COVID-19 IgG/IgM Rapid Test Cassette (Hangzhou Biotest Biotech Co., Ltd., China) for detection of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein antibodies. Manufacturer evaluations reported 98.1% sensitivity (95% CI: 96.3–99.2) and 99.4% specificity (95% CI: 98.7–99.8) [24], with independent studies demonstrating < 0.5% cross-reactivity with common human coronaviruses (HCoV-OC43, HCoV-229E) [2]. Local validation conducted at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) using 100 PCR-confirmed positive samples collected during July-September 2020 and 100 pre-pandemic negative samples from 2019 archives yielded comparable performance characteristics (96.8% sensitivity, 98.7% specificity).

Several important limitations were documented: The assay detects only nucleocapsid protein antibodies and therefore cannot identify vaccine-induced immunity. While demonstrating high accuracy beyond 14 days post-infection, its sensitivity during the early infection window remains undefined. The nucleocapsid-specific assay was selected because: (a) It detects infection-induced antibodies regardless of vaccination status; (b) All vaccines used in Ethiopia (AstraZeneca, Sinopharm, J&J) primarily induce spike protein antibodies; (c) This approach allowed consistent comparison with our first-round serosurvey. All participants received written information about these limitations as part of the informed consent process.

Test kits were stored at 2–30 °C at EPHI regional laboratories with daily temperature monitoring using calibrated digital loggers. Field teams used insulated coolers with temperature strips to maintain appropriate conditions during transport (15–30 °C). Quality assurance protocols included running manufacturer-provided positive and negative controls with each new kit lot, random retesting of 5% of field samples at EPHI’s central laboratory, and systematic documentation of all kit lot numbers.

Trained phlebotomists collected 50 µL capillary blood samples via single-use sterile lancets (Safety-Lancet, HTL-Strefa, Poland) following WHO guidelines. Testing occurred immediately at point-of-care settings by certified technicians who had completed a 5-day standardized training program at EPHI. Invalid tests were excluded and repeated using fresh samples. All controls met manufacturer-specified thresholds, and discordant results were resolved by senior technicians. All tests were interpreted independently by two trained readers, with discordant results resolved by a senior technician. The Ethiopian Food and Drug Authority approved all testing procedures under Emergency Use Authorization protocols.

Data analysis and management

Electronic data collection was performed using Open Data Kit (ODK), with subsequent analysis conducted in STATA version 17. We accounted for the complex survey design through a three-stage weighting approach incorporating inverse probability weights, non-response adjustments based on regional response rates, and post-stratification to match 2022 national population demographics from the Central Statistical Agency.

To identify factors associated with SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity, we implemented a staged analytical approach. Initial bivariate logistic regression screened all candidate variables, retaining those showing association at p < 0.25 for multivariable analysis. We then developed multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression models with random intercepts for enumeration areas to account for clustering, while adjusting for key sociodemographic confounders including age, sex, and urban/rural residence. Model building employed backward elimination with a p < 0.05 retention threshold, preceded by assessment for multicollinearity (all variance inflation factors < 4) and evaluation of biologically plausible interaction terms. The final model demonstrated good fit (Hosmer-Lemeshow χ²=7.82, p = 0.45).

Prevalence Estimation

Seroprevalence estimates were calculated as weighted proportions with 95% confidence intervals, using Taylor series linearization to account for clustering. Missing data were handled through multiple imputation with chained equations after confirming the missing-at-random assumption through pattern analysis. All analyses utilized STATA’s survey commands (svy) with appropriate weighting and the subpop option for domain-specific estimates.

Operational definitions of study variables

The primary outcome, seropositivity, was defined as detection of IgG and/or IgM antibodies against SARS-CoV-2. Predictor variables included: Demographic factors: Age, sex, residence, and administrative region. Clinical factors, Behavioral factors and Environmental factor.

Clinical trial registration

Clinical trial number: not applicable.

COVID-19 antibody prevalence in Ethiopia

The national weighted prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibody seropositivity (IgM or IgG) was 48.0% (95% CI: 46.6 to 49.4). Analysis by vaccination status revealed significant differences. Unvaccinated participants demonstrated lower seroprevalence at 37.2% (95% CI: 35.4 to 39.0), with IgG detected in 35.0% (95% CI: 33.2 to 36.8) and IgM in 0.7% (95% CI: 0.5 to 1.0). Table 4

Factors associated with SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity: multilevel logistic regression results

The adjusted multilevel logistic regression analysis revealed several significant associations with SARS-CoV-2 antibody seropositivity. Older age groups showed higher odds compared to 15–24-year-olds, particularly those aged 25–34 years (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] 1.18, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.00 to 1.40; p = 0.048) and participants ≥ 65 years (AOR 1.49, 95% CI 1.07 to 2.09; p = 0.018). Urban residence was associated with 24% greater odds of seropositivity than rural residence (AOR 1.24, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.40; p < 0.001).

Geographic analysis showed markedly lower seropositivity in several regions compared to Addis Ababa, including Amhara (AOR 0.48, 95% CI 0.40 to 0.57; p < 0.001), Oromia (AOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.56; p < 0.001), and Sidama (AOR 0.33, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.41; p < 0.001). Comorbid conditions demonstrated strong positive associations, with diabetes (AOR 1.87, 95% CI 1.17 to 2.97; p = 0.009) and hypertension (AOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.23 to 2.41; p = 0.002) both showing significantly elevated odds.

Vaccinated participants had 2.64-fold higher odds of seropositivity (95% CI: 2.34–2.99; p < 0.001). Because the assay detects only nucleocapsid (N) protein antibodies which are primarily induced by natural infection rather than the vaccines used in Ethiopia (AstraZeneca, Sinopharm, Johnson & Johnson), this association likely reflects natural infection rather than vaccine-induced immunity. Discussion

This nationwide serosurvey revealed an overall SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence of 48.0% (95% CI 46.6 to 49.4) in Ethiopia during 2022, representing a substantial increase from the 9.3% (95% CI 8.2 to 10.6) reported in the first-round survey conducted in 2021 [20]. The weighted seropositivity data demonstrate dramatic regional variations, with Addis Ababa showing the highest prevalence at 68.12% (95% CI 65.68 to 70.48) compared to Gambela’s 39.64% (95% CI 36.39 to 42.98). These findings highlight Ethiopia’s complex epidemiological landscape during the study period.

The strong association between vaccination status and seropositivity (59.1% [57.1–61.1] in vaccinated vs. 37.2% [35.4–39.0] in unvaccinated individuals highlights both vaccine effectiveness and implementation challenges. This disparity reflects profound inequities in vaccine distribution, with urban centers achieving 70% coverage versus ≤ 25% in pastoralist regions [24, 25]; The urban-rural gradient seroprevalence further emphasizes how healthcare access disparities [26]; and population density [27] shaped transmission patterns.

Urban areas exhibited significantly higher seropositivity (55.60%, 95% CI 53.90 to 57.29) than rural regions (45.69%, 95% CI 43.94 to 47.45), a disparity driven by three key factors documented in previous studies. First, vaccine distribution inequities resulted in pastoralist regions achieving ≤ 25% coverage versus 70% in urban centers [24, 25]. Second, healthcare infrastructure limitations in remote areas constrained both testing and prevention efforts [26]. Third, the intense transmission patterns characteristic of densely populated urban environments amplified exposure risks [27]. Notably, some rural regions like Benishangul Gumuz demonstrated unexpectedly high seroprevalence (66.17%, 95% CI 62.96 to 69.24), potentially reflecting unique mobility patterns or delayed epidemic peaks.

Demographic analysis revealed important variations. Older adults (≥ 65 years) showed significantly higher seropositivity (AOR 1.49, 95% CI 1.07 to 2.09; p = 0.018), likely resulting from combined effects of vaccine prioritization and cumulative exposure. Urban residence remained strongly associated with increased risk (AOR 1.24, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.40; p < 0.001), mirroring the observed coverage disparities (70% urban versus ≤ 25% rural). Comorbidities demonstrated particularly strong associations, with diabetes (AOR 1.87, 95% CI 1.17 to 2.97; p = 0.009) and hypertension (AOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.23 to 2.41; p = 0.002) both showing elevated risk, potentially indicating healthcare-mediated exposure pathways or biological susceptibility. Study participants with higher age group, living in urban areas, residences of regions with higher urban proportion, and having comorbidities such as diabetes and high blood pressure are at increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection resulting in higher seroprevalence [7, 28].

Gender differences emerged consistently throughout the study, with females demonstrating higher overall seropositivity (50.05%, 95% CI 48.18 to 51.91) than males (45.75%, 95% CI 43.62 to 47.85). The IgG-dominant response pattern (urban: 53.20%, 95% CI 51.50 to 55.00; rural: 42.68%, 95% CI 40.96 to 44.40) suggests most detected immunity resulted from prior infection rather than recent exposure. This pattern was particularly evident in Hareri region, where IgG seropositivity reached 51.00% (95% CI 48.00 to 54.00) alongside elevated IgM (3.74%, 95% CI 2.63 to 5.30), potentially indicating active transmission during the survey period.

The observed 1.2% IgM seropositivity (95% CI: 0.88–1.65) likely reflects recent SARS-CoV-2 infections given IgM’s transient detection window (typically 3–5 weeks post-infection), though potential cross-reactivity with endemic human coronaviruses and reported 1.2–4.3% false-positive rates in rapid tests warrant cautious interpretation [25, 26].The geographic heterogeneity (e.g. 3.74% IgM in Harari vs. 0.07% in Addis Ababa) suggests variable transmission timing across regions, while our assay’s 98.7% specificity (local validation) and alignment with global IgM patterns support the validity of these findings as markers of recent exposure these results likely represent active transmission during the survey period, particularly in this regions [7].

Our findings align with global patterns of increasing seroprevalence over time while highlighting Ethiopia’s unique epidemiological profile and regional heterogeneity. When compared globally, Ethiopia’s seroprevalence exceeds early pandemic estimates from France in (4.93%) [27], in India (7.1%) [28], in Spain (4.6%) [2], in Geneva, Switzerland (10.8%) [29], in Brazil (11.9%) [30], in Iran (17.1%) [31], in Nigeria (25%) [32], in Jordon (35.2%) [33], in Egypt (35%) [34] and in South Sudan 38% [35]. However, the study finding remains below reports from Peru (70.0%) [36], and Colombia (55.3%) [37]. The findings align with 2022 European data (48%) (39). while being substantially lower than a 2021 African review (65%) [19].

The robust association between vaccination status and seropositivity (AOR 2.64, 95% CI 2.34 to 2.99; p < 0.001) persists despite the assay’s nucleocapsid specificity, suggesting both substantial breakthrough infections and possible population-level immune priming underscoring how structural health inequities and biological factors jointly shape seroprevalence patterns in resource-limited settings.

The nucleocapsid-specific assay used in this study cannot detect vaccine-induced immunity (primarily anti-spike antibodies), which limits direct assessment of hybrid immunity. However, the strong association between vaccination status and seropositivity (AOR = 2.64) suggests substantial breakthrough infections, implying that vaccinated individuals likely acquired hybrid immunity through both vaccination and natural exposure. This underscores the need for policies that prioritize booster doses in high-transmission settings to enhance hybrid immunity, particularly in regions with low baseline seroprevalence (e.g. Gambela, Sidama).

Methodologically, several considerations inform these findings. The sensitivity and specificity of antibody tests can alter seroprevalence estimates, and the timing of testing relative to infection or vaccination is crucial, since antibody levels may decline over time. and our cross-sectional timing relative to Ethiopia’s third pandemic wave both influence prevalence estimates. Additionally, the known persistence patterns of nucleocapsid antibodies and potential variability in immune responses to different vaccine platforms (primarily AstraZeneca and Sinopharm in Ethiopia’s program) represent important contextual factors.

These findings collectively underscore how structural health inequities fundamentally shape pandemic outcomes in resource-diverse settings. The data particularly highlight the urban-rural immunity gap as a persistent challenge requiring targeted public health strategies.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from rigorous nationwide sampling and standardized testing across diverse regions. However, several limitations warrant consideration: exclusion of Tigray region limits national representativeness; the assay cannot distinguish vaccine-derived antibodies; potential underestimation from antibody waning; self-reported comorbidities may introduce recall bias; and the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. These factors should inform interpretation of regional comparisons.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that Ethiopia’s COVID-19 vaccination campaign has significantly increased population-wide SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence to 48.0% (95% CI: 46.6–49.4) by August 2022. The IgG-dominant antibody profile (45.1% IgG vs. 1.2% IgM) indicates this immunity stems primarily from prior infection rather than recent exposure. While this approaches seroprevalence levels seen in many African and European nations, it remains below estimated thresholds for herd immunity.

Geographic analysis revealed substantial disparities, with urban Addis Ababa showing 68.1% seropositivity compared to just 39.6% in rural Gambela region. Significant variations were also observed across demographic groups, including higher seroprevalence among women (50.1% vs. men 45.8%), urban residents (55.6% vs. rural 45.7%), and vaccinated individuals (59.1% vs. unvaccinated 37.2%). Notably, elderly populations (≥ 65 years) and those with comorbidities like diabetes showed both elevated seropositivity and greater risk of severe outcomes.

These findings necessitate targeted public health interventions. First, priority vaccination efforts through mobile units should address coverage gaps in rural Gambela and Sidama regions (< 40% seroprevalence). Second, urban centers like Addis Ababa require booster campaigns to address potential waning immunity suggested by high IgG levels. Third, enhanced serosurveillance should monitor emerging variants in high-transmission areas and protect vulnerable groups including the elderly and those with chronic conditions.

The convergence of natural and vaccine-derived immunity presents Ethiopia with a strategic opportunity to transition from emergency response to sustainable management of COVID-19. This transition should be guided by continued seromonitoring to identify evolving immunity gaps and the implementation of equity-focused vaccination programs tailored to regional epidemiological profiles.

These findings underscore the critical need for periodic national serosurveys in Ethiopia to monitor immunity dynamics, particularly given: the potential for waning antibody protection, emerging variants that may evade immunity, and shifting epidemiological patterns as COVID-19 becomes endemic. We recommend biennial surveys complemented by targeted sentinel surveillance in high-risk regions to optimally guide vaccination strategies and public health preparedness. Our findings provide the evidence base for this next phase of pandemic control in Ethiopia.
