Introduction

The term nephrotic syndrome refers to a distinct constellation of clinical and laboratory features of renal disease. It is specifically defined by the presence of heavy proteinuria (protein excretion greater than 3.5 g/24 hours), hypoalbuminemia (less than 3 g/dl), and peripheral edema. Hyperlipidemia and thrombotic disease are also frequently observed [1].

Heavy proteinuria with or without nephrotic syndrome may occur in association with a wide variety of primary and systemic diseases. The majority of cases of glomerular proteinuria range from 1 to 15 g of protein/24 hours of urine [2–4]. In adults, approximately 30% have a systemic disease such as diabetes mellitus, amyloidosis, or systemic lupus erythematosus; the remaining cases are usually due to primary renal disorders such as minimal change disease (MCD), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), and membranous nephropathy [5–7]. The characteristic histologic finding in minimal change disease is diffuse effacement of the epithelial cell foot processes on electron microscope [8].

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is among the most common lesions found in cases of idiopathic nephrotic syndrome in adults. It accounts for 35% of all cases in the USA and more than 50% of cases among Black individuals [9]. FSGS can present as an idiopathic syndrome (primary FSGS) or may be associated with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, reflux nephropathy, previously healed glomerular injury, and massive obesity.

Clinical features that are more common in FSGS are hematuria, hypertension, and decreased renal function. There is, however, substantial overlap in these features. In addition to careful review of the renal biopsy, steroid resistance in a patient considered to have minimal change disease should raise suspicion about FSGS [10, 11].

We report the case of a 52-year-old male patient with HIV from Ethiopia on antiretroviral therapy (ART) with undetectable viral load who presented with a 2-year history of intermittent generalized body swelling; thorough investigations showed primary nephrotic syndrome, likely minimal change disease, with massive proteinuria of 25.4 g per 24 hours of urine.

Case presentation

This case describes a 52-year-old male civil servant from Ethiopia with HIV for the past 13 years on ART. His initial treatment regimen was tenofovir + lamivudine + efavirenz, which was recently changed to tenofovir + lamivudine + dolutegravir. He had an undetectable viral load upon yearly determination. He presented with intermittent generalized body swelling for the past 2 years. The swelling was initially in the periorbital area but later progressed to involve the abdomen and bilateral lower extremities with associated foamy urine, specifically in the early morning. He had no palpitations, orthopnea, or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. Medication history was unremarkable apart from the previously mentioned treatment regimen, ART. He had no personal or family history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or cardiac disease.

On physical examination, he had facial puffiness, gross ascites on abdominal examination, and bilateral lower extremity edema; vital signs were all in the normal range. Other parts of the physical examination were unremarkable.

Upon evaluation, 24-hour urine protein determination showed proteinuria of 25.4 g and renal biopsy revealed features of nonproliferative morphology in viable glomeruli with focal periglomerular fibrosis on light microscopy and no significant glomerular immune deposits on direct immunofluorescence studies. Electron microscopy studies showed diffuse effacement of visceral epithelial cell foot processes with no electron-dense or organized deposits in mesangial areas or glomerular basement membrane (GBM). This ultrastructural feature from the electron microscopy (EM) study suggested a primary podocytopathy/diffuse podocyte injury likely representing MCD/unsampled FSGS in the clinical context of nephrotic syndrome.

Baseline serum creatinine and serum albumin were 2.4 mg/dl and 1.8 g/dl, respectively (Table 1). Urine microscopy and dipstick studies showed proteinuria of +4 but no red blood cells (RBCs) or active urinary sediments. Initial lipid panel determination showed hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia. Other investigations, such as complete blood count (CBC), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), liver chemistries, serum electrolytes, antinuclear antibody (ANA) test, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), anti-hepatitis C virus antibody (anti-HCVab), Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) test, abdominal ultrasound, electrocardiography, and echocardiography were unremarkable.

Investigation summary

FSGS focal segmental glomerulosclerosis

A diagnosis of primary minimal change disease (MCD) versus primary FSGS with massive proteinuria was reached with evidence from history and physical findings, as previously mentioned. The renal biopsy and electron microscopy study showed diffuse effacement of visceral epithelial cell foot processes, which is the typical histologic feature of MCD, though unsampled FSGS is still a possibility. The absence of hypertension and hematuria and a lesser degree of renal derangement with such a degree of proteinuria were against a diagnosis of FSGS. After getting the renal biopsy result, the patient was started on prednisolone 1 mg/kg, enalapril, atorvastatin, loop diuretics, and prophylactic anticoagulation. After 3 and 4 months of initiation of treatment, 24-hour urine protein decreased from 969 mg/24 hours to 223 mg/24 hours, respectively. Serum creatinine and albumin went from 0.9 mg/dl to 3.7 g/dl after 4 months of treatment (Table 1). The patient achieved complete remission within 4 months of initiation of prednisolone, and now he is doing well off of steroids. We are following him with monthly urine dipstick and 24-hour urine protein determination at intervals of 3 months.

Discussion

Minimal change disease (MCD) is a steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome in which the only structural abnormality is podocyte swelling and fusion of foot processes on electron microscopy. MCD accounts for 70–90% of cases of nephrotic syndrome in children >1 year of age. However, in adults, it accounts for approximately 15% of patients with idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (NS) [10]. It also may occur in adults as an idiopathic condition, in association with the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or as a paraneoplastic effect of malignancy, most often Hodgkin lymphoma. In MCD, the average urine protein excretion reported in 24 hours is around 10 g, with severe hypoalbuminemia. Less common clinical features include hypertension (30% in children, 50% in adults), microscopic hematuria (20% in children, 33% in adults), atopy or allergic symptoms (40% in children, 30% in adults), and decreased renal function (25–40%), which often returns to normal after remission of the nephrotic syndrome. The appearance of acute renal failure in adults is seen more commonly in patients with low serum albumin and intrarenal edema that is responsive to diuretics. Acute tubular necrosis and interstitial inflammation are also reported [3, 4, 8, 11].

Steroid responsiveness in nephrotic syndrome reflects complete remission of proteinuria (<300 mg/day or trace on dipstick) within 4–8 weeks of corticosteroid therapy, whereas steroid resistance is failure to achieve remission after 4–8 weeks of daily high-dose corticosteroid therapy [10]. Distinguishing between these two is crucial for determining prognosis and treatment.

Overall, 90–95% of children with MCD will develop complete remission after 8 weeks of steroid therapy, and 80–85% of adults will achieve complete remission, but only after a longer course of 20–24 weeks. Adults are not considered steroid resistant until after 4 months of therapy. Patients with steroid resistance may have FSGS on repeat biopsy. If the first renal biopsy does not have a sample of deeper corticomedullary glomeruli, then the correct diagnosis of FSGS may be missed. These diagnostic uncertainty between MCD and FSGS primarily arises owing to their clinical overlap and similar pathological features, particularly in the early stages. Both conditions present with nephrotic syndrome (proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, edema), and podocyte foot process effacement is observed on electron microscopy in both. However, FSGS is characterized by focal and segmental glomerulosclerosis, which may not be apparent in small biopsy samples or early lesions. Misdiagnosis can occur if insufficient glomeruli are sampled or if FSGS lesions are subtle. Additionally, FSGS may develop after steroid-resistant MCD, further complicating differentiation. Genetic testing can help in some cases, but treatment response (steroid responsiveness) remains a key distinguishing factor, with MCD typically responding to steroids and FSGS often being resistant [2–4, 8, 11].

Our case described a 52-year-old adult patient with HIV with typical clinical features of nephrotic syndrome, specifically steroid-responsive primary MCD, but unusual massive glomerular proteinuria of 25.4 g per 24 hours, who achieved complete remission within 4 months of steroid treatment.

Conclusion

Heavy proteinuria of 25.4 g per 24 hours is a rare presenting feature of adult primary nephrotic syndrome, specifically minimal change disease.
