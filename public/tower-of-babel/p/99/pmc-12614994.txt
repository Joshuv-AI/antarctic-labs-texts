Assessing the Potential of Sexual Transmission of Lassa Virus in a Natural Rodent Reservoir
Authors: Jérémie Prévost, Nikesh Tailor, Geoff Soule, Kathy Frost, Kimberly Azaransky, Kevin Tierney, Heinz Feldmann, Stephanie Booth, Kyle Rosenke, David Safronetz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLassa virus (LASV) is maintained in and transmitted by Mastomys natalensis across West Africa. To date, few studies have addressed transmission of LASV between rodents. Here, we experimentally assessed the potential for sexual transmission of LASV in Mastomys. Intravaginal challenge led to systemic infection in females within 7 days of inoculation and persisted throughout the study. Percutaneous injection of males similarly resulted in a systemic infection, with LASV documented in reproductive organs and sperm for 21–42 days. Our data support the potential of sexual transmission of LASV in rodents, and intravaginal infection is potentially conducive to intrauterine transmission of LASV.Keywords: Lassa virus, arenavirus, Mastomys natalensis, sexual transmission, reservoir host

Lassa virus (LASV, genus Mammarenavirus, family Arenaviridae) is a rodent-borne zoonotic virus that is endemic in many West Africain countries [1]. In humans, LASV causes a wide spectrum of disease manifestations, the most significant of which include hemorrhagic indicators and multiorgan failure, a condition referred to as Lassa fever [2, 3]. Although human-to-human transmission of LASV is well documented, particularly in nosocomial settings, most human exposures and infections are caused by transmission from naturally infected rodents [4]. Estimates vary, but annually >300 000 LASV infections are thought to occur across West Africa [5].

In nature, LASV is primarily maintained in the Natal multimammate rat (Mastomys natalensis), though recent ecological studies have identified other potential reservoirs as well [6, 7]. Upon exposure to LASV, M. natalensis develops a systemic infection with no obvious signs of disease. Virus can be found in most organs analyzed, though the duration of infection is not well defined. In experimentally infected rodents, LASV can be detected for at least 1 month and possibly lifelong, despite mounting a humoral immune response generally within 7–14 days postexposure [8, 9]. Based on these characteristics, most ecologically based studies assessing LASV prevalence in regional rodent populations focus on serological detection of exposure and/or molecular detection of virus in selected tissues [10].

The duration of infectiousness of rodents is also not well defined. Viral transmission between rodents, and to humans, is understudied due in part to the complexities of collecting appropriate samples during ecological studies to determine the shedding patterns of virus in excreta/secreta. Nevertheless, it is widely accepted that horizontal transmission between rodents occurs via infectious particles shed within oropharyngeal fluids, urine, and feces. Experimentally, this has also been indirectly implied in recent laboratory-based studies [8, 11]. In addition, vertical transmission of LASV was recently described in experimentally infected Mastomys and, similar to the closely related lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) in mice, intrauterine transmission of LASV resulted in lifelong viral persistence in pups [9].

The aim of the current study was to gauge the potential of sexual transmission of LASV in the Mastomys reservoir model through assessing the intravaginal challenge route in females, and determining the extent and duration of LASV in sperm of infected males. Although sexual transmission of LASV is not essential for vertical transmission, experimentally assessing its feasibility within natural reservoirs will help define mechanisms of transmission that could directly result in persistently infected pups. Further, sexual transmission of high consequence viral pathogens has garnered increased attention based on asymptomatic spread of viruses like Ebola from recovered patients. The development of a sexual transmission model for LASV would provide a unique opportunity to evaluate medical countermeasures to inhibit such events from occurring.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics and Biosafety

Ethical approval for working with animals was obtained by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Canadian Science Center for Human and Animal Health. Animal procedures were conducted by trained staff according to the guidelines of the Canadian Council for Animal Care (CCAC) in a CCAC-approved facility. All work with infectious LASV was conducted in a Biosafety Level 4 laboratory of the Public Health Agency of Canada and adhered to institutional practices and procedures.

Animals, Inoculations, and Sampling

Sexually mature, adult (>12 weeks old) male and female M. natalensis were obtained from an inhouse breeding colony maintained at Rocky Mountain Laboratories (Hamilton, Montana, USA). The founders of the colony originated from Mali, West Africa [11]. A total of 75 rodents (38 males, 37 females) were inoculated with a LASV strain Soromba-R (clade V), which was originally isolated from a naturally infected M. natalensis collected in the village of Soromba in southern Mali [12]. All rodents were inoculated with 2 × 105 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) with females receiving the inoculum via the intravaginal instillation using a p200 pipette with a sterile, blunted tip, whereas males were inoculated via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection (Supplementary Figure 1). Prior to infection, animals were sedated using inhalational isoflurane. At 7, 14, 21, and 28 days postinoculation (dpi), groups of 8 males and 8 females were euthanized via exsanguination under deep sedation. The remaining 10 Mastomys (5 males and 5 females) were monitored for 42 days, after which they were euthanized with samples collected for assessment of viral persistence. At necropsy, cardiac blood (serum), lung, and liver specimens were collected for virological and histopathological analysis. In addition, the testis and epididymis were collected from males and the ovaries and uterine horns were collected from females. For sperm collection, the cauda epididymis was teased apart using 2 sterile 18–20 G needles in 1 mL of Minimum Essential Medium. Sperm was allowed to flow out into the media for 3–5 minutes, after which the media was collected for subsequent analysis.

Lassa Virus Detection

Organs, serum, and sperm were extracted for RNA and tested for the presence of LASV using molecular methods as previously described [8]. The reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assay was performed using LASV clade V–specific primers (forward: 5′-TTCGGGAGCCCACTGATC and reverse: 5′-CCATATTCCGAGGAAGTGCC), and probe (5′-FAM-ACTGGTCACACCAGGTTGTGCAGCA) targeting the S segment. Infectious LASV titers (TCID50) in organs, serum, and sperm were determined using standard methods as described elsewhere [8]. Histological analysis, including hematoxylin and eosin and immunohistochemical staining, was conducted on formalin-fixed specimens using standard methodologies. The LASV nucleocapsid (N) antigen was visualized using rabbit polyclonal antibody (Cusabio, 1:1000 dilution) followed by a biotinylated anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G secondary antibody (1:300 dilution) and horseradish peroxidase–coupled streptavidin (both from Jackson ImmunoResearch).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software). Statistical details of experiments are indicated in the figure legends. P values <.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Lassa Virus Is Readily Detectable in Male Reproductive Organs and Sperm

To evaluate the presence of virus in the male reproductive tract and sperm, 38 male M natalensis were infected by i.p. injection with LASV Soromba-R and samples were collected at regular intervals for analysis (Supplementary Figure 1). Detection of LASV by molecular methods in serum peaked at 7 dpi and declined until 28 dpi with apparent clearance by 42 dpi (Figure 1). Despite relatively high amounts of LASV RNA, infectious LASV was rarely detected beyond the 7 dpi collection point. As in previous studies, LASV was readily detectable within 7 dpi by both molecular and infectious assays in lung, liver, and testis/epididymis samples (Figure 1A and 1B). Viral burden showed a slow gradual decline in lung and liver tissues, with several specimens remaining positive for infectious virus at the final time point (42 dpi). Decreasing levels of viral RNA were noted in the reproductive tract of male Mastomys, which coincided with infectious viral titers dropping below the limit of detection between 21 and 28 dpi. Sperm also demonstrated an abundance of viral RNA detected throughout the course of the study, as well as infectious virus still detectable in 60% of the animals at 42 dpi (Figure 1C).

No significant histopathologic changes were observed at any time point in evaluated tissues from male Mastomys (Supplementary Figure 2). Anti-LASV N antigen was noted in Sertoli cells within the testis, especially early postinfection, though modest staining was still observed at 42 dpi (Figure 1D).

Intravaginal Challenge Results in a Systemic Infection in Female Mastomys

To assess the potential of sexual transmission of LASV to naive females, 37 M. natalensis were inoculated with LASV Soromba-R via direct intravaginal instillation (Supplementary Figure 1). Within 1 week of infection, LASV was detectable not only within the female reproductive tract but also in unrelated organ systems as noted with positive liver and lung samples (Figure 2A and 2B). Detection in serum was similar to that in males, though even with sensitive molecular techniques, virus was seemingly cleared in serum between 14 and 21 dpi and infectious virus was below the limit of detection in most samples at all time points (Figure 2C). Peak detection of LASV in the female reproductive tract occurred at 7 dpi, and although a reduction of viral burden was observed, molecular detection of virus persisted until 28 dpi (Figure 2A). Infectious virus levels fell below the limit of detection by 21 dpi. LASV levels in lung and liver specimens appeared more consistent throughout the course of the 42-day study with infectious virus still detected at the final time point (Figure 2B). Of note, the organ titers of infectious LASV in female rodents inoculated via the intravaginal route mirrored those from males infected by i.p. injection.

No significant histopathologic lesions were observed in evaluated tissues from female Mastomys analyzed during the course of this study (Supplementary Figure 3). At 7 dpi, diffuse immunoreactivity for LASV N antigen was apparent throughout the endometrium of the uterus (Figure 2D). By day 14, this immunoreactivity had largely disappeared.

DISCUSSION

Previously, we have shown that experimental inoculation of adult M. natalensis with LASV results in a transient infection that is seemingly cleared within weeks [8]. Confirming these results, Hoffmann and colleagues further demonstrated that inoculation of rodents within 2 weeks of birth results in persistent infection and that these persistently infected rodents remain infectious, presumably lifelong [9]. They also demonstrate intrauterine transmission of virus directly to pups, and confirmed they too become persistently infected, as was hypothesized based on historic work conducted with LCMV [13].

In the current study, we aimed to assess the feasibility of sexual transmission of LASV within its rodent reservoir. In an initial assessment, we examined the serially collected reproductive tracts and sperm from male Mastomys rodents that were experimentally inoculated via i.p. injection (a route previously shown to result in a systemic infection) for evidence of transmissible virus. Using methods similar to those employed in previous Zika virus studies [14], we found high levels of LASV in the reproductive tracts and seminal fluids collected from acutely infected male Mastomys. These results imply that LASV can be sexually transmitted from adult Mastomys.

In a second iteration of the study, we assessed if intravaginal challenge of female Mastomys would result in a productive and systemic LASV infection. Despite the differing inoculation routes used in the current study for males (i.p.) and females (intravaginal), both groups had readily detectable LASV via molecular and infectious assays in multiple organs within 7 days. Although cycle threshold values were higher in specimens collected from female Mastomys compared to those from males, infectious titers were comparable between the 2 experimental groups. The viral burden in lungs began to diminish between days 14 and 21 in both groups. Similar, but slightly delayed, trends were noted in liver specimens using molecular techniques, and although infectious titers in these samples did not complement this observation, the methods used for viral titration may have been compromised by toxicity of the liver sample itself. Serum titers in both groups were also comparable, though infectious titers were often below the limit of detection of the infectious assay. Nevertheless, the data presented here demonstrate that intravaginal inoculation leads to a systemic and productive infection in female Mastomys rodents that is indistinguishable from inoculation routes previously assessed in this model. This is supported by a previous study showing that the vaginal mucosa is particularly susceptible to infection by RNA viruses, including LCMV [15].

Overall, the data presented in the current study support the potential for sexual transmission of LASV from an infected male to a naive female. Should this event coincide with conception, there is a high probability that the pups will be born persistently infected with LASV. The ecological impact of such transmission events should not be understated given that persistently infected pups represent a constant source of LASV in nature as opposed to Mastomys that contract infection later in life, which, at least experimentally, is likely transient. Our findings of the presence of infectious LASV in reproductive fluids of infected male Mastomys, as well as the ability of intravaginal challenge to result in a productive and systemic infection in female Mastomys, also support the use of this sexual transmission model to evaluate medical countermeasures aimed at preventing such events. These studies may be particularly informative when using persistently infected male rodents in order to determine efficacy of specific countermeasures in reducing viral burden in immune-privileged sites.
