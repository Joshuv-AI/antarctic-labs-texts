Social vulnerability index and inflammation: a proteomic analysis linking social context to biological risk in older black adults
Authors: Anat Yaskolka Meir, Henry A. Adeola, Shinya Tasaki, Liming Liang, Renã A. S. Robinson, Brittney S. Lange-Maia, Melissa Lamar, Ana W. Capuano, Lisa L. Barnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammation is a key driver of age-related disease and has been associated with social conditions. We examined how cumulative community-level social and structural disadvantage is associated with inflammatory proteomic profiles in older Black adults.MethodsWe employed data from the Minority Aging Research Study and the Rush Clinical Core, including the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI; global score and four domains) and 92 plasma inflammatory proteins (Olink® Target-96 Inflammation). Cross-sectional associations between each SVI metric and inflammatory proteins (principal component (PC)-derived global proteomic profile and protein-specific) were assessed using multivariable linear models (demographic, behavioral, and individual-level socioeconomic factors adjusted), with additional sex-by-SVI interaction terms. In a secondary analysis, we replaced SVI with the Index of Concentration at the Extremes (ICE) for household income (ICEincome).ResultsA total of 580 participants (mean (SD) age of 74.9 (6.50) years; 79.7% women) had global SVI and proteomics assessed. Lower household composition (SVIHHC) was associated with the primary global proteomic profile, represented by the 1st proteomic PC (beta = −0.429, p-value = 0.019). A secondary exploratory analysis using the first five proteomic PCs showed that higher minority status/language (SVIMSL) and socioeconomic status (SVISES) were associated with an inflammatory proteomic profile (SVIMSL-PC3: beta=0.160, p-value = 0.048; SVISES-PC2: beta = 0.286, p-value = 0.012). In protein-specific analyses, no SVI-protein associations were found. We found an SVIHHC-by-sex interaction for interleukin-10 receptor alpha (IL-10RA; beta = −0.258, p-value = 5.01×10−4), and among men, SVIMSL was associated with Sirtuin 2 (beta = −0.397, p-value = 8.51×10−04) and STAM binding protein (beta = −0.304, p-value = 9.87×10−04). ICEincome was inversely associated with the global proteomic profile (beta = −0.233, p-value = 0.051), and an ICEincome-by-sex interaction was found for IL-10RA (beta=0.279, p-value = 4.66×10−04).ConclusionsBy analyzing associations between community-level factors and inflammation-related proteins, our study provides new molecular insights into how social context may relate to biological risk, identifies proteomic patterns that could inform the development of community-level interventions, and underscores the utility of integrating multi-omics approaches to investigate biological pathways relevant to health disparities research.

Background

Environmental and social stressors disproportionately affect Black communities in the United States [1]. Neighborhood disadvantage and economic hardship contribute to higher rates of chronic disease and premature mortality in these communities [2]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) is a measure of community-level social and structural disadvantage. Although originally developed for disaster planning [3], SVI has been linked to morbidity, including Alzheimer’s disease and long COVID, in older adults and in the general population [4–6]. Inflammation is a critical driver of age-related disease [7], and multiple social determinants of chronic inflammation have been identified, including dietary, psychosocial, political, neighborhood and built environment, social, and healthcare factors [8].

Proteomics, which captures molecular profiles of an individual’s health status and disease risk [9, 10], represents a promising approach for characterizing how neighborhood disadvantage and social vulnerability are associated with molecular signatures of inflammation. Previous proteomic studies have reported significant associations between social vulnerability and morbidity. For example, one study employing more than 6500 circulating proteins in a sample of ~3000 individuals from the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults study reported that a proteomic signature of social vulnerability was associated with a composite of cardiovascular disease/mortality [11]. Another study of adults from socioeconomically deprived and affluent areas of Glasgow, Scotland, found that both socioeconomic deprivation and diabetes were associated with proteomic signatures indicating worse cardiometabolic health, with the highest predicted cardiovascular risk observed in diabetic participants living in deprived areas [12]. In contrast, a study of two aspects of social relationships (i.e., social integration and perceived social support) and six inflammatory proteins found limited evidence of associations [13]. However, previous studies have primarily included predominantly White or mixed-ancestry populations and have not specifically examined how social vulnerability (particularly as measured by the CDC’s SVI) is associated with the proteomic landscape of inflammation among older Black adults, who experience both disproportionate social adversity and elevated chronic disease risk.

Given the established associations between social vulnerability, adverse health outcomes, and inflammation, we hypothesize that SVI is associated with distinct inflammatory proteomic features. Specifically, we use cross-sectional data from the Minority Aging Research Study (MARS) and the Rush Clinical Core to primarily examine whether SVI scores (global score and four domains) are associated with distinct inflammatory proteomic profiles in older Black adults. As sex differences in social vulnerability were previously observed [14, 15], we further explored sex differences and SVI-sex interactions. Whereas SVI captures the overall burden of social vulnerability across multiple domains, the Index of Concentration at the Extremes (ICE) characterizes the degree to which populations within an area are concentrated at extremes of deprivation versus privilege [16]. In this context, ICE for income (ICEincome) serves as an indicator of income-based spatial polarization, reflecting structural economic inequality and its link to adverse health outcomes [17]. As a secondary analysis, we replaced the SVI scores with ICEincome to assess whether the spatial concentration of economic privilege and disadvantage (regardless of race) captures similar or distinct inflammatory signatures of structural inequity. By quantifying the association between these community-level measures and circulating inflammation-related proteins in older Black adults, our study aims to identify molecular associations of social context and biological risk.

Methods

Study population

Data collection in MARS began in August 2004 with annual structured clinical evaluations, which included a detailed assessment of risk factors, neurological examination, donation of a blood sample, and comprehensive neuropsychological testing. Eligibility criteria were age 65 years or older, no prior diagnosis of dementia, and self-identification as Black/African American (i.e., answering questions from the 1990 U.S. Census) [18]. Data collection in the Rush Clinical Core began in 2008 with older Black Americans ( > 65 years) without dementia recruited from the same catchment area as MARS [19]. These cohorts have harmonized measures allowing them to be combined in analyses. Data collection included annual assessments of risk factors for Alzheimer’s disease, medical history, and cognitive function. Both studies were approved by an Institutional Review Board at Rush University Medical Center, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Plasma inflammatory proteins by Olink

Proteomic data preprocessing and quality control (QC) were previously described [20]. Briefly, blood plasma samples were collected, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C before analysis using the Olink® Target-96 Inflammation panel. Protein quantification was performed on the Olink® Signature Q100 instrument, with normalized protein expression (NPX) values generated and quality-checked according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To address inter-plate variability, normalization was performed using 16 bridging samples, included across the main and rerun plates, adjusting NPX values and limits of detection to ensure consistency across different assay lots. Quality control used pooled and external control samples placed on each plate. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 6% and 25%, respectively. QC at the sample level involved removing samples with all missing values, duplicates, and extreme outliers identified by multidimensional scaling (samples more than 6 SD from the median), and retaining one representative per set of bridging samples. At the protein level, all 92 proteins were adequately quantified ( > 85% across samples) and retained. Missing values for proteins with up to 14% missingness were imputed using the Random Forest-based “missForest” R package. The above procedure yielded 642 eligible samples for downstream analysis.

Primary exposure: SVI assessment

The assessment of global SVI and the four SVI domains was described previously, as was the geocoding of participant addresses, with participants providing their current address at study entry or shortly thereafter [21]. In brief, the SVI, developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, ranks U.S. Census tracts according to 15 sociodemographic indicators grouped into four domains. These indicators reflect various social and demographic attributes of an area where people reside and include: (1) socioeconomic status (SVISES), encompassing poverty level, unemployment, housing cost burden, education, and health insurance coverage; (2) household composition (SVIHHC), including age distribution, disability status, and single-parent households; (3) minority status/language (SVIMSL), representing racial and ethnic composition and English proficiency; and (4) housing and transportation (SVIHAT), reflecting multi-unit or mobile housing, crowding, institutional group living, and access to a vehicle. Participants’ study entry year was used to determine the SVI year for their total score (e.g., the 2017 and 2018 entry years used 2018 SVI data). A higher global SVI score (ranging from 0.00 to 1.00) indicates greater social vulnerability at the Census tract level.

Secondary exposure: ICEincome assessment

We used the ICEincome, a geographic information system-based measure of social spatial polarization, to quantify the concentration of residents into highly privileged (i.e., >80th percentile of U.S. household income) versus extreme poverty (i.e., <20th percentile income) groups at the census-tract level [16, 22]. Values range from -1 (all residents in the most deprived group) to +1 (all residents in the most privileged group), with 0 indicating equal representation of the two extremes at that census-tract.

Demographic, behavioral, and individual-level socioeconomic factors

Demographic factors included age (based on the provided date of birth and the age at the time of blood draw for proteomic quantification) and sex (at birth). The behavioral factor included three-level self-reported smoking (never smoked, former smoker, and current smoker). Other information obtained included individual-level socioeconomic factors: the number of years of formal education (i.e., regular school) and income (measured at the baseline interview with a single question asking participants to select one of ten income brackets) [23].

Statistical analysis

Figure 1 summarizes the overall study design and analytic framework, including the study population, community-level exposure measures, and the primary and secondary analytic approaches used to examine associations between social vulnerability and circulating inflammatory proteomic profiles. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics for the entire cohort and by sex. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on proteomic data (NPX values) to reduce dimensionality. For downstream analyses, we retained the first five principal components (PC1 to PC5), which capture the largest sources of variation in the dataset. We set PC1 as our primary global proteomic outcome, given that it explained the largest proportion of variance and represented the dominant dimension of inflammatory variation. In a secondary exploratory analysis, we examined all of the first 5 PCs. This choice focused on the leading components that captured the greatest variance, rather than on a predefined variance threshold or scree‐plot criterion. For interpretability, we also extracted the PCA loadings (protein coordinates) from the fitted PCA model, ranked proteins by the absolute value of their loadings on each component, and presented the top 15 proteins. Each SVI was standardized (mean 0, standard deviation (SD) of 1) before input into the following models. Since SVI and ICEincome were measured at the census tract level, we evaluated the degree of clustering by census tract. We summarized the number of unique census tracts and the distribution of participants per tract. Given sparse clustering with most having only one or a few participants, multilevel models with random census-tract effects were not used as an analytic approach. Associations between SVI domains and the global proteomic profile were assessed using univariate linear regression models (“null” models), with each SVI domain modeled as the independent variable and each proteomic PC as the dependent variable. We used multivariable linear regression models to adjust for covariates: demographic factors (age and sex; model 1), model 1 + behavioral factors (smoking status; model 2), and model 2 + individual-level socioeconomic factors (income and education; model 3 - fully adjusted model). For the primary global proteomic outcome (PC1), we added a sensitivity analysis that included, in addition to the covariates in Model 3, health and lifestyle factors: body mass index (BMI), alcohol use (grams/day), and major chronic conditions: vascular disease (cumulative score for vascular disease burden ranges from 0 to 3 based on self-reports), hypertension (yes/no, based on self-reports, and type 2 diabetes (yes/no based on self-reports and medication usage), all assessed at the time of proteomic blood draws). For the socioeconomic domain specifically (SVISES), we conceptualized individual socioeconomic position (individual-level education and income) as a background determinant related to both residential selection into more or less socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods and inflammatory biology. Accordingly, we treated education and income as confounders and interpreted SVISES coefficients as estimates of the association between neighborhood‑level socioeconomic vulnerability and proteomic outcomes, conditional on individual SES, rather than as total neighborhood effects unadjusted for individual socioeconomic position. We assessed multicollinearity between SVISES, individual-level education and income, and other covariates in the fully adjusted model using generalized variance inflation factors (VIFs). Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) was performed to visualize relationships among discretized (tertiles or categorized) SVI domains, selected proteomic PCs, and covariates using the “FactoMineR” R package. Protein-specific associations with SVI domains were analyzed using univariate and multivariable linear regression models incorporating the full adjustment strategy as above. To formally assess effect modification by sex, we first added sex × SVI interaction terms to these models. When interaction terms suggested sex‑dependent associations, the protein-specific analyses were repeated, stratified by sex to evaluate potential effect modification. For selected sex‑specific associations, we further conducted permutation tests by randomly permuting SVI values across individuals while holding protein levels and covariates fixed, refitting the regression model at each iteration and deriving empirical p‑values from the resulting null distribution of test statistics. Multiple-comparisons correction was performed using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with q-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant and q-values < 0.1 interpreted as exploratory. As a secondary analysis, we replaced the SVIs with ICEincome (standardized), used the Pearson correlation test to examine the correlation between the SVIs and ICEincome, and repeated our analytical models for the global proteomic profile and protein-specific analysis, with ICEincome replacing the SVIs. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2).

Global proteomic profile of social vulnerability indices

To identify patterns of variation in the global proteomic profile, we first applied PCA to the entire proteomics data. The first five PCs (PC1 to PC5) were extracted, collectively accounting for the majority of the dataset’s variance (Additional file 1: Figure S1).

Next, as we pre-specified PC1 as our primary global proteomic outcome, we first examined associations between each SVI (global SVI and the four domains) and PC1 using multivariable linear regression models (Fig. 2A, Additional file 1: Table S1). In these models, higher SVIHHC was inversely associated with PC1, and this association became stronger and statistically significant with additional adjustment: beta = −0.249, p-value = 0.137 in Model 1; beta = −0.365, p-value = 0.037 in Model 2; and beta = −0.429, p-value = 0.019 in the fully adjusted Model 3. A sensitivity analysis further adjusting Model 3 for lifestyle and health-related factors (BMI, alcohol intake, vascular disease, diabetes, and hypertension) did not attenuate the observed SVIHHC-PC1 association (beta = −0.423, p-value = 0.024).

In a secondary exploratory analysis, we assessed associations between each SVI and all five proteomic PCs (25 SVI-PC combinations in total, “null model”) and applied a single FDR correction across these 25 tests. For each SVI, we identified the PC with the smallest FDR-adjusted p-value (q-value) and the proteomic PC with the most significant absolute beta coefficient (Additional file 1: Table S2). This allowed us to identify the proteomic dimension most strongly associated with each SVI. For subsequent analyses, the most strongly associated proteomic PC for each SVI was carried forward and used as the outcome in a series of multivariable linear regression models, repeating the same models as indicated above (multivariable models 1 to 3, excluding PC1, which was examined in the primary analysis). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), higher SVIMSL (beta=0.160, p-value = 0.048) and SVISES (beta=0.286, p-value = 0.012) were strongly associated with their respective PCs (Fig. 2B). For SVISES, these estimates should be interpreted as an association between neighborhood‑level socioeconomic vulnerability and the selected proteomic component, conditional on individual‑level education and income. In the fully adjusted model including SVISES, age, sex, smoking, and individual-level education and income, generalized VIFs for SVISES, education, and income were in the 1 range (1.03–1.15; SVISES = 1.09, income = 1.03, education = 1.15), indicating minimal multicollinearity.

To support the biological interpretation of these components, we examined the protein loadings for PCs 1–3. PC1, which was associated with SVIHHC, was characterized by positive loadings of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) 5, Sirtuin 2 (SIRT2), and CXCL1. PC2, associated with SVISES, was characterized by positive loadings of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF-21), CXCL1, and CXCL20, and negative loadings of SIRT2, CXCL5, and Sulfotransferase 1A1 (ST1A1). PC3, associated with SVIMSL, was characterized by positive loadings of CXCL5, CXCL11, and CXCL6, and negative loadings of Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1), SIRT2, and Caspase-8 (CASP8). The top 15 proteins by absolute loading for each component are provided in Additional file 1: Table S3.

Exploratory data analysis: joint pattern analysis of SVIs, proteomic PCs, and covariates

Guided by the primary and exploratory SVI-PC associations (SVIMSL-PC3, SVISES-PC2, and SVIHHC-PC1), we conducted an exploratory multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) to visually examine joint patterns among these SVIs, their associated proteomic PCs, and covariates. For each significant SVI–proteomic PC pair, we discretized the relevant proteomic PC and SVI domain into tertiles. We included these, along with tertiles or categories for personal-level covariates (i.e., age, sex, smoking status, income bracket, and education), in the MCA. The resulting visualizations allowed us to explore the joint distribution and mutual associations among SVIs, demographic, behavioral, and individual-level socioeconomic factors, and proteomic PCs, complementing the regression findings.

For the SVISES, MCA showed that the first two dimensions accounted for 14.4% and 10.4% of the total variance, respectively. (Additional file 1: Fig. S2a). As expected, categories related to individual-level socioeconomic factors (i.e., income and education) exhibited the greatest contributions to dimension 1 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2b). In contrast, dimension 2 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2c) was shaped most strongly by demographic and behavioral factors.

For SVIMSL, MCA showed that the first two dimensions accounted for 11.8% and 10.2% of the total variance, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S3a). Dimension 1 was primarily defined by a clear separation between individual-level socioeconomic extremes, with the highest representation from both the lowest and highest income tertiles and education tertiles (Additional file 1: Fig. S3b). Dimension 2 was most strongly shaped by sociodemographic factors (Additional file 1: Fig. S3c).

For the SVIHHC, MCA showed that the first two dimensions accounted for 12.9% and 9.8% of the total variance, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S4a). Similar to SVISES and SVIMSL, dimension 1 was primarily defined by individual-level socioeconomic factors (Additional file 1: Fig. S4b), while dimension 2 was most strongly shaped by demographic and behavioral factors (Additional file 1: Fig. S4c).

Protein-specific analysis

To build on the insights from the global proteomic profile analyses, we next performed a protein-specific analysis of associations between SVIs and proteins.

First, we evaluated the univariate association between each SVI and each protein to identify the most robustly associated inflammatory proteins with social vulnerability (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). We found that SVISES was associated with monocyte chemoattractant protein-3; MCP-3; beta=0.097, p = 0.00145, q-value = 0.0668), interleukin (IL) 17-A (beta=0.117, p = 0.00098, q-value = 0.0668), CUB domain containing protein 1 (CDCP1; beta=0.077, p = 0.00403, q-value = 0.0764), IL17-C (beta=0.116, p = 0.00355, q-value = 0. 0764), and CCL20 (beta=0.130, p = 0.00415, q-value = 0.0764).

Multivariable regression models were used to further assess these associations while accounting for covariates, including demographic, behavioral, and individual-level socioeconomic factors. No significant associations were observed for this model (Additional file 1: Fig. S6), but examining interactions between each SVI and sex across the panel of proteins showed one significant SVIHHC-by-sex interaction for IL-10 receptor alpha (IL-10RA; beta = −0.258, 95% CI [−0.404, −0.113], p-value = 5.01×10−4, q-value = 0.046).

In an exploratory sex-stratified analysis, we evaluated the association between SVIs and individual proteins in men (n = 118) and women (n = 462). Among men, higher SVIMSL was associated with lower levels of SIRT2 (beta = −0.397, 95% CI: [−0.626, −0.169], p-value = 8.51×10−04, q-value = 0.045) and STAM binding protein (STAMBP; beta = −0.304, 95% CI: [−0.482, −0.127], p-value = 9.87×10−04, q-value = 0.045) (Fig. 3A). To contextualize these findings, given the modest male sample size and extensive multiple testing, we conducted permutation tests within men by randomly permuting SVIMSL across individuals while holding protein levels and covariates fixed. The p‑values were 0.0016 for SIRT2 and 0.0011 for STAMBP, indicating that these associations are stronger than expected under random assignment of SVIMSL but still best interpreted as hypothesis‑generating.

Secondary analysis

We repeated our analysis, examining the global proteomic profile and specific proteins by replacing the SVIs with ICEincome. We first examined the correlations between each SVI and ICEincome (Additional file 1: Fig. S7) to characterize how the indices are related, and found that all SVIs, except SVIMSL, were significantly inversely correlated with ICEincome.

Finding the most strongly associated PC (Additional file 1: Table S4) and repeating our three multivariable models for ICEincome-global proteomic associations showed that ICEincome, similar to SVISES, remained significantly associated with the proteomic PC2 in all models (Fig. 4, Additional file 1: Table S5), but with a beta estimate of opposite sign. This pattern is consistent with the indices’ constructs.

In univariate models examining specific proteins, several associations between ICEincome and inflammation proteins existed (Additional file 1: Fig. S8), but these associations did not remain statistically significant when accounting for demographic, behavioral, and individual-level socioeconomic factors. Discussion

In this study of older Black adults, our primary analysis showed that one domain of social vulnerability was significantly associated with the global inflammatory proteomic profile as summarized by the first PC. In a secondary exploratory analysis of the first five PCs, we observed domain‑specific associations that further supported a link between neighborhood social vulnerability and inflammatory variation. In protein-specific exploratory analyses, we found evidence of sex-related differences in these associations. Substituting the SVI with ICEincome in a secondary analysis produced complementary but weaker results, reinforcing a robust, though not identical, association between social context and systemic inflammation.

Our findings, which associate specific census-tract SVI domains at the Census level (i.e., minority status/language, socioeconomic status, and household composition) with the global proteomic profile, reflect chronic stress exposures associated across these community-level conditions and their influence on inflammatory regulation. By design, SVIMSL reflects area-level racial/ethnic composition and language proficiency; SVISES reflects neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage, including poverty, unemployment, lower educational attainment, and lack of health insurance; and SVIHHC reflects household and age-related structural vulnerability, including having a high proportion of households with older adults, children, or persons with disability, and single-parent households. As a result, domain-specific conditions may be associated with different forms of chronic stress and related exposures. For example, higher SVIMSL may be linked to higher racial stressors reflecting structural and cultural barriers; higher SVISES to persistent financial strain and more employment insecurity; and higher SVIHHC to higher caregiving burden, crowding, social isolation, or family strain. More broadly, living in a socially disadvantaged and under-resourced area can amplify a variety of cumulative stress exposures, limit access to supportive resources, and increase financial strain and environmental burdens [24, 25]. These conditions activate neuroendocrine and immune regulatory pathways that shape inflammatory signaling, providing a plausible explanation for the proteomic differences observed across SVI domains [26]. Although constrained by a small number of findings and the cross-sectional design, our results suggest that living in a socially vulnerable area may leave a measurable biological imprint on inflammatory regulation, with chronic stress as a plausible mechanistic link between structural disadvantage and proteomic variation.

To further contextualize these associations, we examined the protein loadings for the SVI‑associated principal components. PC1 (associated with SVIHHC), PC2 (associated with SVISES), and PC3 (associated with SVIMSL) were each dominated by proteins involved in chemokine‑mediated immune activation (CXCL1, CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL11, CXCL20) [27], metabolic and oxidative stress responses (SIRT2, FGF‑21, ST1A1, 4E‑BP1) [28–31], and apoptotic signaling (CASP8) [32]. These patterns suggest that chronic, domain‑specific social vulnerability may be reflected in coordinated alterations in stress‑responsive inflammatory pathways, although this interpretation remains speculative and will require confirmation in mechanistic and longitudinal studies

Although our protein-specific analysis did not identify any inflammation proteins significantly associated with the global SVI or its domains, we found sex- and context-specific molecular signatures of social vulnerability, including an interaction between the household composition index and sex for IL-10RA. This finding suggests that the biological consequences of household structure may differ for men and women in later life. Household composition, capturing factors such as living alone, single‑parent households, and multigenerational households, as well as levels of caregiving responsibility [3], reflects both the availability of social support and the distribution of daily demands. Reduced social support can increase exposure to chronic stress and, in turn, inflammation [33]. IL-10RA, a receptor involved in anti‑inflammatory signaling [34], may therefore represent a pathway through which social environments could exert sex‑specific effects on immune function. A possible interpretation of this observation is that differences in caregiving roles, social connectedness, or perceived burden between men and women modify the way neighborhood-level household composition influences inflammation‑related pathways. In addition to gendered caregiving roles, social connectedness, and perceived burden, our findings may reflect sex differences in hormonal regulation (e.g., estrogen’s anti‑inflammatory actions), immunosenescence, and behavioral coping responses to household structural stress, such as help‑seeking or reliance on social networks. Although these data are not available in the current study to test pathways, our findings highlight the importance of considering both social structure and gendered experiences when examining how community‑level vulnerability is associated with biological profiles. Further work, particularly studies with longitudinal designs and experimental or mechanistic approaches, will be critical to clarifying these pathways.

Further, among men, SIRT2 and STAMBP were significantly associated with SVISML - the minority status/language domain. Although this domain includes area-level language proficiency, it is less relevant to an all-Black cohort living in predominantly Black census tracts. Rather, this SVI domain may capture structural and interpersonal barriers associated with racialized minority status, including real estate discrimination, cultural segregation, and reduced access to culturally congruent services [3]. These community-level barriers can lead to daily individual-level stress, limit access to health‑promoting resources, and intensify exposure to discrimination, all of which activate inflammatory pathways [35–37]. In this context, SIRT2, a stress‑responsive deacetylase involved in metabolic and inflammatory regulation [38], and STAMBP, which participates in cytokine receptor regulation and inflammatory signaling [39], may index molecular adaptations to repeated activation of neuroendocrine-immune pathways in response to discrimination‑related stressors. Thus, the inflammation proteins associated with this domain in men may reflect biological responses to chronic social adversity patterned by racialized disadvantage.

In a secondary analysis, we replaced the SVIs with ICEincome, a measure of economic polarization that quantifies the extent to which residents within a specific geographic area (e.g., census tract or county) are concentrated into either highly privileged or highly deprived income‑based groups [17]. As expected, SVI and ICEincome were inversely correlated. We found some of the same associations, inverted as expected, between ICEincome and the global proteomic profile, as well as an ICEincome-by-sex interaction. Our ICEincome findings complement the SVI observations and align with previous studies showing that individuals living in areas with higher socioeconomic status and higher concentrations of high-income households have lower inflammation scores [40, 41].

Our study has several limitations. First, our findings cannot be generalized broadly, as the sample included only highly educated older Black adults. However, our study centers on a population that is underrepresented in molecular aging research and disproportionately exposed to structural disadvantage. Second, our study included only one targeted proteomic panel. Yet, our focus was on inflammation, employing a well‑validated inflammatory panel that captured a wide range of relevant pathways with high analytic reliability. Third, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish temporal associations or infer causal pathways between social vulnerability and inflammation. Longitudinal studies will be critical to determine whether SVI‑related social conditions precede and contribute to the emergence of these proteomic signatures over time. Fourth, although we adjusted for multiple demographic, behavioral, and individual-level socioeconomic factors, residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as lifetime stress exposure or environmental toxins, remains possible. Fifth, our male‑specific associations were identified in exploratory, underpowered analyses and were evaluated using permutation tests, which suggested that the observed effect sizes are unlikely to arise by chance alone under random assignment of SVIMSL. Given the modest sample size and sequential testing (interaction screening followed by stratification), we interpret these signals as hypothesis‑generating and warrant replication in larger, pre‑specified studies. Nevertheless, this is the largest study in the Black population to examine the association between multiple measures of social vulnerability and a broad panel of inflammatory proteins.

Conclusions

Our findings provide preliminary insight into the associations between community‑level disadvantage and late-life inflammatory proteomic profiles. Although most protein-specific associations did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment, we observed consistent associations between selected SVI domains and global inflammatory proteomic patterns derived from principal components. By focusing on these inflammatory proteomic patterns, rather than specific proteins, this study moves beyond traditional cytokine measures toward a more pathway‑level understanding of how social stress may be associated with inflammation. Our work underscores the value of integrating molecular profiling with social determinants research to explore potential biological pathways linking structural inequities and health disparities, while recognizing that causal inferences cannot be drawn from these cross-sectional data, and that the molecular specificity of our findings remains limited.
