Quinolone inhibitors targeting Vibrio cholerae sodium-dependent NADH: Ubiquinone oxidoreductase (NQR)
Authors: Zachary J. Liveris, Jonathon Kane, Emma Simmons, Ming Yuan, Yuyao Hu, Martin González-Montalvo, Jennifer M. Sorescu, Karina Tuz, Oscar X. Juarez, Daniel P. Becker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial agents have been at the forefront of global efforts to treat bacterial infections. However, their efficacy and reliability have steadily declined due to the widespread emergence of multidrug resistance (MDR). This challenge is particularly critical for Vibrio cholerae, the etiological agent of cholera, a disease that remains a significant global health burden in endemic and outbreak-prone regions. The increasing prevalence of MDR V. cholerae strains, coupled with a stagnating pipeline of new antibiotics, underscores the urgent need for novel antibacterial targets and therapeutic strategies. The sodium-translocating NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase (NQR) is a ubiquitous and essential aerobic respiratory enzyme found in several MDR bacterial pathogens, including V. cholerae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Chlamydia trachomatis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Herein, we report the first systematic investigation of quinolone-based inhibitors targeting NQR, involving the design, synthesis, and biological assessment of a series of 3-substituted quinolones inspired by the antimalarial lead compound endochin and other highly toxic quinolones. Our structure-guided optimization strategy mitigates the high toxicity historically associated with quinolone derivatives, yielding analogs with improved safety profiles. These compounds exhibit enhanced potency and selectivity against NQR, highlighting their potential as a new class of antibacterial agents against MDR pathogens.

Introduction

Cholera, caused by the Gram-negative bacterium Vibrio cholerae, is an acute intestinal infection that produces severe symptoms including diarrhea and vomiting leading to dehydration. Cholera is often lethal, especially in children, due to their lower body fluid reserves and limited access to adequate medical care [1]. There are approximately 2.9 million reported cases and 95,000 deaths annually as a result of this disease, which continues to be a prevalent global threat due to the proliferation of antibiotic resistance [2]. Moreover, cholera is typically encountered in underdeveloped countries, where infections stem from the consumption of unsanitary food and water [1]. With the assistance of the toxin-coregulated pilus (TCP), V. cholerae aggregates onto epithelial cells within the small intestines of the human host [2], followed by cholera toxin (CT) production, which causes electrolyte imbalance in the patient resulting in the life-threatening symptoms of the disease [3].

Our recent work [4] has shown that the essential respiratory enzyme NQR, a sodium dependent NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase, is in an optimal target for antibiotic development, especially against V. cholerae. NQR is coded by the nqr operon and is responsible for both energy transduction and virulence factor production [5]. NQR is a six-subunit (NqrA-F) integral membrane protein complex found in hundreds of species of pathogenic bacteria, including Vibrio cholerae, Chlamydia trachomatis, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, among others [5]. This is the initial enzyme in the aerobic electron transport chain transferring electrons from NADH to ubiquinone, supplying the lower part of the respiratory chain. NQR shuttles electrons through its six cofactors, while concurrently producing an electrochemical gradient by pumping Na+ ions into the periplasmic space [6,7]. In addition to its role in bacterial metabolism, NQR activity regulates the production of virulence factors, supports ATP synthesis, and enables nutrient transport [6]. Very recently, it was shown that clofazimine can be repurposed to treat cholera offering a novel class of antibiotics that target NQR [8].

Several ubiquinone analogs have been reported as NQR inhibitors, including HQNO (2-heptyl-4-hydroxyquinoline N-oxide) [9], with an IC50 in the sub micromolar range, and Korormicin A [10], which is 3000-fold more potent than HQNO (Fig. 1) [7,11]. Earlier studies suggested that both HQNO and Korormicin A act as mixed inhibitors vs ubiquinone [11] that do not compete with ubiquinone for its binding site. However, Cryo-EM data show that these inhibitors interact with one of the two ubiquinone binding sites, indicating that they can occupy the same structural motifs [12]. Importantly, inhibition of NQR by these molecules not only inactivated the natural function of the enzyme, but also leads to the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which is lethal for the bacterium [13].

Masuya and coworkers introduced probes into the N-terminal region of NQR using a 2-step conjugation technique using a korormicin A-templated ligand to monitor dynamic structural changes in the flexible cytoplasmic N-terminal region of the NqrB subunit that appears to play an important role in the final ubiquinone reduction and is the target of specific inhibitors [14]. Using cryo-EM structures and isothermal titration calorimetry to elucidate photoaffinity labeling studies using photoreactive korormicin, Murai also showed that the NqrB-G141A mutant, which confers some resistance to the natural product korormicin A, is due to the altered binding cavity in the NQR mutant leading to decreased potency by the natural product [15]. NQR moves Na+ ions from the cytoplasm to the periplasm establishing an electrochemical gradient [16].

In addition to HQNO and korormicin, the quinolone Aurachin D-42 [17] (Fig. 1) has been reported to be an effective NQR inhibitor, exhibiting potency comparable to Koromicin A and approximately 500 times greater than HQNO. Inspired by the activity of these reported inhibitors, we conducted a targeted screen of fused-ring compounds that bear structural patterns similar to HQNO and aurachin D-42 against NQR, which led to the identification of the antiparasitic endochin [18] as a notable hit. Although HQNO and Aurachin D-42 are relatively potent inhibitors of NQR, their use in the clinic as antibiotics is precluded by their high toxicity profiles. In this work, we leveraged the quinolone scaffold found in the antimalarial endochin as a starting point in delivering endochin-like quinolone (ELQ) analogs with potency against NQR, safety versus human cells including HeLa cells, and acceptable physiochemical properties.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of substituted quinolones

Using endochin as the starting point, we sought to modulate the eastern periphery of the quinolone scaffold to enhance potency and selectivity for NQR. In our initial campaign, we prepared a series of 1,2-dimethylquinolone NQR inhibitors. Commercially available 2-methyl-4-quinolone (1) was subjected to alkaline iodination followed by regioselective alkylation to afford N-methylquinolone 2 as reported [19] alongside the minor O-methyl regioisomer (not shown). The N-methyl-3-iodoquinolone 2 was then subjected to a series of C (sp2)-C (sp2) Suzuki cross couplings utilizing arylboronic acid building blocks (Scheme 1) yielding a diverse set of 3-arylquinolones with various substitution patterns (3a-s) [20]. The structures of all intermediates and final products were verified by 1H and 13C NMR, and the elemental composition of all compounds tested was further confirmed with high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS).

Following the successful introduction of various aryl groups at the C3 position on the quinolone framework, we then intended to expand off this quadrant by exploring the impact on potency through amide linkers. This enables us to explore the amide’s influence on the elusive binding pocket on NQR and allowed us to increase the ratio of heteroatoms within our analogs to enhance solubility. Intermediate 3h, which possesses a benzoic acid motif, was coupled to numerous amines employing standard EDC-mediated peptide coupling conditions to afford a set of quinolone amides (4a-e, see Scheme 2) [21].

Inspired by the parent endochin NQR hit, which contains a flexible aliphatic heptyl sidechain, we sought to design analogs that maintain this level of lipophilicity in this position while also aiming to reduce the entropic flexibility and increase the metabolic stability associated with the parent alkyl tether. To address these concerns, we opted to extend the 3-aryl motif by incorporating the privileged and more rigid isosteric diaryl ether group. The syntheses of five distinct diaryl ether boronates were carried out as shown in Scheme 3, to serve as building blocks in our convergent synthesis. Commercially available aryl boronic acids (5a-e) were converted into their respective organotrifluoroborate salts, and were then subjected to an aerobic Chan-Lam coupling in the presence of 4-bromophenol to afford a series of 4-bromo diaryl ether intermediates 6a-e which were employed in subsequent cross couplings [20,22]. The 4-phenoxy-bromobenzene intermediates were then interconverted into their respective BPin counterparts using classical Miyaura borylation conditions resulting in the BPin diaryl ether derivatives 7a-e. [20]. The complementary 4-phenoxyphenyl pinacolboronic ester units were convergently tethered onto the 3-iodo-1,2-dimethylquinolone (2) framework using standard Suzuki cross-coupling conditions to afford the targeted substituted 3-phenoxyphenyl quinolones (8a-e) [20].

Biochemical evaluation

The synthesized quinolone derivatives were evaluated for their effectiveness against V. cholerae NQR as summarized in Table 1. NQR was purified to homogeneity using methods described by our group [23] and activity was measured with the different inhibitors at two concentrations, 10 and 50 μM, to estimate their potency. Select inhibitors were then tested against V. cholerae growth at 50 μM and against HeLa cells and HFF-1 human fibroblasts at 10 μM to evaluate their antibiotic activity and toxicity, respectively. 10 μM is a relatively high, non-clinically relevant, concentration that allows us to quickly identify molecules that are tolerated by the human cells. Regarding solubility, 5–10 mM stock solutions of the compounds were prepared in DMSO with ease. The concentrations tested in our experiments were as high as 50 μM. No precipitation was noticed upon dilution, indicating good solubility in PBS and other buffer solutions.

We observed a geometrical influence associated with the aryl substituent residing at C3 on the quinolone scaffold. Specifically, the position of the trifluoromethyl group on the benzene motif present in compounds 3b and 3k has been demonstrated to heavily influence enzymatic activity. When poised in the para position, (3b or the closely related trifluoromethoxy compound 3a), it results in a significant drop in potency (<33% inhibition at 50 μM). However, when the substituent occupies the meta position, the potency is more than doubled, as in compounds 3k vs. 3b and 3r vs. 8e. Thus, the meta substituted phenyl quinolones showed improved activity when compared to their para substituted equivalents.

Interestingly, the diminished potency for para versus meta is less pronounced for relatively smaller groups than the trifluoromethyl group, such as methylthiol group (3c), methyl (3d) or chloro (3e), which showed a doubling in potency. However, the methylthiol substituent substantially increased toxicity against HeLa cells (19%), while maintaining no toxicity against HFF-1 cells. In all these cases, we noticed a trend that potency tends to increase as we add in more lipophilic character, while hydrophilic or amphipathic substituents, such as carboxyl (3h), amide (4a, 4c, 4d and 3j), and acetyl (3i), significantly hampers inhibitory potency.

Among the most potent inhibitors, 3k and 3m did not possess significant toxicity when tested against human cells, while 3q and 3r did not exhibit toxicity against HeLa cells but did show some toxicity (13–14%) against HFF-1 cells. Unfortunately, none of these compounds showed significant antibiotic activity when tested against V. cholerae (<10% inhibition). We hypothesize that these compounds may fail to cross the Gram-(−) bacterial cell wall or may suffer efflux, which are common problems when developing Gram-(−) antibiotics [24]. The inability of a compound to accumulate within bacteria is a potential cause of a lack of antibiotic efficacy.

Molecular docking

To gain insight into the molecular interactions determining the potency of the inhibitors, we carried out molecular docking analysis of compounds into the ubiquinone binding site of NQR found in subunit B, the structure of which (PDB 8A1U) has been recently reported [25]. Fig. 2 illustrates the binding mode of 3-(3-(4-fluorophenoxy)phenyl)-1, 2-dimethylquinolin-4(1H)-one (3r) within the ubiquinone site, adopting a similar orientation to ubiquinone-2. Quinolone 3r was selected as the molecule to characterize the structural associations with NQR as it is the most potent inhibitor at low concentrations, 10 μM. The diphenoxy substituent of 3r occupies the same solvent-exposed region as the isoprenoid substituents of ubiquinone-2, while the quinolone core resides deeper in the pocket. In Fig. 3 notably, the ketone and methylated amine groups of 3r align closely with the quinone core of ubiquinone-2. The geometry of the meta-substituted aryl quinolones enables them to better mimic the conformation of the isoprenoid chain present in ubiquinone-2.

PDB 8A1U was selected because it contains bound ubiquinone-2, whose additional isoprenoid unit more closely mimics the native ubiquinone-8 substrate [26]. The presence of this extended hydrophobic tail makes ubiquinone-2 a more relevant comparator for our quinolone derivatives, which also feature hydrophobic substituents that occupy a similar region of the binding pocket.

Conclusions

In summary, we report the first systematic design, synthesis, and biological evaluation of 3-substituted quinolone derivatives as inhibitors of the sodium-translocating NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase (NQR), establishing this scaffold as a novel chemotype for targeting bacterial respiration. Structure–activity relationship analyses revealed that meta-substituted 3-phenyl quinolones exhibit improved potency relative to their para-substituted counterparts, with the meta-substituted phenoxyphenyl quinolones 3q and 3r emerging as the most potent inhibitors. Molecular docking studies indicate that meta substitution enables improved geometric complementarity and more productive interactions within the NQR binding site, increasing residue contacts with the quinolone core compared to para-substituted analogs. Importantly, the most potent compounds (3k, 3m, 3q, and 3r) showed no significant toxicity toward HeLa human cells, representing a substantial advance over previously described quinolone-based inhibitors, which are often limited by cytotoxicity. Although these compounds did not exhibit significant antibacterial activity against Vibrio cholerae, we hypothesize that limited penetration through the Gram-negative bacterial cell envelope may underlie this observation, a well-recognized barrier in antibiotic development [24]. Quinolone antibiotics targeting DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV typically contain a carboxylic acid at the 3-position [27], which profoundly alters physicochemical and pharmacological properties [28], including bacterial uptake and cell envelope permeability. Collectively, these studies provide the first demonstration of selective NQR inhibition by quinolone derivatives, along with mechanistic insight into binding interactions that will guide future optimization toward compounds that combine NQR inhibition, antibacterial efficacy, and safety against human cells, ultimately enabling the development of new therapeutic strategies for the treatment of multidrug-resistant infections, including cholera.

Experimental section

General Information. All reagents and solvents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used without any further purification unless specified. All synthetic reactions were maintained under an inert atmosphere of either nitrogen or argon. All reactions were monitored by both TLC and HPLC. Analytical thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was carried out on aluminum-backed silica gel plates (200 μm) and visualized under UV light at 254 nm. Flash column chromatography was carried out for sample purification using a Teledyne ISCO CombiFlash Rf + automated chromatography system using prepacked disposable RediSep Rf flash columns (60 Å, 40 – 63 μm, 230/400 mesh) coupled to a 200–400 nm variable detector. Melting points were gathered using 90 mm capillary tubes in a MelTemp melting point apparatus and were uncorrected. Preparative HPLC was performed on all samples to determine a purity of ≥95% using an Agilent 1100 series high-performance liquid chromatography system with a reversed-phase C18 column, autosampler, and a Diode-Array detector (DAD) employing a gradient of 90% A (5% MeCN in H2O) and 10% B (MeCN with 0.1% TFA) with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were conducted on a 500 MHz Brucker spectrometer. Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) and tetramethylsilane (TMS) was used as an internal reference. High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were measured on a Time-of-Flight (TOF) instrument using electrospray ionization (ESI). High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) data were obtained at the Integrated Molecular Structure Education and Research Center (IMSERC, Northwestern University, Evanston, IL, USA). HRMS spectra were collected using a Waters Acquity I class UPLC and Xevo G2-XS QTof mass spectrometer with Waters Acquity BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm). Mobile phase A was 0.05% formic acid in water and mobile phase B was 0.05% formic acid in acetonitrile, and a gradient of 5 to 90% B in Mobile phase A over 5 min was applied. Acquired data were processed using MassHunter software version B.04.00.

General procedure D: synthesis of substituted 4-phenoxyphenyl pinacolboronic ester 7a-e

The following procedures were taken from the literature with slight modifications [20,22].

To a flask was added the substituted phenylboronic acid 5a-e (1 eq), potassium bifluoride (3.0 eq), and a 3:2 mixture of methanol and water (0.39 M based on boronic acid). The reaction mixture was allowed to stir at 70 °C under N2. After 24 h the reaction was allowed to cool down to room temperature and the solution was concentrated in vacuo providing a crude solid. The wet material was quenched with acetone followed by the addition of Na2SO4. The resulting suspension was filtered over a cotton plug, and the filtrate was collected and concentrated under reduced pressure to provide the bifluoride salt which was carried forward without further purification.

To a flask containing activated 3 Å MS was added the substituted potassium trifluoroborate (1.0 eq), 4-bromophenol (0.65 eq), Cu(OAc)2 (0.6 eq), DIPEA (3.0 eq), pyridine (3.0 eq), and anhydrous THF (0.2 M based on starting material). The reaction was equipped with a condenser and a drying tube prepacked with CaCl2. The thick blue solution was allowed to reflux with rapid stirring for 22 h. Afterwards, the dark brown solution was allowed to cool to room temperature and was vacuum filtered over a bed of celite and washed with acetone (5 mL, 2x). The filtrate was collected, concentrated under reduced pressure, and the resulting black residue was quenched with 0.5 M HCl and extracted with EtOAc (6x). The organic extracts were combined and washed sequentially with 0.5 M HCl (1x), saturated NaHCO3 (1x), water (1x), brine (2x). The organics were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated in vacuo leaving a brown oil residue. The crude oily material was purified by column chromatography using neat hexane as eluent providing a crude white solid that was carried forward without further purification.

To a flask containing of bromodiaryl ether 6a-e (1.0 eq) and activated 3 Å MS was added anhydrous DMF (0.25 M based on bromobenzene) followed by Pd (dppf)Cl2 (5 mol%), KOAc (3.0 eq), and B2Pin2 (1.1 eq). The solution was backfilled with N2 and allowed to stir at 80 °C overnight. After 17 h, the reaction mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature and was filtered over a cotton plug and washed with acetone (5 mL, 2x) and toluene (5 mL, 1x). The filtrate was collected and concentrated under reduced pressure. The resulting residue was then quenched with water and extracted with EtOAc (5x), and the organics were combined and washed with water (2x), brine (2x), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated in vacuo leaving a blackish-brown oil. The crude oil was purified through flash column chromatography using a step gradient consisting of neat hexane followed by 5:95 EtOAc: Hex with a flow rate of 12 mL/min providing a crude white solid (7a-e) that was carried forward without further purification.

NQR purification

V. cholerae NQR was purified using the methods described before by our group [23]. Briefly, cells expressing NQR were harvested and washed by centrifugation and then lysed via high-pressure homogenizer (Emulsiflex-C5, Avestin, Ottawa, ON). The NQR complex was purified by two steps of chromatographic purification: Ni-NTA affinity chromatography and DEAE ion-exchange chromatography. The composition of enzyme subunits and protein purity (>92%) were verified by 30% urea and 15% SDS-PAGE acrylamide gels analysis.

Activity measurements

NQR activity was measured by monitoring the reduction of ubiquinone at 282 nm and the oxidation of NADH at 340 nm [23,29,30] (Ref 1, 2,3), using UQ-1 and NADH as substrates. The activity measurements were performed in TEG buffer solution (50 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, 5% glycerol, and 0.05% n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside, pH of 8.0). The reactions contained 2.5 nM NQR, 250 μM NADH, 50 μM UQ-1, and quinolone inhibitors at concentrations of 10 μM or 50 μM. The ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectral data were captured using Cary 8454 UV-vis diode array system, (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The initial reaction rates were calculated to represent enzyme activity. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cytotoxicity measurements

Cytotoxicity of the compounds was measured in triplicate using HeLa cells, generously provided by Dr. Xiang (IIT, Illinois) and HFF-1 cells (ATCC SCRC-1041). HeLa cells were maintained in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM) (Corning, 10–009-CV) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and penicillin/streptomycin (100 units/mL and 100 μg/mL, respectively). HFF-1 were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, 11995–065) enriched with 15% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics as described for HeLa cells. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere and were cultured in 96-well plates, at a density of 8000 cells/well, followed by an incubation of one doubling time. After this, antibiotics were removed and the compounds were added at a concentration of 10 μM and incubated for 24-hr. Cell viability was assessed using Alamar Blue™ (Invitrogen, Cat # A50101), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were incubated with Alamar Blue at 37 °C for 4 h. Fluorescence was measured with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 570 nm and at 600 nm. Negative controls consisted of cells exposed solely to solvent (DMSO or ethanol).

Docking

Five cryo-EM structure of NQR from Vibrio cholerae have been solved by Miyoshi [12] which include different states, and complexes with AD-42 and koromicin A, and an additional six structures from V. cholerae have been recently solved by Fritz [25]. Quinolones were docked to the cryo EM structure of Vibrio cholerae NQR obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDBID: 8EVU) parametrized with the AMBER ff14SB force field [31]. The 3D structures of quinolones were generated using the SMILES translator in UCSF Chimera 1.16 [32], and subsequently minimized using AMBER ff14SB [31], hydrogenated, and parameterized using Gasteiger partial charges. Structures were docked to the ubiquinone binding site found at the interface of the B, D, and E subunits of NQR using Autodock Vina [33,34] with a volume of 30-30-30 Å.
