Iranian single-child couples’ perceptions and experiences regarding childbearing incentives
Authors: Fatemeh Seraj Shirvan, Robab Latifnejad Ruodsari, Hadi Tehrani, Hossein Ebrahimipour, Maryam Moradi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe single-child issue is a significant contributor to population decline, which adversely affects both parents and children. Despite government-approved incentives, the single-child problem remains a major population challenge. This study was conducted to explore the perceptions and experiences of single-child couples regarding childbearing incentives.MethodsThis qualitative study used conventional content analysis and was performed on 21 single-child couples in Mashhad, North East Iran, selected through purposive sampling from urban health centers. Data were collected using semi-structured interviews until saturation was reached. The data were analyzed using the Elo and Kyngas (J Adv Nurs 62(1):107-15, 2008. 10.1111/j.1365-2648.2007.04569.x) method with MaxQDA10 software.ResultsTwo generic categories emerged from the data analysis including (1) “Advertising and providing information as an influencing factor to encourage childbearing”, which included two subcategories of “Attention to the key lever of advertising” and “Necessity of adopting advertising policies and creating a culture of respecting maternal role”, and (2) “Focusing on effective incentive policies”, which consisted of three subcategories of “Adopting incentive policies to reduce people’s financial problems”, “Avoiding punitive policies” and “Need to adopt incentive policies for working women and elites”.ConclusionsIncentive policies should focus on the culture building of the general public, implementing effective incentive policies, and addressing economic challenges, particularly for specific groups such as working women and higher education students. In addition, a public mobilization effort is needed to effectively implement population laws.

Background

One contributing factor to declining fertility rates is the prevalence of single-child families, where couples, despite being able to have more children, choose not to conceive again. The average prevalence of single-child families in European countries is 47.5%. In 2021, 42.5% of families in the UK and 57% in Portugal had single-child. In 2019, 26% of the families in Canada and 21% in the United States had only one child. According to the latest census in Iran (2023), the prevalence of single-child families was 25% [1, 2].

Paying attention to the complications and threats caused by single-child families, due to its effects on the size and composition of the population, is an important challenge for policymakers in many countries, as its consequences on production and economic growth are inevitable. Also, single-child has adverse effects on the parents and children [3]. This challenge has led 66% of European governments and 40% of Asian governments to allocate a significant portion of their budgets to various forms of family support because countries with broad and comprehensive family support tend to have higher fertility rates [4, 5].

In a review conducted by Bergsvik and Fauske [6] titled “Can supportive policies prevent fertility decline?”, it was found that providing support policies for families in which both spouses are employed, the expansion of child care, and the increase of parents’ leave have a lasting effect on fertility, while the impact of financial transfers is significant and at the same time transitory. Yoon [7] also found that supporting spouses in household chores and childcare increases the probability of the birth of the second child by 2.7 times. Moreover, job uncertainty has a negative effect on men’s reproductive behavior, and sufficient financial resources are a prerequisite for transitioning to parenthood for men [8–11].

In childbearing incentive policies, it is emphasized that if appropriate programs and policies are not used, this issue may deteriorate the intrinsic motivation for childbearing [12]. Miller argues that positive motivation for fertility is associated with the desire for more children, the preferred number of children, and shorter birth spacing. It seems that different motivations derived from the norms governing the family dimension in society play a key role in identifying the number of children a couple should have [13]. According to the Bandura’s social cognitive theory, human behavior is explained as a triadic reciprocal causation. The assumptions of social cognitive theory include the belief that behavior is influenced by personal attributes, environmental influencing factors, and cognitive processes like emotions, and biological events, which form a triangular relationship of interactions. The interaction between these three dimensions leads to behavioral changes. It is assumed that couples plan their childbearing behavior according to the interaction of their environmental conditions. This theory describes the interaction gap between individual and interpersonal factors of the social environment in the decision-making process of having children, explaining human behavior through a model of triangular causality, encompassing behavior, environment, and personal factors (cognitions, emotions, and biological events) [14].

It is noteworthy to mention that trying to politically influence the norms and preferences related to reproductive choice is usually considered difficult and unacceptable in liberal democracies, which is why pronatalist policies have targeted the economic and time costs of parenting [12]. Regarding fertility incentives in the world, in Scandinavian countries, family policy is an integral part of welfare state policies, access to which is based on citizenship, not participation or merit, and without social stigma. France has the highest fertility rate among European member states, which is due to a well-established leave system for mothers and fathers, providing various types of childcare facilities, and regulation of working hours during childcare [15]. After 2000, Russia drastically increased its fertility rate through pronatalist policies. In countries where the effect of pronatalist policies is poor, a combination of successful policies should be adopted to prevent population aging. These strategies should specifically focus on increasing fertility without reducing opportunities for employment for women and interfering with their reproductive rights because it is clear that direct interventions such as banning abortion or restricting the use of contraceptive methods, as seen in Japan, are not always effective in increasing fertility [16]. In Sweden, childbearing incentive policies are not direct, but the aim is to support women's participation in the workforce, promoting gender equality, and focusing on women’s ability to continue working and family life without depending on the others, and a system of paid leave and child care allowance exists to encourage women for the work market [17]. Most policies are aimed at reducing the factors affecting the conflict between work and motherhood, such as maternity leave and child care, but continuous research will identify the influence of each of these policies in Iran [18].

In the cultural context of Iran, given that many years of widespread advertising have promoted family planning and pregnancy prevention, the “fewer children, better life” model was widely embraced. Furthermore, cultural factors including social modeling, i.e. being influenced by others and the media, the value of children, women's education and employment, and self-preference affected childbearing. However, much like in China, Iran now faces challenges related to population decline. In response, the government has shifted its focus, implementing new fertility policies including the “Population Youth Law”, aimed in increasing population growth [19]. In the study conducted by Taheri et al. [20] in Iran, it was reported that countries should look for an approach appropriate to its organizational structure and based on the reasons for its population decline, and instead of focusing on pronatalism, they should focus on supporting the family, employment, human capital and child development costs. Similarly, Shavazi [21] in his study also stated that to support fertility incentive policies, in the current situation, the focus should be on improving the economic conditions of young people so that less pressure is imposed on them to form a family or have children. Therefore, each country should choose a unique model or a combination of different models in the field of family policy according to its internal structure and intellectual, cultural, social, and economic characteristics; the most important models include the patterns supporting birth, non-intervention, tradition and equality [22]. Considering this point and the difference between Iran and other countries, especially in terms of belief systems influencing society, it is necessary to identify the goals and priorities according to the internal values of the system, which are also mentioned in the major policies communicated by the leadership, and then try to fulfill them using appropriate political tools [23].

There is also a notable gap in fertility incentive policies as well as specific and documented plans as a basis for counseling and educating single-child couples to increase their intention to have more children. In general, there are limited studies related to the single-child couples in Iran and these studies have usually been carried out with quantitative approaches [24–27]. Therefore, performing a qualitative study through conducting in-depth interviews with single-child couples to understand their perceptions regarding childbearing incentives based on their real needs is an inevitable necessity and a key factor for the success of pronatalist programs in any society. Since childbearing is an issue that is influenced by the complex social and environmental factors and fertility motivation is a complex problem with cultural, social, behavioral and religious roots. For this reason, a qualitative method in this study was used to deeply understand this issue and to explain the perceptions and experiences of single-child couples regarding childbearing incentives. Since we aimed to provide a broad and comprehensive description of the single-child problem and incentives for childbearing, the content analysis approach was used [28]. Therefore, the present study was conducted to understand the perceptions and experiences of Iranian single-child couples regarding child-bearing incentives.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study was conducted using conventional content analysis approach. In this methodology, the analysis is done using the inductive approach. In conventional content analysis, the researcher allows categories and subcategories to emerge from the text data so that follows the tenets of the naturalistic paradigm. Therefore, the movement is done from part to the whole, which is in accordance with the purpose of the present study to explain the perceptions and experiences of single-child couples regarding childbearing incentives as a motivation driver with an inductive approach [28, 29].

Setting

The study setting was the health centers of Mashhad, a religious city in northeastern Iran, The reason for choosing Mashhad as the study location was its traditional culture and religious context and it is expected that adherence to religious values is expected to influence fertility decisions. Although some residents of this city are interested in modern family patterns, however, the findings can still be relevant and applicable to religious communities adhering to family values. To access the electronic records of single-child couples in the health centers, the researcher visited the health centers for participants’ recruitment.

Participants

Participants were fertile couples with a single-child aged at least 2 years, 11 months and 29 days, who had not decided to have another child. Other inclusion criteria were having only one spouse, no diagnosis of infertility as well as no incurable physical or mental illnesses of the couple. Efforts were made to interview the married husbands and wives who were legally married couple. Couples who planned to have more children in the future were excluded from the study.

Sampling

The sampling method was purposive with a strategy of maximum variation in terms of age, education, occupation, and income level among couples living in Mashhad. Candidates who met the inclusion criteria were asked to complete an informed consent form. Couples were approached for participation through a systematic sampling process using electronic records of single-child families, minimizing the potential for selection bias. This ensured participants were selected without prior knowledge or random bias.

Data collection

After obtaining the approval from the ethics committee and other necessary permissions, the researcher visited the selected health centers. After introducing herself and explaining the research objectives to the Department of Maternal and Child Health Care, she started sampling. At this stage, eligible couples were asked to complete an informed consent form, and then the interviews were conducted using semi-structured interviews in a quiet room and the participants’ voice recorded after obtaining their consent. Interviews were conducted separately, with each partner. The interviewer was a doctoral student in Reproductive Health with the necessary knowledge, skills and experience in conducting qualitative interviews.

The interview process began with an opening question: "Please tell me about any experience that you have regarding childbearing incentives?”. This was followed with the main questions related to the childbearing incentives such as “Could you please tell me how the use of incentive and punitive policies could affect your intention to have a second child”, “How information and advertisements can influence your choice of having a single child” and “What conditions would affect your intention to have a second child”. Then probing questions such as “Can you explain more” or “Did you mean that…” were used to gain deeper insight. The final question of the interview was as follows: is there any point or issue that I did not ask you and you think it is necessary to say?

The participants were informed about the possibility of needing a supplementary interview in the future to fill the gaps in the data. Body language and non-verbal behaviors were recorded immediately after each interview. The average length of interviews was 45 min. Interviews continued until data saturation was reached, with a total of 21 participants interviewed.

Data analysis

Data analysis began simultaneously with data collection. MAXQDA 10 software was used to manage and organize the data. Elo and Kyngäs [29] approach to analysis was followed, which includes three stages: preparation, organization, and reporting. The preparation stage involved selecting the unit of analysis and ensuring the logical link of data with the research objectives. In the organizing stage, an unstructured matrix was used to enable the formation of new categories, and in the reporting stage, the categories emerged from the data analysis were presented [29].

Non-verbal data were included in the analysis. For instance, if a person’s grin indicated dissatisfaction with the amount allocated to incentive policies, this was considered during coding. Similarly, visible discomfort or regret expressed about having a single child due to economic problems or housing issues was also factored into the coding process.

Trustworthiness of data

To increase the trustworthiness of the data, the proposed four general criteria of Lincoln et al. [30] approach to trustworthiness including credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability-was used. Credibility was ensured through prolonged engagement in the field, member check, and peer debriefing. Dependability was established through an audit trail by an external observer. Confirmability was achieved by providing the written text to a number of external supervisors and researchers familiar with qualitative data analysis. Transferability was maintained by providing a detailed explanation of the participants’ demographic characteristics, experiences and the research environment, allowing readers to evaluate the possibility of applying the data in similar contexts [30].

Ethical considerations

Ethical considerations included obtaining permission from the Local Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, completing written informed consent forms, explaining the research objectives to the participants before conducting interviews, recording interviews with participants’ consent, and ensuring participants' privacy and confidentiality throughout the data collection process.

Results

The participants in this study were 12 females and 9 males, aged 30 to 48 years, each having a single-child including 9 girls and 12 boys, aged 5 to 14 years. The participants’ education backgrounds ranged from diploma level to specialized doctorate, with an effort made to include a diverse range of socio-economic levels. The participants’ occupations varied widely, including fruit sellers, motor couriers, doctors, and university professors. The average age of women was 37.57 years, while the average age of men was 41.09 years. The average age of male children was 9.45 years and the average age of female children was 9.18 years.

General categories and subcategories of motivational stimuli

Generic category of “advertising and providing information as an influencing factor to encourage childbearing”

According to the couples’ opinions, in the past, extensive advertising and mobilization by organizations and even childbearing laws were key success factors in the implementation of family planning policies to reduce the fertility rate. Even now, widespread advertising should be used to inform about the critical situation of population decline. The generic category of “Advertising and providing information as an influencing factor to encourage childbearing” consisted of two subcategories: “Attention to the key lever of advertising” and “Necessity of adopting advertising policies and creating a culture of respecting maternal role”.

Attention to the key lever of advertising

Data analysis revealed two different perspectives on the most effective motivating factors for couples regarding childbearing. On one hand, “Advertising as the most effective way to motivate couples” was emphasized highlighting the importance of demonstrating the adverse consequences of having single-child in public streets, media, and public as well as private centers. On the other hand, the “inefficiency of advertising to encourage childbearing" was raised as a problem. This shows the neglect of this problem and the inadequate explanation of its consequences for families in the society.

Participants also indicated that the best means for advertising is through national media, which is accessible to all levels of society. They also emphasized that the importance of prioritizing healthcare centers should be considered in advertising because these centers were exactly the places where birth control programs initially began. In addition, the introduction of models of successful mothers with several children in different strata of society was mentioned as an effective issue in stimulating the motivation of couples. For example, one of the couples commented on the role of advertising in the media:“In my opinion, the thing that affects our people the most is advertising. It has a significant impact on people through the media. This means that TV and other media should show relevant advertisements so that many people will watch them.” (42-year-old woman, bachelor's degree, hospital employee).

“In my opinion, the thing that affects our people the most is advertising. It has a significant impact on people through the media. This means that TV and other media should show relevant advertisements so that many people will watch them.” (42-year-old woman, bachelor's degree, hospital employee).

Regarding the “Inefficiency of advertising to encourage childbearing”, the participants raised problems such as the lack of public awareness about measures to encourage fertility, the incongruence between words and actions in childbearing advertisements, and the ineffectiveness of advertisements that do not affect livelihoods and well-being. In line with the participants’ views, the notion of the non-profitability of having children (in terms of cost–benefit) causes the conscious and planned choice of single-child. According to the couples, while people are aware of the benefits of having children, no one will choose to have more until economic and employment issues are solved.

Economic uncertainty and increasing childbearing costs are among the determinants of delay in having children. In fact, the decision-making balance for having children consists of two components, including the perceived advantages and disadvantages of having only one child.

Balanced decision-making is based on the assumption that people weigh the advantages against the disadvantages when deciding to have children. If the heavy bottom of the scale is towards its advantages, most likely, they will remain a single child. Still, if the heavy end of the scale is towards the perceived disadvantages of having a single child, the probability of having a single child decreases. One of the couples about the uselessness and slogan-like nature of advertisements stated:“Advertising was not effective at all. People would like the problems of unemployment, housing, and high costs to be addressed. Nothing happens with just slogans and information. I think everyone knows that having children is good, but there must be something wrong with the situation if, due to economic problems, they aren’t getting married or having children.” (Male, 43 years old, bachelor’s degree, self-employed).

I think everyone knows that having children is good, but there must be something wrong with the situation if, due to economic problems, they aren’t getting married or having children.” (Male, 43 years old, bachelor’s degree, self-employed).

Necessity of adopting advertising policies and creating a culture of respecting maternal role

From the participants’ point of view, advertisements in the field of accepting the maternal role should be given special attention. The issue of honoring the role of motherhood against the norm of choosing to have few children in society is a key point. From the couples’ point of view, women’s social roles have become competitors with maternal roles. This subcategory consists of three key aspects including “making planned and effective advertisements in target groups based on honoring gender roles”, “correcting the attitude and culture related to the conflict between employment/education and maternal role”, and “correcting the attitude and culture of the conflict between health/beauty and maternal role”.

Regarding “making planned and effective advertisements in target groups based on honoring gender roles”, the issues such as creating a culture to honor the maternal role from childhood in adolescent girls and boys and creating a culture of adherence to family values were raised by the participants. Women with a higher socio-economic base had a higher level of education and higher income, and when faced with the prospect of long-term effects of childbearing on employment and lifetime income, suffered most and those women with a higher level of suffering had the strongest motivation to delay fertility. For instance, one of the couples said:“Most people now have a problem with the cost of living, so they are not willing to have children. Another issue is the cultural belief that the more civilized people, the fewer children they have. This idea has been ingrained in both the lower and upper classes, and for years, it has been promoted that fewer children lead to a better life and that civilized people have fewer children. This belief has become established among Iranians, and it must be changed.” (43-year-old woman, bachelor's degree, hospital employee).

“Most people now have a problem with the cost of living, so they are not willing to have children. Another issue is the cultural belief that the more civilized people, the fewer children they have. This idea has been ingrained in both the lower and upper classes, and for years, it has been promoted that fewer children lead to a better life and that civilized people have fewer children. This belief has become established among Iranians, and it must be changed.” (43-year-old woman, bachelor's degree, hospital employee).

Regarding “correcting the attitude and culture of the conflict between employment/education and maternal role”, problems such as correcting the culture of women’s employment as the only way for women to progress, teaching to value childbearing and the educational roles of women in the production and education of the young generation in schools, the need for research in the field of creating advertising content to correct the childbearing attitude was expressed by the participants. One of the couples in this regard said:“Even teenagers in high school should participate in motivational classes on motherhood and childbearing, and be encouraged to marry at the right time and to have children early. Additionally, the mothers of students should be informed that delaying childbearing due to girls’ education or employment might has adverse consequences and that necessary education should be provided.” (39-year-old woman, master’s degree, student).

Fitness is important too; right now, my belly is big, and I don’t like it.” (35-year-old woman, bachelor’s degree, hospital employee).

Generic category of “Focusing on the effective incentive policies”

What couples understand about fertility incentives are the significant shortcomings of these policies. This general category consisted of three key phrases, including: “Adopting incentive policies to reduce people’s financial problems”, “Avoiding punitive policies” and “Need to adopt incentive policies for working women and elites”, which are presented in more detail.

Adopting incentive policy to reduce people’s financial problems

“Adopting incentive policy to reduce people’s financial problems” was expressed through the ideas of “Using economic incentive policies” and “Guaranteeing the implementation of financial incentives”.

Regarding the “Use of economic incentive policies”, the issues raised by couples indicated the need to solve housing problems, receive childcare allowances, find a solution for economic problems, and feel economic security. Participants believed that having a child is undoubtedly a necessity for parents and they do not imagine the possibility of being childless. But when it comes to whether to have or not to have a second child, the assessment of the economic resources of children plays an important role. In other words, despite all the limitations and difficult economic conditions, parents do not leave themselves without children, but the importance of economic conditions forces them to limit the number of children. One of the couples in this relationship said:“You know, the government might provide a car but then the issue of repaying the loan and its instalments is a problem. You might end up paying these installments for a lifetime. Setting aside the issue of cars, what will you do about these problems? How will the child’s needs be met? How will university expenses be addressed? For instance, how will the girl prepare her dowry when she gets married?” (41-year-old man, high school diploma, motorcycle courier).

For example, one of the couples said:“Policy should not just involve making statements but also it should guarantee their implementation. Our country is not poor; it is rich in resources, with a young workforce and abundant good resources. Compared to other countries that are much poorer, we have regressed instead of progressing.” (31-year-old woman, employee, PhD student in reproductive health).

Avoidance of punitive policies

Regarding “Avoidance of punitive policies”, the couple believed that mandatory policies should be avoided because they may have the opposite effect. In this regard, two points was expressed by the participants: “Avoidance of anti-incentive measures” and “Reverse effect of punitive policies”.

The point of “Avoidance of anti-incentive measures” referred to the challenges of “high paying out of people’s pockets” regarding fertility services, avoiding exaggerated advertisements, and maintaining the respect and human dignity of couples in making decisions without coercion. One of the couples in this regard said:“Policies should not be prescriptive; the intelligence of the audience should be respected. The reasons must be explained, including the possible consequences of population decline and the implications of having a single child. However, this should not be done exaggeratedly. The truth should be conveyed, as sometimes exaggeration can make something completely untruthful.” (40-year-old woman, university professor).

The reasons must be explained, including the possible consequences of population decline and the implications of having a single child. However, this should not be done exaggeratedly. The truth should be conveyed, as sometimes exaggeration can make something completely untruthful.” (40-year-old woman, university professor).

The point of “Reverse effect of punitive policies” highlighted challenges such as “mocking the mandatory advertisements and coercion” and “avoiding punitive and prohibitive policies”. In this relation, one of the couples said:“Encouragement might be effective, but punishment definitely will not work (with a smile). Of course, no one can be forced to have children.” (47-year-old man, bachelor's degree, school principal).

The need to adopt incentive policies for working women and elites

According to the belief of working and elite women, attention should be paid to all strata in setting incentives and policies. This issue consists of two tips including "Government policies for maternity and breastfeeding leave" and "Policies aimed to attract young and educated people and reduce migration abroad".

Regarding “Government policy for maternity and breastfeeding leave”, the data shows the need to grant maternity leave for 2 years, using the successful experiences of other countries in the field of maternity leave and child care, and being agree with the transfer of working mothers to their native city to live next to grandma to help in taking care of the children. Women with one child have more full-time and part-time jobs because balancing work and having children is difficult, anyway. Many single-child couples want another child, but they do not want to lose their job [25].

For example, one of the couples said:“In society, we all working women should be supported, especially those who are getting older. We have friends in similar situations who, due to work-related concerns, are not thinking about having children yet. If they pass their childbearing years, it will be a regret for both society and themselves. Each person's circumstances should be considered, and ways to help them should be found. Many employees are in cities far from home; for example, if there is a 5-year commitment, it should be reduced to 3 years, and transfers should be facilitated.” (44-year-old man, master’s degree, employee).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to explore single-child couples’ perceptions and experiences about childbearing incentives. Two generic categories emerged from the data analysis including (1) Advertising and providing information as an influencing factor to encourage childbearing, which included two subcategories: attention to the key lever of advertising and Necessity of adopting advertising policies and creating a culture of respecting the maternal role, and (2) Focusing on effective incentive policies, which included three subcategories: adopting incentive policies to reduce people’s financial problems, Avoiding punitive policies and Need to adopt incentive policies for working women and elites.

Regarding the generic category of “Advertising and providing information as an influencing factor to encourage childbearing”, it should be said that as family planning has become a culture in families through extensive advertising and information provision in all strata of society, now it is required to overcome the crisis of population reduction, a mass mobilization should take place in all strata of society and national media and virtual networks. Regarding paying attention to the key lever of advertising, it should be said that advertising as the most effective way to motivate couples, and the inefficiency of advertising to encourage childbearing are both salient issues for the ineffectiveness of fertility promotion policies. In line with the findings of the present study, in the qualitative research of Balakhani [31], the attitude of people towards population policies is cognitively poor, that is, due to insufficient information, people do not have the necessary awareness and knowledge about fertility promotion measures and laws in the society.

Ineffectiveness of advertising to encourage childbearing due to the insufficient efforts of the media has not yet been able to promote childbearing because there is not enough information, and also in terms of the content, the advertisement is superficial and cannot be understood by the audiences. In line with the findings of this research, Hsiao and Sun [32] stated that illiterate people due to the lack of access to media advertisements do not have sufficient knowledge of the financial incentives of the Singapore government. Different issues like the long distance between words and actions in childbearing advertisements, advertisements without executive support like slogans, and the lack of advertisements’ impact on people’s livelihood and well-being were demonstrated as the causes of the ineffectiveness of advertisements and the conscious and planned choice of one child, which is in accordance with the results of the study by Ghahfarokhi [33].

The participants in this research expressed insufficient public awareness of the actions and policies carried out by the government to encourage childbearing in advertisements. Furthermore, ambiguity in incentive policies was one of the factors of lack of trust in policies and incentives, as the nature of policies and how to benefit from them is not clear. Also, the temporary nature of incentive policies was another factor that led to the ineffectiveness of the policies. The concern of changing these policies with the change of government and also the experience of failure in benefiting from the incentives in the past years have made people unable to believe and trust in the new incentives and policies and their implementation [27]. The participants in this study considered the incentive policies to be purely economic and therefore believed that these policies only affect the less privileged classes and create incentives for them to have children. For example, actions such as child care and parental leave cannot be neutral depending on the socio-economic background. The use of formal child care among families that are at the lowest end of the income distribution scale is much less and usually women with higher education are those who use mostly parental leave [33]. According to the respondents of this study, the existing policies are focused on the size of the population and are not effective, efficient and quality-oriented, because the children’s quality of life is not given importance in the policies and only the number of childrenis considered [6].

Another key findings in this study was the need to adopt advertising policies and promoteing the culture of honoring maternal role, which aligns with the findings on the total fertility rate in Hong Kong (2017), highlighting the declining ideal of the two-child family. In fact, the long-term effect of the single-child policy was effective on the fertility intentions of Chinese youth [34]. Khaled [35] in his research focusing on the cultural changes and fertility decline in Iran, reported that the higher the level of religiosity in the cities, the lower the levels of secularism and individualism, and the higher the level of attention to family values, and the higher fertility rate. In this regard, it has been reported that having a negative experience of birth and transition to parenthood can hurt women’s fertility intention, but if fertility and pronatalist culture are prioritized in the cultural context of society, social pressures may reduce the effects of birth experiences on fertility intention [36].

In our study, despite the cultural background and religious context of Mashhad city, there was no observed effect of this background on the findings of the study, particularly regarding the cultural attitudes towards childbearing. This is likely due to the globalization of the public culture under the influence of media and virtual space, as well as economic problems. This finding is in line with the component of the environment's influence on couples’ behavior in Bandura’s social cognitive theory. Behmanesh et al. [19], similarly, found that the issue of single-child in the city of Babol in North Iran is influenced by the media and social patterns as well as self-advancement.

Some researchers, as proponents of the second demographic transition theory, emphasize the role of attitudinal and cultural changes in shaping individual ideals about the family and explaining demographic shifts. Therefore, the spread of individualism and shifts in the family and social attitudes and norms, especially among women, are among the factors that can influence intentions to have children, the spacing between births, and the number of children women choos to have [37]. It is obvious that Iranian society has undergone structural and value changes due to social modernization; which has also transformed to the city of Mashhad.; But unlike Western post-industrial societies that attribute fertility decline to individualism, the Iranian family is still the center of having children as an inevitable value and there is no legitimate alternative; therefore, people’s attitude towards having children should be understood in the context of the family institution [38].

Respecting the issue of culturalization, the implementation of planned and effective advertisements in targeted and specific groups, based on honoring gender roles, is a requirement. This is in line with the findings of Razeghi et al. [39], which found that gender-egalitarian attitudes, individualism, and understanding of social norms are in the favor of having only one child. Correcting the attitude and culture surrounding the conflict between employment/education and the role of motherhood was another important aspect of culturalization, which is consistent with the findings of Bahmanesh et al. [19]. In the studies of Moshfeq and Gharib [40] and Mubasheri et al. [41], factors influencing the decision to have only one child were noted as the conflict between having children and continuing education or the mother’s job. Another idea that needs attention in the cultural context of society is correcting the attitude and culture surrounding the conflict between health/beauty and the role of motherhood. In the studies by Bahmanesh et al. [19] and Newman et al. [42], this conflict was reported as a reason for not having children, due to a lack of interest in sharing one’s body with a child. Also, Mahmoudian and Rezaei [43] considered having fewer children beneficial for parents’ comfort, viewing a large number of children as an obstacle to their mental peace and physical comfort.

The second main category resulting from the data analysis in the current research was focusing on the effective incentive policies. In line with the findings of the current study, Chen et al. [44] states that incorrect targeting and lack of identification of target groups in changing the total fertility rate, cross-sectional measures, and low level of financial support may partly account for the ineffectiveness of incentives in many high-income Asian societies. Concerning the need to adopt incentive policies for working women and elites, in line with the findings of the current research, Samani [45] reported that the best way to prepare the society for increasing the fertility rate is to create a culture using the national facilities so that a deep and basic logical and convincing thought can be created for people in the country. For this purpose, the elites of the society must accept and be convinced of the need to increase the fertility rate; because if the elites of the society accept it, the process of creating a culture shift will be easier. Scholars and people with knowledge related to the issue of population in each sector can provide correct and acceptable logic in this field [45]. Granting facilities to prevent youth migration and paying attention to the needs of the elites were among the demands of the participants. In addition to the culture, legislation is also a factor in increasing the fertility rate. In line with the findings of the current study, Samani et al. [45], found that there is a significant positive relationship between legal protection and the desire of working women to be fertile.

The findings of the present research showed that incentive policies should be adopted to reduce people's financial problems and economic incentive policies should be used, as it has implementation guarantees. In other words, the use of economic incentive policies including solving the housing problem of couples, making policies to receive child care allowances for couples, providing incentive policies to solve economic problems, and a sense of economic security could strengthen the couples’ motivation for childbearing [27]. In accordance with the findings of the present study, based on the findings of a survey on 400 married women aged 15 to 49 in Tehran, Iran it was found that if married women face any socio-economic restrictions, they are likely to restrain their reproductive desires towards having fewer children [46]. Similarly, qualitative research on women of childbearing age in Singapore, showed that the increase in living expenses after childbearing and household income is considered as an important capacity for childbearing [32].

Also, the analysis of industrialized societies showed that the value of fertility is more pronounced for white men at first and second births. Women’s income usually has a negative effect on fertility, while the net asset is most likely positively related to fertility [47]. Moreover, the most effective family policy in Japan was the monthly allowance for children under 15 years old [48]. In addition, an analysis of the implementation of the second-child policy in different provinces of China shows that the factors that hinder the implementation of the second-child policy are personal (late marriage, job advancement, increased costs, cultural aspects, and physical factors) as well as social factors (resources of public services such as education and health care) [11]. Regarding the effect of economic factors on the fertility rate in Iran, there was a positive correlation between the wages of working women and men and the fertility rate (47.8% of the fertility rate) [46]. Balakhani [31] regarding the link between childbearing and the economic problems of families, showed a kind of dissatisfaction with the economic and social situation prevailing in the society. However, in the present study, those who had mentioned economic problems as a reason for not having more children were found to have lower incomes and rented housing. This highlights the significant role of economic issues in the decision to have only one child. On the other hand, people who were in a better socioeconomic class had chosen to have only one child due to the women’s employment and lack of access to a suitable childcare providers. Also, Taheri et al. [20] believed that in an era when the economic crisis has caused families to suffer financial problems, the economic assistance of the government plays a very important role in increasing the birth rate. According to the findings of a systematic review by Seraj et al. [49] regarding the childbearing needs of single-child couples, addressing financial concerns is one of the fundamental issues.

The findings of the present study indicated avoidance of punitive policies and anti-motivational measures, because punitive policies may have the opposite effect. According to the findings of Botev [12], facilitative pronatalist policies increase the internal motivation for childbearing, mainly by increasing people’s competency for childbearing and raising children and strengthening their sense of independence. However, interventions aimed to force a behavior reduce people’s independence and lead to a decrease in internal motivation for childbearing [12]. In line with the findings of the present study, Onder-Erol in a study in Turkey titled “Population policy as a tool for bio politics”, found that the matter of when and how many children people want to have is completely private and reproduction is a basic human right [50]. Another study showed that counseling has a positive effect on students and could improve their motivation for childbearing [51].

Regarding the necessity of adopting incentive policies for working women and elites, according to the census data from 2011 in the Czech Republic, there was a relationship between single-child and professional jobs as well as the influence of societal values and conditions [52]. Although young men and women in Russia consider forming a family and childbearing as a significant value, they postpone it due to prioritizing job and professional achievements [53]. Men and women have different perceptions and experiences in dealing with family-work conflicts. Due to the deep-rooted issue of women’s caring role, women are usually forced to sacrifice more for the family [54]. For example, very low birth rates in Southern and Eastern Europe and East Asia are associated with strong norms against maternal employment and heavy maternal responsibilities. On the contrary, in Europe, the reduction of work-family conflict is associated with increased access to childcare with a higher fertility rate [36, 55].

According to the registered data of Norway, the availability of kindergarten has positive effects on the transition to motherhood [56]. Also, the results of research in China showed that kindergartens improve women’s desire to be fertile [57]. Brainerd [58] reported that pronatalist policies increase the number of second and third births. Also, the findings of qualitative research by Jahangiri and Ahmadi [59] showed that lack of sufficient support for women and families and not having enough time due to being employed or continuing education are among the challenges facing childbearing. Khalajabadi and Saraei [60] reported that interventions in single-child couples should include improving women’s job security, improving the quality of childcare centers, culturalizing the norm of childbearing, increasing the sense of social and economic security of families, and strengthening relationships in the family. The results of a systematic review by Bergsvik and Fauske [6] showed that the expansion of childcare increases the total fertility rate, while the increase in cash transfers has temporary effects. High-income couples more benefit from increased parental leave, while the expansion of childcare programs can reduce social inequalities in other areas [6]. The result of research in China among female employees of the public sector showed that the high costs of raising children and the status of job promotion are the main reasons for the unwillingness of 62.5% of women to have a second child [57].

One of the strengths of the present study was an evaluation of both couples as a unit. However, one of the limitations of the current research was the lack of cooperation of men due to busy work, however, it was tried as much as possible that legally married couples living together were interviewed, but in case of lack of access, other eligible men and women were interviewed. Another potential weakness of the study design that may affect the interpretation of the results, was adopting a qualitative approach, which is associated with limited generalizability, although it was tried to adjust it as much as possible by applying a maximum variation strategy during sampling. Regarding potential cultural factors that may limit the generalization of our findings, it should be noted that the study was conducted in a city with a predominantly Muslim religious context. Therefore, the findings could be generalizable to similar communities, but not all societies.

Conclusion

The practical implications of the findings are highly valuable for policy-making and designing executive programs based on the real views of couples. Effective advertising is the most effective way to motivate couples. Therefore, it is essential to create targeted advertising that respects gender roles to effectively engage the target groups. In addition, correcting the attitude and culture towards conflict of employment/education with maternal role as well as conflict between health/beauty and maternal role, and also government policies to improve maternity and breastfeeding leave are the key factors. Policies should also focus on attracting young and educated people, reducing migration abroad, and using economic incentive strategies. Guaranteeing the implementation of financial incentives can effectively build couples’ confidence by bridging the gap between words and actions.

It is suggested that in future studies the effect of all types of incentives specifically for single-child couples be investigated. Additionally, comparing the impact of different incentives between urban and rural communities would be valuable. Further research should also examine the effects of incentives across various socioeconomic classes and among different occupations, including working women and housewives.
