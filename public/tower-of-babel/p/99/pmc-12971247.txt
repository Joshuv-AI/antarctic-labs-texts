Amivantamab Monotherapy for Lung Adenocarcinoma With Leptomeningeal Seeding: A Case Report

Introduction

Non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is characterised by a number of driver mutations, among which EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations are associated with poor prognosis due to structural alterations that confer resistance to conventional tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [1, 2, 3]. Recently, amivantamab, a fully human bispecific antibody targeting EGFR and MET, has demonstrated promising efficacy in patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations. However, its therapeutic activity against central nervous system (CNS) metastases remains uncertain [4]. Here, we report a case of a patient with NSCLC who developed leptomeningeal seeding (LMS) after the failure of first‐line therapy and showed a marked clinical and radiologic response to amivantamab monotherapy.

Case Report

A 65‐year‐old woman, reporting a persistent cough, presented to the pulmonology outpatient clinic at Samsung Medical Center. She had no significant medical or family history and had never smoked.

Initial laboratory findings showed a normal complete blood count (WBC 6.82 × 109/L; Haemoglobin 13.7 g/dL; Platelets 258 × 109/L). Except for a mildly elevated serum creatinine of 0.94 mg/dL, no other abnormal findings were noted.

Chest CT revealed a large part‐solid mass measuring 84 mm in the right lower lobe (RLL). Additional findings included lymphangitic metastasis in the RLL and fissural nodules along the right major fissure, suggestive of pleural seeding (Figure 1). Initial brain MRI showed no remarkable abnormalities. 18F‐FDG Positron Emission Tomography (PET)‐CT demonstrated increased uptake in the aforementioned lesions and bilateral mediastinal lymph nodes.

Sequential chest CTs showing radiologic response throughout treatment course. (a) Baseline chest CT showing a primary tumour in the right lower lobe (RLL) with lymphangitic metastasis (b) Chest CT obtained after the fourth treatment cycle of pembrolizumab plus pemetrexed/carboplatin showing a mildly decreased in the extent of the RLL primary tumour with lymphangitic metastasis (c) Chest CT obtained at the time leptomeningeal seeding was identified, showing no interval change in the extent of the tumour burden (d) Chest CT obtained after the third treatment cycle of amivantamab monotherapy showing a further decrease in extent of the primary tumour and lymphangitic metastasis.

Endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial lung biopsy (EBUS‐TBLB) of the RLL mass confirmed adenocarcinoma. Molecular analysis showed negative results for ALK, ROS1, and c‐erB‐2. PD‐L1 (22C3) expression level was 1%, and c‐MET staining was 2+. Both droplet digital EGFR (ddEGFR) and cell‐free EGFR (cfEGFR) assays were negative; however, next‐generation sequencing (NGS) using lung tissue identified an EGFR exon 20 insertion mutation (p.A763_Y764insFQEA) (Table 1). The final clinical stage at diagnosis was T4N3M1a adenocarcinoma, and palliative pembrolizumab plus pemetrexed/carboplatin therapy was initiated on January 2, 2025.

On April 1, 2025, response evaluation according to RECIST version 1.1 demonstrated stable disease, and the same regimen was continued (Figure 1). On May 2, 2025, the patient presented with a severe headache disturbing sleep, accompanied by nausea and vomiting. Brain MRI revealed multifocal linear enhancing lesions suggestive of LMS (Figure 2). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed malignant cells identified on cytology, confirming LMS and disease progression.

(b,d,h) Brain MRI after 1 month of amivantamab monotherapy demonstrating a marked decrease in the extent of diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement compared with baseline. (c,f,i) Brain MRI after 3 cycles of amivantamab monotherapy demonstrating further improvement of residual leptomeningeal enhancement. (b,c) Artefacts related to the Ommaya reservoir are observed in the right parietal lobe.

On May 5, 2025, an Ommaya reservoir was inserted, and intrathecal methotrexate (IT‐MTX) therapy was initiated. Second‐line treatment with amivantamab was started on May 21, 2025. During the first week, amivantamab was initially administered over 2 days, followed by weekly infusions of 1050 mg for 4 weeks.

Follow‐up brain MRI on June 17, 2025 demonstrated a marked reduction in diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement (Figure 2), and chest and abdominopelvic CT revealed a decreased overall tumour burden (Figure 1). Therefore, amivantamab monotherapy was continued every 2 weeks. Brain MRI after the third treatment cycle demonstrated further regression of LMS (Figure 2). The patient remains on amivantamab therapy to date.

Discussion

EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations are known to account for approximately 4%–10% of all EGFR mutations in NSCLC. Due to the modified structure of the kinase domain, tumours harbouring these mutations are resistant to first‐ and second‐generation EGFR‐TKIs [1]. In the second‐line setting, real‐world data have demonstrated a median progression‐free survival (PFS) of only 3.5 months and an overall response rate (ORR) of 13% [2], with a reported median overall survival (OS) of 13 months [3]. Given this poor prognosis, the treatment of NSCLC with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations has long remained an unmet clinical need.

Amivantamab, a fully human bispecific antibody targeting EGFR and MET, demonstrated promising efficacy in the phase I CHRYSALIS study, showing an ORR of 40% and a median duration of response (DOR) of 11 months in patients with NSCLC harbouring EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations [4]. Based on these results, amivantamab was approved in both the United States and South Korea for patients who experienced disease progression following platinum‐based chemotherapy. However, monoclonal antibody–based therapies are generally considered to have limited penetration across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [5]. Furthermore, the CHRYSALIS study excluded patients with brain metastases, leaving uncertainty regarding the efficacy of amivantamab in this population.

In the present case, LMS was confirmed by brain MRI and CSF cytology. Amivantamab resulted in a marked reduction of LMS on follow‐up brain MRI. Recently, another case report of durable complete response in LMS to amivantamab was published [6]. Contrary to the general expectation, these findings suggest that amivantamab may have considerable therapeutic efficacy even in patients with CNS metastases, which represents the significance of this case report.

The mechanism by which amivantamab, a monoclonal antibody generally considered difficult to cross the BBB, exerts activity against brain metastases remains unclear. The neurovascular unit (NVU), composed of endothelial cells and surrounding neuroparenchymal cells, forms the BBB, which strictly regulates transport of substances. As brain tumour progresses, the NVU acquires aberrant properties and becomes increasingly heterogeneous, which is called the blood‐tumour barrier (BTB) [7]. Because the BTB differs from the BBB in integrity, permeability, and structural characteristics, amivantamab may gain access to intracranial malignant cells [7]. However, whether the intratumoral concentration of amivantamab differs from that in the normal brain parenchyma remains uncertain, and further studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanism.

The EGFR exon 20 insertion variant p.A763_Y764insFQEA is biologically distinct from most exon 20 insertions and has been reported to be sensitive to EGFR TKIs [8]. Therefore, part of the observed response may reflect the intrinsic EGFR dependency of this mutation rather than a drug‐specific effect. Nevertheless, improvement of leptomeningeal disease was observed during amivantamab treatment. This finding should be interpreted cautiously and supports further evaluation of amivantamab in patients with CNS involvement.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that amivantamab may have therapeutic potential in patients with NSCLC and active LMS. Prospective, large‐scale clinical studies are needed to further validate the efficacy of amivantamab monotherapy in this setting.
