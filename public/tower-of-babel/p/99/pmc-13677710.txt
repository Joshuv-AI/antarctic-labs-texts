Recyclable glass fiber-reinforced epoxy copper clad laminates for printed circuit board
Authors: Yueqi Mo, Wei Lin, Yanping Zhou, Bing Qin, Xin Li, Guoxin Du, Jinrong Ye, Zhongqiang Yang, Bo Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpoxy-based copper-clad laminates (CCLs) comprise >60% of the rigid CCL market, yet such CCLs are rarely recyclable at present. This paper describes the first industrialization of recyclable epoxy systems prepared with acetal-functionalized methylenebis(oxy)dianilines (MDAs) degradable curing agents, which make the corresponding CCLs recyclable and reuseable. We systematically studied the curing behaviors as well as the thermal and mechanical properties of these recyclable epoxy systems. All epoxy/MDA systems thermally decomposed at >288 °C, enabling their CCLs to pass the thermal stress test—currently unachieved with vitrimers. The cured networks degraded in acid via acetal hydrolysis to linear polymers. FR-4-type CCLs cured with the three MDAs met all IPC-4101/128 requirements, and their degradation products were reused to prepare Composite Epoxy Material (CEM)-3–type CCLs: a circular life cycle. We industrialized 2,2’-MDA-cured FR-4, including printed circuit board application testing and qualification, scaled-up degradation and recycling in CEM-3, Underwriters Laboratories certification, and standardization.Subject terms: Electronic materials, Electronic devices, Composites

Introduction

Printed circuit boards (PCBs), the indispensable component of modern electronic devices, are consistently among the top 100 most traded products globally1. PCBs are fabricated from copper-clad laminates (CCLs), which consist of copper foil laminated onto a dielectric substrate by one side or double side, which can be rigid or flexible. Rigid CCLs, the most common type, are made by impregnating reinforcing materials (e.g., glass fiber cloth) with thermoset resins, catalysts, flame retardants, inorganic fillers, solvents, and other ingredients. CCLs must withstand mechanical drilling, chemical etching, chemical desmearing, electroless copper processing, and soldering, yet maintain electrical, thermal, and mechanical reliability under harsh conditions.

Driven by ever-increasing demand on electronic devices, the global CCL market reached $15 billion in 2023 with 656.8 million m² of production, according to Prismark2. However, this growth has exacerbated the electronic waste problem, projected to reach 74.7 million tonnes by 20303. Although the metal fraction (30–50%) of waste PCBs can be recovered through established methods4–6, recycling of the non-metallic fraction—mainly thermoset resins and glass fiber—remains a major challenge7,8. Epoxy (EP)-based CCLs dominate the rigid CCL sector, accounting for over 60% of global production in 2023. However, their cross-linked covalent networks are inherently difficult to be degraded or recycled9,10.

Attempts to improve recyclability include thermoplastic systems and covalent adaptable networks11, particularly vitrimers12–24, which contain reversible or dynamic covalent bonds enabling reshaping and reprocessing. Despite recent advances, such as the transesterification-based epoxy vitrimer for CCLs reported by Iyer et al.25, the relatively low Tg and thermal limits of current vitrimer systems restrict their use in industrial high-performance applications.

High-performance epoxy CCLs, such as flame-retardant glass fiber-reinforced epoxy CCLs (FR-4), must meet IPC-4101E standards for electrical insulation, chemical resistance, dimensional stability, and thermal reliability (e.g., glass transition temperature Tg > 110 °C, and resistance to solder dipping or floating at 288 °C).

To reconcile performance with recyclability, we developed a series of acetal-functionalized aryl diamine degradable curing agents (WO201207189626, WO201300712827, and WO201416984628), which introduce thermally stable yet reversible acetal bonds into the epoxy crosslinked networks, enabling controlled degradation under specific acidic and thermal conditions, yet maintain the mechanical and thermal integrity required throughout the entire product lifecycle—from CCL and PCB fabrication and PCB assembly to end-use service conditions.

Our work is guided by a cradle-to-grave approach, focusing on next-generation programmable materials designed for circularity. This study presents a comprehensive investigation into the curing behavior, thermomechanical properties, and degradation mechanisms of epoxy resins cured with three acetal-containing methylenebis(oxy)dianiline (MDA) curing agents. It also demonstrates a cascading recycling and reuse system for the degraded materials.

FR-4 CCLs incorporating 2,2′-MDA were developed using standard industrial processes and validated by Shengyi Technology, fully meeting IPC-4101/128 specifications. The degraded products from these FR-4 laminates can be directly reused—without further purification—as ingredients in core materials for producing composite laminates such as CEM-3 or CEM-1. This represents the first recyclable epoxy system formally recognized by the CCL industry.

Furthermore, the technology has been endorsed by Lenovo and adopted as the technical foundation for the Specification for Degradable Rigid Printed Circuit Boards for Green PC (T/CIE 223-2024), the first industry standard formulated and promulgated by the Chinese Institute of Electronics (CIE). This work marks the first comprehensive, industrialization-ready solution for recyclable epoxy systems in CCL for PCB applications.

Curing kinetics of EP castings

Curing behavior was studied by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) at heating rates of 2.5, 5, 10, 15, and 20 °C min−1. Supplementary Fig. S11 shows the DSC curves, and Table 1 summarizes the curing parameters—encompassing the onset temperature (Ton), peak temperature (Tp), and endpoint temperature (Tend) of the curing process, the curing temperature interval (ΔT), and the total heat released during the curing process (ΔH∞, calculated by integrating the area under the DSC curve, and normalized by sample mass). EP/4,4′-MDA exhibited the lowest Ton and the highest total ΔH∞ among them, indicating the highest reactivity, but required a comparatively higher temperature to complete curing. Thermal analysis of castings

Thermal stability is a critical property of the insulation resin utilized in CCLs, encompassing thermal decomposition behavior, glass transition temperature (Tg), thermomechanical properties, and other related characteristics.

TGA study of castings

Thermal stability is essential for CCL resins, especially due to lead-free soldering excursions >260 °C during PCB assembly. Achieving Td5%, and Tdon substantially greater than the soldering temperature remains a major challenge for vitrimers containing dynamic bonds such as esters, imines, or Diels–Alder adducts12–25. These vitrimers are typically designed with a low topology-freezing temperature (Tv) to enhance processability, but this often results in low Tdon values, limiting their applicability in CCLs. In case of inadequately high Tdon, soldering at greater than Tdon may induce bond cleavage and resin–fiber interfacial failure, compromising the performance and durability of CCLs.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) results (Fig. 2, Table 2) indicate that the 5 wt% mass loss temperatures (Td5%) for EP/4,4′-MDA, EP/3,3′-MDA, and EP/2,2′-MDA were 368.30 °C, 344.40 °C, and 331.90 °C, respectively. These are much higher than soldering temperature, although 10–47 °C lower than 378.70 °C of EP/DDM, likely due to the acetal group.

More critically, the onset decomposition temperatures (Tdon), derived from derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) curves in Fig. 2b, were 340, 308, and 296 °C for EP/4,4′-MDA, EP/3,3′-MDA, and EP/2,2′-MDA, respectively. Tg values by DSC, DMA, and TMA of the neat castings

Tg is another important parameter for thermoset resins in CCLs, as specified by IPC-4101D/21, which establishes a minimum Tg requirement of 110 °C. Higher Tg values are indicative of more-critical applications. Tg values can be tested by various methods, including DSC, DMA (Dynamic Mechanical Analysis), and TMA (Thermo-Mechanical Analysis). Supplementary Figs. S14–S16 and Table 2 show the DSC, DMA, and TMA curves of cured EP/MDAs and EP/DDM castings.

Via DSC

EP/4,4′-MDA, EP/3,3′-MDA, and EP/2,2′-MDA exhibited Tg values of 150.30, 132.00, and 124.80 °C, respectively, all lower than 165.90 °C of EP/DDM, likely due to the labile acetal linkages present in the MDA structures.

Via DMA

The Tg values of EP/4,4′-MDA, EP/2,2′-MDA, and EP/DDM were 154.00, 128.00, and 170.00 °C, respectively. EP/3,3′-MDA did not exhibit a clear Tg due to its irregular DMA curve, indicating compromised dynamic mechanical properties.

Via TMA

EP/DDM exhibited a Tg of 157.90 °C; EP/4,4′-MDA and EP/2,2′-MDA exhibited values at 132.50 and 113.00 °C, respectively. EP/3,3′-MDA again exhibited an irregular curve, likely caused by low crosslink density and/or residual epoxy and amine groups, which enhanced chain mobility below Tg and precluded accurate determination.

Among the EP/MDAs, key parameters such as Td5%, Tdon, and Tg indicate that EP/4,4′-MDA affords the best thermal performance.

CTE values of castings by TMA

TMA can also afford coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) values (Table 2) of the neat castings except EP/3,3′-MDA with an irregular curve. These CTE values (64.5–73.2 ppm) were much higher than that of copper foil (~17 ppm); nevertheless, CTE can be reduced to acceptable levels by adding reinforcements such as glass fibers and inorganic fillers.

Mechanical properties of the neat castings

Supplementary Figs. S17 and S18 and Table 2 show tensile and flexural properties of EP/MDAs and EP/DDM. EP/2,2′-MDA and EP/4,4′-MDA exhibited tensile strengths of 93.70 and 71.40 MPa, Young’s moduli of 2.43 and 2.13 GPa, and elongations at break of 7.20 and 5.41%, respectively—comparable with or slightly differing from those of EP/DDM. In contrast, EP/3,3′-MDA exhibited much lower tensile strength (28.82 MPa) and elongation (1.57%) despite a similar modulus (2.14 GPa), indicating a rigid yet brittle network likely caused by insufficient crosslinking density because of poor solubility in GELR128.

Flexural testing revealed a largely similar trend to tensile results. Specifically, EP/2,2′-MDA displayed the highest flexural strength (161.70 MPa) and modulus (4.28 GPa), followed by EP/DDM (123.35 MPa and 3.09 GPa). EP/3,3′-MDA and EP/4,4′-MDA showed comparable flexural strength and modulus to EP/4,4′-MDA, despite its distinctly brittle behavior in tension.

Although there are no standards for the mechanical properties of the neat castings, their corresponding CCLs far exceed IPC-4101/128 specifications, as indicated in a subsequent paragraph.

Degradation rates of the neat castings

The degradation of high-temperature cured EP casting powder using acetic acid and HCl as catalysts is slow, but the process can be accelerated by using 5 vol% concentrated HCl at 120 °C with dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) as the solvent. Since sample dimensions and surface area greatly influence the degradation rate, all epoxy castings cured with DDM and MDAs were cut and ground to similar sizes. After 24 h of degradation, the remaining insoluble residues for EP/4,4′-MDA, EP/3,3′-MDA, EP/2,2′-MDA, and EP/DDM were 12, 6, 9, and 98% by weight, respectively. These results demonstrate that EP/MDAs were readily degradable in acidic solution, whereas EP/DDM remained largely resistant under the same conditions.

Characterization of degraded castings

The number-average molecular weight (Mn) and weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of the degraded EP/4,4′-MDA casting were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) using dimethylformamide (DMF) as the solvent after filtration through a 0.22-μm syringe filter. These values were estimated to be approximately 9700 and 50,000, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S19), indicating that the degraded products were soluble linear polymers with moderate molecular weights. Supplementary Fig. S20 shows the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of these polymers. The absence of discernible signals for methylene groups at ca. 5.5 ppm suggests that the acetal bonds underwent substantial degradation.

Hydrolysis of EP/MDAs and MDAs

The degradation of EP/MDAs was attributed to hydrolysis of acetal bonds, yielding phenol groups and formaldehyde (HCHO). This is supported by the hydrolysis of model compound 4,4′-MDA into 4-aminophenol at >120 °C in 5 vol% concentrated HCl in DMSO, as confirmed by NMR spectroscopy. Thus, hydrolysis breaks the crosslinked networks, converting the thermoset into linear polymers (Supplementary Fig. S21). These polymers bearing –PhOH groups can serve as curing agents for epoxy resins, similar to phenolic resins, which are widely used in epoxy CCL formulations.

Although EP/MDAs are readily hydrolyzed under acidic and certain temperature conditions, their hydrolysis in a neutral environment proceeds much more slowly. For instance, 4,4′-MDA mixed with trace amounts of water or equimolar diglycol exhibited no observable reaction after heating at 190 °C (Supplementary Fig. S22). Only partial hydrolysis of 4,4′-MDA was observed at 270 °C after 10 min in the presence of trace water (Supplementary Fig. S23). This resistance to hydrolysis at elevated temperatures contributes to the long-term durability of CCLs33.

Possibility of acetal exchange of MDAs

Acetal bonds formed in situ from vinyl ether and phenol or alcohol derivatives have been employed to construct vitrimers via dynamic acetal exchange reactions34,35. However, these systems exhibit low thermal stability. For example, the PSS-OH/CVDE (Poly (styrene-co-styrene-OH)/1,4-cyclohexanedimethanol divinyl ether) vitrimer exhibited a Td5% of 263 °C35; thus, its Tdon was estimated to be well below the soldering temperature required for CCL applications. This instability likely results from residual vinyl ether groups (CH2=CH–O–) after crosslinking with –PhOH. In contrast, residual –NH2 groups in MDAs remained thermally stable at 270 °C (Supplementary Fig. S23).

We also examined the potential for acetal exchange in MDAs, inspired by reports of acetaldehyde derivatives exchanging at 150 °C35. Since EP castings cure at 190 °C, a mixture of 4,4′-MDA and 3,3′-MDA was heated at this temperature for 2 h but exhibited no reaction (Supplementary Fig. S24). Increasing the temperature to 270 °C in the presence of trace water led to hydrolysis, producing 3- and 4-aminophenol (Supplementary Fig. S25), but no 3,4′-MDA was detected, confirming the absence of bond exchange as indicated in Fig. 3. This suggests that hydrolysis proceeded more readily than acetal exchange due to lower activation energy.

Hydrolysis and thermal treatment of MDAs.

When a mixture of 4,4′-MDA and 3,3′-MDA was heated in the presence of water, 3- and 4-aminophenol were detected but not 3,4′-MDA.

To exclude water’s influence, thoroughly dried 4,4′-MDA and 3,3′-MDA were heated at 270 °C for 4 h under nitrogen, yielding a viscous material with reduced solubility, indicating some polymerization. The NMR spectra of the soluble fraction in DMSO-d6 (Supplementary Fig. S26) indicated peaks corresponding to amino compounds, phenols, and oligomers, despite baseline irregularities. Thus, no evidence of acetal exchange was observed between MDAs. Notably, although MDAs underwent hydrolysis and thermal decomposition/polymerization at 270 °C, their epoxy thermosets exhibited much higher stability, as demonstrated by the TGA results (Supplementary Fig. S27).

Application of 2,2′-MDA in the CCL industry

Given its overall performance on thermal properties, mechanical properties, processability, and overall cost, 2,2′-MDA was selected as the curing agent for the epoxy resin system in CCL fabrication.

Development of 2,2′-MDA-cured FR-4 CCLs

FR-4 CCLs, reinforced with woven glass fabrics, are most widely used in PCBs. To achieve high Tg, multifunctional novolac epoxy resin was used as a secondary resin component. Epoxy resins were mixed with 2,2′-MDA in a stoichiometric ratio, along with accelerators, flame retardants, and fillers in solvent to form a varnish. Woven glass fabrics were impregnated with the varnish, air-dried, and oven-treated to yield prepregs. These prepregs were stacked and sandwiched with copper foils by double side and then hot-pressed into finished FR-4 laminates following established procedures. All fabrication and testing were conducted at Shengyi Technology, with performance data summarized in Table 3.

Degradation of 2,2′-MDA-cured FR-4 CCLs

To enable recycling of CCLs, the copper foils must first be thoroughly removed, as residual copper can compromise the insulating properties of the laminate. The resulting copper-free laminate samples were then physically crushed into fine powder (D100 < 300 μm) to facilitate efficient degradation. The powder was added into glacial acetic acid at about 22 wt% concentration under stirring, and the mixture was heated to 105–110 °C, then the epoxy thermosets underwent degradation for 12 h. 35 wt% hydrogen peroxide solution of twice the weight of the powder was added into the solution batch-by-batch to accelerate decomposition. The powder was chemically degraded in acid, yielding a mixture containing fragmented and powdered glass fibers, inorganic fillers, and soluble oligomers (with the NMR and GPC spectra shown in Figs. S28 and S29, respectively). Notably, this degraded mixture requires no further separation or purification and can be directly reused in the fabrication of CEM-3 or CEM-1 type CCLs.

Recycling of degraded FR-4 samples in CEM-3 samples

Compared with FR-4, CEM-3 comprises two outer layers of woven glass fiber prepreg as a face layer and a non-woven glass fiber mat core (Supplementary Fig. S30). Designed for cost-sensitive mid- to low-end PCB markets, conventional CEM-3 uses dicyandiamide (DICY) as a cost-efficient curing agent, typically with a large amount of filler, whereas other ingredients are largely consistent with those in FR-4. Table 3 summarizes the performance of a representative CEM-3 laminate (N-CEM-3) from Shengyi Technology.

The degraded mixture from 2,2′-MDA-cured FR-4 was reused as a component of the glass mat core in recycled CEM-3 (R-CEM-3; Supplementary Fig. S31), accounting for approximately 7.98 wt% of the total CEM-3 substrate mass (except copper foil). This R-CEM-3 could exhibit comparable performance with N-CEM-3 (Table 3), and both met or exceeded IPC-4101/12 standards for CEM-3.

Discussion

In this work, we developed and characterized the first recyclable epoxy-based CCL that fully complies with industrial standards. Curing behaviors, thermal properties, mechanical properties, degradation behaviors and mechanisms, and specifications were all evaluated.

Developing degradable epoxy resins for CCLs is a pivotal step toward enhancing the sustainability of PCBs. Traditional epoxy resins, known for their robust mechanical and thermal properties, pose substantial environmental challenges due to their resistance to degradation. Innovations in this domain focus on creating materials that maintain performance yet are more environmentally friendly. Products with bio-based natural fibers and water-soluble polymers or recyclable epoxy hardeners are currently in development.

Our approaches for sustainable PCBs

2.2′-MDA-cured FR-4 CCL has been qualification tested for both conventional plated through hole (PTH) PCB and high-density interconnect (HDI) PCB, following the current mature PCB processes. We had finished the sample build and reliability tests for both conventional PTH boards up to 16 layers and HDI 2+ PCB up to 10 layers. They passed all tests, including common tests, thermal resistance test by 10 cycles lead-free reflow, and long-term reliability tests including Thermal Cycling Test (TCT) test (−55 to 140 °C, 1000 cycles), Conductive Anodic Filament (CAF) test (85 °C and 85% RH, 100V DC, 1000 h), Interconnect Stress Test (IST) test (room temperature to 150 °C, 500 cycles) and High Current Test (HCT) test (220 °C, 60 s). The aforementioned results demonstrated that 2,2′MDA-cured FR-4 CCL had excellent PCB processability and reliability as conventional mainstream mid-Tg (Tg > 150 °C) halogen-free materials in the market.

UL certification and standardization

To meet increasing demand for recyclable and green CCLs and PCBs from Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs) worldwide, 2,2′-MDA-cured FR-4 was tested and passed UL (Underwriters Laboratories Inc.) certification. Results have been listed on the UL website, referring to file number E109769 (Supplementary Fig. S32). Notably, the long-term thermal aging tests of over 5000 h have turned out relative temperature indices of the product that were more than 130 °C, which met ANSI FR-15.1. With support from and endorsement by Lenovo and others, the CIE sponsored product standardization of 2,2′-MDA cured FR-4 in early 2023. Specification for degradable rigid PCBs for green PC was released and published by CIE in May, 2024. This standard will help industrialization and the proliferation of recyclable 2,2′-MDA-cured FR-4 in the market.

Cascading recycling of 2,2′-MDA-cured FR-4 degradation and reuse in CEM-3

The industry has established practices for handling and processing of e-waste; most valuable electronic components, metals, and thermoplastics could be recycled and reused through standard technical processes without much difficulty. This process produces non-metal FR-4 residual slags, which are thermosetting resin composites of typically epoxy, glass fiber, and inorganic fillers (yellow boxes in Supplementary Fig. S33). Due to firm and strong crosslinking chemical networks, conventional FR-4 residual slags are too troublesome to be recycled; therefore, most are sent to landfills or sometimes incinerated, which is not environmentally friendly.

Recently, in August 2025, the European Commission finalized three new regulatory frameworks under its sustainable electronics strategy, signaling a major shift for the PCB industry. Effective from January 1, 2026, the new measures aim to align electronic products with the EU’s circular economy goals—changing how PCBs are designed, labeled, and documented. The 2026 sustainability regulations will reshape PCB manufacturing in the EU, with a potential insurmountable impact on the global PCB industry.

Therefore, 2,2′-MDA-cured FR-4 is a breakthrough solution to solve the recycling problem of scrapped PCBs generated from the huge amount of e-waste worldwide every year. This recyclable halogen-free FR-4 has not just every property comparable with established conventional FR-4 (Table 3), but is also compatible with established PCB processing with uncompromising PCB reliability. A cascading recycling of 2,2′-MDA-cured FR-4 degradation and reuse in CEM-3 has been developed, conducted, and validated by joint efforts of Sytech, Lenovo, and Adesso (green boxes in Supplementary Fig. S33). In addition, the CEM-3 manufactured with recycled materials was validated and certificated for the Recycled Material Verification Statement by TŰV Rheinland in August 2024, and renewed in August 2025. (www.tuv.com, Supplementary Fig. S34) The Recycled Material Verification obtained showed that the combined proportion of recycled materials, Post-Consumer Recycled (PCR)-copper foil and PCR-resin composition was 19.77 and 7.98%, respectively.

Methods

Materials

Epoxy resin GELR128 (epoxy value = 0.51 mol·100 g−1) was obtained from Epoxy Base Electronic Material Corporation Limited. 4,4′-diaminodiphenylmethane (DDM) was purchased from TCI. 4,4′-(methylenebis(oxy))dianiline (4,4′-MDA), 3,3′-(methylenebis(oxy))dianiline (3,3′-MDA), and 2,2′-(methylenebis(oxy))dianiline (2,2′-MDA) were manufactured by Adesso; their synthesis and characterization can be found in the Supporting Information.

High-temperature cured EP castings

Preparation of EP castings

Solvent-free EP/MDA castings were prepared by curing epoxy resin GELR128 with MDA at a 1:1 equivalent ratio of epoxy to N–H groups. The components were mixed at 70 °C for 30 min, degassed at 70 °C for 20 min, and then poured into molds. Stepwise curing was conducted at 90 °C (1 h), 120 °C (2 h), 150 °C (2 h), and 190 °C (2 h). EP/DDM castings were prepared similarly for comparison.

Characterization of EP castings

DSC was used to evaluate curing behavior using 5 mg samples at heating rates of 2.5–20 °C min−1 from room temperature to 300 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere.

According to IPC-TM-650 2.4.25D, Tg values were determined by DSC scan using 10 mg to 40 mg sample cutting from the specimen at a heating rate of 20 °C min−1 and one specimen was tested for each formulation. Same for laminate specimens.

According to IPC-TM-650 2.4.24.6, TGA was performed from 50–700 °C using 10–30 mg sample cutting from the specimen at 10 °C min−1 heating rate under nitrogen atmosphere, and one specimen was tested for each formulation.

According to IPC-TM-650 2.4.4.4, DMA was conducted by three-point bending mode from room temperature to 230 °C at 5 °C min−1 heating rate and under 1 Hz frequency to obtain Tg and modulus. The specimen dimensions are provided in the captions of Fig. S15.

According to IPC-TM-650 2.4.24, TMA measurements were taken from room temperature to 250 °C at 5 °C min−1 with the specimen to obtain Tg and z-axis coefficient thermal expansion. The specimen dimensions are provided in the captions of Fig. S16.

Tensile and flexural properties of the neat castings were tested according to GB/T 1040.2 (Type 1A) and GB/T 9341-2008, respectively. Details regarding the test conditions can be found in the captions of Figs. S17 and S18.

Degradation of EP castings

Cut and crushed EP castings (5 × 5 × 4 mm) were weighed and degraded in 2 mL DMSO + 0.1 mL HCl (36 wt%) at 120 °C for 24 h. After cooling, the mixture was filtered. The residue was washed (DMSO and water), dried at 100 °C for 2 h, and weighed. The filtrate was poured into water, and the precipitate was washed and dried for GPC and NMR analysis.

CCL samples and recycling

Preparation of FR-4 samples

FR-4 CCLs were fabricated at Shengyi Technology. A resin mixture of epoxies, curing agents, accelerators, flame retardants, and fillers was dissolved and formulated in solvent to prepare varnish. Electronic class woven glass fabrics 7628 type were impregnated with the varnish, then air-dried, and oven-treated to yield prepregs. These prepregs were stacked to a certain thickness, sandwiched with copper foils, and hot-pressed into FR-4 laminates.

Characterization of CCL and recycled samples

Mechanical tests followed ASTM D638-08 and D790-07. Dk/Df were measured with a Keysight E5071C (IPC-TM-650 2.5.5.5). Z-CTE was determined with a TMA Q800 (IPC-TM-650 2.4.24). Thermal stress tests were performed at 288 °C (IPC-TM-650 2.4.13.1). Volume/surface resistivity was used on an Agilent 4339B (IPC-TM-650 2.5.17.1). Dielectric breakdown was tested with a Hipotronics 7100-5D149 (IPC-TM-650 2.5.6), arc resistance with an NDHU-2 tester (IPC-TM-650 2.5.1), and flammability with an RH-6033B device (UL94).

Degradation of FR-4 samples

Copper was mechanically removed and etched per IPC-TM-650 2.3.7.2. The laminate without copper was pulverized and crushed into powder (D100 < 300 μm). The powder was added into glacial acetic acid at about 22 wt% concentration under stirring, the mixture was heated to 105–110 °C, and then the epoxy thermosets underwent degradation under this temperature for 12 h. A hydrogen peroxide solution of twice the weight of the powder was added batch-by-batch to accelerate decomposition. After degradation, the finished mixture was cooled to room temperature and was ready for recycling.

Recycling of degraded FR-4 in CEM-3 samples

CEM-3, cured with DICY, consists of a non-woven glass fiber mat core and two outer layers of woven glass fabric prepregs. The finished mixture from degraded FR-4 powder, as aforementioned (including scattered crushed glass fiber particles, filler particulates, and depolymerized resin fragments), was reused as ingredients’ substitute in the formulation of the core, accounting for 7.98 wt% of the total CEM-3 laminate (except copper foil). Laminates were fabricated at Shengyi Technology by stacking two woven prepregs with the core containing degraded FR-4, followed by hot pressing to obtain the recycled laminate, designated as R-CEM-3. Its performance was compared with that of the conventional CEM-3 (N-CEM-3); Table 3 summarizes the results.
