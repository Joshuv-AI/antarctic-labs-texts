Single Nuclear RNA Sequencing Reveals Unique Hepatocyte Population in Small Bowel Resection-induced Liver Injury
Authors: Alex Park, Daniel M Alligood, Kerry Swanson, Chunxiao Shi, Jun Guo, Nicholas O Davidson, Deborah R Rubin, David M Alvarado, Brad W Warner
Published: 2025

Intestinal failure associated liver disease (IFALD) represents a spectrum of liver injury associated with short gut syndrome, beginning with steatosis and cholestasis, then progressing to fibrosis and cirrhosis. Advanced disease is associated with increased liver-related and all-cause mortality, ultimately requiring multivisceral transplantation (liver and small bowel).1 The pathogenesis of IFALD is unique from nonalcoholic fatty liver disease due to the lack of metabolic syndrome. IFALD has been mostly studied in relation to long-term parenteral nutrition (PN); however, growing evidence suggests the eitiology of IFALD is multifactorial and includes bowel resection.2 Using single nuclear RNA sequencing (snRNAseq), we investigated early molecular changes associated with small bowel resection (SBR) in a PN-independent murine resection model.

Compared with sham-operated mice, SBR mice exhibit significant hepatic inury as evidenced by serum transaminitis and elevated hepatic expression of oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress markers, Nqo1 and Ddit3 at 2 weeks postoperation (Supplementary Figure 1A). Nuclei were isolated from a subset of sham control and SBR livers for snRNAseq.3 Unsupervised clustering identified 9 major cell types (hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, endothelial, stellate, macrophages, T-cell, B-cell, dendritic, and neutrophils) that were annotated using automated and manual techniques (Figure 1A and Supplementary Figure 1B).4 Within the hepatocyte cluster, we delineated periportal, midzonal, and pericentral hepatocytes, alongside a separate subcluster of hepatocytes we called cluster 4 (Supplementary Figure 1C, D). Cluster 4 hepatocytes increased in proportion of the total hepatocyte population in SBR compared with sham (Supplementary Figure 1E). We identified genes specific to this subcluster, one of which was annexin A2 (Anxa2) (Figure 1B and Supplementary Figure 1D). Anxa2 is a pleiotropic protein found in cells of epithelial origin. In the context of liver disease, Anxa2 is associated with hepatic steatosis and inflammation, as well as premalignant hepatocyte phenotype in liver fibrosis.5 Increased Anxa2 protein expression in SBR was confirmed with immunohistochemistry and Western blot (Supplementary Figure 2A, B). Immunofluorescence for Anxa2 identified pericentral colocalization with albumin, which confirmed the Anxa2+ cells were hepatocytes (Figure 1C; Supplementary Figure 2C, D).

To explore the transcriptome of SBR-cluster 4 hepatocytes, pairwise differential expression between zonal hepatocytes and cluster 4 hepatocytes in both conditions was performed. A Venn diagram was constructed to illustrate unique and common genes between hepatocyte-condition subgroups (Supplementary Figure 3A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) overrepresentation analysis (ORA) of the differently expressed genes (DEGs) common to both cluster 4 and zonal SBR hepatocytes showed upregulation of stress response/antioxidant, cytochrome P450, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) pathways (Supplementary Figure 3C). Interestingly, ORA of DEGs common to cluster 4 in both SBR and sham showed enrichment of cell cycle and motor proteins, suggesting proliferative and migratory capability at baseline (Supplementary Figure 3D, E). However, the 641 DEGs exclusive to SBR cluster 4 hepatocytes were enriched with fibrotic and tumorigenic pathways, including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), p53, and RAS, as well as senescent and apoptotic gene sets (Figure 2A; Supplementary Figure 3B). Gene Ontology Biological Processes ORA for these hepatocytes showed enrichment for signal transduction, peptide responses, and epithelial cell migration (Figure 2B). Cellchat analysis show cluster 4 hepatocytes display many predicted interactions with neighboring hepatocytes, endothelial cells, macrophages, cholangiocytes, stellate, and immune cells (Figure 2C), with the top 10 predicted interactions including secreted signaling (neuregulin 1), cell-cell contact (CDH) and extracellular matrix receptor (Laminin) pathways (Supplementary Table 1). We validated increased secretion of Nrg1 in SBR peripheral serum by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Figure 2D).

Overall, we have characterized a molecular signature of liver injury at an early timepoint following SBR. We show activation of stress response and inflammatory genes in hepatocytes, mirroring the general manifestation of liver injury we see in our PN-independent surgical model of IFALD. Further, we identify a distinct hepatocyte subcluster that proliferates after SBR, distinguishable by an epithelial marker Anxa2, that possesses transcriptional changes, suggesting a unique role in SBR-induced liver injury, with upregulation of MAPK, p53, and RAS pathways as well as complex signaling toward neighboring cells. The function of Anxa2 in liver injury is unclear. A recent study by Matchett et al suggests Anxa2+ migratory hepatocytes contribute to liver regeneration in acetaminophen/alcohol-induced liver injury.6 Transcriptionally, our cluster of Anxa2+ hepatocytes appear to have a migratory component that could initiate regenerative signals as part of stress response activity. However, other studies have also demonstrated Anxa2+ hepatocytes positively correlate with liver disease severity and drive fibrotic transformation to HCC.5,7 Our model demonstrates these Anxa2+ hepatocytes possess upregulation of fibrogenic and carcinogenic pathways, which likely contribute to disease progression. For example, p53-dependent apoptosis and senescence and MAPK pathways all play key roles in fibrosis progression.8 Additionally, induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition through hepatocyte RAS activation has been shown to potentiate tumor growth when co-transplanted with myofibroblasts in murine HCC models.9 Further evidence of the influential nature of these hepatocytes stem from the predicted interactions to stellate cells and cholangiocytes, cell types intrinsically linked to fibrosis. Notable among these interactions is an increase in secreted Nrg1 expression in cluster 4. This is intriguing, as Nrg1 has been recently shown to enhance HCC cell metastases via the phophoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B pathway.10 This distinct signaling from our Anxa2+ hepatocyte subcluster in the setting of its fibrotic/carcinogenic transcriptional changes soon after SBR suggests a unique role in the injured liver that could drive fibrogenesis in later stages of disease. Limitations of our study include sample size. SBR livers had fewer recovered nuclei than shams, with one sample necessitating omission due to low sequencing quality. This is likely due to gross pathological differences in tissue from steatotic SBR vs nonsteatotic sham livers. Future studies will track the lineage of this unique hepatocyte cluster and explore the functions of identified markers and signaling molecules to delineate a mechanism of progression of IFALD from acute to chronic phases.
