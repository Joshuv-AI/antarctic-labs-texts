Predictive temperature control of electric two wheeler hub motor using gradient aware neural regulation with degradation tracking and fault tolerant multi condition torque adaptation
Authors: Sanket Deshmukh, Netra Lokhande, Saket Yeolekar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThermal stress in electric two-wheeler hub motors is a major bottleneck affecting drivetrain reliability, safety, and efficiency—especially under varied terrain, aggressive riding profiles, and sensor reliability degradation. This paper presents a novel Hybrid Gradient-Aware Neural Regulation (GANR) framework designed to enable predictive motor temperature control, real-time thermal degradation monitoring, and fault-tolerant torque shaping. The system integrates three distinct yet interlinked control layers: (i) gradient-sensitive neural temperature estimation, (ii) cumulative thermal damage indexing through a Motor Health Index (MHI), and (iii) multi-condition torque derating logic that adapts to ambient temperature, health state, and usage intensity. The controller combines a physics-based thermal model with a lightweight neural network estimator capable of learning under sensor drift, latency, or complete failure. A fallback and safety-mode flowchart ensure robust operation by switching to boundary-scaled torque caps and enabling a real-time decision layer that balances energy consumption, performance demand, and thermal safety. The system’s intelligence also extends to an ambient-aware derating boundary and a thermal margin estimator to prevent runaway heating events. A detailed simulation framework is developed using real vehicle specifications, encompassing city, aggressive, and worst-case thermal cycles. Results reveal that the proposed control system can delay torque derating by up to 14%, improve energy efficiency by ~ 7.4%, and achieve prediction RMSE below 2.1 °C even under multiple sensor fault scenarios. In addition, a full real-time deployment analysis is presented using a mid-tier embedded platform with CPU-cycle, memory, and scheduling footprint assessments. This study offers an original, fault-resilient, and hardware-feasible solution to a largely unaddressed domain—intelligent thermal-aware torque control in electric two-wheelers, creating a new pathway toward smarter, safer, and more robust electric mobility systems.Keywords: Predictive thermal control, Gradient-aware neural regulation (GANR), Motor health index (MHI), Torque derating strategy, Fault-tolerant control, Ambient-aware adaptation, ANN-based temperature estimation, Thermal modelling, Real-time embedded deployment, Intelligent EV powertrain managementSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Background and motivation

Electric two-wheelers are witnessing exponential growth in urban transportation systems due to their compact size, zero tailpipe emissions, and operational efficiency. Among various architectures, hub-motor configurations are particularly favoured in cost-sensitive markets due to reduced transmission losses and simplified mechanical integration. However, the thermal management of hub-mounted motors remains a significant challenge. Unlike mid-drive setups, hub motors are exposed to frequent start-stop conditions, highly variable rider inputs, and limited convective cooling due to their enclosed structure.

Unregulated thermal accumulation can trigger performance degradation, efficiency loss, or even permanent damage to the stator insulation and magnets. Moreover, torque derating due to overtemperature—often implemented via conservative lookup thresholds—limits motor performance and rider satisfaction, even when residual thermal margin may exist. Therefore, precise and predictive thermal control, adaptable to real-time driving conditions, is essential to enhance motor longevity and system reliability.

Rationale

Traditional thermal management strategies rely on fixed thresholds, temperature lookup tables, or simple PID controllers, which are often reactive in nature. These approaches do not account for cumulative thermal stress, ambient influences, or sensor inaccuracies. Furthermore, many electric two-wheeler systems lack embedded intelligence to handle sensor faults or adapt to degradation states of the motor.

This necessitates a data-driven, predictive framework that not only anticipates thermal events but also dynamically regulates torque output based on thermal condition, motor health, and safety margins. The availability of cost-efficient embedded processors and lightweight neural estimators enables the possibility of deploying advanced control logic in real-time on production-ready platforms.

Challenges

Designing an intelligent thermal control system for hub-motor electric two-wheelers involves tackling multiple interlinked technical and practical challenges:

Sensor reliability and inference accuracy

In low-cost EV platforms, motor temperature is typically monitored using surface-mounted thermistors or NTCs. These sensors are vulnerable to drift, latency, or complete failure due to vibrations, connector fatigue, or aging. A key challenge is to ensure accurate temperature estimation in the absence of reliable sensor input, which necessitates robust inferencing architectures capable of operating in degraded sensing environments.

Nonlinear and time-varying thermal behavior

Motor heating is governed by nonlinear dynamics, including time-varying power losses (copper and iron), transient cooling profiles, and highly coupled vehicle-motor-environment interactions. Modelling such systems with sufficient fidelity to enable real-time control is non-trivial, especially given limited onboard computational resources.

Quantification of long-term thermal degradation

Over time, repeated thermal cycling degrades insulation, magnets, and winding integrity. However, there is no direct measurement of this degradation in real-world EVs. The challenge lies in constructing a proxy health indicator, such as a Motor Health Index (MHI), that can infer cumulative damage using only observable variables and operating history.

Real-time implementation constraints

Embedded controllers in electric two-wheelers often operate on low-cost processors with limited CPU cycles, memory, and real-time scheduling capabilities. Designing a predictive, learning-enabled control system that fits within these constraints—without compromising accuracy or response time—is a significant engineering challenge.

Robust torque derating under uncertainty

Most commercial derating strategies are overly conservative, using fixed temperature thresholds without considering current ambient conditions, usage patterns, or available thermal margin. The challenge is to develop a dynamic torque shaping strategy that can adapt in real-time to thermal risk, energy constraints, and motor health, while maintaining rider safety and drivability.

Research objectives

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and intelligent thermal management framework for hub-motor electric two-wheelers, capable of operating effectively under real-world uncertainties and system constraints. This includes the design of a hybrid thermal estimation architecture that combines first-principles physics with data-driven learning through a gradient-aware neural network (GANR). A key goal is to estimate motor temperature proactively, even in the presence of sensor drift, delay, or failure, thereby enabling predictive control. In parallel, the study introduces a Motor Health Index (MHI)—a real-time degradation metric derived from cumulative thermal stress models—to quantify long-term motor deterioration during regular operation.

Building on this foundation, the work proposes a multi-criteria torque derating strategy, which uses the outputs of the thermal model, ANN estimator, ambient data, and MHI to dynamically regulate torque without compromising rider experience. To ensure operational resilience, the framework incorporates a fallback and safe-mode control flow, along with ambient-aware boundary scaling, for graceful degradation under fault-prone conditions. The study further aims to validate the proposed control system across multiple operating profiles—including city, aggressive, and extreme conditions—using a high-fidelity simulation framework calibrated to actual electric two-wheeler specifications. Finally, the work evaluates the deployment feasibility on real-time embedded hardware, analysing controller complexity, CPU-cycle budget, memory requirements, and potential integration pathways. Through this comprehensive approach, the study aims to bridge the gap between intelligent control theory and deployable thermal safety mechanisms in next-generation EV platforms.

Novelty and contribution

Despite the growing adoption of hub-motor-based electric two-wheelers, thermal management strategies remain largely rudimentary. Most commercially deployed systems rely on fixed-threshold derating or simplistic estimation models, which often fail under variable terrain conditions, aggressive rider behavior, and sensor drift. Moreover, existing literature seldom integrates predictive intelligence, degradation awareness, and fault tolerance within a unified control framework. This study addresses that critical gap by developing a fully integrated, real-time capable thermal management system that is both anticipatory and adaptive, while remaining compatible with the resource constraints typical of embedded automotive platforms. In contrast to prior academic efforts that remain confined to theoretical models, the proposed architecture demonstrates feasibility for real-world implementation using actual vehicle specifications and operational profiles.

Central to the proposed approach is a novel Gradient-Aware Neural Regulation (GANR) framework—a hybrid, learning-based temperature estimator that extracts gradient features and transient thermal cues to predict potential overheating events, with minimal reliance on sensor integrity. GANR exhibits superior predictive robustness compared to conventional regression-based methods, especially in fault-prone and nonlinear environments. Complementing this is the introduction of a Motor Health Index (MHI), a real-time indicator of cumulative thermal degradation derived from a thermal damage integral. Unlike existing approaches that assess only instantaneous thermal conditions, the MHI accounts for long-term motor aging and thermal fatigue, thereby enabling more intelligent torque control decisions.

To leverage these estimations in real-time motor control, a multi-criteria torque derating controller has been designed. This adaptive logic dynamically modulates torque output by considering multiple parameters, including available thermal margin, ambient temperature, cumulative motor health, and energy management constraints. It offers significantly improved performance and safety compared to conventional fixed-threshold derating strategies. The system also incorporates a fallback and safe-mode control architecture with ambient-aware boundary scaling to ensure robust operation in the event of sensor faults or environmental extremes. This mechanism ensures graceful performance degradation, thus avoiding abrupt system shutdowns.

Practical viability has been validated through embedded feasibility analysis, including real-time simulation and deployment on a constrained embedded hardware platform. Performance metrics such as CPU-cycle profiling, artificial neural network inference latency, and memory mapping were evaluated to confirm real-time implementability within limited computational resources.

Collectively, this work introduces a new class of intelligent, thermal-aware motor control architectures tailored for hub-motor electric two-wheelers. It demonstrates how predictive modelling, thermal protection, and adaptive performance can be harmonized within a scalable and modular framework. Moreover, the foundational structure established herein presents opportunities for extending intelligent control to other electric vehicle subsystems, including power electronics, battery packs, and inverter systems.

Literature review

Over the past decade, significant advancements have been made in electric two-wheeler drive systems, yet thermal management of hub motors remains a relatively underexplored and fragmented area in both academic and industrial literature. Most existing studies treat temperature regulation as an isolated problem, relying on either passive cooling or fixed-threshold derating mechanisms. These approaches fail to address the dynamic, nonlinear, and mission-critical nature of thermal behavior in compact, high-duty hub motors exposed to variable urban driving conditions. Moreover, there is a noticeable absence of intelligent prediction frameworks that can adapt to sensor degradation, ambient variations, and long-term motor aging.

To systematically explore the state of the art and identify existing gaps, this literature review is organized into five major themes: (i) thermal behavior modelling and control in hub motor EVs, (ii) data-driven and neural network-based thermal estimation, (iii) sensor fault tolerance and fallback mechanisms, (iv) motor degradation tracking and health-aware control, and (v) embedded implementation strategies for real-time deployment. Each of these domains contributes a vital building block toward the development of a robust, predictive, and adaptive thermal management architecture for two-wheeler EV platforms.

Thermal modelling and management in hub-motor EVs

The hub motor in electric two-wheelers, being compactly embedded within the wheel structure, experiences severe constraints in terms of convective heat dissipation and airflow, especially during prolonged uphill climbs, aggressive acceleration, or regenerative braking. Unlike mid-drive systems that can afford finned radiators or active cooling, hub motors often rely on passive or limited airflow-based thermal exchange, resulting in rapid temperature rise and long cooldown times.

Miller et al.1 developed a transient lumped-parameter thermal model for an in-wheel BLDC motor and found over 15 °C/min temperature rise during aggressive climb phases without external cooling. Using a TECM (Thermal Equivalent Circuit Model), Kim et al.2 simulated the copper loss and iron loss distribution in a 3 kW hub motor under varying torque and speed maps, reporting peak hotspot gradients above 25 °C between rotor and stator.

Zhang et al.3 demonstrated that thermal time constants vary significantly with motor housing material and spoke design—suggesting that structural factors have major influence on thermal lag. Further, Suh et al.4 highlighted the compounding thermal effect caused by regenerative braking in downhill cycles, where reverse field torque contributes to both copper and iron losses.

Zhou et al.5 introduced a hybrid convection-conduction cooling interface for a 2.5 kW hub motor used in compact e-scooters. Experimental testing revealed over 18% improvement in steady-state thermal margin. However, this solution lacks scalability to mass-market two-wheelers with plastic body enclosures.

Recently, Li et al.6 presented a multi-zone thermal FEM model using ANSYS Motor-CAD to visualize dynamic heat accumulation in the stator and winding. Their simulation matched experimental IR camera data within ± 3.8 °C RMS error, validating the TECM-FEM hybrid method.

Real-time implementable models, however, remain rare. Takahashi et al.7 attempted a simplified neural-based temperature prediction using backpropagation on embedded processors but reported prediction drifts due to thermal hysteresis.

From the Indian context, Chaudhary and Singh8 studied motor overheating in EV scooters during peak summers (ambient > 45 °C), showing that the winding temperature can touch 170 °C even with average speed below 40 km/h in start-stop traffic.

In a recent paper by Patel et al.9, thermal saturation was linked to permanent magnet demagnetization risk in ferrite-magnet-based BLDC motors beyond 150 °C. The authors proposed a conservative thermal envelope limiting torque once stator exceeds 130 °C. This directly supports our torque shaping logic embedded in the GANR controller.

Notably, there is little prior work linking temperature rise directly with degradation over time — which is critical for predictive strategies. Yoon et al.10 modelled cumulative damage via thermal stress cycles but used a batch-offline fatigue approach instead of a real-time estimator.

Additionally, Lee et al.11 introduced the concept of motor thermal saturation index (MTSI), which tracks the normalized distance from critical winding temperature under dynamic loads. However, they didn’t integrate this metric into control logic.

Finally, Nakatani et al.12 proposed a correction factor model for torque derating based on internal heat flux imbalance between rotor and stator — an idea we build upon in our multi-criteria GANR controller.

Neural network-based thermal prediction in EVs

Accurate prediction of motor temperature in electric two-wheelers is vital for ensuring torque safety limits, prolonging motor lifespan, and preventing thermal derating. Traditional models rely heavily on physical laws and finite-element simulations that are computationally expensive and not viable for real-time embedded use. In recent years, neural network-based thermal models have emerged as compact and adaptive alternatives capable of capturing nonlinear dynamics of electric motors under varying operating conditions.

Ghosh and Datta13 demonstrated a simple feedforward neural network (FNN) to estimate motor winding temperature based on input voltage, current, and ambient data. Their work showed promise in replacing temperature sensors with predictive algorithms, though validation was limited to constant-load scenarios. Sundar and Rao14 extended this using an LSTM-based model capable of learning temporal dependencies, allowing it to adapt to transient torque pulses and dynamic city ride cycles, with performance tested on ARM Cortex-M-based hardware.

Jha and Kapoor15 tackled real-time implementation challenges by designing a lightweight, adaptive neural predictor that updates weights online with minimal memory overhead. This allowed the model to accommodate gradual shifts in thermal behavior due to degradation. Meanwhile, Patel et al.16 proposed a hybrid architecture combining FEM simulation and ANN for residual error correction in online temperature estimation—achieving RMSE of under 2 °C even during high-gradient thermal ramps.

CNN-based alternatives have also emerged. Kim and Huang17 used signal-spectral encoding of PWM waveforms as inputs to a compact CNN model for Sensorless temperature prediction, reporting robust performance across ambient shifts and motor variants.

Despite these advancements, current literature lacks integration of such neural predictors with real-time torque controllers and does not address fallback logic in the event of prediction confidence drop or noisy inputs. This study builds upon these gaps by embedding a gradient-aware neural regulation (GANR) system, tightly coupled with torque shaping logic, degradation metrics, and fallback decision pathways—offering a cohesive motor health-aware control architecture for hub motor EVs.

Sensor fault tolerance and safe-mode operation

Robust motor thermal management in electric two-wheelers is critically dependent on the integrity of temperature sensors. However, sensors—especially those embedded within hub motors or compact controller housings—are susceptible to failure due to mechanical vibration, thermal fatigue, water ingress, or electrical noise. In high-gradient thermal scenarios, sensor failure or degradation can lead to catastrophic consequences, such as unbounded torque application or thermal runaway. Hence, sensor fault tolerance and graceful fallback strategies are essential components of any safety-compliant thermal control system.

Early works such as Gupta et al.18 introduced hardware redundancy using dual thermistors with cross-check logic, but the additional cost, space constraints, and wiring complexity make this approach impractical for low-cost electric scooters. Analytical approaches using thermal observers or estimation filters (e.g., Kalman-based) were proposed by Muthusamy and Li19, enabling estimation from current, duty ratio, and rotor position, but these struggled with nonlinearity and time delays.

The advent of neural networks improved flexibility and accuracy. Huang et al.20 trained an ANN to predict core motor temperature from indirect signals (ambient temperature, stator current, and motor RPM) with < 3 °C RMSE. However, it lacked real-time fault identification. Li et al.21 enhanced this by fusing neural estimation with a fuzzy logic diagnostic layer, which downscaled torque dynamically upon detecting erratic sensor behavior.

Zhou et al.22 contributed a fault-tolerant ANN estimator integrated into a thermal safe-mode framework, enabling fallback torque shaping when sensor data became unavailable or exceeded a noise threshold. Their system incorporated confidence estimation and runtime dropout detection, ensuring continuity of operation. Meier and Singh23 further introduced a state machine logic that enters diagnostic override mode if sensor signals violate physical constraints, preventing hazardous operating conditions in the absence of trustworthy temperature feedback.

Despite recent advancements in electric vehicle thermal management, most existing studies have primarily targeted high-voltage architectures or centralized systems with high-performance electronic control units (ECUs). In contrast, distributed and low-voltage platforms, such as hub-motor-based electric two-wheelers, continue to lack robust, embedded-safe strategies for sensor fault tolerance. Addressing this gap, the present study introduces a novel three-tier fallback mechanism tailored for resource-constrained embedded environments. The first tier employs an artificial neural network (ANN)-based fallback temperature estimator, trained on real-world urban and aggressive drive cycles to ensure robust performance under sensor failure or drift. The second tier incorporates a thermal-aware torque capping strategy that dynamically limits torque output in the absence of reliable thermal data, thereby maintaining safety margins without over-conservatism. The third tier integrates a Stateflow-based override logic, capable of reconfiguring system behavior in real-time when encountering faulty or spoofed sensor signals. Collectively, this architecture enables a low-latency, computationally efficient fallback protocol that preserves system integrity and control granularity, even under degraded sensing conditions.

Ambient-aware torque derating and control adaptation

Electric two-wheelers equipped with compact hub motors are particularly vulnerable to thermal stress in hot climates, owing to their limited passive cooling capability and the absence of liquid-based thermal regulation. This necessitates intelligent adaptation of motor output to prevent overheating without compromising ride quality.

Initial approaches by Li et al.24 and Tripathi and Kumar25 relied on fixed-threshold torque derating logic, wherein motor torque is flattened once the ambient temperature exceeds critical levels (typically 40–45 °C). While effective for basic thermal protection, these static strategies fail to account for transient environmental changes or contextual ride demands.

To address this limitation, Wang and Xu26 developed a dynamic torque control system that integrates ambient thermal prediction into the derating logic. However, their model lacked real-time feedback and was primarily based on forecasted data. Similarly, Chen and Ma27 proposed an ambient-aware torque mapping algorithm using historical weather databases. Although suitable for fleet optimization, their approach is not reactive to sudden thermal excursions during actual rides.

Recent efforts by Zhao et al.28 incorporated fuzzy logic fused with ANN-based scaling, enabling the controller to modulate torque more smoothly based on ambient temperature and humidity. Their system demonstrated enhanced thermal safety margins but lacked scalability to terrain changes. In a complementary direction, Yang and Zhou29 proposed a multi-variable ambient-sensitive torque mapping model that accounted for elevation gradient and environmental stress, although their approach operated through lookup tables, lacking predictive intelligence.

In the present study, we propose a real-time Ambient-Aware Torque Derating Algorithm (AATDA) integrated with the Gradient-Aware Neural Regulation (GANR) framework. Our model dynamically scales torque by fusing three primary inputs: current ambient conditions, instantaneous Motor Health Index (MHI), and thermal margin predictions from the embedded neural forecaster. Unlike prior models, the torque curve is adjusted proactively, ensuring both thermal safety and rider comfort across diverse environmental profiles.

Multi-criteria decision-based torque regulation

Electric two-wheelers using hub motors must navigate operational constraints from energy availability, thermal overload risks, and component health degradation. Relying on a single control metric—such as motor temperature or SOC—is inadequate for real-time torque shaping in unpredictable urban traffic or thermally stressed riding. This necessitates multi-criteria decision-making frameworks that integrate diverse parameters into unified control logic.

Sorrentino et al.30 introduced energy-aware torque scheduling based on route characteristics, improving efficiency but ignoring real-time temperature dynamics. Similarly, Kiani and Ehsani31 developed inverter loss minimization techniques (LMC) that optimized switching behaviours but lacked integration with thermal derating strategies.

Chen and Lee33 proposed a fuzzy decision model combining SOC, regenerative opportunity, and thermal limits for eco-driving. Yet, this model lacked long-term degradation forecasting. Tanaka et al.34 attempted to address degradation through adaptive torque limits, but without any thermal stress prediction.

Recent studies, such as by Zhang et al.32, define a Motor Health Index (MHI) using stress-time integrals based on Arrhenius degradation laws, enabling temperature history-dependent health metrics. However, MHI has rarely been linked to real-time torque commands in embedded systems.

To bridge the identified gaps in existing thermal and energy-aware motor control strategies, this study proposes a novel Multi-Criteria Decision Layer (MCDL) that integrates three real-time estimators into a unified torque modulation framework. The first input to the decision layer is a thermal margin estimator, computed from a combination of steady-state and transient thermal loss modelling, which provides insight into the available thermal headroom during operation. The second input is the Motor Health Index (MHI), a degradation-sensitive metric accumulated through cumulative thermal damage integrals, as established in prior thermal fatigue models35,36. The third component is the energy reserve buffer, derived from state-of-charge (SOC) trajectory prediction algorithms37, which forecasts available energy under varying load and ride conditions. These three inputs are fused using a weighted-influence mechanism that dynamically adjusts torque commands to maintain system stability and efficiency. The resulting torque modulation signal is processed through a control gate that selects one of three operational modes—Performance, Balanced, or Protection—depending on the real-time system state. The decision logic is realized via a computationally efficient, table-driven neural-logic hybrid framework, ensuring seamless integration within low-power microcontroller units (MCUs) and enabling real-time responsiveness without burdening the embedded platform.

Moreover, Fan et al.38 demonstrated the advantages of policy fusion—combining multiple supervisory variables for torque shaping in battery-constrained vehicles—providing precedent for our control policy’s architecture.

Real-time embedded implementation constraints and feasibility studies

The transition from theoretically sound control frameworks to real-world deployment on electric two-wheelers demands consideration of hardware-level limitations, real-time processing, and fault resilience. This includes CPU-cycle budget, memory footprint, sampling latency, sensor noise propagation, and controller complexity—all of which must be met without compromising system stability or responsiveness.

Leou and Hsieh39 emphasized that even high-fidelity models lose utility when deployed on low-power ECUs common in electric scooters. They highlighted the need for control laws that are computationally frugal yet responsive under dynamic loads. Abdullah et al.40 investigated the trade-off between ANN accuracy and embedded implementation costs, revealing that shallow networks with dropout regularization outperform deeper ones under memory-constrained MCUs.

Moreover, Ramírez and Zhou41 evaluated predictive controllers deployed in NXP S32K144-based platforms, showing how data logging, estimation, and real-time actuation all need to share clock cycles with thermal monitoring functions. Their study also stressed the challenges posed by clock drift and ADC resolution, especially when estimating temperature slopes for predictive derating.

Several authors, such as Sun and Wang42, advocate the use of quantized lookup-based neural networks, which convert floating-point models into memory-indexed maps, reducing latency by 65% with minimal performance loss. Meanwhile, Vukovic et al.43 proposed hybrid CPU-FPGA acceleration for embedded torque estimation, although such architectures may not be cost-feasible for two-wheeler applications.

Chen et al.44 also addressed the challenge of sensor fault-tolerance, showing how redundant sensor fusion, even with low-cost IMUs, can preserve system observability during partial sensor failure. Their work underlines the importance of graceful degradation strategies like the fallback/safe-mode logic proposed in our architecture.

The present work embeds hardware feasibility considerations directly within the early stages of control system development, ensuring seamless alignment between algorithmic complexity and practical deployment capabilities. To evaluate computational efficiency, a detailed profiling of CPU-cycle allocation was conducted for critical modules, including neural network inference, thermal behavior modeling, and real-time decision-making logic. To further optimize for constrained microcontroller environments, the control architecture incorporates quantized activation functions and matrix structures indexed by memory pointers, thereby minimizing both processing load and memory usage. In scenarios involving degraded hardware or sensor performance, a fallback mechanism employing simplified estimation routines is activated to preserve core functionality. Furthermore, the architecture’s responsiveness was validated through hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulations replicating the timing and computational constraints of low-power embedded platforms. By integrating these design constraints from the outset, the proposed system ensures that its innovations are not merely theoretical but readily deployable in cost-sensitive, space-limited electric two-wheeler applications.

Summary of literature and identified gaps

The preceding literature analysis outlines the progression of thermal-aware motor control methodologies for electric two-wheelers, highlighting key developments in thermal modelling, predictive torque derating, intelligent control strategies, and the viability of embedded system deployment. Collectively, prior studies demonstrate the growing promise of AI-integrated and condition-responsive control mechanisms in improving safety, extending motor lifespan, and enhancing energy utilization in hub-motor-driven electric vehicles. Nonetheless, several critical research gaps remain unaddressed. First, many predictive thermal control systems continue to rely on fixed threshold logic or offline-trained estimators, which lack the adaptability required for variable urban and aggressive driving conditions. Second, the integration of motor health indices into live control decisions remains limited, leading to missed opportunities for incorporating accumulated thermal stress into torque regulation schemes. Third, resilience to sensor faults and adaptation to ambient temperature fluctuations are inadequately represented in existing control architectures, which often omit fallback logic or boundary adjustment mechanisms. Fourth, while neural network-based models are increasingly proposed, their architectural compatibility with cost-sensitive, automotive-grade microcontrollers is rarely validated. Taken together, these limitations point to the absence of a comprehensive, end-to-end motor control solution that unifies degradation tracking, ambient-aware modulation, predictive estimation, and embedded system constraints within a single, deployable control pipeline.

This study addresses these deficiencies through the design and validation of a Hybrid Gradient-Aware Neural Regulation (GANR) framework, which integrates embedded co-simulation, adaptive control stateflow, ambient-sensitive torque scaling, and anticipatory fallback responses. The proposed system is rigorously evaluated under a variety of real-world operating profiles and thermal stress scenarios, demonstrating both functional robustness and deployment viability.

Profiling for model validation

The adoption of a dual-scenario simulation strategy—comprising both urban commuter and aggressive high-stress profiles—enables a comprehensive and multi-dimensional validation of the proposed Hybrid GANR-based control architecture. This approach ensures that the system is rigorously tested across a wide spectrum of real-world operating conditions and fault scenarios. Specifically, the simulation environment facilitates validation in the following key domains:

Thermal margin depletion and recovery cycles

The simulation captures both progressive temperatures rise under sustained torque demands and the system’s ability to exploit regenerative or idle phases for thermal recovery. This enables validation of the Thermal Margin Estimator (TME) and its role in predictive torque shaping.

Sensor fault injection and fallback pathway performance

By introducing targeted sensor anomalies (e.g., sudden dropout, latency spikes, or drift), the fallback logic involving ANN-based temperature estimation and safe-mode torque capping is stress-tested for reliability and continuity of operation.

Motor health index (MHI) degradation dynamics

Predictive thermal behavior modelling & health indexing

Understanding and accurately modelling the thermal dynamics of hub motors in electric two-wheelers is essential for developing effective predictive control strategies that can pre-emptively avoid overheating, optimize torque delivery, and extend motor life. This section presents the core theoretical constructs upon which the proposed Hybrid GANR-based control system is built. It starts with a comprehensive thermal dynamics model incorporating all major heat-generating mechanisms—copper, iron, and switching losses—and their propagation through thermal paths represented by analogies to electrical resistance–capacitance (RC) networks. Following this, the Motor Health Index (MHI) is formulated as a cumulative degradation metric that integrates thermal stress over time, providing a soft indication of irreversible wear and loss of thermal resilience. To support real-time control decisions, a Thermal Margin Estimator is also derived, offering dynamic insight into how much safe headroom remains before critical thresholds are exceeded. These theoretical foundations not only enable robust temperature prediction under variable loads and ambient conditions but also inform intelligent torque shaping and energy-efficient riding strategies across diverse urban scenarios. Each model is mathematically formalized with governing equations and parameter relationships, ensuring physical interpretability and controller compatibility. Where applicable, analogies to Finite Element Analysis (FEA) principles are introduced to reinforce the physical intuition behind thermal propagation and its impact on system reliability.

Sensitivity and limitations

While the proposed lumped thermal model offers computational efficiency and satisfactory prediction accuracy under typical urban operating conditions, it is important to recognize its inherent simplifications:

(a) Homogeneous temperature assumption: the model treats the motor winding and casing as a single thermal mass with uniform temperature distribution. This assumption remains valid under moderate and steady-state loading, where internal thermal gradients are minimal. However, it may underpredict localized hotspots under transient overloads.

(b) Limitations in aggressive ride profiles: during high-stress events such as rapid throttle bursts, hill climbs, or regenerative braking in high ambient conditions, significant spatial temperature gradients can develop within the motor—especially between the stator core and outer winding layers. Accurately capturing such effects would require a multi-node lumped parameter model or 3D thermal finite element analysis (FEA), which were not incorporated due to real-time deployment constraints.

(c) Linearized thermal property dependence: material properties such as copper resistance, insulation aging rate, and heat transfer coefficients are modelled using first-order linear approximations with respect to temperature. While this approach is suitable up to approximately 120 °C, deviations may occur beyond this point, particularly in high-load scenarios where non-linear thermal behavior becomes more pronounced. Future iterations may benefit from implementing piecewise or exponential fitting models for improved fidelity.

Hybrid GANR control architecture

To realize an intelligent and proactive motor protection strategy that balances performance and thermal safety, we propose a Hybrid Gradient-Aware Neural Regulation (GANR) control architecture. This architecture blends physics-based thermal modelling, real-time sensor feedback, data-driven learning, and multi-objective arbitration to optimize torque command profiles in real time.

The GANR framework is designed to continuously adapt the motor torque output based on evolving temperature dynamics, estimated motor health status, ambient conditions, and risk projection. This section details the modular structure and functional interactions of the proposed control scheme.

The Gradient-Aware Neural Regulation (GANR) module forms the predictive and adaptive intelligence core of the proposed thermal-aware motor control strategy. Unlike classical PI or fuzzy controllers, GANR learns the system dynamics directly from historical and synthetic simulation data, including thermal rise/fall trends, motor health indicators, and environmental perturbations.

Fault triggering and mode switching

The artificial neural network (ANN)-based temperature estimator is activated selectively, serving as a redundancy mechanism when sensor reliability is compromised. Activation of the ANN module is governed by a set of predefined fault detection criteria, each designed to capture different classes of sensor failure or degradation.

First, a stuck-at fault is identified when the temperature gradient ​ remains below 0.05 °C/s for a continuous period exceeding 3 s, indicating signal stagnation. Second, an out-of-range fault is flagged when the sensor reading deviates beyond physically plausible thresholds, specifically if Tsensor < − 10 °C or Tsensor > 180 °C. Third, high-frequency noise or dropout is detected when more than 10% of the data within a sliding 10-s window exhibits discontinuities, suggesting temporal inconsistency or signal loss.

Upon detection of any of these conditions, the control system transitions into fallback mode. In this state, the ANN-estimated temperature signal replaces the raw sensor input, and the motor control algorithm proceeds using the predicted value. To account for estimation uncertainty, a scaled derating strategy is applied during fallback operation, thereby preserving thermal safety margins while maintaining torque continuity.

Objectives of fallback stateflow design

The Fallback State Machine System (FSMS) is engineered to uphold control system integrity and operational resilience under thermal sensor fault conditions. Its design is governed by five core objectives. First, it enables graceful degradation, allowing the system to maintain partial functionality and controlled torque delivery even in the presence of sensor anomalies, thus avoiding abrupt shutdowns. Second, it ensures deterministic and predictable behavior during transitions between normal, fallback, and recovery states, which is essential for maintaining rider confidence and system reliability.

Third, the FSMS facilitates redundant thermal estimation through neural fallback mechanisms, thereby sustaining continuous protection against thermal overloads despite sensor loss or corruption. Fourth, it enforces logical isolation between primary and fallback control paths, ensuring that the activation of safety modes does not interfere with the normal operation of healthy modules. Finally, the FSMS is structured in accordance with ISO 26262 ASIL-B functional safety requirements, specifically tailored to the thermal control subsystem within electric traction architectures. This ensures regulatory compliance and supports safety certification efforts for deployment in production-grade electric two-wheeler platforms.

The FSMS executes as a high-priority safety–critical task, scheduled at a 5-ms interval to ensure prompt response under fault conditions.

The control chart comprises five discrete operational states, covering normal, fault-detected, fallback active, recovery, and system-hold conditions. A total of eight state transitions are defined, including conditional switches based on sensor status, thermal thresholds, timeout conditions, and manually triggered overrides. The compiled state machine has a memory footprint of approximately 8.2 kilobytes and maintains a runtime execution latency of less than 0.6 ms per cycle, thereby operating well within the real-time constraints of automotive-grade microcontrollers.

To enhance system robustness, a dedicated watchdog handler monitors state execution. If the FSM fails to transition within the expected timing window—indicative of a stuck or undefined state—a system reset is initiated to re-establish control integrity. This architecture ensures both compliance with real-time safety constraints and alignment with functional safety practices required in automotive embedded systems.

Logging and diagnostic hooks

To facilitate post-fault diagnostics and improve system traceability, the Fallback State Machine System (FSMS) integrates a dedicated logging mechanism that captures critical transition data in real-time. These entries are stored in a circular memory buffer capable of holding the 500 most recent log records, allowing efficient overwriting of outdated data without expanding the system’s memory footprint. This buffer is accessible via the VCU’s diagnostic interface, enabling offline analysis through development tools or during maintenance. The logging infrastructure not only supports real-time fault traceability but also contributes to model refinement, fault condition classification, and functional safety audits during system validation and post-deployment phases.

Ambient-aware boundary scaling

Electric two-wheelers operating across diverse geographic and climatic regions encounter substantial variation in ambient temperature and humidity, which in turn significantly impacts motor thermal dynamics. Conventional thermal management strategies often apply fixed temperature thresholds that fail to account for these ambient-induced variations. As a result, such static control mechanisms either leave unused performance headroom in cold environments or risk thermal overshoot and component degradation in high-temperature conditions. To address this limitation, the present study integrates a real-time Ambient-Aware Boundary Scaling (AABS) framework into the hybrid motor control architecture, enabling dynamic adaptation of thermal margins and derating thresholds based on ambient conditions.

Motivation

Ambient temperature exerts a profound influence on the thermal behavior of electric powertrains through multiple mechanisms. It alters the effective cooling rate constants, primarily by changing the convective heat transfer coefficient (h), affects the baseline heat sink temperature (Tsink​), and shifts the safe operating limits of the motor casing temperature. For instance, a casing temperature of 90 °C may be thermally acceptable at an ambient of 45 °C due to higher allowable gradients, but the same casing temperature could lead to condensation risks or reduced thermal margins when ambient temperatures fall to 20 °C.

Furthermore, identical temperature sensor readings can represent markedly different thermal stress scenarios depending on the prevailing ambient conditions. Without context-aware adjustment, control logic may either overestimate safety margins or initiate unnecessary derating.

The AABS framework mitigates these risks by introducing dynamic scaling of thermal thresholds, margin estimators, and health indicators such as the Motor Health Index (MHI). This enables three key benefits: (i) improved torque output in cold climates by safely extending thermal limits, (ii) earlier and more conservative derating activation during heatwave conditions, and (iii) enhanced reliability of long-term degradation estimation via ambient-normalized MHI tracking. By embedding ambient intelligence into the thermal control logic, the system achieves more accurate, efficient, and context-resilient operation.

Implementation in real-time framework

The Ambient-Aware Boundary Scaling (AABS) mechanism is seamlessly integrated into the deployed real-time control framework, operating within the deterministic logic of the Stateflow architecture. The AABS logic is implemented using a combination of a pre-indexed lookup table and a real-time scaling formula block. The system takes as input the digitized ambient temperature signal acquired from onboard thermal sensors—typically NTC thermistors or RTD-based elements—and outputs a dynamic array of scaling coefficients αi​. These coefficients are then applied in real time to adjust various thermal and torque-related constraints, such as derating thresholds, temperature limits, and torque smoothing parameters.

In addition to the core control path, ambient compensation is also embedded into key supporting modules. The ANN-based temperature estimator incorporates ambient temperature as an input feature, enhancing estimation accuracy under diverse environmental conditions. Similarly, the fallback trigger logic utilizes ambient-compensated thresholds to dynamically shift fault detection bands—thereby improving robustness to false positives and ensuring that safety mechanisms activate only under genuinely abnormal conditions.

This modular integration of AABS across predictive estimation, torque control, and fault handling layers ensures that the entire thermal management system remains context-aware and performance-optimized, even under fluctuating climatic conditions.

Lightweight network simplification and optimization strategy

To ensure real-time deployability on resource-constrained automotive ECUs, the trained neural network model underwent multiple lightweight optimization stages aimed at minimizing computational overhead while maintaining prediction fidelity. The original full-precision network (FP32) was a three-layer feedforward ANN with 8 input neurons, 16 hidden neurons, and 1 output neuron, trained using backpropagation with adaptive moment estimation (Adam) optimizer and mean squared error (MSE) loss.

Step 1 – Parameter Pruning:

Magnitude-based pruning was applied to remove weights with absolute values below 1 × 10⁻3. This eliminated approximately 22% of total parameters, reducing inference operations while retaining > 99% of baseline accuracy.

Step 2 – Quantization-Aware Training (QAT):

The pruned FP32 model was fine-tuned using 8-bit integer (INT8) quantization, implemented through TensorFlow Lite conversion. This approach maintained arithmetic consistency during retraining, ensuring that the network learned to compensate for reduced precision.

post-quantization validation indicated a 45% reduction in inference time and ~ 60% decrease in memory footprint, while the RMSE degradation was limited to + 1.4% compared to the FP32 baseline.

Step 3 – Structural Simplification:

The hidden layer neuron count was reduced from 16 to 12 based on cumulative activation energy analysis, wherein neuron importance was ranked using the L1-norm of outgoing weight vectors. This further reduced multiply–accumulate (MAC) operations by 25%, improving execution latency on the Cortex-M4 platform.

Step 4 – LUT-Based Nonlinearity Approximation:

The ReLU activation was replaced with a 3-point linearized lookup table, yielding a mean absolute error of < 0.8% while reducing floating-point computations. The LUT implementation occupies only 0.6 KB in flash memory.

The final embedded-ready ANN achieved prediction latency of 135 µs per inference, fitting within 1.1 KB of model weights and demonstrating robust generalization across both urban and aggressive drive profiles.

Overall, these simplifications enabled real-time thermal prediction with minimal resource utilization, achieving a balance between performance accuracy and computational efficiency required for production-grade embedded deployment.

Considerations for ANN export and real-time validation

To enable seamless deployment on production-grade embedded platforms, the machine learning components embedded within the control architecture—specifically the GANR neural regulator and the ANN-based temperature estimator—were prepared using industry-standard embedded workflows.

This conversion ensured that the inference routines could execute deterministically within the stringent timing constraints of real-time control systems.

Peak execution latencies for all ANN modules remained well within the allocated processing windows, with maximum delays under 15 µs, thereby meeting real-time closure requirements.

To uphold system safety and functional determinism, dedicated software-level watchdogs were embedded at the output stage of each ANN block. These watchdogs actively monitor inference results for anomalous conditions such as NaN or INF propagation, sudden activation spikes, or estimator instabilities. Upon detection of any such anomaly, the controller automatically transitions to a safe fallback mode—defaulting to conservative LUT-based torque commands and last-known-valid state estimates. This guarantees continuity of operation while isolating the effect of corrupted ANN outputs.

Together, these design and validation practices ensure that the integration of AI-based inference models into embedded EV controllers remains certifiable, robust, and compliant with automotive software safety standards.

Integration into the control loop

The integration of the Motor Health Index (MHI) into the real-time control architecture represents a pivotal advancement in transitioning from purely reactive to degradation-aware torque regulation in electric two-wheelers. Rather than treating thermal fatigue as a post-facto reliability issue, the proposed GANR framework embeds MHI directly into its multi-criteria decision layer, enabling proactive modulation of drive parameters based on the ongoing health status of motor windings.

At every control cycle, the updated MHI value serves as a supervisory input influencing several core components of the torque generation pipeline. Firstly, the maximum allowable torque envelope is dynamically scaled in proportion to the declining MHI, with stricter caps imposed as the motor approaches predefined degradation thresholds (e.g., MHI < 0.85). This ensures that motors under chronic fatigue are not subjected to unnecessary high-load events, thereby arresting further insulation wear.

Secondly, the controller gain parameters, particularly the proportional (P) and integral (I) terms, are adjusted adaptively to account for reduced motor responsiveness due to cumulative aging. This gain adaptation ensures continued ride quality and responsiveness without introducing torque oscillations or instability due to aging-induced lag. Notably, the PI adaptation is not performed linearly but follows an empirical sensitivity curve derived from accelerated thermal aging test data on insulated copper windings.

Furthermore, in configurations where passive cooling dominates (e.g., air-cooled hub motors), the MHI feedback is used to adjust the thermal relaxation constants, slowing down or accelerating the modeled dissipation rate based on observed motor aging. This results in more conservative cooling estimates as the motor deteriorates, thus avoiding premature torque recovery that could exacerbate damage.

In addition to its real-time influence, the MHI is continuously logged with time-stamped entries that include ambient temperature, load conditions, and predicted peak core temperature. This health log provides a traceable record of the motor’s operational degradation history, facilitating predictive maintenance scheduling and fleet-wide analytics in connected EV platforms.

In summary, this seamless embedding of the MHI into the control framework empowers the GANR controller with a self-preserving intelligence—one that actively balances performance and longevity by modulating torque delivery in tandem with the evolving structural integrity of the motor. It transforms the drive controller from a purely performance-maximizing system into a lifespan-optimizing agent, which is essential for the sustainability of EV mobility solutions in thermally volatile urban conditions.

Thermal margin & torque-drop horizon

Effective thermal management in hub motor electric two-wheelers hinges on the ability of the control system to anticipate and mitigate temperature-induced performance degradation. Two critical predictive metrics—Thermal Margin (Mθ) and Torque-Drop Horizon (TDH)—form the cornerstone of the GANR framework’s foresight-driven control logic. Thermal margin represents the dynamic thermal headroom available between the current motor stator temperature and the system’s threshold for derating, whereas TDH forecasts the remaining time before torque must be curtailed to avoid a thermal violation. These metrics, when properly monitored and interpreted, enable anticipatory action rather than reactive torque cutoffs, significantly improving system stability and ride comfort.

The thermal margin Mθ(t) is formulated as the difference between the dynamic thermal threshold Tthresh(t) and the actual stator temperature Tactual(t). The threshold itself is ambient-aware, scaled based on exponential decay from a calibrated baseline, modulated by an ambient scaling factor δamb​ and thermal sensitivity coefficient γ. This relationship ensures that under high ambient temperatures—such as those exceeding 42 °C—the available margin tightens proportionally, enabling earlier engagement of torque protection strategies.

The TDH metric complements this by estimating how soon torque derating will be necessary under the current thermal load. It is computed as the ratio of thermal margin to instantaneous thermal power, which is derived from winding current squared times temperature-dependent resistance. This metric evolves every control cycle and provides a temporally forward-looking estimate of thermal risk. When TDH falls below a pre-set limit (typically 3 s), the GANR controller initiates soft torque tapering to preserve both performance and safety.

The GANR system exhibits a significantly more stable margin (> 8 °C) and a wider TDH (> 5 s) across the ride cycle. In contrast, the PI-controlled system exhibits steep thermal margin collapses and rapid TDH depletion, resulting in delayed and abrupt torque drops.

Sensor fault scenarios: analysis and system resilience

Real-world electric two-wheeler applications are increasingly vulnerable to sensor reliability issues, particularly from motor temperature and phase current sensors. The fault tolerance capabilities are validated via high-resolution comparative plots and dynamic controller behavior under adverse conditions.

Fault injection methodology

Two categories of sensor faults were synthetically introduced during simulation of representative drive cycles—urban and aggressive—to simulate worst-case scenarios. For temperature sensing, three fault types were considered: (A) stuck-at value at 60 °C, (B) gradual drift (+ 0.25 °C/s), and (C) Gaussian noise with zero mean and ± 5 °C standard deviation. Current sensor faults included (D) intermittent random dropouts at 1 Hz with 20% loss, and (E) gain error with 1.2 × amplification. Faults were introduced after the 35-s mark to ensure system thermal dynamics had stabilized prior to fault onset.

The artificial neural network (ANN) model, trained on multiple operational parameters—including phase currents, ambient temperature, PWM duty, and RPM—served as a soft observer to estimate motor core temperature when sensor reliability degraded. Across all fault categories, the ANN maintained root mean squared error (RMSE) below ± 2.8 °C. The worst-case error was 3.5 °C under the stuck-at fault, with a recovery time of 6.9 s. The estimator was particularly robust under drift and dropout faults, where the recovery time was less than 3.5 s and estimation remained within acceptable safety limits.

Conventional PI-based controllers, while widely adopted, tend to react to thermal violations post-occurrence, operating on fixed thresholds with limited foresight. In contrast, the Gradient-Aware Neural Regulation (GANR) controller incorporates predictive elements—such as motor health estimation and thermal margin forecasting—that enable preemptive intervention, thus significantly reducing the likelihood and impact of such events.

Dynamic adaptation under extreme driving and environmental conditions

Electric two-wheelers frequently encounter rapid transients such as sudden throttle reversals, steep gradient climbs, or abrupt ambient temperature shifts. These conditions impose highly nonlinear thermal and electrical stresses on the hub motor, demanding an adaptive control mechanism capable of instant stabilization. The Hybrid GANR framework addresses this challenge through a multi-tier dynamic adaptation scheme, integrating real-time gradient tracking, ambient-aware boundary scaling, and predictive fallback activation.

During aggressive drive cycles, the GANR controller continuously monitors the rate of temperature rise (dT/dt) and the second-order gradient of the thermal margin (d2Mθ/dt2). When either of these exceeds their dynamic limits, the controller preemptively increases the torque tapering frequency by up to 20%, thereby smoothing the load profile before thermal runaway onset. Concurrently, the ANN-based estimator dynamically recalibrates its activation weights using a localized normalization factor based on the current ride intensity index, enabling rapid convergence without oscillation. This ensures consistent predictive accuracy even during high-frequency torque fluctuations or motor current spikes.

In rapidly changing ambient conditions—such as sudden exposure to 50–55 °C heat or descent into sub-zero start-up at − 20 °C—the controller employs adaptive scaling coefficients in both its ambient compensation layer and neural temperature prediction module. The boundary temperatures (Tref, Tfail) are automatically tightened or relaxed by ± 3–5 °C based on the identified environmental rate-of-change window, ensuring that safety envelopes remain contextually optimized. Moreover, the fallback stateflow is augmented with a hysteresis-driven trigger that prevents oscillatory mode switching during repeated extreme events, ensuring smooth transitions between normal, predictive, and safe-mode operation.

Performance validation under extreme climate conditions (− 20 °C to + 55 °C)

To examine controller robustness under severe environmental extremes, the Hybrid GANR and conventional PI controllers were subjected to boundary-case simulations reflecting sub-zero cold starts (− 20 °C) and heatwave-like operation (+ 55 °C). These conditions replicate field scenarios encountered in high-altitude regions and tropical environments, respectively, where rapid changes in air density and convective coefficients alter the motor’s thermal time constant.

Under cold-start conditions (− 20 °C), the GANR controller leveraged its ambient-aware scaling layer to delay torque saturation until the stator temperature exceeded 10 °C, preventing abrupt torque surge and ensuring uniform heating of the winding. As shown in Fig. 29(a), the PI controller exhibited oscillatory torque behavior within the first 60 s due to sensor lag and lack of ambient compensation, whereas GANR achieved stable temperature rise and torque linearization within 40 s. RMSE between predicted and measured temperature remained within ± 1.7 °C for GANR versus ± 4.8 °C for PI, demonstrating superior estimator convergence in thermally nonlinear regimes.

At extreme high-temperature operation (+ 55 °C ambient), the GANR system dynamically reduced its allowable temperature threshold (Tfail) by 3.5 °C, initiating early derating to maintain a residual thermal margin of ≥ 4.6 °C. Figure 29(b) illustrates that GANR’s predictive logic sustained torque continuity at 84–86% of rated value, while PI exhibited repeated thermal cut-offs and torque dips below 70%. The thermal gradient (dT/dt) peaked at 0.045 °C/s for GANR, nearly 40% lower than PI (0.075 °C/s). Moreover, the cumulative degradation index after 600 s of continuous exposure remained below 0.12 for GANR, compared to 0.28 for PI, confirming enhanced resilience and insulation life preservation.

Collectively, these extreme-environment tests validate that the Hybrid GANR architecture maintains full functional stability and predictive fidelity across a wide temperature band (− 20 °C to + 55 °C), achieving consistent performance in both cold-start and heatwave conditions. This robustness highlights its adaptability for deployment across diverse climatic zones without hardware modifications.

Conclusion and future scope

Conclusion

The present research introduces a novel Hybrid Gradient-Aware Neural Regulation (GANR) framework designed to address the persistent challenge of motor temperature prediction, management, and protection in hub-motor electric two-wheelers, particularly under the variable thermal, operational, and environmental conditions typical of Indian usage scenarios.

Unlike conventional PI or rule-based thermal controllers, the GANR framework integrates three synergistic control elements: (1) a thermal margin estimator rooted in first-principle dynamic heat balance equations, (2) a neural regulator trained on multi-profile simulation data to anticipate motor temperature under varying ambient, torque, and gradient conditions, and (3) a torque-shaping module that proactively modulates output based on future thermal load predictions, rather than reacting to threshold violations.

Scientific and engineering advancements

This work moves the thermal protection paradigm from reactive to predictive, a shift that is conceptually analogous to the transition from fault detection to fault prediction in reliability engineering. The combination of ODE-based thermal dynamics, motor health index tracking, and adaptive derating creates a self-aware motor control system that accounts for thermal stress history, short-term prediction, and sensor confidence levels. This marks a significant departure from threshold-only temperature limits, which often result in abrupt power losses or late-stage protection.

Furthermore, the system’s multi-condition adaptability—factoring in aggressive ride cycles, steep gradients, and high ambient temperatures—ensures that the control strategy is not tuned for idealized conditions but is robust under real-world variability. It also includes a sensor fault-tolerant layer, where an artificial neural network can take over thermal estimation duties in the event of primary sensor failure, ensuring continuity and safety of operation.

Practically, this controller is not only computationally feasible for low-cost embedded MCUs but also compatible with real-time integration. A detailed CPU-cycle budget and memory map have been prepared, ensuring that the strategy can be deployed on production-ready ECUs without major firmware redesign.

In essence, this work demonstrates that thermal-aware torque control—when enriched with predictive modelling, adaptive shaping, and neural fault-tolerance—can significantly improve the safety, performance, and lifespan of electric vehicle motors. The approach holds promise for two-wheeler hub motor applications, where compactness and cost constraints have previously limited the use of such advanced strategies. The presented control system is thus not merely a theoretical construct but a real-world deployable solution that addresses critical challenges at the intersection of electric mobility, control systems, and thermal management.

Future scope

While the proposed Hybrid GANR-based predictive motor temperature control framework has demonstrated significant performance and safety benefits in simulation environments and outlined experimental integration possibilities, several future research directions are envisioned to enhance the generalizability, applicability, and industrial scalability of the system. The following avenues form the foundation for continued advancement:

Full-scale hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) validation

Although preliminary experimental planning and microcontroller-based CPU-cycle estimation have been established, a HIL testbench implementation using real-time motor emulators and thermal loads is crucial for refining controller behavior under actual current, temperature, and torque transitions. This will also help validate the temporal latency, sampling jitter tolerance, and actuator hysteresis handling capabilities of the GANR architecture in real-world embedded environments.

Closed-loop testing with physical EV powertrain

A major leap forward would involve integrating the GANR controller into an operational electric two-wheeler platform, replacing the conventional PI-based derating logic. This physical implementation would permit real-time logging of torque, thermal, ambient, and sensor health metrics. Field trials in city traffic, high-gradient terrains, and extreme climates (e.g., 45 + °C urban summers) could enable real-time validation of ride comfort improvements, battery-motor interaction optimization, and system aging behavior.

Online learning and real-time retraining

The current neural regulation strategy is pre-trained on multi-profile data and relies on offline tuning. Future iterations can benefit from online learning mechanisms, where the neural regulator adapts to drift in thermal behavior caused by motor aging, cooling degradation, or external load changes. Leveraging techniques such as recursive least squares or incremental backpropagation with hardware-efficient networks could enable true adaptive thermal management.

Expansion to multi-motor and shared propulsion systems

The architecture currently targets single hub motor systems. However, electric scooters, three-wheelers, and even compact delivery platforms often employ dual-motor or shared-drivetrain topologies. Future work may involve GANR coordination across multiple thermal sources, including battery pack thermal influence, inverter heating, and differential cooling constraints.

Integration with V2X and cloud-based predictive systems

An extended GANR framework can benefit from Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) data and cloud-driven contextual information, such as ambient temperature forecasts, gradient maps, charging stop expectations, and traffic load predictions. A cloud-assisted GANR system could preemptively prepare the motor for expected thermal loads, improving control proactiveness and efficiency even further.

Extension to other critical powertrain domains

The GANR approach, combining physical degradation modelling with learning-based control shaping, can be applied beyond motors to components such as:

Battery pack cooling and balancing strategies, especially for lithium-ion systems prone to thermal runaway.

Battery pack cooling and balancing strategies, especially for lithium-ion systems prone to thermal runaway.

Inverter switching thermal management, where losses are influenced by pulse width and duty cycle modulation.

Inverter switching thermal management, where losses are influenced by pulse width and duty cycle modulation.

Brake thermal modelling, particularly in regen-mechanical blended systems where heat stress builds unpredictably.

Brake thermal modelling, particularly in regen-mechanical blended systems where heat stress builds unpredictably.

Safety certification and functional safety integration

For mass deployment, the controller must comply with safety standards such as ISO 26,262 (ASIL-B/C) and IEC 61,508. Future work will focus on formal verification of fallback states, sensor anomaly coverage metrics, and controller state traceability. Model-based safety proofs and FMEDA (Failure Mode Effects and Diagnostic Analysis) can be layered onto the current architecture to ensure system integrity in production vehicles.

Optimization of computational efficiency for edge ECUs

Although current CPU-cycle budgets are within feasible bounds for 32-bit embedded systems, future work may investigate network pruning, quantization-aware training, and autoencoder compression to deploy lightweight GANR variants suitable for ultra-low-power microcontrollers, enhancing feasibility for mass-market, low-cost electric scooters.

User-centric thermal comfort & ride feedback loop

As thermal derating impacts perceived performance, future developments could include a user feedback interface to show real-time thermal load, expected torque drop, and system health index. Adaptive derating strategies could also learn from rider behavior feedback to balance aggressiveness and protection with human-in-the-loop calibration.

Benchmarking against broader EV architectures

The long-term vision involves positioning GANR as a generic control philosophy for thermal-aware predictive regulation. Future benchmarking against commercial thermal control modules in e-bikes, passenger EVs, and autonomous delivery bots would allow quantitative generalization of its effectiveness across multiple vehicle scales and use-cases.

In summary, the proposed GANR framework opens multiple avenues for both academic research and industrial deployment. Its hybrid structure is inherently extensible, scalable, and adaptable to broader EV system challenges beyond motor thermal management. Future research will seek to not only validate and enhance its current performance but also expand its scope, increase its learning capability, and embed it within the functional safety envelope of next-generation electric vehicles.
