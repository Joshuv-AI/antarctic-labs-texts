Design, simulation and feasibility of the innovative agricultural warehouse receipt system through dynamic programming and agent-based models
Authors: Maryam Riazi, Seyed Mojtaba Mojaverian, Somayeh Shirzadi Laskookalayeh, Abbas Mirzaei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address marketing challenges in the agricultural sector, provide financial support for small-scale farmers over marketing seasons, manage price risks of agricultural products, and enhance the functioning of agricultural mercantile exchanges, it is attainable to implement an efficient and compliant warehouse receipt system (WRS) that aligns with the legal, institutional, social, and economic-financial conditions of a country. The aim of this study is to design, simulate, and assess the feasibility of an innovative WRS in the agricultural sector. To achieve this, a WRS was designed and evaluated for maize crop in Iran. The research methodology of this study is divided into three main parts: design, simulation, and feasibility assessment of the WRS. The design process incorporated the FAO-provided (2009) warehouse receipt system development, considering the experiences of various countries and the institutional and financial regulations specific to Iran. Additionally, a dynamic programming model was used to simulate the system, and an agent-based model was utilized for system feasibility assessment. The study results demonstrated that it is possible to design an innovative and efficient WRS by involving five key actors, including farmers, buyers (maize traders), banks, mercantile exchange market, and the warehouse (governmental institution), and establishing clear communications among them. Based on simulation results using dynamic programming model, it was evident that four parameters, namely the annualized loan interest rate, the valuation coefficient for loan collateral, price volatility of the product over the marketing season, and the warehouse cost-to-product value ratio, significantly impact the adoption of the WRS by farmers. In conclusion, the findings from the agent-based model revealed that setting the annualized loan interest rate at 8%, a collateral valuation coefficient of 85%, price fluctuations over the non-harvest time at 60%, and a warehouse cost-to-product value ratio of 2% can result in the participation of nearly 100% of farmers in the proposed WRS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74519-w.Keywords: Agricultural marketing, Short-term financing, Price risk management, Mercantile exchange marketSubject terms: Socioeconomic scenarios, Psychology and behaviour

Introduction

During the early stages of agricultural production and cultivation, farmers encounter financial constraints due to the need for capital and expenses1,2. Often, farmers have to rely on traditional borrowing methods from banks to obtain the necessary capital for production and livelihood expenses throughout the production process. However, they face challenges within the banking system due to inadequate collateral and low creditworthiness3. Traditional approaches like forward contracting, pre-selling, and guaranteed sales to the government are also utilized by farmers to secure financial support in the initial stages of production. Unfortunately, these methods often result in unfair pricing for their products4,5.

On the other hand, agriculture is significantly influenced by natural and environmental factors and constantly faces production uncertainties6,7. Fluctuations in agricultural production and food supply lead to price variations in these products, which have a negative impact on the financial situation of farmers, especially those who are poor and vulnerable8,9. Another cause of price fluctuations in agricultural products is the time gap between planting and harvesting. During the harvesting season, when there is an abundance of the product in the market, prices tend to decrease. Conversely, during seasons of planting and scarcity in the market, prices increase10. Therefore, one of the main and observable characteristics of agricultural markets is the fluctuation in product prices, which are influenced by market inventory levels and supply and demand forces11–13.

The warehouse receipt system (WRS) has been widely recognized as an effective solution in numerous studies for addressing the financial challenges encountered by farmers and the price fluctuations of agricultural products during the production process5,10,13–21. Economists have recommended the implementation of the WRS as a logical solution to address the fundamental marketing and financial issues faced by small-scale farmers, facilitate commodity exchange, and manage price risks in developing countries22. Miranda et al18. argue that farmers can alleviate marketing challenges by utilizing warehouses to store their products and implementing a WRS. In this context, the warehouse receipt serves as a financial instrument, enabling the stored commodity to be utilized as collateral for obtaining loans.

A significant finding has been observed across various studies in the field of WRSs. Despite their ability to address marketing issues, provide short-term financing for farmers, and mitigate price fluctuations of agricultural products throughout different seasons, these systems encounter multiple challenges during implementation13,15,17,19,20. For instance, Coulter and Onumah14 concluded that warehouse receipts can assist underdeveloped and inefficient agricultural markets in reducing the risk of price changes. However, they identified political factors and government interventions in the agricultural market as the primary obstacles to the successful implementation of the WRS. Mor and Fernandes23 stated that the absence of sufficient political will to establish appropriate laws, as well as the lack of well-developed marketing infrastructure, hinders the potential success of such systems. In a study by Thunde and Baulch24 that focused on African countries, it was investigated who utilizes WRSs and who benefits from them. The results revealed that the highest volume of warehouse receipt usage was among large traders and medium-sized merchants, who derived the most advantages from this system. Pillai and Deshpande20 discussed the primary challenge of the WRS, which is the absence of a reliable market following product storage. Safo et al.13 found that participation in the WRS is significantly influenced by factors such as access to loans, product size, settled buyers, and membership in agricultural organizations.

The literature review reveals that most studies have highlighted the advantages of the WRS in various countries. However, None of the studies have addressed a precise evaluation of the successful implementation of the WRS and the identification of influential parameters for policy application in this area. Therefore, the objective of this study is to design, simulate, and evaluate a WRS for an agricultural product. Specifically, maize in Iran has been selected as a case study. With this objective in mind, the present study aims to address the following questions:

Methodology

The research methodology of this study is divided into three main parts: design, simulation, and feasibility assessment of the WRS.

Design of the proposed WRS

The concept behind the proposed WRS is based on the research conducted by Höllinger et al., who recommended the development of this methodology for the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) to be implemented in developing nations22. The system’s framework is as follows: following the harvest, farmers or other agricultural depositors store their produce in a warehouse (A). The warehouse issues an inventory receipt to the depositors (B), which serves as collateral (C). By transferring the inventory receipt to banks, the depositors can secure loans (D). The amount of loans granted is estimated by considering a specific percentage of the market value of the stored products, taking into account the lender’s risk assessment. Storing the products in the warehouse allows producers to postpone the sale of their goods until they reach the desired price. Upon reaching the maturity date or in favorable market conditions, producers sell the stored products based on the inventory receipt (E), with the buyer making direct payment to the lender (F). After repaying the loan, the lender returns the inventory receipt to the buyer (G), who can then visit the warehouse, present the inventory receipt, and collect the purchased products (H). In the event of default and non-payment of the debt on the maturity date, the lender can mitigate their losses by utilizing the inventory receipt and selling the products in the warehouse (Fig. 1). Hence, the initial concept was tailored to suit the social, economic, and financial conditions of Iran’s agricultural sector, while incorporating insights from other countries’ experiences, resulting in the proposed design of the WRS.

Simulation of the proposed WRS

To simulate the proposed WRS, a dynamic programming model was utilized based on the study of Miranda et al.18. The objective of this model is to maximize the utility function of farmer in season i. If a crop year is divided into two seasons (where here “season” refers to a period of more than 3 months), the first season is called the marketing season (i = 1) and the second season is called the non-productive season (i = 2). The marketing season begins with the start of maize harvesting and ends with the start of the non-productive season, while the non-productive season is defined from the end of maize sales to the start of maize harvesting in the next year. The farmers start the marketing season by storing freshly harvested maize. They have to decide how much of their maize they want to sell at the initial market prices and how much they want to store (the stored quantity is divided into two parts: the amount stored in the farm storehouse and the amount stored in warehouses, with the latter incurring additional costs for the farmer). Feasibility of the proposed WRS

Considering the advantages of agent-based model (ABM) over other modeling methods, this approach has been utilized to evaluate the feasibility of the proposed WRS. The overall structure of the proposed agent-based model is based on the models used in Akhbari & Grigg25, Farhadi et al.26, Mirzaei and Zibaei27, and Mirzaei et al.28 studies. In this model, a set of main agents or players is initially identified, and then their interactions within the environment are defined27. In the first stage, the implementation of the proposed WRS is outlined, accompanied by a detailed explanation of the methodology. Farmers’ participation in this system and the percentage of their produced products stored in warehouses are then solicited. Pm is the moderating factor that represents motivational policies implemented by the government to encourage farmers’ participation in the mentioned plan. In the above equations, the first part of the right-hand side represents social pressures, while the second part (referring to Pm) represents the effects of motivational policies. Various motivational policies to encourage farmers’ participation in the WRS can be identified through sensitivity analysis of the simulation model. Pm can range from zero to Pm*, where zero indicates the absence of motivational policies, and Pm* represents the enforcement of strict regulations compelling farmers to accept participation in the plan. The required data for evaluating the feasibility of the WRS has been extracted through a questionnaire administered to top-ranking maize farmers in Iran. In this way, there were 78 top-ranking maize farmers in the database of the Iranian Agricultural Jihad Organization. Based on the Krejcie & Morgan table, the sample size was determined to be 65 farmers. A simple random sampling method was used to select the sample farmers from the target population.

Simulation results and sensitivity analysis of parameters

Simulation was conducted using a dynamic planning model with a limited time horizon, utilizing the initial values of the model parameters (Table 1). The findings in Table 2 revealed that, based on the initial parameter values, the most favorable scenario involves the farmer selling 30% of the maize harvest at the time of collection, while reserving the remaining 70% in on-farm storage. Notably, the farmer does not store any maize in warehouses. This suggests that, given the baseline conditions, it is not feasible for the farmers to adopt the WRS in order to maximize their utility. Hence, it becomes imperative to simulate alternative scenarios through sensitivity analysis of the model parameters, wherein the farmer engages in storing their produce in warehouses.

The parameters were classified into different groups based on their nature, including interest and discount rate parameters, price parameters, and storage parameters. The sensitivity analysis of the interest and discount rate parameters revealed that variations in the parameter related to the annualized subjective discount rate of farmer do not have the ability to alter the optimal decisions and behavior of farmers in the baseline conditions (Fig. 3a). However, changes in the annualized loan interest rate parameter can influence farmers’ behavior regarding the storage of maize in warehouse. For instance, at a 12% interest rate, it is concluded to adopt the proposed WRS (Fig. 3b). The study by Miranda et al.18, which employed a dynamic mathematical programming model to simulate a WRS, also confirmed the significant effect of reduced loan interest rate on increasing the storage of product in warehouse. In that study, it was concluded a decrease in loan interest rate lower than 10% would lead to an increase in the quantity of products stored in warehouses. The results of the studies by Gunawan et al.9 and Safo et al.13 indicated that improved access to loan leads to increased participation in the WSR.

The sensitivity analysis on the annualized subjective discount rate of farmer (a) and the annualized loan interest rate (b).

In the price parameter group, variations in the price growth parameter over the marketing season cannot lead to a change in farmers’ behavior towards storing maize in warehouse. However, an increase in the expected price growth over the marketing season will result in a decrease in product sales at harvest time and an increase in on-farm storage (Fig. 4a). Sensitivity analysis of the price volatility parameter over the non-harvest time showed that an increase in this parameter would lead to the storage of maize in warehouse. In such cases, price volatility of 60% will be the starting point for optimal behavioral changes by farmers and the adoption of the proposed WRS (Fig. 4b). It is worth noting that price volatility at the harvest time is not recognized as influential parameter for the use of the proposed WRS. However, an increase in price volatility at the harvest time will to some extent decrease the level of on-farm maize storage and increase the sale of maize at the harvest time (Fig. 4c). These findings have been corroborated by Miranda et al.18. In that study, it was determined that price growth does not affect the storage of products in warehouses; however, an increase in price volatility of more than 50% over the non-harvest time leads to an increase in product storage in warehouses.

The sensitivity analysis on the price growth over the marketing season (a), the price volatility over the non-harvest time (b), and the price volatility at the harvest time (c).

The results of the sensitivity analysis on the warehousing parameters showed that if the valuation coefficient for loan collateral is determined to be 85% of the value of stored maize, it will lead to a slight decrease in on-farm storage and a slight decrease in product sales at harvest time, resulting in the weak starting point for the adoption of the WRS (Fig. 5a). By reducing the warehouse cost ratio to the value of stored maize, farmers will increase storage in warehouse and decrease on-farm storage in order to maximize their utility. In this case, the proposed starting point for the use of the WRS will be at a cost ratio of 13% (Fig. 5b). In the study of Miranda et al.18 was concluded that an increase of more than 85% in the valuation coefficient for loan collateral can result in the storage of products in a warehouse.

The sensitivity analysis on the valuation coefficient for loan collateral (a), the warehouse cost ratio to the value of stored maize (b).

Generally, sensitivity analysis of the key parameters of the dynamic programming model demonstrated that the annualized loan interest rate, price volatility over the non-harvest time, collateral valuation coefficient, and the warehousing cost to product value ratio are influential factors affecting the implementation of the proposed WRS for maize in Iran (Table 3). In the study by Coulter & Onumah14, attention has also been drawn to government policies such as building trust in bankers, improving WRS regulations, addressing irregularities in warehouse receipt transactions, and encouraging smallholders to participate in the WRS in Africa. Tosun et al.30 concluded that an inefficient mercantile exchange market and high warehousing costs are among the main factors contributing to the lack of success of the WRS in Turkey. Gunawan et al.9 argued that lack of awareness, lack of loans and credits, and limited access to loans in Indonesia are influential factors in reducing the utilization of the WRS. According to the findings of Safo et al.13, the factors that primarily influenced success of the WRS in Ghana were the ease of accessing loans, the quantity of crop output, the presence of established buyers, and membership in farmer organizations.

Feasibility results

To evaluate the implementation of the proposed WRS, an agent-based model was utilized. The design of the proposed WRS indicated that farmers, buyers (maize traders), banks, mercantile exchange market and the warehouse (governmental institution) are the five main agents and players in the agent-based model. Furthermore, the simulation results of the proposed WRS demonstrated that four parameters, namely the annualized loan interest rate, price volatility over the non-harvest time, the valuation coefficient for loan collateral, and the ratio of warehouse cost to the value of stored maize, are recognized as influential policy components in the agent-based model.

The agent-based model was designed to initially examine the participation and non-participation of selected Iranian maize farmers in the proposed WRS in the year 2021. The policy conditions for maize farmers in the current situation in Iran were defined in the description of this system. Additionally, to validate the agent-based model used, we referred to the studies by Huang and Song31 and Huang et al.32. In the current situation in Iran in the year 2021, the annualized loan interest rate is set at 18%, price volatility over the non-harvest time is 40%, the valuation coefficient for loan collateral is set at 65%, and the ratio of warehouse cost to the value of stored maize is 13%. Under these decision variables, which reflect the current situation of the country in 2021, the sample farmers were asked whether they would participate in the proposed plan. About 83% of the farmers responded that they would not participate (54 out of 65 farmers). On the other hand, the results of the agent-based model with 500 replications, considering these decision variable data, showed that 86% of the farmers would not participate in the proposed plan (56 out of 65 farmers). The results also showed that all observational data fell within the 95% confidence interval of the agent-based model results (Table 4).

To implement this system successfully, it is crucial to convince farmers to participate. To achieve this, various motivational policies have been developed based on sensitivity analysis and identification of influential factors. These policies involve reducing the annualized loan interest rate provided to farmers in the proposed system, educating them about the mercantile exchange market to ensure fair pricing over the marketing season through the mercantile exchange company, increasing the valuation percentage of stored maize as collateral for loans offered by banks, and lowering warehousing costs through government intervention.

The desired utility functions of farmers were calculated to assess the social impact and the effectiveness of these motivational policies on farmers’ cooperative or non-cooperative behavior. The findings of Fig. 6 indicate that reducing the annualized loan interest rate, while keeping other parameters constant, leads to an increase in the number of cooperative farmers. For instance, when the interest rate is set at 8%, approximately all the farmers included in the study would participate in the proposed system. In Fig. 7, it is demonstrated that increasing the valuation percentage of stored maize as collateral for loans results in a higher participation rate among farmers. When the valuation rate reaches 85%, nearly all the sampled farmers in the proposed system would participate. Figure 8 reveals that by reducing warehousing costs, the number of participating farmers in the proposed scheme also increases. When the warehousing cost amounts to 2% of the stored maize’s value, nearly all the sampled farmers would participate. Lastly, based on the outcomes presented in Fig. 9, it can be concluded that higher price volatility over the marketing season led to greater farmer participation in the proposed system. When the price volatility reaches 60% over the marketing season, approximately all farmers in the proposed system would participate. In simpler terms, providing mercantile exchange market education and enhancing farmers’ knowledge in this domain can support them in selling their products at fair prices over the marketing season. These price disparities over the marketing season can, in turn, boost farmers’ engagement in the WRS.

Conclusion and recommendations

In this study, in order to address the problem of farmers’ liquidity over seasons other than harvest season and immediate sale of products at low prices, which results in very low profit margins and sometimes losses for farmers, the WRS was designed, simulated, and feasibility assessed based on the proposed system by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) in 2009, experiences of other countries, and Iran’s legal and institutional conditions. The results showed that it is possible to implement the two-tier WRS (the certificate of title and the certificate of pledge (red and blue receipts)) in Iran through the interaction between farmers, buyers (maize traders), banks, mercantile exchange market, and the warehouses. The simulation results of the designed WRS showed that under baseline conditions, the optimal amount of maize stored in the warehouse will be zero, and the farmer will sell 30% of the maize during the harvest season and store 70% on the farm. Sensitivity analysis of its parameters showed that four parameters, namely the annual interest rate on loans, the valuation rate of stored corn as collateral for loan, price volatility of the product over the marketing season, and the cost-to-value ratio of warehousing, can have a significant impact on the participation of maize producers in Iran with the designed WRS. The analysis of cooperative/non-cooperative behavior of maize producers with this proposed system showed that under current conditions (year 2021) with an interest rate of 18% on loan, a valuation rate of 65% as collateral for loan, a 40% price fluctuation of maize over the marketing season, and a warehousing cost-to-value ratio of about 13%, maximum participation of maize producers in this scheme cannot be expected. Findings indicate that assuming the stability of other parameters, reducing the interest rate on loan to 8% by banks can result in the participation of almost all maize farmers with the proposed scheme. Increasing the valuation rate of collateral to 85% by banks, without changing other parameters, can also lead to the approximate participation of all sample farmers. The government can also achieve the approximate participation of all maize farmers with the proposed scheme by reducing warehousing costs to about 2% of the product value, assuming stability in other parameters. Finally, the mercantile exchange company can independently guide farmers in selling their products over the marketing season at a suitable price by providing proper training and implementing appropriate regulations, in order to achieve the participation of all sample farmers with the proposed WRS, without changing other parameters. In general, considering the proposed solutions, it seems that by implementing incentive policies with minimal costs for the government, banks, and the mercantile exchange, the success of the proposed WRS can be achieved. Therefore, the structure of this study can also be used to evaluate the implementation of the WRS in other countries.
