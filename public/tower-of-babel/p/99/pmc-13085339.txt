Localized cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts as a distinct subtype of Caroli disease: a case report
Authors: Shoko Kato, Kenitiro Kaneko, Takuo Noda, Hiroshi Nouso, Yasuyuki Fukami, Tsuyoshi Sano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaroli disease encompasses multiple distinct entities. Background

Congenital bile duct dilatations involving only the intrahepatic ducts, first reported by Caroli et al. [1] in 1958, are classified as Todani type V choledochal cysts [2]. This condition is usually divided into Caroli syndrome and Caroli disease. Caroli syndrome is caused by a genetic abnormality of PKHD1 and is characterized by congenital hepatic fibrosis and autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease [3]. By contrast, Caroli disease inevitably involves several different entities because it includes all conditions that cause intrahepatic bile duct dilatation aside from Caroli syndrome. Guntz et al. [4] classified these conditions into five morphological types, with some presenting as single lesions and others as multiple lesions [3]. These conditions may be congenital or even acquired [5] and exhibit varied clinical presentations [6]. Among the different forms of Caroli disease, one group is morphologically unique: the dilatation is cystic and limited to the second- and third-order intrahepatic bile ducts, and the dilated ducts abruptly connect to normal-sized ducts on both the upstream and downstream sides. We report two representative cases of this subtype of Caroli disease along with a review of literature.

Case presentations

Case 1

Case 1 was a Japanese female neonate. At 27 weeks’ gestation, prenatal ultrasonography detected a cyst in the hepatic portal region of the fetus. The patient’s family history was unremarkable. After birth, ultrasonography and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography revealed localized cystic dilatation of the anterior section branch and its first upstream branches (Fig. 1). The dilated ducts abruptly connected to normal-sized bile ducts on both the upstream and downstream sides. No pancreaticobiliary maljunction was present. Follow-up ultrasonography revealed debris in the dilated duct at 2 days of age (Fig. 1a), and neonatal jaundice was present without elevation of the direct bilirubin level or transaminase levels. The debris disappeared at 14 months (Fig. 1b). At the time of writing this report, the child has remained asymptomatic, and the cyst has not grown for 10 years (Fig. 1c).

Case 2

Case 2 was a 3-month-old Japanese boy. Although no abnormality was detected during the prenatal period, ultrasonography of the infant at 3 months of age revealed a cyst in the anterior section. The patient’s family history was unremarkable. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography revealed cysts of the same shape and in the same location as in case 1, and no pancreaticobiliary maljunction was present (Fig. 2). At the time of writing this report, the child has remained asymptomatic, and the cyst has not grown for 9 years.

Discussion

The characteristic dilatation of an abrupt connection with normal-sized ducts, which was described above, is identical to that seen on the upstream side of the dilated intrahepatic ducts in type IV-A choledochal cysts (Fig. 3) [2, 12]. The localization markedly contrasts with that of secondary dilatation resulting from biliary stricture, which smoothly extends to the entirety of the upstream ducts and is often seen in other types of Caroli disease (Fig. 3). The fact that debris washed out in case 1 supports a lack of downstream stenoses. The limited cystic dilatation may be caused by factors other than stenoses. One possible mechanism is the occurrence of ductal plate malformation at the restricted areas [13], but this explanation remains speculative. Similar limited biliary dilatation has previously been reported at the common hepatic duct [14, 15]. While the mechanism might be the same, it is an extrahepatic dilatation that belongs to Todani type I.

Schematic representation of solitary intrahepatic biliary dilatation. Cases of solitary intrahepatic biliary dilatation can be classified into four types: cystic dilatation, diffuse dilatation, racemose dilatation, and diverticulum dilatation. Diffuse dilatation is continuous with the peripheral ducts and is often secondary to stenosis. Most cases that are reported as localized Caroli disease, including Caroli’s original case [1, 3, 8], correspond to the diffuse dilatation type. Racemose dilatation [9, 10] is a localized lesion observed in Caroli syndrome. Diverticulum dilatation [11] does not function as a drainage route for bile from any hepatic area. Ten other cases with the same intrahepatic dilatation limited to the second- and third-order intrahepatic bile ducts were identified in literature (PubMed and Igaku Chuo Zasshi; Table 1) [4, 16–24]. The lesions were described using various terms, including solitary intrahepatic biliary cyst or dilatation [16–19], solitary diverticular type V bile duct cyst [21, 23], isolated/single-lobe Caroli disease [4], solitary liver cysts [20], and Todani types IVC [22] and VII [24]. However, some of these terms encompassed conditions that were not entirely consistent with this concept, which may cause confusion. Therefore, we propose a new term for this distinct entity, localized cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts.

The definitive treatment for Caroli disease is resection [3, 6, 25]. Carcinoma and repeated cholangitis are absolute indications for such treatment [3]. In all ten previously reported cases of localized cystic dilatation, the patients underwent surgery with symptoms in seven, gallstones in two, and no symptoms in one. Seven patients were treated by cystic-duct resection [4, 16–20, 24]. Another patient underwent cystojejunostomy to avoid bile-duct injury due to the unclear communication site between the left hepatic duct and the cyst. Because the dilated ducts serve as the main bile drainage route from the corresponding hepatic area, hepatectomy is the preferred treatment. However, the lesions in the present cases were nonobstructive and contained sterile bile without causing symptoms, and they needed an anterior sectionectomy for treatment, which we deemed to be too invasive for asymptomatic infants. Thus, we opted to continue observation. In contrast, nine of the ten previously reported cases were symptomatic or had stones. Many asymptomatic cases may have gone undetected and unreported, and their natural course likely differs from other forms of Caroli disease, which are associated with intrahepatic lithiasis, septic complications, and cholangiocarcinoma [3]. The two patients described in our report have been observed for a decade, and both have remained asymptomatic. The need for surgical intervention can be more definitively determined as more information on the patients’ natural history becomes available.

This report has two limitations. First, histopathological examination was not possible because we chose to observe and follow-up for the benefit of the patients. Second, the observation period was relatively short. Further observations after two or three decades are warranted.

Conclusion

We have reported two cases of congenital localized cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts. The dilatation was confined to the second- and third-order intrahepatic bile ducts, with the dilated ducts abruptly connecting to normal-sized ducts both upstream and downstream. A review of the literature identified ten additional cases with similar features. This unique morphology supports the classification of these cases as a distinct entity separate from other forms of Caroli disease, namely, localized cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts.
