Thermal efficiency of a ferrofluid in a horizontal channel with non-uniform magnetic fields and curved heat sources: a computational study
Authors: As’ad Alizadeh, Joy Djuansjah, Mujtaba A Flayyih, Mohamed Shaban, Murtadha M Al-Zahiwat, Abdellatif M Sadeq, Husam Rajab, Khalil Hajlaoui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a comprehensive numerical investigation of the hydrothermal characteristics of water-Fe3O4 ferrofluid in a horizontal channel subjected to a non-uniform magnetic field and two curved wall-mounted heated obstacles. The channel is equipped with constant-temperature heat sources on the walls, a uniform inflow temperature, and a constant outflow pressure. The effects of three spatially varying magnetic field configurations on the flow dynamics and thermal behavior around the heated obstacle are analyzed using the finite element method (FEM). The findings reveal that increasing the magnetic field intensity significantly alters velocity streamlines and isotherms. Although greater magnetic field strength leads to only a marginal reduction in overall heat transfer (less than 1%), modifications to the shape of the heated obstacle markedly affect performance. Specifically, increasing the height of the protrusion enhances the core flow velocity and boosts the average Nusselt number by up to 31.3%. Conversely, elongating the heated region reduces wall-adjacent velocity and causes up to a 94.92% decrease in the average Nusselt number. These results highlight the critical role of obstacle geometry and magnetic field design in enhancing thermal performance in magnetically driven nanofluidic systems.Keywords: Channel flow, Curved heat source, Ferrofluid, Heat transfer, Magnetic fieldSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

In recent decades, the continuous intensification of thermal loads in modern engineering systems has driven sustained efforts toward the development of advanced working fluids capable of delivering enhanced heat transfer performance under constrained operating conditions1,2. Conventional fluids often fail to meet the combined requirements of thermal efficiency, controllability, and compact system design, particularly in applications involving high heat fluxes or limited heat exchange surfaces. Within this context, ferrohydrodynamic (FHD) nanofluids have emerged as a promising class of smart fluids, as they synergistically integrate the magnetic responsiveness of ferrofluids with the enhanced thermal transport characteristics of nanofluids3. This hybridization enables external magnetic fields to actively manipulate flow structures and thermal behavior, offering an additional degree of freedom for optimizing heat transfer processes beyond purely hydrodynamic or geometric control strategies4.

Ganguly et al.5 studied forced convection heat transfer of a ferrofluid in a channel subjected to a non-uniform magnetic field generated by a line dipole. By employing a magnetic field distribution consistent with Maxwell’s equations, they showed that thermomagnetic effects are highly localized and induce vortical structures near the cold wall. These vortices distort the thermal boundary layer, modify advective heat transport, and enhance heat transfer. The study also demonstrated that using multiple dipoles and optimizing their spacing further improves heat transfer performance, which strongly depends on the magnetic moment and spatial arrangement of the magnetic sources.

Ferrofluids consist of stable colloidal suspensions of nanoscale ferromagnetic particles dispersed within a carrier liquid, where surface treatments prevent particle agglomeration and sedimentation even under strong magnetic fields6,7. In parallel, nanofluids formed by dispersing metallic or non-metallic nanoparticles in conventional base fluids have been shown to exhibit improved effective thermal conductivity and convective heat transfer performance compared to their base fluids8. The combination of these two concepts yields ferrofluids whose thermal and hydrodynamic properties can be dynamically tuned through externally applied magnetic fields, thereby enabling localized flow acceleration, vortex generation, or boundary-layer disruption9. Owing to these unique features, ferrofluids have attracted significant interest across a broad spectrum of applications, including microfluidic devices, electronic cooling systems, intelligent heat exchangers, biomedical transport, targeted drug delivery, and magnetically assisted blood flow manipulation9,10. As a result, understanding the coupled thermal and hydrodynamic behavior of ferrofluids under non-uniform magnetic fields and in complex flow configurations has become a topic of both fundamental and practical importance.

Extensive numerical and experimental investigations have demonstrated that externally applied magnetic fields can profoundly modify flow structures, temperature distributions, and overall heat transfer rates in ferrofluid-based systems11. Early contributions by Tzirtzilakis and co-workers12,13 systematically examined the influence of magnetic and Hartmann numbers on vortex formation and thermal transport, revealing the central role of magnetic body forces in reshaping flow topology. Complementary studies by Aminfar et al.14 showed that non-uniform magnetic fields can enhance convective heat transfer in rectangular channels, albeit at the expense of increased pressure losses. Beyond purely thermal engineering applications, biomedical-oriented investigations by Nijm et al.15 explored the interaction between magnetic fields and physiological flows, highlighting the sensitivity of transport phenomena to magnetic forcing and reinforcing the need for accurate magneto thermal modeling.

From an engineering design perspective, it has become increasingly evident that heat transfer enhancement in ferrofluid flows is governed not only by magnetic field strength but also by geometric configuration, thermal boundary conditions, and the presence of internal flow disturbances. The introduction of internally heated obstacles within channels, acting as localized thermal sources, can generate complex flow patterns whose interaction with external magnetic fields leads to highly non-linear thermal responses. Variations in obstacle shape, placement, and thermal loading have been shown to significantly affect velocity fields, thermal boundary layer development, and local heat transfer coefficients. For instance, Sheikholeslami et al.16 investigated ferrofluid heat transfer under external magnetic field gradients and demonstrated that the Nusselt number increases with magnetic and Rayleigh numbers, while increasing Hartmann number suppresses convective transport due to magnetic damping.

Several studies have further expanded this understanding by addressing application-specific or multiphysics scenarios. Sharma et al.17 examined the FHD flow induced by a rotating disk in a porous medium while accounting for thermal radiation and internal heat generation, showing that magnetic interaction and permeability effects can substantially alter both momentum and heat transfer characteristics. In a related vein, Job et al.18 investigated unsteady convection of a hybrid nanofluid in a channel with semi-circular heaters and porous fins, concluding that magnetic effects and nanoparticle loading enhance heat transfer but invariably increase pressure drop. Dalvi et al.19 numerically investigated laminar ferrofluid flow under a three-dimensional non-uniform magnetic field generated by finite solenoids using the complete ferrohydrodynamic (FHD) model. Their results showed that spatial gradients of the magnetic field produce Kelvin force density, leading to distinct vortex structures, flow deformation, and altered flow trajectories. The study highlighted that magnetic-field-dependent viscosity and solenoid geometry play a crucial role in determining ferrofluid flow behavior and momentum transport characteristics.

Despite these advances, several limitations persist in the existing literature. Many studies focus on simplified geometries, uniform magnetic fields, or single obstacle configurations, thereby limiting their applicability to practical systems where magnetic fields are inherently non-uniform and flow passages often contain complex internal structures. Although Asadi et al.20 addressed laminar ferrofluid convection in a sinusoidal channel under a non-uniform magnetic field using a mixture model, their analysis was restricted to wall-induced geometric waviness rather than discrete internal obstacles. Similarly, Chattopadhyay21 explored non-Newtonian hybrid ferrofluid flow in the presence of cavities and obstacles under inclined magnetic fields, but the emphasis was placed on magnetic damping and non-Newtonian effects rather than on the synergistic role of obstacle geometry and localized magnetic field placement. Consequently, the combined influence of curved internal obstacles, localized heat sources, and strategically positioned non-uniform magnetic fields remains relatively underexplored, particularly in forced convection regimes relevant to compact thermal systems.

Motivated by these gaps, the present study investigates forced convection of a nanofluid in a channel subjected to non-uniform magnetic fields, with particular emphasis on the interaction between magnetic forces, flow dynamics, and heat transfer characteristics. A distinctive feature of this work is the examination of a curved wall-mounted obstacle incorporating an internal cavity, within which three magnetic field generators are positioned. This configuration enables localized magnetic actuation directly within the obstacle region, offering enhanced control over flow separation, thermal boundary layer disruption, and heat transfer augmentation. By systematically analyzing the coupled magneto-hydrodynamic and thermal behavior of the system, the study aims to provide deeper insight into the design of efficient and controllable thermal management solutions for applications such as electronic cooling, aerospace systems, and other technologies requiring precise heat regulation.

Problem definition

The subject of interest is a two-dimensional channel with specified input and output boundaries. This channel is characterized by a diameter (d) and length (L1). Curved obstacles are placed along the walls, as shown in Fig. 1. On the opposite side, three non-uniform MGFs are positioned above the obstacles. The dimensions of these obstacles are depicted in the figure. The curved obstacles are positioned to induce fluid flow and influence the thermal effects of ferrofluid flow along the walls. All dipoles are at a distance of 0.01 from the wall. The working fluid is a ferrofluid, whose properties are listed in Table 1. The fluid is assumed to be Newtonian and incompressible. At the inlet, the flow has a constant velocity and a specified cold temperature, passing through the channel and cooling the obstacles. At the outlet, the pressure outlet boundary condition is imposed. The no-slip condition is applied on the walls and obstacles. A constant temperature boundary condition is also applied to these obstacles, creating a thermal gradient and facilitating heat transfer in the fluid.

Numerical method and grid independency

In this study, the Finite Element Method (FEM) is employed within the COMSOL Multiphysics software environment to numerically solve the coupled system of governing equations. A robust multiphysics approach is adopted to accurately simulate the interaction between the ferrofluid flow, heat transfer, and the applied Magnetic Gradient Fields (MGFs). The numerical procedure involves the simultaneous solution of the continuity, Navier-Stokes, and Energy equations coupled with Maxwell’s equations. A distinguishing feature of this numerical implementation is the treatment of the magnetization vector; unlike conventional studies that use a linear approximation, the current model utilizes the complete nonlinear Langevin function to rigorously calculate the magnetization and the resulting Kelvin body force throughout the domain. For mesh independence assessment, the ferrofluid flow between two plates with a length-to-height ratio of 20, φ = 2%, = 10 nm, and Reynolds number of 100 was investigated under the influence of the maximum MGF intensity. The average Nusselt number was evaluated for five different mesh sizes, and the results are presented in Table 2. The difference in results between the last two cases was negligible; therefore, the fourth case, with a mesh size of 80 × 4000, was selected as the optimal mesh for further calculations.

Results and discussion

In this section, various parameters are discussed. First, the effect of mnf (the magnetic number arising from FHD for the base fluid, ) is investigated. Then, the geometrical parameters of the constant temperature source (height and length) are investigated.

The influence of mnf on the flow streamlines and temperature contours is illustrated in Fig. 3. An increase in mnf enhances the Kelvin force acting on the fluid. In a non-uniform MGF, spatial variations in the magnetic field strength generate velocity gradients and disturb the flow, resulting in more pronounced vortices. These magnetic forces may induce lateral displacement and promote vortex formation. Near the inlet, the effect of the MGF is less significant due to the higher fluid inertia, whereas in the vicinity of the heat source, the magnetic field strongly influences the flow because of the induced curvature. In the middle section of the channel, the relatively higher flow velocity reduces the influence of the MGF. Overall, increasing mnf intensifies the Kelvin forces and consequently strengthens vortex structures. As the fluid responds to these forces, Joule heating associated with eddy currents modifies the temperature distribution, producing localized temperature variations and steeper gradients. Consequently, the isotherms become denser and more compressed, indicating enhanced heat transfer. In summary, the variations in streamlines and temperature contours under a non-uniform MGF arise from changes in magnetic field strength, Kelvin forces, and Joule heating. Increasing mnf amplifies these effects and significantly alters both the flow structure and temperature distribution within the channel.

Effects of variations in mnf on the streamlines and isotherms.

In Fig. 4, the effects of varying the heat-source length (L) on the flow streamlines and temperature contours are shown, while keeping its curvature constant. As L increases, the thermal boundary layer along the solid surface extends, causing the fluid temperature to transition more gradually from the source temperature to the bulk fluid temperature over a longer distance. The enlarged heated region also influences the velocity boundary layer by expanding the zone in which velocity gradients—and consequently shear forces—are significant near the surface. The streamlines demonstrate that a longer heat source increases the interaction between the flow and the heated surface, resulting in larger and more elongated vortices downstream of the upstream edge of the source. The isotherm plots indicate that increasing L enhances heat transfer to the fluid and promotes a more uniform temperature distribution. These variations in flow and temperature fields arise primarily from the coupled development of the thermal and velocity boundary layers as L increases. Overall, L determines the distance over which the fluid is heated by the source, directly affecting the temperature gradients and the overall temperature distribution within the channel. By extending the heat source, the fluid has more opportunity to approach thermal equilibrium with the heated surface, which is reflected in both the streamline patterns and the temperature profiles.

Effects of variations in L on the streamlines and isotherms.

The influence of the height of the protruding region (W) on the flow streamlines and temperature contours is illustrated in Fig. 5. As W increases, the central passage becomes narrower, leading to a higher flow velocity. This increase in velocity reduces the influence of the Kelvin force in the central region. Moreover, a larger W promotes the formation of stronger and more pronounced vortices downstream of the protrusion, significantly affecting the overall flow structure. These vortices result from both the geometric modification caused by increasing W and the characteristics of the MGF in that region. An increase in W also affects the fluid’s approach to thermal equilibrium with the heated surface, thereby modifying the temperature gradients and the distribution of temperature contours. In addition, as W increases, the streamlines on the right side of the protrusion become denser in the outlet region. Overall, the higher flow velocity leads to steeper temperature gradients, denser streamline patterns, and enhanced heat transfer.

Effects of variations in on the streamlines and isotherms.

As shown in Fig. 6(a), the dimensionless velocity increases significantly with an increase in W. This behavior is governed by the principle of mass conservation; as the obstacle height increases, the cross-sectional area of the channel decreases, forcing the fluid to accelerate to maintain a constant mass flow rate. In the central core region, where the velocity is highest, the profile remains smooth. Physically, this indicates that the inertial forces in the core dominate the local magnetic body forces. Consequently, the relative impact of the Kelvin force diminishes in high velocity zones, preventing significant distortion of the velocity profile. Figure 6(b) reveals that increasing the magnetic moment leads to distinct distortions and localized peaks in the velocity profile, particularly near the channel walls. These distortions arise from the vorticity generation mechanism induced by the Kelvin force. Since the magnetization is temperature-dependent, a non-conservative force field is generated where the temperature gradient (∇T) and magnetic field gradient (∇H) are misaligned. This “magnetic torque” locally accelerates the fluid and generates vortices, creating the observed breaks or wiggles in the velocity profile. Figure 6c demonstrates that increasing the length of the heat source (L) slightly reduces the peak velocity near the walls while modifying the core flow. A longer obstacle extends the surface area subject to the no-slip condition, thereby promoting the growth of the hydrodynamic boundary layer and viscous dissipation. This extension stabilizes the flow and suppresses the local velocity near the wall. The slight velocity increase in the channel center is a compensatory effect to satisfy continuity, balancing the reduced momentum in the thickened boundary layers near the extended obstacle.

Dimensionless velocity profile variations in terms of (a) W, (b) m, and (c) L.

Figure 7(a) shows that increasing W leads to a steeper dimensionless temperature gradient near the wall. Physically, the constriction of the channel accelerates the flow (due to mass conservation), which significantly thins the thermal boundary layer. A thinner boundary layer reduces the thermal resistance, thereby enhancing the temperature gradient at the wall. In the core region, as velocity increases, the high inertial momentum dominates the flow, suppressing the relative influence of the Kelvin force. Consequently, the temperature profile becomes smoother, as the strong forced convection overcomes the local magnetic perturbations that would otherwise distort the thermal field. Figure 7(b) reveals that as the magnetic number increases, the temperature profile exhibits distinct distortions and a steeper gradient near the wall. This is directly attributed to the Kelvin force-induced mixing. As discussed regarding the velocity profiles, the interaction between the temperature-dependent magnetization and the magnetic field gradient generates localized vorticity. These vortices disrupt the thermal boundary layer, enhancing the mixing of cooler bulk fluid with the warmer fluid near the wall. This “magnetic agitation” effectively steepens the temperature slope, indicating enhanced heat transfer. While Joule heating is present, the dominant mechanism for the profile distortion here is the kinematic alteration of the flow field by the Kelvin force. Figure 7c indicates that increasing the heat source length (L) reduces the magnitude of the temperature gradient. This behavior is governed by the development of the thermal boundary layer. As the fluid flows over a longer heated surface, the thermal boundary layer thickens, and the fluid temperature gradually rises, approaching the wall temperature. This reduction in the temperature difference between the wall and the adjacent fluid decreases the local temperature gradient. Unlike the geometric constriction in Fig. 7(a), the flow here is more stable; the reduction in near-wall velocity (due to the no slip condition over a longer surface) shifts the heat transfer mechanism closer to conduction, resulting in a warmer fluid region near the wall but a lower rate of heat transfer (lower gradient).

Dimensionless temperature profile variations in terms of (a) W, (b) mnf, and (c) L.

Figures 8(a) and 8(b) present the dimensionless velocity and temperature profiles along the channel centerline for various magnetic numbers. As depicted in Fig. 8(a), increasing mnf significantly alters the flow dynamics due to the intensification of the Kelvin force. In the vicinity of the first magnetic field generator (near the inlet), a notable reduction in centerline velocity is observed as mnf increases. Physically, although the converging geometry in this region naturally tends to accelerate the flow, the opposing Kelvin force acts as a magnetic brake, overriding the geometric effect and decelerating the fluid. Conversely, in the region of the third magnetic field generator (downstream of the obstacle), the velocity increases with rising mnf. Here, the strong magnetic field enhances the vorticity generation in the wake region. These intensified vortices act as a hydrodynamic blockage, effectively narrowing the passage for the main flow stream and forcing the core fluid to accelerate to satisfy mass conservation. Figure 8(b) demonstrates that the dimensionless temperature along the centerline rises with increasing mnf. This thermal behavior is directly linked to the flow modifications discussed above. The magnetic retardation of the flow increases the fluid’s residence time within the heated channel, allowing for greater heat absorption from the walls. Furthermore, the enhanced mixing induced by the Kelvin force driven vortices promotes the transport of thermal energy from the hot walls toward the channel center (transverse advection). While Joule heating contributes to the total energy balance, the dominant factor for the centerline temperature rise is the suppression of axial convective cooling (due to lower velocity) and the enhanced radial heat transport.

Figure 9(a) and (b) illustrate the dimensionless velocity and temperature profiles along the channel centerline for different obstacle heights (W). As shown in Fig. 9(a), the centerline velocity increases substantially as the obstacle height (W) increases. This behavior is a direct consequence of the conservation of mass; as the obstacle creates a greater blockage, the effective cross-sectional area of the channel decreases, forcing the fluid to accelerate in the core region to maintain the flow rate. Additionally, the velocity profiles exhibit distinct local distortions or “fluctuations” in regions influenced by the non-uniform magnetic field generators (MGFs). These distortions are significantly more pronounced for larger values of W. This phenomenon arises from the coupling between hydrodynamic instabilities and the Kelvin force. Larger obstacles generate stronger downstream vortices (wake region). When these shedding vortices pass through the magnetic field zones, the interaction between the vorticity field and the magnetic body force amplifies the local flow disturbances, resulting in the observed fluctuations in the centerline velocity. Figure 9(b) indicates that the dimensionless temperature along the centerline rises as W increases. This trend is primarily governed by geometric proximity and enhanced mixing. As the obstacle height increases, the tip of the heated source physically approaches the channel centerline. Consequently, the distance over which heat must diffuse to reach the center is reduced, leading to a higher local temperature. Furthermore, the larger obstacles induce a more extensive recirculation zone (wake) downstream. This enhanced flow mixing facilitates the advection of thermal energy from the heated wall into the bulk fluid, thereby elevating the temperature along the channel length. The highest temperature rise observed for the largest W confirms that the constriction effectively creates a hotter core flow due to the combined effects of reduced diffusion distance and vigorous wake mixing.

The results in Table 4 indicate that the Nusselt number variations arise from the combined effects of magnetic forces, flow structure, and thermal boundary‑layer development. Increasing the dipole moment intensifies magnetic field gradients, which suppress near‑wall velocities, thicken the thermal boundary layer, and consequently reduce the wall temperature gradient, leading to a lower Nusselt number. The influence of the protrusion height is non‑monotonic due to changes in vortex formation and flow mixing. Moderate geometric disturbance enhances shear and heat transfer, whereas stable recirculation zones at intermediate heights trap warm fluid and weaken convection. At larger heights, vortex reorganization improves mixing and restores heat transfer. The most pronounced reduction in the Nusselt number is observed with increasing heat‑source length, as prolonged wall contact promotes thermal equilibrium between the fluid and the wall, shifts the regime toward conduction dominance, and significantly weakens convective heat transfer.

Conclusion

In the present study, a comprehensive numerical investigation was conducted to analyze forced convection of a water-Fe₃O₄ ferrofluid flowing through a channel equipped with two curved wall-mounted obstacles and subjected to three non-uniform magnetic field generators (MGFs). The MGFs were arranged with equal spacing and positioned at a constant distance from the obstacles, enabling a systematic assessment of the coupled effects of magnetic forcing and geometric parameters on the hydrodynamic and thermal behavior of the ferrofluid.

The results demonstrate that the presence of non-uniform magnetic fields leads to a noticeable reduction in fluid velocity throughout the channel. This behavior is primarily attributed to the magnetic body force acting on the ferrofluid, which introduces an additional resistive mechanism against the flow motion. The magnetic damping effect becomes particularly pronounced in regions adjacent to the obstacles and the MGFs, highlighting the dominant role of magnetic forces in modifying the momentum balance and suppressing flow acceleration.

The influence of the magnetic interaction parameter, mnf, on heat transfer performance was found to be unfavorable. Increasing mnf results in a reduction of the average Nusselt number, indicating that stronger magnetic interactions weaken convective heat transfer. This reduction can be explained by the suppression of velocity gradients and flow mixing, which leads to the thickening of the thermal boundary layer and a decrease in the temperature gradient at the heated surfaces. These findings emphasize that excessive magnetic forcing, although beneficial for flow control, may deteriorate thermal performance if not properly optimized. In contrast, the geometric parameter W plays a constructive role in enhancing heat transfer. Increasing W leads to higher fluid velocity in the central region of the channel, which promotes stronger fluid mixing and disrupts the thermal boundary layer. As a result, increasing W up to 0.4 yields a maximum enhancement of 31.3% in the average Nusselt number, while the minimum heat transfer rate is observed at W = 0. This trend underscores the importance of obstacle-related geometric tuning as an effective passive strategy for heat transfer augmentation. On the other hand, increasing the parameter L has a strongly adverse impact on thermal performance. The results reveal that a rise in L causes a dramatic reduction of up to 94.92% in the average Nusselt number. This significant deterioration is associated with excessive thermal boundary layer development and reduced convective transport near the channel walls, which outweigh the potential benefits of obstacle-induced flow disturbance.
