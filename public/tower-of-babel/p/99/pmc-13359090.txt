Rise of the viral giants: common themes underlying genome gigantism in eukaryotic viruses and bacteriophages
Authors: Sofia Rigou, Joud Aldaroub, Matthias Horn, Frank O Aylward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent research has shown that the biosphere harbors an immense diversity of large and complex DNA viruses that infect a wide range of hosts. This is best exemplified by giant viruses of eukaryotes (phylum Nucleocytoviricota) and large phages, also referred to as jumbo phages, that infect bacteria (class Caudoviricetes). Both groups independently evolved large genomes and complex infection strategies, and recent research reveals striking convergences between these viral groups despite their distinct evolutionary origins. Virus factories and phage nuclei separate transcription from translation, mediate spatiotemporal regulation of infection, and involve elaborate interactions with host cytoskeletal systems. Moreover, both viral groups harbor an expanded repertoire of genes acquired from cellular hosts, including transcription and translation-related genes and metabolic enzymes, suggesting convergent strategies to optimize host takeover. Additionally, both lineages are embedded in networks of hyperparasitic interactions, including viral parasites and phage satellites, as well as other selfish genetic elements such as transposons and homing endonucleases, which may, in some cases, facilitate genome innovation and provide fitness benefits. The repeated emergence of genome gigantism across diverse viral clades within both the Nucleocytoviricota and Caudoviricetes suggests that selective pressures favoring gene gain, potentially through a genomic accordion mechanism, are widespread. Yet, genome expansions also harbor many features that are lineage-specific and highly context-dependent. This review explores the mechanistic, functional, and evolutionary parallels that shape the biology of genome gigantism in the virosphere.

Introduction

Although viruses have historically been viewed as simple and diminutive entities, research in the last few decades has revealed that these obligate parasites can achieve striking levels of complexity [1]. Starting with the discovery of Mimivirus in 2003, giant viruses have shown that viruses can be large enough to be viewed under a light microscope and harbor double-stranded DNA genomes in the million base-pair range [2]. Far from representing only a small set of viruses that infect ameba, subsequent culture-dependent and metagenome-driven studies revealed that viruses in the phylum Nucleocytoviricota are widespread in a variety of environments across the planet and infect hosts across the eukaryotic tree of life [3,4]. In parallel, research in prokaryotic viruses has revealed that many bacteriophages (i.e. phages, class Caudoviricetes) also harbor large genomes and use complex mechanisms to hijack their cellular hosts during infection. Some of the largest phages were initially referred to as ‘jumbo’ phages and defined as any phage having a genome > 200 kbp in length, but subsequent work has challenged the utility of this term by showing that phage genome sizes vary along a continuum up to > 700 kbp in length and that large phages belong to numerous distinct lineages [5,6]. Similar to giant viruses, metagenomic surveys have shown that large phages are widespread in the biosphere and infect a wide range of hosts, including Actinomycetes, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Cyanobacteria [6,7].

Intriguingly, genome gigantism has evolved multiple times independently in both nucleocytoviruses and bacteriophages. In the Nucleocytoviricota, genomes > 500 kbp in length have evolved separately in three lineages (Imitervirales, Pimascovirales, and the pandoraviruses) [8,9]. The evolutionary links between clades of large phages are harder to disentangle, but a recent large-scale comparative genomic analysis suggested that genomes > 200 kbp in length evolved independently up to 19 times [10]. The multiple, independent origins of large genomes in both nucleocytoviruses and phages suggest that evolutionary drivers for genome gigantism are relatively common in the virosphere. Larger viruses (both in terms of virion size and genome length) would be predicted to have fewer progeny compared to smaller viruses due to the increased demands on genome replication and virion morphogenesis, but it has been hypothesized that these disadvantages are potentially offset by increased infection efficiency, broader host range, and lower decay rates [11]. Regardless of the underlying evolutionary tradeoffs, large viruses of both eukaryotes and prokaryotes harbor several intriguing similarities that suggest that they have arrived convergently onto similar infection strategies, such as distinct structures for subcellular compartmentalization within host cells and the acquisition of similar cellular machinery from their hosts (Table 1). Here, we discuss several of the similarities between giant viruses and large phages, and provide a perspective on the possible common drivers of gigantism.

The virocells

Viral infection transforms the host cell into a virocell, which corresponds to the reproductive or ‘living stage’ of the virus [17]. Both nucleocytoviruses and Chimalliviridae (a family of large phages) have evolved complex infection strategies that involve the compartmentalization of viral replication (Figure 1). This is not unique to these viral groups; several viruses rely on a virus-derived organelle to concentrate elements that are important for viral replication. These ‘virus factories’ (also called viroplasms) have the advantage of increasing viral replication efficiency and can help protect against the host immune system [18]. The viral factories of large and giant viruses are noteworthy for the diversity of primarily viral proteins present within them [19].

Viral factories can be formed by phase separation for viruses in the orders Mononegavirales, Reovirales, Rowavirales, and Herpesvirales [18]. In this process, membrane-less compartments emerge due to the segregation of molecules with different physico-chemical properties. Very recently, it has been shown that the viral factories across all orders of Nucleocytoviricota are also supported by phase-separation with some evidence for liquid properties [20]. The identification of proteins that could drive this process with intrinsically disordered regions with a shared molecular grammar supports a common ancestry of the cytoplasmic viral factories of Nucleocytoviricota that have further specialized using different strategies.

In large phages, the ‘phage nucleus’ is a structure formed by members of the Chimalliviridae [21] during infection that is analogous to the virus factory of nucleocytoviruses [22]. Virus factories of nucleocytoviruses and “phage nuclei” of chimalliviruses are structurally distinct and bear no shared evolutionary history, and therefore represent an example of convergent evolution of unique structures used for subcellular compartmentalization in these two viral lineages. Phage nuclei are composed of a proteinaceous shell orchestrated by tubulin [23], although a lipid-based vesicular compartment is involved in early infection [24,25]. In contrast to nucleocytoviruses, the phage nucleus does not emerge from phase separation but is formed by a protein layer consisting of chimallin (ChmA) [26]. A phage-encoded tubulin homolog, PhuZ, assembles dynamically unstable filaments that polymerize in the host cell into a bipolar spindle from both cell poles toward the center upon infection. It has two major roles: spatiotemporal central positioning of the phage nucleus, and treadmilling of the newly synthesized capsids [27,28]. Members of the Mimiviridae and other lineages in the Nucleocytoviricota interact with the host cytoskeleton during infection by either down-regulating cytoskeleton remodeling genes at early time points [29], cleavage of tubulin [30], or with virally-encoded myosin, kinesins, proteases etc. [31], but the exact function of those processes remains to be studied.

Although it is structurally different than the eukaryotic nucleus, the phage nucleus has many similar functions, including separation of transcription from translation, export of mRNA, selective import of proteins, and shielding of the phage DNA from host defenses such as DNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas1 systems and cytoplasmic nucleases [32]. Briefly, after the structure has formed, genome replication and transcription occur within the phage nucleus. Transcription of early genes is performed by a virion-packaged multisubunit phage RNA polymerase, and subsequent transcripts are produced by a second phage-encoded RNA polymerase [33]. Translation takes place in the bacterial cytoplasm, producing viral capsids, which are then transported to the periphery of the phage nucleus for DNA packaging [34,35].

The viral factory of Mimivirus also separates translation from replication and transcription, but has further layering: It has itself two phases with an outer layer and an inner layer separating virion assembly from genome replication at the interphase and from viral transcription, respectively [20], but this is still under investigation. Ribosomes are excluded from the viral factory but can be found at its periphery. Cellular localization of viral mRNA has confirmed that translation occurs at the periphery of the viral factory of Mimivirus [36].

Conversely, Noumeavirus and other members of the Pimascovirales have a single-phased viral factory with a scaffold protein that shares properties with the scaffold protein of Mimivirus’ outer layer (the ability to form phase-separation and the disordered region grammar) and inner layer (the ability to bind DNA) [20]. Most probably, replication, transcription, and virion assembly occur in that single-phase viral factory.

While viral factories do not need a large viral genome to support them, the viral factories of Nucleocytoviricota and of large phages are astonishing for their complexity and presumably a high number of processes occurring within them. Despite this complexity, the phage nucleus of viruses in the Chimalliviridae may still largely rely on the host, as 94 genes in Serratia sp. were shown to be necessary for successful phage infection [37]. Nevertheless, despite the broad similarities in how virus factories and phage nuclei are used to compartmentalize viral activities during infection, further work will be needed to assess the degree of functional convergence between these structures.

Cellular genes in viral genomes

Nucleocytoviruses and large phages have acquired a wide range of genes from cellular organisms during their evolutionary history. These include genes involved in translation, central carbon metabolism, cell structure, and nutrient turnover and transport, among other functions [13,38]. Although viruses across a range of genome sizes have acquired diverse cellular genes, the genomes of large DNA viruses encode a particularly impressive complement of them. In some cases, both nucleocytoviruses and large phages have convergently acquired homologous genes or genes involved in the same metabolic process, suggesting that they may be using similar strategies to hijack host cells during infection.

Often, nucleocytoviruses have a substantially different codon usage than their host [39], and virally-encoded tRNAs are one way to adapt to their host’s translational apparatus. tRNAs are found in a range of nucleocytoviruses [40] and have also been noted in disparate lineages of phages, presumably to sustain translation as host machinery degrades during infection [41]. In both phages and Nucleocytoviricota, the number of tRNAs in a genome is slightly correlated (Pearson’s r=0.5) to its size, without being exclusive to large genomes [40,42]. Whether viral tRNAs play a role in translation or immune evasion [43] in large phages is unknown, and further work is required to study a potential correlation to genome size. Giant viruses and large phages also code for tRNA-aminoacyl synthetases; they have been acquired several times in the history of Nucleocytoviricota, mostly from eukaryotes, though some point toward a more ancient acquisition [44], while in phages these have been acquired from a wide range of bacterial hosts [6].

In a Chimalliviridae, at least one unknown viral protein has been shown to interact with the host ribosomes rapidly after infection [45]. Poxviruses, nucleocytoviruses with comparatively “small” genomes and virus particles, do not encode a translational machinery themselves. They would rather manipulate ribosomal conformation through changes in host translation factors expression. This results in a selective expression of certain viral genes at later timepoints despite a host shutoff in translation [46]. This highlights the distinct approaches that nucleocytoviruses use for directing the translation of their proteins. Interestingly, translation-related genes are not part of the core genomes of giant viruses and have been shown not to be essential in Pandoravirus using gene knock-outs [47]. In Mimivirus, the translation initiation factor 4a is also non-essential but comes with a potential fitness gain [48].

Genes involved in energy or central carbon metabolism are commonly found in a range of viruses. These are typically called auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) in phages or simply virologs (viral homologs) in eukaryotic viruses. Marine phages that infect cyanobacteria have been shown to encode a wide range of AMGs involved in photosynthesis, glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [49]. In well-studied phages that infect the marine cyanobacterium Prochlorococcus, viral-encoded AMGs involved in photosynthesis and carbon metabolism enhance photosynthetic efficiency and carbon fixation, effectively sustaining host metabolism during infection to maximize viral yield [50,51]. For nucleocytoviruses, it is within the Imitervirales and Algavirales that most energy and central carbon metabolism genes are found [52,53]. Functional predictions suggest that these enzymes are potentially involved in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, fermentation, and photosynthesis [13]. Viral-encoded rhodopsins are also quite common and are already being studied experimentally to confirm their function as ion pumps, though it remains unclear whether they are involved in energy homeostasis or other processes such as phototaxis or even superinfection exclusion [54,55].

To increase nucleotide synthesis in their host, many large phages encode a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) salvage pathway that is expressed during the early period of infection. This pathway enhances DNA precursor synthesis through reductive reactions that use NADH/NADPH as the electron donor and NAD+ for ADP-ribosylation of proteins involved in transcribing and translating the phage genome [56]. The availability of nucleotides is also a key factor in nucleocytovirus infections, as ribonucleotide reductases and thymidylate synthases are widespread among different viral families and environments [57,58]. Those enzymes are considered as rate-limiting factors for deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate (and thus DNA) synthesis [59] and thymidine biosynthesis, respectively.

In addition to genes acquired from their hosts, both nucleocytoviruses and large phages are known for encoding many genes with unknown functions and no detectable homologs (ORFans). Even after searching for remote homology with highly sensitive search tools, half of the proteome of viruses within the Pithoviridae was still considered as ORFans [60]. Around 70% of the predicted proteome of newly isolated large phages infecting acidobacteria could not be linked to a function [61]. Taken together, this hints that with genome expansion comes functions to better hijack the cell, but also new viral functions that serve as unknown cellular interactors or in specific viral processes. Future research would need to quantitatively study the functions that are shared and could support larger genomes, if they exist, to go beyond what is now simple observations in specific viral lineages. Functional studies on both known and unknown genes will help to differentiate sporadic acquisition of similar genes from possible convergent evolution.

Hyperparasitism

Viruses of both eukaryotes and prokaryotes are, in turn, parasitized by an array of ‘hyperparasitic’ selfish genetic elements that include satellite viruses, transposons, and plasmid-like elements called transpovirons. Satellite viruses associated with nucleocytoviruses, that is, virophages, are widespread in protist genomes and environmental metagenomes [2,62]. They hijack the viral factory and may act as a kind of inducible antiviral defense against giant viruses. Although the term virophage has sometimes been used to refer specifically to viruses of the class Maveriviricetes, recent work has proposed that this term should be repurposed to refer to any virus with a hyperparasitic interaction with a giant virus [63]. Detailed examination of virophages integrated into the genomes of the marine flagellate Cafeteria burkhardae has revealed that these elements are often reactivated in a stochastic and inefficient manner upon giant virus infection, though still with a protective effect [64]. Recent work has found polinton-like viruses invading the occlusion bodies of poxviruses [65], indicating that these elements are also linked with metazoan-infecting viruses within the Nucleocytovirocota.

Hyperparasitic elements acting on phages are typically called ‘phage satellites’ because they often lack their own capsid. They are widespread in bacterial genomes and likely play an important role in shaping host defense and phage as well as host virulence [66]. It remains unclear whether phage satellites become more common as the genome size of helper phages increases; the vibriophage ICP1 (~130 kbp) is inhibited by a family of related satellites, and a hyperparasitic virus that encodes its own encapsidation machinery was recently described for the actinophage MindFlayer (also ~130 kbp) [66,67]. Moreover, a recent report describes an entire genomic island that is transferred by a large Pseudomonas phage (~265 kbp) and may act as a novel phage satellite [68]. Only a few cultivated virus hyperparasitic systems are available for in-depth study, but those interactions are an important avenue of future research that will likely uncover intriguing details of these multi-partite parasitic relationships [29,69].

In addition to hyperparasitic viruses and satellites, many nucleocytovirus and phage genomes house an array of other selfish genetic elements. Plasmid-like 7 kb DNA elements, called transpovirons, have been found as either free elements in the virion or integrated in the genome [70]. These can insert into both mimivirus and virophage genomes and appear to represent an interesting case of viral commensalism because they depend on giant virus replication, without affecting its infectious cycle [29,71]. Pithovirus genomes encode numerous repeats, likely miniature inverted transposable elements (MITEs), that instigate genomic rearrangements and gene capture, and may thus have contributed to their divergence from other related lineages [72]. Several RNA-guided Fanzor-type transposases have also been found in a range of nucleocytoviruses [73]. Importantly, a giant virus that can integrate into the genome of its green algal host encodes at least 12 Fanzor elements and a retrotransposon that show evidence of recent mobility in the host genome [74], suggesting that integrative giant viruses play a role in the proliferation of selfish genetic elements in eukaryotes and vice-versa, as for the MITEs found in a Pandoravirus genome [75]. Several large phages also encode selfish genetic elements; in particular, homing endonucleases are widespread selfish elements that likely impact phage evolution [76]. Recent work on the jumbo phage PhiPA3 has shown that it encodes a homing endonuclease that can provide a competitive advantage by disrupting co-infecting phages [77]. This work shows that selfish genetic elements may provide fitness benefits in the appropriate context and likely play an important role in mediating virus–virus interactions.

Conclusion

Despite the disparate evolutionary origins of viruses within the Nucleocytoviricota and Caudoviricetes, large genomes and similar infection strategies have evolved multiple times independently within these groups of viruses. The examples of evolutionary convergence within these groups hint at common drivers that may underpin the evolution of genome complexity in some scenarios, though in many cases it seems that genome expansions have occurred along unique paths. For example, complex structures to compartmentalize certain processes during infection and avoid host defenses have evolved in both nucleocytoviruses and the phage family Chimalliviridae. Most lineages of large phages, however, appear to lack the machinery needed to make these structures, suggesting that their genomes have expanded due to other evolutionary drivers. Similarly, many large viruses of both eukaryotes and prokaryotes have convergently acquired genes involved in translation, energy metabolism, and myriad other cellular processes, which may partly explain the genome expansions in these lineages, although many other large viruses lack these genes altogether. Lastly, for nucleocytoviruses, the interaction with bacteria competing for the intracellular niche might contribute to genome expansion [78–80], but this would not be a driver of gigantism in phages. Hence, although many parallels exist between nucleocytoviruses and large phages, genome expansions within these groups appear to be highly context-dependent and follow their own unique evolutionary trajectories.

Perhaps paradoxically, the distinctiveness of many lineages of large DNA viruses may be due to the expanded number of evolutionary paths made available when genomes are large. For example, in poxviruses, rapid genome expansions that take place over short timescales are often driven by ‘genome accordions’ in which viral gene families can expand and contract rapidly as part of viral adaptation to counteract host defenses [81]. Due to the highly context-dependent interactions between different host–virus pairs, accordion-like dynamics would be expected to lead to distinct patterns of genome expansion in different viral lineages. Indeed, this mode of evolution appears to be a general pattern among nucleocytoviruses, in which genome innovations are lineage-specific and largely driven by either gene duplications, lateral gene transfer, or even de novo gene emergence [82–85]. Large phages also undergo accordion-like genome expansions, and their highly recombinogenic genomes potentially promote rapid exchange of anti-defense systems and receptor switching [86,87], leading to complex host–virus arms races. In this sense, larger genomes can be viewed as better able to adapt to new host defenses — or even entirely new hosts — more readily than smaller viruses. These dynamics may lead to an overall trend of increasing genome size over longer timescales; indeed, it has been shown that gene duplication is an important driver of genome size expansion in some nucleocytoviruses [82], and this may represent the long-term consequences of repeated accordion-like gene family amplifications.

Further experimental studies of viral infection programs in large DNA viruses of prokaryotes and eukaryotes will be critical to evaluate common trends in genome gigantism in the virosphere. Many unique infection programs and mechanisms for overcoming host defenses will almost certainly be discovered, as well as other examples of curious convergence between disparate viral lineages. It has recently been suggested that large extrachromosomal ‘Borg’ elements that reach lengths > 1 Mbp represent viruses of archaea [88]; Borgs encode several metabolic genes, have a high repeat content, and bear other similarities to large phages and nucleocyto-viruses. If these elements represent viruses, it would offer another unique opportunity to examine the evolutionary paths that lead to genome expansions in the virosphere.
