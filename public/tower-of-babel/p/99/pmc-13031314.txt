Evaluation of commercial probiotics for growth, health, and biometric indices of Thai silver barb (Barbonymus gonionotus)
Authors: Shadman Shakib Shovon, Tasmin Zahan, Raisa Shoyeb, Istiak Ahmed, Khadiza Tul Kubra, Tamanna Tabassum, Hafsa Siddiquee Mumu, Nawshin Tabassum, Shuvo Roy, Julfat Tasnim Suchona, Jubaida Parveen Juthy, Siyam Hossain, A. G. M. Sofi Uddin Mahamud, Md. Ali Reza Faruk, Tanvir Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effects of commercial probiotic supplementation on growth performance, feed utilization, water quality parameters, blood parameters, intestinal morphology, gut bacterial load, and biometric indices of the Thai silver barb (Barbonymus gonionotus). A total of 5,250 healthy juveniles (2.67 ± 0.03 − 2.74 ± 0.02 g) were randomly divided into five groups, each with three replications (350 fish/pond), and fed for 90 days. Treatments included a basal diet supplemented with gut probiotic ZYMETIN (T1), water probiotic POND CARE (T2), soil probiotic Super PS (T3), a combination of all three at half doses (T4), and a control (T5) with no probiotics. Results showed a significant (P < 0.05) improvement across probiotic-treated groups compared to T5, with T1 (gut probiotic) yielding across probiotic-treated groups compared to T5, with T1 (gut probiotic) achieving ~ 133% greater net weight gain and ~ 128% higher RGR than the control. Particularly, water quality revealed substantial variations in pH and ammonia (NH3), with the highest pH in T1 and the highest ammonia found in T3. Gut bacterial load increased notably in probiotic-treated groups, with T1 showing the highest count at the end. Histological analysis revealed improved intestinal morphology in probiotic-treated groups. Hematological parameters showed noticeable differences in blood parameters, with T1 exhibiting the highest WBC and RBC counts and the lowest MCH levels. Principal Component and Cluster Analyses confirmed strong correlations between growth, feed utilization, and hematological parameters. Overall, the findings suggest the potential of probiotics as an eco-friendly approach to enhance B. gonionotus aquaculture.

Introduction

As global aquaculture practices are rapidly shifting toward commercial feed-based intensive or super-intensive culture systems, feed costs constitute nearly 80% of the total expenses1. However, in Bangladesh, inconsistencies between laboratory reports and the on-farm performance of commercial feeds have become a major concern in recent days2. Beyond this, the widespread use of chemical inputs such as growth promoters, steroids, and antibiotics in aquaculture feeds has contributed to drug-resistant pathogens, disrupted pond microbial balance, reduced fish health and product acceptability, and ultimately lowered farm profitability3–6.

Probiotics offer a promising alternative to these chemical inputs in aquaculture, with a convenient application method that allows for easy administration through water, incorporation into feed, or combination with prebiotics and immunostimulants for enhanced effectiveness7. The benefits of probiotic supplementation have been well-documented, including improvements in water quality8–10, growth performance11,12, hematological parameters13–15, gut morphology and nutrient absorption16–18, reduced inflammation, enhanced epithelial barrier function19–21, and reduce the abundance of opportunistic pathogens in the gut systems22. They also enhance immune responses by activating complement activity, respiratory burst, plasma lysozyme, and systemic innate defenses while reducing oxidative stress23–25.

Thai silver barb (Barbonymus gonionotus), an exotic fish of Bangladesh belonging to the Cyprinidae family, has gained popularity since its first introduction in 1977 due to its appealing silver coloration, palatable taste, affordability, and high protein content. In Bangladesh, it is among the top contributing species in aquaculture, accounting for 2.28% of national fish production26. Its economic value is further enhanced by its rapid growth in high-density ponds and compatibility with polyculture systems27,28. Conversely, a sharp rise in feed prices is continuously limiting its desired commercial production29.

Our earlier study showed that B. gonionotus is well-compatible with several freshwater-oriented probiotics-based feed additives30. However, these products differ significantly from many probiotics currently dominating the local market, which are primarily formulated for brackish-water shrimp aquaculture rather than for freshwater finfish. This discrepancy, particularly in the environmental conditions required for probiotic survival and functionality, raises important questions about whether probiotics designed for shrimp pond management can be similarly effective in exclusive freshwater fish species like B. gonionotus31,32. Consequently, farmers remain dependent on a limited number of proven freshwater probiotics, while the efficacy of many shrimp-culture products remains unexplored in freshwater environments. In the absence of adequate freshwater probiotic options, farmers remain dependent on high-cost inputs, including commercial feeds, water conditioners, and antibiotics, to achieve expected outcomes, further limiting profitability in B. gonionotus culture.

To address this gap, the current study systematically evaluates the effects of three commercial probiotics, ZYMETIN, PONDCARE, and Super PS. Among these products, PONDCARE is commonly used in both shrimp and finfish ponds in Bangladesh, making it a practical comparison point. In contrast, ZYMETIN and Super PS are primarily applied in crustacean (shrimp/prawn) culture, and their performance in freshwater finfish has not been well evaluated31–33. Accordingly, comparative assessments of these probiotics in finfish culture remain limited. Our previous study demonstrated that ZYMETIN and Super PS can significantly enhance water quality, growth, blood parameters, and gut health and microbial profile in Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), a species cultured in both brackish and freshwater environments18. Building on this evidence, the current study expands the investigation to B. gonionotus, an exclusively freshwater species with distinct ecological and physiological characteristics. We specifically assess the effects of these probiotics on water quality parameters, fish growth performance, feed utilization, blood parameters, intestinal morphology, gut bacterial load, and overall health indicators, while also comparing their performance when delivered through different application routes (feed-, water-, and soil-based delivery). By providing the first systematic evaluation of these crustacean-culture probiotics in a freshwater cyprinid, this study offers new insights into their cross-species applicability and their potential role in improving the sustainability and profitability of B. gonionotus aquaculture in Bangladesh.

Methodology

Pre-experimental setup

A 90-day feeding trial was conducted at the Department of Aquaculture, Bangladesh Agricultural University, Bangladesh (24.72291° N, 90.43077° E). For this experiment, fifteen earthen ponds were used, each with 0.75 decimal areas and a depth of 3.0 − 3.5 feet. Ponds were divided into five treatment groups, each having three replications, in a randomized block design. A pre-stocking management protocol was followed to prepare the ponds for stocking. Each pond was treated with lime and salt before stocking 350 fish/pond.

Fingerling collection and experimental setup

A total of 5,500 healthy Thai silver barb fingerlings (2.67 ± 0.03 − 2.74 ± 0.02 g) were collected from Sharnalata Agro Fisheries Ltd., Mymensingh, Bangladesh (24.612175°N, 90.343206°E) and transported to the pond site in oxygenated transport polybags. After being acclimated, the dead and weak fingerlings were removed, and a total of 5,250 uniformly healthy fingerlings (350 fish/pond) were stocked.

The fish were provided with commercial catfish pre-starter feed (1.5 mm) from Mega Feeds Limited, Bangladesh. Three commercial probiotics, viz., gut probiotic ZYMETIN (concentrated formulation of Bacillus spp., Clostridium butyricum, and Streptococcus faecalis; CPF Private Limited, India); water additive probiotic PONDCARE (concentrated formulation of Bacillus spp.; marketed by Eskayef Pharmaceuticals Ltd. (SKF), Bangladesh), and soil probiotic Super PS (concentrated formulation of Rhodobacter spp. and Rhodococcus spp.; CPF Private Limited, India), were used. The experiment was designed with five treatments: T1 [fish were fed the basal diet supplemented with ZYMETIN powder at 10 g/kg feed, mixed with rice gruel and administered orally], T2 [PONDCARE powder was mixed with molasses, dissolved in pond water, and spread evenly over the pond surface at 0.5 g/decimal], T3 [Super PS liquid probiotic was mixed with sand and the mixture was spread across the pond to ensure soil contact, at approximately 41 mL/decimal for days 1–30, 61 mL/decimal for days 31–60, and 243 mL/decimal for days 61–90], T4 [all three probiotics were applied at half their recommended doses, using the same application procedures described for T1-T3], and T5 [control; received no probiotics] (Table 1). Fish were fed twice a day [once in the morning and once in the afternoon] at a rate of 5–8% of body weight (BW), and feeding rates were adjusted fortnightly.

Underground water was supplied fortnightly to maintain the water depth at around 3 feet throughout the study. The proximate composition of the formulated diets was analyzed in the Fish Nutrition Laboratory, Department of Aquaculture, BAU, according to the AOAC guidelines34. The gross energy (GE) content of the diets was estimated using the equation provided by the National Research Council (NRC)35.

Monitoring of water quality parameters

Water quality parameters, including pH, temperature (°C), dissolved oxygen (DO), and total ammonia nitrogen (TAN), were measured every fortnight with portable test kits such as a thermometer, aqua DO meter (Lutron PDO-519), ammonia test kit (Advance Pharma Co. Ltd), and pH meter (EcoSense pH/ EC1030A pH). Unionized ammonia (NH3) was estimated from TAN values based on temperature- and pH-dependent equilibrium relationships described by Emerson et al. 36 and Francis-Floyd et al. 37. Only calculated NH3 values are presented in figures.

Growth, feed utilization, and survival studies

For morphometric measurements and growth parameter assessments, fish sampling was carried out fortnightly from each replication using a fine-meshed push net, and the fish were anesthetized using a low dose of buffered MS-222 solution (Tricaine methanesulfonate; 40 mg/L), and body weight was measured using an electric balance (Model- HT224R, Shinko Denshi Co. Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). The calculated growth parameters included net weight gain, specific growth rate (SGR), relative growth rate (RGR), survival rate (%) 38, absolute growth rate (AGR), instantaneous growth rate (IGR)39, feed conversion ratio (FCR), protein efficiency ratio (PER)40, feed conversion efficiency (FCE)18, and length gain (cm)41 were determined. Gut microbiota load

Tryptone Soya Agar (TSA) was selected to determine the heterotrophic gut bacterial load in B. gonionotus. On the day of sampling, three B. gonionotus were collected randomly from each treatment and transferred to aerated aquaria. After a 24-hour starvation period, fish were sacrificed treatment-wise. Intestines were aseptically removed and homogenized at 4 °C in sterile physiological saline (0.85%) at a ratio of 1 mL per 0.1 g of intestine. The resultant aliquots of 100 µL were obtained separately and consecutively diluted before plating on TSA. Duplicate plates were prepared for every dilution and incubated for a day at 37 °C to enumerate total heterotrophic bacteria (THB). Obtained colonies were expressed as colony-forming units per gram (CFU/g) 18.

Histology of intestinal morphology

For histological analysis of experimental fish intestines, fish were humanely euthanized using an overdose of clove oil (0.40 mL/L) and sacrificed after complete loss of reflexes and cessation of opercular movement42. Mid-intestinal tissue samples were aseptically collected and fixed in 10% buffered formalin. An automatic tissue processor (SHANDON, CITADEL 1000) was used to prepare the tissue for dehydration, cleaning, and infiltration. Samples were then embedded in molten paraffin wax and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm using a microtome (Leica JUNG RM 2035). The sections were placed on glass slides and dried at 37 °C on a hot plate for one minute. Processed sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin, mounted with Canada balsam, and finally observed under a light microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, GmbH Axiocam ERc 5s). Microscopic observations of the intestines were conducted at the start and end of the feeding trial.

Hematological study

Blood samples were collected following the tail ablation procedure outlined in the guidelines of the Canada Department of Fisheries and Oceans43. The parameters examined included mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), hemoglobin, glucose, white blood cells (WBCs), and red blood cells (RBCs). Total RBC (106/mm3) and WBC (103/mm3) counts were determined under a light microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, GmbH Axiocam ERc 5s) using an improved Neubauer hemocytometer44. Hemoglobin concentration (g/dL) was measured with Sahli’s hemometer, and blood glucose levels (mg/dL) were assessed using glucose strips with a digital EasyMate® GHb. Using the following formulas, the levels of WBC, RBC, and MCH45,46 were calculated.

Determination of length-weight relationship and condition factor

Weight and length data from 90-day-old fish were analyzed to calculate the relative condition factor and weight–length relationship (WLR) following the equations of Ricker47 and Kilduff48. The fish were caught using a fine-meshed cast net, anesthetized with MS-222 (tricaine methanesulfonate), and then weighed using an electronic balance. Their length was measured with a scale. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses included one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for feed proximate composition, growth parameters, water quality, hematological variables, and gut bacterial load. Significant differences among means were determined using Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) in SPSS Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The level of significance was set at P < 0.05, and the results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Principal component analysis (PCA) and cluster analysis were performed using OriginPro 2024b (Version 10.15; OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA) to assess treatment-wise relationships based on the coefficient of variance.

All procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations for the care and use of animals in research. The reporting of fish experiments in this manuscript follows the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Results

Gut probiotic-supplemented diet for T1 showed the highest protein content (Table 2).

Rearing water quality and microbial load

Significant differences in rearing water quality parameters were observed for pH and ammonia levels. Temperature and DO were mostly constant, with the highest pH recorded in T1 and the highest ammonia in T3 (Fig. 1). Statistically significant (P < 0.05) positive correlations were found between temperature and DO or pH. A negative correlation was recorded between DO and ammonia, where the other correlations were weak and not statistically significant (Table 3). At the end of the experiment, the highest water bacterial load was recorded in T2 (1.096 × 107 CFU/mL), followed by T4 (5.82 × 106 CFU/mL).

Effects on growth, feed utilization, and survival rate

Probiotic-treated groups showed notable improvement in growth performance, survival, and feed utilization during the 90-day culture period, which were summarized in Table 4. Significantly (P < 0.05) better growth performance viz., net weight gain (53.77 ± 0.03 − 125.35 ± 0.6 g), RGR (1999.69 ± 51.28 − 4567.58 ± 55.90%), AGR (0.60 ± 0.00 − 1.39 ± 0.01 g/day), SGR (3.38 ± 0.03 − 4.27 ± 0.01%), IGR (0.0338 ± 0.0003 − 0.0427 ± 0.0001 g/day), length gain (7.96 ± 0.0 − 11.81 ± 0.04 cm), survival (89.28 ± 0.20 − 89.85 ± 0.20%) and feed utilization FCR (1.4 ± 0.01 − 1.63 ± 0.01), FCE (0.61 ± 0.0 − 0.72 ± 0.01), PER (2.02 ± 0.02 − 2.27 ± 0.07) was obtained in B. gonionotus in all probiotics supplement treatment than the control group. The gut probiotic-supplemented group (T1) exhibited the highest growth performance, survival, and feed utilization, whereas the control group (T5) showed the lowest values for these parameters. The combined probiotics group (T4) demonstrated comparable efficiency in enhancing growth, feed utilization, and survival (Table 4).

Principal component analysis (PCA) and cluster analysis (CA)

The multivariate analytical results of water quality, hematological parameters, gut bacterial load, feed efficiency, feed quality, and growth parameters of Thai silver barb after 90 days are shown in Figs. 7 and 8. From the PCA analysis, four principal components were found having an eigenvalue above 1 (Fig. 6), and they represent 90.96% of the data. The PC1 and PC2 explained 76.19% (64.75% and 11.45%) of the existing correlation between parameters and the treatments (Fig. 7). The green lines characterize the variables, and their length specifies the strength of their influence on the principal components, and the red dots represent the different experimental treatments replication. The angle between vectors reflects the correlation between variables. CP, LG, NGW, WEIGHT, AGR, FCE, SGR, and RGR form a cluster and point in the same direction, representing strong positive correlations among them. pH, WBC, Temperature, and HB also point in the same direction, suggesting that favorable growth is closely associated with these physiological and environmental parameters. These variables are representative of the “growth and health” cluster. FCR, NFE, GE and EE represent the feed conversion and energy cluster, where FCR and NFE are positioned on the far left, directly opposing the “growth and performance” cluster, which confirms a strong negative correlation between each group.

This is an expected biological relationship, where SGR and NGW correspond to a low FCR. GE and EE are also located in this left quadrant, suggesting they are related to energy content and are distinct from the primary growth metrics. ASH, GLUCOSE, NH3, CARB, FIBER, and MOIST form distinct groups or are positioned at different angles and represent a metabolic and compositional parameters cluster. For instance, the CARB and FIBER are roughly perpendicular to the main “growth” axis, indicating their variation is mainly independent of the growth variables, and they primarily influence the second principal component (PC 2). This suggests they are important in defining the differences between treatments that do not necessarily relate to growth. In case of the treatment’s replication, T1R1, T1R2, and T1R3 are located in the region dominated by the ‘growth and health’ cluster of variables, suggesting that these replications were the overall best performance metrics among all groups. T5R1, T5R2, T5R3, and T3R1, T3R2, T3R3 are grouped on the far left of the biplot, in the same quadrant as the FCR and NFE vectors, indicating that these treatments are associated with higher values of these parameters, and were associated with low growth rates, confirming a detrimental effect on performance. T2R1, T2R2, and T2R3 are located in the upper-left quadrant, with a tendency towards EE and GE, suggesting they had a distinct effect on energy metabolism and composition. T3R1, T3R3, and T3R2 are clustered near the center-left, with a strong association with NH3 and a negative association with the growth cluster. T4R1, T4R2, and T4R3 are positioned towards the lower quadrant of the biplot, associated with variables including carbohydrate and fiber, which are away from the other major clusters. This suggests a distinct impact on these specific parameters.

Cluster analysis (CA) was performed to assess similarities among the five treatments and their replications by constructing a dendrogram. Replicates were grouped based on percentage similarity (0 to 100%), reflecting treatment relationships. The dendrogram separated the treatments into four distinct clusters: Group I (T1R1, T1R2, T1R3, T4R1, T4R2, T4R3), Group II (T2R1, T2R2, T2R3), Group III (T3R1, T3R2, T3R3), and Group IV (T5R1, T5R2, T5R3) (Fig. 8). Group I contained two sub-clusters with similarity exceeding 98%. Group I and Group II shared similarity above 85%, whereas Groups I-III retained > 45% similarity overall. In contrast, Group IV formed a clearly isolated cluster, indicating minimal similarity with other treatments.

Discussion

Over the past few decades, probiotics have been increasingly utilized in aquaculture as eco-friendly alternatives to antibiotics, enhancing feed utilization and growth performance49. By producing exo-enzymes and encouraging the growth of beneficial microflora, they also contribute to improved digestion within the digestive tract50. Additionally, probiotics have been linked to enhanced disease resistance, improved immune function, and increased epithelial barrier integrity51. In this study, commercial probiotics: Super PS (soil probiotic), ZYMETIN (gut probiotic), and PONDCARE (water probiotic) were used successfully in B. gonionotus rearing under freshwater conditions, resulting in significant improvements in water quality, gut microbiota, and hematological parameters across all probiotic-supplemented treatments (T1, T2, T3, and T4) compared to the control (T5).

The physicochemical parameters of pond water act as indicators of water quality and the effectiveness of a culture system in supporting aquaculture52. A thorough understanding of the link between water quality and aquatic productivity is required for optimal growth, survival, and production53. In this study, the physicochemical parameters of the water were confirmed to be within acceptable limits for B. gonionotus culture, and an interrelation among the parameters was also found, as previously stated by Boyd and Tucker54. While DO is essential for all living organisms55, fish and other aquatic species are often found to be stressed, sensitive to infectious diseases, and exhibit slower growth under low dissolved oxygen levels (below ~ 4 mg/L) 56. No significant difference was found in DO among the probiotic and non-probiotic treated groups.

A suitable environment in aquaculture ponds involves a constant pH value, while instability in pH levels may adversely affect photosynthesis, fish growth, and reproduction57. In this study, the pH levels across the treatments throughout the culture period remained within the optimal range (6.5–8.5) for fish culture, as recommended by Bolorunduro and Abdullah58. These results align with the findings of Zhou et al. 59 and Tabassum et al. 18 in Nile tilapia ponds treated with probiotics.

Unionized ammonia (NH3) and other nitrogenous waste are vital features in aquaculture60, and knowing their dynamics is crucial for preventing fish from sudden mortality and for the sustainability of the aquaculture system61. In the present study, TAN (NH3/NH4+) was measured and subsequently converted to unionized NH3 based on pH-temperature equilibrium relationships37, as NH3 is the biologically active and toxic fraction influencing fish performance. Although NH4+ can contribute to cellular toxicity after NH3 diffuses into the fish and ionizes internally, NH4+ in the water column is generally considered far less toxic and is utilizable by plankton and microbial communities62. Therefore, plotting TAN alone would not provide meaningful interpretation because TAN includes largely non-toxic ammonium (NH4+); thus, NH3 is the more relevant parameter for evaluating treatment effect63. Our results indicate that T1 gut probiotics were more effective than water/soil probiotics in reducing ammonia levels in rearing water. These results partially align with those of Tabassum et al. 18 in tilapia ponds and Khademzade et al. 64 in shrimp ponds. Here, all of the probiotics had Bacillus species. The beneficial impact of this species has been previously stated. Bacillus species can remove various forms of nitrogen from aquaculture ponds65 and play an essential role in the nitrogen cycle66, help in the mineralization of wastes, and improve water quality67. In contrast, although Rhodobacter and Rhodococcus possess nitrogen-cycling capabilities, their optimal activity is often observed under mild salinity, high organic substrate availability, and biofilm-rich environments, such as those found in shrimp or coastal aquaculture systems68,69. In our freshwater earthen ponds, routine water addition and comparatively lower organic substrate levels may have limited the establishment and functional expression of these bacteria, causing ammonia production from fish metabolism and feed decomposition to outpace microbial assimilation in the Super PS treatment. This likely explains why Bacillus-based treatments showed lower ammonia accumulation than the T3 group. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that soil probiotics dominated by Rhodobacter/Rhodococcus may not be sufficient as standalone tools for ammonia management in freshwater finfish ponds and may need to be combined with more robust nitrifying/denitrifying consortia or adjusted pond management strategies.

In the present study, probiotic supplementation resulted in a significant (P < 0.05) improvement in growth among all probiotic-treated groups, with a notable difference compared to the control group (T5). The best feed conversion ratio (FCR) was observed in the T1 group. These findings are consistent with previous studies on B. gonionotus70, O. niloticus18,59,71, and Clarias batrachus72. The superior performance of gut probiotic supplementation, as reported in previous studies, is attributed to an enriched gut microbiota73. These probiotics release extracellular enzymes (amylases, proteases, and lipases) that break down complex nutrients, thereby enhancing digestion, improving nutrient availability and absorption, and ultimately supporting overall growth and health74–76. Additionally, probiotics help enhance oxygen transport by boosting the production of RBCs, which are essential for nutrient respiration and metabolism13,70.

The present study indicated that probiotic supplementation significantly affects the total intestinal bacterial load. T1 (gut probiotic) revealed the highest bacterial load (5.4 × 106 CFU/g), and the lowest load (8.5 × 105 CFU/g) was found in the control group (T5) at the end of the experiment (Fig. 2), which is consistent with previous studies on B. gononotus70, Nile tilapia18, and rainbow trout77. Here, growth rate and feed utilization showed significant (P < 0.05) improvements in T1 with a higher bacterial load. This could be due to the probiotic group having proteolytic and antagonistic properties78–80. It is also clear from our study that T1 has a higher bacterial load in the gut, which is similar to the findings of Michael et al. 13.

Intestinal morphology plays a critical role in regulating the physiology and metabolism of aquatic organisms. Probiotic supplementation has been shown to modulate the intestinal microenvironment in fish, leading to significant improvements in histomorphometric traits81,82. In this study, substantial changes in intestinal morphology were observed in probiotic-treated groups (T1-T4), which exhibited higher villi length and enterocyte height compared to T5 (Fig. 3). This suggests healthier intestines, possibly due to the presence of Bacillus spp. in the probiotics, consistent with the findings of Tabassum et al. 18 and Elsabagh et al. 83. The presence of B. cereus, B. subtilis, and B. amyloliquefaciens in diets may have expanded the absorption surface, improved nutrient retention, increased the number of goblet cells with longer villi, and enhanced enterocyte height in the fish gut84. Similarly, Eissa et al. 85 reported that Bacillus subtilis supplementation improved intestinal tissue structure, villi height and width, thereby enhancing absorptive surface area in red tilapia.

Hematological parameters are widely recognized as vital fish health indicators, with their variations reflecting stress, nutritional status, and overall fish well-being86,87. Several studies have shown that probiotics enhance hematological indices, including RBC, WBC, Hb, and monocytes, in various finfish species88,89. In the present study, the probiotic-supplemented groups (T1-T4) showed significantly (P < 0.05) higher RBC and WBC counts but lower MCH content than the control group (T5), which indicates improved immune competence in B. gonionotus.

Notably, T1, containing concentrated strains of Bacillus spp., Clostridium butyricum, and Streptococcus faecalis (gut probiotic), revealed the highest RBC and WBC content. This agrees with earlier work showing that Bacillus spp. increase RBC abundance in Nile tilapia, Rohu, and other carps, especially when administered in combination with complementary probiotics such as Clostridium spp. 83. Mechanistically, Bacillus spp. may enhance iron bioavailability through organic acid production, lowering intestinal pH, increasing iron solubility, and supporting erythropoiesis92,93. In addition, probiotics modulate gut microbiota, inhibit pathogenic colonization, and stimulate immune signaling pathways, leading to elevated leukocyte activity94,95. Other studies also support that mixed probiotics, including Bacillus and Lactobacillus spp., can elevate RBC and WBC levels and enhance innate immunity through phagocytosis, neutrophil activity, and pathogen inhibition18,70,96,97. Such immunomodulatory effects can help prevent disease outbreaks and improve fish health98,99.

Rhodobacter-based soil probiotics used in T3–T4 may have contributed to resilience through modulation of gut microbiota and intestinal repair, as reported previously100. Likewise, Rhodococcus spp., a fast-growing immunostimulant, already displayed enhanced trout immune response and a significant resistance to V. anguillarum challenge101, which may partially explain immune improvement in these groups.

Although Hb and glucose levels did not differ significantly among treatments, higher glucose levels in the control group may reflect stress-related metabolic adjustment102. Meanwhile, elevated Hb and RBC values in probiotic-treated fish likely improved oxygen transport, supporting growth performance consistent with reports in tilapia85,103. The higher MCH observed in the control group (T5) may indicate macrocytic changes linked to micronutrient imbalance104, although vitamin status and probiotic-mediated vitamin synthesis were not quantified here. This pattern is noteworthy but requires further investigation.

The condition factor and length-weight relationship are a great welfare indicator of the good well-being of aquatic organisms105. In this study, the individuals of probiotic-treated groups have shown a proper length-weight relationship with an acceptable range of 2.5–3.5 106 and 2.95–3.01 for B. gonionotus107, which agrees with our findings. At the end of the experiment, the gut probiotic-treated group (T1) exhibited an isometric (b = 3) relationship, while the water probiotic-treated group (T2) demonstrated a positive allometric (b > 3) relationship. In contrast, T3, T4, and T5 showed negative allometric (b < 3) relationships (Fig. 4). The Fulton condition factor (k) is commonly used to assess the health of fish, and a condition factor above 1 signifies good condition108. In this study, a significant (P < 0.05) difference in condition factors was observed among the treatments; however, all fish from every treatment were in good condition (Table 3).

The multivariate analysis helps illustrate the relationship between variables and dietary groups in two dimensions109, providing a better understanding of the effects of single and mixed probiotic supplements. The connection between the water parameters, hematological parameters, water and gut bacterial load, feed efficiency and feed quality and growth parameters were described by the loading plot from the PCA analysis (Fig. 7). The PCA analysis revealed that most variables were more strongly correlated with T1 than with T4, T2, T3, and the control (T5), suggesting superior performance in T1 compared to the other groups and the factors are responsible for lower performance in T5 and intermediate regimes (T2, T3, T4) that each had a unique impact on specific physiological and metabolic parameters. The cluster analysis revealed that fish groups with gut and mixed probiotics supplemented group (T1, and T4) had more than 98% similarity based on the performance compared to other treatments (T2, T3, and T5) (Fig. 8). The dendrogram revealed that T1 (gut probiotic) and T4 (combined probiotics) were different from T2, T3, and T5 as both of them having gut probiotics as a common factor, suggesting that a combination of Bacillus spp., S. faecalis, and C. butyricum supplementation in the form of gut probiotic has improved health of Thai silver barb.

Conclusions

Probiotics are rapidly gaining popularity in the aquaculture sector as an eco-friendly approach to health management. This 90-day pond trial experiment demonstrated that probiotic supplementation enhanced water quality, growth, survival rates, blood parameters, intestinal bacterial load, and biometric indices of Thai silver barb. The application of various commercial probiotics, commonly employed in the coastal semi-intensive shrimp cultivation of Bangladesh, was effectively evaluated in B. gonionotus reared under freshwater conditions. Both concentrated Bacillus spp., S. faecalis, and C. butyricum-based gut probiotics, as well as combinations of soil, gut, and water probiotics, may be recommended for use in pond culture of Thai silver barb as potential additives for environmentally sustainable health management. Nevertheless, further comprehensive studies are required to elucidate their impacts on digestive enzyme activity, immunological parameters, lysozyme activity, and the resistance of B. gonionotus to microbial pathogens when employing probiotic supplements.
