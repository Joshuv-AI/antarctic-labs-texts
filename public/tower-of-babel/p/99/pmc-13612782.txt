Polyamines in CNS malignancies: positively charged culprits in the hijacking of neural and immune signaling pathways
Authors: Ayush B Rana, Scott M Welford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mammalian polyamines (putrescine, spermidine and spermine) are ubiquitous polycations, long recognized for their indispensable roles in maintaining cell proliferation, differentiation and survival. Traditionally viewed as metabolic supporters of growth, polyamines have recently emerged as active regulators of cell-cell signaling in diverse physiological and pathological settings. Through intercellular polyamine transfer, modulation of ion channels and interactions with cell-surface receptors, polyamines orchestrate intricate signaling networks, from neurotransmission in the nervous system to cytokine signaling in the immune compartment. Many cancers, though most clearly, central nervous systems (CNS) cancers, exploit neuro- and immunomodulatory circuits, effectively hijacking neural and immune signaling pathways to sustain growth and evade surveillance. Thus, an integrated, multi-disciplinary perspective is required to overcome hurdles in the treatment of these aggressive malignancies. In light of ongoing clinical trials aimed at disrupting polyamine synthesis and transport in brain tumors, better defining the role of polyamines in mediating tumor-host interactions is essential for maximizing anti-tumor efficacy while minimizing normal tissue toxicity. This review integrates advances from cancer biology, immunology and neuroscience to comprehensively discuss the mechanisms through which polyamines regulate cell-cell signaling, the role of these pathways in brain tumor progression and the diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities that arise from this knowledge.Keywords: Polyamines, Cell-cell signaling, Brain Tumors, Tumor Microenvironment

Introduction

Despite significant research advances, central nervous system (CNS) malignancies remain among the deadliest and most feared of all cancers, resulting in an estimated 250,000 deaths per year globally (Aldape et al. 2019; Ilic and Ilic 2023). Moreover, the incidence of CNS malignancies has increased over the past few decades, posing a significant burden to global public health (Ilic and Ilic 2023). The ability of these aggressive tumors to resist conventional therapies including surgery, chemotherapy and radiation, is due, in part, to the unique cellular and microenvironmental properties of neural tissue (Aldape et al. 2019). Within the brain tumor microenvironment, crosstalk between tumor cells, neurons and immune cells has been shown to promote tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance (Li and Hanahan 2013; Hara et al. 2021; Venkataramani et al. 2022; Hanahan and Monje 2023; Mancusi and Monje 2023; Huang et al. 2025). Thus, the development of novel therapies for the treatment of CNS malignancies necessitates consideration of multiple disciplines including cancer biology, neuroscience and immunology (Hanahan and Monje 2023; Winkler et al. 2023).

Recent advances in our understanding of the biological roles of polyamines has underscored their multifaced functions in cancer cells, neural cells and immune cells (Casero, Murray Stewart et al. 2018; Chia et al. 2022; Holbert et al. 2022; Rieck et al. 2022; Jimenez Gutierrez et al. 2023; Arthur et al. 2024; Mahalingam and Pandiyan 2024). Pharmacological inhibition of polyamine synthesis is currently being investigated for the treatment of multiple cancer types including brain tumors. The polyamine synthesis inhibitor, DFMO, was first tested for the treatment of recurrent glioma in the 1980’s and received “breakthrough therapy” designation from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of anaplastic astrocytoma in 2014, fueling resurgent research and clinical interest over the past decade (Levin et al. 2018). The current review summarizes prior work investigating the contribution of polyamines to the tumor-cell intrinsic and microenvironment-mediated processes underlying the pathogenesis of CNS malignancies.

Polyamine levels in CNS malignancies

The eukaryotic polyamines putrescine, spermidine, and spermine are positively charged alkylamines that regulate broad cellular functions including proliferation, energy metabolism and autophagy (Pegg 2016; Casero, Murray Stewart et al. 2018). Accordingly, the maintenance of intracellular polyamine homeostasis is essential for normal cellular function (Nowotarski et al. 2013; Pegg 2016; Casero, Murray Stewart et al. 2018). Within the CNS, polyamines have been shown to regulate neurogenesis, neurotransmitter release and ion channel assembly (Rieck et al. 2022; Jimenez Gutierrez et al. 2023; Arthur et al. 2024). Importantly, dysregulated polyamine metabolism has been implicated in multiple CNS pathologies including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease and CNS malignancies (Sandusky-Beltran et al. 2019; Zahedi et al. 2020; Polis et al. 2021; Vrijsen et al. 2023; Rana et al. 2025). Further underscoring their fundamental role in neural function, rare genetic disorders affecting polyamine enzymes, such as Bachman-Bupp syndrome (ODC1 overexpression) and Snyder-Robinson syndrome (SRM loss-of-function) are characterized by neurocognitive dysfunction (Schwartz et al. 1993; Starks et al. 2018; Akinyele et al. 2024; Bachmann et al. 2024). Together, these observations highlight the indispensable role of polyamine metabolism in maintaining functional neurobiology, and how dysregulation contributes to neuropathology and disease progression.

Brain tumor tissue: Elevated polyamine levels have been reported across various cancer types, including brain, breast, lung, prostate and skin cancers (Nowotarski et al. 2013; Murray-Stewart et al. 2016; Casero, Murray Stewart et al. 2018). Owing to well-established links to oncogenic signaling pathways, most notably the MYC proto-oncogene, polyamine metabolism has long been pursued as a therapeutic target in multiple cancers (Casero, Murray Stewart et al. 2018). Here, our survey of studies spanning several decades identifies convergent evidence that polyamine metabolism is hyperactivated in brain tumor tissue and is associated with more aggressive brain tumor types (i.e. glioblastoma relative to astrocytoma and higher-grade glioma relative to lower-grade glioma) (Table 1) (Kurihara et al. 1993; Ernestus et al. 1996, 2001; Kay et al. 2024; Rana et al. 2025). Notably, glioblastoma represents the most common and lethal primary malignant brain tumor in adults with an incidence rate of 3.28 cases per 100,000 individuals and median survival of 8 months (Price et al. 2025). Despite decades of research, therapeutic options for glioblastoma remain limited, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. Thus, multiple ongoing clinical trials are now investigating the pharmacological approaches aimed at disrupting polyamine metabolism in glioblastoma and other aggressive CNS malignancies (discussed in later sections of this review).

Patient biofluids: Polyamines have additionally emerged as a promising biomarker in the context of brain tumors, with mounting evidence that polyamine levels are generally elevated in the blood and cerebrospinal of patients with various brain tumors including glioblastoma, medulloblastomas, ependymoma, pineal germ cell tumors, primitive neuroectodermal tumors, and brainstem gliomas (Table 1) (Marton et al. 1974, 1976; Terabyashi 1976; Fulton et al. 1980; Moulinoux et al. 1984; Phillips et al. 1986; Takaue et al. 1986; Zhao et al. 2016). Importantly, several of these findings were reported in pediatric brain tumors, suggesting that dysregulated polyamine metabolism may represent a shared feature of CNS malignancies across both adults and children. Thus, non-invasive quantification of polyamines in patient biofluids may present an avenue for the early detection and clinical monitoring of CNS malignancies in both pediatric and adult patient populations. In light of the growing recognition of the multifaceted role of polyamines as mediators of cell-cell signaling (discussed later in this review), elevated circulating polyamine levels in patients with brain tumors further underscores the need to consider their role shaping host-tumor dynamics.

1H-magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging: Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based techniques are routinely employed in the clinical management of brain tumor patients, from initial diagnosis through therapeutic evaluation (Villanueva-Meyer et al. 2017). Recently, 1H-MR-spectroscopy (1H-MRS) has emerged as a clinical tool to provide further insight into the molecular composition of brain tumors (Weinberg et al. 2021). To date, use of 1H-MRS for the detection of N-acetylaspartate (NAA), choline (Cho) and creatine (Cr) has been incorporated in the clinical diagnosis and follow-up of various brain lesions (Weinberg et al. 2021). Notably, polyamines produce a distinct, though often overlooked, peak when measured through 1H-MRS (Kobus et al. 2015). Interestingly, analysis of polyamine levels through 1H-MRS in prostate cancer has been proposed as an approach to improve detection of prostate cancer lesions (Kurhanewicz et al. 2002; Verma et al. 2010; Kobus et al. 2015; Bellomo et al. 2016). Together, these observations suggest that polyamine-resolved 1H-MRS may present an unexplored avenue for improved non-invasive detection and monitoring of brain tumors, warranting further investigation.

Amantadine acetylation: The use of amantadine as an activity-based synthetic biomarker to measure spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase 1 (SAT1) activity was approved in a 2004 patent (US6811967B2). Mechanistically, amantadine acts as a substrate for SAT1-mediated acetylation, which is the rate limiting step in polyamine catabolism; thus administration of amantadine and subsequent quantification of acetyl-amantadine can serve as a surrogate readout of SAT1 enzymatic activity (Bras et al. 2001). Recently, the use of amantadine as substrate for SAT-1 activity was shown to be a reliable clinical diagnostic assay for breast and lung cancer (Maksymiuk et al. 2019). Collectively, these findings highlight activity-based monitoring of polyamine acetylation as a potential strategy to aid the diagnosis of brain tumors.

Regulation of polyamine levels in brain tumors

Polyamine synthesis

While a substantial proportion of whole-body polyamines is thought to originate from the gastrointestinal tract through dietary intake and microbial metabolism, recent studies suggest that gut-derived polyamines may have limited ability to directly access the brain, highlighting the importance of local CNS polyamine synthesis and regulation (Ramos-Molina et al. 2019; Tofalo et al. 2019; Weiss et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2024). Stable isotope-based studies have largely confirmed that polyamines are arginine-derived metabolites, however in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), glutamate also has been identified as a critical polyamine precursor via ornithine aminotransferase (OAT) activity (Fig. 1A) (Lee et al. 2023; Hofer et al. 2024). Moreover, researchers have identified two principal routes that mediate the conversion of arginine to putrescine: (1) the classical pathway, in which arginine is converted to ornithine by arginase (ARG1) and subsequently to putrescine via (ornithine decarboxylase 1) ODC1; and (2) the agmatine pathway, in which arginine is converted to agmatine by arginine decarboxylase (ADC), followed by conversion of putrescine via agmatinase (AGMAT) (Halaris and Plietz 2007; Casero, Murray Stewart et al. 2018; Rieck et al. 2022). While the existence of a definitive mammalian ADC enzyme remains debated, ADC-like enzymatic activity and candidate ADC proteins have been reported in the mammalian brain (Zhu et al. 2004; Iyo et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2014). Thus, elucidating the dominant polyamine biosynthetic routes in brain tumors will be essential in guiding the design of therapeutic strategies to deplete the polyamine pool.

Ornithine: Ornithine, a central urea cycle metabolite, links nitrogen metabolism to polyamine and amino acid biosynthesis and feeds into proline and glutamate production, highlighting its broad metabolic significance in both normal and malignant brain tissue. Currently, the only FDA approved inhibitor of polyamine synthesis, DFMO, disrupts the conversion ornithine to putrescine through competitive inhibition of ODC1 (Levin et al. 2018). DFMO is currently undergoing clinical investigation across multiple cancer types, including brain tumors, as further discussed later in this review. While DFMO has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models and some early clinical trials, the critical role of ODC1 in normal neurobiological processes such as neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and glial function raises concerns regarding potential neurotoxicity and constrains the therapeutic window for systemic treatment (Levin et al. 2018; Bachmann et al. 2024). In addition, tumor cells may circumvent ODC1 inhibition through compensatory mechanisms including increased polyamine uptake, underscoring the need for combination strategies.

Agmatine: Although long recognized in bacteria and plants, agmatine was first identified in human brain tissue in 1994 as a natural clonidine-displacing substance (CDS) (Li et al. 1994). Compared to ornithine and canonical polyamines, agmatine remains comparatively understudied and its roles in neurobiology and tumor metabolism are only beginning to be elucidated (Fig. 1B). Recent work has shown that agmatine can act on multiple receptor types including adrenergic and glutamatergic NMDA receptors, thereby influencing diverse aspects of neuronal signaling (Halaris and Plietz 2007). Agmatine is packaged into synaptic vesicles, released upon depolarization and acts upon transmembrane receptors, demonstrating functional overlap with classical neurotransmitters (Halaris and Plietz 2007). Beyond the CNS, emerging evidence implicates agmatine in cancer biology as it has been shown to facilitate PDAC aggressiveness through activation of the TGFB/SMAD pathway and enhance lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) progression through the NO-MAPKs-PI3K/Akt signaling axis (Zhu et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2022).

Polyamine acetylation and oxidation

Intracellular polyamine homeostasis is tightly regulated not only through synthesis, but also via acetylation and subsequent oxidation or export (Pegg 2016). SAT1 catalyzes polyamine acetylation, marking polyamines for oxidation by polyamine oxidases or for cellular export (Pegg 2016). Notably, a recent study demonstrated that both SAT1 and N1-acetylspermidine levels are consistently elevated in human and murine glioblastoma tumors (Rana et al. 2025). Multiple studies have further shown that disruption of SAT1 sensitizes glioma cells to chemotherapy and radiation therapy, highlighting pharmacological inhibition of polyamine acetylation as a potential adjuvant strategy for front line-therapy (Brett-Morris et al. 2014, 2015; Rana et al. 2025). SAT1 appears to regulate tumor aggressiveness through diverse mechanisms including promoting DNA-damage repair, direct gene-specific transcriptional regulation and N1-acetylspermidine-dependant tumor-immune crosstalk (Brett-Morris et al. 2014; Thakur et al. 2019; Rana et al. 2025).

Polyamine oxidation, mediated by spermine oxidase (SMOX) and polyamine oxidase (PAOX) generates hydrogen peroxides and reactive aldehydes such as acrolein, linking polyamine catabolism to cellular oxidative stress (Murray Stewart et al. 2018; Cervelli et al. 2022). SMOX overexpression in cerebrocortical neurons of transgenic mice induces astrocyte dysfunction, while concurrent loss of SAT1 and SMOX results in ataxia in mice, highlighting the importance of polyamine oxidation in maintaining CNS homeostasis (Zahedi et al. 2020; Cervetto et al. 2021). SMOX has also been implicated in facilitating tumorigenic phenotypes (Xu et al. 2004; Pledgie et al. 2005; Babbar and Casero 2006; Goodwin et al. 2011). Collectively, acetylation and oxidation pathways represent critical regulators of polyamine flux in malignant cells, but their essential role in normal CNS physiology must be carefully considered when designing targeted therapeutic interventions.

Polyamines as modulators of cell-cell signaling in brain tumors

Intercellular polyamine transport in the brain tumor microenvironment

Polyamines are increasingly appreciated as not only intracellular regulators but also as critical paracrine signaling molecules in the normal function and development of the central nervous system (Laschet et al. 1992; Masuko et al. 2003; Malpica-Nieves et al. 2020; Kovacs et al. 2021; Cervelli et al. 2022). In the healthy brain, astrocytes are considered the predominant reservoir of polyamines and can release them via vesicles or solute carrier (SLC) transporters (Laube and Veh 1997; Skatchkov et al. 2000). Once in the extracellular space polyamines can be taken up by nearby neurons, glia and immune cells, enabling intercellular polyamine transport that modulates recipient cell physiology.

Recent studies in brain tumors have revealed that glioma cells co-opt and amplify polyamine secretion to support tumor progression and shape cell-cell interactions (Kay et al. 2024; Rana et al. 2025). This creates a polyamine-rich extracellular milieu that may impact surrounding neurons, glia and infiltrating immune cells. In the setting of GBM, it has been recently demonstrated that glioma-derived N1-acetylspermidine was taken up by tumor-associated macrophages/microglia (TAMs) to fuel intracellular polyamine flux, cellular respiration and migration (Rana et al. 2025). Uptake of N1-acetylspermidine was inhibited through use of the SLC3A2 inhibitor AMXT-1501, which synergizes with chemo- and radiotherapy to prolong survival of tumor bearing mice, suggesting that paracrine polyamine transport between tumor cells and tumor-associated myeloid cells promotes tumor aggressiveness (Rana et al. 2025).

Polyamines modulate ion exchange and ion sensing

In addition to direct polyamine transport between cells, polyamines regulate cell-cell interactions via modulation of ion sensing and transport (Williams 1997a, b; Nichols and Lee 2018; He et al. 2024). In light of their polycationic nature, polyamines interact directly with negatively charged residues present in ion channels, transporters and receptors, thereby modulating ion exchange and ion sensing (Johnson 1996; Williams 1997a, b).

Voltage gated ion channels: Polyamines have long been recognized as endogenous modulators of ion channel function most commonly by entering the channel pore and restricting ion permeability in multiple channel types, including inwardly rectifying potassium channels (Kir), where upon depolarization, polyamine enter the channel pore, blocking the movement of K+ ions through the channel (Williams 1997a, b; Hibino et al. 2010; Nichols and Lee 2018; Chen et al. 2020). Similar effects have been reported for voltage-gated sodium and calcium channels (Huang and Moczydlowski 2001; Chen et al. 2007). These effects are critical for synaptic plasticity, activity-dependent calcium signaling and network excitability (Williams 1997a, b). In glioma, the emerging roles of ion channels has been recently reviewed and linked to several hallmarks of cancer including proliferation, cellular migration and the tumor microenvironment (Takayasu et al. 2020; Elias et al. 2023).

Cx43 gap junctions: In the normal brain, polyamines play a pivotal role in the regulation of gap-junction communication mediated by connexin proteins, particularly Cx43 (Benedikt et al. 2012, 2022). Cx43-based gap junctions form intracellular channels that allow for the passage of ions and small metabolites between astrocytes in the astrocytic syncytium, supporting potassium buffering and metabolic coupling (Benedikt et al. 2022). Spermine in particular has been shown to enhance astrocytic coupling, however it remains unclear whether this is due to binding to negatively charged molecules in the cytoplasm or to the Cx43 pores directly (Benedikt et al. 2022). Notably, glioma cells have been shown to form functional Cx43 gap junctions with resident astrocytes, which contribute to glioma invasion and chemotherapeutic resistance (Zhang et al. 1999; Sin et al. 2016; Brandao et al. 2019; McCutcheon and Spray 2022; Gui et al. 2025).

Calcium sensing: Polyamines also modulate calcium-sensing machinery, particularly the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR), a G protein-coupled receptor that detects extracellular Ca2+ (Quinn et al. 1997; Ling et al. 2021; He et al. 2024). Polyamines act as positive allosteric modulators of CaSR, increasing receptor sensitivity and amplifying downstream calcium signaling (Ling et al. 2021; He et al. 2024). This modulation integrates extracellular calcium fluctuations with intracellular metabolic and excitatory states, dependent on receptor configuration and cofactor availability. In neurons and astrocytes, CaSR signaling can influence neurotransmitter release, synaptic plasticity and astrocytic calcium waves (Bandyopadhyay et al. 2010; Ruat and Traiffort 2013). In cancer, CaSR have been shown to regulate several hallmarks of cancer including proliferation, invasion and angiogenesis thereby contributing to tumor aggressiveness (Sarkar and Kumar 2012; Tennakoon et al. 2016).

Crosstalk with neurotransmitter pathways

Polyamines are increasingly recognized as important modulators of neurotransmitter signaling, most notably glutamatergic, GABAergic and cholinergic pathways (Laschet et al. 1992; Mony et al. 2009; Dhara et al. 2020; Kovacs et al. 2021). At the synaptic level, polyamines interact with ligand-gated ion channels including NMDA receptors and nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) where they modulate receptor gating (Mony et al. 2009; Dhara et al. 2020). In addition to these direct effects on excitatory and cholinergic signaling, polyamines also contribute to GABAergic neurotransmission by serving as precursors for GABA synthesis in astrocytes through the catabolism of putrescine via copper amino oxidase (CAO) (Laschet et al. 1992; Kovacs et al. 2021; Szabo et al. 2021). Through these dynamic processes, polyamines help to coordinate the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signals within neural circuits.

In the setting of brain tumors, these signaling pathways are frequently co-opted as cancer cells integrate into and remodel existing neural circuits to promote tumor aggressiveness (Monje et al. 2020; Winkler et al. 2023; Monje 2025). Emerging evidence in gliomas demonstrates that tumor cells can form functional synapses with surrounding neurons, enabling them to directly receive and induce neuronal activity (Winkler et al. 2023). This bi-directional tumor-neuron crosstalk not only supports tumor growth, invasion and survival, but also contributes to normal tissue toxicity including excitotoxicity and tumor-associated seizures (Hatcher et al. 2020). Importantly, neurotransmitter signaling within the tumor microenvironment is not restricted to neurons and cancer cells. Immune cells including microglia, macrophages and infiltrating lymphocytes also express receptors for glutamate, GABA and acetylcholine, allowing neurotransmitters to directly influence immune cell activation, cytokine production and migration (Winkler et al. 2023). Through modulation of these pathways, polyamines may further shape the immune landscape of brain tumors, contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment that favors tumor progression. As such, polyamine-mediated regulation of neurotransmitter signaling presents a critical interface linking tumor biology, neural activity and anti-tumor immunity.

Immune cell signaling

Beyond their established role in neural communication, polyamines are emerging as key modulators of immune cell signaling processes at multiple levels. A recent study demonstrated that spermine binds directly to the FREM and SH2 domains of JAK1, disrupting interactions between JAK1 and cytokine receptors, thereby restraining JAK1 phosphorylation in response to cytokines such as type I and type II interferons, IL-2 and IL-6 (Xu et al. 2024). At the DNA level, both spermine and spermidine bind DNA, inducing a B-to-Z transition that diminishes activation of the cytosolic DNA sensor cGAS and subsequent downstream INFβ production, thereby limiting inflammatory signaling (Zhao et al. 2023). Polyamines also regulate immune cell phenotype through translational control; hypusination of eukaryotic initiation factor 5a (eIF5a), a spermidine dependent post-translational modification, is critical for maintaining helper T cell lineage fidelity and promoting an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype (Puleston et al. 2019, 2021).

Within brain tumors, these regulatory mechanisms are particularly relevant as polyamines have been shown to support myeloid cell survival by buffering intracellular pH, reinforcing an immunosuppressive microenvironment in glioma (Miska et al. 2021). Polyamines further dampen the anti-tumor immune response by disrupting T cell receptor (TCR) clustering through downregulation of membrane cholesterol levels, impairing the establishment of the immunological synapse and blunting CD8 + T cell activation which limits the cytotoxic responses against tumor cells (Hibino et al. 2023). Collectively, polyamines orchestrate multi-level suppression of anti-tumor immune signaling, acting through cytokine modulation, regulation of DNA sensing and inhibition of TCR-MHC complex formation.

Ongoing clinical trials targetting polyamine metabolism in cns malignancies

Given the strong evidence of direct links between polyamines and classical oncogenes, disruption of polyamine metabolism has long been recognized as a promising anti-cancer strategy (Casero, Murray Stewart et al. 2018; Khan et al. 2021; Chin et al. 2022). DFMO (diflouromethylornithine/eflornithine) is an ODC1 inhibitor that was originally approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1990 for African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness). Clinical examination of DFMO in glioma trials has been ongoing since 1989 (Levin et al. 2018). In 2014, Orbus Therapeutics received breakthrough therapy designation from the FDA for Eflornithine for the treatment of patients with anaplastic glioma and in December 2023, DFMO gained approval from the FDA for high-risk neuroblastoma as a maintenance therapy (Shakeel et al. 2024). A 2018 retrospective analysis and review has showed a meaningful survival benefit for DFMO as a single agent (3 g/m2 orally, every 8 h for 6 weeks) and in combination with nitrosourea-based therapies for anaplastic glioma (Levin et al. 2018). These findings have catalyzed ongoing clinical trials involving over 500 patients (Fig. 2). In parallel, the polyamine transport inhibitor AMXT-1501 has gained interest, driven by preclinical efficacy in diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG) and neuroblastoma, and early phase clinical trials have been started to evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of AMXT-1501 in cancer patients (NCT05500508, NCT05717153, NCT06465199) (Fig. 2) (Gamble et al. 2019; Khan et al. 2021).

Conclusions

Polyamines are emerging as central regulators of cell-cell signaling processes across multiple physiological systems, integrating traditionally distinct neural, immunological and oncogenic crosstalk modalities. In the healthy brain, polyamines orchestrate neurotransmission and neuroimmune interactions, supporting synaptic function and immune homeostasis. In brain tumors, where polyamines are frequently elevated, these same pathways are often hijacked to facilitate tumor growth, neuronal hyperexcitability and immune evasion. These convergent effects position polyamines as key mediators of tumor-host interactions within the CNS. In light of ongoing clinical trials aimed at disrupting polyamine metabolism in brain tumors, a better understanding of the multifaceted roles through which polyamines regulate intercellular communication at multiple levels is essential. Such insights may enable the development of combinatorial therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target tumor-intrinsic growth pathways and tumor-induced alterations to the cellular microenvironment. Ultimately, defining the multifaceted roles of polyamines in CNS malignancies offers a promising framework for designing integrated therapies that limit tumor progression while preserving normal brain function. Beyond primary CNS malignancies, neural signaling pathways have been increasingly implicated in the progression of peripheral tumors as well (Monje et al. 2020; Hanahan and Monje 2023). Thus, the role of elevated tumor polyamines in modulating neural and immune signaling may extend beyond the CNS. This concept may be particularly relevant in the context of brain metastasis, where metastatic tumor cells must adapt to and exploit the neural microenvironment. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting polyamine metabolism may hold promise not only for primary CNS malignancies, but also in peripheral cancers that engage in interactions with the peripheral nervous system or metastasize to the brain.
