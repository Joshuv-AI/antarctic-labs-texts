Letter to the Editor Response
Authors: Giorgi Sekania, Giorgi Svanishvili, Irakli Chelishvili, Velodi Gakharia
Published: 2026

We thank Drs. Gomez Puerta and Ramirez Gutierrez for their interest in our illustrative case and their insightful comments regarding the taxonomical and morphological challenges inherent in diagnosing atypical parasitic infections of the CNS.

We agree that the scolex, specifically the rostellar hooks, is the definitive morphological structure for distinguishing Taenia solium from Taenia saginata and other cestodes. In clinical neurosurgery, however, the primary objective is the safe gross-total resection of the lesion to relieve mass effect and prevent neurological deterioration. As noted in our report, the extraction of a 15-cm motile helminth from the brain parenchyma is a procedure that carries a risk of fragmentation. While every effort was made to preserve the specimen, the limitations of extracting a fragile organism from the temporal lobe precluded the perfect preservation of the scolex required for the specific hook measurements mentioned by the authors.

The authors raise a valid point regarding the potential for misidentification of calcareous corpuscles as eggs. We appreciate this observation. However, our histopathological team identified structures consistent with taeniid eggs based on the presence of thick, striated shells, distinct from the concentric laminations typically seen in calcareous corpuscles. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that in rare, aberrant presentations such as this, molecular analysis—specifically polymerase chain reaction targeting the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I gene—represents the gold standard for species confirmation.1,2

The authors suggest the intriguing possibility that the organism may represent a strobilocercus larval stage of the genus Hydatigera (formerly Taenia taeniaeformis), rather than an adult T. solium. We recognize that Hydatigera larvae can present as a segmented strobilocercus with an evaginated scolex, morphologically mimicking an adult tapeworm.3 While human infection with Hydatigera species is exceptionally rare, with few historical precedents,4 it remains a sig­nificant differential diagnosis in cases of atypical cysticercosis.

If this case were indeed a Hydatigera infection, it would represent a zoonotic rarity comparable in significance to the ectopic migration of an adult T. solium. Regardless of whether the organism is a sexually mature T. solium or a strobilocercus of Hydatigera larvae, the clinical lesson remains vital: large, motile, segmented helminths can mimic metastatic malignancy in the brain, necessitating a broader differential diagnosis in neuro-oncology.

We fully support the authors’ call for the integration of molecular diagnostics in future cases of aberrant parasitic infections. As global travel and zoonotic transmission dynamics evolve, the collaboration between neurosurgeons and veterinary parasitologists becomes increasingly essential to unravel the complex biology of these pathogens.
