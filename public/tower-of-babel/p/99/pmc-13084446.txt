Predicting and Controlling the Thermal Conductivity of Portland Cement for Underground Conditions
Authors: Shengda Shen, Yuhuan Bu, Chang Lu, Huajie Liu, Shenglai Guo, Shujie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In low-temperature geothermal development (typically <150 °C), significant heat loss through the wellboreespecially across the cement sheathreduces produced fluid temperature and system efficiency. Conventional oil well cement, with high thermal conductivity (0.8–1.5 W/(m·K)), offers poor insulation, undermining geothermal project economics. To address this, we investigate prediction and control strategies for the thermal conductivity of concrete under subsurface conditions. Systematic experiments reveal that, within practical ranges, thermal conductivity positively correlates with moisture content, curing temperature, and age but negatively with porosity, permeability, and water-to-cement ratio. A regression model for Jiahua G-grade cement accurately predicts thermal conductivity based on these parameters, identifying moisture content as the dominant factor. Crucially, incorporating impermeable insulating additives to maintain low internal moistureby preserving air-filled, uninvaded poresemerges as the most effective approach to enhance insulation. This strategy enables tailored cement formulations with thermal conductivity below 0.4 W/(m·K) while ensuring mechanical integrity. The findings provide a robust technical foundation for designing high-performance insulating cement systems that minimize wellbore heat loss, improve geothermal efficiency, and support sustainable utilization of low-temperature geothermal resources.

Introduction

Geothermal energy, as a new type of clean energy, features high environmental benefits, economic affordability, good stability, and abundant resources in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and western Yunnan regions of China, showing broad prospects for development. Geothermal energy refers to the heat stored within the Earth’s rocks, fluids, and magma bodies that can be harnessed by humans. The exploitation of geothermal resources closely resembles oil extraction, both requiring well cementing to ensure zonal isolation and structural integrity. For medium-temperature and low-temperature geothermal systems, the utilization efficiency is highly sensitive to the thermal performance of the cement sheath. Conventional oil well cement, however, exhibits high thermal conductivity (typically 0.8–1.5 W/(m·K)) and poor insulation, leading to significant heat loss during hot water production and reinjection. This results in a substantial drop in wellhead fluid temperature compared to reservoir conditions, severely undermining the cascading utilization efficiency of geothermal energy. −

Current strategies to mitigate wellbore heat loss primarily include casing insulation and annular thermal barriers. However, casing insulation is costly, prone to thermal bridging at joints, and may accelerate casing corrosion due to insulating fluids in the annulus, limiting its widespread adoption. − In contrast, adjusting the thermal conductivity of well cement offers a more cost-effective and integrally compatible alternative, drawing increasing research attention. ,

To date, research on insulating cement systems for geothermal wells remains in its early stages. Most studies focus on the influence of individual factorssuch as additives, porosity, or testing conditionson thermal conductivity. − Yang investigated how filler type, particle size, and dosage affect cement thermal conductivity based on thermal conduction path and dense packing theories. Fuchs et al. reported in 2010 that smaller average pore sizes in cementleading to larger specific surface areas and more tortuous heat transfer pathsresult in lower thermal conductivity. Zhang further demonstrated a positive correlation between cement thermal conductivity and test temperature. Nevertheless, under actual downhole conditions, the thermal behavior of cement sheaths is also governed by formation temperature, moisture saturation, and long-term fluid–rock interactionsrelationships that remain poorly understood. Moreover, systematic approaches to actively regulate the insulation performance of cement sheaths are still lacking.

It should be noted that while most thermal insulation research has historically focused on the building sectorwhere insulating materials are applied as external wall claddingsthe adaptation of such concepts to geothermal wellbores presents unique challenges. Two primary approaches have been explored: (1) incorporating low-conductivity fillers into cement and (2) engineering porous structures (e.g., foam cement). However, both faced significant technical hurdles. In high-temperature geothermal environments, conventional cement matrices suffer from strength degradation, compromising the structural stability of the porous framework; once this skeleton collapses, the insulating performance deteriorates, accelerating heat loss. Meanwhile, foam cement systems are prone to bubble coalescence or collapse during the critical setting period before gelation, leading to nonuniform or collapsed pore structures and drastically reduced insulation efficacy. Furthermore, the interconnected porosity inherent in many foam cements allows formation fluids to infiltrate and replace air in the poresthereby increasing the thermal conductivity and undermining long-term thermal barrier performance.

Against this backdrop, this study aims to systematically investigate how key variablesincluding cement mix design (notably water-to-cement ratio), curing conditions (temperature and time), and physical properties (moisture content, porosity, permeability)collectively govern the insulation performance of conventional cement under subsurface conditions. The goal is to establish a predictive model for thermal conductivity as a function of water-to-cement ratio, moisture content, and curing temperature and to propose practical, field-applicable strategies for tuning the thermal behavior of cement sheaths. This work provides essential theoretical and methodological support for enhancing the efficiency and sustainability of geothermal resource development.

Sample Preparation and Parameter Selection

Preparation of thermal insulation concrete refers to API standards. When preparing the cement slurry, the required amounts of distilled water and solid materials are measured according to the formulation, then the distilled water is poured into the mixing cup, the stirrer is set to rotate at a low speed, and the evenly mixed solid materials are poured in within 15 s. Subsequently, the stirring speed is appropriately increased and continued for 35 s to avoid breaking the insulation material at higher stirring speeds. The prepared cement slurry is then poured into curing molds and placed under certain temperature and pressure conditions for curing. After the curing process is completed, the test samples are removed and placed in a vacuum drying oven to dry to a constant weight.

In the experiment investigating the impact of porosity and permeability on the thermal insulation performance of cement, variation in cement porosity and permeability was achieved by adjusting the water-cement ratio. It was observed that when the water/cement ratio is less than 0.4, the fluidity of the cement slurry is poor, and when it exceeds 0.55, the stability is insufficient and prone to stratification. Therefore, water-cement ratios of 0.4, 0.45, 0.5, and 0.55 were selected for further study. In the experiment studying the influence of the moisture content on the thermal insulation performance of cement, the variation in the cement moisture content was achieved by adjusting the relative moisture content. Considering the broad spectrum and 3D adaptability of the study, relative moisture contents of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 were selected. In the experiment investigating the effect of curing conditions on the thermal insulation performance of cement, curing temperatures of 20 °C, 50 °C, 70 °C, 90 °C and curing times of 3, 5, 7, and 9 days were chosen.

Characterization of the Thermal Insulation Cement

Thermal Insulation Performance

The heat transfer in the cement ring is mainly dominated by thermal conduction. Therefore, the choice of using the thermal conductivity of materials to characterize the thermal insulation performance of cement is essential. Thermal conductivity refers to the inherent ability of a material to transfer or conduct heat. A higher thermal conductivity indicates poorer thermal insulation performance, while a lower thermal conductivity indicates better thermal insulation performance.

Considering that prolonged testing may lead to the evaporation of moisture inside the cement, thus reducing the accuracy of test results when studying the effect of water content on the thermal insulation performance of cement, this study adopted the transient hot wire method for testing. This method is advantageous due to its short measurement time, high accuracy, wide testing range, and simple sample preparation. The testing process follows the national standard GB/T 10297-2015 “Determination of Thermal Conductivity of Nonmetallic Solid Materials: Hot Wire Method”.

Porosity and Permeability

In order to study the influence of pore and permeability structure on the thermal insulation performance of cement paste, XNY-PDPII ultralow permeability tester and AU-300VP porosity tester were used to test the permeability and porosity of cement paste. The test process was carried out according to the oil and gas industry standard SY/T 5843-1997 “Determination of gas-water phase permeability” and the national standard GB/T 34533-2023 “Determination of shale porosity, permeability, and saturation”.

Influence of porosity on the thermal conductivity of concrete under different relative moisture content conditions.

From Table , it can be seen that the higher the water/cement ratio, the larger the porosity of the formed cement. Analysis suggests that when the water-cement ratio is relatively high, the effect of increasing the hydration degree of cement particles by free water gradually diminishes. The residual free water that does not participate in the hydration reaction of cement particles mainly forms pores inside the cement, increasing the porosity of the cement.

Furthermore, as shown in the results from Figure , under the same relative moisture content, the thermal conductivity of cement is negatively correlated with the porosity. This is because as the porosity increases, the cross-sectional area of the cement sample skeleton decreases, but the heat transfer capacity of the cement skeleton remains unchanged. Therefore, its internal heat transfer capacity decreases, leading to a reduction in the amount of heat that can be transmitted by the cement in a unit of time. However, on a macroscopic level, the heat transfer area of the cement remains unchanged, hence the thermal conductivity of the cement decreases. The larger the porosity of the cement, the more significant the impact of relative moisture content on the thermal conductivity of the cement. This is because when liquid penetrates the pores of the cement, it enhances the thermal conductivity of the pores. The larger the porosity, the more susceptible the cement is to the influence of liquid penetration, thereby resulting in a higher increase in the thermal conductivity of the cement.

Influence of permeability on the thermal conductivity of cement paste under different relative moisture content conditions.

As shown in Figure , under low moisture content conditions, the thermal conductivity of concrete continuously decreases with increasing permeability, indicating that its thermal insulation performance is positively correlated with the permeability within this range. This behavior can be attributed to the fact that higher permeability reflects enhanced pore connectivity and increased porosity within the concrete. Since the thermal conductivity of air is significantly lower than that of cement hydration products, the presence of abundant pores substantially reduces the overall effective thermal conductivity of the concrete, thereby weakening its heat conduction capability.

Furthermore, it is evident that the influence of the moisture content on thermal conductivity becomes more pronounced as permeability increases. This is because higher permeability corresponds to a more developed interconnected pore structure, which facilitates the easier ingress and filling of pore spaces by fluids. As water (which has a much higher thermal conductivity than air) replaces the low-conductivity air phase within the pores, the thermal conductivity of the concrete rises significantly.

The Impact of the Water-Cement Ratio on the Thermal Insulation Performance of Cement

The different thermal conductivity coefficients of concrete with different water/cement ratios (R) are shown in Figure . The curing environment is a 20 °C water bath, and the curing time is 7 days. Each condition was tested in triplicate, and the results are reported as the average value.

Effect of the relative moisture content on the thermal conductivity of cement under different water-cement ratios.

As shown in Figure , under varying relative moisture contents, the thermal conductivity of concrete decreases with an increase in the water-to-cement ratio. This trend is due to the fact that a higher water-to-cement ratio increases the porosity of the concrete. Given that the thermal conductivity of air within these pores is significantly lower than that of the cement matrix, this results in a substantial reduction in the overall effective thermal conductivity.

Additionally, as the water-to-cement ratio increases, the difference in the thermal conductivity curves corresponding to different moisture contents gradually widens. This indicates that an increase in the water-to-cement ratio exacerbates the impact of moisture content on thermal conductivity. The underlying reason is that a higher water-to-cement ratio corresponds to greater porosity, providing more pore space for water to fill. Consequently, the enhancing effect of moisture on thermal conductivity becomes more pronounced, as the presence of water, which has a much higher thermal conductivity compared with air, increasingly contributes to heat transfer within the material.

The Impact of Maintenance Conditions on the Thermal Insulation Performance of Cement

Curing Temperature

To investigate the effect of curing temperature on the thermal insulation performance of cement, a basic cement slurry with a water-cement ratio of 0.5 was cured at 20 °C, 50 °C, 70 °C, and 90 °C for 7 days. The thermal conductivity of the cement was measured, and the results are shown in Figure . Each condition was tested in triplicate, and the results are reported as the average value.

Thermal conductivity of cement under different curing temperature conditions.

Figure shows that within the temperature range of 20–90 °C, the thermal conductivity of concrete under different relative moisture contents tends to slowly increase with rising temperature. However, the difference in the relative moisture content has a more significant impact on the thermal conductivity. From the perspective of molecular thermal motion, an increase in the temperature accelerates the thermal motion of molecules in both the cement matrix and the pore medium, thereby enhancing the heat conduction efficiency of the solid skeleton. For air or water within pores, increased temperatures also enhance thermal convection and thermal radiation, contributing to the slow increase in the overall thermal conductivity of concrete with a rising curing temperature.

Analyzing from the angle of microstructural changes, the curing temperature significantly affects the hydration process of cement. Higher temperatures accelerate the hydration of cement, leading to the formation of denser hydration products, reducing porosity, and pore connectivity within the concrete and thus improving the overall thermal performance of the skeleton. Figure and Figure illustrate the internal microstructures of concrete under different curing conditions. As can be seen from these figures, concrete cured at 90 °C is denser, with pores filled by hydration products, resulting in an increase in the thermal conductivity.

Curing Time

Using the same water-cement ratio of 0.5, basic cement paste was cured for 3, 5, 7, 14, and 28 days at temperatures of 20 °C, 50 °C, 70 °C, and 90 °C. The thermal conductivity of the concrete was measured. The results are shown in Figure . Each condition was tested in triplicate, and the results are reported as the average value.

Thermal conductivity of concrete under different curing time conditions.

As shown in Figure , over the 28 day observation period, the thermal conductivity of concrete under different relative moisture contents initially increases with time and then gradually stabilizes. This trend closely aligns with the hydration process of cement: in the early stage, rapid hydration reactions generate substantial hydration products that progressively fill internal pores, reducing porosity and enhancing the densification of the solid matrix, thereby causing a gradual rise in thermal conductivity. After a certain period, the hydration reaction enters a deceleration phase and the microstructure of the concrete becomes relatively stable, leading to a plateau in thermal conductivity.

Technical Countermeasures for Regulating the Thermal Insulation Performance of Concrete for Underground Conditions

The research from Sections reveals that the thermal conductivity of cement is positively correlated with the water content and negatively correlated with the porosity and permeability within a certain range; the increase in the water/cement ratio can simultaneously enhance the porosity and permeability of cement; within a certain range, the thermal conductivity of cement is positively correlated with the curing temperature and curing time. Considering that the cement particles in cement will fully hydrate after a certain period of time, the thermal conductivity of cement under long-term curing conditions is mainly related to the water-cement ratio, water content, and curing temperature. Therefore, the thermal conductivity of cement for underground conditions can be predicted by using relevant principal influencing coefficients, and some technical measures can be taken to regulate and improve its thermal insulation performance according to requirements.

Prediction Model for Thermal Conductivity of Cement Suitable for Jiahua G-Grade Cement

During the on-site operation process, in order to ensure that the thermal insulation performance of cement meets the on-site requirements, it is necessary to adjust the thermal conductivity of the cement. For conventional cement slurries, statistical software can be used to perform regression analysis on indoor experimental results and establish a multiple regression model of factors and the thermal conductivity of cement. Through this model, the impact of each factor on the thermal conductivity of cement can be quantified and the thermal conductivity of the cement can be predicted.

By using SPSS software to perform regression analysis on the test results of Sections , 3.2, and 3.3, the regression model (eq ) for the thermal conductivity of Jiahua G-grade cement can be obtained. This model can predict the thermal conductivity of cement under certain ranges of water-cement ratio, moisture content, and curing temperature conditions. 1 kG=1.024+0.405H−0.843S+0.002T

In the equation, H represents the moisture content, dimensionless; S represents the water-cement ratio, dimensionless; T represents the curing temperature, °C. The regression model R 2 = 0.95, indicating a high precision and reliable practical value of the regression equation.

To directly compare the relative impact of different independent variables on the dependent variable, each coefficient is standardized. This method scales the data features uniformly, enabling the analysis of different variables on the same scale. Standardizing eq yields the standardized regression eq . 2 kG=0.927H−0.247S+0.228T

As indicated by eq , the influence of each parameter on the thermal conductivity of the cement slurry varies significantly. Among them, the moisture content has the highest standardized coefficient of 0.927, making it the dominant factor; the water-to-cement ratio exhibits a secondary effect with a standardized coefficient of −0.247; and curing temperature shows the weakest influence, with a standardized coefficient of 0.228.

Countermeasures for Regulating the Thermal Conductivity of Concrete for Underground Conditions

During on-site operations, ensuring that cement has sufficient thermal insulation performance to meet the site requirements is crucial. This necessitates precise adjustment of the thermal conductivity of concrete. Based on the impact and degree of influence of the concrete material ratio, curing conditions, and physical parameters of the concrete on the thermal insulation performance of concrete, the following strategies for controlling the thermal conductivity of concrete for underground conditions are established:(1)For conventional cement slurry systems used in well cementing, statistical software can be employed to develop a multiple regression model. Through regression analysis, the specific influence of each factor on the thermal conductivity of set cement can be quantified. Based on actual field requirements for thermal conductivity, these parameters can then be adjusted to achieve the desired thermal performance.(2)During field operations, formation temperature is a fixed and uncontrollable parameter; therefore, the curing temperatureand consequently the thermal conductivity of the cementcannot be actively manipulated. Instead, the thermal conductivity must be predicted based on the in situ formation temperature. Typically, formation temperature increases with depth, leading to a corresponding rise in the thermal conductivity of the hardened cement. As a result, even when the same cement slurry is used throughout the wellbore, the lower cement sheath (at greater depths) generally exhibits poorer insulation performance compared to the upper sections. Hence, thermal conductivity design targets should be established on a per-zone basis, with tailored insulating cement formulations and material dosages matched to the measured formation temperatures at different depths.(3)Among all controllable parameters, the water-to-cement ratio is relatively easy to adjust. While reducing the water-to-cement ratio may be considered for strength or stability reasons, it is also recognized thatwithin a certain rangeincreasing the water-to-cement ratio effectively lowers the thermal conductivity of the concrete by increasing porosity. However, this approach can adversely affect slurry rheology, stability, and final compressive strength. Therefore, the water-to-cement ratio must be carefully optimized to ensure that the resulting slurry meets all required field placement and performance criteria. In field operations, if adjustment of the water-to-cement ratio is constrained, controlling the internal moisture content of the concrete offers an alternative means to regulate its thermal conductivity. Properly reducing the moisture content can effectively lower the thermal conductivity and thereby significantly enhance the thermal insulation performance of the cement sheath. To achieve this, minimizing the amount of liquid present in the pores of the concrete is critical. However, under downhole conditions, the cement sheath remains in prolonged contact with formation fluids, leading to pore saturation over time. To maintain a low moisture content in such environments, specialized technical measures are required to prevent fluid ingress into specific pore structures and avoid the consequent increase in thermal conductivity. Therefore, incorporating impermeable insulating materials is proposed to preserve a sufficient volume of “uninvaded” air-filled pores within the concrete under subsurface conditionsensuring that the content of low-conductivity phases remains unaffected by formation fluids, and thereby mitigating the adverse impact of groundwater on the thermal insulation performance of the cement sheath.

Feasibility Verification of Using Impermeable Insulation Materials to Reduce the Thermal Conductivity of Concrete

To demonstrate that adding impermeable insulation materials in cement can reduce the adverse effects of formation fluids on the thermal insulation performance of cement, the following experiments were conducted. Cement sample A contains impermeable insulation materials (Y6000 hollow glass microspheres, 10 wt %), while cement sample B contains foam cement. The densities of the two cement samples are similar. Thermal conductivity measurements were carried out in dry and saturated conditions, as shown in Figure .

Comparison of thermal conductivity of cement under different insulation methods.

From Figure , it can be observed that due to the similar densities, the pore spaces under dry conditions are largely consistent. Consequently, the thermal conductivity of concrete with added impermeable insulation material is comparable to that of foam concrete under dry conditions. However, after saturation with water, the thermal conductivity of concrete with impermeable insulation material shows little variation, while the thermal conductivity of foam concrete significantly increases. The underlying reason for this can be attributed to the presence of numerous interconnected pore structures within the foam concrete. Upon saturation, these pores are filled with water, which has a much higher thermal conductivity than air, facilitating heat transfer and resulting in a substantial increase in its thermal conductivity. In contrast, the concrete with added impermeable insulation material possesses inherent properties that prevent moisture penetration and provide effective thermal insulation. Even in a saturated state, it can effectively limit the impact of moisture on the internal heat transfer process, thereby maintaining a relatively stable and low thermal conductivity.

The results of this experiment clearly demonstrate that incorporating impermeable insulating materials into cement can effectively reduce the moisture content of cement for underground conditions, thereby minimizing the impact on the thermal conductivity of the cement. Consequently, it can be concluded that the addition of impermeable insulating materials to cement is the most effective method for maintaining a low moisture content in cement, which enhances its thermal insulation properties.

Conclusion and Discussion

(1)The thermal conductivity of concrete is influenced by multiple factors: with respect to physical parameters, it increases with moisture content but decreases with porosity and permeability within a practical range; regarding mix design, it exhibits an inverse relationship with the water-to-cement ratio; and in terms of curing conditions, it rises with increasing curing temperature and duration, as more complete hydration leads to a denser microstructure.(2)A multiple regression model has been developed for Jiahua G-grade oil well concrete, enabling accurate prediction of thermal conductivity under specified combinations of water-to-cement ratio, moisture content, and curing temperature (within experimentally validated ranges). Among these variables, the moisture content is identified as the dominant factor governing thermal conductivity.(3)To mitigate heat loss in subsurface environments, a targeted strategy has been proposed: incorporating impermeable insulating additives (e.g., surface-coated hollow microspheres) into the cement slurry to suppress fluid ingress and maintain low internal moisture. This approach preserves air-filled poresthe primary source of low thermal conductivityand is the most effective means of enhancing the insulation performance of the cement sheath. The strategy provides actionable guidance for tailoring cement formulations to meet site-specific thermal requirements, ensuring reliable wellbore thermal barrier performance under real-world operational conditions.

(2)A multiple regression model has been developed for Jiahua G-grade oil well concrete, enabling accurate prediction of thermal conductivity under specified combinations of water-to-cement ratio, moisture content, and curing temperature (within experimentally validated ranges). Among these variables, the moisture content is identified as the dominant factor governing thermal conductivity.

(3)To mitigate heat loss in subsurface environments, a targeted strategy has been proposed: incorporating impermeable insulating additives (e.g., surface-coated hollow microspheres) into the cement slurry to suppress fluid ingress and maintain low internal moisture. This approach preserves air-filled poresthe primary source of low thermal conductivityand is the most effective means of enhancing the insulation performance of the cement sheath. The strategy provides actionable guidance for tailoring cement formulations to meet site-specific thermal requirements, ensuring reliable wellbore thermal barrier performance under real-world operational conditions.
