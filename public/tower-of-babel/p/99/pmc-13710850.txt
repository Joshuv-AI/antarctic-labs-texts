Sulfated polysaccharide from Gelidium crinale: modulation of angiogenesis and fibrosis in skin repair
Authors: Yi Liu, Haiyan Zheng, Yizhu Chen, Bomi Ryu, Zhong-Ji Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScar tissue formation is a common pathological outcome of skin repair after injury, characterized by excessive collagen deposition and closely associated with inflammation and angiogenesis. This study investigated the effects and related signaling changes of a food-derived sulfated polysaccharide from the red alga Gelidium crinale (GNP) on angiogenesis and scar formation using in vitro and in vivo models. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and human keloid fibroblasts (HKFs) were used to evaluate the biological activity of GNP, while an OSA-CMCS self-crosslinking hydrogel was employed as a delivery vehicle to improve local administration in a mouse wound-healing model. GNP significantly inhibited HUVEC migration, invasion, and tube formation, accompanied by reduced expression of angiogenesis-related factors, including PDGF, ANG-II, and HIF-1α, suggesting impaired angiogenic activation. In HKFs, GNP decreased TGF-β1 expression and modulated downstream fibrosis-related signaling, thereby suppressing collagen and fibronectin production. Moreover, incorporation of GNP into the OSA-CMCS hydrogel reduced inflammatory responses, angiogenesis, and collagen deposition in vivo. These findings demonstrate the potential of GNP as a natural anti-scar candidate and provide a promising strategy for the high-value utilization of Gelidium crinale polysaccharides. Subject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Drug discovery, Medical research

Introduction

Wound healing is an intrinsic tissue-repair process, and scar formation is a common outcome of this process. Scars are fibrotic tissues that arise during healing; excessive scar formation can markedly impair both physiological function and psychological well-being. Pathological scarring is characterized by dysregulated extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, excessive deposition of collagen, proteoglycans, and glycoproteins, disorganized collagen fiber architecture, and frequently aberrant angiogenesis1. Inflammation is a principal driver of scar formation, and modulation of the inflammatory response during wound healing may offer a therapeutic approach to prevent or reduce pathological scarring2. Transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) plays a central role in tissue repair and fibrotic remodeling, and persistent or excessive inflammatory stimulation can increase TGF-β1 expression and promote fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation. These myofibroblasts synthesize and secrete large amounts of ECM components, including collagen types I and III and fibronectin, thereby contributing to scar fibrosis3.

Pathological scar development is closely associated with angiogenesis, and scarred areas are often more microvascularized than normal skin4. Angiogenesis refers to the formation of new blood vessels from preexisting vasculature. In the early phase of normal wound healing, inflammatory cell infiltration stimulates angiogenesis, thereby supplying oxygen and nutrients required for tissue repair. Newly formed vessels support cell proliferation and migration into the wound bed, promote granulation tissue formation, and accelerate wound closure. However, excessive angiogenesis can lead to increased vascular density and abnormal vascular remodeling within scar tissue. Perivascular cells release pro‑angiogenic and pro‑fibrotic mediators such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which further drive fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition5. Moreover, the hyperpermeability of neovessels sustains recruitment of inflammatory cells, maintaining a localized inflammatory microenvironment that enhances fibroblast activation6. Thus, angiogenesis is not only a consequence of inflammation but also an important factor that sustains the inflammation-fibrosis cycle during scar progression.

Compared with terrestrial polysaccharides, marine polysaccharides have attracted increasing attention because of their structural diversity, broad biological activities, and relatively low toxicity. Among them, red algal polysaccharides exhibit anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunoregulatory properties, highlighting their potential for biomedical applications7. Several sulfated polysaccharides from red algae demonstrate anti-inflammatory, analgesic, or immunoregulatory activities in animal and cell models. For example, sulfated polysaccharides from Gracilaria caudata inhibited carrageenan-induced edema in mice8, and those from Solieria filiformis and Gracilaria cornea showed antioxidant and neuroprotective properties9,10. Hydrogels, owing to their three-dimensional ECM-like architecture, are widely used for wound care due to their biocompatibility, hydration ability, and capacity to reduce infection11. Recent studies have shown that hydrogel-based systems loaded with bioactive components, such as small molecules, siRNA, proteins, or exosomes, can modulate hypertrophic scar- and keloid-related processes. Despite advances in wound dressing technologies and the development of various hydrogel types, many of these systems primarily target a single process, such as inflammation, angiogenesis, or fibrosis12. Hydrogel systems capable of simultaneously regulating angiogenesis and fibroblast activation remain relatively limited, particularly those using marine sulfated polysaccharides as bioactive components.

GNP is a sulfated polysaccharide extracted from the red alga Gelidium crinale, and has been reported to exhibit antitumor, anti-inflammatory, and anti-atherosclerotic activities13,14. Both atherosclerosis and cancer are angiogenesis-dependent diseases, and similar signaling pathways govern pathological vessel formation in scarring15,16. Given its abundant sulfate and hydroxyl groups, GNP may influence VEGF- and TGF-β1-related signaling processes associated with angiogenesis and fibrosis, thereby providing a rationale for its potential anti-angiogenic and anti-fibrotic effects. In addition, previous studies have shown that sulfated polysaccharides can modulate multiple signaling responses involved in inflammatory and vascular remodeling, further supporting this possibility. Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that loading GNP into a hydrogel matrix could locally modulate pathological angiogenesis and fibroblast activation, thereby improving scar remodeling. Unlike many studies that examine anti-angiogenic activity alone, the present work was designed to evaluate whether GNP could coordinately regulate angiogenesis-related endothelial responses and fibrosis-related fibroblast activation in a scar-related context. Using in vitro and in vivo models, we examined whether GNP could reduce skin scar-related remodeling by suppressing angiogenesis and fibroblast collagen synthesis. Our findings support the potential of a GNP-loaded hydrogel as a scar-focused strategy for regulating the angiogenesis–fibrosis axis and provide a basis for the high-value utilization of Gelidium crinale polysaccharides.

Results

Effects of GNP on HUVEC proliferation, angiogenesis, migration, and invasion

The effect of GNP on HUVEC proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 1A, GNP did not significantly affect the viability of HUVECs at concentrations of 10–100 μg/mL. In the tube-formation assay (Fig. 1B), HUVECs in the VEGF-treated group formed a well-defined reticular network. Treatment with 10 μg/mL GNP did not significantly affect network formation, whereas exposure to 100 μg/mL reduced tube formation. In the scratch-wound assay, as shown in Fig. 1C, VEGF significantly enhanced HUVEC migration, while GNP treatment reduced the migratory capacity of HUVECs. In the invasion assay, cells that penetrated the Transwell membrane were stained purple; as shown in Fig. 1D, GNP reduced VEGF-induced HUVEC invasion. Collectively, these results indicate that GNP suppresses angiogenesis-related cellular behaviors in HUVECs. These inhibitory effects showed a concentration-dependent trend.

Effects of GNP on angiogenic and hypoxia-inducible factors in HUVECs

VEGFR-2 is a key receptor involved in VEGF signaling. Both Western blotting and immunofluorescence analyses revealed that GNP reduced VEGFR-2 phosphorylation in HUVECs (Fig. 2A). In the VEGF-treated group, as shown in Fig. 2B, intense green fluorescence was concentrated at the cell edges, whereas this signal was reduced following GNP treatment. Immunofluorescence colocalization analysis showed that VEGF increased the overlap between VEGFR-2 and actin-rich regions, whereas GNP treatment reduced this effect in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2C, D). Human angiotensin II (ANG II) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) are angiogenesis-related factors. In addition, the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) is associated with angiogenic responses under hypoxic conditions. As shown in Fig. 2E, F, GNP reduced the expression of angiogenic factors and HIF-1α in HUVECs. Collectively, these findings suggest that GNP modulates VEGF-related signaling in endothelial cells.

Effects of GNP on the MAPK signaling pathway and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) expression in HUVECs

The MAPK signaling cascade and MMPs are associated with VEGF-related signaling and cellular migration processes. As shown in Fig. 2G, H, GNP reduced the activation of the MAPK pathway and reduced the protein levels of MMP-2 and MMP-9, which was consistent with reduced cellular migration observed in functional assays. These findings are consistent with the scratch and invasion assays, suggesting that GNP is associated with the modulation of VEGF-related signaling in endothelial cells.

Effects of GNP on HKF cell proliferation, migration, and ROS levels

The effects of GNP on HKF cell proliferation were determined using CCK-8 and colony-formation assays. As shown in Fig. 3A, GNP did not significantly affect the viability of HKF cells at concentrations of 10–100 μg/mL. After continuous exposure to 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL GNP for 14 days, higher concentrations reduced colony formation by HKF cells (Fig. 3B, D), suggesting an inhibitory effect on HKF cell growth.

DCFH-DA fluorescence assays showed that GNP was associated with reduced intracellular ROS levels in HKF cells, and this trend was concentration-dependent (Fig. 3C, E). Moreover, wound-healing assays demonstrated that GNP at ≥10 μg/mL reduced HKF cell migration in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3B).

Effects of GNP on TGF-β1 and collagen-related signaling pathways in HKF cells

Abnormal TGF-β signaling is implicated in fibrotic and inflammatory diseases. The accumulation of collagen and fibronectin (FN) is a characteristic feature of scar formation. As shown in Fig. 4A, B, GNP reduced TGF-β1 and its receptor TGFβR1, as well as key profibrotic markers including α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), ET-1, and VEGF. These results suggest that GNP is associated with the regulation of TGF-β–related profibrotic factors.

The Smad signaling pathway is associated with TGF-β–mediated fibrotic responses. As shown in Fig. 4C, GNP reduced Smad2/3 phosphorylation and Smad4 expression while increasing the expression of the negative regulator Smad7. Furthermore, GNP decreased Col-1 and FN expression and increased MMP-1 levels (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that GNP is associated with the regulation of collagen-related processes in fibroblasts.

Effects of GNP on inflammatory responses in HKF cells

Excessive inflammation has been associated with scar progression. As shown in Fig. 4E, F, GNP reduced the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of IκB-α and p65 in the NF-κB pathway. As shown in Fig. 4G, GNP reduced the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, iNOS, and COX-2 in HKF cells, suggesting an association with reduced inflammatory responses.

Biocompatibility of OSA-CMCS and OSA-CMCS-GNP hydrogels

As shown in Fig. 5A, the OSA-CMCS-GNP hydrogel appeared light yellow compared with the OSA-CMCS hydrogel. The CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 5B) showed that both hydrogels did not significantly affect the viability of HKFs and HUVECs, indicating good cytocompatibility with fibroblasts and endothelial cells.

Characterization of hydrogels

Scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 5C) revealed that the incorporation of GNP was associated with changes in the microstructure of the hydrogel, leading to smaller and more uniformly arranged pores. Since polysaccharide bioactivity generally correlates with sulfate content, the characteristic S=O stretching vibration band of sulfate groups (1200–1250 cm−1) was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 5D, Fourier-transform infrared spectra of GNP exhibited a distinct S=O band at 1240 cm−1. This band was absent in OSA-CMCS but appeared clearly in OSA-CMCS-GNP at ~1250 cm−1, indicating the successful incorporation of sulfate-containing GNP into the hydrogel.

Effects of GNP-loaded hydrogel dressing on mice epidermal wound healing and scar formation

In a dorsal full-thickness wound model (Fig. 6A), GNP treatment did not significantly alter wound closure compared with the saline-treated group. Both OSA-CMCS and OSA-CMCS-GNP groups showed slightly accelerated scab detachment and improved wound healing. By day 20, scars in the GNP-treated group were smaller than those in the saline-treated group.

Effect of GNP-loaded OSA-CMCS hydrogel on the healing of mice epidermal wounds.

A Schematic representation of the dorsal skin of mice from day 2 to day 20; B H&E and Masson staining images of skin tissues in the mice after treatment.

Histological analyses using H&E and Masson staining (Fig. 6B) revealed that the epidermis in the saline-treated group was thicker than in the GNP and hydrogel-treated groups; the OSA-CMCS group also had a slightly thicker epidermis than OSA-CMCS-GNP. In Masson staining, collagen fibers appeared blue and muscle fibers red. Abundant, disorganized collagen was observed in the saline-treated group scars, whereas GNP treatment was associated with reduced collagen deposition and improved fiber alignment. Collagen content was lowest in OSA-CMCS-GNP compared with OSA-CMCS or GNP alone. These in vivo findings suggest that GNP is associated with reduced scar formation and that its activity is retained after hydrogel incorporation.

Effects of GNP-loaded hydrogel dressing on TGF-β1 and α-SMA expression in mice scar tissue

Immunohistochemistry and Western blotting were employed to examine TGF-β1 and α-SMA expression in scar tissue. As shown in Fig. 7A, positive staining (brown) for TGF-β1 and α-SMA was reduced in the GNP and hydrogel groups relative to the saline-treated group. Western blotting (Fig. 7B) demonstrated reduced levels of TGF-β1 in both GNP and OSA-CMCS-GNP groups, whereas α-SMA was reduced only in OSA-CMCS-GNP. These data suggest a more pronounced reduction in TGF-β1 than in α-SMA. As α-SMA is a marker of myofibroblasts, these findings suggest a potential association with reduced myofibroblast-related responses.

Effect of GNP-loaded OSA-CMCS hydrogel on scar-related and inflammatory protein expression in mice skin.

A Immunohistochemical staining for TGF-β1 and α-SMA in mice skin sections; B expression of TGF-β1 and α-SMA proteins in mice skin; C expression of inflammatory cytokine proteins in mice skin; D immunohistochemical staining for CD31 in mice skin sections. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001.

Western blot analysis of inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 7C) showed that both GNP and OSA-CMCS-GNP reduced IL-1β and IL-6 compared with the saline-treated group, with IL-6 levels being lowest in OSA-CMCS-GNP. These findings suggest an association with reduced inflammatory responses in scar tissue, with a more pronounced effect observed in the OSA-CMCS-GNP group.

Effects of GNP-loaded hydrogel dressing on inflammatory markers and microvessel density in mice scar tissue

CD31 immunohistochemistry is widely used to quantify microvessel density17 To further assess the effect of GNP on angiogenesis during scar formation, CD31 expression was examined in scar tissue sections. As shown in Fig. 7D, DAB-positive staining (brown) indicated microvessels. The saline-treated group exhibited the highest vessel density, slightly lower in OSA-CMCS, and reduced in GNP and OSA-CMCS-GNP groups. These results suggest reduced angiogenic activity in the GNP- and OSA-CMCS-GNP-treated groups during scar formation. Taken together, these histological and molecular findings provide in vivo evidence that GNP and GNP-loaded hydrogel are associated with modulation of collagen organization, inflammatory responses, and vascular remodeling during scar-related wound repair.

Discussion

The formation of scar tissue represents the outcome of the physiological repair process following skin injury, essentially resulting from the fibrotic evolution of granulation tissue18. Although various physical, pharmacological, and surgical interventions for scar management are currently available in clinical practice, they generally suffer from substantial interindividual variability in efficacy, high recurrence rates, and significant side effects19. Considering the limited ability of existing therapies to precisely target the core signaling networks underlying fibrosis, this study aimed to develop a multifunctional strategy capable of simultaneously modulating multiple pathological processes, including inflammation, fibrosis, and aberrant angiogenesis. The OSA-CMCS hydrogel, serving as a wound dressing matrix, integrates the broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of carboxymethyl chitosan with the excellent biocompatibility and gel-forming properties of alginate. Its three-dimensional network structure can efficiently absorb wound exudates and maintain a moist environment conducive to healing, while providing a physical barrier that prevents bacterial invasion, thereby reducing infection risk and creating favorable conditions for tissue repair20. To this end, GNP, a marine-derived sulfated polysaccharide extracted from red algae, was incorporated into the carboxymethyl chitosan–oxidized sodium alginate (OSA-CMCS) hydrogel. Based on this design, the hydrogel was expected to act not only as a local delivery platform for GNP, but also as a microenvironment-supporting dressing that could facilitate tissue repair while enhancing the anti-scar activity of the loaded polysaccharide.

The selection of the active molecule was a critical aspect of this study. Marine algae have attracted considerable interest in the field of tissue repair due to their favorable biocompatibility and diverse bioactivities. Studies have indicated that algal polysaccharides possess excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, which are advantageous for wound healing and render them suitable for various wound dressing applications21. Moreover, hydrogels incorporating sulfated polysaccharides derived from algae exhibit superior gelation properties and wound-healing performance compared with conventional hydrogel systems22. Shen et al. demonstrated that a bilayer thiolated alginate/PEG diacrylate (BSSPD) hydrogel promoted rapid, scarless wound healing by suppressing capillary over proliferation and late-stage collagen deposition23. Following skin injury, tissue repair involves coordinated processes including inflammation, cell migration, and extracellular matrix deposition, which often culminate in scar formation rather than complete regeneration. Pathological scars are classified into two major types: keloids and hypertrophic scars. Keloids are characterized by intense inflammatory responses and elevated levels of proinflammatory mediators, which promote the establishment of chronic inflammation24. The magnitude of the inflammatory response is a key determinant of scar formation, and excessive inflammation can lead to pathological scarring. The marked paucity of inflammation during fetal wound healing underlies its capacity for scarless repair25. Fibroblasts are highly responsive to TGF-β1, exhibiting rapid proliferation and delayed apoptosis; they may differentiate into myofibroblasts that express α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) to promote wound contraction26. Under the influence of cytokines, fibroblasts and myofibroblasts synthesize large amounts of collagen and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components, thereby accelerating fibrosis and generating pathological scar tissue27. These pathological features, including excessive collagen deposition and aberrant fibroblast activity, contribute to the persistent growth characteristics of scar tissue28.

Aberrant activation of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway is widely implicated in scar formation. This pathway represents a classical target for antifibrotic drug development and has been extensively investigated; three main strategies for inhibiting TGF-β signaling have emerged to date, namely antisense oligonucleotides, monoclonal antibodies, and small-molecule inhibitors of the TGF-β receptors29. TGF-β1 binds to its specific receptors to activate Smad signaling and induce fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation. Concurrently, the inflammatory NF-κB signaling pathway plays an important role in scar formation: upon inflammatory stimuli, NF-κB is activated and upregulates proinflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, iNOS, and COX-2, thereby exacerbating inflammation and further promoting fibroblast activation and scar proliferation30. The present results showed that GNP reduced TGF-β1 expression and was associated with decreased expression of fibrosis-related markers, including α-SMA, collagen, and fibronectin. In addition, alterations in Smad signaling components were observed following GNP treatment. Furthermore, GNP was associated with reduced activation of NF-κB signaling and decreased expression of proinflammatory mediators.

Fibroblasts are not only the main source of extracellular matrix, but also play a key regulatory role in the scar microenvironment through paracrine mechanisms. The capacity of fibroblasts to promote angiogenesis via secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) has been well documented in studies of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which support tumor growth through secretion of multiple proangiogenic factors31. Although VEGF-targeting agents are used clinically, they may adversely affect normal tissue repair. An important finding of the present study is that GNP reduced VEGF expression in fibroblasts, suggesting a potential role in modulating the interaction between fibrogenesis and angiogenesis. Unlike strategies that directly neutralize VEGF, GNP appears to influence angiogenesis through the regulation of fibroblast-related responses, which may represent a complementary approach to existing anti-angiogenic strategies. This distinction is important because suppression of angiogenic signaling alone does not necessarily imply effectiveness in scar-related fibrotic remodeling.

Aberrant angiogenesis supplies the nutritional support required for the persistent growth of hypertrophic scars and constitutes a key condition for their progression32. Rapid expansion of scar tissue often generates a hypoxic microenvironment that activates hypoxia-inducible factor‑1α (HIF‑1α), which in turn induces expression of proangiogenic factors such as VEGF and promotes neovascularization within the scar33. Angiogenic mediators, including vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR‑2), angiopoietin‑2 (ANG‑2), and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), play critical roles in angiogenesis by promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and lumen formation, thereby driving new vessel formation34. In addition to its role in wound angiogenesis, VEGF can influence scar formation: VEGF expression is suppressed in scarless fetal wounds, while exogenous VEGF can induce scar formation35. PDGF contributes to the stabilization of newly formed capillaries in granulation tissue, stimulates fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation to promote fibrosis, and regulates MMP expression to affect remodeling during wound healing36. Matrix metalloproteinases MMP‑2 and MMP‑9 degrade the extracellular matrix during angiogenesis, providing space and pathways for endothelial cell migration and neovessel formation37. The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway is also implicated in the regulation of angiogenesis; MAPK influences endothelial function and angiogenesis by modulating the expression of cell-cycle and proliferation-related genes38. Consistent with these mechanisms, the present results showed that GNP was associated with reduced expression of VEGFR-2, ANG-2, and PDGF, as well as decreased levels of HIF-1α, MMP-2, and MMP-9. In addition, reduced activation of the MAPK signaling pathway was observed following GNP treatment, which is consistent with the inhibition of endothelial cell proliferation and migration. Taken together, these findings support the involvement of GNP in the regulation of angiogenesis-related processes during scar formation and are consistent with its observed anti-scar effects in vitro and in vivo.

In the present study, the hydrogel primarily served as a delivery matrix to facilitate the local application of GNP. To further evaluate these observations, in vivo evaluations were conducted using a murine model. The results showed that GNP-loaded hydrogels improved scar phenotype and reduced the expression of relevant molecular markers. Histological analyses by H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed more organized collagen fiber alignment in the scar regions of the GNP-treated group and the GNP-hydrogel group. The OSA-CMCS hydrogel alone produced effects comparable to those of the saline-treated group, indicating limited intrinsic anti-scar efficacy of the hydrogel matrix. By contrast, both the GNP-only group and the GNP-loaded hydrogel group exhibited clear scar-inhibitory effects, with more pronounced outcomes observed for the OSA-CMCS–GNP composite. This enhanced effect may be related to the role of the hydrogel as a local delivery matrix, which may prolong the retention of GNP at the wound site. Hydrogels are widely recognized as drug-delivery platforms for tissue repair, primarily because they enable localized and sustained release of therapeutics39. To further investigate these effects, key protein markers were quantified by Western blot. The results of the Western blot experiment demonstrated that both the GNP group and the OSA-CMCS-GNP group reduced the expression level of TGF-β1 protein within the core anti-fibrotic pathway, and that the inhibitory effects of the two groups were similar. Notably, the inhibitory effect of the OSA-CMCS-GNP group was more pronounced than that in the GNP group. Furthermore, with regard to inflammatory regulation and angiogenesis, the OSA-CMCS–GNP group showed a greater reduction in proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 in tissue homogenates and in microvascular density in comparison to the GNP-alone group. These in vivo observations are consistent with the in vitro findings and suggest that the hydrogel scaffold may enhance the inhibitory effects of GNP on both inflammation and angiogenesis. Importantly, because wound closure was not obviously delayed in the GNP-treated groups, the reduced microvascular density is more appropriately interpreted as restraint of excessive angiogenic responses associated with scar formation, rather than impairment of physiological vascularization during wound healing. Collectively, the in vivo experiments support the anti-scar potential of GNP and suggest that the OSA-CMCS hydrogel may enhance its local biological effects, thereby providing a basis for further exploration of this composite dressing. Although OSA-CMCS was used as the delivery matrix in the present study, other hydrogel systems may also be explored as potential carriers for GNP in future work.

In summary, the red algal sulfated polysaccharide GNP was associated with reduced inflammatory responses, collagen-related fibrosis, and angiogenesis-related remodeling, collectively suggesting coordinated regulation of scar-related processes. In vivo experiments showed that GNP incorporated into the OSA-CMCS hydrogel retained these biological effects, indicating that this hydrogel may serve as a feasible local delivery strategy for GNP. Taken together, these findings suggest that GNP may serve as a natural anti-scar candidate and provide a scientific basis for the high-value utilization of Gelidium crinale sulfated polysaccharides. Further studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms in greater depth, to strengthen in vivo validation, and to further optimize GNP-based delivery systems. These efforts may support the future development of GNP for cosmetic, anti-scar, and angiogenesis-related biomedical applications.

Methods

Materials

GNP was prepared from Gelidium crinale, collected from coastal waters near Naozhou Island at an approximate depth of 1 m, as previously described40. GNP had a weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of 25.8 kDa, a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of 13.4 kDa, and a polydispersity index (Mw/Mn) of 1.92, as determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC). The chemical composition of GNP included 75.78% total sugars, 16.50% sulfate groups, 5.27% protein, and 8.42% reducing sugars40. The GNP used in this study was obtained from the same batch as reported previously.

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and keloid fibroblasts (HKFs) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), trypsin (0.25%), penicillin/streptomycin, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Sodium alginate (SA) and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Matrigel was obtained from Corning Incorporated (Corning, NY, USA). DAPI and DCFH-DA fluorescent probes were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology (Beijing, China). Antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA) and Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining kits were purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). All other chemicals and solvents were of analytical grade.

Cell culture, treatment, and cell viability assay

HUVECs and HKF cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HKF cells were treated with GNP (10, 50, or 100 μg/mL) for 24 h. For HUVECs, GNP (10, 50, and 100 μg/mL) was added for 2 h before VEGF (10 ng/mL) stimulation for 24 h.

For the cell viability assay, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with various concentrations of GNP for 24 h. Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated cells.

Colony formation in HKF cells

Cells were diluted to a density of 1 × 10³ cells/mL and seeded at 1 mL per well in 6-well plates. GNP (0, 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL) and 10% FBS were added, and the plates were incubated under standard conditions. The culture medium was replaced every 7 days with fresh medium containing the same concentrations of GNP and FBS. After 14 days, cells were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for colony visualization and quantification.

ROS accumulation in HKF cells

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h. They were then treated with GNP (0, 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, 10 μM DCFH-DA was added and incubated for 30 min in the dark. After washing with PBS three times, fluorescence intensity was observed and recorded using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Wound healing assay

HUVECs and HKF cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until cell confluence reached >90%. Three linear scratches were created using a sterile pipette tip, and floating cells were removed by washing with PBS. Medium containing GNP (0, 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL) was added, and for HUVECs, VEGF (10 ng/mL) was introduced 2 h later. After 24 h of incubation, wound closure was observed and recorded using an inverted microscope.

Cell invasion assay

Transwell chambers were pre-coated with 100 μL of Matrigel (300 μg/mL) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C to allow gel formation. The lower chamber contained 500 μL of DMEM with 10% FBS. Serum-starved HUVECs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded into the upper chamber with 100 μL DMEM containing GNP (0, 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL). After 24 h of incubation, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed. The membranes were stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and invaded cells were observed and imaged using an inverted microscope.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (60 μL/well) was added to precooled 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow solidification. Serum-starved HUVECs (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded in 100 μL of DMEM containing GNP (0, 10, and 100 μg/mL). After 4 h of incubation, tube formation was observed and photographed under an inverted microscope.

Hydrogel preparation and characterization

The OSA-CMCS hydrogel was prepared according to the method described by Wu et al20. in which this hydrogel system was previously applied in wound healing studies. Equal volumes of 4% CMCS and 4% oxidized sodium alginate (OSA) were mixed to obtain a 1:1 (v/v) hydrogel (OSA-CMCS). To prepare the OSA-CMCS-GNP hydrogel, 1% GNP (w/v) was incorporated into the OSA and CMCS mixture. The surface morphology of the freeze-dried hydrogels was examined by scanning electron microscopy (Regulus8100, Hitachi, Japan). The chemical structure was characterized by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Tensor27, Bruker Optics).

Biocompatibility evaluation

Freeze-dried OSA-CMCS and OSA-CMCS-GNP hydrogels were immersed in high-glucose DMEM at a concentration of 1 mg/mL for 24 h. The extracts were filtered through 0.2 μm filters and diluted with DMEM to concentrations of 0%, 20%, 60%, 80%, and 100%. HUVECs and HKF cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with hydrogel extracts for 24 h. Afterward, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Animal experiments sample collection

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Ocean University (Approval No. 2022-SCUEC-021). SPF-grade male ICR mice (8 weeks old) were obtained from Guangzhou Charles River Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Mice were maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle. Three days before experimentation, dorsal hair was removed. Mice were divided into four groups (n = 5): Saline-treated group, GNP (100 μg/mL), OSA-CMCS, and OSA-CMCS-GNP. A circular wound (~5 mm in diameter) was created on the dorsal skin, and treatments were applied daily. The saline-treated group received saline; the GNP group received GNP diluted in saline; and the hydrogel groups received OSA-CMCS or OSA-CMCS-GNP dressings for 2 h. Wound healing was monitored daily and photographed. After 20 days, mice were euthanized, and healed scar tissue was collected and stored at −20 °C.

Tissue sectioning and staining

Collected tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Sections were dried at 60 °C. Histological evaluation was conducted by H&E staining, and collagen distribution was analyzed using Masson’s trichrome staining.

Western blot analysis

HKF and HUVEC cells were lysed with RIPA buffer containing 1% PMSF on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to collect supernatants. Mouse scar tissue samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer (1% PMSF) using a tissue grinder (60 Hz, 5 min) and centrifuged. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system and analyzed using ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence staining

HKF and HUVEC cells were seeded on 35 mm glass-bottom dishes and cultured for 24 h, followed by drug treatment. Cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h. Primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA were applied overnight at 4 °C. After washing, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h in the dark. Nuclei and cytoskeleton were stained with DAPI and actin red, respectively, and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded mouse scar sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. After cooling, endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 25 min. Sections were blocked with 5% goat serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against TGF-β1 (1:200, Abcam, ab92486), α-SMA (1:500, CST, #19245), and CD31 (1:300, Servicebio, GB113151). After washing, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Servicebio, GB23303) for 50 min at 37 °C, followed by DAB color development. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted for imaging under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Data analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D., n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using ImageJ and GraphPad Prism 8.0. One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test was used to determine statistical significance. The statistical significance of the test was set at p < 0.05.
