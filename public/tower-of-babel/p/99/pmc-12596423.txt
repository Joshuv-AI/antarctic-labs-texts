Prognostic factors for survival and recurrence in papillary thyroid carcinoma: a retrospective study
Authors: Ebtesam Almajed, Alya AlZabin, Norah Alqntash, Rawan Bin Salamah, Abdullah Alotibi, Lama Alzelfawi, Shahad Aldhafyan, Hadeel Bin shuiel, Abdullah Alshamrani, Adel Alqahtani, Abdulrahman Alzahrani, Saud Alrasheedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPapillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) accounts for 80–85% of cases and generally has an excellent prognosis. However, outcomes vary with clinicopathological factors, and identifying prognostic indicators is clinically important for tailoring treatment strategies and long-term follow-up. Regional data on prognostic factors in PTC remain limited. This study investigates the impact of patient and tumor characteristics on survival and recurrence outcomes at a single institution.MethodsA retrospective cohort study included patients aged 18 years or older diagnosed with PTC between January 2015 and September 2023 at a secondary hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Eligible patients had confirmed PTC with sufficient clinical records and no prior thyroid cancer treatment. The data collected included demographics, comorbidities, risk factors, baseline clinical and laboratory factors, and tumor pathology [tumor, node, metastasis (TNM) stage and variants]. Treatment details and follow-up outcomes were reviewed through electronic medical records. Patients were followed for a median of 5.45 years to assess survival, recurrence, and postoperative complications. Associations between clinical characteristics and prognostic outcomes were analyzed using Chi-squared and Fisher tests.ResultsA total of 293 patients were included (mean age at diagnosis 41.7±12.2 years); 82.2% were female. Most patients (79.1%) presented with early-stage disease, and 62.3% had the classic variant of the disease. The overall 10-year survival rate was 95.8%, and recurrence occurred in 2.1%. Patients over 50 years had more significant comorbidities than those aged 18 to 50 years, such as diabetes mellitus (P=0.001, 37.8% vs. 8.8%), hypertension (P=0.001, 31.5% vs. 6.6%), dyslipidemia (P=0.001, 32.4% vs. 3.3%), asthma (P=0.002, 20.7% vs. 8.3%), and cardiovascular disease (P=0.046, 6.3% vs. 1.7%). Additionally, PTC patients aged above 50 years had significantly more lymph node involvement (53.2%, P=0.041). Male patients had greater nodal involvement (61.5% vs. 42.1%, P=0.041), while females more frequently developed hypothyroidism (18.3% vs. 5.8%, P=0.02). Despite these adverse prognostic factors, no significant differences in recurrence or mortality were observed. Temporary postoperative hypoparathyroidism occurred in 11.6% and permanent hypoparathyroidism in 4.1%, while temporary and permanent recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis occurred in 2.3% and 0.7%, respectively.ConclusionsPatients with PTC in this Saudi cohort demonstrated excellent long-term outcomes, characterized by high survival rates and low recurrence rates. Older age and male sex were associated with more aggressive disease features, yet with appropriate management, outcomes remained favorable. These findings emphasize the importance of comprehensive risk assessment and reinforce the effectiveness of current treatment strategies, providing region-specific evidence to inform clinical guidelines and optimize long-term patient care.Keywords: Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), prognostic factors, retrospective, Saudi Arabia

Highlight box

Key findings

• In a cohort of 293 patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) at a secondary center in Saudi Arabia, the 10-year survival rate was 95.8%, and the recurrence rate was 2.1%. Most patients were female (82.2%) and presented with early-stage disease. Older patients (>50 years) had significantly more comorbidities and lymph node involvement, but did not experience higher recurrence or mortality. Total thyroidectomy was the most common surgical approach (71.2%), and complication rates were low, with permanent hypoparathyroidism in 4.1% and recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis in 0.7%.

What is known and what is new?

• PTC typically has excellent long-term outcomes. Factors such as older age, male gender, and nodal metastasis are traditionally associated with poorer prognosis.

• This study adds region-specific evidence from Saudi Arabia, demonstrating favorable outcomes even in higher-risk subgroups when standard treatments are applied. It also reports notably low recurrence and mortality rates in a secondary care setting, reinforcing the safety and efficacy of current management strategies.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings support the continued use of current treatment protocols for PTC, including total thyroidectomy and selective radioactive iodine therapy. Even patients with adverse features can achieve excellent outcomes with comprehensive care. Long-term follow-up remains essential. Future efforts should focus on multicenter studies and molecular profiling to further personalize treatment and refine risk stratification models.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, thyroid cancer incidence has increased sharply and continuously worldwide (1). According to the Saudi National Cancer Registry, the incidence of thyroid cancer in Saudi Arabia has increased dramatically, with an estimated incidence of 10.1%. Between 1990 and 2016, thyroid cancer incidence in Saudi Arabia rose dramatically, approximately 26-fold (2,3). Furthermore, it ranked as the third most prevalent cancer among Saudis, after colorectal and breast cancer, with women being affected more frequently than men (3).

Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common thyroid malignancy, comprising 80–85% of cases (3,4). PTC is a well-differentiated carcinoma that typically carries an excellent prognosis. On a molecular level, aberrant activation of the MAP kinase pathway, resulting from genetic events such as RET/PTC rearrangements, which are more common in younger patients, or BRAF point mutations in adults, plays a key role in PTC pathogenesis (5). There are several known risk factors for PTC, including genetic factors, age, radiation exposure, smoking, alcohol intake, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, and high levels of thyrotropin (2). Additionally, PTC can present with various clinical presentations. It often presents as a painless thyroid mass, with or without regional lymph node enlargement, and is most commonly discovered incidentally or during routine examinations (6). Rarely, patients may experience a painful thyroid mass, hoarseness, stridor, or dysphagia (6). In PTC, age is a significant prognostic factor, and it is incorporated into various prominent staging systems (7,8).

Recent large-scale studies confirm that advanced age is an independent predictor of poor prognosis in PTC. Analysis of Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (SEER) data showed that patients ≥65 years had significantly reduced cancer-specific survival. At the same time, a registry study found that those aged 60 years or older had worse disease-specific and disease-free survival, regardless of tumor stage or treatment (7). However, an analysis of 4,870 low- to intermediate-risk PTC patients demonstrated that those younger than 55 years had excellent cancer-specific outcomes, although very low-risk patients in this age group experienced a higher incidence of local recurrence (8). Several controversies exist regarding the treatment of each age group, and before selecting a therapeutic strategy, it is crucial to assess the prognostic indicators of age that are biologically different (8).

Moreover, large-scale registry studies have confirmed the adverse prognostic impact of patient age in PTC. A California registry study of 15,675 patients found that individuals aged 60 years and older exhibited significantly reduced disease-specific and disease-free survival, independent of tumor characteristics and treatment modalities (9). Similarly, an analysis of 31,802 SEER patients showed a linear, age-related decline in 10-year cancer-specific survival, decreasing from nearly 100% in younger individuals to below 80% in nonagenarians, with no clear age threshold separating different risk levels (10).

Multiple international studies have identified key prognostic factors for PTC, including age, gender, tumor size, lymph node involvement, and extrathyroidal extension. However, these findings may not apply equally across different regions and healthcare systems. Most data come from large tertiary referral centers in Western countries, where patient demographics, environmental exposures, and healthcare access differ from those in Middle Eastern populations. Research on the Saudi population is limited, with few studies examining long-term outcomes or the prognostic significance of clinicopathological variables in patients treated locally. The impact of regional factors, including high obesity rates, consanguinity, and variations in iodine intake, on disease behavior and prognosis remains poorly studied. This study aimed to explore the prognostic factors related to survival and recurrence among PTC patients at a secondary care center in Saudi Arabia, to provide region-specific data to improve local clinical guidelines and fill gaps in the international literature. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2025-216/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

A single-center, retrospective cohort study focused on patients diagnosed with PTC who received treatment at a secondary institution in Saudi Arabia from January 2015 until September 2023. The author’s hospital is a healthcare institution that provides medical services to military personnel and their families across the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The hospital and its affiliated primary healthcare centers offer comprehensive medical care, ensuring continuity of treatment and facilitating access to detailed patient data through electronic medical records. Most of our patients were born and followed up within our hospital system and its Primary Healthcare Centers, allowing for a reliable collection of clinical, demographic, and treatment-related factors. The inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with PTC aged 18 years or older. To ensure the integrity of the study, patients with incomplete medical records, prior diagnoses of thyroid cancers other than PTC, or a history of cancer treatment prior to PTC diagnosis were excluded. This study included all eligible patients treated at our institution during the study period; therefore, a formal sample size calculation was not required. Initially, 951 thyroid cancer cases were screened; of these, 293 cases met the inclusion criteria as confirmed PTC without prior treatment and had sufficient records for review.

Ethics statement & informed consent

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the institutional review board at Security Forces Hospital (IRB approval No. H-01-R-069). This approval was used to gain permission from the endocrine surgery unit to access the medical records. Given the study’s retrospective nature, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All patient data were handled with strict confidentiality in accordance with institutional policies.

Data extraction

Data were collected by revising patients’ electronic medical records and archiving paper charts. The following data were extracted: (I) sociodemographic and risk factors data, including age at diagnosis, current age, gender, weight and height at presentation, occupation, comorbidities, smoking, previous radiation exposure, family history of cancer, hepatitis C, increased parity, late age at first pregnancy, obesity or overweight. (II) Laboratory parameters include thyroglobulin (IU/mL), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) (in µIU/mL), free triiodothyronine (T3), and thyroxine (T4) (in pmol/L). (III) The management plan includes the extent of thyroid surgery (total or subtotal thyroidectomy vs. lobectomy), lymph node dissection (central compartment and/or lateral neck dissection, if any), use of radioactive iodine (RAI) ablation, and any other therapies (e.g., radiofrequency ablation or embolization for distant metastases, external beam radiotherapy, systemic treatment if applied). (IV) Thyroid cancer histology and tumor, node, metastasis staging system (TNM staging), which a consultant pathologist concluded. Histopathological reports include tumor size, multifocality, extrathyroidal extension, histological variant of PTC, and TNM staging according to American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition criteria. (V) Outcomes and follow-up: compromised length of follow-up (from surgery to last visit), adverse events, whole body scan 123I, response to therapy at previous follow-up (disease-free vs. persistent disease vs. recurrent disease), any evidence of locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis during follow-up, and survival status. Regarding RAI complications, we were unable to include them as we referred eligible patients to a tertiary care center. Therefore, the data were not accessible.

Definition

Tumor size was defined by the maximum diameter of the tumor on pathological examination of the thyroidectomy specimen. Multifocal disease is defined as the presence of two or more distinct tumor foci in the thyroid gland. Lymph node metastasis was defined by histopathological confirmation of tumor in dissected lymph nodes. Distant metastasis was defined by either pathological confirmation or imaging evidence of metastatic disease, such as uptake on post-RAI whole-body scan (WBS) or computed tomography (CT) imaging. Recurrence was defined as new evidence of PTC, either locoregional or distant, that presented after an initial treatment and a period of disease-free status, confirmed by imaging and/or biopsy. Disease-free status at the last follow-up was defined by the absence of any clinical, biochemical, or radiologic evidence of remaining or recurrent disease, including non-elevated thyroglobulin levels and negative imaging where applicable.

Statistical analysis

The data were extracted and revised in an Excel sheet. Statistical analysis was conducted using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences computer program (SPSS) version 26.0 (Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were described in numbers and percentages. Continuous, non-normally distributed variables were reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), median [interquartile range (IQR)], minimum, and maximum. The Chi-squared and Fisher’s Exact tests were used to compare different categorical variables. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 293 patients with PTC were included in the analysis. The mean age at diagnosis was 41.7±12.2 years (range, 14–92 years), and the mean age at the last follow-up was 47.1±12.3 years. Most patients were female (82.2%), and nearly all were never smokers. The average body mass index (BMI) was 31.1±6.3 kg/m2, with 81.4% of patients classified as overweight or obese (BMI ≥25 kg/m2). Table 4 summarizes patient management and follow-up. Most patients (71.2%) had total thyroidectomy, and 36% underwent central node dissection. RAI treatment was given to 47.2% of patients. Other therapies, including radiofrequency ablation of osseous metastases and management of pulmonary metastases, were performed in 0.3% of patients each. All patients received thyroid hormone suppression therapy, and none underwent adjuvant external beam radiation therapy, systemic chemotherapy, or radiofrequency ablation of cervical metastases. The follow-up was 5.45±3.336 years. Most patients (96.9%) were cured, with a metastasis rate of 2.1%. Early surgery-related adverse events included infection (0.7%), pain (1.7%), scarring (1.7%), temporary hypoparathyroidism (11.6%), and temporary recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis (2.3%). Late adverse events were hypoparathyroidism (4.1%) and recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis (0.7%). For RAI therapy, 0.9% reported adverse events. Only one patient died at the age of 79.

Discussion

The cohort of 293 PTC patients was predominantly middle-aged adults with a mean age of their mid-40s. This is consistent with the typical age distribution of PTC globally, which ranges from the 30s to the 50s (11). Notably, females comprised the vast majority (82%) of our patients, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 4.6:1. This female predominance is well-documented in thyroid cancer epidemiology, with global ratios on the order of 3:1 in favor of females (11). Our gender distribution aligns with both international and regional data; for example, a recent Saudi Arabian series reported 78.5% of thyroid cancer cases in women (12). The reasons for this gender disparity remain incompletely understood; hormonal and reproductive factors have been hypothesized but not definitively proven to explain women’s higher incidence (9).

It has been reported that thyroid cancer incidence has increased over the past few years, with PTC accounting for 80% of diagnoses (10). In general, patients with PTC have a 5-year survival rate greater than 95%, indicating a better prognosis than those with other malignant tumors; however, 30–80% of patients with PTC have lymph node metastasis, which has an adverse effect on long-term survival (13,14). Some clinicopathological features, such as gender, autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD), histological variants, multifocality, and vascular invasion, are prognostic for PTC recurrence. A higher percentage of thyroid cancers are diagnosed in women than in men. Despite this, males typically develop more aggressive tumors and survive less than females. Researchers found that multifocality was not a reliable marker for PTC recurrence or death in a multicenter study involving 2,638 patients. A retrospective study has shown that more than four lymph node metastases were considered a poor prognosis. Male sex, younger age, high stimulated thyroglobulin (sTg) levels, and extrathyroidal extension were all associated with poor prognosis (15). Furthermore, it was reported that the cutoffs for a poor prognosis of central lymph node metastasis (CLNM), lateral lymph node metastasis (LLNM), and CLNM with LLNM were greater than 6, greater than 1, and greater than 5, respectively (16).

Research on PTC has reported recurrence rates ranging from 7% to 28% (13). In contrast, our study showed a significantly lower recurrence rate of 2.1%. This finding may be attributed to the high proportion (35.6%) of papillary thyroid micro-carcinomas (PTMC), which are defined as carcinomas ≤1 cm (17). Our results align with a meta-analysis of 6,839 PTMC cases, which reported a recurrence rate of 2.8% (18). PTMCs are recognized as less aggressive tumors (18). Another possible explanation for the lower recurrence rate in our study is the high percentage (71.2%) of patients who underwent total thyroidectomy as their initial treatment. Evidence suggests that more extensive primary surgical resections can reduce locoregional recurrence by eliminating residual microscopic disease (19-21).

Our study demonstrated a 10-year survival rate of 95.8% in patients with PTC, consistent with previous research indicating long-term survival rates exceeding 90% (22). Prior studies have established total or near-total thyroidectomy as the preferred surgical approach, as it has been shown to reduce recurrence risk and improve overall survival (19,23). An extensive single-institution follow-up study involving 5,897 patients identified several key prognostic factors influencing overall survival. These include distant metastases (M1), advanced age, male sex, extrathyroidal extension, tumor size greater than 4 cm, nodal involvement (N1), and extranodal tumor extension (24).

In our paper, we highlight the balance between the outcomes of PTC management and minimizing long-term morbidity. Thyroidectomy, with or without lymph node resection, is the cornerstone of PTC management in most cases (25). The technique varies from total to partial thyroidectomy based on the tumor’s histological features and the disease’s spread (25). For instance, if the tumor is unifocal, smaller than 4 cm, and shows no evidence of spreading into extra-thyroidal structures or lymph nodes, lobectomy is the recommended option (25). For patients with clear extrathyroidal extension and PTC more than 4 cm, the surgical procedure will include near-total or total thyroidectomy unless there are contraindications to this approach.

This study highlights the various complications, both temporary and permanent, associated with this surgical approach. Regarding our study, temporary hypoparathyroidism was reported in 11.6% and 2.3% respectively. Transient laryngeal nerve paralysis was reported as an early postoperative adverse event. These complications, even though temporary in most cases, might significantly impact the patient’s quality of life as a result of hypocalcemia, muscle cramps, and voice-deepening changes, necessitating postoperative management and follow-up (26,27). Although permanent complications rarely occur, they remain concerning; 4.1% of the cases developed persistent hypoparathyroidism, and 0.7% reported lasting recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis. These complications require continuous and rapid management, including calcium and vitamin D supplementation, as well as voice rehabilitation therapy (28).

RAI therapy is well tolerated in nearly half of the patients (47.2%), but it has its complications, with only 0.9% indicating the protocol’s safety. However, RAI use has been associated with salivary gland dysfunction, dry mouth, and secondary malignancy in other studies (29,30). A review of the current literature supports these findings since RAI has been shown to produce transitory or persistent sialadenitis and xerostomia because of salivary gland uptake. In rare cases, it may raise the risk of secondary malignancies such as leukemia and other solid tumors, particularly at large cumulative dosages (29,30). These data indicate that while RAI is generally safe for most patients, its long-term consequences necessitate cautious patient selection and adherence to optimal dose measurement (29,30). Moreover, the current study’s lack of systemic chemotherapy and external beam radiation therapy restricts the evaluation of adverse effects associated with these treatments.

The research results also highlight serious questions concerning the impact of patient-specific variables on complication rates. Age and gender appear to be relevant; older patients had a higher incidence of comorbidities, such as diabetes and hypertension, which may boost their vulnerability to adverse effects. Particularly, individuals above the age of 50 years had greater lymph node involvement, necessitating more extensive procedures with increased complication risk. These findings are consistent with previous research, which has shown that advanced age and male gender are linked with more aggressive illness and a higher chance of lymph node involvement (31). Gender differences, although less prominent, deserve investigation. Female patients had greater rates of hypothyroidism after the course of treatment, possibly due to pre-existing thyroid malfunction or hormonal factors. Conversely, male patients showed a greater proportion of lymph node involvement (61.5% vs. 42.1%), which aligns with research anticipating a poorer prognosis in males due to more severe patterns of disease (32).

This study’s results align with the broader literature, which emphasizes the importance of individualized treatment to prevent adverse effects while ensuring optimal oncological outcomes. The low mortality (0.3%) and recurrence rates (2.1%) observed highlight the effectiveness of treatment. However, to properly address late-onset problems, continual monitoring is required. Future research directions include longitudinal studies that assess the long-term quality of life and psychological effects on survivors, which may provide a more comprehensive overview of PTC care with a larger sample size.

Recent literature has introduced several promising tools to enhance risk classification in PTC. Adding further complexity, Shi et al. (33) reported a rare case involving the simultaneous occurrence of papillary and medullary thyroid carcinoma in a patient with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. This unusual presentation underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by multifocal thyroid disease. For instance, Ge et al. (34) developed a predictive model based on platelet count and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio. This model showed strong diagnostic accuracy in identifying patients at higher risk for extensive lymph node metastases. At the molecular level, Xin et al. (35) found that secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) was significantly overexpressed in aggressive tumors and linked to poorer outcomes, suggesting it could serve as a biomarker for individualized risk assessment. Similarly, Guan et al. (36) proposed a nomogram that integrates clinicopathological features with multimodal ultrasonography results, offering valuable preoperative insight into the likelihood of lateral lymph node involvement. These findings suggest that integrating clinical, laboratory, imaging, and molecular data significantly improves risk prediction and informs more tailored treatment strategies in PTC. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts will be essential to validate and refine these emerging approaches. A thorough interpretation of our study necessitates an explicit acknowledgment of its inherent limitations. As a retrospective study conducted at a single institution, the generalizability of our findings is constrained, particularly when considering the diverse patient populations and treatment protocols prevalent in tertiary referral centers. While informative, the median follow-up of 5.45 years may be insufficient to capture late recurrences or mortality trends, particularly in high-risk subgroups. Furthermore, this study exclusively focused on PTC without direct comparisons to other thyroid malignancies, such as follicular, medullary, or anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, limiting broader insights into thyroid cancer prognostication. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insights into PTC outcomes and underscore the need for future multicenter studies that incorporate molecular profiling and extended follow-up to refine risk stratification and optimize patient care.

Conclusions

PTC in our single-center Saudi Arabian cohort demonstrated an excellent prognosis, with high cure rates and minimal disease-specific mortality. Patient age and specific clinical factors, such as comorbidities and nodal metastasis, were associated with a more advanced presentation; however, with standardized, comprehensive treatment, even these patients attained favorable outcomes. These results underscore that adherence to established management guidelines yields outstanding long-term survival in PTC. Our study contributes region-specific data that reinforce global knowledge and can inform local clinical practice and health policy.
