Disrupting intestinal cell pyroptosis-NETs crosstalk reverse mucosal damage and restore Treg/Th17 equilibrium in ulcerative colitis via a calcium pectate microsphere composite hydrogel
Authors: Yun Huang, Shuangyan He, Jia Liu, Yalan Chen, Wen Chen, Mohan Ding, Zhi Zheng, Hua Wei, Cui-Yun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The crosstalk between dysregulated intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) death and hyperactivated immune cells is crucial to maintain the intestinal barrier integrity in ulcerative colitis (UC), but remains seldom explored for the anti-inflammatory therapies reported so far. For this purpose, this study first identifies the crucial role of the crosstalk between Gasdermin D (GSDMD)-dependent intestinal cell pyroptosis and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in exacerbating intestinal barrier damage. An orally administrable inulin gel composite (BCPD@gel) was further developed, encapsulating calcium pectate-based microspheres that are loaded simultaneously with berberine (BBR) and Deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) via hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions, respectively. BCPD@gel breaks the crosstalk between GSDMD-dependent intestinal cell pyroptosis and NETs to modulate gut microbiota composition and restore Th17/Treg equilibrium, collectively leading to compromised inflammation cascade for dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis treatment. BCPD@gel administration greatly restored epithelium barriers by alleviating the vicious cycle between GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis and NETs in a DSS-induced colitis model, which was evidenced by a mouse colon length of 6.76 ± 0.4 cm. This improved value is close to that of the normal group and 1.4-fold longer than that of the DSS group. Overall, this study highlights the leverage of IEC-neutrophil interactions as a potent strategy to circumvent ongoing inflammation and maintain the intestinal barrier integrity for effective DSS-induced colitis treatment.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04743-1.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a relentless inflammatory disease that affects the rectum and colon to varying degrees, which has developed into a significant and continuously growing medical challenge [1]. The growing UC incidents have severely affected the quality of life in patients and even posed a risk of progression to colon cancer [2, 3]. Despite the leverage of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) antibody infliximab and subsequent immunomodulatory therapies, such as TNF antibodies, interleukin-12/interleukin-23p40 subunit (IL-12/IL-23p40) antibody, janus kinase‌ (JAK) inhibitors, and sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor modulators, which can induce and maintain remission to a much greater degree than those of conventional drugs like 5-aminosalicylic acid, thiopurines, and corticosteroids, the remission rates remain as low as 20–30% [4, 5]. Besides, a compromised intestinal barrier resulting from heightened permeability has long been considered a potential risk factor for UC, as evidenced by a nearly 50% increase in intestinal permeability among healthy first-degree relatives of patients with Crohn’s disease or UC [6, 7]. The onset of UC is closely related to the loss of intestinal barrier integrity. Such a barrier defect permits noxious substances to provoke a sequence of physiological responses, including subsequent inflammation, immune activation, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, and disturbances in mucus secretion [8]. Maintaining intestinal mucosal integrity is thus pivotal for targeting these interconnected pathways in UC treatment.

Pyroptosis, a newly recognized form of programmed cell death, has attracted considerable research focus for not only cancer immunotherapy but also dysregulated inflammatory response in the colon [9]. Predominantly mediated by gasdermin (GSDM) family proteins, pyroptosis is characterized by cell swelling, membrane rupture, and release of cellular contents, which ultimately initiate an inflammatory response [10]. Pyroptosis mediated by Gasdermin E (GSDME) from the GSDM family plays a key role in promoting colitis development in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines to disrupt the intestinal barrier [9, 11]. Notably, Xu’s Butyrate-modified liposome (4-OI/BLipo) enabled targeted delivery to IECs and significantly restored the epithelial barrier by alleviating GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in colitis [7]. Yang et al. revealed that Gasdermin D (GSDMD), another member of the GSDM family, acted as a negative regulator in macrophages to regulate cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-dependent inflammation, whereas the highly activated GSDMD in IECs failed to show a comparable negative regulatory effect on inflammation as seen in macrophages [12]. However, the role of the interaction between GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis in IECs and immune cells in the context of UC-induced intestinal barrier damage remains largely unexplored. Neutrophils, the most abundant members of the immune cell family, act as the initial responders at injury sites, playing an indispensable role in eliminating pathogens and orchestrating immune responses [13, 14]. NETosis is an activated-neutrophil-driven process wherein they release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), mainly composed of double-stranded DNA, citrullinated histones, and myeloperoxidase (MPO), to capture pathogens and eliminate cellular debris [15, 16]. Although NETs are crucial in effectively confining, capturing, and neutralizing pathogens, which helps thwart infection, an overabundance of NETs can inevitably result in unintended tissue damage [17]. The integral role of excessive NETs has been highlighted recently in disrupting the intestinal barrier integrity [18], which aggravates intestinal inflammation and promotes the UC progression [19]. Wang et al. reported that reducing neutrophil recruitment and inhibiting the formation of NETs yielded favorable therapeutic effects in mice with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis [20]. Targeted elimination of NETs may represent a promising therapeutic direction for UC. Taken together, there is considerable scope to develop a novel therapeutic strategy based on the intricate involvement of IEC pyroptosis and NETs in the pathogenesis of UC.

Intestinal epithelial integrity as the foundation of the mucosal barrier mediates microbiota-immune dialogue via pattern recognition receptors and mucus layer interactions, which is critical for preventing dysbiosis-driven intestinal inflammation [21]. UC patients generally present with intestinal dysbiosis characterized by disrupted microbial community structure and diminished ecological diversity [22]. In addition, dysregulation of intestinal mucosal immunity, characterized by a pathological shift in the Th17/Treg cell axis, represents a key etiological factor in UC pathogenesis. Th17 cells are essential drivers of autoimmunity and inflammation, but hyperactivated Th17 cells disrupt intestinal barrier function and fuel inflammatory pathologies. On the other hand, Treg cells maintain homeostasis by suppressing autoimmunity and modulating tissue inflammation via immune tolerance. Impaired Tregs lose this restraint and amplify inflammation [23]. Therefore, a multidimensional “all-in-one” strategy capable of targeting cell pyroptosis, intervening in NETs formation, modulating gut microbiota composition, and restoring Th17/Treg equilibrium is highly desirable to suppress intestinal inflammation and maintain intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, which remains rarely reported to our knowledge.

For this purpose, the role of crosstalk between GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis and NETs in exacerbating intestinal barrier damage in UC was first disclosed by a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced experimental colitis mouse model and bioinformatic analysis approaches (Scheme 1). Next, an orally administrable inulin gel composite, BCPD@gel, was developed for encapsulating calcium pectate-based microspheres that are loaded simultaneously with berberine (BBR) and Deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) via hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions, respectively (Scheme 1a). BCPD@gel can disrupt the crosstalk between GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis and NETs to block the inflammation cascade for UC treatment (Scheme 1c). Specifically, BCPD@gel, integrating BBR to inhibit GSDMD-mediated IEC pyroptosis and incorporating DNase I to eliminate NETs by degrading their nucleic acid backbones, collectively breaks the vicious cycle of the crosstalk between GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis and NETs for restoring and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity. Another notable merit of this design is the inulin matrix that has been generally recognized as a safe (GRAS) substance with the unique characteristic of being degradable solely by colon-specific inulinase [24–26]. Additionally, the process of inulin fermentation by gut microbiota contributes to the enhanced diversity and richness of the intestinal microbial community. Therefore, the leverage of inulin gel effectively enhances the bioadhesion of the BCPD microspheres and prolongs the retention time of BCPD@gel within the colon.

Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies were performed in this study, which highlights the leverage of IEC-neutrophil interactions as a potent strategy to circumvent ongoing inflammation and maintain the intestinal barrier integrity for effective DSS-induced colitis treatment.

Results and discussion

The crosstalk of GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis and NETs exacerbates intestinal barrier damage in UC

To investigate the role of the crosstalk between GSDMD-dependent enterocyte pyroptosis and NETs in UC, the expression of GSDMD and neutrophil infiltration status in UC patients was assessed by the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE series). GSDMD was identified to show an overall escalation in expression in mucosal tissues derived from UC patients compared to that of non-inflammatory controls (Con) (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, the correlation between GSDMD and MPO (a marker of activated neutrophils) was further validated via the GSE database to delve into the relationship between pyroptosis and neutrophil activation. The positive correlation of pyroptosis with MPO (Fig. 1b) suggested a potential functional link between GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis and neutrophil-driven inflammation in UC pathogenesis. Notably, neutrophil activation is essential for the body’s inflammatory process, and NET formation serves as a major pathway enabling neutrophils to eliminate pathogens and regulate inflammatory reactions [16]. NETs not only serve as a critical defense mechanism through pathogen capture and participation in immune responses [17], but also further exacerbate inflammatory responses and disrupt the intestinal mucosal barrier in UC [18]. The DSS-induced colitis model was established to further explore NETs’ role in UC, and the expression levels of NETs-related biomarkers (CitH3 and MPO) were detected in intestinal tissues. The MPO (green) and CitH3 (red) infiltration in the intestinal tissue of colitic mice were markedly elevated compared to those of normal mice (Fig. 1c), indicating that NETs play a crucial role in the progression of DSS-induced colitis.

Firstly, peripheral blood neutrophils were harvested from healthy mice via a neutrophil isolation kit and treated with 100 nM PMA for 4 h to trigger NET formation (Fig. 1d), which was further confirmed via immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 1e). Next, the membrane blebbing and the N-terminal pore-forming GSDMD fragment (GSDMD-NT) level as major characteristics of pyroptosis were evaluated in NCM 460 cells. These cells were pre-treated with LPS and then exposed to NETs with an increasing concentration gradient of 0, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 µg DNA/mL for 24 h. The ratio of LPS-induced membrane blebbing was increased in a NET dose-dependent manner in NCM 460 cells (Fig. 1f and g). Pyroptosis was significantly compromised following DNase I‑mediated NETs degradation. The pyroptosis positive rate in the DNase I-treated group decreased by 64% compared to that in the group without DNase I treatment, but showed insignificant differences relative to that of the LPS group (Figure S1). Therefore, we conclude that the promotion of epithelial pyroptosis depends substantially on NET rather than other soluble factors released by activated neutrophils. The levels of GSDMD-NT also increased in a dose-dependent manner by Western blot (WB) assay (Fig. 1h and i), suggesting NETs treatment could significantly exacerbate NCM 460 cells’ pyroptosis in a dose-dependent manner. Meanwhile, co-incubation of neutrophils with supernatant from LPS-pretreated IECs was performed to assess the potential crosstalk between NET formation and IEC pyroptosis. Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 1j) showed that control neutrophils maintained intact nuclear morphology with punctate MPO staining, while PMA-positive control and CCS groups both displayed a characteristic NET morphology with diffuse nuclear decondensation and web-like MPO-positive extracellular filaments colocalizing with decondensed DNA. Quantitative analysis of extracellular dsDNA levels was consistent with the immunofluorescence observations. The dsDNA levels in PMA and CCS treatment groups were increased by 3.5‑fold and 3.4‑fold relative to those of the control group, respectively, with insignificant differences between the two groups. (Figure S2). These findings demonstrate that the factors released from pyroptotic IECs can effectively induce NET formation in neutrophils, supporting a potential crosstalk between NET formation and GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis in intestinal inflammation.

Finally, the expression levels of GSDMD-NT, MPO, and intestinal barrier-related proteins in the mucosal tissue from UC mice were assessed via WB to further explore the associations between the crosstalk of NETs and GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis and intestinal barrier integrity. The intestinal barrier-related proteins, encompassing ZO-1, E-cadherin, and Occludin, are involved in the events of tight junction maintenance within the intestinal epithelial barrier [27, 28]. The prominent pyroptosis-inducing fragment GSDMD-NT and MPO were detected in the inflamed colonic mucosa of mice, while these proteins were absent in noninflamed mucosal tissue. Notably, the expression levels of GSDMD-NT and MPO were inversely correlated with the levels of ZO-1, E-cadherin, and Occludin (Fig. 1k). Specifically, the expression levels of ZO-1, E-cadherin, and Occludin in the inflamed colonic mucosa were 29.2%, 45.8%, and 38.1% lower than those in noninflamed mucosal tissue (Fig. 1l), suggesting a potential association between the crosstalk of NETs and GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis and intestinal barrier damage in UC. Collectively, targeting the crosstalk of NETs and GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis to curb inflammation and preserve intestinal mucosal integrity may serve as a potential effective therapeutic strategy for UC.

NETs represent extracellular web-like structures predominantly released by activated neutrophils. To investigate whether NET inhibition alters the epithelial pyroptosis in vivo, an acute neutrophil depletion mouse model was constructed via intraperitoneal injection of anti-Ly6G antibody to ablate neutrophil-derived NETs according to the previously reported methods (Fig. 2a). The FCM-quantified neutrophil ratio in the colonic tissue of the DSS + anti-Ly6G group was 87.3% lower than that in the DSS group (Figure S3), indicating the successful neutrophil depletion. Neutrophil-depleted mice in the DSS + anti-Ly6G group exhibited significantly attenuated DSS-induced colitis, as evidenced by a reduced rate of body weight loss and decreased DAI scores compared to those of the DSS group (Fig. 2b-c). The colon length in the DSS + anti-Ly6G group was 1.2-fold longer than that in the DSS group (Fig. 2d-e). The neutrophil elastase (NE) level of mice serum in the DSS + anti-Ly6G group was significantly decreased by 15.3% relative to that in the DSS group, but similar to that in the normal group, which illustrates that neutrophil depletion effectively suppresses NET formation (Fig. 2f). The pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-17 A, IL-6, and IL-1β levels in the DSS + anti-Ly6G group, decreased to 46.1%, 77.2%, and 35.4% levels of those in the DSS group, respectively (Fig. 2g-i). Importantly, neutrophil depletion markedly downregulated the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins, including Cl-caspase1 (Cl-casp1), GSDMD-NT, and NLRP3. These protein levels in the DSS + anti-Ly6G group were decreased by 58.9%, 78.9%, and 59.1%, respectively, compared to those in the DSS group (Fig. 2j-m), indicating that NETs are essential for driving intestinal epithelial pyroptosis in DSS-induced colitis.

Therefore, disrupting the crosstalk between NETs and GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis may serve as an effective therapeutic strategy to curb inflammation and preserve intestinal mucosal integrity for UC.

Synthesis and characterization of BBR@CP/DNase I (BCPD) microspheres

BBR, an extract derived from traditional Chinese medicine, exhibits a broad spectrum of biological activities, with particularly notable efficacy in anti-inflammatory processes [29]. Notably, blocking the activation of upstream pathways involved in pyroptosis and inhibiting GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis are important ways that BBR fights inflammation [30, 31]. Neutrophils exert pivotal defensive functions in the early phase of UC pathogenesis, including rapid recruitment to the intestinal mucosa, pathogen phagocytosis, and antimicrobial substance release. In the late stage, however, neutrophils become a central pathogenic mediator via excessive activation, dysregulated apoptosis, and robust release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and NETs [16, 32]. This dual role shift underscores neutrophils’ irreplaceable significance in investigating UC pathogenesis mechanisms. DNase I, which can degrade the NET nucleic acid framework, emerges as a highly promising drug candidate to facilitate NET deletion [33, 34]. An oral composite gel drug delivery system based on calcium pectinate microspheres was constructed to elevate BBR and DNase I local concentrations, prolong intestinal retention time, and eventually achieve the synergistic effect of BBR and DNase I. Specifically, he crosslinking agent Ca²⁺ binds to the electronegative cavities within the dual-clasp structure of pectin’s demethylated residues, forming an “egg-box” structure [35]. Firstly, pre-mixing BBR with pectin solution enabled the formation of microspheres, in which BBR was encapsulated by calcium pectinate (BBR@CP) during the assembly of the “egg-box” structure. Subsequently, the BBR@CP microspheres were coated with a positively charged polyelectrolyte layer (PAH) to facilitate the adsorption of negatively charged DNase I, yeilding BBR@CP/DNase I (BCPD) microspheres (Fig. 3a). The formation mechanism of BBR@CP microspheres was studied by FTIR analysis. Peaks at 1718 and 1640 cm⁻¹ correspond to esterified and free carboxyl groups in pectin, respectively. The peak area at 1640 cm⁻¹ was significantly reduced in the CP spectrum, which might result from electrostatic interactions between pectin’s free carboxyl groups and calcium ions after forming the “egg-box” structure. Reduced peak intensities at 1599 and 1505 cm⁻¹ in the BBR@CP microsphere spectrum were assigned to the phenyl ring of BBR, which might be attributed to the increased steric hindrance for phenyl ring vibration after microsphere formation. The hydroxyl peaks in the 3200–3400 cm⁻¹ region in the BBR@CP microsphere spectrum were broadened and intensified, probably due to the enhanced intermolecular hydrogen bonding after microsphere formation. Notably, peaks at 2925 cm⁻¹ and 1631 cm⁻¹ in the BBR@CP microsphere spectrum were assigned to C-H vibrations of BBR’s methoxy group and -N+-H in-plane bending vibrations of BBR’s quaternary ammonium, respectively. BBR achieves effective encapsulation in calcium pectate to fabricate BBR@CP microspheres (Fig. 3b). The successful fabrication of BCPD microspheres was further verified by zeta potential analysis. The negatively charged BBR@CP (− 26.7 ± 0.8 mV) transitioned to a positive surface charge following PAH coating (+ 33.4 ± 1.0 mV). Significant decrease in ζ-potential was observed after DNase I adsorption (+ 1.5 ± 0.1 mV) (Fig. 3c), indicating the successful adsorption of DNase I on BBR@CP microspheres. The prepared BCPD microspheres were spherical and exhibited a relatively rough surface under scanning electron microscopy (SEM), which substantiated the successful synthesis of BCPD microspheres (Fig. 3d).

Synthesis and Characterization of BCPD@gel

Given the need to maintain optimal physicochemical stability in the gastrointestinal environment, BCPD microspheres were critical for facilitating effective delivery to colonic lesions while preserving their structural integrity [36]. A naturally occurring linear fructan, inulin, was applied to develop the hydrogels as the primary polysaccharide based on its exceptional biocompatibility, proven safety, and enhanced bioadhesion and colonic retention [24, 25]. Thus, a BCPD@gel composite was designed to enhance the colon retention of BCPD microspheres for DSS-induced colitis treatment. The stable three-dimensional porous network could be observed under SEM in both the inulin gel and BCPD@gel, and the gels were able to pass through syringes of specifications with needle gauges 26 G (Fig. 4a), which confirmed their favorable gel characteristics and superior injectability, respectively. Microbulge structures of BCPD microspheres (highlighted by yellow arrows) were clearly observed in BCPD@gel hydrogel networks, suggesting the successful loading of BCPD microspheres (Fig. 4a). Subsequently, the rheological characteristics of the blank inulin gel and BCPD@gel were compared under different frequencies and strains. The loss modulus (G’’) was consistently lower than the storage modulus (G’) whether the BCPD microspheres were incorporated or not, indicating that moderate compositional modification did not alter the intrinsic mechanical properties of inulin-based gels (Fig. 4b). BCPD@gel had a higher loss modulus (G″) compared to the blank inulin gel, indicating that it possesses a more favorable viscous property and may persist in the gastrointestinal tract for prolonged durations (Fig. 4b). Satisfactory shear-thinning behavior is essential for the uniform distribution of embedded microspheres and subsequent oral gavage administration [37, 38]. The BCPD@gel exhibited favorable shear-thinning behavior like that of inulin gel, which facilitated subsequent oral gavage administration (Fig. 4c). The complex pH environment of the gastrointestinal tract poses a significant obstacle for oral drug formulations [39]. Given the abnormal intestinal motility triggered by diarrhea in IBD, retention within the colon is essential to prolong the effective time of drugs to achieve inflammatory relief. Early drug release in the upper gastrointestinal is attributed to increased systemic drug exposure and further impairs drug efficacy [40, 41]. The in vitro drug release behavior of BCPD@gel was analyzed under simulation gastrointestinal environment, with BCPD microspheres for comparison (Fig. 4d). The BCPD microspheres displayed a rapid initial release pattern in simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), with 57.0% of the substance released at 4 h. In contrast, BCPD@gel displayed a significant sustained release, with BBR release limited to only 23.7% in the SIF at 4 h (Fig. 4d). In vitro drug release studies revealed that the BCPD@gel composite supported prolonged and colon-specific pH-responsive drug release. The enzymatic activity of DNase I was challenged by the organic solvent invasion, mechanical shear force, and interfacial denaturation during nanoparticle encapsulation. Agarose gel electrophoresis was further performed to detect the DNase I activity. BCPD@gel showed a comparable marked reduction in the dsDNA band intensity to the free DNase I, indicating DNase I still retains activity after being fabricated into nanoparticles (Figure S4).

The in vivo retention study revealed that IR780-labeled BCPD microspheres (IR780@CP/DNase I) exhibited lower colon retention than IR780@CP/DNase I@gel in DSS-induced colitis mice (Fig. 4e). The fluorescence intensity of IR780@CP/DNase I@gel at the colon site was higher than that of the IR780@CP/DNase I microsphere at different time points. Specifically, the fluorescence intensity of the IR780@CP/DNase I@gel in colon tissues was 2.3-fold higher than that of IR780@CP/DNase I microspheres at 24 h post oral administration (Fig. 4f), suggesting that BCPD@gel exhibited an enhanced retention effect. Similarly, IR780@CP/DNase I@gel exhibits prolonged colon retention compared to IR780@CP/DNase I in healthy mice. The fluorescence intensity of the IR780@CP/DNase I@gel in colon tissues was 1.7-fold higher than that of IR780@CP/DNase I microspheres at 24 h post oral administration (Figure S5). Additionally, stronger fluorescent signals were observed in healthy mice relative to those of colitic ones at all tested time points. Such differences probably result from the accelerated gastrointestinal motility and impaired mucosal barrier integrity under DSS-induced inflammatory conditions [42, 43].

In vivo therapeutic effects in prophylactic treatment models of DSS-induced colitis

To explore the potential prophylactic therapeutic effects of BCPD@gel in a DSS-induced acute colitis mouse model, different formulations (BBR, DNase I + BBR, BCPD, Inulin gel, and BCPD@gel) were orally administered on the 1st, 3rd, and 5th days, respectively (Fig. 5a). Although all mice treated with DSS initially suffered weight loss, the mice in the two groups that were administered BCPD began to regain weight from day 3 onwards. Notably, the body weight of mice treated with BCPD@gel most closely resembled that of the healthy control group, potentially attributable to the targeted effect of inulin hydrogel on the colon (Fig. 5b). Given that the successful induction of DSS-induced colitis in mice typically leads to elevated DAI scores, a potentially effective preventive effect was strongly supported by the fact that the DAI values of the mice treated with BCPD@gel reverted to normal levels (Fig. 5c). The spleen index was then used to delve into the immune-regulation effect of BCPD@gel, which was calculated as the ratio of spleen weight to body weight. BCPD@gel treatment effectively ameliorated DSS-induced splenomegaly (Fig. 5d-e), suggesting the compromised immune activation.

Colon photographs and length measurements indicated that the mice in the DSS group had an average colon length of only 5.0 ± 0.9 cm on day 9, while this value recovered to 8.1 ± 0.7 cm in the BCPD@gel group, which was similar to that in the normal group (Fig. 5f-g). The pathological changes were observed via H&E staining in DSS-treated colon tissues with reduced goblet cells, crypt distortion, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. On the contrary, the mouse colons in the BCPD@gel group showed insignificant damage to the intestinal micro-barrier (Fig. 5h). Collectively, the BCPD@gel exhibited superior therapeutic effects on DSS-induced colitis compared with those of the BBR and the BCPD, which may be attributed to the essential role of inulin‑based hydrogel encapsulation in protecting DNase I from degradation and the synergistic regulation of NETs and pyroptosis.

In vivo therapeutic effects in delayed treatment models of DSS-induced colitis

Inspired by BCPD@gel’s promising prophylactic effects, the therapeutic potential of various formulations (DNase I + BBR, BCPD, Inulin gel, and BCPD@gel) was further evaluated for delayed treatment of acute DSS-induced colitis. Acute colitis was established in mice via free drinking of 3% DSS solution for the initial 7 days. Daily oral gavage administration was performed from day 8 to day 11, and all mice were sacrificed on day 12 (Fig. 6a). The body weight of mice in the BCPD@gel group reached 21.4 ± 0.5 g on day 12, which was close to the average weight of 24.5 ± 0.4 g in the normal group. (Fig. 6b). The BCPD@gel group also exhibited decreased DAI scores (Fig. 6c) and alleviated anal bleeding symptoms (Fig. 6d) compared to other treatment groups, indicating the improved general physiological status. Consistent with the alleviated diarrhea and loose stools, the colon length of mice in the BCPD@gel group reached 6.7 ± 0.39 cm, which was 1.4-fold longer than that of the DSS group and slightly shorter than the 8.6 ± 0.77 cm observed in the normal group (Fig. 6e-f). BCPD@gel exerted a prominent alleviative effect on DSS-induced colitis. In addition, the colon length of mice in the free BCPD group was 5.4 ± 0.27 cm, merely 1.2 times longer than that of the DSS group and markedly shorter than that of the BCPD@gel group. Collectively, the inulin gel plays a pivotal role in enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of BCPD@gel because of its capacity to protect BCPD particles against gastrointestinal degradation and prolong BCPD retention time in the colon.

Spleen weight and inflammatory cytokine levels in colonic inflamed tissues were detected to further explore the immune-regulation effect of BCPD@gel. BCPD@gel treatment effectively alleviated the intestinal and systemic inflammation, resulting in reduced splenomegaly and a spleen index restored toward the level of normal mice (Fig. 6g-h). The histological alterations within the colon tissues were further investigated to assess the treatment effectiveness. Intact colonic crypt structure serves as the foundation for maintaining normal intestinal mucosal barrier function [44]. Goblet cells are indispensable for mucus secretion and intestinal mucosal protection [45, 46]. Severe colonic tissue damage was observed in the DSS group with disrupted crypt structure and decreased goblet cell quantity. BCPD@gel treatment effectively preserved the colonic tissue integrity and sustained goblet cell abundance close to the level of the normal group, as expected (Fig. 6i). The histopathological score of intestinal tissues in the BCPD@gel-treated group was significantly lower than that in the DSS control group, indicating improved intestinal barrier integrity (Fig. 6j). The intestinal tight junction protein levels, including ZO-1, E-cadherin, and Occludin, were further detected via immunohistochemical staining, which are pivotal for tight junction assembly in IECs and central to the regulation of intestinal barrier integrity. BCPD@gel treatment markedly restored the immunohistochemically positive levels of these proteins to those comparable to the normal group, whereas such positive signals were obviously decreased in the DSS group (Fig. 6k). Collectively, these results demonstrate that BCPD@gel treatment efficiently‌ recovered the intestinal epithelial barrier function.

BCPD@gel restores gut microbiota homeostasis for treating DSS-induced colitis

Gut microbiota dynamics on mouse feces were analyzed via 16 S ribosomal DNA (rDNA) sequencing to figure out the correlation between BCPD@gel and DSS-induced colitis amelioration. DSS intervention altered the overall structure of gut microbiota and decreased alpha diversity, as manifested by reduced ACE, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices (Fig. 7a–d). These alterations implied a close correlation between DSS-induced colitis and gut microbiota dysbiosis. The beta diversity analysis, visualized through a principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plot, demonstrated the notable differences in microbial community structure among the DSS group, the control group, and the BCPD@gel group (Fig. 7e). Subsequently, the gut microbiota of DSS-induced colitis mice was analyzed at the phylum level to compare the microbiota abundance before and after BCPD@gel treatment (Fig. 7f). Specifically, the BCPD@gel treatment significantly reduced the abundances of the harmful microbial groups Campilobacterota (phylum) (Fig. 7g) and Proteobacteria (phylum) (Fig. 7h), alleviating intestinal inflammation. The community bar plot analysis showed the genus-level relative microbiota abundances in different groups (Fig. 7i). Lactobacillus, a genus of Gram-positive bacteria within the Lactobacillaceae family of the Firmicutes phylum, modulates gut homeostasis by digesting and metabolizing proteins and carbohydrates to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which suppress intestinal inflammation while enhancing both innate and adaptive immunity to inhibit pro-inflammatory mediators and exerting antibacterial activity against a broad spectrum of pathogens [47]. The relative abundance of the genus Lactobacillus was elevated in the BCPD@gel treatment group, which may be associated with improved intestinal microbial composition and alleviated intestinal inflammation (Fig. 7j). The analysis of linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) further elucidated the disparities in microbial composition (Fig. 7k-l). Intestinal microbial dysbiosis in DSS-induced colitis mice was significantly improved following BCPD@gel intervention. Such alterations might be attributed to the synergistic action between the inulin-gel carrier and encapsulated BCPD microsphere. Apparently, these observations still require further validation with a larger sample size, which might be the research subject of our upcoming studies.

Fecal microbiota transplantation and gut microbiota depletion experiments were supplemented to clarify the causal relationship between gut microbiota modulation and anti-inflammatory effects.

To investigate whether the gut microbiota contributed to the protective effect of BCPD@gel against DSS-induced colitis, wild-type mice were gavaged with a quadruple-antibiotic cocktail (ABX treatment) to deplete the gut microbiota before DSS treatment. Since the enhanced sensitivity of gut microbiota-depleted mice to DSS [48, 49], antibiotic-treated mice were only administered with 1.5% DSS via drinking water to induce colitis for 7 days, followed by 7 days of normal water feeding (Fig. 8a). Notably, insignificant differences were observed between the ABX (DSS) group and ABX (DSS+BCPD@gel) group in terms of body weight loss (Fig. 8b), DAI score (Fig. 8c), colon length (Fig. 8d-e), and mortality (Fig. 8f). The diminished therapeutic effects of BCPD@gel following microbiota depletion indicate that BCPD may alleviate DSS-induced colitis by modulating intestinal microbial homeostasis.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments were further performed in gut microbiota-depleted wild-type (GD WT) mice, which were reconstituted with the microbiota of DSS-treated mice (DSS) and BCPD@gel-treated mice (DSS + BCPD@gel) via intragastric administration daily for 5 days (Fig. 8g). All experimental mice were gavaged with the antibiotic cocktail microbiota depletion before FMT for better flora colonization. FMT from BCPD@gel-treated donor into a GD WT host mouse (FM(DSS + BCPD@gel)) resulted in significantly less colon inflammation compared with FMT from a DSS-treated donor (FM(DSS)), as evidenced by the decreased weight loss (Fig. 8h), DAI score (Fig. 8i), and mortality (Fig. 8l) but greater colon lengths (Fig. 8j-k). Specifically, the colon length in the FM(DSS + BCPD@gel) group was 1.5-fold longer than that in the FM(DSS) group. Collectively, we conclude that BCPD@gel exerts therapeutic effects on DSS-induced colitis by regulating gut microbiota.

BCPD@gel recovers intestinal epithelial barrier function via disrupting the crosstalk of GSDMD-dependent enterocyte pyroptosis and NETs

Given the prominent modulatory efficacy of nanoparticles on intestinal microbial communities, this study further elucidates the underlying molecular mechanisms by which they intervene in the progression of DSS-induced ulcerative colitis. Neutrophil function was evaluated to clarify how BCPD@gel restores intestinal epithelial barrier function in acute DSS-induced colitis. The proportion of neutrophils in the colon of the BCPD@gel group was 5.8 ± 1.7%, which was 59.4% lower than that in the BCPD group and 82.9% lower than that in the DSS group (Fig. 9a). This result indicated that BCPD@gel impedes neutrophil accumulation in the colon tissue following DSS induction. Meanwhile, immunofluorescence staining was employed to observe neutrophils (Ly6G+) and NETs (MPO+ and CitH3+) in colon tissue sections, aiming to evaluate the capacity of BCPD@gel in eliminating NETs triggered by DSS. The colocalization signal of MPO+ and CitH3+ was markedly reduced in the BCPD@gel group, while only a marginal decrease was observed in the BCPD group (Fig. 9b). The decrease in neutrophils in the BCPD@gel group was accompanied by a corresponding reduction in NET formation, further confirming the regulatory effect of BCPD@gel on neutrophil activation and NET release. Additionally, NETs and macrophages are closely involved in the pathological process of colitis. Macrophage infiltration was detected by F4/80 immunohistochemical staining to further clarify the role of BCPD@gel in intestinal inflammation. The proportion of F4/80-positive cells in the BCPD@gel group was significantly lower than that in the DSS group, indicating that BCPD@gel markedly reduced macrophage infiltration in colonic tissues (Figure S6). BCPD@gel exerts a potent anti-colitis effect by inhibiting NETosis-related inflammatory responses and suppressing macrophage-mediated intestinal inflammation. The regulatory effect of BCPD@gel on GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis remains to be further explored in subsequent studies. The relative protein expression levels were evaluated, including NLRP3, Caspase-1, and GSDMD (Fig. 9c), to further confirm that BCPD@gel inhibits the GSDMD-dependent classical pyroptosis pathway [39]. As expected, the GSDMD-NT protein expression levels in the BCPD@gel-treated group were 63.1% lower than those in the DSS group (Fig. 9d). The levels of Cleaved caspase-1 (Cl-casp1) and NLRP3 proteins were 77.6% and 58.6% lower than those in the DSS group, respectively (Fig. 9e-f).Such altered protein expression supported that BCPD@gel attenuated intestinal inflammation and maintained intestinal integrity by inhibiting GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis. Taken together, BCPD@gel restored the intestinal epithelial barrier function via disrupting the crosstalk of GSDMD-dependent enterocyte pyroptosis and NETs. Besides, BCPD@gel significantly reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6(Fig. 9g), IL-1β (Fig. 9h), and IL-17 A (Fig. 9i) while elevating anti-inflammatory IL-10 (Fig. 9j)in colonic tissues, demonstrating the capacity to suppress DSS-induced cytokine production and mitigate intestinal inflammatory responses.

BCPD@gel regulated Treg/Th17 balance

Balanced Treg/Th17 cell ratio in the intestinal mucosa is pivotal for preserving intestinal microecological homeostasis [49]. The Treg/Th17 balance in the colonic lamina propria (LP) and spleen was analyzed via FCM, presenting the proportion of each cell type as a percentage of total CD4+ T cells. First, the Treg cell (CD4+Foxp3+CD25+) proportions in colonic LP and spleen of the BCPD@gel group were 5.3-fold and 1.6-fold greater than those of the DSS group, respectively (Fig. 10a). Next, given IL-10 is vital for driving Treg-mediated anti-inflammatory responses [50], the proportions of IL-10⁺Foxp3⁺ cells were assessed in colonic LP and spleen. A 5.6-fold higher proportion of IL-10⁺Foxp3⁺ cells in the colonic LP was observed in the BCPD@gel group compared to that of the DSS group (Fig. 10b). Consistently, the proportion of IL-10⁺Foxp3⁺ cells in the spleen of the BCPD@gel group was 2.9-fold higher than that of the DSS group (Fig. 10b). The Th17 cell (IL-17 A+ CD4+) proportion in colonic LP and spleen of the BCPD@gel group decreased by 79.4% and 59.8% relative to those of the DSS group, respectively (Fig. 10c). Finally, the Treg/Th17 cell ratio was then analyzed to clarify the regulatory effect of BCPD@gel on Treg/Th17 balance. Specifically, the ratio of the BCPD@gel was notably elevated to 23.1-fold and 4.9-fold compared to that of the DSS group in colonic LP and spleen, respectively (Figure S7). In summary, BCPD@gel treatment restored colonic Treg/Th17 balance in DSS-colitis mice by selectively increasing Treg proportions and decreasing Th17 cells for maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Given that the Treg/Th17 polarization is closely coupled with inflammatory progression, the observed immune normalization is most likely a consequence of alleviated inflammatory burden and improved tissue homeostasis [49, 51].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study elucidates the crucial role of crosstalk between GSDMD-dependent IEC pyroptosis and NETs in exacerbating intestinal barrier damage in UC, filling a gap in the existing research on the interaction between IEC death and immune cell responses in this disease. By integrating BBR to inhibit GSDMD-mediated IEC pyroptosis and DNase I to eliminate NETs, the orally administrable BCPD@gel effectively breaks the vicious cycle between these two pathological processes. This action modulates gut microbiota composition, restores the Th17/Treg equilibrium, and maintains intestinal barrier integrity. This study provides valuable insights into the pathogenesis of UC and offers a novel and hopeful approach for its effective treatment. Future research could focus on investigating alterations in the intestinal microbiota, optimizing the formulation and dosage of BCPD@gel, and conducting large-scale clinical trials to further substantiate its safety and efficacy in human patients. Nonetheless, the DSS-induced colitis model only partially mimics the pathophysiological features of human ulcerative colitis, which represents a limitation of the current study.
