The effect of exosome-related therapy in cardiac revascularization procedures: a systematic review
Authors: Shayan Shojaei, Seyed Alireza Mirhosseini, Asma Mousavi, Diar Zooravar, Maryam Ranjbar, Saba Aghajani, Armin Attar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading global cause of mortality despite advances in revascularization strategies like coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and percutaneous interventions. Exosomes have emerged as dual-function agents, acting both as paracrine mediators of vascular repair and as drug-delivery vehicles. This systematic review evaluates the therapeutic efficacy of exosome-based therapies in cardiac revascularization procedures.MethodsA comprehensive search of Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted from inception to January 2025. We included experimental studies, evaluating animal subjects undergoing revascularizations. Risk of bias was assessed using SYRCLE’s tool. Study characteristics, population characteristics, intervention details, various outcomes, and limitations of each study were extracted from each included study.ResultsEight preclinical studies met our inclusion criteria. We observed that exosome therapies demonstrated significant benefits. Sirolimus-loaded exosomes (SIR-EXO) reduced stenosis to 5% vs. 59% in controls. Exosome-coated stents (ACC-Exo-REDV) suppressed neointimal hyperplasia by 60% compared to bare stents. Brown adipose tissue-derived exosomes increased left ventricular ejection fraction, while CABG + exosome patches restored diastolic function. Exosome-eluting stents reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α) by 93% and promoted M2 macrophage polarization. Exosome-enhanced stents improved endothelial cell proliferation by 40% and reduced platelet adhesion by 70%.ConclusionExosome-based therapies show promise in enhancing vascular repair, mitigating restenosis, and modulating post-revascularization inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05249-8.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Revascularization, Exosome, Systematic review

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD), encompassing coronary artery disease (CAD), stroke, and peripheral vascular disease (PVD), accounts for approximately one-third of global deaths [1]. Atherosclerosis and subsequent plaque formation within vascular lumens are central to the pathogenesis of these conditions [2]. Despite advancements in revascularization strategies in atherosclerotic conditions, such as coronary artery bypass graft (CABG), percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) including balloon angioplasty and stent implantation, alongside optimized medical therapies, atherosclerotic CVD remains the leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide, accounting for approximately 85% of all CVD-related deaths [3, 4].

Modern revascularization techniques often incorporate drug-delivery platforms to reduce restenosis, inflammation, and neointimal hyperplasia. Drug-eluting stents (DES), releasing agents such as Everolimus or Sirolimus, have become standard in the management of CAD and PVD [5]. However, emerging adjunctive therapies, notably stem cell-based interventions, are being explored to enhance vascular repair and functional recovery following ischemic events [6]. Recent evidence suggests that the beneficial effects of stem cells may be mediated predominantly through paracrine mechanisms, with exosomes playing a key role [7].

Exosomes, a subclass of extracellular vesicles measuring 30 to 150 nm in diameter, are lipid bilayer-enclosed vesicles originating from endosomal compartments [8]. They are now recognized as potent mediators of intercellular communication, capable of modulating inflammation, apoptosis, and tissue remodeling in the context of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and other ischemic pathologies [9, 10]. In addition to their signaling roles, exosomes carry a diverse cargo, including lipids, proteins, mitochondrial DNA, microRNAs, mRNAs, and other non-coding RNAs, which contribute to cardiovascular repair [11, 12]. Beyond mimicking stem cell effects, exosomes offer a promising platform for drug delivery, potentially enhancing the efficacy of agents used in revascularization procedures [13, 14]. Thus, exosomes may represent a pivotal adjunct in future cardiovascular interventions, functioning both as bioactive mediators and as nanocarriers for targeted therapeutics.

Although prior studies have predominantly focused on preclinical models of MI, recent meta-analyses indicate that exosome-based therapies improve cardiac function, modulate fibrosis and inflammation, and reduce apoptosis and autophagy in animal models of AMI [15, 16]. These findings provide a rationale for advancing exosome research toward clinical applications. However, there is a gap of knowledge regarding the usage of exosomes in cardiac revascularization procedures. Therefore, in this study, we systematically review the current evidence regarding the use of exosomes in cardiovascular revascularization. We clarified the methodology of our study, reported our findings, and compared it with similar studies in the subsequent sections of this study with the aim of elucidating their therapeutic potential and informing future directions in regenerative cardiovascular medicine.

Methods

Protocol

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [17]. The study protocol has been registered in PROSPERO under registration number: CRD420251001861 [18].

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic search of the literature was performed to identify all relevant studies. The following electronic databases were searched from inception to January 10, 2025, without any language restriction: Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Our search strategy was based on two primary cores, “Exosome” and “Percutaneous Coronary Intervention”, along with their related terms, combined utilizing the “AND” Boolean operator. The detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table 1. To further ensure comprehensive coverage, a manual search of the reference lists of included studies and related systematic reviews was conducted to identify any additional eligible studies.

Study selection

All search results were imported into Rayyan [19], a web-based application for systematic review screening. After duplicate removal, a two-stage screening process is performed. First, two independent reviewers (S.A.M. and M.R.) screened the titles and abstracts of all identified articles to assess their potential eligibility. Articles deemed potentially relevant underwent full-text review by the same two independent reviewers (S.A.M. and M.R.). Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, with the involvement of a third reviewer (S.S.).

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (S.A.M. and S.S.) using a data extraction form. The following data points were extracted from each included study: Study characteristics (first author, year of publication, country where the study was conducted, aim of the study, study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria, model used, and follow-up duration), population characteristics (number of participants/animals in each group (intervention and control), mean age, sex distribution, and relevant baseline characteristics), intervention details (description of the revascularization procedure, method of exosome delivery, composition of the exosome-laden collagen patch, characterization of exosomes, and signaling pathways targeted by exosomes), outcomes (primary and secondary outcomes reported in each study including (cardiac function, restenosis, inflammatory responses, histology findings, mitochondrial analysis, molecular findings), and limitations of each study.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of included studies were assessed independently by two reviewers (S.A.M. And S.S.). The Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation risk of bias (SYRCLE’s ROB) tool was used to assess the risk of bias in animal studies [20]. This tool evaluates the following domains: selection bias (sequence generation, baseline characteristics, allocation concealment), performance bias (random housing, blinding of participants and personnel), detection bias (random outcome assessment, blinding of outcome assessment), attrition bias (incomplete outcome data), reporting bias (selective reporting), and other sources of bias.

Statistical analysis

We used means and standard deviations, median, and interquartile ranges to demonstrate our findings. The standardized mean difference (SMD) with Hedges’ g was used to compare stenosis percentage, N/M ratio, lumen area, and media area between the two groups. A random-effects model with the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method was applied to account for methodological differences between studies. Funnel plot and Egger’s test were used to assess publication bias. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using a leave-one-out approach to confirm the robustness of the analyses. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered a significant level. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 17.

Results

Study selection and quality assessment

As presented in Fig. 1, a preliminary search yielded 5,644 records. After the removal of 2,055 duplicates, 3,609 studies were screened based on title and abstract. Of these, 69 articles underwent full-text review for eligibility, resulting in the inclusion of 8 animal and experimental studies in the final analysis. The risk of bias assessment, performed using SYRCLE’s Risk of Bias (RoB) tool, is summarized in Fig. 2. Most studies demonstrated an unclear or high risk of bias due to insufficient reporting of randomization methods, despite adequately describing baseline characteristics and outcome measures.

Description of studies

Among the included studies, two investigations by Aggarwal et al. [21, 22] evaluated the application of stem cell-derived EXP during CABG for the treatment of ischemic myocardium. Both studies utilized a surgical swine model of hibernating myocardium, with follow-up durations of 4 weeks [21] and 12 weeks [22], respectively. Hu et al. examined the therapeutic potential of a stent designed to release mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes, demonstrating favorable effects on vascular healing, including enhanced endothelial cell tube formation and proliferation, and inhibition of smooth muscle cell migration [23]. Kong et al. investigated the impact of exosomes derived from human fetal aorta endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) on vascular repair in a rat carotid artery balloon injury model [24]. Their study also delineated the differential effects of these exosomes on both endothelial cells (ECs) and smooth muscle cells (SMCs) in vitro. Yan et al. focused on the role of M2 macrophage-derived exosomes in promoting c-KIT expression and inducing dedifferentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) during vascular repair following stent implantation, with follow-up periods of approximately 28 days [25]. Three additional studies reported shorter follow-up durations of up to 2 weeks [26–28]. Linsen Li et al. developed an innovative nitinol alloy stent modified with exosomes and an endothelial-specific peptide (REDV), designed to enhance endothelialization, reduce thrombosis, and inhibit restenosis [26]. Hungshun Li et al. explored the distinct roles of exosomal microRNAs derived from brown versus white adipose tissue in modulating myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury [27]. Finally, Mehryab et al. uniquely assessed the utility of exosomes as a drug delivery platform [28]. They investigated Sirolimus (SIR)-loaded exosomes for the prevention of post-angioplasty restenosis, evaluating drug-loading efficiency, sustained release kinetics, cytotoxicity, and in vivo efficacy in a rat carotid artery injury model. Restenosis

Yan et al. found that the neointimal area increased significantly in M2 macrophage-derived exosomes (M2Es)-treated vessels at both 7 and 28 days, though neointimal thickness and the intima-to-media ratio remained unchanged [25]. In another study, rats treated Sirolimus-loaded exosomes (SIR-EXO) group exhibited a significantly lower N/M ratio (0.18) and stenosis percentage (~ 5%) compared to the Empty-EXO (N/M ratio: 0.81; stenosis: 44%) and saline control (NS) groups (N/M ratio: 1.91; stenosis: 59%). Moreover, statistical analysis confirmed that SIR-EXO markedly reduced stenosis compared to NS (P < 0.001) [28]. Furthermore, treated arteries in the SIR-EXO group remained acceptably open with normal blood flow, unlike the control group [28]. In addition, histological analysis revealed that Nitinol stents modified with exosomes and REDV peptide (ACC-Exo-REDV) exhibited significantly less neointimal hyperplasia compared to bare nitinol stents and those coated only with heparin-chitosan (ACC). This suggests that the ACC-Exo-REDV coating effectively suppresses the proliferation of SMCs, a primary contributor to neointimal thickening [26].

Cardiac function

Systolic function

Li et al. showed that Brown adipose tissue (BAT)-Exosome treatment showed Significant improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) (p < 0.05) [7]. On the other hand, White adipose tissue (WAT)-Exosome Treatment indicated a Significant decline in LVEF and LVFS (p < 0.01), indicating worsened cardiac function [7]. In the study of Aggrawal et al., under stress conditions, the CABG + MSC group exhibited a significantly enhanced regional systolic response compared CABG group, indicating improved myocardial contractility [21]. Similarly, under stress, the hibernating myocardium (HIB) group experienced a significant decrease compared to the normal group (P = 0.02). The CABG group did not show significant improvement compared to HIB (P = 0.24) [22].

Diastolic function

The CABG + MSC group demonstrated a significantly elevated peak filling rate normalized to end-diastolic volume (PFR/EDV) compared to the HIB group, suggesting improved diastolic function and ventricular filling dynamics [21]. In another study, under stress, the HIB group showed a significant decrease compared to normal (P = 0.03). The CABG group did not exhibit significant improvement over HIB (P = 0.07), but the CABG + EXP group showed significant enhancement compared to HIB (P = 0.02) [22].

Histology

In addition, immunohistochemical analysis showed a significant reduction in Ki67-positive cells (indicative of proliferation) and α-SMA-positive cells (associated with fibrosis) in the SIR-EXO group [28]. Specifically, Ki67-positive cells constituted 8% in the SIR-EXO group, compared to 20% in the Empty-EXO and 37% in the NS group. The α-SMA-stained/total cell ratio was 0.11 in the SIR-EXO group, versus 0.45 and 0.55 in the Empty-EXO and NS groups, respectively [28].

Histological analysis revealed that stents coated with ACC-Exo-REDV exhibited significantly less neointimal hyperplasia compared to bare nitinol stents and those coated only with heparin-chitosan (ACC). This suggests that the ACC-Exo-REDV coating effectively suppresses the proliferation of SMCs, a primary contributor to neointimal thickening [26]. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining demonstrated reduced thrombus formation and inflammation in the ACC-Exo-REDV group. The diminished presence of thrombi and inflammatory cells indicates enhanced anticoagulant properties and biocompatibility of the modified stents [26].

BAT-Exosome Group showed a smaller infarct area compared to the I/R + PBS control group (p < 0.01) and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating protective effects [7]. Oppositely, the WAT-Exosome Group indicated a larger infarct area compared to the I/R + PBS group (p < 0.01) and Increased fibrosis, suggesting detrimental effects [7].

Exosome-eluting stents (EES) on carotid artery healing showed significantly lower I/M ratio and Neointimal Formation, measured by integrated optical density (IOD), compared to the control group at both 14 and 28 days [24].

EES enhances endothelial cell (HUVEC and HCAEC) proliferation and tubulogenesis. It also inhibits SMC migration and induces phenotypic modulation, increasing alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression [23].

The CABG + EXP group exhibited the least fibrosis and inflammation, with an absence of α-SMA positive myofibroblasts [22].

Inflammatory response

Overall, the exosome-containing group showed higher anti-inflammatory responses and lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. M2Es-treated vessels contained more MAC-2 macrophages in the neointima at 28 days than controls (1.66% vs. 0.89%, p < 0.05). YM-1 + macrophages (M2 markers) were significantly increased at both 7 and 28 days, indicating an altered inflammatory cell phenotype [25]. Moreover, gene expression analyses revealed that SIR-EXO treatment significantly down-regulated pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α, as well as matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2). The fold changes in gene expression for TNF-α and MMP-2 were 0.07 and 0.09 in the SIR-EXO group, compared to 1.06 and 1.02 in the NS group, respectively [28]. In addition, in vitro studies showed that macrophages cultured on ACC-Exo-REDV surfaces had a higher proportion of cells expressing the M2-like phenotype marker Arg-1, indicating a shift towards an anti-inflammatory state. Additionally, these macrophages released lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α), suggesting that the coating promotes a favorable immune response conducive to healing [26]. Similarly, EES reduce inflammatory mediators (CCL2, IL-1β, IL1R1), which are linked to vascular inflammation and remodeling. It also increases markers of M2 macrophages (MRC-1, CD163) and IL-10, promoting an anti-inflammatory response. It also reduces total macrophage numbers and shifts polarization towards the M2 phenotype (higher CD206+/CD68 + ratio) [23].

Endothelial proliferation

Two studies indicated significantly improved proliferation of endothelial cells. Li et al. showed that the ACC-Exo-REDV coating significantly improved the adhesion, proliferation, and migration of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) [26]. In another study, EES enhances endothelial cell proliferation and tubulogenesis [23].

Migration and cell spreading

Two studies showed suppressed migration of muscle cells. In scratch wound assays, SIR-EXO significantly inhibited smooth muscle cell migration. At the experiment’s conclusion, wound closure was 73% in the SIR-EXO group, compared to 88% in the NS group [28]. Moreover, EES inhibits SMC migration and induces phenotypic modulation, increasing α-SMA expression [23].

Platelet aggregation

A smaller number of platelets were observed on ACC-Exo and ACC-ExoREDV surfaces, suggesting the excellent antiplatelet adhesion properties and better blood compatibility of heparin and exosomes-immobilized surface [26]. EES demonstrated a significant reduction in the adhesion of activated platelets compared to bare-metal stents (BMS) [23].

Discussion

A comprehensive evaluation of preclinical studies on utilizing exosome-based therapies in the field of cardiac revascularization was conducted in this systematic review. Overall, our findings suggest that exosome-based therapies can improve vascular and cardiac healing after cardiac interventions such as CABG and PCI in animal models. Exosome-laden biomaterials such as collagen patches and stents, and also drug-loaded exosomes seemed to reduce hyperplasia, enhance endothelial recovery and improve cardiac function. Reduction in fibrosis and inflammation was also reported following exosome-based therapies. Although these results sound promising, most of the included studies used small animal models, primarily rodents, with limited follow-up time and varied in terms of exosome sources and delivery strategies. Despite the differences across the studies in terms of animal models, study design, and exosome formulations, the majority of the studies reported consistent improvement in vascular healing and cardiac function, suggesting strong therapeutic potential. We made some efforts in order to do a meta-analysis on aforementioned outcomes. However, due to the limited reported data, besides the observed heterogeneity, we did not discuss regarding their results in the study. You can find our analysis in great details in Supplementary Figs. 1–10, whereas in individual forest plots we compared the media area, lumen area, N/M ratio, and stenosis percentage. To assess the potential for publication bias, we generated a corresponding funnel plot for each parameter to visually demonstrate the risk of bias within the included studies.

There have been previous systematic reviews discussing the protective roles of exosomes in cardiovascular disease in general [29] and in MI models [30]. Lai et al. also highlighted the beneficial effects of exercise-induced exosomes on vascular healing [31]. However, as a major strength, our study focuses on utilizing exosome-based therapy particularly in revascularization procedures as it lacks prior comprehensive investigation. While previous reviews have discussed the broader role of exosomes in cardiovascular disease, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to systematically evaluate their application as procedural adjuncts. We also included studies with innovative methods of administration, like EES and collagen patches [32–34]. These advanced delivery methods could pave the way for future translation of the findings into clinical application, as they facilitate targeted delivery to improve vascular outcomes.

Our review offers several key insights into therapeutic potential of exosome-based interventions. In terms of the mechanism underlying the cardioprotective effect that exosomes exert, studies suggest that they help prevent neointimal hyperplasia and vascular stenosis by suppressing smooth muscle cell proliferation and enhancing endothelialization [24, 35]. They not only demonstrated vascular effects, but also improved cardiac function since increased LVEF and LVFS was observed by Aggarwal et al. and Li et al. [22, 36]. These effects could be explained by the ability of exosomes to regulate key biological pathways like fibrosis, inflammation, apoptosis and shifting macrophage polarization towards a reparative (M2) type [32, 34, 37, 38].

Exosome based approaches are emerging as a promising regenerative strategy, but their effective application requires caution identification of suitable patients and close monitoring of clinical outcomes. Developments in artificial intelligence (AI), offer an opportunity to integrate predictive analytics with regenerative therapies, placing them within the framework of precision cardiovascular medicine. Recent progress indicates that AI is contributing to precision cardiology through integration of omics, imaging and clinical data [39]. In particular, interpretable AI models for multi-objective risk prediction illustrate how computational tools can reconcile conflicting clinical outcomes and guide clinical choices with transparency [40]. Current AI applications in cardiology contribute to patient risk stratification and optimize treatment strategies [41]. Furthermore, monitoring tools powered by AI, including wearable devices, allow for continuous observation and early detection of cardiovascular complications. This could significantly be helpful for the evaluation and follow up of patients receiving exosome-based therapies [42]. Overall, these advances indicate that coupling regenerative therapies with computational innovation such as AI, may contribute to clinical translation of exosome-based therapies into precision cardiology.

The therapeutic effects varied among multiple studies, which may be due to the difference between the cellular sources of the exosomes, affecting their biological properties, molecular cargo, and signaling pathways. Li et al. demonstrated that exosomes derived from BAT had beneficial effects on myocardial recovery, while white adipose tissue-derived exosomes were associated with adverse outcomes [36]. Kundu et al. and Gallet et al. also confirm the idea that the cellular origin of the exosomes determines their biological behaviour as it influences their content and function [37, 43]. Overall, exosomes originating mesenchymal, embryonic, and pluripotent stem cells, have their own distinct function even though they all promote cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation. Pluripotent stem cell (PSC)-derived exosomes are better known for their ability to promote nerve regeneration and wound healing. Exosomes originating from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are known to have angiogenesis and anti-inflammatory effects, whilst exosomes derived from embryonic stem cells (ESCs) have a wide range of regenerative effects and immunomodulatory capacities [44, 45]. Similarly, based on a systematic review of previous studies [46], cardiac- and vascular-derived exosomes exhibit source-specific roles. They all generally enhance angiogenesis and reduce apoptosis in cardiac tissues. However, only cardiac fibroblast-derived exosomes (CF-Exos), cardiac endothelial cell-derived exosomes (CEC-Exos), and adipose-derived stem cell exosomes (ADSC-Exos) influence immune and inflammatory responses in myocardial infarction via Toll-like receptor pathways. CF-Exos, ADSC-Exos, and cardiomyocyte-derived exosomes (CM-Exos) impede cardiac fibrosis in myocardial infarction and chronic heart failure. CM-Exos also reduce cardiac hypertrophy through mechanisms related to angiotensin II and heat shock proteins. CEC-Exos and vascular smooth muscle cell-derived exosomes (VSMC-Exos) have protective effects against atherosclerosis, and CEC-Exos show adaptive regulation in angiogenesis depending on the disease context. Notably, cardiac progenitor cell-derived exosomes (CPC-Exos) exhibit superior regenerative capacity compared with CPCs, MSC-Exos, and CF-Exos, likely due to their unique miRNA and cytokine cargo. Furthermore, the therapeutic efficacy of ADSC-Exos, particularly when preconditioned under hypoxic conditions, shows the potential of engineered exosomes for ischemic heart disease. Variations in miRNA content across exosomes from different smooth muscle cell sources, such as VSMC-Exos, human aortic smooth muscle cell-derived exosomes, and perivascular adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell exosomes, highlight additional path for targeted interventions and the development of exosome-based therapies. Collectively, these findings emphasize that careful selection and engineering of exosome sources is crucial for optimizing cardiac revascularization. Moreover, exosomes are known to be exercise-mimetic, as exercise-induced exosomes demonstrate cardiac protection [31]. This suggests that their therapeutic utilization could be taken into consideration as an option for patients with restricted physical activity, such as those recovering from surgery or with severe cardiac conditions.

The future of exosome therapy in cardiac revascularization is a promising and rapidly evolving field. Establishing the main therapeutic effects is the first crucial step, as our study aimed to explore potential areas of cardiac improvement and rehabilitation. Key questions remain regarding which exosome sources are most effective based on their biological characteristics, cargo content, and signaling pathways. Finding the precise mechanisms underlying their effects, including anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, or other functions is another step. Additionally, the optimal timing and route of exosome administration, whether post-infarction or following revascularization procedures such as CABG or PCI, require more investigations. Current evidence suggests potential therapeutic benefits, yet definitive answers need further preclinical studies. An important step is to minimize the biases in the studies, as methodological limitations can significantly influence outcomes. A key strength of our review was the systematic assessment of risk of bias, which highlighted several limitations in existing animal studies and informed a more cautious interpretation of the results.

It is noteworthy that despite the promising results of our study, it had many limitations. Only two of the included preclinical studies were conducted in large animal models [21, 22]. The remaining studies experimented on small animals, which despite providing useful details, may not fully replicate the complex pathophysiological environment of human cardiovascular system. Furthermore, studies were heterogenous not only in terms of exosome source, dosage, and method of administration, but also their choice of outcome measure and follow-up durations.

Another notable limitation is the absence of long-term safety data. The majority of the included studies focused on short-term outcomes and did not evaluate the potential adverse effects associated with exosome-based therapies. Furthermore, although our included studies used various routs of administration, there are existing studies that suggest local administration methods such as intramyocardial injection may be necessary to achieve optimal outcome and this delivery rout may not be practical in clinical practice [43].

In addition to the mentioned limitations regarding the included studies, it should also be kept in mind that conventional revascularization techniques, particularly drug-eluting stents, are associated with inherent risks. These include delayed endothelial healing, persistent vascular inflammation and a higher risk of late thrombosis, mainly due to permanent polymer coatings [47]. Whether exosome-based therapies can overcome these issues is yet to be determined.

Our review highlights several key considerations. Most notably, the varied efficacy of exosome-based therapy across the studies suggest that a deeper understanding of how the source and cargo of the exosomes determine their therapeutic behaviour is needed. This may also allow for developing personalized approaches, with exosome-based therapies based on specific patient and disease characteristics. Another key consideration is that exosome-based therapies are not intended to serve as an alternative for current revascularization methods, but to be used alongside them. Their role in enhancing endothelial healing and reducing vascular inflammation may be helpful following CABG and PCI procedures. Additionally, the majority of the included studies experimented on animal models without any significant comorbidities. Whether the beneficial effects of exosome therapy would also apply to patients with conditions like diabetes and hyperlipidemia remains unclear. Future research should focus on more clinically relevant models to answer this question.

Finally, important practical challenges remain regarding the translation of these preclinical findings into routine clinical practice. These include standardizing production protocols, ensuring quality control, scaled up manufacturing, and storage limitations [37]. It is crucial to overcome these challenges before moving towards clinical practice.

Conclusion

The findings of this review indicate meaningful clinical implications. Exosome-based therapies offer a promising approach and biologically compatible method to improve the outcomes of revascularization procedures. Exosomes may help overcome key limitations of current treatments, such as impaired endothelial healing and persistent inflammation, with their regenerative and immunomodulatory actions [47],. However, several critical issues should be resolved before translating these findings into clinical practice, including lack of large-animal studies, limited long-term safety data, and lack of well-designed randomized clinical trials. Moreover, comparative studies evaluating exosome-based therapies against conventional drug-eluting stents are necessary to determine their relative efficacy. In conclusion, while the existing data suggests a promising therapeutic role for exosome-based interventions in cardiac revascularization, more clinical studies are needed to validate their safety, efficacy, and long-term impact.
