Redefining clinically meaningful improvement in traumatic cervical spinal cord injury: a participant-centered perspective from community feedback sessions
Authors: Ian Burkhart, Marco D. Sorani, Gabriela Ocampo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Through a structured engagement initiative, the North American Spinal Cord Injury Consortium (NASCIC) consolidated perspectives from individuals with traumatic cervical spinal cord injury (SCI) regarding recovery priorities and the impact of functional gains on daily life. This exploratory outreach effort utilized the NASCIC network to capture community insights with respect to the development of targeted SCI therapies. The group represented a diverse range of lived experiences, with a median time since injury of 12 years (range 4–38). The group distribution (60% AIS A; 40% AIS B) ensured that insights captured the specific challenges of both complete and incomplete high-cervical injuries. Hand/arm function, bowel/bladder control, and trunk stability consistently emerged as top priorities, with participants emphasizing that even small improvements in these areas can be transformative for independence, caregiver burden, and emotional well-being. These insights challenge the traditional regulatory, clinician, and academic focus regarding large, functionally transformative improvements as the sole marker of clinical benefit. The findings support adopting a broader, participant-centered definition of clinically meaningful improvement in SCI and the use of outcome measures that reflect real-world priorities. Barriers to clinical trial participation and evolving expectations post-injury further highlight the need for patient-centered outcome measures and ethical, accessible trial design. Prioritization of participant-centered endpoints and addressing practical barriers to clinical trial participation should be considered in future research.

Introduction

The development of novel therapeutics for acute spinal cord injury (SCI) is frequently guided by regulatory, clinician, and academic definitions of clinically meaningful improvement, often operationalized as substantial gains in motor or sensory function. However, the lived experience of SCI, as consolidated by the North American Spinal Cord Injury Consortium (NASCIC) through engagement activities, informs further discovery that recovery priorities—particularly hand/arm function, bowel/bladder control, and trunk stability—are deeply linked to daily independence and emotional health [1–3]; these community-driven insights build upon existing literature by contextualizing functional priorities within the practical realities of current drug-development and regulatory-endpoint hurdles in cervical SCI. This Perspective is informed by structured stakeholder discussions with 15 individuals who self-reported as living with traumatic cervical SCI in the United States and Canada. Participants were engaged through community networks affiliated with NASCIC and participated in one-on-one virtual discussions guided by a conversation framework co-developed with members of the SCI community. A summary of the structured discussion framework, including thematic domains and illustrative prompts, is provided in Table 1. These discussions explored lived experience, recovery priorities, quality of life considerations, prior clinical trial experiences, and expectations regarding potential therapeutic advances. This initiative was conducted as community engagement and market research and was not designed as clinical research or to generate generalizable scientific evidence. Recovery priorities and daily life impact

NASCIC’s engagement with individuals with traumatic cervical SCI found that hand/arm function, bowel/bladder control, and trunk stability are consistently the top recovery priorities [1–3]. Even minimal improvements in upper extremity function—such as increased wrist extension or finger pinch—can dramatically reduce caregiver dependence and restore dignity in daily activities [1, 4].

Among the 15 participants engaged, 60% identified hand and arm function as their primary recovery priority. Autonomic functions, particularly bowel and bladder control, were ranked as a top-two priority by 80% of participants. Independent bowel/bladder control was universally recognized as foundational to quality of life, enabling social participation, employment, and community engagement [1, 5].

Trunk stability and the ability to maintain balance for transfers were highlighted as “critical” or “highly important” by 67% of contributors. Their comments clarified that this was primarily a means to achieve safer, independent transfers [1, 6].

In practical reality, improvements in upper extremity function were linked to feeding and computer use, while greater trunk stability was associated with safer transfers, dressing, and reduced fall risk across daily activities. The ability to manage bowel/bladder independently was seen as transformative, reducing caregiver burden and enhancing autonomy.

The value of small muscle function gains

A recurring theme was the transformative impact of small, sometimes subclinical, gains in muscle function. NASCIC’s synthesis revealed that recovery of even weak or isolated muscle activity, while not always translating to new activities of daily living, contributed to improved circulation, reduced pain, enhanced sleep, and a renewed sense of hope and motivation [1, 2, 7]. These subtle changes were frequently associated with psychological benefits, including increased engagement in rehabilitation and a more positive outlook for future recovery [1, 2, 8]. Because several contributors had lived with SCI for decades, the perspectives shared reflected long-term experience rather than only early post-injury adaptation. In these discussions, individuals described a shift over time from early aspirations focused on walking to later priorities centered on autonomy, restorative sleep, and relational stability. Contributors emphasized that as they age with SCI, incremental improvements that reduce overnight caregiving needs, prevent secondary complications, and support sustained independence can carry meaningful weight in daily life. Even modest changes in priority areas were described as having ripple effects on independence and overall quality of life.

Contributors frequently highlighted what might be considered modest functional changes, such as regaining enough wrist extension to hold a fork, small amounts of finger movement to allow typing, the ability to pull a sheet up or adjust clothing in bed, or sufficient trunk stability to feel safer during transfers. In these discussions, even incremental gains, which might appear limited when viewed through conventional clinical metrics, were associated with reduced reliance on continuous assistance and greater flexibility in deciding when to eat, go to bed, or leave the house. These shifts were consistently linked to dignity, autonomy, and a stronger sense of adulthood.

Evolving quality of life and expectations

Quality of life among individuals with SCI was found to evolve significantly post-injury, shifting from achievement-oriented goals to valuing autonomy, the ability to perform small but significant daily tasks independently, and supportive personal connections [1, 2, 9]. Sleep disruption, often related to bladder management and pain, was identified as a near-universal challenge, with participants averaging only 4 to 5 h of sleep per night. further underscoring the importance of incremental functional gains that can reduce nighttime care needs [1, 2]. Also, the complex balance between accepting necessary care and maintaining autonomy was described, with recovery priorities directly influencing this dynamic.

Barriers to clinical trial participation

NASCIC’s engagement identified several barriers to clinical trial participation, including challenges in acute-phase decision-making, geographic and financial constraints, and the need for trust and clear communication with clinical teams [1, 2, 10]. The ethical consent models tailored to the acute care context, with a clear explanation of the clinical trials to the individual and their family, may reduce these barriers. These insights support specific design adaptations: hospital‑centric drug delivery integrated into standard trauma center pathways; provision of travel and caregiver stipends; and early, structured involvement of peer mentors to explain risks and benefits in accessible language, particularly during the acute phase when individuals and families are overwhelmed.

Implications for regulatory and clinical practice

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) focuses on clinical outcome assessments that translate to endpoints that capture how patients “feel, function, or survive”. These findings align with FDA guidance recommending that measures used to evaluate new therapies capture outcomes that are meaningful to patients—those that reflect tangible improvements in day‑to‑day function or well‑being. By highlighting specific gaps between current clinical endpoints and the outcomes valued by people with SCI, this work helps inform endpoint selection and evidence generation relevant for future clinical trials and approval pathways. Emerging consensus from participant advocacy challenges the prevailing regulatory focus primarily on large, functionally transformative improvements as the sole marker of clinical benefit [1–3, 9]. Instead, there is a clear need to recognize and value incremental gains—particularly in priority areas such as hand/arm function, bowel/bladder control, and trunk stability—that yield meaningful improvements in quality of life and psychological well-being. Outcome measures in SCI trials should be expanded to include participant-reported and real-world functional assessments, ensuring that interventions are evaluated in terms of what matters most to those living with SCI [10].

Patient‑centered endpoints in cervical SCI are not only relevant for regulatory approval but also for payer decision‑making and societal costs. Across our community discussions, participants emphasized that when endpoints capture reductions in nighttime care or catheter-related complications, they reflect more than just clinical shifts; they represent a collective narrative of reclaimed time, reduced hospitalization risks, and a path toward pursuing employment or education. These dimensions are central to reimbursement decisions and to estimating the broader economic impact of new therapies, particularly in an aging SCI population with decades of ongoing care needs.

Current endpoints: alignment and gaps with participant priorities

In acute and subacute SCI trials, primary endpoints often include AIS grade conversion or total motor scores. While these capture global neurological change, participants questioned their relevance when small gains in selective upper‑extremity muscles or bowel/bladder control substantially reduce dependence without necessarily changing AIS grade. Consistent with priorities identified in broader systematic reviews [1, 8], our engagement sessions suggested that task-oriented scales, e.g., Box-and-Blocks, Action Research Arm Test (ARAT), Graded and Refined Assessment of Strength, Sensibility, and Prehension (GRASSP), and Spinal Cord Independence Measure (SCIM) may be more sensitive to the functional gains that community members value most, particularly regarding hand dexterity and autonomy.

When asked to reflect on the relevance of current clinical trial metrics to their daily lives, informants frequently contrasted standardized physical milestones with personal functional goals. Specifically, while participants acknowledged that short-distance walking tests are common protocol requirements, they noted a disconnect: for those with high cervical injuries, these markers often lack meaningful impact. Contributors often framed success in terms of everyday functional independence, such as feeding oneself, managing bladder care, or transferring into bed on their own schedule. In these discussions, while all participants recognized the value of AIS grade improvement, 87% emphasized that a ‘successful’ outcome should be measured by functional tasks (e.g., feeding oneself or using a phone) rather than muscle grading alone. These reflections highlight potential gaps between conventional outcome measures and the aspects of recovery that individuals may experience as most meaningful.

Conclusion

The NASCIC’s engagement initiative highlights that individuals with SCI consistently prioritize hand/arm function, bowel/bladder control, and trunk stability as the most meaningful domains for recovery, underscoring the need for outcome measures and clinical trial designs that reflect real-world priorities and participant-defined success. Moving forward, research and clinical practice should continue to develop and validate patient-centered outcome measures, address practical barriers to participation, and explore the physiological and psychological impacts of incremental gains. By centering the experience of people living with SCI, the field can ensure that new therapies and interventions deliver improvements that truly matter to those they are intended to help.
