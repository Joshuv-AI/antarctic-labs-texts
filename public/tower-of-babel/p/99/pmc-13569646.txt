The Natural History of Residual and Recurrent Disease in Advanced Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma: A Systematic Review
Authors: Shivani Angelique Kumar, Peter Petocz, Raewyn G. Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis systematic review explores the natural history of residual and recurrent juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibromas (JNAs) to inform clinical decision‐making.Data SourcesPubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science.Review MethodsA systematic literature review was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from inception to February 20, 2025 and was re‐run on September 21, 2025. Studies included patients with advanced JNA and documented follow‐up of residual or recurrent disease. Descriptive statistics, chi‐squared analysis, and analysis of variance were used to evaluate treatment outcomes across different modalities including surgery, radiotherapy, gamma knife surgery, and medical therapies.ResultsTwenty‐one studies encompassing 131 male patients (mean age 16.3 years) were included. Residual or recurrent disease demonstrated complete involution in 41%, stable disease in 29%, and reduction in size in 25% of cases. Only 2% of patients had progressive disease. A statistically significant association was observed between treatment modality and outcome (p = 0.015), with radiotherapy, either alone, or as part of a multimodal approach, showing the highest rates of spontaneous involution.ConclusionResidual and recurrent JNAs often remain stable or regress without further intervention. Close surveillance with imaging is a safe and effective strategy for asymptomatic patients, minimizing the risks of additional treatment in a young patient population with disease near critical anatomical structures.

Introduction

Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibromas (JNAs) are rare, histologically benign, highly vascular tumors with multifactorial pathogenesis that often present at an advanced stage and almost exclusively affect adolescent males [1, 2]. Advanced stage disease is characterized by lesions that involve the skull base, orbit, and/or extend intracranially [3]. Surgical resection of advanced stage disease can be challenging and pose risk to vital structures, such as the carotid canal, cavernous sinus, hypophysis, optic chiasm, infratemporal fossa, and anterior and middle cranial fossae [1]. If left untreated they pose potential fatal sequelae from hemorrhage and/or compression of vital structures [4]. Surgical resection of these advanced tumors is also challenging and can cause significant complications such as massive intraoperative blood loss and morbidity in young patients [3, 5, 6, 7]. The most common long‐term complications include facial paraesthesia (16%), ophthalmoplegia (12%), intranasal crusting (12%), cerebrospinal fluid leak (10%), palatal insufficiency and fistula, blindness, secretory otitis media, and craniofacial cosmetic deficits, especially after open resection [8, 9]. Due to the significant short‐term and long‐term complications, the aim of surgery for advanced JNAs is often debulking or subtotal resection to alleviate symptoms, resulting in residual or recurrent disease. Eighty percent of patients will have residual disease and up to 58% will develop recurrent disease [9, 10, 11]. Consequently, many controversies exist regarding the management of advanced JNAs, particularly the management of residual and recurrent disease.

The primary objectives of this review were to determine the natural history of recurrent and residual JNAs and to evaluate whether these tumors require active treatment or whether they can be safely monitored. The secondary objective was to assess what factors may aid in this decision‐making process.

Methods

Study Design

A systematic review of the literature was performed regarding management of residual and recurrent disease in advanced JNAs. A search was conducted in PROSPERO (the National Institute for Health and Research international prospective register of systematic reviews) to ensure that a similar review had not been registered. We did not find any papers addressing our research question through a preliminary literature review conducted on prominent medical databases, highlighting the novelty of this research question. The systematic review was then prospectively registered in PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251117979).

Search Strategies

A literature search was performed from inception to February 20, 2025 and re‐run on September 21, 2025 using PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science databases in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Figure 1). Fifteen search terms were combined with the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR.” The keywords within the first four searches were (1) “Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma” OR “angiofibroma” OR “JNA” OR “nasopharyngeal neoplasm” (2) “advance” (3) “residual” OR “recurrent” OR “unresectable” OR “involution” (4) “management” OR “natural history” OR “resection” OR “surgery” OR “treatment” OR “radiotherapy” OR “adjuvant therapy.” Two authors (S.A.K., R.G.C.) reviewed each paper against the inclusion and exclusion criteria in Covidence. The search was limited to English language sources only. A Population, Exposure/Intervention, Comparison, Outcome model was utilized to evaluate studies for inclusion as follows—(1) Population: Patients with advanced JNA who had residual or recurrent disease after initial treatment, (2) Intervention: monotherapy or multimodal therapy with surgical and/or nonsurgical modalities, with regular follow‐up and imaging, (3) Comparison: no comparison group, and (4) Outcomes: status of residual or recurrent disease at final follow‐up (i.e., complete involution, decrease in size, no change, increase in size, lost‐to‐follow‐up or fatality), follow‐up intervals, and surveillance method. Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) approval was not required as no human subject participants were involved and reported data was extracted from existing literature.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included original studies, case reports, and case series which described patients who were diagnosed with advanced JNA and had residual or recurrent disease posttreatment. Advanced JNA was defined as extension beyond the nasal cavity, nasopharynx, and sinonasal cavities [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. All treatment modalities were included. Papers describing posttreatment follow‐up and the status of residual or recurrent disease at final follow‐up were included.

We excluded systematic reviews which had duplicate data from the studies already included. Reference lists were searched for additional papers. Papers that did not include adequate clinical data were excluded.

Study Selection

Duplicate references were removed manually and via Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, VIC, Australia). Remaining titles were screened for eligibility against inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Risk of Bias

Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist standardized tool developed for case reports and case series [17] (Supporting Information Material S1). Two authors (S.A.K., R.G.C.) assessed for bias independently, then discussed any conflicts before selecting the final papers to be included in the review.

Certainty of the Evidence

Certainty of the evidence was assessed by two authors (S.A.K., R.G.C.) using the GRADE approach [18]. Each author assessed papers independently, then discussed any conflicts and developed the final results in Supporting Information Material S2.

Data Extraction

Study data included year of publication, patient demographics, presenting symptoms, clinical examination findings, imaging results (preoperative, postoperative, and at follow‐up), stage, surgical technique, other treatment modalities (radiation etc.), follow‐up period and intervals, and outcome (complete involution, decrease in size, no change, increase in size, lost to follow‐up, and fatality) at final follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis of the data was performed using Excel (Microsoft Excel for Mac 16.4, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and statistical analysis was conducted with the support of a biostatistician (P.P.) using Statistical Package for Social Sciences for Mac (SPSS version 29.0.2.0; IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics was performed on the entire cohort. Due to the heterogeneity of study designs and the absence of comparable effect measures, data analysis was conducted in accordance with the synthesis without meta‐analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline using descriptive and exploratory comparative methods [19]. The cohort was first grouped by outcome at final follow‐up, that is, complete involution, decrease in size, no change, increase in size, lost to follow‐up and fatality. Then patients were categorized into six treatment groups to determine if treatment modality impacted outcome: (1) surgery then gamma knife surgery (GKS), (2) surgery then radiotherapy, (3) radiotherapy then surgery, (4) multiple surgeries, (5) multiple courses of radiotherapy, and (6) surgery followed by sirolimus therapy. Only 42 patients were categorized into subgroups as the remaining 89 either (a) did not have clear treatment pathway described or (b) had therapies that did not fit into a statistically significant group, that is, surgery then treatment with vincristine, n = 1. In the subgroup analysis, there were only three outcomes—complete involution, decrease in size, and no change. Chi‐squared test of independence and analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were performed to determine if there was a relationship between treatment group and outcome. For ANOVA analysis, outcomes were categorized from best to worst outcome: complete involution—1, decrease in size—2, and no change—3. In the subgroup analysis no patients had an outcome of increase in size.

Study Characteristics

Risk of Bias

All included studies were case reports or small case series (Level IV evidence) and were of moderate methodological quality. Most studies met key checklist criteria, clearly describing patient history, presenting clinical condition, diagnostic investigations, interventions, and outcomes. However, many case series did not explicitly report consecutive inclusion of patients and often employed inconsistent follow‐up intervals or surveillance methods, raising concerns for selection bias and heterogeneity in outcomes assessment. Additionally, some reports lacked clear definitions for residual versus recurrent disease, and a few omitted any description of adverse events. Despite these limitations, a consistent pattern of outcomes emerged across studies. However, the overall strength of evidence is limited by the predominance of lower‐level study designs and the noted methodological issues.

Certainty of the Evidence

The certainty of the evidence for outcomes studied in this review is considered low to very low. This reflects the nature of the studies (case reports and small case series). The included studies demonstrated consistent findings across homogenous patient populations and heterogeneous treatment contexts. The consistency of treatment outcomes strengthens confidence in the overall direction of effect, but the absence of standardized definitions, objective measures of residual and recurrent disease, and variable follow‐up intervals reduces the certainty of conclusions.

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

Twenty‐one papers met the inclusion criteria including 131 male patients diagnosed with advanced JNA based on various staging systems (Chandler, Radowski, Antonelli, Fisch, etc.) or by radiological evidence.

The mean age of patients was 16.3 years ([standard deviation, SD; range]: SD 8.6 years; 2.5–48 years). These patients had initial monotherapy or multimodal treatment, which included surgery (open or endoscopic), radiotherapy, GKS, and medical therapy (e.g., sirolimus, vincristine). The mean follow‐up period was 5.1 years (SD: 3.6 years; 0.8–24 years). Patients were monitored with either magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) at regular intervals. There was significant heterogeneity, between and within studies, regarding the imaging modality and follow‐up intervals used for surveillance.

There was one fatality in the cohort. An otherwise healthy 10‐year‐old patient died due to massive epistaxis 1 month after completing initial radiotherapy [28]. His tumor did not respond to radiation and surgical management was recommended. The patient lived far from the hospital and did not return for follow‐up, succumbing to massive epistaxis 1 month later [23].

Two patients (2%) were lost to follow‐up [27, 37]. Of the remaining 128 patients, 29% had stable asymptomatic disease, 25% had a decrease in tumor size, and 41% had complete involution of disease without further intervention. Only 2% of patients were found to have disease that increased in size over the follow‐up period in one study [25]. One patient from the primary radiotherapy group and one patient from the surgery with adjuvant radiotherapy group had an increase in tumor size at 38‐ and 43‐month follow‐up, respectively [30]. They elected to have further treatment; however, treatment details were not provided and therefore, they were excluded from further analysis.

Chi‐squared test of independence of the subgroups demonstrated that there was a statistically significant relationship between treatment and outcome at final review (p = 0.015). Furthermore, ANOVA test of the subgroups revealed that the best outcome (complete involution of disease) was achieved in two treatment groups—(1) radiotherapy followed by surgical resection or (2) multiple courses of radiotherapy (mean: 1.0; p < 0.001) (Table 2). The worst outcome (no change) was in the surgical resection followed by GKS group (mean: 2.57; p < 0.001). [Correction added on 8 June 2026, after first online publication: In the preceding sentence, “decrease in size” has been changed to “no change” in this version.] The third, fourth, and fifth best treatment groups were: multiple surgical resections, surgery followed by radiotherapy, and surgery followed by sirolimus, respectively. Even with exclusion of the radiotherapy only and surgery followed by radiotherapy groups, 38% remained stable, 18% reduced in size, and 41% underwent complete involution demonstrating the natural history of these tumors (Figure 2).

Discussion

This is the first systematic review of the natural history of residual and recurrent disease in advanced JNAs. We noted that, in the majority of cases, the natural history of recurrent or residual disease is to spontaneously involute, decrease in size or remain stable and patients have remained asymptomatic through the surveillance period. This is particularly prevalent in patients who have had initial treatment with surgery and radiotherapy or radiotherapy alone, where all patients in these two groups had complete involution of their residual or recurrent disease without further intervention. However, even when excluding the two aforementioned treatment groups, 97% of patient achieved a favorable outcome, that is, lesions completely involuted, decreased in size or remained stable, without further treatment. Therefore, asymptomatic patients with residual and recurrent disease can be managed conservatively, that is, watchful waiting with regular imaging surveillance. The pathophysiology of JNAs may give us insight into why this may be the case.

Based on the tendency for JNAs to affect predominantly adolescent males, the role of hormones in its pathophysiology has been widely investigated. Early observations of the disease found that most JNAs spontaneously regressed after full development of secondary sex characteristics, leading to the theory that androgen deficiency played a role in tumor regression [38]. This led to the conclusion that treatment with high‐risk surgery and/or radiation should be avoided as the risk of complications outweighed the benefits in a disease that would ultimately involute [39]. Studies have focused on the identification of sex‐hormone receptors in JNAs and found the presence of androgen receptors, estrogen receptors, a mix of both, or no sex‐hormone receptors at all [40]. A systematic review on the molecular and genetic determinants of JNAs found that the presence of androgen receptors is dominant in tested tissue samples [40]. The literature to date does support the role of hormones in the progression and regression of JNAs, as rising testosterone levels during puberty are thought to stimulate tumor growth. This theory can explain the male predominance and pubertal onset of this disease process and the higher risk of recurrence in those diagnosed before the age of 14 compared with those diagnosed at an older age [41].

Another theory proposes that JNAs arise from vascular malformations and/or incomplete regression of embryological remnants from the first branchial arch artery [42] which explains the typical blood supply of JNAs [43]. Supporting this are findings of elevated expression of angiogenic markers, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), which promote endothelial proliferation and abnormal vascular development [40].

There are early studies on the role of genetics in the pathogenesis of JNAs [38, 44]. These studies have found chromosome gains on chromosomes 6, 12, and X and losses on chromosomes 17 and Y [38, 44], which could potentially explain a genetic etiology of this disease. However, these studies had very small sample sizes, and further large‐scale studies are required to explore the role of genetics in the pathogenesis of JNAs.

While no single theory explains all aspects of JNA pathogenesis, the convergence of hormonal, vascular, and genetic factors best accounts for its unique patient, histologic, and clinical features. The culmination of these theories also supports the notion that, as a patient matures, tumor growth may cease or regress [34]. During puberty, rising androgen levels, particularly testosterone, may stimulate tumor growth via androgen receptors expressed on tumor cells. After puberty, androgen levels plateau and the tumor's responsiveness to these hormones may diminish as it becomes more fibrotic, leading to diminished or halted growth and even spontaneous regression, as found in our study [34, 45]. Furthermore, as the patient ages, the vascular components of the tumor may undergo fibrosis or thrombosis, reducing blood flow and leading to tumor shrinkage. This aligns with histological observations of increased fibrotic content in regressing or longstanding JNAs [45]. As a result, residual and recurrent JNAs are likely to undergo spontaneous involution, or at least a progressive decrease in size, as patients mature and complete puberty. Spontaneous involution of recurrent or residual disease has been documented to occur between 3.25 and 12 years after initial treatment [34, 46].

There are long‐term morbidities associated with radiotherapy such as xerostomia, dental caries, panhypopituitarism, cataract formation, temporal lobe necrosis, endophthalmitis, keratopathy, blindness, meningioma, and squamous cell carcinoma [4, 24, 30]. A case series evaluating the long‐term outcomes of patients with advanced JNA treated with conformal radiotherapy reported one patient who developed squamous cell carcinoma of the nasal alae 15 years after radiation [47]. In another similar study, 33% of patients developed long‐term sequelae of radiotherapy: three patients developed cataracts 5–10 years posttherapy, one patient demonstrated delayed transient central nervous system syndrome and another patient developed a skin basal cell carcinoma 14 years after radiation therapy [23]. Thus, despite its efficacy, radiation therapy carries a significant risk of long‐term sequelae that can cause profound morbidity in a young patient population [4, 48, 49]. However, with the development of newer radiation techniques with more targeted fields, such as intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and proton beam therapy, significant morbidity can be avoided or reduced [48, 49]. Therefore, radiation therapy should only be considered for symptomatic patients with residual or recurrent tumors in anatomically precarious locations.

In the management of residual or recurrent JNAs, the risks and benefits of surgical resection or repeat radiotherapy must be carefully considered. Particularly, if the disease lies in high‐risk anatomical areas. As this systemic review demonstrates, close observation of patients with asymptomatic residual or recurrent disease with serial imaging is reasonable.

There is no consensus on the posttreatment follow‐up period for these patients. In our review follow‐up varied from 8 months to 23 years. JNAs typically recur 6–36 months after primary therapy, with the highest recurrence rates within the first 12 months after surgery [3, 50]. Therefore, a minimum follow‐up of 3 years, at 6 monthly intervals, with contrast‐enhanced CT or preferably, with MRI is recommended. This interval can then be extended to 12 months in stable, asymptomatic disease after the initial 3‐year period of surveillance. In patients with stable, asymptomatic disease we recommend follow‐up until (a) complete involution of disease or (b) up until the patient completes puberty and then for an additional 3 years thereafter. In males, puberty occurs between 9 and 14 years of age (SD −3 to −3 years), lasts 3–4 years and is completed when males reach spermarche [51].

An algorithm for radiological follow‐up has been proposed for asymptomatic patients with negative endoscopy and positive radiological evidence of disease beyond the nasal cavity at follow‐up 3–4 months postoperatively [44]. Prior to ceasing regular follow‐up, patients must be counseled regarding the symptoms that could indicate recurrence and specifically the risk of recurrence if commencing exogenous hormonal therapy in the future. One case report described a patient who had complete resection of disease (with no residual tumor) as a teenager, who then developed a recurrence after commencing exogenous testosterone therapy 20 years later [52].

Study Limitations

The main limitation of this review lies in the reliance on case reports and small case series, which are prone to reporting and selection bias. Variability in study design, follow‐up intervals, imaging modalities, and subjective definitions of residual versus recurrent disease contributed to heterogeneity that limits direct comparability. Anatomical location of residual or recurrent disease was inconsistently reported across studies, limiting our ability to assess whether site‐specific factors influence the likelihood of involution or progression. Additionally, temporal changes in tumor size during surveillance were inconsistently reported across studies, and therefore outcomes were analyzed based on disease status at final follow‐up rather than at intermediate time points. Reporting of adverse outcomes and long‐term morbidity was also inconsistent, and two patients were lost to follow‐up. Nevertheless, the aggregation of data across multiple centers and time periods offers valuable insights into a rare condition where higher‐level evidence is unlikely to be feasible. Importantly, despite methodological differences, the findings across studies were strikingly consistent, with the majority of residual or recurrent JNAs demonstrating stability or complete involution over time. Overall, these limitations suggest that while caution is warranted in interpreting the results, the observed consistency strengthens confidence in the overarching conclusion that surveillance is generally safe in select patients.

Future Directions

Future research should focus on establishing multicenter prospective registries to systematically capture long‐term outcomes of residual and recurrent JNAs across diverse populations and treatment settings. Standardization of definitions for residual and recurrent disease, along with uniform imaging and follow‐up protocols, would improve comparability and facilitate meta‐analyses with greater statistical power. Advances in molecular and genetic profiling may also provide insight into tumor behavior and identify biomarkers predictive of spontaneous involution versus progression and for targeted treatment options. Collaborative research integrating clinical, radiological, and molecular data will be key to refining patient selection for surveillance and optimizing personalized treatment strategies in this rare disease.

Conclusion

This study confirms that residual or recurrent disease in advanced JNAs has a tendency to either completely involute, decrease in size, or remain stable over long periods of time after initial management. Initial treatment with radiotherapy plus surgical resection or multiple courses of radiotherapy achieves the best outcomes. However, due to the short‐ and long‐term potential adverse outcomes associated with radiotherapy and the natural history of the disease, clinicians are encouraged to consider close surveillance of asymptomatic patients with stable recurrent or residual JNA. In stable, asymptomatic disease, we recommend 6‐monthly follow‐up after initial treatment and to continue until at least 3 years after completion of puberty.
