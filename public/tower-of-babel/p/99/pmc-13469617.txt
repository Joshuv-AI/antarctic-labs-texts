Design synthesis and evaluation of a novel 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivative as a potent anticancer and antibacterial agent supported by electronic property analysis and simulation studies
Authors: Abdullah S. Alawam, R. Rajesh, Abhishek Kumar Verma, Abhishek Dadhich, V. Choudri, Hassan A. Rudayni, Mubarak A. Alamri, Manoj Kumar Bohra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of heterocyclic scaffolds with anticancer and antimicrobial potential remains an important pursuit in medicinal chemistry. Among these, 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivatives are notable for their structural adaptability and wide spectrum of biological activities. In this study, a novel thiadiazole derivative was synthesised through a two-step process involving the condensation of (R)-carvone with thiosemicarbazide followed by cyclisation with diarylnitrilimines, affording high yields of 86–87%. The structure was confirmed by ^1H/^13 C NMR, IR spectroscopy, and density functional theory (B3LYP/6-311 + + G(d, p)), validating the formation of the thiadiazole ring. Vibrational and NMR analyses further confirmed the electronic environments, while computational insights revealed molecular stability with a HOMO-LUMO gap of 3.64 eV. Natural bond orbital analysis highlighted hyperconjugative interactions, and molecular electrostatic potential mapping identified reactive nucleophilic and electrophilic sites. Non-linear optical properties were found superior to those of urea, suggesting additional functional applications. Biological assays demonstrated significant pharmacological potential, with cytotoxic evaluation showing pronounced activity against prostate cancer PC3 cells (IC50 = 18.45 µM) and antibacterial studies confirming notable inhibition of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Network pharmacology and docking analyses identified BCl2 as a primary target, supported by a binding affinity of − 8.89 kcal/mol and stable interactions in 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations. ADMET predictions indicated favorable pharmacokinetics, although potential carcinogenicity requires further investigation. Overall, the integration of synthetic, spectroscopic, and computational methods highlights this new thiadiazole derivative as a promising lead with potent cytotoxic and antimicrobial activity, supporting its advancement in future drug design and therapeutic development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53341-6.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the world’s most widespread and deadly malignancies1, with limited treatment options and a high mortality rate2. Despite advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapy, drug resistance and tumor heterogeneity continue to pose major challenges3. Late diagnosis, tumor metastasis and the limited efficacy of current treatments further complicate disease management, underlining the urgent need for new therapeutic strategies4. The complexity of cancer stems from its genetic diversity, resistance mechanisms and adverse effects associated with conventional therapies, making the search for more effective and selective drugs a top priority in oncology research5.

Over the past decade, major advances have been achieved in the design of bioactive molecules built upon heterocyclic scaffolds, particularly through the integration of advanced computational approaches into modern drug design strategies. Recent studies have highlighted the central role of heterocyclic derivatives as privileged structural platforms for the development of novel inhibitors, owing to their chemical versatility, their ability to establish specific molecular interactions, and their potential to overcome resistance mechanisms observed in various diseases6–10.

Among the various heterocyclic scaffolds explored for anti-cancer drug development, 1,3,4-thiadiazole has emerged as a promising pharmacophore due to its versatile biological activities10. This five-membered heterocyclic system, containing sulphur and nitrogen atoms, has unique electronic properties and remarkable stability, making it a key structure in medicinal chemistry. Its derivatives have demonstrated a broad spectrum of pharmacological effects, including anticancer11, antituberculosis12 and antidiabetic activities13.

In addition, several commercially available drugs, such as Filanesib14, Litronesib15, Acetazolamide16, cefazedone17, cefazolin18, sulfamethizole19, megazol20, methazolamide21 and acetazolamide22, incorporate a 2,5-disubstituted 1,3,4-thiadiazole ring, underlining its importance as a crucial intermediate in the synthesis of bioactive compounds (Fig. 1). Because of these exceptional properties, 1,3,4-thiadiazole continues to attract considerable interest in the design of new therapeutic agents.

The anticancer potential of 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivatives is particularly noteworthy, as they can act via multiple mechanisms, such as kinase inhibition23, DNA intercalation24, induction of apoptosis and modulation of signaling pathways involved in tumor progression25. In addition, these compounds offer potential solutions to major challenges in cancer treatment, including combating drug resistance, reducing side effects and improving cancer cell selectivity. Over the years, numerous studies have explored the anticancer potential of 1,3,4-thiadiazole-based hybrid molecules. In 2012, Xian-Hui Yang et al. synthesized a series of cinnamic acid/1,3,4-thiadiazole hybrids and assessed their cytotoxic effects26. Among these, compound A (Fig. 2) exhibited the strongest activity against breast cancer (MCF-7) and lung carcinoma (A549) cell lines, with IC50 values of 0.28 and 0.52 µg/mL, respectively. Notably, compound A demonstrated superior anticancer efficacy compared to the reference drugs colchicine and combretastatin A4, emphasizing its potential for further therapeutic development. In 2015, N. Jain, Bandi Yadagiri, et al. synthesized a series of 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivatives incorporating a benzosuberone moiety27. These compounds were evaluated for their in vitro antiproliferative activity against HeLa (cervical cancer), MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative breast cancer), PANC1 (pancreatic cancer), and A549 (lung cancer) cell lines. Among them, Compound B (Fig. 2) exhibited remarkable cytotoxic potency, with IC50 values of 1.323 µM, 0.947 µM, 0.158 µM, and 2.354 µM, respectively, reinforcing its potential as a promising anticancer candidate. In 2018, Hai-Ying Liu et al. reported the synthesis of 1,3,4-thiadiazole-containing compounds and evaluated their anticancer activity28.

Importantly, all compounds exhibited minimal cytotoxicity against the normal cell line L929, indicating selective anticancer activity. More recently, in 2023, Berkant Kurban et al. synthesized novel thiadiazole-containing molecules, assessing their cytotoxic potential using the in vitro MTT assay and flow cytometry analysis of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)29.

However, despite the promising activity of the reported analogs, a gap remains in the literature concerning the hybridization (or conjugation) of the 1,3,4-thiadiazole core with a monoterpene scaffold, such as Carvone. Inspired by the low toxicity of this natural compound and the well-established activity of 1,3,4-thiadiazole, we designed and synthesized a new series of Carvone-thiadiazole derivatives with the aim of exploring their anticancer potential. This new molecular scaffold is structurally distinct from the existing literature (Figs. 1 and 2) and represents a novel strategy for obtaining more effective drug candidates.

Given its pharmacological relevance, the development of novel 1,3,4-thiadiazole-based compounds for cancer therapy remains a promising research avenue. Structural modifications aimed at improving selectivity, bioavailability, and potency could further enhance their therapeutic potential. Ongoing studies seek to optimize the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of these derivatives, with the goal of developing more effective, targeted anticancer therapies that address the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies in cancer management.

Methodology

Reagents and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Acros Organics and used without further purification. Infrared (IR) spectroscopy was conducted on a 5500 Series Compact FTIR Spectrometer. 1H- and 13C- nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III 300 MHz spectrometer with deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) as the solvent.

Synthesis of carvone-thiosemicarbazone 2

A mixture of 20 mmol of carvone 1 dissolved in 50 mL of 96% ethanol and 20 mmol of thiosemicarbazide dissolved in 10 mL of hydrochloric acid was prepared. The reaction mixture was stirred and refluxed for 4 h, leading to the formation of compound 2. The crude product was extracted and purified by column chromatography on silica gel using a hexane–ethyl acetate (75:15) solvent system, affording the corresponding thiosemicarbazone in 86% yield. The spectroscopic identification (NMR, IR and HRMS) of this compound has been described in detail in the following reference30.

Synthesis of 1,3,4-thiadiazole 3

To a solution of thiosemicarbazone 2 (1 mmol) and N′-Phenylbenzohydrazonoyl chloride (1 mmol) in ethanol (20 ml), a solution of triethylamine (3 mmol) in ethanol was added dropwise at room temperature. The resulting mixture was heated under reflux for 3 h, the evolution being monitored by thin layer chromatography (TLC). After completion, the reaction mixture was cooled, filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by column chromatography using a hexane/ethyl acetate mixture (95:5) as the mobile phase, in order to obtain compound 3 with a yield of 87%.

Computational method

The molecular geometry, vibrational frequencies, and infrared intensities of the compound were computed using Gaussian 09 W software with the B3LYP functional and the 6-311 + + G(d, p) basis set. The theoretical vibrational modes were assigned with potential energy distribution (PED) analysis using VEDA software. Topological properties within the framework of the atoms-in-molecules (AIM) approach were investigated employing Multiwfn software. Additionally, Natural Bond Orbital (NBO), Frontier Molecular Orbital (FMO), Nonlinear Optical (NLO), and Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) analyses were performed using Gaussian 09 W.

Cytotoxic activity

The newly synthesized thiazole derivative 3 was examined against the following PC3 654 (prostate), A549 (lung), and MCF-7 (breast) cell lines operating via the MTT technique to evaluate 655 their in vitro cytotoxic effectiveness. Cells were maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) 656 supplemented with Earle’s salts and Glutamax I, 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin657 streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and 658 routinely subcultured at pre-confluency using 0.05% trypsin and 0.53 mM EDTA. The cytotoxic 659 potential of the synthesized compounds was determined using the CellTiter 96® Non-Radioactive Cell 660 Proliferation (MTT) Assay (Promega, Charbonnières-les-Bains, France). Briefly, 2500 cells per well 661 were seeded into 96-well plates containing 100 µL of culture medium with increasing concentrations of the tested compounds (6.25–100 µM). After 24 h of incubation, 15 µL of MTT reagent was added 663 to each well and cells were further incubated for 4 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of solubilization/stop 664 solution was introduced, and plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Absorbance was 665 measured at 570 nm using a Revelation 96-well multiscanner.

Antibacterial activity

Cultivation of bacteria was achieved on Müller-Hinton Agar (MHA) medium, with incubation conditions set at 37 °C for 24 h, as outlined by Celiktas et al.26. The bacterial suspension was then standardized to 106 cells/mL, and for each strain, an antibiotic was included as a reference positive control (Table 1). To evaluate the antimicrobial activity of the synthesized molecules, their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using a microdilution method in 96-well plates, adapted from the protocol of Pellegrini et al.27. Stock solutions of the compounds (20 µmol/mL) were prepared in 2% (v/v) DMSO and serially diluted across the wells. Each well received 10 µL of microbial suspension previously adjusted to 106 cells/mL. As controls, well 11 contained culture medium with microbial inoculum (positive control), while well 12 contained broth supplemented with 2% DMSO and microbial inoculum (negative control). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 20 h before adding 20 µL of 0.7% MTT solution. After an additional 4 h of incubation, the MIC was determined as the lowest concentration at which no visible microbial growth was detected, confirmed by the absence of color change.

In-silico study

Target screening for designed thiadiazole core (THC) compound 3

A network pharmacology strategy was adopted to uncover potential molecular targets of the synthesized Thiadiazole-Hydrazono-Cyclohexene derivative (compound 3) in lung cancer. Predicted protein targets were obtained via SwissTargetPrediction using SMILES input, then cross-referenced with lung cancer-related genes from the GeneCards database to identify overlapping candidates. These shared targets were visualized using a Venn diagram31,32.

To explore the functional implications, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed through the DAVID platform, focusing on biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components (p ≤ 0.05). The most enriched pathways and gene categories were selected to understand the therapeutic potential of the compound33–35.

Interaction networks were built with Cytoscape to map the relationships between compound 3 and its gene targets. Key hub genes were identified based on network connectivity. Further analysis of protein–protein interactions via STRING, followed by CytoHubba scoring, revealed central regulatory proteins likely involved in lung cancer progression, suggesting their relevance as therapeutic targets36–42.

ADME properties

Evaluating ADME properties is a crucial step in early drug development, as it helps predict pharmacokinetic behavior and potential toxicity. These parameters guide the optimization of molecular structures to improve efficacy and safety. In the present study, the ADME profiles of the synthesized compounds were assessed using the SwissADME platform, providing insight into their absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion characteristics30.

Toxicity

Computational toxicity screening offers an efficient means to anticipate potential adverse effects during early drug development. By minimizing reliance on experimental models, it accelerates candidate selection while improving safety profiling. In this study, the ProTox-II web server was used to evaluate the toxicity of the synthesized compound, focusing on endpoints such as hepatotoxicity, mutagenicity, immunotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and cytotoxicity37,43.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock 4.2 to evaluate the binding affinity of the selected ligand to its primary target, Bc2 (PDB ID: 6O0K). The ligand was built from its SMILES code using UCSF Chimera, converted into a 3D structure, and energy-minimized to generate a PDB file. The target protein, involved in apoptosis and resolved at 1.62 Å with no Ramachandran outliers, was retrieved from the RCSB database. After removing water molecules and irrelevant residues, polar hydrogens and Kollman charges were added. The system was prepared by converting files to PDBQT format and defining a grid box centered on the active site. Docking employed the Lamarckian genetic algorithm, and resulting conformations were analyzed through BIOVIA Discovery Studio, enabling 2D and 3D visualization of ligand protein interactions44–47.

Molecular dynamics

Molecular dynamics simulations were carried out with Desmond (v5.9) to assess the structural stability and dynamic behavior of the ligand–protein complex over 100 ns. The system, neutralized with Na+ and Cl− ions, was embedded in a TIP3P water-filled orthorhombic box with a 10 Å buffer from the solute. Energy minimization using the OPLS-2005 force field preceded equilibration under NVT conditions at 10 K. The production phase ran under NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 atm, regulated by the Nosé–Hoover thermostat and Martyna–Tobias–Klein barostat. Interaction patterns and conformational fluctuations were tracked through trajectory analysis, offering insights into the stability and binding consistency of the complex41,48.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

Since the first synthesis of 1,3,4-thiadiazole by Emil Fischer in 188249, several synthetic routes have been developed. The most common method is acyl hydrazine cyclization, involving diacylhydrazines or monoacylhydrazines8. Other approaches include the cyclization of thiosemicarbazides50, condensation of hydrazine derivatives with thiocarbonyl compounds, and oxidative cyclization of thiosemicarbazones using oxidants like iodine or iron51,52, More recent methods involve metal-catalyzed cyclizations like copper53, and green chemistry techniques such as microwave-assisted synthesis and electrochemical methods54,55. These advancements enhance efficiency, selectivity, and sustainability in the synthesis of 1,3,4-thiadiazoles, which are widely used in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and materials science.

In this work, the synthesis of 1,3,4-thiadiazole in this study follows a two-step process. The first step involves the condensation of (R)-carvone 1 with thiosemicarbazide in ethanol, catalyzed by a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid, to produce thiosemicarbazone 2. After 4 h reflux, the product was extracted and purified, giving an excellent 86% (Scheme 1).

1H-NMR analysis confirmed the presence of two broad singlets at around 7.50 ppm and between 8.45 and 9.00 ppm, characteristic of the thiocarbamide group (–NH-C(SH)═NH). The 13C-NMR spectrum further confirmed the structural transformation, showing the disappearance of the carbonyl signal (~ 195 ppm) from (R)-carvone 1 and the emergence of two new signals: one between 147 and 150 ppm, attributed to the hydrazone function (C═N-NH), and the other in the 175–180 ppm range, corresponding to the thiocarbonyl group (C=S). The second step involves the formation of 1,3,4-thiadiazole 3 through the reaction of thiosemicarbazone 2 with diarylnitrilimines in ethanol, using triethylamine as a catalyst. After 3 h of reflux, followed by extraction and purification, the desired product is obtained in an excellent 87% yield. To elucidate the formation mechanism of compound 3, two possible pathways have been proposed: (i) a heterocyclization mechanism (Fig. 3a), where a series of condensation reactions leads to cyclization, or (ii) a 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition mechanism (Fig. 3b), where product 3 forms via a concerted reaction pathway. The 1H-NMR spectrum of compound 3 confirms the disappearance of the two broad singlets at δ 7.5 and 8.75 ppm, previously characteristic of the thiocarbamide protons. However, key features of the carvone skeleton remain evident in both 1H and 13C-NMR spectra. Specifically, the cyclohexenic double bond (HC═C) is observed at δ 6.1–6.3 ppm in the 1H-NMR spectrum, while the hydrazone (C1═N–N) group appears between δ 160.7–163.5 ppm in the 13C-NMR spectrum. Additionally, the disappearance of the thiocarbonyl (C═S) signal at δ 175–180 ppm in the 13C-NMR spectrum further supports the structural transformation. New signals corresponding to the formation of the 1,3,4-thiadiazole nucleus confirm the successful completion of the reaction.

Vibrational analysis

Vibrational analysis of 1,3,4-thiadiazole has identified several important vibrational modes, as summarised in Table 3; Fig. 5, providing further insight into the structural and spectral properties of the molecule. C-H stretching vibrations are observed in the range 3000–2800 cm− 1, corresponding to the free methyl group, with theoretical calculations predicting peaks at 3079 and 3077 cm− 1, showing a PED (Potential Energy Distribution) contribution of 100%, indicating strong stretching modes56. C–C stretching vibrations occur between 1200 and 1600 cm− 1, with theoretical values between 1146 cm− 1 and 1306 cm− 1, and slightly lower PED contributions, suggesting strong but slightly mixed stretching modes57. On the other hand, N-C stretching vibrations appear mainly in the 1600–1300 cm− 1 region, where several peaks with high PED contributions have been predicted, confirming the presence of strong N–C stretching modes58. These vibrations confirm the structure, composition and interaction of bioactive nature.

ELF and LOL

The Electron Localization Function (ELF) is a chemistry tool for visualizing how the electrons in atoms and molecules are localized68. ELF indicates where the electrons will be, crucial for the comprehension of chemical bonding. ELF values from 0 to 1 are given, with 1 meaning that the electrons are perfectly localized. For molecules, ELF is able to visualize areas of localized electron pairs, i.e., lone pairs or covalent bonds as shown in Fig. 8. This capability is especially effective at understanding complicated systems and anticipating molecular behavior. With ELF, researchers can get a glimpse at the electronic structure of compounds, helping to design new compounds and research chemical reactions. As a whole, ELF offers a simple and straightforward means of visualizing electron distribution, bettering our knowledge of molecular chemistry.​.

The Localized Orbital Locator (LOL) is a computational tool used to analyze chemical bonding by identifying regions where electrons are localized in molecules. It helps visualize how electrons are distributed among atoms and bonds, providing insights into molecular structure and reactivity69. LOL is particularly useful for distinguishing between different types of bonds, such as covalent, ionic, and van der Waals interactions. By applying quantum mechanical methods like Density Functional Theory (DFT), the diagram Fig. 9 LOL generates 3D maps that illustrate the spatial distribution of electron density. These maps can reveal the presence of lone pairs, bond strengths, and areas of electron delocalization. In the analysis of 1,3,4-thiadiazole, LOL identified regions with strong hydrogen bonding interactions, enhancing the understanding of the molecule’s behavior. Overall, LOL is a valuable tool for chemists seeking to interpret complex molecular interactions and predict chemical properties.

Non-covalent interactions

Non-covalent interactions are weak forces that help molecules stick together without sharing electrons. These interactions are essential in various biological and chemical processes. One common type is hydrogen bonding, where a hydrogen atom is attracted to an electronegative atom like nitrogen70. Another type is van der Waals forces, which occur due to temporary shifts in electron density, leading to weak attractions between molecules as shown in Fig. 10. Additionally, hydrophobic interactions happen when nonpolar molecules avoid contact with water, leading them to group together. Although these forces are weaker than covalent bonds, they are crucial for the structure and function of many biological molecules, such as proteins and DNA. They influence how molecules interact, fold, and maintain their shapes. Understanding these interactions is vital in fields like drug design and molecular biology.

Reduced Density Gradient (RDG) analysis is a computational method applied to visualize weak interactions in molecules. Through analysis of the electron density gradient, RDG facilitates the identification of areas where non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, take place59,71. In this research, RDG analysis was carried out using Multiwfn 3.8 software, and visualization was achieved with VMD. The color map of RDG identified numerous important interaction sites are shown in Fig. 11. Green areas show van der Waals interactions, being weak, non-covalent forces between nitrogen and carbon atoms. Red areas show Mark steric effects, in which atoms are in close contact, resulting in repulsive interactions between nitrogen and nitrogen atoms. These results give good insight into the molecular interactions and stability of the compound and help understand its chemical behavior.

The evaluation was performed using a standard in vitro cytotoxicity assay, and the results are summarized in Fig. 12. This selection of cell lines was chosen to provide a broad assessment of the compound’s anticancer activity across distinct tissue types, thereby offering insights into its potential as a multi-targeted chemotherapeutic candidate.

The compound exhibited its strongest effect against prostate cancer PC3 cells, with a IC50 value of 18.45 ± 1.34 µM, which is approximately twofold less potent compared to the reference drug doxorubicin (IC50 = 6.32 ± 1.23 µM). Despite this difference, the observed activity remains significant and highlights the potential of derivative 3 as an anticancer agent with specific activity toward this cell line.

In addition, the compound demonstrated moderate cytotoxicity against lung cancer A549 cells, with an IC50 value of 24.65 ± 2.12 µM, although with lower selectivity compared to PC3 cells. Finally, regarding the MCF-7 breast cancer cell line, derivative 3 displayed relatively weaker cytotoxic activity, with an IC50 value of 21.23 ± 2.03 µM, indicating a reduced sensitivity.

Overall, these findings suggest that thiadiazole derivative 3 possesses an interesting cytotoxic profile, with pronounced selectivity toward prostate cancer PC3 cells. This selectivity positions the compound as a promising candidate for the development of new targeted therapeutic strategies against prostate cancer, while further investigations are warranted to better elucidate its mechanism of action and optimize its structure for enhanced potency and selectivity.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial properties of compound 3 were investigated against a panel of bacterial strains, with the aim of exploring its potential applications in areas such as food preservation and as an affordable complement to conventional antibiotics. The results demonstrated that the compound exerted only moderate inhibitory effects on the majority of microorganisms tested. Notably, the derivative 3 exhibited a marked activity against Pectobacterium carotovorum, producing an inhibition zone of 25.2 mm, while the standard antibiotic generated a larger zone of 46.1 mm, thereby underlining the relatively enhanced efficacy of this derivative. In the case of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, compound 3 maintained a moderate effect, reflected by an inhibition diameter of 9.6 mm, indicating a restricted yet detectable impact on this Gram-negative bacterium. With regard to Escherichia coli, its antibacterial action was comparatively weak, as the inhibition zone measured 8.6 mm nearly four times lower than that obtained with the reference drug (35.6 mm). In line with these findings, the thiadiazole-based compound 3 demonstrated an inactive profile (MIC > 40 µM) against Staphylococcus, as presented in Fig. 13, thereby suggesting that its therapeutic potential against Gram-positive bacteria is limited.

Toxicity studies

In terms of toxicity predictions, compound 3 is predicted to be non-hepatotoxic (probability 0.55) and non-immunotoxic (probability 0.99), indicating a favourable safety profile in these aspects. However, the compound exhibits carcinogenic potential (probability 0.58) and mutagenicity (probability 0.55). Although these probabilities are modest, they highlight potential structural alerts that merit further investigation. Such predictions are not conclusive evidence of toxicity but rather serve as early warnings based on structural similarity to known toxicophores. The presence of an aromatic thiadiazole core and extended conjugation may contribute to these predictions, as planar heteroaromatic systems are sometimes associated with DNA-binding or reactive metabolite formation. To mitigate these risks in future analogs, rational scaffold modification could be employed such as reducing aromatic planarity, replacing the sulfur atom with less reactive heteroatoms, or incorporating hydrophilic substituents to minimize metabolic activation and improve clearance. The compound is non-cytotoxic (probability: 0.75), reducing concerns related to general cell viability. These findings suggest that compound 3 demonstrates promising pharmacokinetic properties. Molecular docking

Molecular docking studies were conducted to evaluate the binding affinity and interactions of compound 3 with the BCl2 protein (PDB ID: 6O0K) compared to the reference drug Gefitinib. Compound 3 exhibited a docking score (DS) of − 8.89 kcal/mol, indicating a stronger binding affinity than Gefitinib (DS = − 6.39 kcal/mol), suggesting its potential as a potent BCl2 inhibitor. Additionally, compound 3 formed seven non-covalent interactions (NIR = 7) with key amino acid residues, demonstrating strong molecular stability at the binding site. The key interactions include Pi-Sigma bonding with Val 133, Pi-Alkyl interactions with Arg 149, Val 156, Tyr 108, and Leu 137, and Pi-Sulfur interactions with Phe 104 and Met 115, contributing to its high binding affinity. In contrast, Gefitinib formed six interactions (NIR = 6), including hydrogen bonding with Gln 118, Pi-Alkyl interactions with Val 133, Leu 119, Val 156, and Phe 112, and Pi-Sigma interaction with Met 115. While Gefitinib exhibited a diverse interaction profile, its lower binding energy suggests weaker overall stability compared to compound 3. Venetoclax displayed a docking score of − 11.73 kcal mol−1 and formed 13 protein–ligand interactions. Key contacts include an H-bond with Arg107, an amide–π stacked interaction with Phe104, an alkyl interaction with Arg146, and a C-H contact with Tyr108. Additional hydrophobic contacts were observed with Val148, Ala149, Val156, Val133, Met115, Phe153, Ala100, Asn143, and Asp111, which together likely stabilize the ligand in the binding pocket. Though compound 3 showed less binding affinity than the venetoclax, it interacted with common residues including Phe104, Val133, Met115, Tyr108, and Val156, indicating that they occupy overlapping regions of the binding site. These findings highlight compound 3 as a promising BCL2 inhibitor with superior binding affinity and interaction strength. Molecular dynamics

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to assess the stability, flexibility, and interactions of compound 3 within the BCL2 binding pocket over 100ns. Key parameters, including root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), protein-ligand contacts, and ligand properties, were analyzed to evaluate the behavior of the ligand in the binding site.

Protein ligand RMSD of compound 3

The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) is a vital parameter that reflects the structural stability of both the protein and ligand during the simulation. The initial RMSD of the protein started at 2.4 Å and gradually increased to 4.8 Å, where it remained stable throughout the 100 ns simulation. This indicates that the protein structure did not undergo significant conformational changes, maintaining its integrity during ligand binding. The ligand RMSD initially measured 2 Å, showing minimal fluctuations in the early phase of the simulation. By 20 ns, the RMSD increased to 5 Å, indicating an adjustment phase as the ligand optimally fit into the binding site. Subsequently, the RMSD further increased to a range of 6–7 Å, where it stabilized for the remainder of the simulation. This moderate increase in ligand RMSD suggests ligand flexibility within the binding site, which may be essential for favourable interactions. The Protein Ligand RMSD of compound 3 is shown in Fig. 19A.

Protein and ligand RMSF

The root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) was analyzed to evaluate the flexibility of individual residues and the ligand during the simulation. The fluctuation of amino acid residues remained below 1.8 Å, indicating minimal structural deviations and confirming the overall rigidity of the protein in the presence of the ligand. The ligand RMSF was less than 2 Å, demonstrating a stable interaction with the active site residues. The low RMSF values suggest that compound 3 maintained consistent interactions within the binding pocket, supporting its strong binding affinity. The Protein Ligand RMSD of compound 3 is shown in Fig. 19B.

Protein-ligand contacts

Understanding protein-ligand interactions is essential to evaluating binding affinity and stability. The interaction analysis revealed strong hydrophobic, ionic, and water-mediated interactions between compound 3 and key residues in the BCl2 binding pocket. Phe 104 and Tyr 108 formed strong hydrophobic interactions, playing a crucial role in stabilizing the ligand within the active site. Met 115 and Leu 137 also contributed to hydrophobic interactions (0.25 Å), reinforcing ligand binding. Asp 111 formed an ionic bond (0.25 Å) with the ligand, a crucial interaction that enhances ligand binding stability. A water bridge was observed at 0.40 Å, indicating water-mediated interactions that contributed to additional stabilization of the ligand within the binding pocket. The presence of multiple hydrophobic, ionic, and water-mediated interactions strongly suggests that compound 3 forms a highly stable complex with BCL2, making it a promising candidate for further drug development. The Protein Ligand contacts of compound 3 is shown in Fig. 19C.

Ligand properties

The radius of gyration (Rg) provides insight into the compactness and structural integrity of the ligand. Initially, the Rg was 4 Å, and it increased slightly to 4.4 Å, where it remained stable throughout the 100 ns simulation. This suggests that the ligand maintained its structural conformation without significant expansion or contraction. The MolSA started at 375 Å2, increased to 405 Å2, and then fluctuated between 375 and 390 Å2, maintaining stability until the end of the simulation. This behavior indicates consistent ligand exposure within the binding site. The SASA was initially 240 Å2 and fluctuated between 160 and 240 Å2, showing moderate solvent exposure throughout the simulation. The PSA ranged between 30 and 42 Å2, indicating moderate polarity, which may contribute to the ligand’s drug-likeness and potential bioavailability. Conclusion

This entitled study successfully developed a new 1,3,4-thiadiazole compound via an efficient synthetic route, with full characterisation using spectroscopic methods and DFT calculations. The derivative demonstrated favourable electronic properties, including a stable HOMO-LUMO gap (3.64 eV) and significant non-linear optical activity. The cytotoxic potential of thiadiazole derivative 3 was investigated against various human cancer cell lines. Among them, the compound exhibited its strongest inhibitory effect on prostate cancer PC3 cells, with an IC50 value of 18.45 µM. The antibacterial activity of thiadiazole derivative 3 was further assessed against selected Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains, where the compound demonstrated significant growth inhibition, indicating its potential as a promising antimicrobial candidate. Moreover, anti-cancer potential through strong binding to the BCl2 target (− 8.89 kcal/mol), as shown by molecular docking and confirmed by MD 100 ns simulations. While ADMET predictions revealed good gastrointestinal absorption but potential carcinogenicity, the combined synthetic, computational and pharmacological results make this thiadiazole derivative a versatile candidate worthy of further study for both therapeutic and materials science applications. Future work should focus on experimental validation of bioactivity, structural optimisation to reduce the risk of toxicity and exploration of its optoelectronic properties.
