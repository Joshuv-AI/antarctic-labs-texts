Trans‐Ulnar Single Incision Fasciotomy for Decompression of Forearm Compartment Syndrome: A Cadaveric Study
Authors: Summer M Drees, Cade R McGarvey, Noah Miller, Sahil Kapur, Anderson Lee, Martin Skie, Ahmed Suparno Bahar Moni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAcute compartment syndrome (ACS) is a medical emergency that requires timely intervention, and delays in treatment can lead to severe complications such as nerve injury, muscle necrosis, amputation, and even death. Definitive treatment of ACS requires a fasciotomy. Currently, there is no consensus on the best approach for a forearm fasciotomy; compartment release is most commonly done through volar or combined volar and dorsal incisions. A trans‐ulnar single incision approach has been demonstrated to be effective in a case report. This study investigates if a trans‐ulnar single incision decompresses both deep volar and dorsal forearm compartments to less than 30 mmHg in a cadaveric model of ACS.MethodsTen fresh, frozen cadaveric upper extremities were injected with egg whites and compartment pressures were measured to determine successful simulation of ACS. A single trans‐ulnar incision was made between the Flexor Carpi Ulnaris (FCU) and Extensor Carpi Ulnaris (ECU), extending from 4 to 5 cm (2″) proximal to the ulnar styloid to 6 to 8 cm (3″) distal to the olecranon. After blunt dissection to release the compartments, pressures were measured to confirm decompression.ResultsACS was successfully simulated in all upper extremities to above 30 mmHg. The mean volume of saline injected to simulate local anesthetic was 38.0 ± 4.2 mL. The mean operative time was 10.1 min. The mean compartment readings 1‐min post‐fasciotomy were 7.1 ± 3.0 mmHg for the deep volar compartment and 9.4 ± 5.6 mmHg for the dorsal compartment. All fasciotomies reduced deep volar and dorsal compartment pressures below the clinical threshold of 30 mmHg, with significant differences between pre‐ and post‐fasciotomy pressures.ConclusionsAll 10 fasciotomies successfully reduced deep volar and dorsal compartment pressures to below the clinical threshold of 30 mmHg, demonstrating the success of the trans‐ulnar single incision fasciotomy to decompress compartment syndrome in cadaveric forearms.Keywords: compartment syndrome, fascial release, fasciotomy, forearm, trans‐ulnar incision

Introduction

Acute compartment syndrome (ACS) is a medical emergency, where increased compartmental pressure leads to a decrease in blood flow to the muscle compartments, causing ischemia and eventual muscle necrosis. It usually affects the lower leg, forearm, thigh, or hand [1]. It has an incidence of 7.3 per 100,000 in men and 0.7 per 100,000 in women. The mean age of patients affected by ACS is 32 years [2]. Younger individuals are more commonly affected, likely due to their greater intracompartmental muscle mass and higher likelihood of being involved in a high‐energy trauma [2, 3, 4]. ACS requires prompt diagnosis and timely intervention, as delay can lead to severe consequences, including severe infection, nerve injuries, muscle necrosis, loss of limb, or development of contractures. Increased time to fasciotomy is associated with a poorer prognosis [2]. Too long of a delay can lead to increased risk of amputation or even death [5]. Nerve and muscle damage is generally irreversible after 8 h of pressurization [6].

In addition to the potential life‐long medical consequences, ACS imparts a large economic burden on healthcare systems. The diagnosis of ACS is often missed or delayed. The litigation for medical negligence speaks volumes to this failure to diagnose expeditiously. The magnitude of financial impact for ACS was found to be behind only damage to vital structures during surgery, wrong site surgery, and failure to diagnose and treat pulmonary embolism [2]. This delay in diagnosis can be very costly to the patient and the healthcare system; therefore, recognizing signs of ACS and treating it promptly is paramount.

Forearm ACS has a multitude of traumatic and non‐traumatic etiologies, including bone fracture, soft tissue injury, vascular injury, prolonged immobilization, vascular procedures, and hematologic diseases [7, 8]. The two most common fractures leading to compartment syndrome are supracondylar humerus fractures and fractures of both forearm bones. Soft tissue injuries such as blunt trauma, burn injuries, crush injuries, or even snake bites can lead to ACS. Vascular injuries leading to forearm ACS can occur in an injury to the brachial artery, radial artery, anterior interosseous artery, or ulnar artery [7]. Common clinical presentations of ACS are pain out of proportion to physical examination that is worsened with passive stretching, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, and eventually pulselessness [5, 6]. Along with the clinical symptoms, the criteria utilized to diagnose ACS are compartment pressure above 30 mmHg [5].

Forearm ACS necessitates release of compartmental pressure by surgical fasciotomy [9]. Due to the speed at which cellular damage occurs, early intervention is necessary for maximal benefit [10]. Fasciotomy restores normal blood flow and removes anaerobic metabolites, resulting in restored cellular function [6].

Forearm ACS is commonly managed by an open fasciotomy. However, for chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS), minimally invasive fasciotomy techniques are practiced. Endoscopic approaches have shown favorable results and low recurrence of CECS; however, they are not widely practiced in the treatment of forearm ACS [11]. Compartment release of forearm ACS can be done through volar or combined volar and dorsal compartment incisions to decompress the compartments to lower the pressure [12]. In the current literature, there is no clear consensus on the best incision approach to decompress both volar and dorsal components and minimize morbidity [10].

While compartment release is typically achieved through a volar or combined volar and dorsal approach, trans‐ulnar single incision fasciotomy is another possible approach that has shown efficacy in the treatment of ACS. In one case study, a patient developed forearm compartment syndrome following traumatic diaphyseal radius and ulna fractures. At the time of initial presentation, the radius was successfully reduced utilizing closed reduction while the ulna remained displaced. When the patient was presented to the hospital 2 days later, the patient was diagnosed with forearm ACS. Depressurization of the superficial volar, deep volar, and dorsal compartments was accomplished by a trans‐ulnar incision between the extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) and flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU) during open reduction of the ulnar fracture. Both fasciotomy and fixation of the ulna were accomplished through a single, simpler trans‐ulnar incision [13].

Egg white has previously been used in cadaveric fasciotomy studies to simulate compartment syndrome as the viscosity of egg white is similar to blood. Other studies have utilized 0.9% saline to simulate compartment syndrome in forearms [14]. However, in this study, egg white was chosen due to its viscosity allowing diffusion between the fasciae to be minimized. The minimization of diffusion allows for a constant pressure to be maintained [15].

The objectives of this study are: (i) simulate ACS in cadaveric forearms and (ii) evaluate the efficacy of a trans‐ulnar single incision fasciotomy to decompress the deep volar and dorsal forearm compartments in cadaveric forearms with simulated ACS. The success of this technique could result in decreased time for the operation, decreased morbidity, and an increased ability for physicians to perform a fasciotomy as a bedside procedure under local anesthetics [16].

Methods

Cadaveric Preparation

As research involved de‐identified cadaveric tissue and no personal health information was utilized, IRB approval was deemed not to be required. Ten fresh, frozen cadaveric upper extremities were used for this study [14, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Cadavers were frozen at −17°C within 48 h post‐mortem. Each cadaver was subject to a single freeze–thaw cycle, which involved 48 h thawing at 4°C followed by 2–4 h at room temperature [22].

Simulation of Compartment Syndrome

Two tourniquets were applied on each limb, one above the elbow and one at the wrist [14]. An 8‐French feeding tube was placed into the forearm posterior to the tendon of the Flexor Digitorum Profundus (FPD) to reach the muscle. A graduated syringe was used to inject egg white, as a blood surrogate, through the catheter into the forearm until a palpable tension of the forearm was felt. The volume of egg white injected was recorded [15].

Measurement of Compartment Pressures

After egg white injection, volar and dorsal compartment pressures were measured using a calibrated STIC pressure monitor (C2Dx, United States of America). The pressure of the volar compartment was measured by supinating the forearm and identifying the palmaris longus (PL) tendon, then inserting the pressure sensor needle ulnar to the PL tendon at the junction of the proximal and middle thirds of the forearm and advancing the needle perpendicularly, directing it towards the posterior border of the ulna to a depth of 1 to 2 cm. The pressure of the dorsal compartment was measured by pronating the forearm and palpating the posterior aspect of the ulna at the junction of the proximal and middle thirds of the forearm, then inserting the pressure sensor needle 1–2 cm laterally (radial) to the posterior aspect of the ulna and advancing the needle perpendicularly to a depth of 1 to 2 cm [15, 23].

Simulation of Local Anesthetic

As this approach may be performed under local anesthetic, 30–40 mL of saline was injected along the planned incision line to simulate the volume that a lidocaine injection would add to the forearm compartments. Pressures of the volar and dorsal compartments were recorded after the saline injection.

Fasciotomy Technique

A single, linear incision was made, starting 4 to 5 cm (2″) proximal to the ulnar styloid process between the Flexor Carpi Ulnaris (FCU) and the Extensor Carpi Ulnaris (ECU) muscles and ending 6 to 8 cm (3″) distal to the olecranon process of the ulna, depending on the specimen's forearm length. The fascia was opened along the length of the incision. The FCU and ECU were retracted on both sides to completely expose the ulna (see Figures 1 and 2) and the fascia surrounding the Flexor Digitorum Profundus (FDP) and Extensor Retinaculum was completely released by scissors (see Figure 3) [9, 13]. By finger dissection, the superficial and deep volar compartments and the dorsal compartment of the forearm were released completely over the entire length of the incision. The interosseous membrane was not released.

Outcome Measures

Pressures of the deep volar and dorsal compartments were recorded in mmHg at one and 30 min after decompression, using the same method [14]. Pressures below 30 mmHg in both the deep volar and dorsal compartments were used as the threshold for successful decompression of compartment syndrome. The mean and standard deviation for the volar and dorsal compartment measurements were calculated [15].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed. Due to the nonnormality of the data and a sample size of less than 15, the Kruskal‐Wallis Test was applied for comparison. The significance level was set at p < 0.05 and was employed for all tests run. A Kruskal‐Wallis Test was run for the four different volar and dorsal pressures taken, once after the egg white injection, after the saline mimicking lidocaine was injected, 1 min after the fasciotomy incision was performed, and 30 min post fasciotomy incision. Descriptive statistics were run for the fasciotomy time, volume of saline, and volume of egg white, and results were shown in each for mean and standard deviation.

Results

Preoperative Outcomes

The mean volume of egg white injected to simulate ACS was 175.5 ± 16.9 mL; the mean volume of saline injected to simulate local anesthetic injection was 38.0 ± 4.2 mL.

Operative Outcomes

The mean fasciotomy time was 10.1 ± 3.1 min. The pressure measurements for deep volar and dorsal compartments can be visualized in box and whisker plots in Figure 4. The mean compartment pressures ± standard deviation after the egg white injection were 107.6 mmHg ± 37.5 mmHg for deep volar and 72.6 mmHg ± 26.5 mmHg for dorsal compartment pressures. Both pressures exceeded the 30 mmHg threshold, which is used clinically to diagnose ACS. These pressure readings indicate that the egg white injection successfully induced high intracompartmental pressure similar to ACS.

Box and whisker plot of compartment pressures for all timepoints for the deep volar forearm compartment.

After the saline was injected to mimic the anesthetic lidocaine, mean pressures rose to 124.4 mmHg ± 55.6 mmHg and 91.2 mmHg ± 59.3 mmHg for deep volar and dorsal compartments, respectively.

Mean compartment readings 1‐min post‐fasciotomy were 7.1 mmHg ± 3.0 mmHg for deep volar and 9.4 ± 5.6 mmHg for dorsal forearm compartments. Finally, mean compartment readings 30‐min post‐fasciotomy were 4.5 ± 1.2 mmHg for deep volar and 3.9 ± 2.0 mmHg for dorsal forearm compartments (Figure 5).

The volar compartment had sample medians of 102, 131, 7, and 5 mmHg for the four time points that the pressure was measured. The two lowest average ranks are the two post‐fasciotomy pressures. These two post‐fasciotomy pressures are lower than the overall average rank for all observations. Discussion

Trans‐Ulnar Single Incision

Compartment syndrome is a medical emergency caused by increased compartmental pressure that can lead to decreased blood flow to the muscle, ischemia, and possibly necrosis. Delay in diagnosis and treatment can lead to severe consequences. Complications increase with increased time to fasciotomy [24]. Forearm ACS is commonly managed by an open two‐incision fasciotomy, where one incision is made along the volar aspect of the forearm and another along the dorsal aspect to release the intracompartmental pressure. However, this method has the disadvantage of requiring two incisions, which requires more time to perform and is more invasive. The double‐incision fasciotomy also requires more expertise, as the incisions are made over important neurovascular structures in the forearm. This study aimed to evaluate the use of a trans‐ulnar single‐incision fasciotomy as a treatment method for forearm ACS, which could provide a timelier and more efficient alternative to the current standard of care.

Simulation of Compartment Syndrome

In this study, forearm ACS was successfully simulated in cadaveric forearms through injection of egg whites. Egg white has a similar viscosity to blood; thus, it can be used as a substitute to mimic compartment syndrome [15]. The diagnosis of ACS was confirmed by pressure measurements of both dorsal and deep volar compartments. Pressures above 30 mmHg in both the deep volar and dorsal compartments were used as the threshold for successful simulation of compartment syndrome.

The setup for the fasciotomies mimicked a standard operating theater with use of sterile surgical instruments, surgical drapes and gowning, and a room with necessary equipment for the orthopedic surgeon to perform the fasciotomies on the cadavers. Saline was injected at the site of incision to simulate the use of lidocaine, which could increase the compartment pressure.

Post‐Saline Injection Compartment Pressures

The mean for both the deep volar and dorsal compartments was greater after the saline injection than before the saline injection. However, there were three upper extremities that had a lower pressure after the injection of saline. For instance, the deep volar compartment pressure in specimen 4 decreased from 77 to 44 mmHg after the injection of saline. This variability did not appear to be related to the amount of saline injected as upper extremities with the upper limit of 40 mL of saline and the lower limit of 30 mL of saline had a decrease in pressure. A small amount of fluid could have leaked from the catheter utilized to inject the egg white into the upper extremity, but the amount of egg white or saline would likely not have been significant enough to account for the negative pressure difference in some of the upper extremities prior to and after the saline injection. The variability was likely caused by redistribution of fluids between the deep volar, dorsal, and mobile wad compartments. One possible explanation for this redistribution could be a change in viscosity. Saline is a less viscous liquid than egg white [25, 26]. Additionally, most liquids become less viscous at higher temperatures [27]. As the room temperature saline was added to egg white that had recently been refrigerated, the combined viscosity was lower, potentially allowing redistribution within each compartment or even some fluid movement out of the compartment, reducing the compartment pressure. In all 20 measurements after the saline injection, the relevant compartment remained greater than 30 mmHg.

Bedside Fasciotomy

Local anesthetics are successfully used in many upper extremity surgeries. Some common surgeries that utilize wide awake local anesthesia include carpal tunnel release, trigger finger, arthrodesis, metacarpal fractures, and Dupuytren's contracture surgeries [28]. Surgeries for which local anesthesia is used are expanding as it can reduce the surgery turnover time and cost; it can also be a better option for patients with medical comorbidities that would require extensive surgical clearance [29].

In another study, bedside fasciotomies were performed in selective cases for upper extremity and lower extremity ACS. As an alternative to general or regional anesthesia, lidocaine without epinephrine was injected along the incision line with conscious sedation consisting of midazolam and fentanyl. Infection rates were not found to increase in a bedside fasciotomy as opposed to a fasciotomy performed in the operating room [16]. Local anesthesia allows patients to receive a fasciotomy quickly. This speed is especially beneficial for patients who have a delay in presentation for evaluation or who may face a delay due to instances such as polytrauma requiring nonoperative resuscitation [16].

Using local anesthesia in this study allowed the fasciotomy to be performed quickly. As the mean fasciotomy time in this study was just above 10 min, it is obvious that it could be performed under local anesthesia. Additionally, the operating time for this approach is shorter because only one incision is required, and there are fewer significant neurovascular structures that need to be identified and avoided.

Comparative Efficacy of the Trans‐Ulnar Approach

The most important aspect of any fasciotomy incision technique is if the decompression itself is successful. In this study, the trans‐ulnar single incision approach successfully decompressed the cadaveric volar and dorsal forearm compartments in every instance. A previous cadaver study utilized egg white to evaluate how many incisions were required to decompress the forearm anterior compartment [15]. In this previous study by Benamran and Masquelet, after the egg white injection, the mean deep volar compartment pressure was 62 mmHg; after the second incision, the mean pressure was 5 mmHg; and after the third incision, the mean pressure was 3 mmHg [15]. This illustrates that the previous technique of decompressing the deep volar compartment showed similar results to the novel technique of utilizing a trans‐ulnar single incision for decompression.

Technical Considerations and Safety Profile

However, if multiple surgical techniques can successfully be used for the release of ACS, other factors should be evaluated. The trans‐ulnar single incision is simpler as it is only one incision and runs over the ulna, where there is little chance of damaging important neurovascular structures. The incision was made between the Flexor Carpi Ulnaris and the Extensor Carpi Ulnaris muscles over the ulna. In proximity to the incision site was the median neurovasculature, ulnar neurovasculature, and the posterior interosseous neurovasculature. The risk of injury to these structures is extremely low. The median and ulnar neurovasculature are out of the neurovascular plane as they are volar to the Flexor Digitorum Profundus, and the posterior interosseous neurovasculature is radial to the Extensor Carpi Ulnaris. The anatomic locations of these structures relative to the incision site provide protection from injury, as seen in the cross‐section of the forearm depicted in Figure 3. In operations in a similar location, the risk of nerve palsy in locking plate compression and intramedullary nail placement on the ulna was low [30]. Any volar incision is closer to the median nerve, which puts the median nerve at risk, and any dorsal incision comes with a risk of injuring the posterior interosseous nerve.

As this single incision is less invasive and less complex of a procedure than previous fasciotomy techniques, it is likely that less experienced junior surgeons or even senior residents would be able to perform this procedure safely; which would allow for less delay between the patient presentation and fasciotomy procedure.

Limitations of Technique

Of note, this incision technique does have its limitations. It would not be applicable for decompression of ACS following a radial fracture or fractures of the radius and ulna simultaneously. It would also not be applicable in any situation where entering the radioulnar joint is required. This technique is more well‐suited for other causes of ACS such as an ulna fracture or soft tissue injury.

There is also a limitation in that the trans‐ulnar fasciotomy is not in proximity to vasculature, decreasing the effectiveness of the single incision in clot removal for hemorrhage induced ACS. In these clinical scenarios, repair, a staged dual‐incision approach remains superior for combined fasciotomy and hemorrhage control [7].

Time to Fasciotomy

In cases where the patient presents late, time is of the utmost importance. Time between diagnosis and operation is the most important factor of the extent of morbidity and mortality5. Oftentimes, surgery cannot be done immediately as the patient requires an experienced surgeon, an anesthesiologist, an operating team, and an operating room. Many times, operating rooms are at capacity, and suites are fully booked [31]. One study found that even at trauma centers, the time passed from diagnosis to fasciotomy procedure was 2 h, showing the difficulty of timely compression even in well‐equipped hospitals [32]. Another study found that the mean interval from presentation to fasciotomy for foot compartment syndrome was 5.8 h [33]. Reduced duration of compartment syndrome has been linked to a decreased risk of necrosis and tissue loss [6]. If the surgery can be done under local anesthetic, only the surgeon is required to manage the case. The current procedure can even be performed at the bedside. Therefore, waiting time will be minimal, and the surgery could be done closer to the time of presentation. This could improve patient outcomes as worse outcomes are linked to ACS that is not treated within 8 h [6].

In addition to less delay before fasciotomy, a simpler procedure performed in the Emergency Department or at bedside would allow for a cost reduction as there would be no need for an operating theater set‐up, anesthetist, or other auxiliary staff [16]. A standard double incision fasciotomy is performed with general anesthesia or a regional block, which always requires the services of an anesthesiologist [9]. One study compared the cost of local anesthesia and general anesthesia for surgical management of a single trigger finger. A1 pulley release for management of trigger finger performed in the operating room was found to be $3344.46 more than the release by local anesthesia in a procedure room. The same study also found no difference in complication rates [34].

Risk of Infection

Additionally, infection rates could be reduced as one incision is made rather than two; infection rates still may be higher than minimally invasive fasciotomy techniques. Infection rates have not been studied for a trans‐ulnar single incision fasciotomy as this technique has only been used in this cadaveric experiment and a previous case study [13]. However, in the treatment of ACS after a tibial plateau fracture, infection rates were studied; it was found patients treated with a single incision fasciotomy had a lower risk of infection than patients treated with a double incision fasciotomy. While the risk of infection is greater in dual‐incision fasciotomies, the risk is still present in single‐incision fasciotomies [35].

Study Limitations

One limitation of this study is that the fasciotomy was performed on cadaveric forearms. The physiology of a cadaveric forearm is different from that of a live patient. Cadaveric models lack the perfusion present in a live patient. These models also lack the dynamic interaction between the fascial tissue and arterial, venous, and capillary pressures and flows as well as lymphatic drainage. As compartment syndrome progresses, further tissue swelling or bleeding can occur, which will further increase intracompartmental pressure [4, 36]. In live patients, a local inflammatory response can occur with perfusion of the area, causing further tissue swelling [36]. Thus, the cadaveric model of compartment syndrome simulated with egg white is a simplification of the dynamic, progressing live patient model of acute compartment syndrome. However, care was taken to replicate forearm ACS as closely as possible utilizing egg whites and standard operating room equipment. Fresh, frozen cadaveric upper extremities were used to try to maintain soft tissue integrity [14, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

Clinical Relevance and Future Application

The next step of this research is to conduct clinical trials to assess the safety and efficacy of the trans‐ulnar single incision for decompression of forearm compartment syndrome in live patients. The value of applying this approach lies in its simplicity, minimal invasiveness, and reduced risk to critical neurovascular structures. However, potential challenges may arise with the use of this approach with live patients, including anatomical variations and ensuring complete compartmental release. In future studies, long‐term outcomes such as functional recovery, wound healing, and complication rates must be closely monitored. Patients with ACS caused by ulnar fractures should be included to clearly define appropriate indications for the trans‐ulnar technique, such as closed and open fractures. The successful result of this study holds promise that clinical trials could demonstrate successful decompression of the forearm with the single trans‐ulnar incision, which could warrant the use of this fasciotomy technique in clinical practice [15].

Conclusion

A single trans‐ulnar incision effectively decompresses the deep volar and dorsal compartments of cadaveric forearms with simulated ACS. This simpler procedure may be performed in minimal time by a junior surgeon under local anesthesia at bedside, which may provide an alternative approach for fasciotomy in certain clinical scenarios. Additional clinical studies are necessary to validate the results and significance of this research.
