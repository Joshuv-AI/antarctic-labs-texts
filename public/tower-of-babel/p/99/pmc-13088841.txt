The impact of mesenchymal stromal cells on the proliferation and functional maturation of liver organoids
Authors: Sang Luo, Chunyang Mao, Fang Wu, Xiaofen Huang, Wenjun Wu, Ting Huang, Dan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe health burden of end-stage liver disease continues to grow, and orthotopic liver transplantation remains the only curative treatment. Three-dimensional (3D) culture systems support the in vitro culture of primary liver cells, allowing them to recapitulate native tissue architecture. This technology holds considerable potential for regenerative medicine and disease modeling. However, the practical application of liver organoid (LO) technology is hindered by the inherent limitations of LOs derived from single-tissue sources.Key resultsIn this study, we integrated human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cells (hUC-MSCs) into single-source LOs and assessed their effects on organoid formation and function. During the expansion phase, co-culture with hUC-MSCs yielded a 2-fold increase in organoid number compared to LOs cultured alone, although no significant changes were observed in the expression of genes related to organoid lifespan. Liver-specific functions of LOs after 7 day incubated with differentiation medium were further investigated. Despite constructing organoids from mature hepatocytes, co-culture with MSCs promoted non-hepatocyte differentiation, as evidenced by the expression of the cholangiocyte organoid marker cytokeratin 19. Furthermore, LOs co-culture with hUC-MSCs demonstrated increased albumin and urea secretion. In contrast, glucose consumption and ammonia clearance rates showed no significant differences. Following in vivo transplantation, serum biochemical markers normalized in both transplanted and control groups. However, histological analysis revealed superior liver tissue repair in the transplanted group.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that MSCs positively enhance the proliferative capacity of LOs and confer limited functional benefits, and incorporation of MSCs may promote non-hepatic cell differentiation. This effect was particularly evident within the extracellular matrix components of the hydrogel culture system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00755-x.

Introduction

The liver is a vital metabolic organ involved in numerous physiological and pathological processes. Following acute liver injury, its intrinsic regenerative capacity can repair up to 70% of damaged tissue [1]. However, chronic inflammation and fibrosis severely compromise this regenerative ability, making orthotopic liver transplantation the sole therapeutic option for patients with severe liver failure [2, 3]. A major challenge is the scarcity of liver donors; nearly 20% of patients on waiting lists die due to a critical shortage of suitable organs [4]. This severe donor shortage underscores the urgent need to develop novel organ models for clinical therapy research [5].

Advances in regenerative medicine have established liver organoids (LOs) as a promising resource to alleviate the shortage of donor livers. LOs can serve as transplantation models for cell therapy or as building blocks for engineered liver tissue [6]. Typically, LOs are generated in vitro by culturing mouse or human bile duct-derived bipotential facultative progenitor cells [7–9] or primary hepatocytes [7, 8, 10–12] within animal-derived matrices such as Matrigel. However, both the yield and the functional maturity of LOs derived from single-tissue sources remain inadequate to meet clinical transplantation demands, which limits the translational potential of current LO research.

Recently, the use of stem cells to address acute liver failure (ALF) has become a pivotal research focus. Stem cells show promise for treating a range of diseases, including liver failure, liver fibrosis, graft-versus-host disease, and type 2 diabetes [13–15]. They are characterized by self-renewal capacity, high proliferative potential, and the ability to differentiate into multiple lineages. Their therapeutic effects are mediated through diverse mechanisms such as immunomodulation and tissue repair [16, 17]. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are widely employed in disease treatment research due to their diverse tissue sources, low immunogenicity, and absence of major ethical concerns [18]. Multiple MSC-derived products, including cytokines, conditioned media, and exosomes, have been shown to promote hepatocyte regeneration and exert anti-apoptotic effects [19]. However, it remains unclear whether co-culturing human umbilical cord-derived MSCs with LOs can enhance the efficiency of LO formation.

In this study, we generated composite LOs by co-culturing hepatocyte-derived organoids with human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (hUC-MSCs), which are known for their low immunogenicity and multi-lineage differentiation potential, to investigate their impact on LO functionality and longevity in vitro. Furthermore, we employed an animal-free hydrogel to construct the extracellular matrix, providing a stable and defined microenvironment for LO growth. Our aim was to determine whether the organoid-like structures formed by co-culturing MSCs with LOs represent a promising model for clinical research by systematically examining the effects of MSCs on LO growth across different extracellular matrix compositions.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement and experimental animals

Sixty male C57BL/6J mice (16–18 g, 6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Ningxia Medical University. Mice were housed under controlled conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, ambient temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. Individually ventilated cages were used, with standard bedding and enrichment materials (e.g., nesting material) provided. Food and water were available ad libitum. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center, Ningxia Medical University (Approval No. IACUC-2025280). The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the ARRIVE guidelines. All subjects completed the experimental protocol and were included in the analysis.

ALF animal model and treatment

Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): Control, ALF, ALF + LOs, and ALF+MSCs-LOs. Acute liver failure (ALF) was induced via intraperitoneal injection of carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄, Sigma, 2891116) at a dose of 4.0 mL/kg, diluted 1:1 in olive oil (Sigma, O1514), according to an established protocol [20].

Establishment of liver organoids

LOs were established according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the animal liver organoid culture medium (Expansion medium, EM; Accuroid®, #M201, Guangzhou, China). In brief, liver tissue was minced into approximately 1 mm³ pieces and washed three times with cold PBS. The minced tissue was then washed with 5 mL of sterile Hank’s balanced salt solution and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min. To the resulting pellet, 2 mL of collagenase (Sigma) and 400 µL of dispase II (Sigma) were added, followed by incubation in a shaking incubator (37 °C, 120 rpm) for about 40 min with gentle mixing. Digestion was terminated by adding 5 mL of sterile Hank’s balanced salt solution to the suspension. The mixture was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of sterile Hank’s balanced salt solution, filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer, and the filtrate was collected and centrifuged again at 1500 rpm for 3 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was collected and resuspended in complete animal liver organoid medium to obtain a cell suspension at a concentration of 1–20 × 10⁵ cells/mL. A total of 20,000 cells were thoroughly mixed with 50 µL of either Matrigel matrix (Corning® 354262, Glendale, AZ, USA) or hydrogel (HycopGel, COOP BIOTECH, Guangzhou, China), taking care to avoid bubble formation. The mixture was plated in a 24-well plate. Following gelation, 500 µL of expansion medium (EM) was added to each well. The EM was prepared in-house from a basal medium supplemented with N2, B27, GlutaMax, HEPES, R-spondin 1, Wnt3A, Noggin, N-acetylcysteine, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), gastrin, forskolin (FSK), and A83-01. Cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for up to 10 days, with medium changes every three days.

For liver organoid differentiation, single cells were seeded and cultured for 10 days in EM, after which the medium was replaced with differentiation medium (DM). The medium was replaced with Liver Organoid Differentiation Medium (ALODM; Accuroid®, M202, Guangzhou, China). This medium is composed of a basal medium supplemented with N2, B27, GlutaMax, HEPES, dexamethasone, DAPT, BMP7, HGF, FGF, FSK, and gastrin. Differentiation was carried out for 7 days prior to further analysis, with medium replenished every two days.

Co-culturing of liver organoids with hUC-MSCs

hUC-MSCs were obtained from Nuwacell Biotechnologies (RC02003, Nuwacell Biotechnologies Co., Ltd., Hefei, China). The cells were cultured following a protocol previously established in our laboratory [21, 22]. Briefly, hUC-MSCs were maintained in serum‑free human MSC culture medium supplemented with the recommended reagents (Nuwacell™ ncMission Basal Medium, Nuwacell Biotechnologies Co., Ltd., RP020101) at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

To evaluate the effect of mesenchymal stromal cell co‑culture on LO formation, hUC‑MSCs were combined with hepatocyte‑rich cells at a ratio of 1:5. Specifically, 4,000 hUC‑MSCs were mixed with 20,000 hepatocyte‑rich cells. LO formation efficiency was assessed on days 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 using established methods. To evaluate proliferation potential, LOs were subcultured at a 1:2 ratio every 10 days.

Identification of the gene expression profiling of liver organoids (RNA-seq)

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), including high-throughput sequencing and data analysis, was conducted by Wuhan Biobank (Wuhan, China). Samples for RNA-seq were collected from three groups of LOs cultured for 10 days in expansion medium: the LOs group, the LOs + hUC-MSCs (Matrigel) group, and the LOs + hUC-MSCs (Hydrogel) group(n = 2000/group). Total RNA was extracted, and its integrity and quality were assessed prior to library preparation. Library products corresponding to 200–500 bp fragments were enriched, quantified, and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, Inc.) using a PE150 configuration.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were identified using the edgeR package (version 3.40.2). A fold-change threshold of ≥ 2 was applied to define statistically significant differences in gene expression. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis for the identified DEGs were performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.7.1.3).

Quantitative real-time reverse-transcription PCR

2000 Organoids at passage 0 to passage 8 (expansion stage) were collected to analyze the expression of genes related to liver function and longevity. Quantitative real-time reverse-transcription PCR (qRT‑PCR) was performed according to established protocols [21, 22]. Briefly, after RNA extraction, total RNA concentration was measured using an ND‑1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of purified total RNA. A 20 µL aliquot of the cDNA was then diluted to 100 µL, and 2 µL of this diluted solution was used as template in a 20 µL qRT‑PCR reaction system. The specific primers used are listed in Table 1. Glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method, with three independent replicates (n = 3) per condition.

Cell proliferation assay

Proliferation during the expansion phase was assessed by measuring adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels. ATP serves as a key energy molecule in living cells and reflects cellular metabolic activity. Upon cell lysis, ATP released from viable cells reacts with luciferase in the detection reagent, generating chemiluminescence that is proportional to the number of living cells in the organoid culture system. In this study, three organoids of uniform size were placed into each well of a 96-well plate, with a total cell number of 100,000 cells per well. An equal volume of pre‑warmed ATP detection reagent (BioGenous, E238003, China) was added to replace the organoid growth medium. Plates were then agitated vigorously for 5 min using an ELISA plate shaker (Feyond‑A300, Hangzhou Allsheng Instruments Co., Ltd.) to ensure complete lysis. After incubating at room temperature for 20 min, chemiluminescence was measured at 560 nm.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to characterize hepatocytic and cholangiocytic marker expression in LOs. The following groups were analyzed after 17 days of in vitro culture: LOs alone, LOs co‑cultured with hUC‑MSCs in Matrigel, and LOs co‑cultured with hUC‑MSCs in hydrogel. 3000–5000 Organoids from each passage were gently harvested, washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate‑buffered saline (DPBS; Invitrogen), and fixed in ice‑cold 3.7% formalin (Sigma‑Aldrich) for 20 min.

After fixation, samples were washed three times with DPBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma‑Aldrich), 0.1% Triton X‑100 (Bio‑Rad), and 0.05% Tween‑20 (Sigma‑Aldrich) to permeabilize cells and reduce nonspecific binding. Organoids were then blocked in 3% BSA prepared in DPBS for 1 h at 37 °C.

Primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following antibodies were used: anti‑albumin (ALB; 16475‑1‑AP, Proteintech; 1:200), anti‑hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α; EPR16885‑99, Abcam; 1:200), anti‑cytokeratin 19 (CK19; ab52625, Abcam; 1:200), Anti-Ki67 antibody(Ki67; ab16667;1:200) and Endoglin/CD105 Polyclonal antibody (CD105; 10862-1-AP;1:200). After primary antibody incubation, organoids were washed three times with DPBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor™ 568–conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (A‑11011, Invitrogen; 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 1 µg/mL 4′,6‑diamidino‑2‑phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen) for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were washed thoroughly and mounted for imaging.

To validate antibody specificity, mouse liver tissue sections were used as positive controls for Ki67, ALB, HNF4α and CD105 staining, while cholangiocyte‑rich regions served as positive controls for CK19.

Physiological characterization assay

Approximately 3000–5000 organoids from each passage were fixed in ice-cold 3.7% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess organoid morphology, LO samples stained with H&E working solution (Solarbio, Cat. #G1120, Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd.). Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining was conducted to evaluate glycogen storage capacity. LO samples were stained with PAS working solution (Solarbio, #G1262, Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd.). Additionally, Matrix gel droplets containing 3–5 × 10⁶ cells were plated in 12-well plates (three droplets per well) and incubated in differentiation medium for 7 days as previously described. Fresh LOs were subjected to indocyanine green (ICG) clearance and acetylated low‑density lipoprotein (Ac‑LDL) uptake assays to assess liver‑specific functions. All staining procedures followed the protocol described by Ullah et al. [23]. For the ICG clearance assay, LOs were incubated with 1 mg/mL ICG solution (Abmole, M3577) for 4 h at 37 °C and then washed twice with DPBS. ICG uptake was examined under a light microscope. To evaluate ICG release, LOs were subsequently transferred to ICG‑free organoid growth medium and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h before microscopic examination.

For the Ac-LDL uptake assay, 20 µL of 0.5 mg/mL DiI‑Ac‑LDL (acetylated LDL labeled with 1,1′‑dioctadecyl‑3,3,3′,3′‑tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate; Yeasen Biotechnology) was added to 1 mL of complete medium to prepare the working solution. LOs (n = 30/group) were incubated with 2 mL of DiI‑Ac‑LDL working solution at 37 °C for 3 h. After incubation, samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Oil Red O working solution (Servicebio, G1015, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.) to visualize lipid content. Staining was performed in the dark for 1 h.

Analysis of secretory function in liver organoids

Matrix gel droplets containing 3–5 × 10⁶ cells were plated in 12-well plates (three droplets per well) and incubated in differentiation medium for 7 days as previously described. Supernatants from LOs were collected at each passage (P0-P8) and stored at -70 °C for subsequent analysis. Albumin secretion was quantified using a Mouse Albumin ELISA Kit (Proteintech, KE10117, Wuhan, China), and urea concentrations were measured with a Urea Assay Kit (Bioassay Systems, QuantiChrom™ Urea Assay Kit, DIUR-100, USA), both according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Glucose consumption was calculated by comparing glucose concentrations in fresh organoid growth medium with those in conditioned supernatants using a Glucose Assay Kit (Sigma‑Aldrich, MAK476, Sigma‑Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd.). All measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3) using a microplate reader (Feyond‑A300, Hangzhou Allsheng Instruments Co., Ltd.).

Rhodamine123 transport assay

Matrix gel droplets containing 3–5 × 10⁶ cells were grown in either Matrigel (Corning® #354262) or hydrogel, plated in 12‑well plates (three droplets per well), and incubated in differentiation medium for 7 days. Prior to the assay, LOs were pretreated for 30 min with organoid differentiation medium containing either 10 µM verapamil (Abmole, M3118) or DMSO as a control. Organoids were then gently removed from the matrices, resuspended in organoid differentiation medium containing 100 µM Rhodamine 123 (Rh123; Abmole, M11522), and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Fluorescence was measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 508 nm/530 nm using a Leica DMI8 live‑cell imaging system.

GLDH expression

To measure intracellular glutamic acid dehydrogenase (GLDH) levels, matrix gel droplets containing 3–5 × 10⁶ cells were similarly grown in either Matrigel or hydrogel, plated in 12‑well plates (three droplets per well), and cultured in differentiation medium for 7 days. Fresh differentiation medium was replaced 24 h before organoid lysis. LOs were lysed in Milli‑Q water, and GLDH concentration was quantified using a Micro Glutamic Acid Dehydrogenase Assay Kit (Solarbio, BC1465) with a Feyond‑A300 microplate reader (Hangzhou Allsheng Instruments Co., Ltd.). GLDH values were normalized to total protein concentration.

Analysis of cytochrome P450 activity

For analysis of cytochrome P450 activity, matrix gel droplets containing 3–5 × 10⁶ cells grown in either Matrigel (Corning® #354262) or hydrogel were plated in 12‑well plates (three droplets per well). Each LO group was treated with differentiation medium supplemented with either 10 µM 3‑methylcholanthrene (3‑MCT, Abmole, M49463) or 60 µM pyrazole (Abmole, M58316). Cultures were maintained for 72 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. After treatment, LOs were harvested for total RNA extraction, which was used for cDNA synthesis and subsequent qRT‑PCR analysis.

Transplantation of liver organoids onto the liver surface

Twenty‑five male C57BL/6J mice (16–18 g, 6–8 weeks old) were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol [24] (20 µL per 10 g body weight, M5358, AbMole). The surface of the left hepatic lobe was gently abraded using an ultrasonic homogenizer [25] (LUH150, Yamato Scientific Co., Ltd.) at minimum power. Hemostasis was achieved with Spongel (Astellas Pharma Inc.). A volume of 40 µL containing 1 × 10⁷ mature LOs was transplanted onto the abraded area and covered with the middle liver lobe. Serum was collected via orbital venous plexus sampling, and biochemical parameters (albumin, AST, ALT, GGT) were measured using a BS‑2000 M analyzer (Shenzhen Mindray Bio‑Medical Electronics Co., Ltd.). After sample collection, euthanasia was performed humanely by cervical dislocation without chemical agents.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 6.0.1). The Kaplan–Meier method was used to analyze mouse survival rates. Differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t‑test. For parametric data involving more than two groups, two‑way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was applied. For non‑parametric data with more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used.

Results

Generation of liver organoids co-culture with hUC-MSCs

We established LOs in co-culture with hUC-MSCs. Mouse liver parenchymal cells and hUC‑MSCs were combined at a 4:1 ratio and seeded in either Matrigel or hydrogel to observe LO expansion (Fig. 1a). The control group consisted of mouse liver‑derived organoids cultured alone in Matrigel. Light microscopy from day 0 to day 10 revealed that co‑culture with hUC‑MSCs resulted in LOs with larger diameters(457 ± 38 μm), and higher yields(45 ± 8) compared with LOs cultured alone, in both Matrigel and hydrogel systems. Morphologically, organoids grown in Matrigel were indistinguishable from those grown in hydrogel. In co‑culture, hepatocytes and MSCs self‑assembled into distinct structures: a central cluster of MSCs surrounded by an adjacent but separate epithelial cell population. LOs plated as a single cell type grew as mono-lineage organoids (epithelium-only compartment) and formed spheroids. Co‑cultured LOs maintained stable growth during later culture stages, whereas LOs cultured without hUC‑MSCs displayed central darkening and features of necrosis within cell aggregates (Fig. 1b–d, Figure S1).

We further analyzed the gene expression profiles of the LOs, LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Matrigel), and LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Hydrogel) groups during the expansion phase using RNA‑seq. Transcriptomic analysis showed that the LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Hydrogel) group significantly upregulated five genes compared with LOs alone: Gkn3, Muc6, and TFF2, which are involved in gastric mucus barrier stabilization; NPC1L1, a gene that regulates cholesterol absorption and is critical for cholesterol homeostasis; and Akp3, a regulator of folate synthesis. In contrast, the LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Matrigel) group upregulated only one gene relative to LOs alone: chloride channel accessory 4 (CLCA4a). Chloride channels play important roles in maintaining cellular osmotic pressure, acid‑base balance, and excitability. Calcium‑activated chloride channels, a subclass regulated by intracellular calcium concentration, have been implicated in various physiological and pathological processes. For example, in respiratory epithelial cells, they participate in mucus secretion and airway surface liquid regulation, and may contribute to the pathogenesis of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Compared with the LOs+hUC-MSCs (Hydrogel) group, the LOs+hUC-MSCs (Matrigel) group showed upregulation of CLCA4a and downregulation of Gkn2, NPC1L1, and Akp3 (Fig. 1e, Figure S2).

hUC-MSCs enhance liver organoids expansion in both matrigel and hydrogel

To further examine how hUC-MSCs influence the expansion capacity of LOs in vitro, we performed serial passaging at a 1:2 ratio every 10 days for nine passages. Using light microscopy, we monitored the morphology of LOs and LOs co‑cultured with hUC-MSCs (LOs + hUC‑MSCs) from passage 0 to passage 8 and analyzed the expression of genes related to cell proliferation and longevity. By passage 5 (day 50), LOs cultured alone showed markedly reduced organoid‑forming capacity, with minimal formation observed thereafter. In contrast, LOs co‑cultured with hUC‑MSCs in either hydrogel or Matrigel remained viable until passage 7 (day 70), although organoid size and yield were reduced (Fig. 2a and b; Figure S3a, b). Alamar Blue ATP assays confirmed that co‑culture with hUC‑MSCs significantly enhanced LO proliferation (Fig. 2c), consistent with Ki67 staining results (Fig. 2d).

We further analyzed the expression of the hepatocyte stem cell marker EPCAM and longevity‑associated genes, including telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), Mtor, and Cdkn1a, during long‑term culture (Fig. 2e). Before passage 5, EPCAM expression showed only transient upregulation at P3 and P4 in hUC-MSC-co-cultured LOs (hydrogel and Matrigel, respectively). TERT expression exhibited delayed induction followed by decline, with distinct transient peaks observed across substrates. Specifically, during passages 0–1, co-cultured LOs showed slightly lower TERT levels compared with LOs cultured alone. In co-cultured LOs (Hydrogel), TERT expression increased approximately 15-fold at passage 4 relative to LOs alone, then gradually declined to levels comparable to LOs. In co-cultured LOs (Matrigel), TERT expression rose about 5-fold at passage 2 and 10-fold at passage 4 compared with LOs alone, before converging with expression levels in LOs. Mtor expression was transiently upregulated (≥ 12.2-fold) at passage 4 in co-cultured LOs, while Cdkn1a (p21) showed a substantial increase (40–80-fold) only at passage 2 in co-cultured LOs versus LOs alone.

Liver organoids co-cultured with hUC-MSCs exhibit similarly hepatocyte-specific physiological characteristics to LOs alone

LOs can be induced to differentiate into functional hepatocyte‑like cells in vitro using specific differentiation medium. Before initiating differentiation, we first analyzed the expression of mature hepatocyte markers and function‑related genes in LOs maintained in expansion medium for 10 days. Similar to LOs derived from single‑tissue sources, co‑cultured LOs showed comparable expression levels of genes related to hepatic functions, including urea synthesis, bile acid production, lipid and cholesterol metabolism, glucose metabolism, and drug metabolism. As expected, markers of fully differentiated hepatocytes such as Cyp2b10 were not expressed (Fig. 3a). To further evaluate whether organoids co-cultured with MSCs enhance their differentiation capacity, we switched organoids cultured in both Matrigel and hydrogel from expansion medium to differentiation medium for an additional 7 days of differentiation (Figure S4, Figure S5a). The primary liver protein albumin (ALB), the key hepatic regulator HNF4α, cytokeratin 19, and CD105 were also expressed, but without statistically significant differences across groups (Fig. 3b; Figure S5b–d).

To determine whether hepatocyte‑like cells derived from hydrogel co‑cultures exhibited hepatocyte‑specific physiological functions, we assessed their lipid metabolism, glycogen storage, and detoxification capabilities. Glycogen content did not differ significantly with or without hUC‑MSC co‑culture (Fig. 3c, Figure S5e). Both the LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Matrigel) and LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Hydrogel) groups showed similar lipid uptake and indocyanine green (ICG) absorption/release capacities compared with LOs alone, as indicated by Oil Red O‑positive LDL areas and ICG uptake‑positive/negative areas (Fig. 3d, e). These results indicate that hUC‑MSCs have a limited effect on liver‑specific protein expression and do not substantially enhance physiological features related to lipid metabolism or detoxification, regardless of the culture matrix used.

Liver organoids co-cultured with hUC-MSCs in hydrogel differentiate into functional hepatocyte-like cells

An ideal organoid model should not only maintain robust proliferative capacity and exhibit morphological features of native tissue, but more importantly, retain its functional characteristics. The liver is a central metabolic organ that performs essential functions such as glucose, lipid, and drug metabolism. We measured albumin secretion, urea synthesis, and glucose consumption in culture supernatants from the LOs, LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Matrigel), and LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Hydrogel) groups across passages P0 to P8. Notably, LOs co‑cultured with hUC‑MSCs in either Matrigel or hydrogel showed a gradual decline in albumin secretion from P0 to P8 during in vitro culture, although secretion levels remained significantly higher than those of LOs cultured alone (Fig. 4a). These co‑cultured organoids also exhibited urea secretion and sustained glucose consumption, both enzymatic processes characteristic of mature hepatocytes. Urea secretion was consistently higher in co‑cultured organoids than in LOs alone throughout P0–P8, despite a progressive decrease over time (Fig. 4b). Glucose consumption did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 4c). To evaluate ammonium clearance, we assessed urea cycle activity. Ammonium reacts with α‑ketoglutarate to form L‑glutamate in a reaction catalyzed by L‑glutamate dehydrogenase (GLDH) [26]. Finally, we assessed the vectorial transmembrane transport capacity of the organoids. Both hydrogel‑ and Matrigel‑cultured organoids were exposed to rhodamine 123 (Rh123), a fluorescent compound actively secreted from the apical membrane of hepatocytes via the multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR1) transporter [27]. Organoids in both matrices accumulated fluorescence within their lumens, indicating functional transporter activity. To confirm that this accumulation was specifically mediated by MDR1, organoids were pretreated with the competitive inhibitor verapamil. This pretreatment led to cytoplasmic accumulation of Rh123, while no luminal fluorescence was observed, confirming MDR1‑dependent transport (Fig. 4e, Figure S6).

Enzymatic activities related to xenobiotic detoxification were assessed after treatment with the CYP1A2 inducer 3-methylcholanthrene (3-MCT) and the CYP2E1 inducer pyrazole (Fig. 4f). In LOs treated with 3-MCT or pyrazole alone, CYP1A2 and CYP2E1 expression levels did not differ significantly from those in untreated naive LOs. In contrast, LOs co-cultured with hUC-MSCs and then treated with either 3-MCT or pyrazole showed approximately 2.4-fold higher expression of CYP1A2 and CYP2E1 compared with co-cultured naive LOs.

Liver organoid transplantation improves liver function and survival

The therapeutic potential of LOs, hUC‑MSCs, and LOs co‑cultured with hUC‑MSCs (Matrigel) was evaluated in a CCl₄‑induced acute liver failure (ALF) mouse model. Six hours after CCl₄ administration, LOs or LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Matrigel) were transplanted; liver samples were collected for analysis 7 days post‑transplantation (Fig. 5a). Mice receiving transplants of LOs alone or hUC‑MSCs alone showed similar survival rates (61.8%, n = 5 for LOs; 62.3%, n = 5 for hUC‑MSCs) compared with the ALF control group (CCl₄‑7d, 48.2%, n = 5), whereas mice transplanted with LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Matrigel) exhibited a survival rate of 78.9% (n = 5) (Fig. 5b). Biochemical markers of liver injury, including AST and ALT levels, were significantly lower in the transplanted groups than in the CCl₄‑7d group (Fig. 5c). Histological analysis of the left and median liver lobes near the transplantation site revealed reduced fibrosis in transplanted mice, as indicated by a smaller Masson‑positive area compared with the ALF group (Fig. 5d-f, Figure S7). These results indicate that transplantation of LOs + hUC‑MSCs (Matrigel) promotes the regeneration of liver tissue structures, such as bile ducts and vasculature. Furthermore, transplantation improved liver function, attenuated fibrosis, and increased survival in this liver failure model.

Discussion

The proliferative capacity and longevity of organoids are critical for generating clinically relevant quantities of therapeutic‑grade tissue [28]. To enhance the expansion potential of LOs, we established a co‑culture system with MSCs in this study. This approach improved the formation efficiency of LOs during the proliferative phase and supported functional maturation during the differentiation phase.

Previous studies indicate that the proliferative capacity of LOs derived from adult animals declines after 2–3 months [29]. Our findings are consistent with these reports. During the expansion phase, co‑culture with MSCs significantly increased organoid formation efficiency, reflected by increases in organoid number (from 29 ± 7 to 45 ± 8) and diameter (from 156 ± 12 μm to 457 ± 38 μm). These morphological changes were supported by biological assays, including ATP‑based proliferation measurements and Ki67 analysis. This effect is likely attributable to soluble factors released by MSCs, such as cytokines and extracellular vesicles, which play pivotal roles in promoting hepatocyte proliferation and differentiation [30]. In addition, MSCs enhance cell‑cell adhesion and stimulate extracellular matrix synthesis, processes important for robust organoid assembly [31]. Further analysis of the hepatocyte stem cell marker EPCAM and longevity-associated genes, including TERT, Mtor, and Cdkn1a, revealed that their expression levels did not show sustained upregulation following MSCs co-culture.

The liver is primarily composed of two epithelial cell types: hepatocytes and cholangiocytes. Cholangiocytes can differentiate into hepatocytes following Wnt pathway activation combined with inhibition of TGF‑β and Notch signaling [32]. Therefore, after expansion, LOs require culture in specific differentiation media to acquire more mature hepatocyte functions. RNA-seq analysis of LOs following 10 days of expansion culture confirmed that, at the gene expression level, both LOs cultured alone and those co-cultured with MSCs retained the ability to express mature liver-specific functional genes, such as Alb, Serpina1, Trf, and Cyp2e1. However, the addition of MSCs did not significantly elevate the expression of these genes. We next subjected 10‑day‑expanded LOs to 7 days of differentiation culture and assessed their hepatocyte‑like physiological characteristics. Although LOs were generated from hepatocytes, the bile‑duct cell marker protein cytokeratin 19 was detected within the organoids. This likely results from hepatocyte plasticity, whereby hepatocytes can transdifferentiate into bile‑duct epithelial cells in response to microenvironmental signals [33]. Interestingly, co‑culture with MSCs appeared to enhance glycogen synthesis, ICG release, and LDL uptake capacity in LOs, although these functional attributes gradually declined with increasing passage number.

Key hepatocyte functions include serum protein synthesis, vectorial uptake and secretion of various compounds, and urea cycle activity [34]. Following differentiation of P0–P8 organoids, although co‑culture with MSCs elevated ALB and urea expression compared with LOs cultured alone, secretion levels progressively declined with passage number. This decrease may reflect a gradual loss of organoid stemness during serial passaging [35]. However, the introduction of MSCs did not significantly improve glucose consumption, urea cycle activity (as assessed by GLDH), or directed transmembrane transport in the organoids. Notably, regarding detoxification capacity against xenobiotics, co‑culture with MSCs enhanced the responsiveness of LOs to 3‑MCT and pyrazole. This enhancement was primarily characterized by approximately 2.4‑fold higher expression levels of CYP1A2 and CYP2E1 in hUC‑MSC‑co‑cultured LOs compared with untreated naive LOs. This effect may be related to a stress response mounted by MSCs themselves under 3‑MCT or pyrazole stimulation. Gene expression analysis of LOs and LO+hUC-MSCs (shown in Fig. 1e) indicated that hUC-MSCs induce expression of chloride channel accessory 4 (Clca4a). Overexpression of Clca4a or activation of its pathway has been reported to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy or immunotherapy. For example, CLCA4 has been proposed to act as a tumor suppressor in esophageal carcinoma (ESCA) and may represent a therapeutic target gene for ESCA treatment [36]. By investigating the localization of Clca4a in UC-MSCs, its potential role in promoting proliferation and maturation may be revealed.

In the in vivo repair experiments using LOs, we observed an interesting phenomenon: surviving ALF mice showed normalization of biochemical markers such as ALT and AST after 7 days, regardless of whether they received LOs transplantation. However, significant differences emerged in liver tissue pathology, including the extent of fibrosis, inflammatory infiltration, and angiogenesis. This suggests that although the ALF group displayed intrinsic physiological repair responses independent of hUC‑MSCs, these spontaneous mechanisms may be insufficient to effectively reverse parenchymal tissue damage. Such differences could substantially affect long‑term survival and quality of life in the mouse model.

This study also identified potential adverse effects associated with MSCs introduction. For example, RNA-seq data from proliferating LOs revealed a notable concern: the most strongly upregulated genes under MSCs-hydrogel co-culture conditions were Gkn3, Muc6, and TFF2, classical markers of gastric mucosal epithelium rather than liver-specific genes. NPC1L1 and Akp3 are also linked to intestinal or gastric epithelial function. Thus, rather than indicating enhanced hepatic differentiation, the transcriptomic profile suggests a shift toward a gastric-like epithelial lineage. The data are more consistent with MSCs-supported hepatocyte aggregates undergoing partial, possibly non-hepatic differentiation, alongside the known therapeutic effects of MSCs in vivo. These findings indicate that the necessity of introducing MSCs into LO cultures requires further critical evaluation such as examine whether the transplanted donor cells engrafted and whether hUC-MSCs were present on day 7 after transplantation.

In summary, this study demonstrates that introducing MSCs positively influences organoid formation during the expansion phase of LOs. Notably, even when an animal‑free hydrogel was used to replace Matrigel for co‑culturing MSCs with LOs, equivalent organoid proliferation efficiency and liver functional development were achieved compared with the Matrigel‑based system. This approach effectively addresses key limitations associated with Matrigel, including structural instability, batch‑to‑batch variability, and clinical incompatibility, thereby supporting progress toward scalable, clinical‑grade LOs production. Looking ahead, elucidating the mechanisms whether MSCs differentiate into hepatocytes or bile duct epithelial cells, or whether they remain undifferentiated and exist as niche cells surrounding the hepatic organoids will further advance the development of more robust and functionally mature LOs platforms.
