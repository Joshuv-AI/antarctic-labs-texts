Canada’s youth are part of the opioid crisis and need treatment
Authors: Shannon Charlebois, Shawn Kelly
Published: 2025

Despite investment in harm-reduction initiatives, safer supply, and treatment for adult patients, the opioid overdose crisis in Canada continues. More than 18 people in Canada still die every day from opioid toxicity, according to national statistics from April 2024 to March 2025.1 Hidden within this number lies an even more shocking crisis — many deaths from the illicit opioid supply occur in youth. In British Columbia, unregulated drug toxicity became the leading cause of death among 15- to 18-year-olds in 2021.2 The problem runs deeper than the toxic supply. Early-onset initiation and escalation of opioid use among youth is being overlooked. If this crisis is not properly addressed now, Canada’s health systems will play a part in perpetuating the opioid crisis for decades to come.

The 2023 McCreary Adolescent Health Survey of 38 488 youth in BC aged 12 to 19 years found that 14% reported having consumed substances other than alcohol and cannabis. A total of 1% had specifically used heroin, fentanyl, or other opioids (not including prescription medications used without a medical indication or prescribed to someone else), and 4% of youth who had ever gone to bed hungry had tried illicit opioids. The Ontario Student Drug Use and Health Survey found that the nonmedical use of prescription opioid pain relievers significantly increased from 12.7% in 2021 to 21.8% in 2023, with students in grades 7 to 9 more likely to report use than those in grades 10 to 12.3 Many young people who use drugs begin using opioids at age 10 to 12 years, as described in a 2022 commentary in which those with lived experience were coauthors.4 Early substance use dramatically increases the risk of future and lifelong substance use disorders.

Unsurprisingly, trying opioids is not where it ends for youth. In 2021, 9% of all opioid-related emergency department visits and 8% of deaths related to opioids in Ontario occurred among those aged 15 to 24 years.5 In 2023, the rate of emergency department visits for opioid toxicity among youth in Ontario was 67.7 per 100 000 people.5 Young people are predisposed to act impulsively and take risks, and lack knowledge that older groups can draw from. This means youth frequently experiment with substances, which for some will lead to addiction or death if not addressed.

Guidelines from the Canadian Paediatric Society and the American Society of Addiction Medicine map out a core of effective evidence-based treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD).6,7 For teens, the first-line treatments for moderate-to-severe OUD are medications for OUD and community-based psychosocial care. Pharmacologic treatment is done with opioid agonists (i.e., buprenorphine and methadone).7 In cases of nonfatal overdose in youth, the initiation of evidence-based pharmacotherapy should be prioritized.7 While this is ideally done in concert with psychotherapy, a patient’s refusal to engage with such treatment should not preclude or delay pharmacologic treatment of OUD.7

Psychosocial modalities with demonstrated efficacy in the pediatric population include family-based therapy, motivational enhancement therapy, and cognitive behavioural therapy. 7 Integrated care for co-occurring mental health disorders increases treatment retention and improves outcomes.8 However, psychosocial therapies are rarely provided by provincial health systems (unless as part of an inpatient service); private insurance, for those who have it, may offer limited coverage; and few families have the resources to pay out of pocket for them.

Despite their proven efficacy, medications to treat OUD are underprescribed for youth in Canada. In Ontario, the use of medications for youth has paradoxically decreased, even as rates of opioid-related deaths in the province have increased 369.2% from 2.6 to 12.2 per 100 000 population from 2013 to 2021.9 Medication prescriptions for OUD issued to youth were at their lowest in 2024.9 Clinicians’ unwillingness to prescribe to this age group has been cited as an explanatory factor.10 Treating youth with medications for OUD may, to some, seem like resignation or giving up, but such treatment keeps young people alive long enough to recover from their addiction. Any physician who sees youth in Canada can prescribe buprenorphine, but many lack the basic training to feel comfortable doing so. Efficient and funded continuing medical education must be available to foster competencies in general practitioners and nurse practitioners willing to offer adolescent-specific care for substance use disorder, including pharmacotherapy.

The 2024 American Society of Addiction Medicine Criteria seek to match the severity of an adolescent’s treatment needs for substance use disorder and concurrent care needs with the appropriate level of treatment.10 Treatment may therefore comprise outpatient care, intensive outpatient care, or partial hospitalization, depending on patient need. In Canada, the families of youth with early-stage OUD can rarely access evidence-based care delivered by skilled providers in community outpatient settings, where they are most needed. Substance use disorder programs for youth remain scarce, fragmented, and often privately funded.11 Wait-lists stretch for months. Psychosocial therapies are largely inaccessible. Youth consistently face difficulty accessing and remaining connected with opioid agonist therapy for reasons that include concurrent psychiatric diagnoses or structural factors, such as low socioeconomic status, rural and remote residence, and racism. The result: Families in crisis cobble together care from emergency departments, underresourced school counsellors, and private-pay therapy accessible to few.

This is unacceptable for a leading cause of pediatric death in Canada. Treatment for OUD in children and youth should be funded by Canada’s public health systems — including family therapy and medications for opioid and other substance use disorders — to remove out-of-pocket costs. Interventions that are effective, replicable, and cost-effective should be provided, and they can mostly be delivered in community settings. Health systems’ inaction and lack of investment to provide evidence-based addictions services for youth is inexcusable, as the opioid crisis involves this population. Physicians who treat youth need support to be able to care for their patients with OUD. Anything less represents complicity in the next decade of preventable deaths.
