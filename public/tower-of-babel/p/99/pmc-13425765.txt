Understanding child mental health care pathways in community-based services: a qualitative study in a French child and adolescent medico-social centre (CMPP)
Authors: Camille Augustin, Vera Ivanaj, Fabienne Ligier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChild and adolescent mental health is increasingly recognized as a major public health concern worldwide. However, access to timely and coordinated mental health care remains uneven, particularly for children experiencing psychosocial, developmental or learning difficulties. Fragmentation across health, social care and education systems often contributes to discontinuous care pathways, especially in resource-constrained and rural settings. While a substantial body of literature on child mental health services originates from high-income countries, the organization of services differs significantly across national systems. In France, Child and Adolescent Medico-Social Centres (Centres Médico-Psycho-Pédagogiques, CMPP) are publicly funded outpatient services operating at the interface of healthcare, disability support and education sectors. Despite their central role in organizing care pathways, little is known about how these pathways are shaped and sustained in everyday practice.MethodsThis qualitative study adopted an interpretive case study design within a French CMPP located in a predominantly rural territory. Data were collected as part of a collaborative action-research project and included 50 semi-structured interviews with families, CMPP professionals and territorial partners, as well as approximately 250 h of participant and non-participant observations. Data were analyzed using an abductive thematic approach supported by the qualitative coding software Taguette. Coding and interpretation were conducted collaboratively within an interdisciplinary research team combining perspectives from public health and management sciences. Regular analytic meetings were recorded and documented, enabling the research team to retrace the iterative development of codes and themes and to compare insights across interviews, observations and reflexive notes.ResultsTen main themes were identified, highlighting organizational, relational and territorial mechanisms shaping child mental health care pathways over time. Results show that pathways are rarely linear and are marked by delays, uncertainty and discontinuities, particularly in contexts of limited resources. Families play a central role in sustaining continuity of care through coordination, information sharing and engagement, often compensating for systemic fragmentation. Professional practices rely on situated adjustments and informal coordination to manage constraints, while territorial characteristics such as rurality influence access to specialized resources and intensify reliance on local networks.ConclusionsThis study provides an empirically grounded analysis of child mental health care pathways within a French medico-social service. The findings highlight how organizational constraints, territorial characteristics and intersectoral coordination practices shape care trajectories in practice. By examining pathways as lived and negotiated processes, the study contributes to health services research on care coordination and equity in child mental health systems, while highlighting the specific role of medico-social organizations such as CMPPs within the French context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14804-8.

Background

Child mental health as a public health concern

Child and adolescent mental health is increasingly recognized as a major public health challenge worldwide, with rising prevalence of anxiety, depression, behavioral disorders and other psychosocial difficulties among children and adolescents [26]. Despite growing awareness, many young people still experience delays in evaluation and timely access to mental health services due to workforce shortages, fragmented systems and other structural barriers, which can undermine long-term outcomes for wellbeing and development [25].

In addition to clinical symptoms, many children’s mental health difficulties intersect with psychosocial, developmental and educational challenges that affect participation in family, school and community life. Research suggests that children and youth with special mental health care needs frequently encounter discontinuities in service provision across multiple sectors, including healthcare, education and social support systems. These institutional divides contribute to fragmented care pathways and difficulties maintaining continuity over time [23].

Barriers and facilitators in child mental health care pathways

A substantial body of literature has examined barriers to access and engagement with child and adolescent mental health services. Most of this research originates from high-income countries, where health systems are structured differently but face similar challenges related to service capacity, coordination and equity of access.

A scoping review identified several recurring service-level barriers, including long waiting lists, shortages of specialized professionals, geographic inequalities and persistent stigma surrounding mental health care [1]. More recent systematic reviews confirm that barriers occur across the continuum of care, from early identification and referral to coordination, follow-up and transition between services [5].

While this international literature provides an important analytical framework, the organization of services varies considerably across national systems. Understanding how care pathways are shaped therefore requires attention to the institutional and territorial contexts in which services operate.

Child mental health care in France: ambulatory transformation and sectoral fragmentation

In France, child and adolescent mental health care has evolved in a context marked by a long-term shift from inpatient psychiatry toward outpatient and community-based care. This transformation reflects broader changes in mental health policies, with increasing emphasis on continuity of care and territorially coordinated service organization [11].

At the same time, care pathways remain embedded within a historically fragmented institutional landscape spanning the health, medico-social, disability and education sectors. These sectoral divisions can complicate coordination between services, generate discontinuities in care pathways and impose additional coordination requirements through the creation of new identities of professional coordinators [3].

Within this configuration, Child and Adolescent Medico-Social Centres (Centres Médico-Psycho-Pédagogiques, CMPP) play a key role in organizing care pathways at the local level. CMPPs are publicly funded outpatient services providing multidisciplinary assessment, therapeutic follow-up and coordination for children and adolescents presenting psychological, developmental or learning difficulties, with or without formally recognized disability status. Positioned at the interface of healthcare, education and medico-social services, they frequently act as frontline entry points and coordination hubs within the French system.

Study aim

Against this backdrop, the present study aims to investigate how child mental health care pathways are shaped within a French CMPP.

More specifically, the study seeks to:

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative interpretive case study design to examine how child mental health care pathways are shaped in practice within a local service organization. The study draws on data produced within a collaborative action-research project (COOPER-ACTION) conducted in partnership with a French CMPP.

While the broader project included participatory and transformative components, the present article focuses on its descriptive and analytical dimension, examining how care pathways are organized and experienced over time. This approach aligns with qualitative case study methodology aimed at analyzing contemporary phenomena in their real-life context, particularly when the boundaries between the phenomenon and its context are intertwined [27].

Study setting

The study was conducted in a French Child and Adolescent Medico-Social Centre (CMPP). CMPPs are part of the French medico-social sector and provide multidisciplinary outpatient assessment and therapeutic support for children and adolescents presenting psychological, developmental or learning difficulties.

At the national level, recent activity data indicate that approximately 475 CMPPs were operating in France in 2022, providing community-based services for children and adolescents across the territory [9]. These services play a key role within the French system of disability and child mental health care by offering early assessment, therapeutic follow-up and coordination with educational and social institutions.

In the Grand-Est region, 16 CMPP main sites have been identified, corresponding to 54 service locations when satellite structures are included [14]. In the department studied, the territorial configuration includes one main CMPP site and two satellite locations.

Public reports have also highlighted significant territorial disparities in access to child mental health services, particularly in rural areas where specialized resources may be scarce and coordination between services more challenging [4].

The CMPP involved in this study is located in a predominantly rural territory characterized by relatively low density of specialized child mental health services and longer travel distances between services. This setting provides an information-rich case for examining how care pathways are organized under both organizational constraints (such as waiting lists, workforce shortages and limited coordination time) and territorial constraints (including rurality and uneven distribution of specialized resources).

Overview of qualitative data

Interviews lasted between 45 and 120 min depending on participants’ roles and availability. Interview guides explored experiences of care pathways, including access to services, diagnostic processes, coordination practices, family involvement and perceived barriers and facilitators (e.g. Supplementary Material: interview guides).

In addition, approximately 250 h of participant and non-participant observations were conducted within the CMPP between September 2022 and September 2025. Observations included multidisciplinary meetings, interprofessional exchanges and coordination discussions with territorial partners.

Throughout the research process, the primary researcher maintained a reflexive field diary documenting contextual elements, emerging interpretations and reflections on the research process. These materials supported interpretation and contextualization of findings.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using an abductive thematic analysis approach. Abduction was understood as an iterative process through which empirical observations and theoretical insights were progressively articulated, allowing analytical categories to emerge through repeated engagement with the data rather than from predefined coding schemes [8].

All interview transcripts and observation notes were imported into Taguette, an open-source qualitative coding software used to organize the corpus, support coding and facilitate comparison of coded excerpts across data sources. The coding process was conducted collaboratively within an interdisciplinary research team combining perspectives from public health and management sciences. Initial open coding was performed to identify recurrent situations, practices, tensions and meanings associated with child mental health care pathways.

Coding and interpretation were discussed regularly during analytic meetings within the research team. These meetings were documented in order to retrace the iterative development of codes and themes over time. Using a shared coding environment in Taguette also enabled systematic comparison of interviews, observations and reflexive notes across the dataset.

For example, themes such as waiting times, diagnostic uncertainty and the role of families in coordinating care emerged through converging accounts from parents, professionals and territorial partners and were further informed by observations of multidisciplinary meetings and coordination discussions.

Through successive cycles of comparison and interpretation, codes were progressively grouped into broader analytical categories and ultimately consolidated into transversal themes describing the mechanisms shaping child mental health care pathways in practice [20, 27].

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical and data protection procedures were implemented in line with French regulatory requirements. The research protocol was reviewed within the institutional framework of the University of Lorraine and the collaborative research partnership with the participating CMPP.

All participants received written and oral information about the objectives of the study prior to participation. Written informed consent was obtained from all interview participants. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without consequences.

Particular attention was paid to confidentiality, given the relatively small territorial context of the study and the potential for inter-knowledge between actors involved in local child mental health services. All interview data were anonymized prior to analysis, and identifying information was removed from transcripts. Data management and storage were conducted under the supervision of the Data Protection Officer of the University of Lorraine.

Throughout the research process, the researcher maintained a reflexive stance regarding her position within the collaborative action-research project and the interpretation of the data.

Results

The analysis identified a set of interrelated barriers and facilitators shaping child mental health care pathways within the CMPP. These findings show that pathways are not solely determined by clinical needs, but are continuously shaped by organizational constraints, coordination practices, family involvement and the uneven mobilization of resources over time.

Results are presented thematically, drawing on accounts from families, professionals and territorial partners. Ten transversal themes were identified, reflecting key mechanisms shaping access to care, coordination and continuity of child mental health care pathways. Barriers and facilitators shaping child mental health care pathways in CMPP

Open access under capacity constraints

Although the CMPP is formally open to all requests, access to child mental health care is strongly shaped by capacity constraints and long waiting times. Families described delays ranging from several months to over a year between the initial request and the first clinical appointment, contributing to uncertainty and repeated attempts to access care.

From the professionals’ perspective, the CMPP operates within a longstanding philosophy of open access, grounded in a commitment to equality of care. All requests are systematically registered, and families are placed on a waiting list prior to an initial assessment. However, this principle of openness is increasingly challenged by the size of the active caseload, workforce shortages and limited consultation slots, resulting in significant tensions between demand and available resources.

In practice, professionals reported engaging in implicit prioritization to manage these constraints. Prioritization decisions were based on perceived urgency, clinical severity, family context or institutional criteria linked to specific referral pathways. These processes were rarely formalized and relied on professional judgement, contributing to heterogeneous access experiences across families.

As one administrative staff member explained:We accept all referrals, but at some point we know very well that we won’t be able to respond within acceptable timeframes. We are caught between the principle of equal access and the reality of what we experience on a daily basis. (Secretary, E4)

We accept all referrals, but at some point we know very well that we won’t be able to respond within acceptable timeframes. We are caught between the principle of equal access and the reality of what we experience on a daily basis. (Secretary, E4)

These findings indicate that access to care is shaped by a combination of formal openness and informal selection mechanisms, creating early points of fragility in child mental health care pathways.

Diagnostic assessment as a prolonged and uncertain process

Diagnostic assessment emerged as a central yet unstable phase of child mental health care pathways. Families and professionals described assessment processes unfolding over extended periods, often marked by uncertainty, repeated evaluations and limited access to specialized medical expertise.

From the families’ perspective, diagnostic assessment was frequently experienced as a long and fragmented process, characterized by successive consultations, referrals and tests without a clear timeline or definitive outcome. Several parents described situations in which diagnostic hypotheses were formulated, questioned or revised over time, contributing to confusion and emotional strain. The absence of a formal diagnosis, or the delay in reaching one, was perceived as a major obstacle to accessing appropriate educational or medico-social resources.

One parent explained:They started talking about autism very early, but then we were refused by the specialized center. We went to a private specialist, and the diagnosis was wrong. In the end, there is still no diagnosis at all. (Parent, U9)

They started talking about autism very early, but then we were refused by the specialized center. We went to a private specialist, and the diagnosis was wrong. In the end, there is still no diagnosis at all. (Parent, U9)

Professionals acknowledged these difficulties but framed them within the constraints of clinical practice and territorial resources. Diagnostic assessment was described as a process requiring time, observation and multidisciplinary input, particularly in complex situations. Limited availability of child psychiatrists and specialized services was identified as a major structural constraint, leading to reliance on partial assessments or to prolonged diagnostic uncertainty.

As one professional noted:After a few sessions, the case is discussed in synthesis, but sometimes the child is not ready. We adjust the orientation, but all this takes time and human resources. (Section head, E5)

After a few sessions, the case is discussed in synthesis, but sometimes the child is not ready. We adjust the orientation, but all this takes time and human resources. (Section head, E5)

In this context, diagnostic assessment functioned less as a discrete step leading to a clear outcome than as an ongoing process of interpretation and adjustment, shaping subsequent care decisions without always providing the clarity expected by families. Divergent perceptions between families and professionals regarding the meaning and purpose of assessment contributed to early tensions within care pathways.

Limited clarity and transparency along care pathways

A strong demand for clarity and transparency emerged across family accounts. Parents repeatedly expressed the need to better understand what was being done, why specific decisions were made, and how their child’s care pathway was expected to evolve over time.

Families reported difficulties accessing clear and understandable information regarding care objectives, professional roles and possible orientations. This lack of clarity contributed to feelings of passivity and loss of control, with some parents describing the impression of “undergoing” the pathway rather than actively participating in it. Uncertainty was particularly pronounced during assessment and reorientation phases, when decisions were perceived as insufficiently explained.

As one parent stated:We are not really told what is happening, or we don’t get all the information. (Parent, U1)

We are not really told what is happening, or we don’t get all the information. (Parent, U1)

Professionals recognized these communication challenges but highlighted structural and relational constraints. Limited time, evolving clinical situations and the need to protect the child’s psychological space were cited as factors complicating information sharing. Some professionals described deliberately limiting communication with external partners or delaying information transmission in order to preserve therapeutic relationships.

A professional explained:Sometimes I don’t attend school meetings because I need to protect the therapeutic space. The child might feel betrayed if they think everything they say is shared. (Psychologist, E6)

Sometimes I don’t attend school meetings because I need to protect the therapeutic space. The child might feel betrayed if they think everything they say is shared. (Psychologist, E6)

These differing logics resulted in asymmetrical information flows, with families often positioned as recipients of fragmented or partial explanations. The lack of shared understanding regarding pathway goals and decision-making processes further reinforced uncertainty and undermined families’ capacity to anticipate transitions or engage meaningfully in care planning.

Fragmented coordination across health, education and social care sectors

Coordination between the CMPP, educational settings and social or medico-social services emerged as a major challenge shaping child mental health care pathways. Across interviews and observations, coordination was described as uneven, fragile and highly dependent on interpersonal relationships, rather than on formalized mechanisms.

Families frequently reported having to act as intermediaries between institutions, transmitting information and documents across sectors. This fragmented coordination contributed to duplicated assessments, inconsistent recommendations and difficulties anticipating pathway transitions, particularly in school-related contexts.

One parent explained:I’m the one bringing all the reports to the school, explaining everything, without any professional from the CMPP present. (Parent, U7)

I’m the one bringing all the reports to the school, explaining everything, without any professional from the CMPP present. (Parent, U7)

Professionals similarly described constraints affecting intersectoral coordination. Uncertainty regarding confidentiality, differences in professional cultures and limited time dedicated to coordination were identified as key obstacles. Some professionals expressed tensions between maintaining therapeutic confidentiality and engaging in collective discussions with external partners.

As one psychologist noted:Either we stay fully in a therapeutic posture, or we work with partners, but it’s difficult to combine both without risking misunderstandings. (Psychologist, E2)

Either we stay fully in a therapeutic posture, or we work with partners, but it’s difficult to combine both without risking misunderstandings. (Psychologist, E2)

As a result, coordination often remained partial and discontinuous, increasing the risk of pathway fragmentation and shifting responsibility for coherence onto families.

School-related pressures shaping care pathways

School-related difficulties played a central role in shaping the timing, urgency and orientation of child mental health care pathways. In many cases, school concerns acted as the initial trigger for referral to the CMPP and strongly influenced subsequent care decisions.

Families described situations in which behavioral or learning difficulties became problematic primarily within the school environment, generating pressure to seek rapid solutions. This urgency was often experienced without clear explanations regarding the nature of the child’s difficulties or the objectives of care, contributing to parental distress.

One parent explained:At school it became a problem very quickly, and we were put in distress without really understanding what was happening, so we tried to find solutions by ourselves. (Parent, U4)

At school it became a problem very quickly, and we were put in distress without really understanding what was happening, so we tried to find solutions by ourselves. (Parent, U4)

Professionals acknowledged the structuring role of school norms and expectations, noting that educational timelines and performance requirements often shaped pathway priorities. School-related pressures sometimes accelerated referrals or assessments, while also increasing the risk of misalignment between educational demands and clinical or developmental considerations.

These dynamics positioned schools as powerful actors within care pathways, influencing access to services and shaping families’ experiences of urgency and constraint.

Families as central pathway coordinators

Families emerged as key actors in maintaining pathway continuity, assuming responsibilities that extended far beyond their formal role in care. Parents described engaging in extensive coordination work, including organizing appointments, relaying information between professionals, pursuing referrals and managing administrative procedures.

This coordination work was often described as time-consuming and emotionally demanding, particularly in the absence of a clearly identified institutional coordinator. Families perceived this role as necessary to prevent delays, missed opportunities or pathway breakdowns.

As one parent explained:If I don’t push things forward, nothing happens. (Parent, U2)

If I don’t push things forward, nothing happens. (Parent, U2)

Professionals recognized the central role played by families but highlighted their own limited capacity to provide additional coordination support due to workload constraints. As a result, responsibility for pathway coherence was frequently implicitly delegated to families, contributing to unequal pathway experiences depending on families’ resources and capacities.

Unequal mobilization of resources and compensatory strategies

In response to pathway fragmentation and delays, families and professionals mobilized a range of compensatory strategies to sustain care over time. These included recourse to private practitioners, seeking services outside the local territory and relying on personal or professional networks.

Families with sufficient financial, social or mobility resources were more able to pursue alternative options, such as private assessments or out-of-area consultations. While these strategies sometimes facilitated access to care or accelerated diagnostic processes, they also contributed to marked inequalities between pathways.

One parent noted:We paid a lot for private consultations, and just a few of them were reimbursed. (Parent, U7)

We paid a lot for private consultations, and just a few of them were reimbursed. (Parent, U7)

Professionals also described encouraging or relying on external resources when local options were unavailable, while recognizing that such strategies were not equally accessible to all families. As a result, pathway continuity increasingly depended on families’ capacity to compensate for system-level constraints.

Informal organizational practices sustaining care continuity

Beyond formal procedures, care pathways were sustained through a range of informal organizational practices developed by professionals to cope with structural constraints. These practices included ad hoc exchanges, informal consultations between colleagues and flexible adjustments to care arrangements.

Professionals described relying on everyday interactions, such as corridor discussions or informal phone calls, to share information and make timely decisions when formal coordination spaces were insufficient. These practices allowed teams to respond to urgent situations and maintain continuity despite limited institutional resources.

As one professional reported:When there isn’t time for formal meetings, we talk between sessions or during breaks to prioritize certain situations. (Psychometrician, E11)

When there isn’t time for formal meetings, we talk between sessions or during breaks to prioritize certain situations. (Psychometrician, E11)

While these informal practices contributed to pathway stability, they also remained largely invisible and depended heavily on individual engagement. Their effectiveness varied according to staff availability, experience and team stability, making continuity fragile in contexts of turnover or workload pressure.

Fragile transitions and pathway endings

Transitions and endings emerged as particularly vulnerable moments within child mental health care pathways. Families and professionals described limited anticipation and formalization of pathway endings, whether related to improvement, age thresholds or reorientation toward other services.

Families often experienced uncertainty regarding what would happen after care ended, especially when no clear relay was identified. Some parents described abrupt transitions, perceived as emotionally difficult and insufficiently prepared.

One professional reflected:Even after many years of practice, knowing when to stop care remains a difficult question. (Psychologist, E14)

Even after many years of practice, knowing when to stop care remains a difficult question. (Psychologist, E14)

Professionals highlighted tensions between institutional timelines and children’s developmental or emotional readiness, which sometimes constrained their capacity to support gradual transitions. As a result, pathway endings could generate anxiety and discontinuity, particularly in the absence of coordinated follow-up.

Local relational facilitators supporting pathway stability

Despite multiple constraints, certain local relational factors acted as facilitators of pathway stability. Trust-based relationships between families and professionals, as well as between professionals across institutions, supported communication, flexibility and mutual understanding.

When stable relationships were established, coordination improved and families reported feeling more supported and less isolated. Professionals emphasized the importance of knowing local actors and having trusted contacts to facilitate referrals and information sharing.

As one professional explained:When you know the people you’re working with, things move more smoothly. (Disability office representative (MDPH))

When you know the people you’re working with, things move more smoothly. (Disability office representative (MDPH))

However, these facilitators remained highly context-dependent and vulnerable to changes in personnel or organizational restructuring. Their effectiveness relied on continuity of relationships rather than formalized systems, limiting their generalizability across settings.

Discussion

This study examined how child mental health care pathways are shaped over time within a community-based outpatient service operating under strong organizational and territorial constraints. By analyzing care pathways as they are organized, experienced and sustained in practice, the findings highlight key mechanisms influencing access to care, continuity of support and pathway stability. Rather than identifying isolated dysfunctions, the results point to structural and relational dynamics that actively configure pathways and contribute to differentiated care experiences.

Child mental health care pathways as non-linear and constrained organizational processes

The findings confirm that child mental health care pathways cannot be understood as linear sequences of assessment, intervention and discharge. Instead, pathways emerge as non-linear organizational processes shaped by capacity constraints, professional scarcity and competing institutional logics. While services such as CMPPs operate under a principle of openness to all referrals, this commitment is continuously challenged by long waiting lists, workforce shortages and limited access to specialized expertise.

These results are consistent with international evidence showing that pathways to child and adolescent mental health services are frequently prolonged, fragmented and marked by repeated reorientations [18]. Diagnostic assessment, in particular, appears as a prolonged and uncertain phase, shaped as much by organizational constraints as by clinical complexity. Previous studies have similarly shown that diagnostic processes for children with neurodevelopmental or mental health difficulties often extend over time, without leading to clear diagnostic closure, especially in contexts of limited specialist availability [6, 7].

From a health services perspective, these findings underline that organizational constraints do not merely affect service performance but actively shape how pathways unfold and how care is experienced by families.

Fragmented coordination and limits to integrated care

A central contribution of this study lies in its analysis of coordination across health, education and social care sectors. Despite the involvement of multiple actors, coordination remains weakly formalized and highly dependent on interpersonal relationships rather than on stable organizational mechanisms. This fragmented coordination increases the risk of duplicated assessments, inconsistent orientations and poorly anticipated transitions.

These observations align with the broader literature on integrated care, which highlights persistent barriers to intersectoral collaboration, including professional boundaries, confidentiality concerns and limited time allocated to coordination activities [2, 10]. More recent evidence confirms that mental health service integration often relies on informal practices and relational work rather than on institutionalized coordination frameworks [22].

In this context, care continuity appears fragile and unevenly distributed, depending largely on local configurations and the strength of existing relationships between actors. This finding reinforces calls to move beyond normative models of integration and to better account for how coordination is actually achieved in practice [13].

These findings may also be interpreted through the lens of “common factors” research in mental health, which emphasizes the role of non-specific, relational and contextual elements, such as trust, continuity and social support, in shaping care outcomes [24]. While this perspective is typically applied at the level of therapeutic relationships, our results suggest that similar mechanisms operate at the level of care pathways and service organization.

In this study, coordination often relies on informal interactions, interpersonal trust and locally embedded relationships between families, professionals and community actors. These relational dynamics, although insufficient to compensate for structural fragmentation, nonetheless constitute essential resources that sustain continuity of care in practice. This perspective invites a shift from viewing informality solely as a limitation toward recognizing its role as a functional component of pathway organization in resource-constrained contexts.

Families as de facto coordinators and unequal pathway experiences

The results further show that families play a central role in sustaining pathway continuity, assuming responsibilities related to coordination, information transfer and administrative follow-up. This role is rarely formalized or explicitly supported, yet it proves essential to maintaining continuity in a fragmented system.

This finding echoes previous research on care coordination for children with complex needs, which shows that families often compensate for gaps in service organization rather than participating in fully integrated or family-centered models of care [15, 19], a pattern also reflected in recent evidence on mental health services for children with developmental disabilities, where gaps in access, follow-up support and family-targeted interventions remain substantial [12]. Importantly, the capacity to assume this coordinating role varies considerably across families, depending on social, economic and relational resources.

As a result, pathways are not only shaped by service availability but also by families’ ability to mobilize compensatory strategies, such as recourse to private providers or services outside the local territory. Similar patterns of unequal access and unmet need have been documented in other health systems, where reliance on individual resources contributes to differentiated care trajectories [17].

School-related pressures and the production of urgency

School-related concerns emerged as a major structuring factor in child mental health care pathways. Educational difficulties often act as the initial trigger for referral and exert strong pressure on services to respond quickly. These pressures shape the timing of assessments and influence care orientations, sometimes independently of clinical or developmental considerations.

Previous research has highlighted the central role of schools as gatekeepers to mental health services for children and adolescents [16]. The present findings add to this literature by showing how educational timelines and expectations can generate urgency, reinforce diagnostic uncertainty and contribute to families’ experiences of distress and loss of control over the pathway.

Implications for health services research and public health practice

Taken together, these findings suggest that improving child mental health care pathways requires more than increasing service capacity or developing new coordination tools. Attention must also be paid to the organizational conditions that shape how care pathways are maintained over time, including information flows, role distribution and reliance on informal practices.

From a health services research perspective, this study contributes to a growing body of work emphasizing the importance of analyzing care pathways as situated processes embedded in territorial and organizational contexts [2, 21]. Strengthening pathway continuity therefore implies reducing reliance on family-based compensation mechanisms, supporting more explicit coordination roles and addressing structural sources of fragmentation.

By focusing on pathways as they are lived and sustained in practice, this study provides insights relevant to policymakers and service managers seeking to improve equity, continuity and responsiveness in child mental health services, particularly in resource-constrained or underserved territories. It also adds to health services research on care pathways by showing how coordination is practically achieved, negotiated or hindered in everyday service work within a medico-social organization positioned at the intersection of healthcare, disability and education sectors.

Strengths and limitations

This study draws on a rich qualitative dataset combining interviews, field observations and prolonged engagement with the study setting over three years. The use of multiple data sources enabled a detailed understanding of how child mental health care pathways are shaped in practice across different institutional perspectives.

The interdisciplinary analysis conducted by researchers from public health and management sciences, supported by a shared coding environment in Taguette, also strengthened the interpretation of organizational, relational and care-related dynamics within the pathways.

Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. First, the study focuses on a single case study conducted within one CMPP located in a specific territorial context. The objective of the study was not statistical generalization but analytical understanding of mechanisms shaping care pathways in practice.

Second, although Taguette supported systematic coding and comparison of qualitative materials, interpretation remained dependent on qualitative judgement and iterative discussion within the research team.

Third, although the study captures the perspectives of families, professionals and territorial partners, it does not include direct interviews with children and adolescents themselves. Consequently, the analysis cannot fully capture how young service users experience care pathways, therapeutic relationships or transitions between services. Future research should therefore integrate children’s and adolescents’ perspectives to provide a more comprehensive understanding of mental health care pathways.

Finally, interviews were conducted in French and translated into English for publication. Although attention was taken to preserve the meaning of participants’ accounts, some nuances may have been lost in translation.

Conclusion and implications

This study provides an in-depth understanding of how child mental health care pathways are shaped over time within a community-based outpatient service operating under strong organizational and territorial constraints. By examining pathways as they are organized, experienced and sustained in practice, the findings highlight the non-linear, fragmented and resource-dependent nature of care trajectories.

The results show that access to care, continuity of support and pathway stability are not solely determined by service availability but are actively shaped by organizational trade-offs, weakly formalized coordination mechanisms and reliance on informal practices. Families emerge as central actors in maintaining pathway continuity, often compensating for system-level fragmentation through their own resources and efforts. This dynamic contributes to unequal pathway experiences and raises important questions for health service organization and equity.

From a health services and public health perspective, these findings underline the need to move beyond normative models of integrated care and to better account for how coordination is achieved, or fails, in everyday practice. Strengthening child mental health care pathways requires not only increasing service capacity but also clarifying roles, supporting explicit coordination functions and reducing reliance on informal or family-based compensation mechanisms.

While these findings resonate with broader international research documenting fragmentation and coordination challenges in child mental health services, they also highlight the specific role played by medico-social organizations such as CMPPs within the French system. Positioned at the intersection of healthcare, disability support and education sectors, these services play a critical role in structuring access to care and coordinating support for children and families.

By focusing on pathways as lived and maintained over time, this study contributes to a more context-sensitive understanding of child mental health service organization. It offers insights relevant to policymakers, service managers and practitioners seeking to improve continuity, equity and responsiveness of care, particularly in resource-constrained or underserved territories.

Future research could build on these findings by examining how organizational innovations, coordination roles or partnership-based approaches may transform care pathways and redistribute responsibilities among actors, including families, professionals and institutions. It should also incorporate the perspectives of children and adolescents themselves in order to better understand how care pathways are experienced by the primary recipients of services.
