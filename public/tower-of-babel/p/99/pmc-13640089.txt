FlhF interacts with TonB2 to defend against oxidative stress in Campylobacter jejuni
Authors: Xiaofei Li, Ying Zhang, Fangzhe Ren, Ozan Gundogdu, Jinlin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Campylobacter jejuni is the leading cause of bacterial foodborne diarrheal disease worldwide. Despite its microaerophilic nature, C. jejuni is ubiquitous in aerobic environments and must possess specific adaptation mechanisms against oxidative stress. Here, we identified a novel role for FlhF, a GTPase essential for proper flagellar assembly, in promoting resistance to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Comparative transcriptomic analysis under H2O2 stress revealed that deletion of flhF leads to significant downregulation of oxidative stress-related genes. FlhF directly interacts with TonB2, an iron transport-associated protein, via its B and N domains. Codeletion of flhF and tonB2 leads to increased sensitivity to H2O2, suggesting a synergistic interaction. Moreover, the FlhF-TonB2 interaction promotes H2O2 detoxification, potentially by modulating intracellular iron homeostasis and influencing redox processes. Together, these findings reveal a novel function of FlhF in the oxidative stress response of C. jejuni, offering new insights into flagella-associated defense mechanisms in this pathogen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05391-z.

Introduction

Campylobacter jejuni is one of the leading causes of diarrheal disease worldwide and is responsible for millions of cases of bacterial gastroenteritis each year [1–3]. As a microaerophilic bacterium, C. jejuni thrives in environments with oxygen concentrations ranging from 2 to 10% [4]. In their natural environment, whether in the gastrointestinal tracts of mammalian hosts or in the external environment during transmission, C. jejuni cells are likely faced with oxidative stress [5]. The key reactive oxygen species (ROS) include superoxide anions (O2⁻), H2O2, and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) [6–8]. These ROS exert potent antimicrobial effects and can damage intracellular components, including DNA, RNA, and proteins, ultimately leading to bacterial cell death [9]. To ensure their survival, C. jejuni has evolved specific adaptive mechanisms against oxidative stress.

The classical oxidative stress response in C. jejuni is mediated by a suite of canonical detoxification enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SodB), alkyl-hydroxy peroxidase (AhpC), catalase (KatA), ferritin (Cft), ferredoxin (FdxA), and the DNA-binding protein from starved cells (Dps) [4, 10–12]. Interestingly, C. jejuni appears to employ a variety of auxiliary strategies to cope with oxidative stress. For instance, TonB2, a component of the iron transport system, has also been implicated in oxidative stress tolerance, as its deletion leads to increased bacterial sensitivity to H2O2 [13]. Moreover, bacterial motility indirectly contributes to C. jejuni resistance to superoxide stress, as mutations of genes involved in flagellar biosynthesis and modification (e.g., motAB, flgR, flhB, and flgD) render C. jejuni highly sensitive to ROS [11, 13, 14]. However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

C. jejuni flagella are multifunctional organelles that enable the bacterium to avoid hostile environments [15]. FlhF is a key protein affecting flagella biosynthesis, and its deletion leads to a complete loss of motility and a nonflagellar phenotype [16–18]. FlhF is described as a putative GTP-binding protein containing B, N, and G domains, all of which are essential for its function [17, 19–21]. The G domain with GTPase activity mainly affects the location and number of flagella synthesized [22, 23]. The B and N domains may directly or indirectly cause flagellar abnormalities by affecting flagellar gene expression or GTPase activity [24]. However, the involvement of flhF in other cellular processes, such as stress response pathways, remains largely unclear.

Interestingly, this study revealed that deletion of flhF significantly reduced bacterial tolerance to H2O2, a previously unrecognized function of FlhF. To elucidate this novel role, we analyzed the effect of flhF deletion on the expression of oxidative stress-related genes via RNA-Seq. GST pull-down was further employed to identify oxidative stress-related proteins that interact with FlhF. Additionally, Fenton reaction assays and H2O2 quantification were conducted to explore the potential role of TonB2 in iron-associated oxidative stress responses and to assess the possible contribution of the FlhF-TonB2 interaction to oxidative stress tolerance in C. jejuni. Collectively, these findings expand our understanding of FlhF beyond motility and provide new insights into flagellum-associated responses to oxidative stress in this pathogen.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and plasmids

All strains and plasmids utilized in this study are described in Table S1. C. jejuni 81–176 and the ΔflhF mutant used in this study were laboratory stock strains. All C. jejuni strains were grown on Charcoal Cefoperazone Deoxycholate Agar (CCDA) (Oxoid, UK) under microaerobic conditions at 42 °C, whereas the E. coli strains were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth or on LB agar at 37 °C [24]. For molecular cloning and strain construction, the suicide vector pMD-20T (TaKaRa, China) was utilized. Triparental conjugation was facilitated via the helper plasmid pRK2013 (Biomedal, Seville, Spain), while the shuttle vector pUOA18 for C. jejuni was obtained from Qijing Zhang (Iowa State University, Ames, USA). Antibiotics were added at the following concentrations: kanamycin (50 µg/mL), ampicillin (100 µg/mL), and chloramphenicol (20 µg/mL).

H2O2 sensitivity determination

To determine the sensitivity of bacteria to H2O2, the C. jejuni strain was cultured in Mueller-Hinton (MH) broth (BD, United States) until the logarithmic growth period (5 h), and the OD600 was 0.5. Equal portions of bacteria were exposed to H2O2 at a final concentration of 1 mM for 30 min at 42 °C under microaerobic conditions [25]. After exposure, the culture was continuously diluted and placed on a CCDA plate for a viable count.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

C. jejuni 81–176 and the ΔflhF mutant strain were cultured in MH broth (BD, United States) to mid-log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.5) at 42 °C, followed by treatment with 1 mM H2O2 for 30 min. Total RNA was extracted via the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany), and cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of RNA via the RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, China), as previously described in our earlier study [26]. qRT-PCR was conducted on an ABI PRISM 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with SYBR Green chemistry (Roche). Gene expression was analyzed using the 2–ΔΔCT method, and the primer sequences are listed in Table S2, with glyA as the endogenous control.

RNA-Seq

RNA-Seq libraries were prepared from the cDNA of six bacterial samples, comprising three biological replicates each of C. jejuni 81–176 and its ΔflhF mutant strains treated with 1 mM H2O2. Total RNA quality was assessed as previously described [26], and only high-quality RNA (OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RIN ≥ 6.5) was used for library construction. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina, United States) and independently replicated by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China). Genes with a P value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. KEGG analysis grouped these significantly expressed genes into various functional groups [27].

Construction of C. jejuni mutants and complemented strains

Target gene knockouts were generated by amplifying gene flanking regions and a kanamycin resistance cassette (from the C. jejuni genome and pRY107) and then ligating them into pMD-20T via C115 one-step ligase to form a suicide plasmid. This plasmid was electroporated into competent C. jejuni cells, and transformants were selected on CCDA plates containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin [26].

The complemented strains were constructed via the shuttle vector pUOA18 as previously described [28]. The target gene was inserted downstream of the PmetK promoter and introduced into mutant strains via triparental mating, with E. coli DH5α (carrying the recombinant pUOA18 plasmid) as the donor and DH5α (pRK2013) as the helper. Mutant recipient cells were adjusted to OD600 = 1.0, and E. coli donor and helper strains were grown to OD600 = 1.2. The cells were mixed (donor: helper: recipient = 1:1:10), spotted on MH agar, and incubated overnight at 42 °C under microaerophilic conditions.

To construct truncated FlhF variants, B, N, and G domains were cloned and inserted into pUOA18 and introduced into the ΔflhF mutant via the same mating protocol. After mating, the cells were resuspended in MH broth and plated on CCDA media supplemented with polymyxin B (6.7 µg/mL), rifampicin (10 µg/mL), trimethoprim (5 µg/mL), and chloramphenicol (20 µg/mL). Colonies were screened after 3–5 days and confirmed by PCR. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Protein binding assays

Protein-protein interactions between FlhF and TonB2, CJJ81176_RS00275, or CJJ81176_RS04090 were assessed via GST and His pull-down assays. Recombinant proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) transformed with pET30a constructs encoding full-length FlhF or its domains (B, N, and G) (for His-tagged proteins), pGEX-6P-1 (for GST alone), or pGEX-6P-1 derivatives encoding GST-tagged TonB2 and the above candidate proteins. For the GST pull-down assay, 200 µL of clarified lysate containing GST-tagged bait proteins was loaded onto mini spin columns, followed by incubation with 300 µL of PBST (PBS + 1% Triton X-100) and 50 µL of anti-GST agarose beads (Thermo Scientific) at 4 °C for 3 h. After six washes with 500 µL of PBST, 200 µL of lysate containing His-FlhF was added, and the mixture was incubated for 6 h at 4 °C. The columns were then washed ~ 10 times with PBST, and the bound proteins were eluted with 60 µL of 10 mM glutathione. The eluted proteins were analyzed by Western blotting using the anti-His monoclonal antibody as the primary antibody. For His pull-down assay, His-FlhF was used as bait and immobilized on Ni²⁺-NTA agarose beads packed in mini spin columns. Then, 200 µL of clarified lysate containing GST-TonB2 was added to each column and incubated at 4 °C for 6 h with gentle rotation. After extensive washing with PBST to remove nonspecifically bound proteins, the bound proteins were eluted with 60 µL of elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 250 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, pH 7.4). Eluates were analyzed by Western blotting using an anti-GST monoclonal antibody as the primary antibody.

Fenton reaction assay and H2O2 quantification

Fe3+ enterobactin uptake was first performed as previously described with slight modifications [29]. Mid-log-phase C. jejuni wide-type, ΔtonB2 mutant, and complemented strains were cultured in iron-depleted MEMα medium (Invitrogen), harvested, washed with 10 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.4), and resuspended in uptake buffer to OD₆₀₀ = 0.6. The uptake buffer contained 5 g/L Na2HPO4, 5 g/L KH₂PO₄, 1.18 g/L NH4Cl, 0.089 g/L Na2SO4, 0.042 g/L MgCl2·6H2O, and 10 g/L casamino acids. The cells were equilibrated at 37 °C for 10 min before the uptake assay. A radiolabeled 55Fe3+-enterobactin complex was prepared by mixing enterobactin and 55FeCl3 as previously described, followed by purification and dilution [29]. The cells were incubated with 18 nM 55Fe3+-enterobactin at 37 °C, and 1 mL aliquots were collected at 12 min for scintillation counting. Preloaded cells were then washed, resuspended in fresh buffer, and treated with 1 mM H2O2 at 37 °C for 2 min to induce a transient Fenton reaction. In this reaction, Fe2+ reacts with H2O2 via the classical Fenton reaction as follows: Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + OH⁻ + •OH. The Fe3+ generated during the reaction may subsequently undergo hydrolysis with water, forming ferric hydroxide (Fe(OH)3) precipitates. The formation of a yellow Fe(OH)3 precipitate was used as a visible indicator of intracellular Fe2+-dependent Fenton reaction activity. The H2O2 content was further quantified via a H2O2 content assay kit (Sangon Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected by centrifugation, washed with PBS, and lysed on ice with the provided lysis buffer. After centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were incubated with the detection reagent at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance at 415 nm was measured via a microplate reader. H2O2 concentrations were determined on the basis of a standard curve generated from known H2O2 standards. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted at least three times, and the data were analyzed using Prism GraphPad software (version 6.01), and statistical significance was determined by Student’s t-test (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results

FlhF contributes to oxidative stress resistance in C. jejuni

To investigate the role of FlhF in oxidative stress defense, we examined cell viability of C. jejuni strains following exposure to H2O2 (Fig. 1). Following H2O2 exposure, the viability of the wild-type strain decreased approximately 81-fold compared with the untreated control. In contrast, the ΔflhF mutant exhibited a ~ 5,320-fold reduction in survival, demonstrating significantly increased sensitivity relative to the wild-type (P < 0.001). Complementation with flhF restored survival to wild-type levels. These results indicated that deletion of flhF markedly reduces the ability of C. jejuni to tolerate H2O2 stress, highlighting the crucial role of FlhF in oxidative stress resistance.

Deletion of flhF alters the expression of iron-associated genes involved in oxidative stress defense in C. jejuni

We next performed a transcriptomic comparison between wild-type and ΔflhF mutant strains following exposure to 1 mM H2O2 to examine transcriptional changes in the ΔflhF mutant under oxidative stress. A total of 176 genes were differentially expressed in the ΔflhF mutant compared to wild-type in the presence of oxidative stress induced by H2O2, including 50 upregulated and 126 downregulated genes (Fig. 2a, Table S3). Of these, 54.8% (n = 69) were highly downregulated (> 3-fold) and 45.2% (n = 57) were downregulated (2- to 3-fold) (Table S3). Within the subset of highly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), three genes associated with oxidative stress were identified: CJJ81176_RS00275, CJJ81176_RS07825 (tonB2), and CJJ81176_RS04090 (Table S4). These genes are predicted to encode proteins involved in iron homeostasis and redox regulation, with tonB2 specifically implicated in iron transport. All three genes exhibited significant downregulation in the ΔflhF mutant upon H2O2 exposure, as further confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 2b).

To further determine the roles of these genes in oxidative stress resistance, we generated corresponding deletion and complementation strains and assessed their survival following exposure to 1 mM H2O2. Compared with the wild-type strain, all three deletion mutants exhibited markedly increased sensitivity to H2O2, showing approximately 60- to 77-fold lower viability, whereas complementation restored viability to wild-type levels (Fig. 2c). These findings demonstrated that these genes contribute positively to resistance against H2O2, leading us to hypothesize that FlhF may cooperate synergistically with specific proteins among these targets to protect C. jejuni from oxidative stress.

FlhF interacts with TonB2 and contributes to oxidative stress responses in C. jejuni

To validate the interaction between FlhF and three oxidative stress-related proteins, GST pull-down assays were performed. GST-TonB2, GST-CJJ81176_RS00275, GST-CJJ81176_RS04090, and His-FlhF were cloned and expressed. The results demonstrated that His-FlhF could be pulled down by GST-TonB2 but not by the negative control containing only GST tag (Fig. 3a). No interaction was detected between FlhF and the other two proteins (Fig. S1), indicating that FlhF interacts specifically with TonB2. These results suggested a potential functional relationship between FlhF and TonB2 in the oxidative stress response of C. jejuni. To address this, a double mutant (ΔflhFΔtonB2) and corresponding complementation strains were constructed, and their viability was evaluated following exposure to 1 mM H2O2 (Fig. 3b). Indeed, the ΔflhFΔtonB2 double mutant showed a greater reduction in viability than either single mutant (ΔflhF or ΔtonB2), with an approximately 122-fold reduction relative to the wild-type strain (Fig. 3b). These findings supported the involvement of both FlhF and TonB2 in the oxidative stress response of C. jejuni and suggest that their functional interaction may contribute to bacterial survival under H2O2 stress.

To further confirm which region of FlhF is responsible for interaction with TonB2, we created and expressed three FlhF fragments corresponding to the B, N, and G domains, and assessed their interactions with TonB2 using GST pull-down assays. The results revealed that both the B and N domains of FlhF were capable of interacting with TonB2, whereas the G domain exhibited no detectable binding (Fig. 3c), suggesting that the B and N domains of FlhF mediate its interaction with TonB2. We next examined whether the B and N domains serve as the key regions through which FlhF confers H2O2 resistance. To this end, we generated a series of domain-specific complementation strains in the ΔflhF background, including single-domain (FlhFᴮ, FlhFᴺ, FlhFᴳ) and dual-domain (FlhFᴮᴺ, FlhFᴮᴳ, FlhFᴺᴳ) constructs (Fig. S2), and subjected them to 1 mM H2O2 stress to evaluate their survival. Interestingly, complementation with either the B (FlhFᴮ) or N (FlhFᴺ) domain alone significantly improved survival compared with the ΔflhF mutant by 29- or 21-fold, respectively (P < 0.001), although neither restored resistance to wild-type levels (Fig. 3d). In contrast, expression of the G domain (FlhFᴳ) had no detectable effect, consistent with its lack of interaction with TonB2. Consistent with this, dual-domain complementation further corroborated these findings. The FlhFᴮᴺ strain exhibited the most pronounced recovery, with survival up to 44-fold higher than that of the ΔflhF mutant (Fig. 3d). Collectively, these results suggested that the B and N domains of FlhF are important for both TonB2 interaction and oxidative stress resistance, supporting a functional association between the FlhF-TonB2 interaction and bacterial tolerance to H2O2.

FlhF and TonB2 contribute to iron-dependent H2O2 degradation in C. jejuni

Given its proposed role in iron transport, we next sought to determine whether TonB2 influences intracellular iron utilization under oxidative stress. Within 2 min, the wild-type cells produced a distinct yellow Fe(OH)3 precipitate, reflecting active intracellular Fe2+-driven Fenton chemistry (Fig. 4a). In contrast, the ΔtonB2 mutant exhibited markedly reduced precipitate formation, indicative of limited intracellular Fe2+ availability and diminished Fenton activity, while complementation of tonB2 restored precipitate formation to near wild-type levels. These results suggested that TonB2 facilitates Fe3+ uptake and intracellular reduction to Fe2+ under oxidative stress.

To further evaluate the relationship between TonB2-dependent iron utilization and oxidative stress, we quantified residual H2O2 levels using an H2O2 content assay. Consistent with the diminished Fenton activity, the ΔtonB2 mutant retained significantly higher H2O2 concentrations than the wild-type, showing a 2.2-fold increase (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4b). We next asked whether FlhF exerts its protective effect against H2O2 stress through TonB2-mediated regulation of iron homeostasis. Indeed, deletion of flhF also led to elevated H2O2 accumulation, with a 1.9-fold increase relative to the wild-type (P < 0.01), while the ΔflhFΔtonB2 double mutant exhibited the most pronounced accumulation (2.6-fold, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4b). Complementation of either gene restored H2O2 levels to those of the wild-type. Together, these findings supported the involvement of FlhF and TonB2 in iron-dependent oxidative stress responses in C. jejuni. The data further suggested that TonB2-mediated iron utilization may contribute, at least in part, to the ability of C. jejuni to cope with H2O2 stress.

Discussion

Our study reveals a previously unrecognized role for the flagellar biosynthesis protein FlhF in oxidative stress resistance in C. jejuni. We propose that FlhF interacts with the iron transporter TonB2 and may contribute to iron-dependent H2O2 detoxification in C. jejuni. (Fig. 5). This FlhF-TonB2 interaction represents a novel interface between motility-related signaling and redox balance in bacteria.

FlhF is critical for flagellar biosynthesis, and its deletion results in a nonmotile and nonflagellar phenotype [30, 31]. Previous studies have focused mainly on its role in flagellar assembly and localization [16, 32]. Here, we revealed a novel function of FlhF in oxidative stress resistance, expanding its biological importance beyond motility regulation. We found that deletion of flhF markedly increased bacterial sensitivity to H2O2, suggesting its involvement in the oxidative stress response. Although previous studies have reported that mutations in other flagellar genes (e.g., motAB, flgR, flhB, and flgD) increase susceptibility to ROS [11, 13, 14], the specific molecular mechanism involved remains unclear. Our findings reveal that FlhF directly interacts with the iron transport-associated protein TonB2 through its conserved B and N domains, suggesting a functional connection between flagellar-associated proteins and oxidative stress regulation.

FlhF is a multiple-domain (B-N-G) protein, with its G domain previously implicated in controlling flagellar number and spatial positioning [22, 23, 33]. However, the biological roles of the B and N domains remain poorly defined [24]. Here, we show that the B and N domains of FlhF are involved in its interaction with TonB2 but also partially restore H2O2 resistance in the ΔflhF background, indicating that these domains are critical for FlhF’s role in oxidative stress adaptation.

The TonB system enables energy-dependent transport of iron and other nutrients across the outer membrane in gram-negative bacteria [34, 35]. While most C. jejuni strains encode three TonB homologs (TonB1, TonB2, and TonB3), C. jejuni 81–176 strain possesses only TonB2 [29, 36]. Previous studies have shown that disruption of tonB2 significantly increases sensitivity to H2O2, but the underlying mechanism remains unclear [13]. Here, our results in strain 81–176 suggest that TonB2 is involved in intracellular iron-dependent oxidative processes associated with H2O2 stress. Notably, this functional contribution appears to differ from that reported in strain NCTC11168, where TonB2 plays a more limited role in ferric enterobactin-mediated iron uptake [29], suggesting strain-dependent variation in TonB2-associated iron utilization and oxidative stress responses. Loss of tonB2 impairs Fenton activity and leads to H2O2 accumulation, underscoring its critical role in iron-dependent oxidative detoxification. Intriguingly, the even greater H2O2 accumulation observed in the ΔflhFΔtonB2 double mutant supports a functional association between FlhF and TonB2 in oxidative stress resistance.

Although our data demonstrate a physical interaction between FlhF and TonB2, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the current results do not allow us to definitively distinguish the contribution of the direct FlhF-TonB2 interaction from the broader transcriptional regulatory effects of FlhF on oxidative stress-associated genes, including the possibility that FlhF may directly or indirectly regulate tonB2 transcription, as previously reported for certain flagellar genes [26]. Future studies will be required further to clarify the specific contribution of the FlhF-TonB2 interaction and to determine whether FlhF directly participates in tonB2 transcriptional regulation. Although domain-mapping analyses suggested that the B and N domains of FlhF are involved in TonB2 interaction and oxidative stress resistance, we cannot exclude the possibility that these domains also participate in additional FlhF-dependent functions. The precise roles of the B and N domains of FlhF in mediating this interaction and whether they modulate TonB2 activity require further elucidation. Furthermore, since the Fenton reaction generates cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH), additional antioxidant or iron-dependent detoxification systems are likely required to mitigate oxidative damage. Whether the FlhF-TonB2 axis influences other ROS-detoxifying enzymes or iron-sequestration proteins (e.g., Dps and bacterioferritin) warrants further investigation.

In summary, this study reveals a novel role of FlhF in oxidative stress resistance in C. jejuni and supports a functional association between FlhF and the iron transport-associated protein TonB2. Our findings provide new insight into how bacterial motility-associated proteins contribute to stress adaptation and highlight a potential connection between flagellar regulation, iron homeostasis, and oxidative stress defense.

Conclusion

Campylobacter jejuni, the leading cause of foodborne diarrheal disease, must overcome oxidative stress to persist in oxygen-rich environments and establish infection. Our study identifies a previously unrecognized role of the flagellar regulator FlhF in resistance to H2O2, which is associated with its interaction with the iron transport-associated protein TonB2. These findings link flagellar regulation with iron homeostasis and oxidative stress defense, providing new insight into how C. jejuni persists in hostile environments.
