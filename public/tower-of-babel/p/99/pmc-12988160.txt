Development and evaluation of a multistage transfer learning framework for robust medical image analysis
Authors: Gelan Ayana, So-yun Park, Kwangcheol Casey Jeong, Soon-Do Yoon, Se-woon Choe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical image analysis is essential for accurate disease diagnosis, yet progress in developing high-performing deep learning models for medical image analysis remains limited by the scarcity of large, high-quality annotated datasets. Conventional transfer learning (CTL) from natural image pretrained models offers partial benefits but frequently encounters domain mismatch, resulting in limited generalizability to medical imaging tasks. This study reports the development and evaluation of a multistage transfer learning (MSTL) framework designed to improve domain adaptation and enhance diagnosis performance. The MSTL framework introduces an intermediate pretraining stage using cell line microscopic images to provide a more relevant source domain between ImageNet pretraining and downstream medical imaging tasks. The workflow consists of sequential pretraining on ImageNet, fine-tuning on cell line images, and final adaptation to medical datasets, including mammograms, ultrasounds, and X-rays. The study assessed MSTL performance using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers (ViTs) and compared results against CTL and training from scratch. The findings show that ViTs consistently outperform CNNs, with ViTB-16 achieving the highest accuracy across all datasets. Additionally, transferability metrics, Log Expected Empirical Prediction, Negative Conditional Entropy, and H-Score, exhibited strong positive correlations with model accuracy, particularly for mammography and X-ray tasks with ViTB-16 exceeding Pearson correlation coefficients 0.95. Overall, the MSTL framework substantially narrowed the gap between general image pretraining and specialized medical imaging tasks. Introduction

Advanced healthcare procedures rely heavily on medical imaging to diagnose a wide range of diseases1. Traditionally, interpreting these images to identify patient conditions required medical personnel with specialized training2, emphasizing the need for expertise in image analysis to ensure diagnostic accuracy. With technological advancements, however, there has been a shift toward employing computational methods, particularly deep learning algorithms, to support disease diagnosis through medical imaging3. Deep learning algorithms have demonstrated significant outcomes in identifying patterns and features in medical images, thereby assisting in disease diagnosis4. Meanwhile, the effectiveness of these algorithms relies significantly on the availability of large high-quality datasets for training5. However, the acquisition of such datasets for each disease is challenging6.

Transfer learning has emerged as an effective approach to addressing the deficiency of large medical image datasets7. It involves leveraging the knowledge gained from models trained on one task/domain and applying it to another related task/domain8. Initially, pretrained models trained on general image datasets such as ImageNet, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and MS Coco were commonly used for transfer learning in medical image analysis9. However, these models have insufficient specialized knowledge required to capture the complex features present in medical images10.

In recent years, multistage transfer learning has garnered attention as an effective approach to improving transfer learning performance in the medical imaging domain11,12. This technique has emerged as a promising avenue for enhancing the efficacy of diagnostic and prognostic tools by leveraging the vast array of features available across various medical domains. By cascading multiple transfer-learning stages, this approach addresses the challenges of limited annotated data and domain shifts inherent in medical imaging datasets13. Multistage transfer learning introduces an intermediate transfer learning stage that adapts the weights of pretrained models from general image datasets to better suit the characteristics of medical images14. This intermediate stage aims to address the difference between the features learned from general images and specific features relevant to medical imaging tasks. At its core, multistage transfer learning performs a sequential transfer of knowledge from a source domain with an abundance of labeled data to a target domain where labeled data are few15. Thereby, it refines the model representations at each stage to better capture the complexities of the target task.

Multistage transfer learning presents a promising approach to addressing complex transfer scenarios, but the challenge of domain generalization remains a significant obstacle, limiting its full potential11,16. One major issue is domain misalignment, where discrepancies in data distributions between the source and target domains can lead to negative transfer, resulting in poor model performance in the target domain. This misalignment hampers the transfer of useful knowledge and can even degrade accuracy when the source domain’s features do not align with those of the target domain. Additionally, data heterogeneity, which encompasses variations in data structure, modality, and quality across domains, further complicates the transfer process. Differences in data types—such as transferring between images from different modalities or organs or high- and low-resolution data—can create obstacles that prevent the model from generalizing effectively. Another key concern is model overfitting, especially when fine-tuning is performed on limited target data. In such cases, the model may over-specialize to the target domain, losing its ability to perform well on unseen data or adapt to variations. These challenges underscore the need for ongoing research to develop more robust strategies for knowledge transfer and effective domain adaptation techniques.

In this study, a robust multistage transfer learning framework for medical images is proposed and the effectiveness of the proposed approach is evaluated on different modalities of medical images using transferability measures in addition to conventional machine learning metrics. The proposed framework utilized cancer cell line microscopic images as an intermediate transfer learning domain. By incorporating cell line microscopic images that share certain visual characteristics with medical images, the model adapts more effectively to the complexities of medical imaging tasks during the transfer learning process. Cancer cell line microscopic images exhibit characteristics similar to those of medical images. This enhances the learning process when applied to a target medical image dataset. This is because these images share more resemblance in terms of features than natural images. Moreover, the developed method effectively addresses the challenge of acquiring a large labeled dataset of medical images by utilizing a substantial number of cell line microscopic images. These images serve as an intermediate domain between the natural domain (ImageNet) and medical domain (mammography, ultrasound, etc.). Generating large quantities of cancer cell line images is more affordable than other medical imaging techniques such as mammography, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging. In addition, because cancer cell line images do not contain personal patient information, these are more accessible for research purposes.

By introducing multistage transfer learning method as a robust domain generalization framework designed to address (1) the difficulties in handling the domain shift problem and transferring knowledge from the natural image domain to the medical image domain and (2) the deficiency of large medical image datasets, this paper advances the field of transfer learning in medical image analysis overall. By leveraging cell line microscopic images as an intermediate domain, the proposed framework demonstrated enhanced performance in identifying diseases from medical images. This would ultimately contribute to improved healthcare diagnostics.

The main contributions of this work include:

1. Development of a robust MSTL framework: A robust framework that addresses the challenge of domain shifts and generalization between general image datasets (e.g., ImageNet) and medical imaging domains (Mammogram, ultrasound, X-ray, etc.) by incorporating an intermediate stage that adapts pretrained models to better capture the specific characteristics of medical images has been developed.

2. Introduction of cancer cell line microscopic images as an intermediate domain for MSTL on medical images: The proposed framework uses cancer cell line microscopic images as an intermediate transfer domain. These images share more visual characteristics with medical images than general natural image datasets, enabling a more effective adaptation process. By leveraging these images, the framework improves the transferability of models trained on natural image datasets, thus enhancing performance on medical imaging tasks.

3. Addressing data unavailability challenges: The paper provides a solution to the challenge of limited annotated medical image datasets by utilizing a large number of cancer cell line microscopic images. These images are more accessible, affordable, and do not contain personal patient information, making them ideal for use as an intermediate dataset between natural images and medical images. This approach reduces the dependency on large labeled medical image datasets for model training.

4. Comprehensive evaluation of the proposed framework: The effectiveness of the multistage transfer learning framework is evaluated using various modalities of medical images. In addition to conventional machine learning metrics, the paper employed transferability measures to assess how well knowledge is transferred across domains, ensuring that the framework’s performance is rigorously tested across diverse medical imaging tasks. This comprehensive evaluation solidifies the framework’s potential for improving disease diagnosis using medical images.

Related works

The shift toward computational methods, particularly deep learning algorithms, for disease diagnosis in medical imaging has motivated significant research efforts aimed at overcoming the challenges posed by limited labeled data17–24. Transfer learning has emerged as a viable strategy to mitigate data scarcity by leveraging pretrained models from general image datasets. However, the inadequacy of these models in capturing the complexities of medical images has motivated the introduction of multistage transfer learning approaches. Samala et al.25 introduced a multistage transfer learning framework that incorporates an intermediate transfer learning stage. They adapted pretrained models to the specifics of medical imaging tasks. Their method demonstrated an improved performance in disease identification tasks by addressing the difference between general and medical image features. Similarly, Yi et al.26 proposed a multistage transfer learning approach adapted to medical image analysis utilizing an intermediate domain to enhance the model’s adaptability to medical datasets. These studies emphasized the efficacy of multistage transfer learning in addressing the challenges of domain shifts and data insufficiency in medical imaging.

Although multistage transfer learning can potentially enhance diagnostic tools in medical imaging, challenges such as domain misalignment and model overfitting persist. To mitigate these challenges, Maaliw et al.27 proposed a domain-adaptation framework specifically adapted for multistage transfer learning in medical images. Through the multistage transfer learning approach, they have established an effective strategy to improve medical image analysis by learning from both natural and domain-specific datasets using microscopic images. Similarly, Chugh et al.28 developed a three-stage transfer learning to adapt model from natural image domain to medical image domain. In recent years, efforts to enhance performance of transfer learning-based rare disease diagnosis have gained traction. Vidal et al.29 and Meng et al.30 proposed multistage transfer learning framework for rare medical image analysis such as COVID-19 cases. These studies highlight the ongoing endeavors to refine multistage transfer learning methodologies for rare cases and address diagnosis challenges in clinical settings.

To expand the applicability of multistage transfer learning to diverse medical imaging modalities, recent studies have evaluated cross-modal domain adaptation techniques. For example, works by Ayana et al.13–15 proposed a cross-modal multistage transfer learning framework for medical images. They leveraged medical image resembling microscopic images as intermediate transfer learning stages to adapt to medical image tasks. Recently, dual transfer learning31,32 and feature level fusion33,34 were proposed to improve the performance of transfer learning for medical images.

It is unequivocal that multistage transfer learning has established itself as a versatile methodology for medical image analysis, with potential applications across various healthcare domains. By facilitating knowledge transfer from related domains, this approach effectively addresses the challenges inherent in specific medical imaging tasks, thereby contributing to advancements in diagnostics, treatment planning, and overall healthcare outcomes. Utilizing diverse related datasets, multistage transfer learning models demonstrate robustness and generalizability within the medical domain. Their applications encompass disease diagnosis, classification, segmentation, detection, registration, and a wide array of medical imaging tasks essential for understanding and managing diverse health conditions. Methodology

Datasets

Cancer cell line image dataset

A cancer cell line image dataset was generated by the authors14. The image acquisition process involved culturing cancer cell lines for seven days and taking daily bright-field images of them using an inverted fluorescent microscope. Three distinct types of human cancer cells were selected for the experiments: cervical cancer (HeLa), breast cancer (MCF-7), and lung cancer (NCI-H1299). These cell lines were sourced from the Korean Cell Line Bank. To ensure optimal growth, the cells were cultivated under precise conditions in a specialized medium. Throughout a 7-day cell culture period, the cell lines were imaged daily. A total of 608 cell line images were collected; among them are 212 images of NCI-H1299, 149 images of MCF-7, and 247 images of HeLa. From each cell line images, 6800 patches were created, amounting to a total of 20, 400 images before augmentation. The size of the created patch is proportional to the number of cells and their size. Uninformative cells and debris that floated (with a width and height of less than 100 pixels) in the selected area by a bounding box were not included. The patch images created through this approach were saved as independent images and utilized as deep learning datasets after preprocessing.

Medical image datasets

This study utilized three medical imaging modalities to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method: mammography (Digital Database for Screening Mammography (DDSM)49 dataset (available at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/ywsbh3ndr8/2; last accessed on June 20, 2025)), ultrasound (Breast Ultrasound Image (BUSI)50 dataset (available at https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/aryashah2k/breast-ultrasound-images-dataset; last accessed on June 20, 2025)), and X-ray (Chest X-ray Images (Pneumonia) (CXRIP)51 dataset (available at https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/paultimothymooney/chest-xray-pneumonia/data; last accessed on June 20, 2025)).

Domain similarity analysis

To quantitatively assess the suitability of cancer cell line images as an intermediate domain, we conducted a domain similarity analysis using cosine similarity based on deep feature representations, as in52. For each domain, we computed the mean feature vector by averaging the extracted embeddings across all samples. We then calculated pairwise cosine similarity between the mean feature vector of the cancer cell line domain (intermediate) and each target domain (mammography, X-ray, ultrasound), as well as between ImageNet and each target domain. Higher cosine similarity values indicate stronger alignment in the feature space between two domains, which suggests reduced domain shift when transferring learned representations. By comparing the cosine similarity between cancer cell line representations and each target domain with that of ImageNet, we determine whether the intermediate domain reduces feature-space discrepancy. This analysis evaluates whether cancer cell line images lie closer in feature space to each target medical imaging modality (mammography, X-ray, and ultrasound) compared to the generic ImageNet domain, thus providing evidence for reduced domain mismatch and improved transferability. We employed two widely used neural architectures pretrained on ImageNet, EfficientNetB2 (CNN) and ViTB-16 (ViT). For each domain (cancer cell line images, mammography, X-ray, and ultrasound), we extracted deep features from the respective model backbones. For EfficientNetB2, we used global average pooled features from the final convolutional layer. For ViTB-16, we used the class token representation as the domain embedding.

Table 2 presents the cosine similarity values computed for both EfficientNetB2 and ViTB-16 representations. In all cases, cancer cell line features exhibit higher similarity to target medical imaging domains than ImageNet, with the mammography being the most similar across both models. This ordering supports the assertion that cancer cell line images provide a more aligned intermediate domain, thereby reducing negative transfer.

The images come in different sizes, thus, resizing the images was performed to fit into our models. Images were downsized from their original pixel size to a standard size of 224 × 224 pixels. Overview of the proposed approach

This study developed a multistage transfer learning approach that uses cancer cell line microscopic images as an intermediate transfer learning stage to improve medical image classification performance. The proposed approach is designed to address the challenges associated with transferring knowledge directly from natural image datasets, such as ImageNet, to medical image datasets, where differences in image characteristics often limit performance. By introducing an intermediate transfer learning stage, the proposed method enables a gradual adaptation of pretrained models toward medical image-related features before final training on target medical imaging modalities. Figure 3 depicts the general process of the multistage transfer learning developed in this study.

The schematic of the multistage transfer learning approach developed in this study.

The multistage transfer learning process follows a sequential three-stage procedure. In the first stage, deep learning models pretrained on ImageNet are used as the initial source models. These models have already learned general visual representations from a large-scale natural image dataset and are capable of capturing basic image features such as edges, contours, textures, and shapes. At this stage, the pretrained models are not trained further on medical images; instead, they provide a starting point for subsequent transfer learning stages. This initial pretraining stage establishes a common baseline for all models used in the experiments.

In the second stage, the ImageNet-pretrained models are further trained on cancer cell line microscopic images. This stage serves as the intermediate transfer learning step. To perform this adaptation, the original classification layers of the ImageNet-pretrained models are removed and replaced with new classification layers corresponding to the cancer cell line image classes. The entire network, including both feature extraction layers and newly added classification layers, is then fine-tuned using the cancer cell line image dataset. Through this process, the pretrained models adapt their weights to image features that are more closely related to medical images, such as cellular structures, local intensity variations, and fine-grained patterns observed in microscopic images. This intermediate training stage allows the models to move away from purely natural image representations and toward representations that are more relevant to medical image analysis.

After completing training on cancer cell line images, the resulting models are used as pretrained models for the final transfer learning stage. In the third stage, the classification layers trained on cancer cell line images are removed and replaced with new classification layers suitable for the target medical image datasets. Separate models are trained for each medical imaging modality, including mammography, ultrasonography, and X-ray radiography. During this stage, all model parameters are fine-tuned using the corresponding medical image datasets. This final fine-tuning step enables the models to learn modality-specific features while benefiting from the knowledge acquired during both the ImageNet pretraining and the intermediate cancer cell line training stages.

Throughout the multistage transfer learning process, consistent training procedures are applied to ensure fair comparison across models and modalities. The same preprocessing steps, input image size, and optimization settings are maintained across stages, with only the classification layers and training data changing at each stage. Regularization techniques, including dropout and weight regularization, are used during training to reduce overfitting, particularly when adapting the models to smaller medical image datasets. The staged training process ensures that the models progressively adapt their parameters rather than undergoing abrupt changes caused by direct transfer from natural images to medical images.

The selection of cancer cell line microscopic images as the intermediate domain for the MSTL framework was based on several important criteria. First, there is a critical need for a large dataset that is relevant to medical images to facilitate effective transfer learning. Medical image datasets are often small and insufficient for direct transfer from general datasets like ImageNet, so cancer cell line images, which offer a large volume of medically related data, help bridge this gap. Second, these microscopic images are available many of the in vitro research laboratories, making them easily accessible for use as an intermediate dataset. Their availability allows for effective pretraining before fine-tuning on actual medical images. Third, cancer cell line images bear significant visual and structural resemblance to medical images, particularly in cellular textures and patterns, which enhances the model’s ability to adapt more effectively compared to using datasets that lack these similarities. Fourth, generating cancer cell line images is far easier and quicker than obtaining medical images, as cell cultures can be grown and imaged in large quantities within controlled laboratory settings without the ethical or logistical constraints associated with patient data. Finally, cancer cell line image generation is highly cost-effective, requiring fewer resources compared to expensive medical imaging techniques such as mammography. This makes it a financially viable option for creating large datasets that improve domain adaptation while minimizing costs. Together, these factors make cancer cell line microscopic images an ideal choice for the intermediate domain in MSTL, enhancing the framework’s overall effectiveness and scalability.

The proposed MSTL framework improves domain adaptation and generalization compared to conventional transfer learning by introducing an intermediate stage that uses cancer cell line microscopic images, which share more visual and structural similarities with medical images than general image datasets like ImageNet. This intermediate step allows for progressive feature refinement, helping the model better capture the complex patterns found in medical images. Model architectures

Multistage transfer learning leverages pretrained weights obtained from a specific dataset to initiate training on a different dataset. By adopting this approach, knowledge acquired from previous tasks can be utilized to enhance the performance of the target task. Majority of previous studies were based on CNN architectures. Vision transformers have recently gained significant recognition in the field of computer vision. These surpass CNNs in tasks involving the classification of natural images. ViTs have demonstrated capabilities exceeding those of even the most advanced CNN models for the natural image domain because of their capacity to extract global features from images in the early layers and overcome the limitations of CNNs, which primarily focus on a limited portion of an image54. Therefore, in this study, we utilized both CNN and ViT-originated neural networks to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed MSTL approach.

Convolutional neural network

A preliminary study was conducted on six commonly used pretrained CNN models for classifying medical images, namely, AlexNet, VGG19, MobileNet, InceptionV3, EfficientNetb2, and ResNet5055. Then, the study selected three models for transfer learning on cancer cell lines. These pretrained models were originally trained on ImageNet. In the first stage of transfer learning, the final layer was removed, global average pooling was added, and a dense layer with SoftMax was included to fine-tune all the weights from the pretrained models. The cancer cell line images were categorized into 90% training and 10% test sets and five-fold cross validation was used to evaluate performance. In the second stage of transfer learning, the dense layer was removed and replaced with three dense layers. One of these included a dropout. Additionally, the SoftMax layer was updated for the specific target. This yielded the final CNN architecture shown in Fig. 4. RTX 3090 GPUs were used for the experiments. The models underwent 50 epochs with the Adam optimizer at each transfer learning stage after analyzing the results of 20–150 epochs. The learning rate was initially 0.0001 during training and reduced gradually by a factor of 0.96. This remained consistent across transfer learning stages.

Vision transformer

This study designed a multistage transfer learning method using three ViT models: ViTB-16, ViTB-32, and ViTL-3256. The first two were base models with input patch sizes of 16 × 16 and 32 × 32, respectively. The third model was a large model with a patch size of 32 × 32 pixel. To implement multistage transfer learning using ViT, at both intermediate and task levels, the final layer of each ViT model was replaced with a series of layers including a flattening layer, batch normalization, a dense layer, further batch normalization, and a final dense layer that functioned as a classifier13. Figure 5 illustrates this process. The models underwent 50 epochs of training using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001. The training process involved a batch size of 16 and exponential decay. To activate the ViT models, this study used GELU with an L2 regularizer. Meanwhile, ReLu with an L2 regularizer was used for the CNNs. To prevent bias in the comparison experiments, the parameter settings in the CNN-based method were maintained.

Evaluation metrics

Conventional machine learning metrics

The model performance was assessed using statistical and quantitative machine learning performance indicators. Five-fold cross-validation was used to measure the performance. The performance measures were calculated using a 95% confidence interval. The accuracy was used to evaluate the performance.

Transferability measures

Transferability in the context of transfer learning model evaluation refers to the capability of a pretrained model to adapt and generalize effectively to a new related task or domain by utilizing knowledge gained from its training on a different but related task or domain. Transferability measures the effectiveness of transferring knowledge from a pretrained model on a source task to a target task. It quantifies how effectively the transferred knowledge improves the performance on the target task.

Training the target task with the source feature representation is a common method of transferring information from the source task to the target task . If the model architectures on the first layers of the trained source and target models are identical, this concept can be applied to a neural network setup by copying the parameters from the first to the second. Subsequently, the parameters in the replicated layers are frozen, and the target classifier is trained. The transferability of this model can be formulated as in57. In this study, we selected three transferability measures popularly used in the literature to measure transferability: Log Expected Empirical Prediction (LEEP), H-score, and Negative Conditional Entropy (NCE). These metrics allow a more meaningful assessment of a model’s transferability by examining both the representational and predictive alignment between domains, thereby providing a balanced view of knowledge transfer effectiveness. Each measure addresses specific transfer learning dimensions—distributional compatibility (LEEP), feature similarity (H-score), and uncertainty reduction (NCE)—which collectively guide the development of models that better generalize to target tasks.

LEEP

LEEP is a metric customized for assessing transferability in machine learning models58. It quantifies how effectively a model’s performance is generalized from a training dataset to a new dataset. LEEP calculates the logarithm of the expected value of empirical prediction errors. Thereby, it provides a robust measure that accounts for the distributional differences between datasets.

During the training phase, the LEEP computes the empirical prediction errors in the training dataset by comparing the model predictions with the ground truth. In the transfer phase, these errors are leveraged to estimate the expected performance on a new dataset. Logarithmic transformation normalizes the scale, enhances interpretability, and allows for a more convenient comparison of transferability across models and datasets.

The key advantage of LEEP lies in its capability to directly assess transferability, which is crucial for evaluating a model’s practical utility. By considering the distributional discrepancies between datasets, LEEP provides a more robust measure than simpler error metrics. Its applicability spans various domains and machine-learning techniques. This makes it an effective tool for understanding how effectively models generalize to unseen data.

H-score

H-score is a transferability measure that evaluates the similarity between two datasets based on their feature distributions59. It computes the Hellinger distance between the feature distributions in the source and target datasets. The advantages of the H-score include its simplicity and efficiency in quantifying the transferability between datasets. This provides a straightforward means to assess the potential effectiveness of transferring knowledge from one dataset to another and thereby, aids in the selection of appropriate models for transfer learning tasks.

NCE

NCE is a transferability measure that plays a crucial role in evaluating the effectiveness of knowledge transfer in machine learning models, particularly in the context of transfer learning60. NCE quantifies the uncertainty reduction in the target domain provided by the source domain features. A higher NCE value indicates better transferability, as it reflects a greater reduction in uncertainty when applying a pretrained model to new, specific tasks. This measure is especially important in medical image analysis, where accurately transferring learned features from one domain to another can significantly enhance diagnostic performance.

NEC is a valuable transferability measure due to its simplicity and direct relevance to model performance on the target task. However, its reliance on labeled data and sensitivity to model calibration are notable drawbacks. When compared to other measures like LEEP and H-Score, NCE offers a more direct and interpretable evaluation of transferability but at the cost of requiring labeled data and higher computational expense. Each transferability measure has its trade-offs, and the choice of measure depends on the specific context and constraints of the transfer learning scenario. LEEP and H-Score measure transferability by examining models trained on data sets that represent the tasks. NCE instead use information theory to produce estimates from the source, which is the data itself.

By using NCE, one can assess how well a model adapted from general or intermediate domains captures the complex and unique characteristics of medical images, ultimately guiding the development of more robust and effective transfer learning frameworks. Discussion

This work developed a high-performance MSTL method for medical images that utilizes readily available natural images and a large number of microscopic cancer cell line images pretrained models. The proposed MSTL method offers several advantages. Firstly, it leverages the vast ImageNet dataset, consisting of millions of images, to learn general image features during the initial stage of transfer learning. Secondly, it incorporates cancer cell line images in the second stage of transfer learning, allowing the model to acquire more detailed features similar to medical images. Through the integration of features acquired from natural and microscopic image data, the proposed MSTL technique was able to classify medical images with remarkable accuracy.

The experiments conducted in this study revealed that the proposed MSTL method outperformed CTL approaches that were solely pretrained on ImageNet. CTL models, pretrained on abundant natural images, tend to overfit when applied to a limited number of medical image datasets. As a result, their performance drops when faced with new instances of data. Furthermore, due to the substantial differences between the domains of source (natural) and target (medical) images, the features learned from pretrained models on natural images have limited generalizability to medical images that differ significantly from natural images. The MSTL method was implemented using two kinds of deep neural networks, CNNs and ViTs. From the results of experiments using both neural networks, it can be observed that ViTs-based MSTL performs better than CNNs-based MSTL. The success and excellence of using ViT-based multistage transfer learning technique for medical images is evident from the results in this work. ViTs outperform CNN methods due to their capability of gathering overall information from initial layers and employing deep self-attention mechanism, which allows thorough analysis of features in each patch to make informed decisions. ViTs outperform CNNs in this study primarily due to their superior ability to capture long-range dependencies, learn flexible and complex feature representations, and generalize across different medical image modalities. While CNNs are strong in detecting local features and have useful inductive biases, their performance is hindered by the subtle, globally distributed, and complex features that are often critical in medical imaging tasks. ViTs, with their self-attention mechanisms and patch-based processing, offer greater adaptability and scalability, making them more effective for medical image analysis in this context. Based on the Grad-CAM output image depicted in Fig. 8, it can be inferred that the vision transformer model directs its attention towards the specific areas that clinicians typically focus for detecting diseases in each images. This highlights the potential of ViTs as robust MSTL architectures. Furthermore, the positive correlations between transferability measures (LEEP, NCE, H-Score) and model accuracies, especially in mammography and X-ray modalities, validate the effectiveness of the MSTL framework in transferring features learned to target medical image domain. However, the variability in performance among different architectures and modalities suggests the need for tailored optimization strategies. The performance gains of the MSTL approach are further supported by the ablation studies conducted in this work. Specifically, evaluation on additional external datasets across all three modalities demonstrated that the MSTL framework maintains strong performance across different data sources, indicating that the observed improvements are not dataset-specific. Moreover, reducing the size of the intermediate cancer cell line dataset led to a consistent decline in target-domain performance, highlighting the importance of sufficient intermediate-domain data for effective representation refinement. Overall, this study advances the field of medical image analysis by demonstrating the efficacy of MSTL, providing a foundation for future improvements in computer-aided diagnosis systems.

The multistage transfer learning framework in medical imaging holds transformative potential for improving diagnostic accuracy across diverse healthcare applications. By transferring insights from nonmedical datasets, this approach significantly enhances the performance of medical imaging tasks, such as disease diagnosis, classification, segmentation, detection, and registration. This versatility makes it especially valuable for diagnosing complex conditions like cancer, neurological disorders, and other diseases that require precise identification of anatomical features, even in cases where only limited medical data is available. The framework’s ability to generalize across various imaging tasks enables it to support critical processes like lesion identification, organ segmentation, and anomaly detection, thus aiding in treatment planning and continuous monitoring. Additionally, multistage transfer learning can convert images across modalities—such as from MRI to CT—offering enriched training datasets and supporting accurate multimodal analyses that inform treatment decisions. It has proven essential for radiomics, where extracting nuanced features from images is vital for personalized diagnostics and precision medicine, and has even adapted to emergent challenges, such as COVID-19, where diagnostic data was initially limited. Overall, multistage transfer learning fosters robust, adaptable diagnostic tools that meet the evolving demands of medical imaging and contribute to more accurate, timely, and comprehensive healthcare outcomes.

However, there are limitations to this study. A small number of widely used models, optimizers, and parameters, including fixed learning rate, training batch size, and augmentation, were used in the experiments carried out for this work. However, future investigations should expand upon this by exploring additional pretrained models and optimizers beyond those utilized in this research. Furthermore, it is recommended to vary the different parameters and conduct further studies to examine the effects of other hyperparameters. Moreover, the findings of this study revealed that incorporating cancer cell line microscopic images in the second stage of transfer learning enhanced the performance of medical image classification. This improvement stemmed from the fact that these cell line images provided features more closely aligned with medical images compared to natural images. Nevertheless, it is essential to conduct additional experiments to assess the impact of utilizing other types of cancer cell line images and varying their quantities. Further investigation should explore the effects of different cancer cell lines and determine if the quantity of cancer cell images used has any influence. One additional limitation of this study relates to the patch-based construction of the cancer cell line dataset used at the intermediate stage. Patches extracted from the same parent microscopic image were treated as independent samples during intermediate-stage training and evaluation. While this strategy enhances feature diversity for representation learning, it may introduce data dependency if patches from the same parent image appear in both training and testing folds, potentially inflating intermediate-stage performance metrics. Importantly, the cancer cell line dataset is used solely as an intermediate domain rather than as a target domain, and all reported medical image classification results are obtained from fully independent target datasets. Future work will enforce parent-image–level separation during patch-based dataset construction and explore slide-aware or aggregation-based training strategies to further strengthen the rigor of intermediate-domain evaluation while preserving the benefits of dense patch extraction. Furthermore, it should be noted that this study employed limited number of datasets for each of the three imaging modalities to generate the reported results. Future studies should consider utilizing a diverse range of datasets due to variations in imaging equipment, personnel, location, and other related factors. A robust deep learning model should be able to handle such variations and perform consistently, regardless of the dataset’s source. However, this necessitates the availability of diverse datasets from various locations, which are currently limited. Therefore, the next step will involve incorporating different datasets from around the world, sourced from various locations, to train our proposed model.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research significantly contributes to the field of medical image analysis by introducing a robust multistage transfer learning framework that effectively addresses the challenges of domain shift, domain generalization, and limited annotated datasets. By leveraging cancer cell line microscopic images as an intermediate domain, the proposed framework enhances the performance of deep learning models in diagnosing diseases from medical images. This advancement not only demonstrates the potential of multistage transfer learning providing a robust domain generalization capability to improve diagnostic accuracy across various medical imaging modalities but also provides a scalable solution for healthcare applications where data scarcity is a persistent issue. From a practical standpoint, the framework offers several advantages. It enables the efficient use of large datasets of readily available cancer cell line images, reducing the reliance on extensive labeled medical image datasets. Consequently, this approach can facilitate faster model training and deployment in clinical settings, thereby potentially improving patient outcomes through timely and accurate diagnoses. Despite these advancements, this work has certain limitations. The study primarily utilized a limited set of models and parameters, indicating a need for future research to explore a wider variety of architectures and hyperparameters. Additionally, the framework was tested on a single dataset per imaging modality, which may not fully represent the variability present in real-world medical data. Future investigations should incorporate diverse datasets from multiple sources to validate the framework’s robustness and generalizability. Looking ahead, future research should explore the integration of additional types of cell line images and their impact on model performance. Expanding the framework to encompass various imaging tasks, such as anomaly detection and segmentation, will further establish its applicability in medical diagnostics.
