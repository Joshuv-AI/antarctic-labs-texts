Decoupling of hygromechanical stimuli without cross-interference enabled by distinct ion-electron charge transport
Authors: Joo Sung Kim, Yusuke Ebihara, Lulu Sun, Du Baocai, Daishi Inoue, Daisuke Hashizume, Tomoyuki Yokota, Kenjiro Fukuda, Sunghoon Lee, Takao Someya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately decoupling mechanical stimuli and environmental factors such as humidity remains a major challenge in the development of tactile sensors, as cross-interference of these stimuli in sensing signals leads to reduced measurement accuracy and reliability. To address this, we present a distinct ion-electron charge transport-driven sensing mechanism that decouples pressure and humidity under simultaneous hygromechanical conditions. To exploit the distinct charge transport kinetics of ions and electrons, we designed a biphasic dual-conductive elastomer (BiDCE). Through phase separation between ionically and electronically conductive domains, BiDCE achieves multimodal sensing ability without cross-interference between sensing signals. In addition, an impedance spectroscopy-based decoupling method, developed based on the charge transport kinetics of BiDCE, enables real-time quantification of hygromechanical stimuli. The fabricated hygromechanical sensor was integrated into a self-adaptive robotic hand, enabling the simultaneous detection of both contact (pressure) and non-contact (proximity) states of human hands and fingers. This system ensures precise grip control while maintaining safe human-machine interaction and represents a significant advancement in multimodal tactile sensors for robotic skin interfaces.Subject terms: Engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Human skin exhibits a sophisticated multimodal sensory system, capable of simultaneously detecting diverse stimuli (e.g., pressure, stretch, vibration, temperature, and humidity) while maintaining distinct signal channels for each stimulus1,2. This capability allows for integrated analysis of stimuli for accurate texture and shape recognition and facilitates precise motor control in tasks requiring dexterity. Inspired by this biological sensory system, tactile sensor platforms have been developed to detect multiple tactile information without cross-interference3,4. The ability to accurately identify signals from complex stimuli is essential for applications in healthcare5, robotics6, and prosthetics7.

Recent advancements in polymeric sensing materials, such as conductive polymer8, metal- and carbon-polymer composites9,10, and solid-state electrolytes11 have enabled multimodal tactile sensors to detect combined stimuli, including pressure-stretch, pressure-vibration, and stretch-temperature. Among the various tactile inputs, decoupling hygromechanical stimuli (i.e., the combined influence of mechanical stimuli such as pressure or strain and humidity) represents a significant challenge, as their simultaneous presence can lead to signal interference, reducing both accuracy and reliability of sensing signals. For instance, humidity-induced changes in the mechanical properties (e.g., softness and elasticity) of polymeric sensing materials can compromise the consistency of their responses to external mechanical stimuli, leading to signal instability. In addition, from a practical application perspective, hygromechanical stimuli can markedly alter inter-facial properties, such as adhesion properties and friction coefficient between sensors and objects12.

To ensure the multimodal capabilities of tactile sensors under hygromechanical stimuli, a straightforward strategy is to integrate multiple sensing units, each designed for specific sensing functions, to simultaneously monitor mechanical stimuli and humidity13–16. This configuration typically requires multiple sensors arranged in parallel on a shared substrate, which results in limited spatial resolution and increased fabrication complexity due to the need to pattern different materials. An alternative approach for creating a multimodal sensing platform is the use of a single-unit sensor with multiple sensing parameters, which simplifies the device architecture and improves spatial resolution. By altering operational modes and monitoring distinct output signals (e.g., current and voltage), multimodal sensors can decouple the hygromechanical stimuli17. This is achieved by connecting the sensor to separate measurement setups for each sensing parameter, allowing independent signal acquisition tailored to the specific response of each stimulus. Such a configuration, though effective for signal separation, is constrained by the limited scan rate and the requirement for multiple measurement systems, which reduce its practicality. Alternatively, multimodal sensing mechanisms using a single sensing parameter have been reported, exploiting differences in the response or recovery times of sensing signals to each stimulus. Mechanical stimuli induce rapid changes in electrical signals from immediate deformation of sensing materials, whereas humidity leads to slower, sustained changes as water molecules absorb and diffuse into the sensing material. Thus, by analysing these temporal differences18 or distinct signal patterns19 (e.g., sharp initial responses versus gradual sustained changes), each sensing signal can be identified while minimising complexity in the measurement setup. Recent studies have introduced artificial intelligence–assisted approaches to compensate for signal distortion and cross-interference in soft and deformable sensors. In particular, neural-network-based decoupling strategies have been applied to distinguish mechanical stimuli under deformation or coupled environmental conditions, thereby restoring sensing accuracy and robustness in dynamic operation environments20,21.

However, achieving single sensing parameter-driven decoupling under the simultaneous presence of hygromechanical stimuli remains an unresolved challenge, which limits its applicability in practical environments. Fundamentally, the difficulty in decoupling humidity and mechanical stimuli originates from molecular-level interactions between water molecules and polymeric sensing materials. Humidity-induced absorption of water molecules into the amorphous regions of polymeric sensing materials significantly alters their electrical properties, including conductivity (resistive type) and dielectric constant (capacitive type)22. These simultaneous alterations in multiple electrical properties overlap with the changes induced by mechanical stimuli, creating cross-interference that hinders accurate detection and demands advanced decoupling methodologies.

Here, we present a distinct ion-electron charge transport-driven hygromechanical sensor capable of accurately decoupling pressure and humidity without cross-interference. Pressure and humidity independently influence the ionic and electronic resistances of biphasic dual-conductive elastomer (BiDCE), which can be clearly distinguished by varying the measurement frequency due to the differing kinetics of electron and ion transport. In addition, we established an impedance analysis framework that extracts the specific resistive components of BiDCE at selected frequencies and enables rapid decoupling of hygromechanical stimuli without full-spectrum impedance scanning. This charge transport kinetics-based decoupling methodology not only eliminates the need for extensive calibration and adjustments of measurement setups but also enables real-time decoupling. The sensing and decoupling characteristics of the hygromechanical sensor array (1225 pixels) were validated even under simultaneous exposure to pressure and humidity, enabling the differentiation of objects with similar appearances based on weight and moisture content. This capability is attributed to the uniform pixel-level response of BiDCE, which removes the requirement for independently patterned sensing layers and allows high-density array implementation. In addition, the hygromechanical sensor was integrated into a robotic hand to enable self-adaptive operation. The self-adaptive robotic hand can detect contact (pressure) and non-contact (proximity), adjust grip force with precision, and ensure safe human-machine interaction. This provides a practical solution for self-adaptive robotic systems without requiring additional sensors.

Results

Material design and decoupling mechanism

To implement multimodal sensing and decoupling capabilities for hygromechanical stimuli in a soft tactile sensory system (Fig. 1a), we developed the sensing material by blending an ionic liquid, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate, (IL; [EMIM][BF4]) and carbon nanotube (CNT) within a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) polymer solution to introduce ionic and electronic conductive pathways, respectively. The resulting ternary blending solution underwent thermal processing at 20% relative humidity (RH) (Supplementary Fig. 1), which led to the development of BiDCE with a phase-separated micro-structure, consisting of distinct ionically conductive domains (i-conductive domains) and electronic conductive domains (e-conductive domains). The BiDCE integrates Au-deposited polyurethane (PU) nanomesh electrodes23–26 on its upper and lower surfaces, enhancing conformability and permeability to humidity (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figs. 2–4). The BiDCE layer and the Au-deposited PU nanomesh electrodes have thicknesses of approximately 40 µm and 10 µm. The thin and porous nanomesh design of substrates and electrodes ensures effective transmission of external pressure and humidity to the BiDCE, which improves humidity and pressure sensitivity.

Phase separation of BiDCE occurs because the IL and polymer matrix exhibit limited miscibility upon solidification. Imidazolium ionic liquids adsorb on CNTs via cation–π interactions and assist dispersion at the solution stage. In the final solid, however, the stability of CNT–IL coupling is not guaranteed, as it depends on the balance of matrix–IL compatibility and inter-facial interactions. In the BiDCE system (TPU–[EMIM][BF4]–MWCNT), the relatively low compatibility between TPU and [EMIM][BF4] prevents the IL from being uniformly incorporated into the polymer matrix during solidification. As a result, the CNT–IL inter-facial layer becomes unstable and decouples, while differences in component mobility and solubility parameters drive compositional redistribution toward lower free energy, leading to micro-phase separation into e- and i-conductive domains (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6)27,28. In contrast, the comparison system (polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)–1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate [EMIM][OAc]–water-dispersible MWCNT) maintains a homogeneous morphology because strong hydrogen-bonding and ion–dipole interactions among PVA hydroxyl groups, acetate anions, and hydrophilic CNT surfaces stabilize an integrated polar network and suppress segregation during solidification29. This phase-separated micro-structure was confirmed by comparing X-ray microscopy (XRM) and surface potential distribution of the BiDCE and homogeneous structures (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8).

To achieve decoupling of pressure and humidity, we developed a hygromechanical sensor in conjunction with a charge transport kinetics-based decoupling mechanism. BiDCE facilitates both ionic and electronic conduction under an applied electric potential across nanomesh electrodes positioned on its top and bottom surfaces (Fig. 1d). Since water molecules can penetrate the nanomesh electrodes, under humid conditions, water dissociates the cation-anion clusters within the BiDCE, leading to a reduction in ionic resistance (Supplementary Fig. 9b)30. When pressure is applied, more conductive paths are created by the CNTs, enhancing electronic conductivity and decreasing electronic resistance (Supplementary Fig. 9c)31. Due to the differences in transport kinetics between ions and electrons, their responses vary with frequency under AC bias32.

In the Nyquist plot (imaginary part of complex impedance, Z″ vs. real part, Z′), the two semicircles for BiDCE are described to represent the parallel combination of the electronic and ionic resistances, along with a constant-phase-element (CPE) circuit in series with the ionic resistance (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Note 1)33–35. Specifically, the first x-intercept (on the left) corresponds to the total resistance (Rtotal), which includes both ionic and electronic contributions, measured at higher frequencies, while the second x-intercept (on the right) is interpreted as the electronic resistance (Re), measured at relatively lower frequencies. Validation of decoupling mechanism and hygromechanical sensing characteristics

To validate the decoupling mechanism of the hygromechanical sensor, we first analyzed the responses to hygromechanical stimuli of sensors fabricated with pure ionic and electronic conductors, prepared by blending [EMIM][BF4] with TPU and CNTs with TPU, respectively. The bode plots of pure ionic conductor showed negligible change in impedance under pressure due to the invariant contact area between the electrode and BiDCE and the intrinsic ionic conductivity, which is independent of pressure (Fig. 2a). In contrast, under varying humidity, a significant decrease in ionic resistance was observed due to the hygroscopic and hydrophilic nature of [EMIM][BF4]. The ionic component was selected based on a comparative study using ILs with different anions (BF4−, PF6−, and TFSI−) (Supplementary Fig. 10), in which the humidity-dependent response followed the hydrophilicity trend of the anions (BF4⁻ > PF6⁻ > TFSI⁻)36. Consequently, [EMIM][BF4] was chosen, as it exhibited the greatest humidity sensitivity, which arises from its efficient cation–anion dissociation under humid conditions. In addition to hygroscopicity and hydrophilicity of ionic conductors, the porous nanomesh electrodes facilitated penetration of water molecules into the sensing material, leading to effective dissociation of anion-cation clusters (Supplementary Fig. 11).

In contrast to the pure ionic conductor, the pure electronic conductor exhibited negligible changes in impedance when exposed to humidity, which can be attributed to its minimal water uptake (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). The electronic component was selected based on a comparative study using different conductive fillers (MWCNTs, carbon flakes, and AgNWs) (Supplementary Fig. 14). In this comparison, MWCNTs exhibited the highest pressure sensitivity and the weakest response to humidity, indicating their suitability as the e-conductive domain for selective pressure sensing. AgNWs typically have lengths in the order of tens of micrometers, which are comparable to or larger than the thickness of the polymer film. Under random dispersion, they preferentially align in-plane rather than along the thickness direction, which suppresses the formation of effective through-thickness conductive pathways and reduces pressure sensitivity in the sandwich-structured device configuration. In addition, the difference in humidity response originates from the dominant conduction mechanism. MWCNT-based composites form a robust three-dimensional network governed by inter-tube contact resistance and tunneling conduction, whereas carbon flake systems are dominated by inter-flake tunneling barriers, making their resistance more sensitive to environmental changes such as moisture adsorption.

Based on the intrinsic sensing characteristics of ionic and electronic conductors, we developed BiDCEs by blending solutions of [EMIM][BF4], CNTs, and TPU in various ratios. Figure 2c shows that the ionic resistance decreases and the electronic resistance increases with increasing IL content and decreasing CNT content (Supplementary Figs. 15–17 and Supplementary Note 1). The relative fractions of CNT and IL serve as a key design rule for achieving decoupling capability between pressure and humidity signals (Supplementary Fig. 18). (i) When the IL concentration in the polymer matrix is too high, visco-poroelastic effects can induce pressure-dependent changes in the ionic resistance37. (ii) In addition, a larger fraction of hygroscopic, swellable IL-based i-conductive domains induces volumetric swelling that increases the average CNT-to-CNT separation38,39, thereby decreasing the number of electrical percolation pathways and increasing the electronic resistance. Both effects introduce pressure–humidity cross-interference. For these reasons, comparison of resistance variations across BiDCEs with different IL/CNT ratios under pressure and humidity revealed that the composition of 33 wt%/0.048 wt% (IL/CNT) achieved the most accurate decoupling of these stimuli and was therefore selected as the sensing material for the hygromechanical sensor (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

The high-frequency intercept Rs primarily reflects the contact resistance at the interface between the electrode and the e-conductive domain of BiDCE. In the phase-separated BiDCE structure, increasing the CNT content expands the volume fraction of the e-conductive domain and increases the effective inter-facial area between this domain and the electrode surface, thereby reducing Rs (Supplementary Fig. 21). The variation in contact resistance occurs only across different BiDCE compositions and remains unchanged under hygromechanical stimuli (Supplementary Fig. 22). Based on equivalent-circuit fitting of the Nyquist plots, Rs was extracted under various pressure and relative humidity conditions. The standard deviations of Rs for the pressure-dependent and humidity-dependent measurements are 1.46 and 2.11, respectively. The negligible variation of Rs under hygromechanical stimuli indicates that the contact resistance remains essentially constant during sensing. This stability of Rs ensures reliable sensing performance in practical applications. In addition, we established a theoretical framework showing that temperature can be independently decoupled using the CPE exponent n, which is governed by high-frequency inter-facial polarisation rather than pressure- or humidity-dependent resistive pathways (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 23).

To quantitatively evaluate this decoupling, we define decoupling accuracy as the ratio of humidity sensitivity derived from ionic resistance to that derived from electronic resistance, and the ratio of pressure sensitivity derived from electronic resistance to that derived from ionic resistance (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Table 3). The decoupling accuracy was approximately 22.9 for humidity and 15.8 for pressure. As shown in Fig. 2d, e, the Nyquist plots for the BiDCE show that both total and electronic resistance decreased under pressure, while total resistance alone decreased in response to humidity, indicating that ionic resistance is exclusively responsible for humidity sensitivity.

The decoupling characteristics of BiDCE originate from phase-separated ionic and electronic domains, which independently preserve the humidity sensitivity of the ionic phase and the pressure sensitivity of the electronic phase. The importance of phase separation becomes more evident when compared to bilayer configurations. In vertically stacked structures, the effective transmission of external hygromechanical stimuli to each sensing component is inherently limited (Supplementary Fig. 25a). When a humidity-sensitive pure ionic conductor (i-conductor) is placed on top of a pressure-sensitive electronic conductor, the upper ionic layer absorbs mechanical deformation and attenuates the pressure signal reaching the pure electronic conductor (e-conductor) (Supplementary Fig. 25b). Conversely, when the e-conductor is placed on top, it acts as a barrier to water molecule diffusion, thereby suppressing the humidity response of the underlying i-conductor (Supplementary Fig. 25c). Therefore, bilayer structures suffer from intrinsic limitations in multimodal sensing due to signal shielding and inefficient stimulus transfer. In contrast, BiDCE enables co-localized ionic and electronic conduction pathways within a single material, which allows simultaneous exposure to external stimuli and effective decoupling without signal loss.

The mechanism underlying the humidity selectivity of BiDCE was further validated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements conducted under controlled relative humidity conditions. As the relative humidity increased from vacuum to ambient conditions (~50% RH) and then to a high-humidity environment (above 90% RH), the peak intensity at 20.6°, corresponding to the inter-planar spacing (d-spacing) of the crystalline region of the TPU matrix (0.42 nm), progressively decreased, whereas the peak at 25.4°, associated with the d-spacing of aggregated CNTs (0.34 nm), remained unchanged (Fig. 2f). This observation suggests that water molecules selectively penetrate the i-conductive domain without affecting the e-conductive domain.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to provide an in-depth understanding of the selective water absorption and diffusion behaviour in i-conductive domains. Detailed simulation methodologies are described in the Supplementary Note 2. The simulation cell was constructed to emulate the ideal phase separation of BiDCE, ensuring that [EMIM][BF4] were absent in the e-conductive domains and CNTs were absent in the i-conductive domains. The absorption process of water molecules was analyzed by calculating their relative density, defined as the number of water atoms per unit slab divided by the total number of atoms (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27). During a 2000 ps simulation, water molecules were selectively absorbed throughout the entire i-conductive domains, reaching an absorption ratio of approximately 20% (Fig. 2g, h, and Supplementary Figs. 28 and 30). In contrast, only limited uptake (absorption ratio of 2%) occurred in the e-conductive domains and was confined near the surface (~6 Å) (Fig. 2g, i, and Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30). Furthermore, the calculated water diffusivity in the i-conductive domains (3.4 × 10⁻⁵ cm²/s) was significantly lower than in the e-conductive domains. These differences arise from the molecular-level interactions. Water molecules experience strong electrostatic attraction from the EMIM+ cations and BF₄− anions, which promotes their penetration into the i-conductive domains. Hydrogen bonding between water and the imidazolium cation, mainly at the aromatic ring hydrogen located between the two nitrogen atoms, provides additional enthalpic stabilization inside the i-conductive domains (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Figs. 31–33)30,40. In contrast, the e-conductive domains present a relatively hydrophobic microenvironment that suppresses water uptake and restricts penetration to the inter-facial region. The IL also functions as a plasticizer that widens the free volume of the TPU matrix41, further facilitating water absorption and swelling in the i-conductive domains. The overall MD simulation results are fully consistent with the experimental impedance response.

Real-time sensing and decoupling under combined hygromechanical stimuli

The AC impedance spectroscopy-based decoupling methods typically require scanning across a wide frequency range, which introduces time delays and makes them unsuitable for real-time monitoring. To address this, we developed a more efficient method that enables decoupling of pressure and humidity by analysing impedances at specific frequencies rather than scanning the entire spectrum. Impedance values were obtained at frequencies (20 Hz, 13 kHz, 170 kHz, and 290 kHz) corresponding to the minimum and maximum values of the imaginary part of the impedance semicircles (Supplementary Note. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 34). Under pressure, impedances changed linearly across all frequencies (Fig. 3a). In contrast, humidity affected only the impedances at 13 kHz, 170 kHz, and 290 kHz, leaving the low frequency (20 Hz) unchanged (Fig. 3b). By focusing on these two frequencies (20 Hz and 170 kHz), we achieved effective decoupling of hygromechanical stimuli without the need for comprehensive frequency sweeps. This optimised approach allows for real-time monitoring with improved signal processing efficiency.

Hygromechanical sensors retained their decoupling functionality even after repeated cyclic exposure to pressure and humidity. The sensors showed stable impedance changes after 200 cycles of repeated application of 100 kPa pressure and 40% relative humidity, with response times of 480 ms and 24 s, respectively (Fig. 3c, d, and Supplementary Figs. 35 and 36). This stability is attributed to the absence of IL leakage under pressure, as verified by external absorption tests and FTIR analysis showing no detectable transfer of [EMIM][BF4] from the BiDCE films (Supplementary Fig. 37). Specifically, the consistent decrease in impedance at both 20 Hz and 170 kHz under pressure, and the exclusive decrease in impedance at 170 kHz under humidity, validated both the stability of the hygromechanical sensor and the effectiveness of our signal processing methodology. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) reaches 37.3 dB under 100 kPa and 45.0 dB under a 40% RH change. This high SNR originates from the decoupling capability of the BiDCE system. In the pressure sensing mode, humidity fluctuations do not influence the baseline signal, while in the humidity sensing mode, mechanical perturbations do not affect the resting signal. As a result, cross-interference between the two stimuli is minimised, leading to a significantly improved SNR.

Spatial mapping of pressure and humidity was achieved using a high-density (35 × 35) hygromechanical sensor array consisting of 1225 pixels over a 5 × 5 cm2 active area, with an overall device yield of 98.45% (Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39). This array device allows for the simultaneous detection of both stimuli, which is essential for distinguishing objects with similar appearances or detecting the approach and pressure of moisture-containing materials such as human skin. To demonstrate this, we applied weight objects coated with swollen polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) to the array device, representing objects with differing weights and moisture/water contents (Fig. 3e). The spatial mapping results not only accurately displayed the weight and position of the objects (Fig. 3f) but also reflected the higher moisture content of the PVA-coated object in the form of humidity sensing signals (Fig. 3g). Moreover, as human skin contains moisture that evaporates continuously, the hygromechanical sensor was able to detect proximity via humidity-induced impedance changes as the skin approached the sensor (Fig. 3h). The pressure and humidity spatial mapping further revealed that, when a fingertip made contact with the sensor, both pressure and humidity were detected simultaneously (Fig. 3i, j). In contrast, when the fingertip approached but did not make direct contact, only humidity was detected. This combination of pressure and humidity spatial mapping provides a powerful tool for distinguishing between contact and non-contact states of a human fingertip, enabling accurate real-time detection in scenarios involving both stimuli.

Demonstration of self-adaptive robotic hand

We developed a self-adaptive robotic hand system integrated with hygromechanical sensors, enabling safe and responsive interaction with human hands (Fig. 4a). The system separates signals from pressure, which corresponds to physical contact, and humidity, which reflects noncontact conditions such as proximity to moist objects like human skin. These stimuli are decoupled by assigning each to a distinct frequency range, with 20 Hz used for pressure detection and 170 kHz for humidity. Thus, the robotic hand can identify the interaction type and perform appropriate grip motions such as pinch grip, lateral grip, and handshake grip with precise modulation of grip force (Fig. 4b). To integrate the hygromechanical sensors into a robotic hand, we attached conformable sensors to the fingers and palm of the graspable robotic hand. The nanomesh electrode of the hygromechanical sensor was connected to the measurement equipment using stretchable conductive yarn. Its resistance remained stable under varying tensile strain (0%–35%) and humidity (30%–98% RH) (Supplementary Fig. 40). In addition, the hygromechanical sensor maintained its initial impedance under a bending radius of 6.5 mm, which is smaller than that of the robotic finger (Supplementary Fig. 41). The developed robotic hand has a total of five sensors: one each on the tips of the thumb (TT), index (IT), and middle (MT) fingers, one on the middle phalanx (MP) of the index finger, and one on the palm (P) (Fig. 4a).

The operation of the robotic hand proceeds through five steps (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Note 4). Prior to the initiation of the active operation, the robotic hand is positioned in an initial state at a distance where neither humidity nor proximity can yet be detected. The human hand or finger is placed within the general field of operation, but still far enough to avoid triggering any sensor response. In the first step, all five fingers begin to bend slowly. As the hygromechanical sensors approach the human skin, their impedance at 170 kHz gradually decreases due to the moisture content of the skin. This humidity-dependent impedance change is used to estimate the distance between the human and robotic hands. In the second step, when the distance falls below 20 mm (limit of detection, LOD: 25 mm), only the fingers near regions with detected humidity changes continue to bend, enabling a targeted pre-grasp motion (Fig. 4d). The hygromechanical sensor exhibited onset delays of 0.54 s in response to an increase in humidity and 0.86 s in response to a decrease in humidity (Supplementary Fig. 42). In the third step, the selected fingers continue bending toward the human hand while maintaining the detected grip type. In the fourth step (grasping), physical contact is established, and the impedance at 20 Hz decreases, indicating pressure application. In the final step, to avoid exceeding the pain pressure threshold (PPT), the bending motion is terminated when the sensed pressure reaches or exceeds 150 kPa (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 43, and Supplementary Table 4).

Figure 4f illustrates the impedance profiles at 20 Hz and 170 kHz during the operation of the self-adaptive robotic hand performing a pinch grip. Upon detecting humidity at the thumb tip (TT) and index tip (IT), the hygromechanical sensors recognise the approach of the human hand and initiate a pre-grasping motion corresponding to the pinch grip. The robotic hand then establishes contact with the human index finger, continuously monitoring the applied pressure to ensure it remains within the pain pressure threshold (PPT), successfully completing the pinch grip. Similarly, when humidity is detected at the thumb tip (TT) and middle phalanx (MP), the robotic hand initiates pre-grasping for a lateral grip (Fig. 4g). Subsequently, the robotic hand forms contact with the human thumb and maintains pressure below the PPT, completing the lateral grip. Lastly, when humidity is detected at the palm (P), the robotic hand performs a pre-grasping motion corresponding to a handshake grip, establishing contact with the human hand and maintaining pressure within the PPT to complete the handshake grip (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Movie).

As an additional demonstration, we integrated the hygromechanical sensor into the inner surface of a face mask to monitor the respiratory rate and distinguish between nasal and oral breathing (Supplementary Fig. 44a). The pressure and humidity traces exhibit periodic and synchronous pulses, which clearly correspond to the user’s respiratory rate under 80% RH ambient conditions (Supplementary Fig. 44b). More importantly, although the airflow-induced pressure profiles were similar between nasal and oral breathing, the humidity amplitude during oral exhalation was more than twice that of nasal exhalation (Supplementary Fig. 44c). This indicates that the nasal cavity retains more moisture during exhalation than the oral cavity42. By analysing the characteristics of exhaled air, our system can monitor the respiratory rate and clearly distinguish nasal from oral exhalation. In principle, this sensing capability can support monitoring of sleep-related breathing patterns, including apnea, hypopnea, hyperventilation, and chronic mouth breathing. In addition, a compact impedance measurement module composed of an impedance converter and a micro-controller enables practical implementation of the hygromechanical sensing system for real-world applications (Supplementary Fig. 45).

Discussion

This study presents a cross-interference-free hygromechanical sensor capable of precisely decoupling pressure and humidity. The phase separation of the i- and e-conductive domains in BiDCE enables selective sensing of each stimulus through distinct charge transport kinetics. The decoupling mechanism was systematically validated through material characterisation and simulation analysis, which showed that water molecules selectively penetrate the i-conductive domain without affecting the e-conductive domain. In addition, impedance spectroscopy provided quantitative evaluation of the sensing and decoupling characteristics. Despite this accurate decoupling without cross-interference, the sensitivity to each stimulus remains moderate. Sensitivity can be improved by using more hygroscopic ionic materials and mechanically compliant electronic materials, while preserving phase separation and minimising cross-interference.

The sensor was integrated into a self-adaptive robotic hand, enabling real-time detection and precise control of grip force based on the proximity and pressure of a human hand. The robotic hand could differentiate between contact and non-contact states, dynamically adjusting its grip gesture and grip force to remain below PPT. We believe this work opens new avenues for the development of advanced robotic systems and human-machine interface technologies, where precise, multi-modal sensing is crucial for enhanced functionality and safety.

Methods

Preparation of BiDCE

BiDCE was prepared by blending a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) matrix with N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent to create a 30 wt% solution. Ionic liquid 1-Ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate ([EMIM][BF4]) (Sigma-Aldrich) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) (Sigma-Aldrich) were added at concentrations of 17–54 wt% and 0.028–0.064 wt%, respectively. To promote effective phase separation, MWCNTs were first dispersed in the TPU solution, followed by the addition of [EMIM][BF4], enabling preferential interactions of CNTs with hard segments and ionic liquid with soft segments of TPU. The mixture was blended using a temperature-controlled mechanical stirrer (ASONE, LSH-6D) at 60 °C for 24 h (100 rpm). The resulting solution was then spin-coated onto a glass substrate (MS-B100, Mikasa; 500 rpm for 50 s) and annealed at 80 °C and 20% RH (the actual monitored humidity range was 18%–22% RH) for 24 h to induce phase separation and film solidification. Pure ionic conductor films were fabricated using a 30 wt% TPU solution in DMF containing 33 wt% [EMIM][BF4]. Pure electronic conductor films were prepared using the same TPU solution with 0.048 wt% MWCNTs. Both films were processed under identical blending and annealing conditions as the BiDCE formulation. For the comparative study of different anions, ionic conductors containing [EMIM][BF4], [EMIM][PF6], and [EMIM][TFSI] (Sigma-Aldrich) were fabricated. Each ionic liquid was blended into the 30 wt% TPU/DMF solution at 33 wt%, and the films were prepared using the same stirring, spin-coating, and annealing conditions as those used for the pure ionic conductor films. For the comparative study of different conductive fillers, electronic conductors containing MWCNTs, carbon flakes, and AgNWs were fabricated. MWCNTs were incorporated at 0.048 wt% with respect to the polymer matrix. Carbon flakes (East Japan Carbon, AED-200) and AgNWs (AW030-LP, Zhejiang Kechuang Advanced Materials Co., Ltd.) were incorporated at 0.52 wt% and 0.17 wt%, respectively.

Fabrication of hygromemchanical sensor

The hygromechanical sensor was fabricated with a sandwich structure, where the BiDCE layer was placed between two PU nanomesh electrodes. To establish a stable electrical interface, each nanomesh electrode was placed onto the BiDCE surface and hot-pressed at 60 °C (a temperature below the melting point of the TPU–IL composite) for 1 min (MASADA JACK, MH-15P). The PU nanomesh sheet was fabricated via electrospinning (NANON-03, Mecc) using a 15 wt% PU solution, prepared by dissolving pristine PU (Rezamin M-8115LP, Dainichiseika) in a DMF/MEK mixed solvent (7:3 weight ratio). The electrospinning process was performed under an applied voltage of 20–25 kV, with a 15 cm gap between the needle tip and the fiber collector. The nanofiber density was controlled by adjusting the electrospinning time and solution volume. The fabricated PU nanomesh sheet was then transferred onto a 125 μm thick polyimide frame, which had a window to expose only the nanomesh sheet. Finally, 100 nm thick Au and 5 nm thick Cr layers were thermally deposited onto the PU nanomesh sheet using a shadow mask.

Electrical characterisation

The impedance and phase characteristics of the nanomesh electrodes were measured over a frequency range of 20–300 kHz using an LCR meter (E4980A, Keysight). To evaluate the electrical response to humidity, measurements were conducted at a constant temperature of 25 °C while controlling the relative humidity (30%–90% RH) using a humidity chamber (GSU-24-5V, ESPEC). Based on the acquired frequency, impedance, and phase angle data, Nyquist plots were generated, and semicircle fitting was performed using ZMAN modeling software to extract electronic and ionic resistance values from the equivalent circuit.

Characterization of phase separation

Characterization of Phase Separation was conducted using various analytical techniques, including humidity-controlled grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD), X-ray microscopy (XRM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). GIXRD analysis was performed using an XRDynamic 500 (Anton Paar) equipped with a humidity control chamber (CHC plus⁺ module) and a vacuum-compatible sample stage (EVAC module). Measurements were conducted at 30 kV and 10 mA using a Cu Kα radiation source, with diffraction scans acquired over a 2θ range of 15–85° for BiDCE films with a thickness of 40 μm. Nano X-ray computed tomography (Nano-CT) was carried out using a V tome x m240 (GE) to examine heterogeneous BiDCE films as well as homogeneous hydrophilic dual-conductive films. The homogeneous film was prepared using a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) matrix blended with [EMIM][OAc] and water-soluble MWCNTs at 33 wt% and 0.048 wt%, respectively. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were performed using an SPM-9700HT (Shimadzu) in semi-contact mode for surface topography imaging and in Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) mode for surface potential mapping. Cross-sectional imaging was conducted using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a Quattro S (FEI), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed for elemental analysis. The cross-section processing was performed with an EM TIC 3X (Leica).

Self-adaptive robotic hand

The fabricated hygrogmechanical sensor, composed of BiDCE with the optimised 33 wt%/0.048 wt% (IL/CNT) composition, was attached to a wireless robotic hand (Mark 7, Mand.ro) using double-sided adhesive tape and electrically connected to measurement equipment via stretchable conductive yarn (For detailed robotic hand operation, refer to Supplementary Note 4). The impedance of five hygromechanical sensors embedded in the robotic hand was measured using an LCR meter, with each sensor sequentially scanned using a switching matrix (707 A, Tektronix) across ten channels. For each sensor, impedance values at 20 Hz and 170 kHz were recorded, and the measurement sequence was continuously repeated. (For further details on the operational mechanism, refer to Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. 27).
