The mediating effect of serum total testosterone in the association between the ZJU index and PCOS
Authors: Tao Yin, Chunmei Liang, Dongmei ji, Yunxia Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ZJU index is an innovative computational method that integrates triglycerides (TG), body mass index (BMI), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and the alanine aminotransferase (ALT) to aspartate aminotransferase (AST) ratio. Previous studies indicated that it is strongly associated with markers of lipid metabolism and glucose intolerance. However, no studies have yet explored the association between the ZJU index and the chances of getting PCOS. Therefore, a total of 1352 female participants (1169 controls and 183 PCOS patients) who underwent in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF-ET) were included in the study. Basic demographic information and laboratory biochemical indicators of the participants were collected, and the ZJU index was calculated. Multivariate logistic regression model was employed to examine the association between the ZJU index and the chances of getting PCOS, with the ZJU index analyzed both as a categorical variable (quartiles) and as a continuous variable. Multivariate linear regression model was used to explore the association between the ZJU index and baseline serum sex hormone levels, including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), LH/FSH ratio, estradiol (E2), progesterone (P), prolactin (PRL), and total testosterone (TT). The restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model was used to examine both linear and nonlinear associations between the ZJU index and the chances of getting PCOS. A mediation analysis model was used to explore whether TT is a possible intermediary factor in the association between the ZJU index and the chances of getting PCOS. The results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for logistic regression and as β coefficients with 95% CIs for linear regression. The ability of the ZJU index, BMI, and HOMA-IR to identify PCOS was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and the area under the curve (AUC), with comparisons of the AUCs performed using the Delong test. According to the multivariate logistic regression analysis, the ZJU index, when analyzed as a continuous variable, was positively associated with the chances of getting PCOS (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05–1.14, P < 0.001). When the ZJU index was categorized into quartiles, and Q1 (24.19–31.67; mean ± SD: 29.77 ± 1.36) was used as the reference, the chances of getting PCOS were 1.69 (95% CI: 0.90–3.17) in Q2 (31.68–34.58), 1.91 (95% CI: 1.01–3.60) in Q3 (34.59–38.51), and 4.44 (95% CI: 2.27–8.66) in Q4 (38.52–54.77), suggesting a dose-dependent increase, especially above 34.59. Multivariate linear regression analysis showed that the ZJU index was positively associated with TT (β = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.00–0.02, P < 0.001), while it was negatively associated with FSH (β = -0.06, 95% CI: -0.10 - -0.01, P = 0.008). There were no significant associations between the ZJU index LH/FSH (β = 0.00, 95% CI: -0.01- 0.01, P = 0.656). RCS regression model demonstrated a significant linear and positive association between the ZJU index and PCOS. Serum TT levels may partially account for the positive association between the ZJU index and the chances of getting PCOS, with an estimated 10.00% of this association that may be explained through an indirect pathway involving TT. ROC analysis showed that ZJU index (AUC = 0.637) was a stronger predictor of PCOS than BMI (AUC = 0.625) and HOMA-IR (AUC = 0.621). The ZJU index was significantly associated with increased chances of having PCOS in infertile women undergoing IVF-ET. Additionally, our findings suggest that TT may be a possible intermediary factor in the association between the ZJU index and the chances of getting PCOS. This suggests that metabolic dysfunction, particularly insulin resistance, may contribute to the chances of getting PCOS through hyperandrogenism rather than by disrupting hypothalamic-pituitary regulation.Keywords: ZJU index, Polycystic ovary syndrome, Metabolism, Mediation analysisSubject terms: Endocrinology, Risk factors

Introduction

The global incidence of infertility is rising, with approximately 17.5% of couples being diagnosed with the condition1. Ovulatory disorders account for about 25% of infertility cases, and 70% of anovulatory women suffer from polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)2. PCOS is a common reproductive endocrine disorder that affects 6–20% of women of childbearing age3. It is characterized by disrupted folliculogenesis and ovulatory dysfunction, and accounts for approximately 80% of anovulatory infertility cases4. According to the internationally recognized Rotterdam consensus criteria established in 2003, a diagnosis of PCOS requires the presence of at least two out of the following three features: clinical or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism, oligo/anovulation, and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound, after excluding other causes of hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction. Patient diagnosed with PCOS exhibit irregular menstrual cycles, metabolic disturbances such as obesity and insulin resistance, and the presence of polycystic ovaries5. PCOS is a complex reproductive–metabolic disorder that is linked to various diseases, such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and endometrial cancer, and is known to significantly impair quality of life.Previous studies have indicated that abnormal alterations in glucose and lipid metabolism play a key role in the pathogenesis of PCOS. These alterations may involve insulin resistance (IR), chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress6–8. To date, the etiology of PCOS remains unclear, and is believed to involve a complex interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors. Although in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF-ET) is an effective treatment for infertility in women with PCOS, a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms remains essential for optimizing both therapeutic and preventive strategies9–11.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a well-established metabolic feature of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), with reported prevalence rates ranging from 44 to 70% among affected individuals12. Several metabolic indicators, such as the visceral adiposity index (VAI), and the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), have been previously used to assess metabolic dysfunction in women with PCOS. HOMA-IR is a model that describes the relationship between glucose and insulin dynamics under fasting conditions to estimate insulin resistance. It is simplified by the following formula: fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mmol/L) ÷ 22.5 13. Previous studies have shown that HOMA-IR index was positively associated with clinical features of PCOS. For instance, a cross-sectional study reported that women with longer menstrual cycles (≥ 35 days) had significantly higher HOMA-IR levels than controls, suggesting a potential link between the severity of oligomenorrhea and IR14.

The visceral adiposity index (VAI), a sex-specific mathematical model that integrates body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), has been proposed as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance. As a simple surrogate marker of visceral fat dysfunction and accumulation, is a predictor of insulin resistance and has been shown to be associated with hyperinsulinemia, hyperandrogenism, and anovulation15. Durmus et al. reported that VAI levels were significantly elevated in overweight and/or obese women with PCOS compared to both healthy controls and non-obese PCOS patients16. However, these indices often require multiple biochemical and anthropometric measurements, which may limit their applicability in routine clinical settings.

The ZJU index is a novel metabolic parameter, including triglyceride (TG), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), body mass index (BMI) and blood glucose, which can coffer a more comprehensive perspective and effectively capture trends in metabolic disorders than isolated biochemical indicators17. Previous studies have indicated that ZJU index was associated with insulin resistance (IR) and lipid metabolism, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) risk, and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) syndrome risk18.

A health examination cohort from Wuhu, China, conducted by Cao et al., found a linear relationship between the ZJU index and kidney stone recurrence. In this study, the ZJU index was significantly higher in stone formers (36.58 ± 4.21) than in controls (35.64 ± 3.83, P < 0.01), indicating a possible metabolic contribution to stone risk17. The ZJU index, a composite metabolic score, has been proposed as a potential marker of insulin resistance and metabolic disturbance. However, its clinical threshold for risk stratification remains to be standardized. A cross-sectional analysis based on NHANES data found that, when the ZJU index exceeded 33, each unit increase in the index was associated with an 8% increase in the risk of sarcopenia18. However, the association between the ZJU index and PCOS has not been thoroughly examined. Additionally, Patients with PCOS tend to exhibit decreased follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, leading to an increased LH/FSH ratio, enhanced androgen synthesis by theca cells in the ovaries, and ultimately excessive androgen production19. Therefore, to address this gap, a case-control study including 183 PCOS patients and 1169 controls was designed to investigate the association between the ZJU index and PCOS, and to examine whether the LH/FSH ratio and total testosterone (TT) levels are possible intermediary factors in this relationship.

Overall, the ZJU index provides a comprehensive assessment of early metabolic disturbances, including abnormalities in blood glucose and lipid levels, during the initial stages of PCOS. Consequently, it can be speculated that the ZJU index is associated with the chances of getting PCOS and its application may enhance early risk identification and facilitate timely interventions to prevent severe metabolic and reproductive complications in PCOS.

Methods

Participants

The case-control study comprised 1352 infertile women (183 PCOS and 1169 controls) who underwent in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF-ET) treatment between June 2021 and December 2022 at the Reproductive Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. IVF refers to the fertilization of oocytes outside the body, and ET involves transferring embryos into the uterus. PCOS was diagnosed based on the Rotterdam criteria, which require the presence of at least two out of the following three features: (1) oligo/anovulation, (2) clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and (3) polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) on ultrasound. To ensure accurate classification of the control group, participants were clinically evaluated and confirmed to have no diagnosis of PCOS or other endocrinopathies. In addition, none of the women in the control group met the Rotterdam criteria for PCOS, and no ovulatory dysfunction was identified. Their infertility was attributed exclusively to tubal factors, based on comprehensive clinical and diagnostic assessments. Participants with genetic disorders, immune disorders, and hypertension were excluded in our study. Demographic and clinical information of all subjects was obtained from the electronic medical system. Notably, all eligible control participants during the study period were included to maximize statistical power. Although no matching was conducted, potential confounders such as age, BMI, education level were adjusted for in all statistical analyses. This single-centre, retrospective case-control study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. 20160270). The study used de-identified data, and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee owing to the retrospective design and the anonymized nature of the data. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Laboratory measures

In this study, all participants underwent routine blood tests and biochemical measurements. General characteristics, including age, BMI, type of infertility, passive smoking status, and past medical history, were also collected. Venous blood was sampled from participants in a fasting state early the next morning and sent to the central laboratory of the Clinical Laboratory Department at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University for analysis. All tests were conducted by professional doctors in the Clinical Laboratory Department. The laboratory tests included fasting blood glucose (FBG), aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), lipid markers such as triglycerides (TG) and total cholesterol (TC), along with other biochemical markers. The ZJU index was then calculated by combining BMI, FBG, TG, ALT, and AST levels using the formula: ZJU Index = BMI (kg/m²) + FBG (mmol/L) + TG (mmol/L) + 3× (ALT/AST ratio) + 2 (for women). Insulin resistance was estimated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), which was calculated as fasting insulin (µU/mL) multiplied by fasting glucose (mmol/L), then divided by 22.5.

Baseline levels of FSH, LH, and total testosterone (TT) were measured in serum from venous blood samples collected on the second or third day of each participant’s menstrual cycle.

Statistical analysis

Normality tests were conducted on all numerical variables, and those that followed a normal distribution were presented as means ± SD and analyzed using the t-test. For non-normally distributed variables, data were presented as medians (interquartile ranges) and analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H-test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, and differences between groups were compared using the chi-square test. The ZJU index was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable (quartiles). Logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the association between ZJU levels and PCOS. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model with three knots was performed to explore the linear and nonlinear relationship between the ZJU index and PCOS. To evaluate the discriminatory performance of the ZJU index for identifying PCOS, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the predictive ability of the ZJU index in comparison with BMI and HOMA-IR. A modern mediation analysis was conducted to examine whether serum TT is a possible intermediary factor between the ZJU index and PCOS. Logistic regression was used for the outcome variable (PCOS), and linear regression was used for the mediator (serum total testosterone). The indirect effect and its 95% confidence interval were estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples.

All paths (i.e., X→Y, X→M, and M→Y) were adjusted for the same set of confounders used in the multivariable logistic regression models. These covariates were selected based on statistically significant baseline differences between the PCOS and control groups, including age, passive smoking status, type of infertility, fasting insulin, free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), serum creatinine, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). Notably, triglycerides (TG), body mass index (BMI), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were excluded from adjustment, as they are integral components of the ZJU index-this was to avoid overadjustment and potential attenuation of the observed association between the ZJU index and PCOS.

The mediation proportion was calculated as the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect. To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation to address missing covariate data, under the assumption that data were missing at random (MAR). Multiple imputation was performed using chained equations (MICE), with five imputed datasets generated and combined using Rubin’s rules. Among the 1,352 participants, several covariates had missing values: HDL-C in 164 participants (12.13%), LDL-C in 168 (12.43%), fasting insulin in 40 (2.96%), total cholesterol in 138 (10.21%), blood urea nitrogen in 27 (2.00%), serum creatinine in 14 (1.04%), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) in 27 (2.00%), FT4 in 54 (3.99%), and FT3 in 53 (3.92%) (Supplementary Table 1). Multivariable logistic regression models were re-estimated using the imputed data to evaluate whether the association between the ZJU index and PCOS remained consistent. All data were analyzed using SPSS for Windows (version 27.0) and R (version 25).

The association between the ZJU index and PCOS using binary logistic regression

Binary logistic regression model was used to assess the association between the ZJU index and PCOS, with PCOS presence as the dependent variable. As shown in Table 2, the ZJU index, when analyzed as a continuous variable, was positively associated with PCOS (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05–1.14, P < 0.001) after adjusting for variables such as age, passive smoking status, fasting insulin, infertility type, education level, total cholesterol, Scr, high-density lipoprotein(HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), FT3 and FT4. When the ZJU index was categorized into quartiles, a significant positive trend in the likelihood of having PCOS was observed with increasing quartile levels. Using the lowest quartile (Q1: ZJU index 24.19–31.67; mean ± SD: 29.77 ± 1.36) as the reference, the ORs for PCOS progressively increased across quartiles: Q2 (31.68–34.58; OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.90–3.17, P = 0.105), Q3 (34.59–38.51; OR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.01–3.60, P = 0.045), and Q4 (38.52–54.77; OR = 4.44, 95% CI: 2.27–8.66, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2). These results underscore a dose-dependent association between the ZJU index and the probability of having PCOS among infertile women. These findings highlight a dose-dependent relationship and suggest that ZJU index values above approximately 34.59 may indicate a substantially increased probability of PCOS. Additionally, TT was significantly associated with PCOS (OR = 3.25, 95% CI 2.06–5.12, P < 0.001)(Table 3).

Mediation analyses

Given that a mediator is linked to both the exposure and the outcome, we conducted a mediation analysis to explore whether TT is a possible intermediary factor in the association between the ZJU index and PCOS. The result indicated that TT may be a possible intermediary factor in the association between the ZJU index and PCOS, with an estimated 10.00% of the association potentially explained through an indirect pathway involving TT (P < 0.010) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder, affecting 6–20% of women of reproductive age. It is characterized by polycystic ovaries, hyperandrogenism, insulin resistance, and chronic oligo-anovulation20. Our findings demonstrated a significant positive association between the ZJU index and the chances of getting PCOS (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05–1.14, P < 0.001), indicating that each unit increase in the index corresponds to a 9% increase in the chances of getting PCOS. Although there is no universally accepted clinical cut-off for the ZJU index, its clinical relevance is supported by the observed difference in median values between the PCOS and control groups (37.47 (IQR: 33.19–41.87) vs. 34.25 (IQR: 31.46–37.95)). When the ZJU index was categorized into quartiles, and Q1 (24.19–31.67; mean ± SD: 29.77 ± 1.36) was used as the reference, the chances of getting PCOS were 1.69 (95% CI: 0.90–3.17) in Q2 (31.68–34.58), 1.91 (95% CI: 1.01–3.60) in Q3 (34.59–38.51), and 4.44 (95% CI: 2.27–8.66) in Q4 (38.52–54.77), suggesting a dose-dependent increase, especially above 34.59. This finding suggests that a ZJU index above 34.59 may help clinicians identify infertile women who are more likely to have PCOS, thereby supporting early screening and targeted metabolic assessment in clinical practice. Further studies are warranted to establish clinically meaningful thresholds and validate its predictive performance across diverse populations.

Furthermore, our study found that total testosterone levels may act as a possible mediator in the association between the ZJU index and PCOS, suggesting that hyperandrogenism could be part of the mediating pathway linking the ZJU index and PCOS. In contrast, while the LH/FSH ratio was indeed significantly associated with PCOS, it showed no significant correlation with the ZJU index (β = 0.00, 95% CI: -0.01- 0.01, P = 0.514). According to the established mediation framework21a mediator must be associated with both the exposure and the outcome. Given that the ZJU index and LH/FSH ratio were not significantly related, the LH/FSH ratio did not qualify as a mediator in this model. In summary, while both TT and LH/FSH were associated with PCOS, only TT met the statistical criteria for mediation, suggesting that TT may be a possible intermediary factor in the association between the ZJU index and the chances of having PCOS.

The ZJU index, a novel metabolic indicator that incorporates TG, AST, ALT, BMI, and blood glucose, provides a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic disorders compared to individual biochemical markers22. Previous studies indicated that the ZJU index were positively associated with the risk of kidney stone17. Another study from NHANES indicated that the ZJU index were positively associated with increased risk of gallstones23. A cross-sectional study among middle-aged Chinese adults found that the ZJU index was significantly associated with an increased risk of obstructive sleep apnea syndrome24. Ji et al. found that the ZJU index was a useful indicator for identifying IR in the general Chinese population25. Although IR is known to contribute to the risk of PCOS, the association between the novel insulin resistance indicator -the ZJU index - and PCOS has yet to be explored. Additionally, whether TT is a possible intermediary factor in the association between the ZJU index and PCOS is also yet to be determined. Our study found a strong link between the ZJU index and PCOS, suggesting that higher ZJU index values increase the likelihood of having PCOS. Assessing the ZJU index can help identify at-risk individuals early, enabling timely prevention and treatment.

In addition to the ZJU index, other metabolic indices such as the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and the visceral adiposity index (VAI) have been widely used in PCOS research15. While HOMA-IR is a well-established surrogate for insulin resistance, its reliance on fasting glucose and insulin measurements limits its practicality in routine clinical settings. Additionally, in our ROC curve analysis, the ZJU index demonstrated a stronger discriminatory ability for identifying PCOS than HOMA-IR. This suggests that the ZJU index may serve as a simple yet powerful tool for early identification of women at higher likelihood of having PCOS. Similarly, the VAI, which combines anthropometric (BMI, WC) and metabolic (TG, HDL) parameters, has been linked to PCOS. However, its reliance on both waist circumference and lipid profiling (TG and HDL) significantly limits its clinical interpretability26. Moreover, unlike the ZJU index which incorporates routinely available liver function markers, VAI fails to directly assess hepatic dysfunction - a key feature increasingly implicated in PCOS pathophysiology27. In contrast to these indices, the ZJU index incorporates routinely measured metabolic parameters (e.g., BMI, TG) and liver function markers (e.g., ALT/AST), offering a simpler calculation method and greater potential for widespread clinical adoption. Future studies are warranted to compare its predictive performance directly with other indices and to determine clinically meaningful thresholds.

The mechanism underlying PCOS remains largely uncertain. However, emerging studies suggest that IR and hyperandrogenism plays an essential role in PCOS28. It is widely recognized that IR and hyperinsulinemia play a crucial role in the molecular mechanisms driving the endocrine hypersecretion characteristic of PCOS. IR can stimulate the ovaries to produce more androgens and reduce the production of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) in the liver29. This reduction in SHBG levels increases the amount of free testosterone available in the body, which can contribute to hyperandrogenism. These studies showed that IR can lead to hyperandrogenism. Additionally, some studies also indicated that lowering fasting insulin levels in PCOS patients treated with insulin-sensitizing medication may reduce androgenemia and improve ovarian function30. These studies indicate that addressing IR could be a crucial approach to managing hyperandrogenism in women with PCOS. Overall, our findings support the ZJU index as a useful tool for early PCOS screening and highlight the need for PCOS patients to regulate blood glucose, lipids, and weight through exercise and diet for timely management. To further confirm the ZJU index’s role in PCOS diagnosis, larger multi-center cohort studies are needed. Additionally, the molecular mechanisms underlying glucose and lipid metabolism disorders and hypoxia-induced inflammation in PCOS remain unclear and require further investigation.

Several advantages and limitations should be acknowledged in this study. It is the first to establish a link between the ZJU index and PCOS, confirming its predictive potential. Additionally, the findings indicate a dose - dependent relationship, where higher ZJU index values are associated with an increased PCOS. However, certain limitations exist. First, as a case-control study, it cannot establish causality. Second, reverse causation cannot be ruled out in our study. Since PCOS is often associated with metabolic dysfunction-including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and hepatic abnormalities-it is possible that the condition itself contributes to increased ZJU index values. Therefore, the observed association may also reflect the metabolic consequences of PCOS, rather than its antecedents. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the directionality of this relationship. Third, the small sample size and single-center design limit its generalizability. Fourth, the study population consisted exclusively of infertile women undergoing IVF, with controls limited to those with tubal-factor infertility. This may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader population, as both PCOS patients requiring IVF and tubal-factor controls may not represent the typical metabolic profiles seen in the general population. Lastly, the absence of data on diet and exercise may influence biochemical markers such as lipids and blood glucose. Lastly, a major limitation of this study is the lack of data on important lifestyle factors and family history due to its retrospective design. Information on physical activity, dietary habits, and family history of metabolic or ovarian disorders was not available in the medical records and thus could not be included in the analysis. These factors may influence both metabolic status and PCOS, and their absence may result in residual confounding. Future prospective studies incorporating comprehensive lifestyle and familial data are warranted to validate and expand upon our findings.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated a significant correlation between the ZJU index and PCOS, with higher ZJU index linked to increased chances of getting PCOS. The ZJU index is a suitable indicator for identifying individuals with PCOS among the infertile population. The findings support clinicians in using the ZJU index to screen and manage PCOS patients requiring follow-up. Additionally, promoting patient self-management through health education and nursing interventions can help reduce PCOS by maintaining normal weight, blood glucose, lipids, and liver function.
