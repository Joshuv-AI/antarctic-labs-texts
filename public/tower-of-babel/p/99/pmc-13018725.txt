High‐intensity and blackout drinking impact on next‐day cognitive functioning among college‐attending young adults
Authors: Ashley N. Linden‐Carmichael, Jacqueline Mogle, Sara E. Miller, Jennifer L. Shipley, Stephen J. Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYoung adults (ages ~ 18–25) are at highest risk of heavy alcohol use relative to other age groups. Recent laboratory evidence revealed that alcohol use behavior is associated with brain morphometry among young adults. Scant research has focused on identifying whether young adults experience functional cognitive consequences after drinking and alcohol‐induced memory loss (“blackout drinking”) events. We collected intensive 21‐day data to examine acute effects of any alcohol use and higher risk use behavior on next‐day cognitive functioning.MethodParticipants were 304 (79% female) college‐attending young adults who reported recent heavy drinking and blackout drinking. Participants completed a baseline survey and up to 21 daily diaries assessing prior‐day alcohol use, self‐reported cognition (prospective/retrospective memory lapses, general cognitive lapses), and performance‐based cognition.ResultsMultilevel models examined associations between any alcohol use, number of drinks, high‐intensity drinking (8+/10+ drinks in a sitting for females/males), and blackout drinking on multiple next‐day cognitive outcomes. All day‐level drinking indicators were associated with a higher likelihood of next‐day cognitive lapses. High‐intensity drinking and blackout drinking were both associated with higher odds of retrospective memory lapses, and high‐intensity drinking was associated with higher odds of prospective memory lapses. Blackout drinking was associated with poorer next‐day working memory with manipulation. All other drinking indicators were unassociated with performance‐based cognition.ConclusionsOverall, while high‐intensity drinking and blackout drinking were both associated with more self‐reported retrospective memory lapses, only high‐intensity drinking was linked with self‐reported prospective memory lapses and only blackout drinking was linked to performance‐based cognition. These findings generally indicate that young adults experienced more cognitive lapses the morning after intense drinking and blackout drinking events. As a key indicator of longer‐term cognitive outcomes is observed functional impacts in young adulthood, early prevention and intervention in this age group is warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Young adults (approximate ages 18–25 years) report the highest rates of heavy alcohol use relative to any other age group (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). More than one‐quarter of young adults report past‐two‐week binge drinking (consuming five or more drinks in a row), and 9% of young adults report past‐two‐week high‐intensity drinking (consuming 10 or more drinks in a row; Patrick et al., 2024). An estimated 5.1 million young adults met criteria for a past‐year alcohol use disorder in 2023 (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). Even more common are acute negative outcomes of heavy drinking, including social‐interpersonal consequences, adverse physical effects, and academic/occupational consequences (White & Hingson, 2013).

One significant yet understudied health outcome of heavy alcohol use is impairment of cognitive functioning. The effects of years of heavy alcohol use on late life cognitive functioning are well‐documented (e.g., poorer working memory, attention, episodic memory; Coppens et al., 2019; Pitel et al., 2007, 2009) but alcohol‐related cognitive consequences remain understudied among young adults. Emerging experimental work supports that heavy alcohol use may be proximally associated with poorer, postintoxication cognitive performance among young adults (Field et al., 2008; Gunn et al., 2018) such as working memory (Day et al., 2015), attention (McKinney et al., 2012), and executive functioning (Finnigan et al., 2005). One recent study focused on the role of alcohol use on cognition in a heavy drinking event (i.e., a 21st birthday)—during which young adults had an average peak estimated blood alcohol concentration of 0.22–0.23 g/dL—and found decreased volume in the posterior and central corpus callosum within a few days of the event after adjusting for substance use history (Hua et al., 2020). As these brain structures are key to communication between hemispheres, such a heavy drinking event may have important functional consequences (Hearne et al., 2019).

Although findings from these experimental studies provide evidence for associations between heavy drinking and short‐term structural brain changes, there have been recent calls for exploring whether young adults exhibit effects of heavy alcohol use on their cognitive functioning in daily life (Pearlson, 2021). Young adults who experience deficits in daily cognitive functioning may reduce engagement in cognitively stimulating activities (e.g., social activities), establishing lifelong patterns that lead to lower cognitive functioning. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) is an ideal approach for measuring such granular associations between alcohol use and daily cognitive functioning including performance‐based tests (e.g., executive functioning, memory) and self‐reported measures (e.g., attentional lapses, difficulty concentrating) while reducing recall biases and teasing apart day‐ from person‐level associations (Piasecki, 2019; Sliwinski et al., 2018). Few studies have examined daily links between alcohol use and cognitive functioning. This small body of work has documented that among young adults, heavier alcohol use was associated with poorer next‐day attention and concentration (Polak & Conner, 2012) and any alcohol use (relative to no use) was associated with poorer working memory (Schuster et al., 2016). Similar findings were observed among middle‐aged and older adults—such that they were more likely to report memory lapses on days when they consumed more alcohol than usual (Miller et al., 2023).

While prior work suggests acute effects of alcohol use on cognitive functioning, there are several gaps in our knowledge and limitations of prior work that remain. First, prior work has focused exclusively on either performance‐based measures of cognitive functioning (Schuster et al., 2016) or self‐reported measures (Polak & Conner, 2012). A more comprehensive assessment of cognitive functioning inclusive of both performance‐based and self‐report measures would clarify the impacts of alcohol use across multiple domains of cognitive functioning. Commonly used indicators of self‐reported cognitive functioning often capture one's ability to meet daily cognitive demands (e.g., decision making, paying attention) by assessing day‐to‐day lapses of memory (retrospective, prospective) and other general cognitive lapses (García de la Garza et al., 2025; Mogle et al., 2019). Retrospective memory is considered a fundamental resource that stores and recalls information such as words and experiences (e.g., where you put your keys) that are necessary for everyday functioning. In contrast, prospective memory monitors your daily to‐do list and responds to prompts in the environment to complete a particular task at the correct time (e.g., remembering to take a medication at a specific time). However, while memory is a key component of everyday cognitive functioning, a number of other types of cognitive lapses are known to occur on a daily basis. Lapses in attention and difficulties with planning activities can also substantively impact daily functioning but have cognitive underpinnings separable from memory. Together, these aspects of daily cognition were selected to assess a broad range of potential lapses related to cognition that could relate to drinking behavior (Linden‐Carmichael, Chiang, et al., 2023).

Second, limited prior work has examined the impact of higher risk alcohol use events on cognitive functioning, such as blackout drinking and high‐intensity drinking. Extant experimental work has documented that the most profound impacts of a single heavy alcohol use episode on structural brain changes occurred when individuals experienced a blackout (Hua et al., 2020). Blackout drinking—or alcohol‐induced memory loss during a drinking episode—is common among young adults who consume alcohol (~50% lifetime prevalence; Wetherill & Fromme, 2016) and is a concerning drinking behavior given the potential for experiencing additional harms (e.g., increased risk for physical injury, sexual assault, overdose; Hingson et al., 2016; Merrill et al., 2019). Similarly, high‐intensity drinking—or consuming 8+/10+ drinks in one sitting for females/males—is prevalent among approximately 1 in 10 young adults (Patrick et al., 2024) and is associated with heightened risks relative to lower drinking intensities (e.g., Evans‐Polce et al., 2022; Linden‐Carmichael et al., 2018; Patrick & Terry‐McElrath, 2021). In a separate sample from this study, our team recently identified cross‐sectional associations between greater frequency of blacking out and poorer cognitive functioning among young adults (Linden‐Carmichael, Mogle, & Miller, 2023), but within‐individual, daily associations between blackout or high‐intensity drinking and cognitive functioning have been insufficiently examined. Blackout drinking and high‐intensity drinking each may have significant, yet differential effects on cognitive processes. Blackout drinking is thought to disrupt neurobiological mechanisms of the hippocampus, specifically the process of encoding short‐term memories to long‐term memories thus impacting retrospective, working memory abilities such as updating and storage, as well as episodic memory (Wetherill & Fromme, 2016; White, 2003). High‐intensity drinking has been found to affect the prefrontal cortex, including executive functions such as prospective memory, attention, planning, and working memory abilities such as manipulation (Cservenka & Brumback, 2017).

Finally—relatedly—most prior EMA and diary studies examining alcohol use and cognition have captured behavior across very short windows of time (7 or 8 days; Miller et al., 2023; Schuster et al., 2016). Sampling behavior across a longer period of time from a higher risk population of young adults (i.e., engage in frequent, higher‐risk alcohol use) would increase the probability of multiple types of drinking episodes (e.g., high‐intensity drinking, blackout drinking) and would thus better tease apart day‐ relative to person‐level effects.

Current study

The goal of this study was to explore acute, next‐day, postintoxication effects of alcohol use on cognitive functioning among young adults. We collected intensive EMA data across a 21‐day period to explore multiple indices of alcohol use and their association with both self‐reported and performance‐based measures of cognitive functioning. Specifically, this study had two aims. First, we aimed to examine day‐level associations between multiple alcohol‐use indicators, including alcohol use (none vs. any), number of alcoholic drinks consumed, high‐intensity drinking (<8/<10 drinks for females/males vs. 8+/10+ drinks), and blackout drinking behavior (no blackout episode vs. blackout episode), and self‐reported daily lapses including prospective memory, retrospective memory, and general cognition. We hypothesized that each day‐level drinking indicator would be significantly associated with more next‐day daily lapses. Second, we aimed to examine day‐level associations between each indicator of alcohol use and performance‐based cognitive outcomes, including working memory with updating, executive functioning, and working memory with manipulation. We hypothesized that each day‐level drinking indicator would be significantly associated with poorer next‐day performance‐based cognitive outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants and procedure

Full study information is provided in our team's study protocol paper (Linden‐Carmichael et al., 2025). Participants were college students aged 18–25 years residing in the Eastern time zone of the United States. To be eligible, participants had to report engaging in heavy episodic drinking (4+/5+ drinks in one sitting for females/males) at least twice in a typical month and to have experienced at least one blackout drinking episode within the past year. Participants first completed a baseline survey via REDCap, followed by 21 consecutive days of online EMA surveys on Qualtrics and brief performance‐based cognitive assessments conducted on the Gorilla Experiment Builder. Survey links were sent via email and text message at 11 a.m., 1 p.m., 3 p.m., and 5 p.m. daily, with participants having 60 min to complete each survey and set of cognitive tasks. Compensation for full study participation totaled $193. Data collection occurred from November 2023 to May 2024. Procedures were approved by the university's institutional review board. Of the 304 eligible participants, 97.7% (n = 297) completed at least one EMA survey and cognitive assessment and were included in the final sample. Participant demographics are presented in Table 1.

Alcohol use

At the first prompt each day, prior‐day substance use was captured by instructing participants to select from a comprehensive list all substances they had used the prior day. When participants endorsed any alcohol use (1 = Yes; 0 = No), they were provided example pictures of standard alcoholic drinks and were asked to report the total number of drinks they had consumed yesterday (range = 0–15+). High‐intensity drinking was operationalized as consuming 8+ drinks for females or 10+ drinks for males in a day (1 = Yes; 0 = No). Participants who endorsed any alcohol use were also asked, “Did any of the following things happen to you as a result of your using alcohol or using other substances yesterday?” with 13 potential positive and negative consequences (Lee et al., 2017). Blackout drinking (1 = Yes; 0 = No) was evaluated with the consequence item, “I couldn't remember what I did/forgetting what I did.”

Self‐reports of cognitive lapses

Self‐reports of cognitive lapses were assessed at all prompts using a version of the Daily Memory Lapses Checklist (Mogle et al., 2019). The Daily Memory Lapses Checklist captures lapses of prospective memory (i.e., memory for planned events), lapses of retrospective memory (i.e., memory for previously learned information), and other nonmemory cognitive lapses (i.e., difficulties with planning or attention).

Participants selected from a list of seven prospective lapses in response to the question, “During the past two hours, did you forget something you had to do?” (e.g., “to take medicine or take medicine on time”). If participants endorsed any of the items at any prompt throughout the day, they were coded as having experienced at least one prospective memory lapse that day (1 = Yes; 0 = No). Similarly, participants were coded as having experienced at least one retrospective memory lapse that day (1 = Yes; 0 = No) if they endorsed forgetting any of the five retrospective lapse items (e.g., “someone's name”) at any prompt that day. Finally, participants indicated at each prompt any nonmemory cognitive lapses they had experienced during the past 2 h from a list of seven problems (e.g., “During the past two hours did you have any problems with…making decisions”). When participants endorsed any of the seven cognitive lapses at any prompt that day, they were coded as having experienced at least one general cognitive lapse that day (1 = Yes; 0 = No).

Performance‐based cognition

After completing the Qualtrics surveys at each prompt, participants were automatically routed into completing objective, performance‐based cognitive assessments of working memory with updating, executive functioning, and working memory with manipulation. A modified spatial n‐back task was used to evaluate working memory with updating by requiring participants to recognize whether the current stimulus in a sequence matched the stimulus presented n‐steps earlier (Kirchner, 1958). Performance was assessed based on the average reaction time for accurate responses.

Executive functioning was evaluated using a modified Flanker inhibition task, which assesses selective attention and inhibitory control by measuring how quickly participants respond to a target stimulus while ignoring adjacent flanker stimuli (Eriksen & Eriksen, 1974). Cost switching was constructed indicating average difference in reaction time between trials in which the target and flanker stimuli were congruent (i.e., no conflicting information) versus incongruent (i.e., flanker stimuli conflicted with the target stimulus).

Working memory with manipulation was evaluated using a modified backward number span task, in which participants were asked to recall increasingly long sequences of numbers in the reverse order that they had been presented (Hilbert et al., 2015). Performance was assessed based on the total number of numbers correctly recalled.

Baseline covariates

Baseline covariates included biological sex (1 = Male; 0 = Female).

Statistical analysis

We implemented multilevel modeling in SASv9.4. EMA prompts were aggregated to reflect once‐daily measures of yesterday's alcohol use (any, number of drinks, high‐intensity drinking, blackout drinking) and today's performance‐based and self‐reported cognition. Analyses were completed in a series of steps. We first examined descriptive statistics across all measures to screen for outliers. We next computed daily summary variables to examine how different types of drinking related to cognitive functioning the following day. We addressed our primary hypotheses using general and generalized multilevel modeling (MLM) depending on the scale of the outcome variable. MLM was used as data are nested as in this study with days nested in persons. In all models, we specified random intercepts and fixed slopes. General MLMs were fit to our continuous outcomes using a normal distribution (identity link) and generalized MLMs were fit our binary outcomes using a binomial distribution (logit link). A Laplace Maximum Likelihood Approximation estimator was used across all models for consistency in model fitting. We considered including alpha corrections for multiple comparisons, but elected not to given (1) the study represents the primary study findings from this data collection with a prior specified hypotheses and, as noted by Armstrong (2014), Bonferroni and other alpha corrections should not be used routinely and only if a large number of tests are conducted without preplanned hypotheses; and (2) alpha corrections control Type I error rate and thus contribute to the likelihood of Type II errors (Perneger, 1998; Rothman, 1990). In our first model, we examined the unadjusted effect of daily drinking behavior on cognitive functioning. We then added our covariates of interest at Level 1 (i.e., “social” weekend [Thursday–Saturday] vs. weekday [Sunday–Wednesday]; Del Boca et al., 2004) and Level 2 (i.e., sex) to determine whether any identified associations were impacted by the inclusion of day‐ or person‐level characteristics. Baseline cognitive ability was examined as a Level 2 (person‐level) covariate during model building. This variable did not significantly contribute to study outcomes; thus, for parsimony, we excluded this variable from our final models. To account for person‐level differences in drinking behavior, individuals' daily alcohol use reports were averaged across the study period and included as Level 2 predictors. As such, all models were fit with a within‐person Level 1 predictor (e.g., any drinking behavior) along with the between‐person average (e.g., proportion of drinking days). Continuous variables were grand‐mean‐centered and categorical variables used consistent reference groups across models (i.e., males for sex; weekday for day of week). Effect sizes were computed as odds ratios where outcomes were categorical or as a standardized difference across drinking days v. nondrinking days divided by the within‐person standard deviation for continuous outcomes.

For retrospective memory lapses, any alcohol use and total number of drinks on a given day were not related to greater likelihood of lapses on the following day (OR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.88–1.17; OR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.99–1.04). Both daily high‐intensity and blackout drinking were associated with greater likelihood of next‐day retrospective memory lapses. Days following a high‐intensity drinking episode were associated with a 75% greater likelihood of a lapse (OR = 1.75; 95% CI: 1.24–2.47), and days following a blackout drinking episode were associated with a 61% greater likelihood of a lapse (OR = 1.61; 95% CI: 1.21–2.14).

For general cognitive lapses, all daily drinking indicators were associated with a greater likelihood of next‐day cognitive lapses. Any drinking the previous day was associated with 14% greater likelihood of cognitive lapses the following day relative to days without any alcohol use (OR = 1.14; 95% CI: 1.04–1.24), and each additional drink on a given day was associated with a 5% increase in likelihood of any next‐day cognitive lapses (OR = 1.05; 95% CI: 1.03–1.07). Consistent with this, high‐intensity drinking was associated with twice the likelihood of reporting cognitive lapses the next day (OR = 2.32; 95% CI: 1.91–2.83). The effect of a blackout drinking episode was smaller but indicated a 40% greater likelihood of any cognitive lapses on days following a blackout (OR = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.15–1.71).

DISCUSSION

The current study tested daily associations between alcohol use behavior and cognitive functioning across multiple indices of use (any use, number of drinks, high‐intensity drinking, blackout drinking) and cognition (self‐reported cognitive lapses and performance‐based cognitive assessments). Overall, findings revealed that while the presence of any alcohol use relative to no alcohol use and number of drinks (measured continuously) were unassociated with most indicators of cognitive functioning, days with high‐intensity drinking and blackout drinking were linked with significantly greater likelihood of self‐reported retrospective memory lapses (i.e., forgetting someone's name). In addition, high‐intensity drinking days were also associated with greater likelihood of self‐reported prospective memory lapses (i.e., forgetting to take a medication). All drinking indicators were associated with poorer next‐day general cognitive lapses. Regarding performance‐based, objective cognitive assessments, only blackout drinking episodes were linked with poorer next‐day working memory with manipulation (e.g., forgetting the steps to a recipe while cooking a meal).

There may be several reasons as to why several drinking indicators were significantly associated with self‐reported cognitive lapses yet only blackout drinking was associated with one performance‐based cognitive indicator. First, our self‐report and performance‐based measures are assessing different spheres of cognition. Self‐reported measures of cognition are assessing cognitive lapses more globally, whereas performance‐based measures are tapping directly into specific features of cognition (e.g., working memory with updating). The performance‐based measures assess a subset of the processes that underlie the abilities needed to complete daily cognition as assessed by the self‐reported lapse items; the impacts of heavier alcohol use on cognition may only be more observable on a global level. Further evidence of this is seen at person‐level associations between some drinking indicators and performance‐based cognitive outcomes: although day‐level number of drinks were unassociated with each performance‐based measure, between‐person findings suggest that, on average, individuals who consumed more drinks across the study period exhibited poorer performance‐based cognition. Second, our findings that high‐intensity drinking was more consistently associated with self‐reported measures than blackout drinking and that only blackout drinking was linked to performance‐based cognition may be related to their differential impact on the brain (e.g., Cservenka & Brumback, 2017; White, 2003). This may play out in daily life such that blackout drinking has a more subtle effect on cognition compared to high‐intensity drinking, which more globally impacts cognition. Third, the lack of findings on performance‐based measures may be reflective of our sample of young adults. It is possible differences may be small or negligible because young adults are less sensitive to the impacts of heavy alcohol use and because their brain is more adaptable, they may recover more quickly than older adult samples (Gazerani, 2025). As limited work has focused on alcohol use and cognition in young adulthood, further measurement and investigation is warranted. Nevertheless, as described more below, significant findings of higher‐risk alcohol use occasions and self‐reported lapses is important as this suggests that participants noticed the impacts of a heavy and/or blackout drinking occasion on their cognitive functioning. This may represent an incredibly useful motivational intervention tool in future work.

Study findings highlight nuance in daily associations between alcohol use and next‐day cognitive functioning. Prior cross‐sectional research has found that young adults who report more frequent blackout drinking episodes report more frequent memory lapses and cognitive lapses (Linden‐Carmichael, Mogle, & Miller, 2023). Accordingly, in the current study, blackout drinking was linked with greater odds of next‐day retrospective memory lapses and general cognitive lapses. On the other hand, findings that any alcohol use (vs. no use) and number of drinks were linked with general cognitive lapses, but not memory lapses, contrasts with some other work on older adult samples (e.g., Miller et al., 2023). It may be that certain elements of everyday cognitive functioning (e.g., attention, decision making) are more sensitive than memory to low levels of alcohol use among young adults, which highlights an important area of future study. Notably, study findings also suggest greater self‐reported cognitive impairment after higher‐risk drinking episodes (high‐intensity drinking, blackout drinking). This finding is consistent with EMA study findings among college students, in which impairments in self‐reported cognitive functioning were observed after heavy drinking and extreme drinking, but not after low‐risk drinking (i.e., 1–4 standard drinks for women and 1–6 for men) (Polak & Conner, 2012). It is worth noting that our sample included a higher risk sample of young adults who engaged in recent and frequent higher risk alcohol use. Although we controlled for participants' alcohol use severity score, these findings may underscore that young adults are resilient cognitively, but at particularly heavy and higher risk use occasions, young adults have observable impacts on next‐day cognition. As both high‐intensity drinking and blackout drinking are prevalent among young adults who consume alcohol (Patrick et al., 2024; Wetherill & Fromme, 2016), these findings provide important insight into the impact of alcohol use on daily cognitive functioning and highlight high‐intensity and blackout drinking as having a unique impact on cognitive functioning and represent key behavioral targets for intervention.

When considered in conjunction with previous research, the current findings may provide important context for understanding how alcohol use impacts not only day‐to‐day cognitive functioning but also the underlying brain structures and functions that support it. As noted above, prior work suggests that even a single episode of significant alcohol consumption is associated with measurable changes in brain structure in young adults (Hua et al., 2020), yet very little is known about the effects of more extended patterns of alcohol use of varying intensities on the brain and cognition in this population. Future work integrating EMA methods similar to those used in the current study with neuroimaging techniques would provide valuable insight into the mechanisms linking alcohol use and cognitive functioning, including concurrent structural and functional brain changes that emerge over time.

Implications and future directions

Findings may have important implications for prevention and intervention programming. Results from the current study suggest that among young adults with a recent history of blackout drinking and heavy drinking, all drinking indicators are associated with higher odds of next‐day general cognitive lapses, but involving high‐intensity drinking and blackout drinking are more consistently linked with poorer, self‐reported cognitive functioning. Given the primary cognitive functioning outcomes associated with the heaviest levels of alcohol use include self‐reported experiences, these findings may have helpful benefits as an intervention tool. Specifically, momentary interventions could incorporate timely feedback on young adults' self‐reported prior‐day alcohol use on days with high‐intensity or blackout drinking and current cognitive functioning. As a future direction, day‐level moderators, such as hangover symptoms, mood, sleep, and prior day cannabis and other substance use (e.g., Devenney et al., 2019; Scott et al., 2018; Stephens et al., 2008; Townshend & Duka, 2005; van Schrojenstein Lantman et al., 2017; Wade et al., 2020), may affect these associations, and momentary intervention content can be tailored to participants' own risk factors and delivered in moments of greatest vulnerability. Similarly, intervention content on typical drinking patterns and personal risk for cognitive concerns may be incorporated into existing brief motivational interventions tailored to young adults. Person‐level variables that independently and synergistically impact alcohol use and cognition (e.g., sex, alcohol use severity, general cognitive functioning; e.g., Latvala et al., 2009; Verplaetse et al., 2020) should be used to identify higher‐risk groups in need of targeted interventions.

Following recent calls to include blackout‐specific content in preventive interventions (Wetherill & Fromme, 2016), personalized information on typical drinking patterns, including blackout drinking, could maximize intervention impact. For some young adults, blackout drinking is associated with later drinking‐related consequences (Read et al., 2013); incorporating performance‐based and self‐reports of deleterious outcomes of blackout drinking may help detect and intervene on problematic alcohol use patterns earlier. Given that brief alcohol interventions are more effective among individuals who have experienced a blackout (Miller et al., 2018), and that interventions incorporating blackouts are estimated to save ~$500 k/year in emergency department visits (Mundt & Zakletskaia, 2012), addressing within‐person risks of blackouts and other problematic alcohol use behaviors appears promising and impactful.

Limitations

There are several limitations that should be noted. First, the current study focuses on college‐attending young adults who are predominately non‐Hispanic White and assigned female sex at birth. Noncollege‐attending young adults and young adults from minoritized communities represent a sizeable and underserved population of young adults. Further, given the goal of the current study was in part to examine heavy alcohol use and blackout experiences in daily life, only participants with frequent engagement in heavy episodic drinking and recent blackout history were included. To increase generalizability of findings, future research should replicate study findings in a more representative sample of college‐ and noncollege‐attending young adults who consume alcohol as well as heavy drinkers with no history of blackouts or individuals who experience blackouts and less frequent heavy drinking. Second, many of our assessment tools relied on participant self‐report, which may be subject to recall biases or social desirability. Participants' recall may be particularly impacted on blackout drinking days given memory loss is a defining feature of blackout drinking. Use of wearable alcohol sensors and other passive devices may be a useful addition in future work focused on blackout drinking. Related, it is possible that our study design did not capture all self‐reported cognitive lapses experienced—for example, participants may have experienced a lapse outside of the data collection period (11 a.m. to 5 p.m.) or participants may have experienced a lapse but did not complete the EMA survey. Examining the accumulation of effects of alcohol use on cognition throughout a day and across days, within an individual represents an important next step. Third, our assessment of blackout drinking did not distinguish between en bloc and fragmentary blackouts (Miller et al., 2018). Blackout drinking experiences are quite heterogeneous among young adults (Linden‐Carmichael, Chiang, et al., 2023), and a more comprehensive assessment of blackout experiences should be included in future work. Fourth, the performance‐based assessment of cognition targeted specific processes thought to be impacted by alcohol use (e.g., working memory). It is possible that other processes, such as associative memory or sustained attention, are more likely to be impacted in young adults. Further, the highly educated sample and the selection for risky drinking behaviors may have led to the counterintuitive findings at the between‐person level for the performance‐based tasks. Individuals who are better able to recover from riskier drinking to maintain engagement in a college‐level education may have better working memory functioning compared to those in other demographic groups. This could also account for the lack of a daily impact on specific processes in working memory. Future work exploring these relationships in noncollege samples would contribute to this evidence. Finally, given the role of sleep and cognitive functioning (Sewell et al., 2021), baseline sleep may be a critical variable to consider in future work examining the role of substance use on cognition.

CONCLUSIONS

Building from experimental, laboratory work documenting that a single heavy drinking episode has been associated with short‐term structural changes in the brain among young adults, the current EMA study found that high‐intensity drinking occasions and blackout drinking episodes were associated with poorer cognitive functioning (retrospective memory lapses, prospective memory lapses, poorer performance‐based working memory with manipulation) in daily life and all drinking indicators were associated with higher odds of next‐day cognitive lapses. Young adults' ability to self‐report—or notice—such changes may represent a critical momentary intervention tool to incorporate in future real‐time intervention. Future work would benefit from building upon this work to explore longer term associations and accumulating effects of alcohol use on cognitive functioning into midlife.
