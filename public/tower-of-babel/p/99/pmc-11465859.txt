Targeted regulation of 5-aminolevulinic acid enhances flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins accumulation in Vitis davidii callus
Authors: Chengchun Lai, Jing Zhang, Gongti Lai, Liyuan He, Heng Xu, Siyu Li, Jianmei Che, Qi Wang, Xuefang Guan, Juqing Huang, Pufu Lai, Guixin Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpine grape (Vitis davidii) is a promising source of high-quality anthocyanins, with vast potential for application in food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries. However, their availability is limited by resource constraints. Plant cell culture has emerged as a valuable approach for anthocyanin production and serves as an ideal model to investigate the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis. Elicitors are employed to achieve targeted enhancement of anthocyanin biosynthesis. The present study investigated the impact of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) as an elicitor on the accumulation of anthocyanins and flavonoids during spine grape callus growth. Specifically, we examined the effects of ALA on anthocyanin and its component accumulation in callus, and biosynthetic anthocyanin gene expression.ResultsALA at 25 µg/L increased the biomass of spine grape callus. ALA induction enhanced the levels of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in callus, with maximum values reaching 911.11 mg/100 g DW, 604.60 mg/100 g DW, and 5357.00 mg/100 g DW, respectively, after callus culture for 45 days. Notably, those levels were 1.47-, 1.93- and 1.83-fold higher than controls. ALA induction modulated the flavonoid profile, and among 97 differential flavonoid metabolites differing from controls, 77 were upregulated and 20 were downregulated. Six kinds of anthocyanins, namely cyanidin (8), delphinidin (6), peonidin (5), malvidin (4), petunidin (3) and pelargonidin (3), were detected in callus, with peonidin most abundant. Compared with controls, anthocyanin components were increased in ALA-treated callus. The key genes PAL1, PAL2, PAL4, CHI, CHS3, F3’H, F3H, FLS, DFR, UFGT, MYBA1, LDOX, OMT3, GT1 and ACT involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis were upregulated following ALA treatment, resulting in anthocyanin accumulation.ConclusionThis study revealed a novel mode of ALA-mediated promotion of plant anthocyanin biosynthesis and accumulation at the cellular level, and a strategy for enhancing anthocyanin content in spine grape callus. The findings advance commercial-scale production of anthocyanins via spine grape callus culture. we also explored the accumulation patterns of flavonoids and anthocyanins under ALA treatment. Augmentation of anthocyanins coincided with elevated expression levels of most genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis within spine grape callus following ALA treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05667-4.

Background

Spine grape (Vitis davidii Foëx.) is a wild grape species of the East Asian group of the Vitaceae family, possessing excellent edibility and processing characteristics [1]. It is rich in phenolic compounds such as flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins, and exhibits higher anthocyanin content than most cultivated grapes [2, 3]. Anthocyanins are responsible for red, purple and blue pigmentation in flowers, fruits and vegetables [4, 5]. They serve as indicators of both external colour and internal quality in plant harvesting organs [6], playing a pivotal role in enhancing the overall quality of harvested organs and their processed products [7]. Anthocyanins also confer significant human health benefits by mitigating chronic diseases [8, 9] through their potent free-radical scavenging and antioxidant activities [10]. There is increasing interest in using anthocyanins and their metabolites as food colorants [11], dietary supplements [12] and antioxidants [13] in the food, pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries [14].

The most common sources of anthocyanins and their metabolites include waste grape skins from the wine industry, black carrots, red cabbage, sweet potato and berries [15]. Among them, the main sources of anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins on the market are derived from grapes and their seeds [16]. However, anthocyanins from plant sources differ quantitatively and qualitatively due to factors such as plant growth seasons [17], geographical locations [18], and varieties [19]. Several biotechnology strategies have been employed to increase the production of anthocyanins and achieve stable annual yields. Among these strategies, producing anthocyanins through in vitro cell/callus culture is considered a promising approach with good application prospects [15, 20]. To this end, we established a callus cell line derived from spine grape with the capacity to synthesise anthocyanins [21]. Although there has been some research on the production of secondary metabolites using grape cell/callus culture [22–24], like other plant cell culture approaches, there remain major obstacles to commercialisation, especially the regulation of anthocyanins and other secondary metabolites [25].

Elicitation is considered as the most practically feasible strategy for increasing the production of desirable anthocyanins from cells, organs and plant systems [26, 27]. For instance, levels of anthocyanins in some plants and their cells can be enhanced using elicitors such as ethephon [28], salicylic acid [29] and methyl jasmonate [30], which act as molecular signals in plant stress responses. The natural, non-toxic, residue-free phytohormone 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) acts as a plant growth regulator in various organisms [31]. Previous researches have demonstrated a significant role for ALA as an elicitor in enhancing crop quality and yield [32], improving stress tolerance [33, 34], and regulating plant cell metabolism [35], especially enhancing fruit pigmentation [36, 37]. However, researches on the effects of ALA on pigment biosynthesis have primarily focused on their content and production [38–40], few studies have explored the effects of ALA on the composition of anthocyanins, and the stimulatory mechanism remains poorly understood.

In the present study, we investigated the potential role of ALA as an elicitor for enhancing anthocyanin content in spine grape callus. Anthocyanin composition was monitored to assess the impact of ALA induction on the accumulation of different forms. In parallel, flavonoid and proanthocyanidin levels were measured, along with accumulation of differential metabolites within the flavonoid pathway. We also evaluated the effects of ALA on callus growth, and the expression patterns of key genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis pathways. Through integrated analysis of gene expression and anthocyanin metabolites, we provide novel insights into the targeted regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis and accumulation patterns in spine grape callus.

Methods

Spine grape callus and treatments

Plant materials utilised in this study were derived from a spine grape red callus cell line, which was induced and screened from immature spine grape embryos, and has been long-term subcultured and maintained in our laboratory [21]. Murashige and Skoog (MS) [41] solid medium containing 1.0 mg/L 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), 30.0 g/L sucrose and 6.0 g/L agar (pH 5.8) was used for subculture.

For preparation of ALA (98%; Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co.,Ltd., Shanghai, China) mother liquor, ALA was included at 0.5 mg/mL (m/v). ALA powder (50 mg) was accurately weighed and dissolved in ultra-pure water to 100 mL. The ALA mother liquor was sterilised by 0.22 μm sterilising filter. According to the results of our previous study [42], medium with ALA concentrations of 5 µg/L, 10 µg/L, 25 µg/L and 50 µg/L was prepared using sterile ALA mother liquor. Red callus with good growth state and uniform morphology was selected and transferred into medium containing different concentrations of ALA, with six callus clusters of uniform size were inoculated in each bottle, and each callus cluster weighing 0.05 g. Callus cluster were cultured in medium without ALA (0 µg/L) as control. Ten bottles were inoculated in each treatment, with three biological replicates. Culture bottles were placed in a culture room with a light intensity of 2000–2500 lx, a photocycle of 12 h light / 12 h darkness, a temperature of 25 ± 1℃ and a relative humidity of 50−60%. According to preliminary test, red callus treated with various ALA concentrations was cultured for 25, 35 and 45 days. Samples were taken, excess water was absorbed by filter paper, and they were weighed to obtain the fresh weight (FW, g) of callus cultures. Callus biomass (g) was calculated by subtracting the initial inoculation amount from the weight of cultured callus. Callus cultures were divided into ziplock bags and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Some samples were freeze-dried for flavonoid, anthocyanin, proanthocyanidin and metabolome analyses, while some fresh samples were stored at -80℃ for gene expression analysis.

Extraction and content determination of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins

Levels of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in callus were determined according to methods in our previous studies [21]. A dilution gradient of rutin standard was used to generate a standard curve. Standard diluent (300 µL) was pipetted into a 5-mL centrifuge tube and 300 µL 5% NaNO2 solution was added. The solution was shaken well, allowed to stand for 6 min, and 300 µL 10% Al(NO3)3 solution were added. The tube was shaken well again, allowed to stand for 6 min, 400 µL 4% NaOH was added, followed by shaking the solution and allowing to stand for 15 min. Using methanol as the blank, the absorbance was determined at 510 nm. The linear regression equation y = 0.0036x + 0.0012 (where x = rutin concentration, y = absorbance at 510 nm and r2 = 0.9934) was used for calculation. The flavonoid content of each spine grape callus culture was expressed as mg rutin-equivalents per 100 g dry weight (DW) of callus cultures.

Cyanidin-3-glucoside (HPLC purity ≥ 98%, Solarbio, Beijing, China) served as an anthocyanin standard. The anthocyanin content of each callus culture was calculated according to a linear regression equation (y =0.0243x - 0.0089, where x = concentration of cyanidin-3-glucoside, y = ODλ value and r2 = 0.9970), and the anthocyanin content of callus cultures was expressed as mg cyanidin-3-glucoside-equivalents per 100 g DW of callus cultures.

Proanthocyanidin standard (UV ≥ 95%; Solarbio) served as a standard. The linear regression equation of the proanthocyanidin standard was obtained (y = 0.0063x + 0.0158, where x = proanthocyanidin concentration, y = absorbance value, r2 = 0.9937). The proanthocyanidin content of callus cultures was expressed as mg proanthocyanidin-equivalents per 100 g DW of callus cultures.

Analysis of flavonoid metabolites by ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS)

Based on the widely target metabolism technique, flavonoid metabolites were identified from spine grape callus treated with 25 µg/L ALA for 45 days. Callus cultured without ALA (0 µg/L) for 45 days served as a control. For extract preparation, a certain amount of freeze-dried callus culture was crushed into powder using a Mixer Mill MM 400 (Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) and zirconia beads for 90 s at 30 Hz, then 100 mg of powder was weighed and dissolved in 1.2 mL 70% methanol solution. After vortexing six times for 30 s every 30 min, sample solutions were placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C overnight. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, extracts were filtrated through a 0.22 μm microporous filter membrane before UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Extracts of callus cultures were analysed using a Nexera X2 UPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a 6500 Q-TRAP (AB Sciex, CA, USA). Samples of three biological replicates were mixed in equal amounts and three technical repeats were performed. Determination of extracts was carried out as described in previous studies [43]. The analytical conditions were as follows, An Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) was employed with mobile phase solvent A (pure water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). A gradient was applied for elution at a flow rate of 0.35 mL per min, a column oven temperature of 40 °C, and an injection volume of 2 µL. The eluate was subjected to an electrospray ionisation (ESI) triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP) mass spectrometry (MS). Linear ion trap (LIT) and triple quadrupole (QQQ) scans were acquired using an AB6500 Q-TRAP UPLC/MS/MS System equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, operating in positive and negative ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (AB Sciex).

Analysis of anthocyanin composition by UPLC-MS/MS

Based on the target metabolism technique, the anthocyanin compositions of spine grape callus treated with 25 µg/L ALA for 45 days were assessed. Callus cultured without ALA (0 µg/L) for 45 days was used as the control. For extracts preparation, a certain amount of freeze-dried callus cultures was crushed into powder using a Mixer Mill MM 400 (Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) with zirconia beads for 90 s at 30 Hz. Next, 100 mg of powder was weighed and dissolved in 0.5 mL methanol/water/hydrochloric acid (500:500:1, v/v/v) extract solution, vortexed for 5 min, ultrasonicated for 5 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 3 min at 4 °C. The residue was re-extracted by repeating the above steps under the same conditions. Supernatants were collected and filtrated through a 0.22 μm membrane filter before UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Sample extracts were analysed using an ExionLC AD UPLC system (AB Sciex) coupled with a 6500 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB Sciex). Samples of three biological replicates were mixed in equal amounts and three technical repeats were performed. Qualitative and quantitative analysis of extracts were all performed as described previously [44]. The analytical conditions were as follows, A Waters ACQUITY BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) was applied with a solvent system consisting of water (0.1% formic acid) and methanol (0.1% formic acid). Gradient elution was performed with a column oven of 40 °C and an injection volume of 2 µL. LIT and QQQ scans were acquired using a QTRAP 6500 + LC-MS/MS System equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, operating in positive ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (AB Sciex).

Gene expression analysis

Based on transcriptome data for spine grape red callus (unpublished), we selected 21 annotated genes, namely PAL1, PAL2, PAL4, PAL7, CHI, CHS3, F3’H, F3’5’H, F3H, DFR, UFGT, MYBA1, LDOX, ANR, LAR1, LAR2, OMT2, OMT3, ACT, GT1 and FLS, associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis to further investigate changes in transcription levels of these genes following ALA induction. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed using a LightCycler 480II System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Next, cDNAs of each sample were synthesised according to the instructions supplied with PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio Inc., Dalian, China). Florescence detection was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). Primers were designed using Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/). Genes and primer sequences are listed in Table S1. The reaction system and protocol were described in our previous study [45]. The reaction system was 20 µL and each reaction contained TB Green (10 µL), upstream primer (0.8 µL), downstream primer (0.8 µL), template (2 µL) and water (6.4 µL). Thermal cycling included pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 20 s. The crossing point (Cp) value of each amplified sample was automatically calculated by LightCycler 480II Software Version 1.5 software (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to calculate relative expression levels. Relative expression quantification was performed using 60SRP and α-tubulin as reference genes [46].

Statistical analyses

All tests were repeated in triplicate, and the results of each test are expressed as the average of three replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using Duncan’s new multiple range test with DPS software (Zhejiang University, China) [47]. Significantly differentially regulated metabolites between groups were identified based on VIP ≥ 1 and absolute log2FC (fold change) ≥ 1. VIP values were extracted from orthogonal-partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) results, which included score plots and permutation plots, generated using the R package MetaboAnalystR. Data were log transformed (log2) and mean centred before OPLS-DA. In order to avoid overfitting, a permutation test (200 permutations) was performed. Chemometric and multivariate statistical analyses were conducted using multivariate statistical methods such as principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and OPLS-DA. Identified metabolites were annotated using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), and annotated metabolites were then mapped to the KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) [48]. Pathways with significantly differentially regulated metabolites were then subjected to metabolite sets enrichment analysis (MSEA) and their significance was determined by hypergeometric test p-values. For qRT-PCR analysis, error bars represent the standard deviation (SD) of three replicate reactions. Integrated analysis was performed using the O2PLS method [49]. OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, MA, USA) for Windows was used for visualising results.

Results

Effects of different ALA concentrations on the growth and proliferation of spine grape callus

The surface layer of callus exhibited a slight reddish hue after 25 days of culture, which intensified and covered the entire callus surface by day 35. After 45 days, callus displayed a deep red coloration with the emergence of some dark black areas (Fig. 1). In terms of callus growth, the biomass of callus cultured in medium containing various concentrations of ALA for 35 and 45 days exhibited higher values than controls, but the differences were not statistically significant. At 35 days, the biomass of callus treated with ALA ranged from 13.52 g to 14.72 g, compared with 13.93 g for controls. At 45 days, the biomass of ALA-treated callus was increased to 14.83–16.07 g, compared with 14.60 g for controls (Table S2). The increase in callus biomass between day 25 and day 35 was significantly higher (3.13 g to 5.21 g) than that observed between day 35 and day 45 (0.63 g to 1.98 g). In conclusion, ALA treatment from 5 µg/L to 50 µg/L had a negligible impact on callus growth. However, ALA treatment resulted in a more compact texture than observed in control callus.

Accumulation of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in spine grape callus treated with ALA

Accumulation of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in spine grape callus increased progressively with prolonged culture duration, reaching peak levels after 45 days. Different concentrations of ALA exerted significant effects on levels of these three metabolites in callus, with differences increasing with prolonged culture duration. Notably, the most pronounced disparity was observed at 45 days (Table 1). Therefore, the key stage of the accumulation of these three metabolites in spine grape callus occurred during the advanced culture stage.

Except for callus treated with 50 µg/L ALA, which showed no significant difference in flavonoid content from controls, all other concentrations of ALA significantly increased the flavonoid content in callus. The highest increase was observed in callus treated with 25 µg/L ALA, reaching 911.11 µg/100 g DW, 1.47-fold higher than controls. Although there was no significant difference in flavonoid content between callus treated with 50 µg/L ALA and controls, the value was slightly lower than controls.

Anthocyanin content within spine grape callus cultured in media supplemented with varying concentrations of ALA exhibited significant differences compared with controls (Table 1). The effect of ALA on accumulation of anthocyanin in cells was found to be dose-dependent. The highest anthocyanin content (604.60 µg/100 g DW) was observed for 25 µg/L ALA treatment, 1.93-fold higher than controls. The anthocyanin content was also lower than controls at 50 µg/L ALA.

Callus treated with 25 µg/L ALA exhibited the highest accumulation of proanthocyanidins, reaching 5357.00 µg/100 g DW, 1.83-fold higher than controls (Table 1). These analyses showed that an appropriate concentration of ALA could significantly enhance the accumulation of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in spine grape callus, with greater accumulation following 25 µg/L ALA treatment.

Flavonoid profiling and identification of differential flavonoid metabolites

Taking spine grape callus treated at an optimal concentration of ALA (25 µg/L) and a culture duration of 45 days as test material (sample named ALA25), the regulatory network of metabolic pathways related to flavonoid metabolism was investigated using a widely targeted UPLC-MS/MS-based method. Spine grape callus without ALA served as a control (CK). Overlay analysis of the sample multi-peak detection diagram (Fig. S1A) and the QC-TIC diagram (Fig. S1B) showed that the data had good repeatability and reliability. Analysis of the PCA plot (Fig. 2A), OPLS-DA score plot (Fig. 2B) and the hierarchical clustering heat plot (Fig. S2) revealed that the three biological replicates within each group were closely clustered together, indicating a high level of repeatability and reliability for metabolic data. Meanwhile, in model verification (n = 200, 200 permutation experiments) for OPLS-DA, Q2 = 0.987 > 0.9, R2Y = 1 and R2X = 0.783, p < 0.005 (Fig. 2C), further indicating that the model was of a high standard, and effective and reliable.

The model was used to screen ALA25 and CK samples and 283 annotated flavonoid metabolites were identified, including 45 tannins and 238 flavonoids (Table S3). There were two categories of tannins (Fig. 2D): tannin (27) and proanthocyanidins (18), accounting for 9.5% and 6.4%, respectively. Meanwhile, there were nine classes of flavonoids (Fig. 2D): flavonols (75), flavones (49), flavanones (26), flavanols (23), anthocyanidins (15), flavonoid carbonoside (14), chalcones (13), isoflavones (12) and flavanonols (11), accounting for 26.5%, 17.3%, 9.2%, 8.1%, 5.3%, 4.9%, 4.6%, 4.2% and 3.9%, respectively.

In ALA-treated spine grape callus, 97 tannin and flavonoid metabolites were significantly differentially accumulated among the 283 metabolites (Table S4). KEGG functional annotation analysis revealed that the 97 differentially accumulated metabolites consisted of flavonols (34), flavones (29), anthocyanidins (11), flavanols (6), flavanones (5), flavonoid carbonoside (4), tannin (4), isoflavones (3) and flavanonols (1). Among them, 20 metabolites were downregulated while 77 metabolites were upregulated (Fig. 3A and B). Among the 97 differentially accumulated metabolites, the top 10 upregulated metabolites and the top 10 downregulated metabolites are displayed in Figure S3. Further analysis of differential metabolite clusters in KEGG signalling pathways revealed eight pathways with five or more significantly accumulated metabolites, including neohesperidin, kaempferol, gallocatechin, myricetin, ampelopsin, tricetin, epigallocatechin and rutin pathways (Fig. 3C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that enrichment of anthocyanin biosynthesis was the most significant (Fig. 3D), and global change analysis of KEGG metabolic pathways also showed that anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway had the most upregulated trend (Fig. S4). These results indicated that ALA treatment not only increased the accumulation of flavonoids, but also altered the accumulation of each flavonoid component, especially accumulation of anthocyanins and their components.

Analysis of anthocyanin composition

To further explore the regulatory effects of ALA treatment on anthocyanin biosynthesis, levels of 108 anthocyanins and derivatives in spine grape callus were measured by a targeted UPLC-MS/MS-based method. Of 108 standard substances, 93 were comprised of anthocyanins and their derivatives, while nine were classified as flavonoids, and the remaining six belonged to proanthocyanidins, which are closely associated with the biosynthetic pathway of anthocyanins (Table S5). A total of 38 anthocyanins (related flavonoids and proanthocyanidins) were identified in ALA25 and CK samples. Among them, 29 were detected as anthocyanins and their derivatives, while the remaining nine compounds included four flavonoids and five proanthocyanidins (Table S6). The levels of the four flavonoids were low. In conjunction with the results of total flavonoid content analysis, this suggests that there are multiple forms of flavonoids present in spine grape callus and that those related to anthocyanin synthesis exhibit robust metabolism. Levels of five proanthocyanidins were found to be significantly lower than the total amount, which may be attributed to the presence of hyperpolymers or other forms in addition to the detected oligomers.

The detected 29 anthocyanin metabolites included eight cyanidins, six delphinidins, five peonidins, four malvidins, three pelargonidins, and three petunidins (Fig. 4A, Table S6). The accumulation amount of the six anthocyanins was ranked peonidins > malvidins > petunidins > cyanidins > delphinidins > pelargonidins (Fig. 4B). According to the content of anthocyanin components, four accumulative ranges were identified: >100 µg/g DW (large accumulation), 10–100 µg/g DW (medium accumulation), 1–10 µg/g DW (small accumulation) and < 1 µg/g DW (trace). Compared with the CK group, spine grape callus treated with ALA exhibited significant increases in both large accumulation and small accumulation of anthocyanin, and accumulation of delphinidin-3,5-diglucoside, delphinidin-3-glucoside, petunidin-3-glucoside in ALA-treated callus reached large accumulation (Table S7). Interestingly, trace accumulation of anthocyanin delphinidin-3-rutinoside was detected in spine grape callus treated with ALA. Further analysis found that ALA treatment not only significantly increased the accumulation of six kinds of anthocyanins in spine grape callus, but also altered their relative proportions (Table S7). These results indicate that biosynthesis of these anthocyanin metabolites was significantly enhanced in spine grape callus by ALA treatment. Further analysis revealed that the top five most abundant individual anthocyanin components were peonidin-3,5-diglucoside, malvidin-3-glucoside, malvidin-3,5-diglucoside, petunidin-3-galactoside and peonidin-3-glucoside. Moreover, ALA treatment significantly enhanced the accumulation of these individual components of anthocyanins (Table S7). Levels of these components were 1,247.16 µg/g DW, 695.62 µg/g DW, 648.64 µg/g DW, 506.22 µg/g DW and 499.15 µg/g DW, 51.3%, 98.1%, 58.7%, 88.9% and 39.0% higher than the CK group, respectively. It is worth mentioning that among individual anthocyanin components accumulating to > 10 µg/g DW, accumulation of delphinidin-3,5-diglucoside, malvidin-3-(6-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside, cyanidin-3,5-diglucoside and delphinidin-3-glucoside in ALA-treated callus was increased by 399.2%, 234.8%, 226.2% and 116.5%, respectively, compared with the CK group.

Although the biosynthetic pathways of major anthocyanins in spine grape callus were not altered by ALA treatment, it regulated the biosynthetic efficiency and accumulation of different kinds of anthocyanins and different anthocyanin components.

Expression of anthocyanin-related genes and integrative analysis of these genes with anthocyanin metabolites in callus treated with ALA

Expression levels of 21 key genes (identified through transcriptome analysis of spine grape callus, which has not been published) involved in the biosynthesis pathway from phenylalanine to anthocyanin were further investigated in spine grape callus. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that expression levels of 15 genes in ALA-treated callus were higher than controls; these were PAL1, PAL2, PAL4, CHI, CHS3, F3’H, F3H, FLS, DFR, UFGT, MYBA1, LDOX, OMT3, GT1 and ACT. Expression levels of four genes, PAL7, F3’5’H, OMT2 and ANR, were slightly higher than controls, but differences were not statistically significant. Expression levels of only two genes, LAR1 and LAR2, exhibited a significant decrease compared with controls (Fig. 5B, Fig. S5). This suggests that ALA induction comprehensively enhanced the expression of genes associated with the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, and promoted the biosynthesis and accumulation of anthocyanins in spine grape callus.

Discussion

An appropriate concentration of ALA promotes growth of spine grape callus

ALA is widely distributed in plant, animal and microbial cells, and plays a pivotal role in regulating the entire process of plant growth and development. A high concentration of ALA was initially used as a pollution-free and reside-free herbicide and insecticide. ALA has been subsequently discovered to function as a plant growth regulator at low concentrations, facilitating plant growth promotion and yield enhancement [33, 50, 51]. Some studies also demonstrated that ALA treatment enhanced the quality and anthocyanin content of peach peel, while having no impact on fruit biomass [32]. In the present study, a concentration of ALA ranging from 0 to 50 µg/L did not exert a significant impact on the growth status of spine grape callus. Although no notable difference was observed in biomass between spine grape callus grown with or without ALA callus biomass was increased by ALA treatment to some extent. Our previous study revealed that an inappropriate concentration of ALA, specifically concentrations > 1 mg/L, could inhibit cell growth, and even lead to browning and death at 4 mg/L ALA [42]. Therefore, a low concentration of ALA could serve as an exogenous plant growth regulator to effectively promote the growth of spine grape callus. In the present study, the optimal concentration of ALA was 25 µg/L. The resulting callus morphology showed no browning under optimal growth conditions, leading to an increase in biomass. These findings underscored the importance of selecting an appropriate ALA concentration for ensuring robust and healthy spine grape callus growth.

ALA induction enhances accumulation of flavonoids, anthocyanidins and proanthocyanidins in spine grape callus

Although grape callus culture has the potential to produce anthocyanins as raw materials for pharmaceutical, food, and cosmetic industries, the low and unstable content of secondary metabolites such as anthocyanins remains a major obstacle to commercialisation [52]. It is a viable approach to utilise elicitors to modulate the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in spine grape callus, thereby ensuring consistent and high-yielding cellular growth [53, 54]. Studies demonstrated that ALA, as a plant growth regulator, not only facilitated plant growth but also enhanced the accumulation of anthocyanins in plant organs [40, 55]. For instance, post-debagging application of ALA on apples can significantly augment their anthocyanin content [39]. The present study showed that ALA, as an elicitor, could effectively regulate the biosynthesis of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in spine grape callus. Specifically, when the concentration of ALA was 25 µg/L, maximal accumulation of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins occurred. In apple callus culture, ALA treatment was found to induce the accumulation of anthocyanins and flavonols [56]. Our study also revealed that the accumulation of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in spine grape callus was significantly influenced by different concentrations of ALA treatment and various culture stages; extending the culture duration led to a significant increase in these three metabolites accumulation. Furthermore, the magnitude of the increase resulting from ALA treatment with varying concentrations differed significantly. However, when the ALA concentration reached 50 µg/L, there were no significant differences in levels of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in spine grape callus compared with controls during the middle and late stages of culture. In fact, the contents of flavonoids and anthocyanins were even slightly lower than those in controls. Additionally, ALA could promote the accumulation of flavonoids, anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in spine grape callus in a dose-dependent manner, hence selecting an appropriate concentration is crucial.

ALA treatment alters the composition of accumulated flavonoids and anthocyanins in spine grape callus

Most previous studies on the effects of ALA on flavonoid and anthocyanin biosynthesis and accumulation in plants have primarily focused on their total amounts [35], with few examining the impact of ALA on individual flavonoid/anthocyanin components. In the present study, we analysed the flavonoid/anthocyanin components of spine grape callus treated with 25 µg/L ALA and cultured for 45 days. Of the 283 flavonoid components detected, 97 were differentially accumulated compared with controls, including flavonols, flavones, anthocyanidins, flavanols, flavanones, flavonoid, tannin, isoflavones and flavanonols. Among these differential metabolites, 77 were upregulated and only 20 were downregulated. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that ALA exerted a particularly significant regulatory effect on the accumulation of anthocyanins. After ALA treatment, 29 anthocyanins were detected and classified into six categories, with the largest number belonging to cyanidins, but the most abundant was peonidin. The five most abundant anthocyanin components were peonidin-3,5-O-diglucoside, malvidin-3-O-glucoside, malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside, petunidin-3-O-galactoside and peonidin-3-O-glucoside. Compared with the control group, 24 anthocyanin components were significantly upregulated in ALA-treated spine grape callus, while four components showed no significant differences, and only one component was significantly downregulated. It is noteworthy that some studies have reported only trace amounts of pelargonidin anthocyanins in grapes [57], and pelargonidin was not detected in two Vitis davidii cultivars [3]. In the present study, three components of pelargonidin were detected in in vitro cultured spine grape callus, and ALA increased the content of pelargonidin-3,5-diglucoside and pelargonidin-3-glucoside. Furthermore, analysis of changes in flavonoid and anthocyanin accumulation, as well as other components within spine grape callus, provided a more comprehensive understanding of how ALA promoted the accumulation of secondary metabolites. This enriches our knowledge of the role of ALA as an elicitor for regulating flavonoid/anthocyanin biosynthesis.

ALA induction enhances anthocyanin accumulation by upregulating genes related to the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway

Biosynthesis and accumulation of flavonoids and anthocyanins in plants involves a series of structural genes and transcription factors [58, 59], but also influenced by external environmental conditions [60, 61]. ALA treatment can enhance the transcription levels of some structural genes and transcription factors related to anthocyanin biosynthesis in red pear fruits, thereby inducing anthocyanin accumulation [40]. Additionally, ALA treatment can boost the expression of the MdFLS1 gene in apple and facilitate flavonol accumulation [62]. Transcriptome analysis of ALA-induced anthocyanin accumulation in apple showed that a majority of genes involved in the anthocyanin synthesis pathway were upregulated [63]. In the present study, compared with controls, ALA treatment was found to upregulate the expression levels of PAL1, PAL2, PAL4, CHI, CHS3, F3’H, F3H, FLS, DFR, UFGT, MYBA1, LDOX, OMT3, GT1 and ACT genes in spine grape callus throughout the entire culture process. This suggests that these genes are primarily responsible for the significant increase in flavonoid/anthocyanin metabolite accumulation. Integrated analysis of genes related to the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway and anthocyanin components revealed that ALA comprehensively regulated these genes in spine grape callus, particularly during the late stage of spine grape callus culture. It was evident that ALA enhanced the accumulation of anthocyanins by globally stimulating genes associated with the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway.

It is worth mentioning that previous studies revealed a limited accumulation of pelargonidin anthocyanins in grapes, which has been speculated to be associated with a high expression level of F3’5’H gene [57]. However, our study detected low expression of the F3’5’H gene in spine grape callus, and ALA exhibited minimal impact on its promotion. Additionally, we detected larger amounts of pelargonidins, suggesting that downregulation of this gene may be one of the reasons for greater pelargonidin accumulation in spine grape callus, possibly alongside interactions with other key genes. This phenomenon is worthy of further study.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that appropriate ALA induction improved the status of callus growth and significantly enhanced the accumulation of flavonoids, anthocyanins, and proanthocyanidins in red callus of spine grape. Among the 283 identified flavonoid metabolites, 97 exhibited significant differential accumulation, with the most notable differences observed in anthocyanin metabolism; 38 anthocyanin metabolites were identified, belonging to six distinct groups (peonidin, malvidin, petunidin, cyanidin, delphinidin, and pelargonidin). ALA induction significantly increased the abundance of most anthocyanin metabolites. Furthermore, the expression patterns of genes associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis exhibited a direct correlation with anthocyanin accumulation. In summary, our results demonstrate that ALA supplementation of spine grape red callus not only enhances the content of total anthocyanins per unit biomass, but also promotes the accumulation of individual anthocyanin components. These findings have significant implications for devising strategies aimed at enhancing food colouring production using spine grape callus.
