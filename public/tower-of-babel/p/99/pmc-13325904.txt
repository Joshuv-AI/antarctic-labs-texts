Cellulose–PVA-based polymer nanocomposites reinforced with Ba(Ti0.5Fe0.5)O2.25 nanoparticles: structural and dielectric properties
Authors: Amel Mohamed Abouelnaga, Thoraya Maamoun Elhelali, Amany M. El Nahrawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellulose/polyvinyl alcohol (CNs/PVA)-based nanocomposites reinforced with Ba(Ti0.5Fe0.5)O2.25 (FeBTO) nanoparticles were successfully fabricated via a solution-casting in situ polymerization approach to develop sustainable dielectric materials for flexible electronics and energy-storage applications. The novelty of this work lies in correlating FeBTO loading with structural ordering, interfacial polarization, and thermally activated charge transport within a biodegradable polymer matrix. Structural and morphological properties were investigated using XRD, FTIR, SEM, TEM, and impedance spectroscopy. XRD results revealed that low FeBTO loading enhanced polymer ordering, whereas higher concentrations reduced CNs/PVA crystallinity because of interfacial strain effects. TEM/SEM analyses confirmed nanosized FeBTO crystallites (∼15 nm) aggregated into hierarchical structures within the polymer matrix. FTIR spectra verified strong hydrogen-bond interactions between FeBTO nanoparticles and the CNs/PVA network. Electrical measurements demonstrated a substantial reduction in bulk resistance and enhanced conductivity with increasing FeBTO content owing to improved interfacial polarization and hopping conduction mechanisms. The obtained results highlight the potential of FeBTO@CNs/PVA nanocomposites as environmentally friendly multifunctional materials for next-generation flexible dielectric devices and sustainable electronic technologies.

Introduction

The development of organic–inorganic nanocomposites has become increasingly critical in the realm of optoelectronics, where the demand for lightweight, flexible, and environmentally friendly devices is on the rise.1–4 Among the promising biodegradable polymers, cellulose and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) stand out due to their remarkable physicochemical properties and biodegradability, making them suitable candidates for sustainable optoelectronic applications.5,6

Cellulose, a natural polysaccharide derived from plant cell walls, exhibits excellent mechanical properties, high tensile strength, and biodegradability.7,8 It is composed of an extensive network of hydrogen bonds, which contribute to its high crystallinity and thermal stability.9,10 Moreover, cellulose is non-toxic and renewable, aligning with the principles of green chemistry in materials science.9 PVA, a synthetic polymer, is known for its water solubility, biocompatibility, and considerable film-forming capabilities.11,12 When blended, cellulose and PVA can form nanocomposites that enhance mechanical strength and provide unique functional properties such as enhanced moisture resistance, catalytic, and potential for transparency in optoelectronic applications.13–15 These attributes make cellulose–PVA matrices particularly attractive in the development of sustainable advanced materials for optoelectronics.16,17

Barium titanate ferrite (BaTi0.5Fe0.5O3, FeBTO) nanoparticles represent a class of multifunctional materials that have garnered attention due to their distinct crystal structure and excellent functional properties.18 The perovskite structure of FeBTO exhibits a high dielectric constant and significant ferroelectric characteristics, making it ideal for applications in sensors and actuators.19 Additionally, the magnetic properties of FeBTO nanoparticles extend their utility, offering enhanced functionalities such as magnetic memory and microwave absorption capabilities.20 The ability of FeBTO nanoparticles to enhance dielectric and magnetic interactions when incorporated into polymer matrices positions them as effective fillers to improve optoelectronic performance.21–23 Recent advancements in polymer nanocomposites incorporating ferroelectric or ferrite nanoparticles have led to substantial improvements in electrical, optical, and magnetic properties.24–26 However, major challenges remain in achieving uniform nanoparticle dispersion, controlling interfacial interactions, and correlating filler loading with structural and dielectric responses in biodegradable polymer systems.

Recently, sustainable polymer nanocomposites based on biodegradable matrices have attracted considerable attention for replacing conventional petroleum-based dielectric materials in flexible electronics, wearable sensors, and eco-friendly energy-storage systems.27 The combination of cellulose nanocrystals and PVA offers biodegradability, mechanical flexibility, low toxicity, and excellent film-forming ability, while FeBTO nanoparticles provide high dielectric permittivity and defect-assisted polarization behavior.28,29 Integrating these components into a single hybrid structure can produce multifunctional materials with tunable electrical performance and improved environmental sustainability.

Despite recent progress in polymer–ceramic nanocomposites, limited studies have systematically correlated nanoparticle loading with structural ordering, interfacial polarization, relaxation behavior, and thermally activated charge transport in biodegradable CNs/PVA systems.30,31 The novelty of the present work lies in combining Fe-substituted BaTiO3 nanoparticles with a CNs/PVA matrix and establishing a comprehensive relationship between microstructure, interfacial interactions, impedance response, and dielectric relaxation over a wide temperature range.32,33 This study therefore, contributes to the development of sustainable multifunctional dielectric materials suitable for next-generation flexible electronic and energy-storage technologies.34,35

This study explores the impact of incorporating BaTi0.5Fe0.5O3 (FeBTO) nanoparticles into cellulose–PVA (CNs/PVA) nanocomposites and evaluates their structural and dielectric responses. The nanocomposites, synthesized via a sol–gel route and reinforced with 1–4 wt% FeBTO, are investigated to elucidate how interfacial interactions and polarization mechanisms govern dielectric behavior over a wide temperature range. The incorporation of FeBTO nanoparticles is expected to enhance both structural stability and dielectric functionality, leading to synergistic improvements in composite performance. These findings advance the rational design of multifunctional materials and highlight the potential of FeBTO@CNs/PVA nanocomposites for next-generation electronics, eco-friendly devices, sensors, electro-optic modulators, and optical dielectric technologies.

Experimental

Synthesis of barium iron titanate nanoparticles

BaFe0.5Ti0.5O3 (FeBTO) nanoparticles were prepared using the sol–gel synthesis route. High-purity reagents, including tetra-n-butyl titanate (Ti(OC4H9)4, 99.9%), barium nitrate hexahydrate (Ba(NO3)2·6H2O, 99%), acetylacetone, iron nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, 99%), and ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH, 99%), were supplied by Sigma and employed without any additional purification. BaFe0.5Ti0.5O3 (FeBTO) nanopowders were used as the Ba, Fe, and Ti precursors, respectively, with a molar ratio of Ba : Fe = 1 : 0.5 : 0.5. Barium acetate and ferric nitrate were separately dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water under continuous stirring. Titanium isopropoxide was dissolved in 20 mL acetaldehyde and then slowly added dropwise to the mixed aqueous precursor solution under vigorous stirring to avoid premature hydrolysis. During synthesis, acetaldehyde and citric acid served as alternative solvents while also acting as catalytic agents.36,37 The sol–gel procedure promoted the concurrent complexation of (Ba2+ and Fe3+) ions, followed by in situ polymerization mediated by citric acid.1,36,38

The resulting solution was stirred continuously at 80 °C for 2 h until a homogeneous sol was obtained, then aged for 24 h at 80 °C to form a viscous gel. The gel was dried at 150 °C for 24 h to remove residual solvents and organic species. The resulting FeBTO xerogel was sintered at ∼400 °C, as shown in Fig. 1a.

Planned synthesis scheme for (a) FeBTO nanoparticles and (b) for (FeBTO)@CNs–PVP conductive nanocomposites.

Assembly of CNs–PVA-based nanocomposite films

To synthesize the CNs–PVA-based nanocomposite films, 0.41 g of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was dissolved in 50 mL of H2O at 75 °C, under stirring, to form a clear and uniform solution. Separately, 0.70 g of cellulose nanocrystals (CNs) was distributed in H2O (20 mL) of H2O under stirring to obtain a homogeneous suspension. Subsequently, after complete dissolution, the CNs suspension was added to the PVA solution under continuous magnetic stirring to ensure thorough mixing. Stirring was maintained throughout the addition to promote a homogeneous polymer blend.

In parallel, (BaTi0.5Fe0.5O2.25, FeBTO) nanoparticles were distributed in H2O. The specified weights (0.122, 0.242, and 0.482 g, equivalent 1%, 2%, and 4% w/w) of FBT were dispersed in 25 mL of H2O by tip-sonication to produce stable colloid suspensions. Next, the FBT dispersal was gradually added to the CNs–PVA matrix with continuous stirring, ensuring the homogeneous distribution of ceramic nanoparticles throughout the polymer matrix. The resultant nanocomposite solutions were simply troupe into Petri-dishes, at that point permitted to age int air until whole solvent disappearance, producing adaptable nanocomposite as presented in Fig. 1b. The nanocomposites were nominated as fin the follows: the CNs–PVA (zero barium iron titanate) was denoted as CNs/PVA, whereas loaded nanocomposites (1%, 2%, and 4% (w/w)) FBT were selected as (F1-CNs/PVA, F2-CNs/PVA, and F4-CNs/PVA).

Characterization

Surface-morphology of these nanocomposites was inspected using an (FEI-Quanta 250) scanning electron microscope (SEM). The X-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy was applied to examine the composition of the nanonanocomposites (FEI-Quanta-250).

X-ray powder (XRD) diffraction patterns were recorded in the 2θ range of 5°–75° using an X'Pert PRO-MPD diffractometer operating at 40 kV; Cu Kα radiation, at λ = 1.540 Å.

The average crystallite size of the Fe0.5BaTi0.5O2.25 nanoparticles on the main X-ray diffraction (XRD) reflection was calculated using Scherrer's equation:39,40D = (Kλ)/(β cos θ)where, D is the average crystallite size (nm), k is the wavelength of the X-ray radiation, k is a constant taken as 0.89, b is the full width at half maximum intensity, and h is the half diffraction angle.

The internal nanostructure of the FeBTO NPs and the nanocomposite was visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with a Philips EM208S microscope. ImageJ software was utilized to quantitatively analyze the microstructural features of the CNs/PVA/FeBTO nanonanocomposites from TEM images.

FT-IR analysis was determined using a Nicolet/510 spectrophotometer to identify the functional groups and bonding characteristics of the nanocomposite films.

Electrical measurements

The impedance (Z) and dielectric modulus (M) were measured using a Hioki LCR Meter (IM3536). All measurements were recorded using the two-parallel-active-electrodes technique with an electrode radius of 0.5 cm.

Results and discussion

Phase structure

The XRD pattern of the Ba(Ti0.5Fe0.5)O3 (FeBTO) nanoparticles calcined at 400 °C is shown in Fig. 2a. Several diffraction peaks are observed at 2θ ≈ 18.97°, 21.96°, 24.58°, 31.03°, 36.74°, and 38.49°, corresponding to the (100), (110), (112), (006), (104), (212), and (202) planes of hexagonal barium iron titanate (JCPDS No. 89-4615). The presence of these reflections confirms the formation of the FeBTO perovskite phase.41,42 The diffraction peaks are relatively broad, indicating the nanoscale crystallite size and the incomplete crystal growth expected at the relatively low calcination temperature of 400 °C. The calculated average crystallite size is 24.5 nm. No additional diffraction peaks related to secondary phases such as BaCO3, TiO2, or iron oxides are detected within the instrumental resolution, suggesting the successful formation of a predominantly single-phase FeBTO structure.41,43,44 The nanocrystalline FeBTO particles subsequently retain their characteristic reflections after incorporation into the CNs/PVA matrix, where the corresponding peak intensities increase progressively with increasing FeBTO loading as given in Fig. 2b.

XRD patterns of (a) BaFe0.5Ti0.5O2.25 nanoparticles and (b) CNs/PVA nanocomposites containing different loadings of (0–4%) FeBTO nanoparticles.

The XRD diffraction patterns in Fig. 2b show three superimposed contributions: cellulose nanocrystals (CNs), semicrystalline PVA, and Ba(Ti0.5Fe0.5)O3 (FeBTO) nanoparticles, whose relative concentrations vary with FeBTO loading. In the CNs/PVA control sample (CNs/PVA, black line), the profile is dominated by the cellulose-Iβ reflections at 2θ ∼ 15.82° (overlapping (1̄10)/(110)), 2θ ∼ 22.58° ((200)), and a weaker hump (004) near 2θ ∼ 34.27°, which are the hallmark peaks of native cellulose I. These positions match widely reported values for CNs ((1̄10)/(110) ≈ 14.8–16.6°, (200) ≈ 22.2–22.8°), and (004) 2θ ∼ 34–35°.45–48 The broad polymer halo from PVA overlaps this region; PVA's principal crystalline reflection is the (101) near 2θ ∼ 22.58°, consistent with its semicrystalline nature.49–51 Introducing FeBTO produces new, well-defined reflections (blue markers ▼) that grow monotonically with increasing their content, confirming successful incorporation of the perovskite phase without obvious secondary phases (e.g., BaCO3/Fe oxides). The XRD diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 18.97°, 21.96°, 24.57°, 31.02°, 36.73°, and 38.49°, corresponding to the (100), (110), (112), (006), (104), (212), and (202) planes of the FeBTO perovskite phase (JCPDS No. 00-089-4615), progressively increase in intensity with higher FeBTO loading. This trend confirms the systematic incorporation and crystallization of the perovskite phase within the CNs/PVA matrix.52 Matrix peaks also evolve with FeBTO. At low loading (F1-CNs/PVA, 1 wt%), the PVA-related crystalline peak becomes sharper relative to CNs/PVA, while the cellulose (200) around ∼22–23° remains visible but increasingly overlaps with polymer and FeBTO contributions. Because of the very low concentration in F1-CNs/PVA of conductive fillers, their characteristic peak (for the (104) plane at 31.11°) is visible. The peaks associated with the crystalline lattices of FeBTO are sharper with increasing FeBTO NPs. During polymerization and aging, at each synthesis stage, the CNs/PVA matrix rearranged, thereby inducing crystallization.53 This sharpening is consistent with a classic “nucleation at low filler” effect in PVA nanocomposites, where small amounts of rigid nanoparticles (and CNs itself) can promote more ordered PVA packing and slightly increase apparent crystallinity; several recent studies observe maxima in PVA crystallinity near ∼1 wt% nanofiller.6,54 With further FeBTO addition (F2-CNs/PVA → F4-CNs/PVA), the relative intensity of CNs–PVA peaks diminishes compared with the ceramic peaks, and the polymer features broaden slightly. The original CNs/PVA sample exhibits the highest crystallinity, while low F1-CNs–PVA loading induces slight nucleation effects. At higher FeBTO contents (F2-CNs/PVA → F4-CNs/PVA), polymer crystallinity decreases due to lattice distortion and interfacial strain, whereas the FeBTO phase shows progressively stronger and sharper reflections, indicating enhanced perovskite crystallinity. A possible explanation is that the polymer chains (CNs/PVA) adjust their conformations across the FeBTO filling, and therefore, the rate of crystallization increases.55 This indicates that the incorporation of FeBTO preserved the normal mineralization behavior of the CNs/PVA system while promoting the systematic incorporation and crystallization of the perovskite phase within the polymer matrix. This behavior is typical when higher inorganic loadings constrain chain mobility and disrupt lamellar perfection, reducing the effective crystallite size/ordering of PVA, even as the ceramic peaks strengthen.56–58

TEM/SEM

The TEM micrograph of BaFe0.5Ti0.5O2.25 (FeBTO) (Fig. 3a) reveals a nanostructured morphology characterized by agglomerated ultrafine crystallites with sizes in the range of a few tens of nanometers. The image shows a high-density cluster of nanoparticles, suggesting that the synthesis method yielded particles with a high surface-to-volume ratio, but with a tendency toward aggregation, a common feature in perovskite oxide nanomaterials due to their surface energy and strong dipole–dipole interactions. The contrast variation across the particles indicates the presence of crystalline domains, consistent with polycrystalline perovskite structures, while the irregular particle boundaries suggest heterogeneous nucleation and growth.

TEM images of (a) FeBTO NPs, (b) F4-CNs/PVA nanocomposite, (c and d) the SEM image and the particle distribution of F4-CNs/PVA nanocomposite, and (e) the EDX of F4-CNs/PVA nanocomposite.

The fine particle size is advantageous for functional applications, since reducing the crystallite dimensions enhances surface reactivity, increases grain boundary density, and can influence dielectric, ferroelectric, and magnetic properties. In FeBTO, Fe substitution at the Ti-site not only distorts the perovskite lattice but also introduces mixed valence states (Fe3+/Fe4+), which are known to play a key role in tailoring the electrical conductivity, leakage behavior, and magnetoelectric coupling of the material. Such nanoscale features, as observed here, can facilitate improved polarization dynamics and enhance multifunctional responses compared to bulk counterparts.59–61

At the 200 nm scale, the F4-CNs/PVA film (Fig. 3b) shows a ramified, “smoke-like” aggregate composed of many fused primary domains. The darkest, high-attenuation regions occupy the cores of the clumps; in bright-field TEM, these correspond to zones of higher mass–thickness and/or higher average atomic number, which here is most consistent with BaTi0.5Fe0.5O3 (Ba, Ti, Fe ≫ C, O) rather than the organic CNs/PVA matrix. The lighter gray, diffuse halo that outlines each clump is attributable to the polymer/CNs phase, which is comparatively electron-transparent.62 The FeBTO is not dispersed as isolated single particles at 4 wt%; instead, it forms fractal super-aggregates several hundred nanometers across (comparable to the scale bar), made up of much smaller primary features (tens of nanometers). The fused appearance, soft boundaries between dark sub-domains, and a polymer “glue” at the periphery imply partial wetting/encapsulation by PVA and CNs but insufficient steric/electrostatic stabilization to prevent magnetically/surface-energy driven clustering (Fe-containing ferrite-like perovskites often agglomerate unless strongly capped). Such mesoscale clustering is common in oxide-filled PVA or CNs/PVA systems and, while it can create locally high filler volume fractions useful for interfacial load transfer or dielectric pathways, it also introduces heterogeneity that may limit transparency and produce stress-concentration sites.6

The SEM image (Fig. 3c) appears to show a 4 wt% FeBTO surface as surface-localized, micron-scale islands/aggregates and a rough, inhomogeneous coating. At a 5 µm field of view, the film surface has a band-like, brighter/rough ridge crossing the image and many small granular protrusions sitting on an otherwise smoother background. These features are much larger than the 10–20 nm “primary” sizes that were found from TEM and instead indicate micron-scale agglomeration or surface segregation of filler (or filler + CNs bundles). In other words, tens-of-nm primary particles (from TEM) are assembled into clusters large enough to be resolved by SEM as islands.6 The size histogram (Fig. 3d) extracted from Fig. 3b (peak ≈ 13–16 nm, tails to ≈25 nm) is consistent with a population of small primary spheres embedded inside much larger, fractal-like agglomerates visible in the TEM. The bright-field micrograph shows hundreds-of-nm clusters built of tens-of-nm subunits with a mean particle = 15 nm.

EDX analysis was conducted to verify the elemental composition of the CNs/PVA/FeBTO nanocomposite, confirming the presence of carbon, oxygen, iron, barium, and titanium within the matrix structure (Fig. 3e). As illustrated in Fig. 3e, the EDX spectrum clearly reveals characteristic peaks corresponding to C, O, Ti, Ba, and Fe, validating the successful incorporation of FeBTO nanoparticles into the CNs/PVA composite framework.

For pristine CNs/PVA and FeBTO-loaded samples, broad absorption bands detected at 3285 cm−1 are assigned to –OH stretching modes, originating from intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding of CNs and PVA, as well as absorbed moisture.17,30 A distinct contribution from adsorbed hydroxyl groups is also evident at ∼1641 cm−1. The bands linked to COO− and OH functionalities are attributed to both intermediate FeBTO phases and the residual solvent/water environment.17,18,34 Both CNs/PVA blend and FeBTO-loaded samples, a broad absorption band centered at ∼3285 cm−1 is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations arising from intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding within cellulose nanostructures and PVA chains, as well as contributions from absorbed moisture. A corresponding band at ∼1641 cm−1 is assigned to O–H bending vibrations of adsorbed water molecules and bound hydroxyl groups.63–65 Additionally, peaks at 2849 and 2905 cm−1 are related to C–H stretching vibrations of the –CH2– groups present in PVA and cellulose within the polymeric framework.65

In the case of the hybrid systems, new spectral features associated with Ba–O, Ti–O, and Fe–O vibrations were observed, alongside bands near 1715 cm−1 and 2851 cm−1 that correspond to C–O vibrations/OH stretching. For CNs/PVA, the characteristic absorption in the 1000–1120 cm−1 region is associated with (–CH2–OH) vibrations. Upon the addition of FeBTO nanoparticles, the band intensity in this range increases significantly compared to neat CNs/PVA, indicating strong interfacial interactions, likely through hydrogen bonding, between the polymer matrix and FeBTO.68

With progressive FeBTO incorporation, characteristic lattice vibrational peaks emerged at 555, 605, 667, 842, 1031, and 1105 cm−1, confirming the presence of FeBTO nanoparticles.69,70 These absorptions correspond to Ti–O–(Ba/Fe) stretching and bending modes, Ba–OH, Fe–OH, COO−, OH, and Ti–OH vibrations embedded in the (FeBTO)@CNs/PVA framework.71,72

Electric properties

Impedance spectroscopy analysis of (FeBTO)@CNs/PVA composite films

The substitution of Fe3+ ions at the Ti4+ sites in BaTiO3 introduces a charge imbalance in the perovskite lattice. Therefore, the charge neutrality in Ba(Ti1−xFex)O3 systems is commonly preserved through defect compensation mechanisms, such as the formation of oxygen vacancies. Such defect structures are well documented for Fe-doped BaTiO3 synthesized via sol–gel routes and calcined at moderate temperatures.74,75 Such defects significantly influence the electrical and dielectric behavior by introducing localized states that facilitate thermally activated hopping conduction and interfacial polarization.

Fig. 5 shows the Nyquist plots (Z″ vs. Z′) for CNs/PVA nanocomposite films loaded with different concentrations of (FeBTO) at different temperatures ranging from 30 °C to 220 °C. The impedance spectra are characterized by semicircular arcs at high frequencies, followed by inclined lines at lower frequencies. The semicircular arcs at the high frequency represent the bulk response to the external electric field, while the inclined lines at low frequency represent the interfacial and electrode polarization under the effect of the external field. This behavior is indicative of both bulk conduction and interfacial polarization, typical of polycrystalline and composite dielectric materials.76

Nyquist plots of CNs/PVA nanocomposites with different FeBTO concentrations measured at various temperatures: (a) CNs/PVA, (b) F1-CNs/PVA, (c) F2-CNs/PVA, and (d) F4-CNs/PVA. The insets show the enlarged low-frequency region, highlighting the contribution of interfacial polarization and electrode effects with increasing FeBTO content and temperature.

According to Fig. 5–7, the Nyquist plots shows that the semicircle centers lies below the real impedance axes which prove that samples have a non Dybe type behavior and mostly obey the Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars (MWS) interfacial polarization model. This behavior can be attributed to the heterogeneous nature of the blended composite samples.

The Nyquist plot of (a) CNs/PVA, blended with different concentrations of FeBTO at 40 °C: (b) F1-CNs/PVA, (c) F2-CNs/PVA, and (d) F4-CNs/PVA). The inset represents the enlarged Nyquist plot.

The Nyquist plot at 40 °C of CNs/PVA blended with different concentrations of FeBTO and the inset represented their equivalent circuits, (a) CNs/PVA, (b) F1-CNs/PVA, (c) F2-CNs/PVA, and (d) F4-CNs/PVA.

The current study also discusses the effect of increasing the ambient temperature on the impedance response. For each sample (CNs/PVA to F4-CNs/PVA), the diameter of the semicircles decreases with increasing temperature, signifying a reduction in bulk resistance (Rb). This behavior proves that the charge transport is thermally activated, where the elevated temperature increases the mobility of ions and facilitates hopping conduction in the polymer–filler matrix. The decreasing arc radius implies enhanced conductivity due to thermal excitation of charge carriers.

The effect of increasing the filler additive (FeBTO) to the CNs/PVA matrix is observed in changing the physical properties, as shown in Fig. 6. At low loading (1 wt% of FeBTO), the CNs/PVA matrix remains the continuous phase, while FeBTO nanoparticles act primarily as nucleation centers that locally enhance polymer ordering and introduce interfacial dipoles. This regime is dominated by polymer-related dipolar polarization with a moderate contribution from Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars (MWS) interfacial effects. With increasing FeBTO content (2 wt% and 4 wt%), the contribution of the perovskite phase becomes more pronounced, increasing the density of interfaces and oxygen-vacancy-related defect states, which significantly enhance interfacial polarization and hopping conduction, leading to the observed minimum bulk resistance and highest conductivity.77,78 This behavior is observed as follows: a progressive leftward shift and reduction in semicircle diameter occur with increasing FeBTO content from 0 wt% (CNs/PVA) to 4 wt% (F4-CNs/PVA), Fig. 6. This signifies a decrease in total impedance, indicating enhanced electrical conductivity and better percolation pathways for charge movement as more ceramic particles are introduced. The F4-CNs/PVA sample, with the highest filler content, shows the smallest arc diameters, implying the lowest resistance and hence the best conductive performance among the samples, as observed in Fig. 6. This is attributed to the formation of an effective conductive network via FeBTO particles.

A free EIS spectrum analyzer was employed to fit the experimental data with the equivalent circuit. An equivalent circuit model comprising three resistive elements (R1, R2, and R3) and two constant phase elements (Q1 and Q2) was better suited to all samples. The circuit configuration reflects the distinct electrical contributions from the bulk polymer matrix, the interfacial polarization between phases, and the electrode-sample interface. Table 1 summarizes the fitted parameters. The series resistance (R1) in the circuit refers to the electrode and the electrode-sample interface resistance; it has a very low value compared to R2 and R3, which represent the bulk and interfacial polarization effects. Fig. 8 shows the variation of the dielectric loss tangent (tan δ) as a function of frequency for CNs/PVA and FeBTO-loaded CNs/PVA nanocomposites.

Frequency dependence of the dielectric loss tangent (tan δ) for CNs/PVA and FeBTO-loaded CNs/PVA nanocomposites at different temperatures, (a) CNs/PVA, (b) F1-CNs/PVA, (c) F2-CNs/PVA, and (d) F4-CNs/PVA. The appearance of two relaxation peaks in CNs/PVA and F1-CNs/PVA indicates the coexistence of interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars) and dipolar relaxation processes, whereas the single broad peak observed for F2-CNs/PVA and F4-CNs/PVA reflects the overlap and coupling of these relaxation mechanisms owing to enhanced filler–matrix interactions and increased interfacial polarization.

The dielectric loss tangent (tan δ) was calculated from the real and imaginary components of the complex impedance according to the relation:where (Z′) and (Z″) are the real and imaginary parts of the complex impedance, respectively. The loss tangent represents the ratio of energy dissipated to energy stored during dielectric polarization and provides valuable information regarding dielectric relaxation and charge transport mechanisms within the nanocomposite system.

The CNs/PVA and F1-CNs/PVA samples exhibit two distinct relaxation peaks located in the low- and high-frequency regions, indicating the coexistence of two dielectric relaxation processes. The low-frequency relaxation peak is attributed to Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars (MWS) interfacial polarization resulting from charge accumulation at the interfaces between the polymer matrix and FeBTO nanoparticles. In contrast, the high-frequency peak originates from dipolar relaxation associated with the segmental motion of polymer chains and localized hopping of charge carriers within the bulk matrix.

With increasing FeBTO content (F2-CNs/PVA and F4-CNs/PVA), the two relaxation peaks gradually merge into a single broad peak located at intermediate frequencies. This behavior suggests a strong overlap between the interfacial and dipolar relaxation processes due to the increased density of FeBTO nanoparticles and the reduced distance between neighboring filler particles. The enhanced interfacial interactions facilitate charge transfer and polarization coupling between the filler and matrix phases, causing the characteristic relaxation times of the two mechanisms to become comparable.

Furthermore, the formation of interconnected polarization pathways and the increase in oxygen-vacancy-related defect states introduced by Fe substitution promote hopping conduction and broaden the distribution of relaxation times. Consequently, the individual relaxation processes can no longer be resolved separately, leading to the appearance of a single merged relaxation peak at intermediate frequencies. Such behavior is characteristic of heterogeneous polymer–ceramic nanocomposites in which increasing filler concentration enhances interfacial polarization and modifies charge carrier dynamics throughout the composite network.

The shift from two distinct relaxation peaks to a single broad relaxation peak therefore, confirms the transition from independent bulk and interfacial polarization processes at low FeBTO loading to a strongly coupled dielectric relaxation mechanism at higher FeBTO concentrations.

According to Fig. 5 and 9, the impedance and modulus behaviors confirm the thermally activated behavior of the samples. The shift of the modulus peaks toward higher frequencies with temperature (Fig. 9) corresponds to the reduction of the semicircle diameters in the Nyquist plots (Fig. 5), both indicating thermally activated charge-carrier dynamics. In Fig. 9, the relaxation peak at high frequency region is associated to the intrinsic dipolar motion and hopping conduction within the polymer matrix (bulk relaxation), which is consistent with the bulk resistance (R2) that obtained from the impedance fitting. Also, the low-frequency shoulders (Fig. 9) correspond to interfacial polarization at the FeBTO@CNs–PVA boundaries, matching the interfacial resistance (R3) in the equivalent circuit. Therefore, both impedance and modulus behavior confirm the formation of conductive networks and enhanced interfacial polarization that facilitate charge transport through hopping between localized states.

Therefore, the addition of FeBTO significantly improves the electrical performance of the CNs/PVA matrix, particularly at high temperatures, making these materials suitable candidates for flexible electronics, electrochemical energy storage, or high-temperature dielectric applications.

The dielectric response of FeBTO@CNs/PVA nanocomposites is governed by the combined contributions of dipolar polarization within the polymer matrix and interfacial polarization arising from the heterogeneous polymer–ceramic interfaces. The incorporation of FeBTO nanoparticles introduces numerous interfaces between the high-permittivity ceramic phase and the insulating CNs/PVA matrix, leading to charge accumulation at these interfaces under an applied electric field. This phenomenon is characteristic of Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars polarization and becomes increasingly significant with increasing FeBTO loading. Furthermore, Fe substitution within the BaTiO3 lattice generates oxygen-vacancy-related defect states that facilitate localized hopping of charge carriers and contribute to dielectric relaxation. The observed decrease in bulk resistance, shift of relaxation peaks toward higher frequencies with increasing temperature, and reduction of activation energy collectively confirm a thermally activated polarization process. At moderate FeBTO loading (F2-CNs/PVA), the balance between interfacial polarization and nanoparticle dispersion produces the most effective dielectric response. In contrast, excessive filler content promotes particle agglomeration, reducing the effective interfacial area and limiting further enhancement. These results demonstrate that the dielectric behavior of the nanocomposites is strongly influenced by the interplay between nanoparticle dispersion, interfacial polarization, and charge transport mechanisms.

Based on the structural analysis and electrical properties, a strong correlation exists between the XRD results and the electrical/dielectric properties of the FeBTO@CNs/PVA nanocomposites. XRD analysis revealed that low FeBTO loading (F1-CNs/PVA) slightly enhanced the structural ordering of the polymer matrix due to the nucleating effect of the nanoparticles, whereas higher FeBTO concentrations progressively reduced the crystallinity of the CNs/PVA phase because of interfacial strain and restricted polymer-chain packing. Simultaneously, the intensity and sharpness of the FeBTO diffraction peaks increased, confirming the increasing contribution of the perovskite phase within the composite.

The dielectric and electrical measurements reflect these structural modifications. The reduction in polymer crystallinity and the increase in the number of polymer-FeBTO interfaces create additional localized states and interfacial regions that facilitate charge accumulation and Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars (MWS) polarization. Consequently, the bulk resistance decreases significantly with FeBTO incorporation, while conductivity increases due to thermally activated hopping of charge carriers between defect sites associated with Fe ions and oxygen vacancies in the FeBTO lattice.

Furthermore, the enhanced interfacial area resulting from the increasing FeBTO content explains the appearance of stronger dielectric relaxation phenomena observed in the modulus and dielectric-loss spectra. At low filler loading, separate dipolar and interfacial relaxation processes are observed, whereas at higher FeBTO concentrations these processes become strongly coupled because of the increased density of interfaces and conductive pathways. Therefore, the XRD results provide direct structural evidence for the enhanced interfacial polarization, reduced resistance, increased conductivity, and modified dielectric relaxation behavior observed in the electrical characterization.

The novelty of the present work arises from the synergistic incorporation of Fe-substituted BaTiO3 (FeBTO) nanoparticles into a biodegradable cellulose nanostructure/poly(vinyl alcohol) (CNs/PVA) matrix and the systematic investigation of the resulting structure–property relationships. Unlike previous studies on cellulose/PVA-based composites that primarily focus on room-temperature dielectric performance, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the temperature-dependent electrical behavior over a broad temperature range (30–220 °C).

By integrating impedance spectroscopy, electric modulus analysis, equivalent circuit modeling, and activation energy calculations, the work elucidates the fundamental mechanisms governing charge transport, dielectric relaxation, and interfacial polarization in the nanocomposite system. The results reveal the coexistence of bulk dipolar relaxation and Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars interfacial polarization, highlighting the critical role of FeBTO nanoparticles as both high-permittivity fillers and active interfacial centers that regulate polymer chain dynamics and hopping conduction processes.

Furthermore, the study identifies an optimal FeBTO loading that maximizes dielectric response and electrical conductivity before the onset of nanoparticle agglomeration, thereby establishing a direct correlation between filler concentration, microstructural evolution, and electrical performance. These findings provide valuable insights into the design of sustainable polymer–perovskite nanocomposites with tunable dielectric characteristics.

From an application perspective, the combination of enhanced dielectric properties, thermally activated conductivity, mechanical flexibility, and environmental sustainability makes the developed CNs/PVA/FeBTO nanocomposites promising candidates for flexible dielectric layers, polymer-based energy storage systems, wearable electronics, and multifunctional sensing devices operating under elevated-temperature conditions.

Conclusion

In this work, CNs/PVA polymer nanocomposites reinforced with Ba(Ti0.5Fe0.5)O3 (FeBTO) nanoparticles were successfully fabricated via in situ polymerization, producing homogeneous and flexible dielectric films. XRD analysis confirmed the successful incorporation of the FeBTO perovskite phase within the CNs/PVA matrix, where low FeBTO loading enhanced polymer ordering while higher concentrations reduced polymer crystallinity because of interfacial strain and restricted chain mobility. TEM and SEM analyses revealed nanosized FeBTO crystallites with average particle sizes around 15 nm aggregated into hierarchical clusters distributed throughout the polymer matrix. FTIR spectra verified strong hydrogen-bond interactions between FeBTO nanoparticles and the CNs/PVA network, confirming effective interfacial coupling and preservation of the perovskite structure. Electrical and dielectric investigations demonstrated that increasing FeBTO loading significantly reduced bulk resistance and enhanced conductivity through thermally activated hopping conduction and Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars interfacial polarization mechanisms. The F2-CNs/PVA composition exhibited the highest conductivity due to the formation of optimized conductive pathways, whereas excessive filler loading caused partial agglomeration effects. The impedance and modulus analyses further confirmed the coexistence of bulk dipolar relaxation and interfacial polarization processes over the temperature range 30–220 °C. These findings demonstrate that FeBTO nanoparticles effectively tailor the structural ordering, dielectric relaxation, and charge transport properties of CNs/PVA nanocomposites, making them promising environmentally friendly materials for flexible dielectric layers, polymer-based capacitors, energy-storage systems, and sustainable electronic applications.
