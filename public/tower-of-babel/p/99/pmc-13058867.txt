Self‐Assembly of Anti‐Inflammatory Peptide Amphiphiles for Mucosal Health
Authors: María José Gómara, Ignacio Pérez‐Pomeda, Ramon Pons, Paul Ziprin, Carolina Herrera, Isabel Haro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBroad spectrum anti‐inflammatory peptide amphiphiles (AIF‐PAs) have been developed as an innovative strategy for the treatment of chronic mucosal inflammation and the prevention of HIV transmission. Novel lipopeptides based on two linear sequences of anti‐inflammatory peptides were obtained by solid‐phase peptide chemistry. In a pilot study, ex vivo exposure of colorectal tissue explants from Crohn's disease patients to AIF‐PAs induced a downregulation of the proinflammatory profile. Furthermore, the HIV inhibitory potency of AIF‐PAs was evaluated ex vivo in colorectal tissue explants, obtaining sub‐micromolar IC50 values. Characterization of AIF‐PAs self‐aggregation in aqueous solution revealed that the net charge distribution within the peptides influenced their conformation in water and their self‐assembly into larger structures. AIF‐PAs with neutral net charge displayed a β‐sheet conformation, whereas AIF‐PAs with positive net charge were characterized by a random coil conformation. The morphology of the nanostructures fully correlated with the peptide secondary structure and self‐aggregation levels. Interactions with model membranes and epithelial cells demonstrated that the cationic charge and amphiphilic nature of self‐assembled AIF‐PAs facilitated their binding and localization to lipid bilayers and cell membranes. Finally, AIF‐PAs were unable to cross epithelial cell monolayers in permeability assays, indicating a low mucosal permeation potential. Their lack of permeation reduces systemic absorption, thus concentrating and retaining the bioactive peptides at the mucosal surface, where local activity is desired. In summary, peptide‐based therapies leverage the beneficial anti‐inflammatory properties of peptides, combined with amphiphilic structures that allow precise targeting and improved delivery to mucosal tissues.Keywords: anti‐inflammatory activity, lipopeptides, mucosal health, nanostructures, self‐assembly

Introduction

The treatment of inflammatory disorders often implies the use of nonspecific small molecule drugs that can cause a wide range of undesired side effects due to high toxicity and low selectivity. Currently, alternative and more selective anti‐inflammatory therapies, including the use of peptides, are being particularly addressed for their use against chronic inflammatory conditions [1]. Synthetic peptides are able to inhibit and/or modulate functional protein complexes, showing higher potency, selectivity and specificity for the molecular targets involved in inflammatory processes. The therapeutic use of peptides as anti‐inflammatory agents in the treatment of different inflammation‐related diseases has been reviewed focusing attention on several endogenous peptides secreted during inflammatory response and other peptides deriving from interacting regions of protein complexes triggered by inflammatory mediators [2]. Most of these peptides are short sequences that require chemical optimization to improve their therapeutic efficiency. Thus, the introduction of conformational constraints, such as mono‐ or‐ bicyclic structures, to decrease the conformational flexibility of linear peptides, as well as the substitution of natural amino acid residues with unnatural ones and/or with N‐methyl‐α‐amino acids to enhance membrane permeability and plasma stability, are common strategies to increase the proteolytic stability, bioavailability and anti‐inflammatory potency of peptide‐based therapeutics [3, 4].

In the last few years, self‐assembled peptides have become a research hotspot in the biomedical field. Stupp et al. have extensively developed functional peptide‐based supramolecular polymers based on peptide amphiphiles (PAs), molecules containing peptide segments covalently bound to lipid‐like hydrophobic segments [5, 6]. Lipopeptides have been used to design synthetic advanced dynamic supramolecular systems, nanostructured materials or self‐responsive, delivery systems in the area of medical biotechnology [7]. These supramolecular polymers have been shown to be highly bioactive in vivo when designed to promote biological events such as regeneration of axons in the injured spinal cord, growth of blood vessels, and regeneration of bone and cartilage [8, 9]. The biocompatibility and biodegradability of self‐assembled peptide nanomaterials are also advantageous for targeted drug delivery [10, 11, 12]. However, few examples of self‐assembled peptides for use as drugs have so far been described. In addition to self‐assembled peptides as therapeutic agents for treating cancer [13, 14], high aspect ratio supramolecular anti‐inflammatory peptide amphiphiles (AlF‐PAs) have recently been designed to treat localized intestinal inflammation [15, 16]. In a previous work, we addressed the design of novel PAs based on a peptide sequence [17] as anti‐HIV inhibitor with a broad spectrum of activity against viruses from different clades, subtypes, and tropisms and whose interaction with its viral target takes place at the membrane level [18]. Characterization of the self‐assembly in condensed phase and in aqueous solution of synthesized PAs as well as their interaction with model membranes allowed us to define structural characteristics that determined the PAs self‐assembly in aqueous solution, their membrane affinity and their disassembly promoted by the presence of either model or cellular membranes [19]. We demonstrated that the type of lipophilic residue on a peptide amphiphile mainly affects the nanostructure disassembly promoted by the presence of lipid membranes. Lipophilic conjugates that were more tightly packed led to a less effective disassembly and subsequent association to the cell membrane was diminished. Otherwise to dialkyl‐derivatized lipopeptides, PAs with one alkyl tail at the C‐terminus or at the N‐terminus of the peptide sequence showed an increased biological activity in cells and in a pre‐clinical human mucosal tissue explant model.

Based on this background, in the present work, we aim to develop broad spectrum anti‐inflammatory peptides amphiphiles (AIF‐PAs) and to explore their targeting capacity to mucosal surfaces as a novel strategy for treatment of chronic mucosal inflammation and for prevention of HIV transmission. With this objective, we designed and synthesized novel AIF‐PAs based on two short linear anti‐inflammatory peptide sequences, Met‐Thr‐Ala‐Asp‐Val (MTADV) and Ser‐Gln‐Ser‐His‐Pro‐Arg‐His (SQSHPRH). The MTADV peptide has been described as an inhibitor of the release of proinflammatory cytokines IL‐6 and IL‐1β from SAA (protein Serum Amyloid A) activated human fibroblasts, monocytes, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells and suppresses inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) pathology in mouse models [20, 21, 22]. The SQSHPRH peptide was previously identified by phage‐display with a specific high affinity for IBD [23]. The AIF‐PAs self‐aggregation in aqueous solution and the interaction of the self‐assembled nanostructures with model membranes and epithelial cells have been studied; the anti‐inflammatory activity of the AIF‐PAs in human colorectal tissue from patients with Crohn's disease has also been assessed. In addition, considering that decreased risk of HIV infection is associated with lower baseline level of T cell immune activation and proinflammatory chemokines [24, 25, 26, 27], the anti‐HIV activity of AIF‐PAs has also been studied in human colorectal tissues.

Results

Synthesis of AIF‐PAs

The synthesis of lipopeptides derivatized at the N‐ or C‐terminus with a C16 alkyl group was carried out by solid‐phase peptide synthesis methodologies. AIF‐PA lipopeptides presenting two copies of the same AIF peptide sequence conjugated with a C16 alkyl group at the C‐terminus were also synthesized and the resulting AIF‐PAs were purified by flash chromatography and characterized by electrospray mass spectrometry (ESI‐MS) (Figures S1–S6). Anti‐Inflammatory Activity of AIF‐PAs in Mucosal Tissue

We evaluated the activity of each peptide in the context of chronic bowel inflammation (IBD) in an ex vivo pilot study with colorectal tissue samples from three patients with Crohn's disease. Human colorectal tissue specimens from patients undergoing surgery for IBD were obtained after receiving signed informed consent from all patients at St. Mary's Hospital (Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust, London, UK) under the Imperial College Healthcare Tissue Bank approved by Research Ethics Committee Wales (IRAS 17/WA/0161). Exposure of colorectal explants to AIF‐PAs for 24 h induced a downregulation of the proinflammatory profile in all tissues examined. Due to donor‐to‐donor variability in the baseline secretory profile, responses to peptides were analyzed for each tissue specimen individually. However, for all three donors tested, the six AIF‐PAs induced a decreased secretion of proinflammatory cytokine interleukin‐6 (IL‐6); chemokines IL‐8, interferon γ‐induced protein (IP‐10), and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1), and antimicrobial proteins granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) and granulocyte G‐CSF, compared to levels observed in culture supernatants of untreated tissue explants and with different level of statistical significance in each tissue and for each peptide (Figure 2, Figures S7, S8 and Table S2). The three AIF‐PAs derived from SQSHPRHK induced a decreased secretion of proinflammatory cytokine IL‐1α which reached statistical significance for some participants. However, derivatives of MTADVK induced an increase of this cytokine in tissue explants of two donors out of the three included in this pilot study. IL‐1β levels, a proinflammatory cytokine, tended to increase following exposure of IBD‐colorectal explants to AIF‐PA2–1 and AIF‐PA2–2, reaching significance in some donors. However, AIF‐PA2–3 induced inconsistent responses with a decrease of IL‐1β in one donor (p = 0.0002) and an increase in the other two donors (p = 0.0083 and p = 0.0730). Similar inconsistencies were observed for IL‐1β with the three MTADV derivatives, resulting in decreased levels in culture supernatant for one donor and increased concentrations for the other two compared to untreated explants. Secretion of the adaptive cytokine, IL‐17, which has also been associated with Crohn's disease, was negatively modulated by AIF‐PA2‐3 reaching statistical significance in one donor (p = 0.0354) (Figure S7, Table S2). For each surgical specimen obtained, the activity of the unconjugated peptides MTADV and SQSHPRH was also assessed (Supplementary Figure S9).

Inhibitory Potency of AIF‐PAs Against HIV‐1

The antiviral activity of the six AIF‐PAs against HIV‐1BaL was pre‐clinically assessed in human colorectal tissue explants and compared to the activity of the unconjugated peptides, MTADV and SQSHPRH. Dose–responses curves were observed for all peptides with ex vivo ‘pulse’ exposure to peptides before and during viral challenge (3h) mimicking dosing immediately prior to and during viral exposure. Within the range of concentrations tested an IC50 could be calculated for each peptide at each harvest time during the 15 days of explant culture (Table 1, Table S3, Figure S10). For all lipopeptides, except AIF‐PA2–2, an increased antiviral activity was observed as shown by a trend to a reduction in IC50 values compared to those measured for the unconjugated peptides. Only two lipopeptides, with the C16 alkyl group in the N‐terminal end, AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA2–1, reached 90% of inhibition (Table 1, Figure S10c).

Characterization of AIF‐PAs Self‐Aggregation in Aqueous Solution

Before analyzing the self‐aggregation of AIF‐PAs in aqueous solution, the secondary structure of the peptides was characterized as a function of the concentration. The secondary structure of each derivative was studied by circular dichroism (CD) with a concentration range from 100 to 300 µM. The CD spectra of the underivatized short peptide sequences (Figure 3a,e) were characterized by a negative band located at 200 nm characteristic of random coil conformation. These peptides were expected to have no secondary structure in solution since they have a short peptide backbone composed of a small number of amino acids. Lipophilic derivatization of the peptide sequences showed different CD spectra than those obtained from underivatized peptides. The CD spectra of lipopeptides based on MTADV sequence, i.e., AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2 and AIF‐PA1–3, showed a negative band located at 220 nm that is characteristic of a β‐sheet conformation (Figure 3b‐d). A dependence between the intensity of the negative band at 220 nm and concentration was observed for AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA1–2, indicating that the β‐sheet conformation increased with concentration (Figure 3b,c). On the contrary, the analysis revealed no significant concentration effect on the CD spectra for AIF‐PA1–3 (Figure 3d) indicating that the β‐sheet content is stable across the tested concentration range. Regarding the lipopeptides derived from the SQSHPRH sequence, CD spectra of AIF‐PA2–1, AIF‐PA2–2 and AIF‐PA2–3 were characterized by a negative band at 200 nm indicating a random coil conformation and the spectra remained largely unchanged independently of the concentration analyzed (Figure 3f‐h).

Having in mind the secondary structure results, we used Thioflavin‐T (ThT) probe that possesses selective affinity toward rich β‐sheet forming structures in order to study the capacity of AIF‐PAs to form fibrillar aggregates. As previously described [28], ThT fluorescence intensity correlates linearly with amyloid fibril concentration and the slope of the linear regression fitting, namely “fluorescence per amyloid concentration” (FPAC), reflects the change in ThT fluorescence as a function of amyloid concentration. FPAC is a specific amyloid property at a given ThT concentration. Here, ThT fluorescence was analyzed upon AIF‐PAs titration, and we obtained different results depending on the peptide sequence of the AIF‐PAs. Thus, a fluorescence intensity increase of ThT was observed upon titration with AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2, and AIF‐PA1–3 that contain the MTADV sequence (Figure 3i‐k). These AIF‐PAs set notably enhanced and blue‐shifted the peak appeared around 490 nm of the ThT, which showed a very weak peak at 530 nm when it is free. On the contrary, ThT fluorescence intensity was two order of magnitude lower upon addition of increasing concentrations of AIF‐PA2–1, AIF‐PA2–2, and AIF‐PA2–3, which derived from the SQSHPRH peptide sequence (Figure S11), remaining practically unchanged the fluorescence of ThT. Figure 3l shows the fluorescent fold change versus the concentration of AIF‐PAs. ThT fluorescence only showed a linear correlation with the concentration of AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2, and AIF‐PA1–3, suggesting that the intensity of fluorescence was associated with the amount of fibrils determined by the peptide concentration. The data were fitted with linear regression obtaining r2 values higher than 0.99 for these three AIF‐PAs. The slopes values of the regression lines were 3.20 (±0.05) for AIF‐PA1–1, 1.77 (±0.04) for AIF‐PA1–2, and 2.65 (±0.05) for AIF‐PA1–3, demonstrating that N‐terminal acylation of the MTADV lead to a higher fibrillar aggregation than the C‐terminal acylation of the same peptide sequence. These results of ThT fluorescence perfectly correlate with the secondary structure of the AIF‐PAs studied by CD. Thus, AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2, and AIF‐PA1–3 have β‐sheet secondary structure that led to the selective binding of ThT, indicating the formation of fibrillar aggregates.

Conversely, AIF‐PA2–1, AIF‐PA2–2, and AIF‐PA2–3 have a random coil conformation and in agreement with this, no fibril aggregation was observed by the ThT fluorescence assay, despite having amphiphilic characteristics. To analyze the self‐aggregation of these AIF‐PAs in aqueous solution, a fluorescence assay using the 1‐anilino‐8‐naphthalene sulfonate (ANS), which its fluorescence depends on the hydrophobic environment was carried out. AIF‐PAs titration of an ANS solution was performed and the fluorescence fold change was plotted versus the peptide concentration to determine the critical aggregation concentration (CAC). The CAC value is calculated as the intersection point between the two linear regressions fitting the fluorescence intensity values obtained for each peptide concentration. Figure S12 shows the results for peptides derived from the SQSHPRH sequence that allow to obtain CAC values of 15.2 ± 0.2 µM, 10.6 ± 0.9 µM, and 8.3 ± 1.1 µM for AIF‐PA2–1, AIF‐PA2–2, and AIF‐PA2–3, respectively, confirming the self‐aggregation of these PAs in aqueous solution despite no showing fibrillar aggregation. In contrast, no discontinuity in fluorescence intensity was observed as a function of the concentration analyzed for AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2, and AIF‐PA1–3, so this fluorescence assay did not allow the calculation of CAC for these peptides (Figure S12a, b and c).

Then, the morphology of self‐assembled structures in aqueous environment was analyzed by TEM. TEM images demonstrated that AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2, and AIF‐PA1–3 assembled into elongated structures while AIF‐PA2–1, AIF‐PA2–2, and AIF‐PA2–3 form globular structures (Figure 4). TEM images with higher magnification as well as the measures of the main structures are shown in Figure S13. As previously described, AIF‐PA derived from the MTADV sequence form fibrillar structures. TEM analyses show that they differ on the morphology depending on the position of the alkyl derivatization. Thus, images of AIF‐PA1–1 are characterized by the presence of a network of elongated, cross‐linked fibrils or spicules that measures at least 0.25 µm while AIF‐PA1–2 forms more discrete needle‐like fibers that range from 0.10 µm to 0.45 µm (Figure 4a,b and Figure S13a,b). AIF‐PA1–3 self‐aggregates into wider fibers o tapes of 0.05–0.1 µm of width (Figure 4c and Figure S13c) that can self‐assemble into larger structures. Conversely, AIF‐PA derived from SQSHPRH peptide forms globular structures that ranged from 100 to 200 nm (Figure 4d‐f). AIF‐PA2–1 self‐aggregated into more homogeneous spherical nanostructures of 100 nm, while AIF‐PA2–2 and AIF‐PA2–3 forms larger globular structures, up to 200 nm in size (and Figure S13d‐f).

Characterization of AIF–PAs Interaction with Model Membranes

To test the effect of AIF‐PAs on model membranes different concentration of AIF‐PAs were mixed with preformed POPC liposomes in HEPES 10 mM pH = 7.4. The amount of POPC was maintained constant at 20 mM for all the samples. The samples were analyzed by SAXS. Typically for liposomes SAXS curves provide information on the bilayer electron density [29]. The center of the bilayer is characterized by low electronic densities (methyl and methylene groups), while the polar headgroups of the phospholipids correspond to high electronic density. Further, the scattering of unilamellar liposomes can be modulated by the presence of multilamellar or oligolamellar liposomes. In Figure 5a, the scattering profile of POPC vesicles is compared to the scattering of AIF‐PA1–1 vesicles. POPC sample show a wide band centered at 1 nm−1 and some faint oscillations at higher q values. This appearance is characteristic of bilayer systems. Also the minimum, close to zero intensity, that appears left of the main band is characteristic of symmetrical bilayers and is absent in asymmetric bilayers. The black line in Figure 5a corresponds to the fit of symmetrical bilayers based on a Gaussian description of the headgroups and methyl groups and an error function for the methylene groups. The form of the main band, with a slightly pointed form and a hunchback right of the maximum indicates the presence of some correlation due to some multilamellarity. The parameters obtained for POPC are similar to those obtained from highly multilamellar phases by other authors [29] or for oligolamellar samples by ourselves [19]. The fitted profile is shown in Figure 5b. As it is evident from the scattering patterns of the AIF‐PA1–1, the loading of the POPC vesicles results in the formation of asymmetric systems. In order to fit those samples, an additional headgroup contribution has been added to the bilayer description. The AIF‐PA molecules consist of a hydrophobic chain linked to a peptide. In some of the configurations of the AIF‐PA, the hydrophobic chain and the lateral chain of lysine may be considered the hydrophobic group and have an electronic density similar to the palmitoyl and oleyl hydrophobic chains and good mixing with them. The peptide contains charged groups and some hydrophilic side chains and therefore will tend to be in a hydrophilic environment. According to our calculations, the dry electronic density of both sequences is similar (448 and 466 e/nm3 for AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA2–1 respectively); therefore, their incorporation to the bilayer will leave the hydrophobic density rather unaltered and contribute to increase the electronic density in the polar part of the bilayer.

In Figure 5a, it can be observed that the quality of the fits is also very good for the loaded vesicles. The resulting bilayer profiles show the increasing assymmetrization of the bilayer with the increased concentration of AIF‐PA1–1. To keep the number of parameters of the fit at a reasonable level, we fix the width and height of the symmetric polar headgroups to those obtained from POPC. This restriction implies the treatment of the scattering of the loaded systems as a perturbation of the basic system. This is quite reasonable for the inner leaflet of the vesicles although it could be reasonably challenged for the outer leaflet. However, we think this approach can be meaningful.

The scattering patterns for AIF‐PA1–2 loaded vesicles are similar, but the electronic density profiles reveal some differences as it is shown in Figure 5c,d. In this case, the additional electronic density contribution lies closer to the hydrophobic part of the bilayer providing a narrower contribution than the original phosphatidylcholine headgroups. The case of AIF‐PA1–3 is different from those of AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA1–2. In the scattering pattern (Figure 5e), we observe a difference in the small q region, with the minimum at the left of the main band becoming shallower and displaced to bigger q values. This reflects in the reduction of the width of the hydrophobic part of the bilayer. The asymmetric part of the bilayer implies in this case the increase of intensity in the external part of the headgroups.

Concerning AIF‐PA2–1 (Figure 6a,b ), the situation is similar to that of AIF‐PA1–2 with the increase of electronic density lying close to the hydrophobic part of the bilayer. And is also very similar to what is observed in the case of AIF‐PA2–2 (Figure 6c,d). The case of AIF‐PA2–3 is different, with a much lesser effect on the bilayer structure.

The parameters shown in Tables S4 and S5 correspond to the fits shown in Figures 5 and 6. It can be observed that all of the samples show a slight but significant presence of bilamellar vesicles. For POPC, even if taken over this low lamellarity, the value of the Caillé parameter η (related to the bilayer elasticity) is perfectly compatible with the values obtained for heavily multilamellar samples [29]. The repeating distance is similar for all the samples except for AIF‐PA1–3, which shows a significant reduction concomitant with the reduction of the bilayer thickness observed in the electronic profile (Figure 5e,f). The lamellarity of the samples is also similar in all the experiments except for the higher loaded samples with AIF‐PA1–3. The value of the width of the methylene contribution to the center of the hydrophobic part of the bilayer did not show very significant differences, all of them show that there is not a well‐defined deep in the chains, implying the absence of chain crystallinity. The position of the choline headgroups tends to increase as the amount of AIF‐PAs is added, except for AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA1–3 where the tendency is the contrary. This is linked to the different position of the additional Gaussian, far from the interface for these two products and close to the hydrophile–hydrophobe interface for the rest of the AIF‐PAs. The electronic contrast of the additional Gaussian correlates with the quantity of AIF‐PA present in the mixtures for all the cases in which the additional Gaussian is situated in the inner part of the bilayer.

Characterization of AIF–PAs Interaction with Epithelial Cells

First, the viability of VK2/E6E7 cells was assessed after treatment with fluorescent AIF‐PAs (FAM‐AIF‐PAs), obtained by the co‐assembly of each AIF‐PA with 5% mol/mol of the lipophilic 5‐carboxyfluorescein derivative (FAM‐C18) [19]. Table S6 shows the cytotoxic concentration (CC50) values obtained by the MTT assay. Overall, FAM‐AIF‐PAs derived from the SQSHPRH peptide sequence exhibited lower CC50 than those derived from the MTADV peptide sequence. Among them, FAM‐AIF‐PA1–1 and FAM‐AIF‐PA1–3 did not show cytoxicity at concentrations up to 100 µM.

Following CC50 determination, the ability of AIF‐PA nanostructures to target human VK2/E6E7 epithelium was evaluated by flow cytometry at a nontoxic concentration (10 µM) for all six AIF‐PAs. Cellular uptake of each FAM‐AIF‐PAs derivative is shown in Figures S14,S15 by means of representative dot plots and green fluorescence intensity histograms to illustrate the proportion of untreated (nonfluorescent) versus AIF‐PAs‐treated (fluorescent) cells. After 1 h of incubation, approximately 95% of cells exhibited green fluorescence, confirming efficient uptake of the nanostructures.

To further visualize the localization of FAM‐AIF‐PAs in VK2/E6E7 cells, in vitro imaging studies were carried out by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Cell membranes and nuclei were counterstained. Cell membrane was stained with WGA Alexa Fluor 594 for visualization in red, cell nuclei with Hoechst 33 342 for visualization in blue and AIF‐PAs were dyed with 5‐carboxyfluorescein for visualization in green. As shown in the merged images of Figure 7, epithelial cells displayed peptide‐associated fluorescence upon treatment with FAM‐AIF‐PAs. Particularly, cells treated with AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2, AIF‐PA2–1, and AIF‐PA2–2 exhibited intense green fluorescence, mainly localized at the plasma membrane. In contrast, cells treated with AIF‐PA1–3 and AIF‐PA2–3, both containing two copies of the same peptide sequence derivatized with a C‐terminal alkyl group, showed weaker fluorescence on the images.

Based on CLSM images, quantitative fluorescent intensity was measured as corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF), normalized to the area of the selected cells (Figure S16), to compare the cellular uptake of the different AIF‐PAs. Among the tested compounds, AIF‐PA1–3 showed the lowest uptake in VK2/E6E7 cells, whereas AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA2–2 showed the highest uptake levels.

Besides, the mucosal permeation potential of AIF‐PAs was evaluated using an in vitro permeability assay. Then, to study the mucosal permeation, transwell membrane inserts containing monolayers of VK2/E6E7 epithelial cells were employed, and the distribution of FAM‐AIF‐PAs in the apical and basolateral compartments was quantified by fluorescence (Table S6). The results of the permeability assessment demonstrated that the majority of FAM‐AIF‐PAs remained in the apical compartment, while only a small fraction was detected in the basolateral zone, suggesting that they were not able to cross the epithelial monolayer.

Discussion

Considering that treatment and prevention approaches targeting infectious and noninfectious mucosal inflammatory diseases are challenging, we designed and chemically synthesized several peptide amphiphiles based on two anti‐inflammatory peptide sequences (AIF‐PAs), with the aim to obtain self‐assembled nanostructures with mucosal surface targeting capability as a novel strategy to promote human mucosal tissue health.

In general, the exposure of AIF‐PAs to colorectal explants from Crohn's disease patients induced a downregulation of the proinflammatory profile in all tissues examined. The AIF‐PAs decreased the secretion of proinflammatory cytokine interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), interferon γ‐induced protein (IP‐10), and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1), and antimicrobial proteins granulocyte‐macrophage GM‐CSF and granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF), compared to levels observed in culture supernatants of untreated tissue explants and with different level of statistical significance in each tissue and for each peptide (Figure 2, Figure S7,S8 and Table S2). The observed inter‐donor variability was consistent with published results [30] demonstrating variability in response to treatment or prevention with the MTADV peptide in two chemically induced colitis models in mice (TNBS‐ and DSS‐induced) of both sexes. As reported by these authors, the MTADV induced reparative activity in both mouse models of induced colitis despite inducing inflammation when used as a treatment. IL‐1 cytokines have been shown to have a significant role signaling inflammation and tissue damage in IBD; however, the different modulation of IL‐1α and IL‐1β induced by the peptides in different donors warrant further investigation to understand the mechanism of action of these peptides. Measurement of IL‐1RA levels, which binds to the type I IL‐1 receptor inhibiting IL‐1α and IL‐1β, after treatment of tissue explants with peptides will help defined the observed modulation of IL‐1 cytokines. Characterization of the effect of AIF‐PAs on key molecules of the inflammatory signaling cascade, including mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and nuclear factor‐κB (NF‐κB), will be essential to define their mechanism of action [31, 32]. Other limitations linked to the use of the tissue explant model were: 1) we conducted a pilot study with 3 patients for whom we only have medical history stating that they had Crohn's disease, but no other baseline information was available under local Ethics; 2) the impact of MTADV and SQSHPRH lipid‐conjugation was not consistent for all donors, hence, further studies with an increased number of donors will be required to establish the significance of lipid‐conjugation on the anti‐inflammatory activity of these peptides; 3) we only tested the peptides at 1 concentration due to sparse availability of tissue, hence, in future studies it will be interesting to further characterize the dose–response curve of the anti‐inflammatory activity of these peptides; 4) we did not assess proteomic responses to long‐term dosing with the peptides but only the early responses 24h post‐exposure, a time points that avoids detection of background inflammation induced by tissue processing including surgical resection and cutting of explants [33, 34]; and 5) our multiplex bead array panels did not include cytokines reported as being associated with Crohn's disease such as IL‐21, IL‐22, IL‐23.

Furthermore, there has been increasing interest in modulating the mucosal environment as an HIV prevention strategy, as elevated levels of vaginal or colorectal inflammation have been strongly associated with an increased risk of HIV infection [35, 36]. In this sense, antiviral activity of the six AIF‐PAs against HIV‐1BaL was pre‐clinically assessed in human colorectal tissue explants. Dose–response curves were observed for all peptides and sub‐micromolar values of IC50 were obtained for AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA1–2 derived from the MTADV sequence thus indicating that the antiviral activity of the peptides might be correlated with the down‐regulation of proinflammatory cytokines.

Taking into account that the novel AIF‐PAs described in this work have both, anti‐inflammatory and antiviral activity, we decided to further characterize their self‐aggregation in aqueous solution. The lipopeptides derivatized at N‐ or C‐terminal end with a C16 alkyl group led to AIF‐PAs with different net charge. While AIF‐PAs derived from MTADV peptide sequence, AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2, and AIF‐PA1–3, had neutral net charge in aqueous solution at pH = 7, AIF‐PAs derived from SQSHPRH had a positive net charge of 2+ for AIF‐PA2–1; AIF‐PA2–2 and 4+ for AIF‐PA2–3. The different net charges of the synthesized AIF‐PAs determined its amphiphilic properties, which in turn affected their aggregation capacity in aqueous solutions. The net charge distribution within the peptide influences its conformation in water and its self‐assembly into larger structures. AIF‐PAs with neutral net charge, derived from MTADV, showed a β‐sheet conformation, while AIF‐PAs with positive net charge, derivatized from SQSHPRH, presented a random coil conformation. Of note, the morphology of the structures observed by TEM fully correlates with the secondary structure of the peptides and the ThT fluorescence assays. Thus, AIF‐PA1–1, AIF‐PA1–2, and AIF‐PA1–3 with a β‐sheet conformation showed the ability to form fibrillar aggregates by the ThT assay. As the concentration increases, the peptides began to aggregate, forming β‐sheet rich structures like fibrils. These fibrils are characterized by their cross‐β‐sheet structure, which is a key feature of amyloid aggregation. In contrast, AIF‐PA2–1, AIF‐PA2–2, and AIF‐PA2–3 exhibited a random coil conformation by CD and did not form fibrils by ThT assay. However, these amphiphilic peptide structures self‐aggregate in aqueous solution, as analyzed by the fluorescence ANS assay, and forms globular structures as visualized by TEM. As described by others, lipopeptide micelles comprise peptide groups with disordered conformation due to the constraints imposed by the spherical geometry [37].

To analyze the ability of AIF‐PAs to target the mucosal surface, the interaction between AIF‐PAs and POPC model membranes was first studied by SAXS, which provided structural information on their binding to membrane‐like structures. The SAXS results showed that the addition of AIF‐PAs to POPC vesicles caused relatively minor perturbation of the bilayer. The perturbation was roughly proportional to the amount of AIF‐PA added. Furthermore, this perturbation corresponded to an increase of the electronic density in one of the leaflets of the bilayer. Since the perturbation was small, it was presumably associated with the adsorption to the external leaflet. The position of the increased electronic density for AIF‐PA1–2 laid closer to the hydrophobic part of the bilayer than for AIF‐PA1–1, providing a narrower contribution than the original phosphatidylcholine headgroups. The origin of this difference could be attributed to the different charge distribution of AIF‐PA1–1 with positive charges located far from the hydrophobic chain and linked by a rather hydrophobic sequence compared to that of AIF‐PA1–2 in which the positive charges were widely separated by the rather hydrophobic peptide. The results suggested that in such case, the peptide chain laid rather flat parallel to the surface of the hydrophobic layer of the bilayer. In the case of AIF‐PA1–3, it was not clear why this peptide did not behave more similarly to AIF‐PA1–2. One reason could be that because this molecule had a bigger polar headgroup than the two other molecules, it would occupy a larger area, with the concomitant reduction of hydrophobic volume per area. Regarding AIF‐PA2–1, the electronic profile was similar to that of AIF‐PA1–2, and also very similar to the observations for AIF‐PA2–2. There was not a clear reason for this result. While in the case of AIF‐PA1–2, there was a segregation of the apolar groups close to the acyl chain, and a difference when the acyl chain was bonded to the lysine side chain; for AIF‐PA2–1, there was not a predominantly hydrophobic sequence, only a strongly polar and moderate polar segregation. AIF‐PA2–3 was different, with a much lesser effect on the bilayer structure. Part of those effects or absence of effect could further be attributed to the bigger water solubility of the different amphiphiles. Overall, the AIF‐PAs developed in this study, incorporated to preformed POPC bilayers inducing asymmetry. The peptides were close to the hydrophilic‐hydrophobic interface of the bilayer, except AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA1–3, which presented presence further into the water side. There was little perturbation of the hydrophobic part of the bilayer except for AIF‐PA1–3, which induced a strong reduction of the hydrocarbon layer. The reasons for those differences remain unknown based on the different structure of the molecules, and their charges and polar groups location. Regarding interactions with model membranes, the cationic charge and amphiphilic nature of self‐assembled AIF‐PAs facilitated their binding and penetration into lipid bilayers mimicking cellular membranes. These interactions played a role in activity, cell penetration, and cellular uptake, often mediated through electrostatic interactions between positively charged peptide regions and negatively charged membrane surfaces.

To quantify the extent and visualize the localization of AIF‐PAs in human epithelial cells, cellular uptake was assessed by flow cytometry analysis and laser scanning confocal microscopy. The results were consistent with those of SAXS, as quantitative fluorescence intensity was observed in human epithelial cells after treatment with all AIF‐PAs. As visualized in the confocal images, FAM‐AIF‐PAs were mainly located in the plasma membrane although cells treated with AIF‐PA1–3 and AIF‐PA2–3 showed lower fluorescence intensity. Notably, these two AIF‐PAs have two peptide sequences in the molecule, so the proportion of the peptide backbone to the hydrocarbon chain was doubled (dAIF‐PAs). According to SAXS analysis of these dAIF‐PAs in model membranes, a significant presence of AIF‐PA1–3 was observed in the aqueous exterior of the liposomes, and AIF‐PA2–3 had a much smaller effect on the liposome bilayer structure due to their greater aqueous solubility. These results obtained in membrane models explain the lower cellular uptake quantified in the confocal images of human epithelial cell cultures. Finally, AIF‐PAs could not cross the epithelial cell monolayer in permeability assays, indicating poor mucosal permeation potential. This lack of permeation was significant because it involves a reduced systemic absorption, thereby concentrating and retaining the bioactive peptides at the mucosal surface where local activity is desired.

In summary, AIF‐PAs mostly self‐assembled in nanostructures driven by hydrophobic and hydrogen bonding interactions, with their functional peptide accessible. These structures interacted effectively with model membranes and epithelial cells, maintaining their anti‐inflammatory and antiviral biological functions. The greater antiviral activity of AIF‐PA1–1 and AIF‐PA1–2 might be related with the exposure of the peptide sequences in the plasma membrane of human epithelial cells. However, a direct correlation between activity and secondary structure remains speculative at this stage. The amphiphilic nature of AIF‐PAs seemed to allow the delivery of the therapeutic peptides directly to the inflamed mucosal sites, reducing systemic exposure, and addressing the challenges of targeted treatment and prevention of mucosal inflammatory pathologies. Ongoing research has shown promising results using peptide amphiphile nanotherapies for mucosal inflammatory diseases by effectively decreasing inflammation. These therapies show a high safety profile and potential for targeted precision medicine in chronic inflammatory conditions such as IBD [38]. Thus, AIF‐PAs offer a novel and promising approach to the treatment and prevention of mucosal inflammatory diseases by combining peptide bioactivity with enhanced delivery and targeting through amphiphilic self‐assembly properties. This approach could overcome limitations of current therapies and improve patient outcomes significantly.

Conclusions

The design and synthesis of novel AlF‐PAs based on the MTADV and SQSHPRH peptide sequences represents an innovative approach to addressing the challenges of targeted treatment and prevention of mucosal inflammatory diseases. This strategy aimed to create self‐assembling nanostructures that can specifically target mucosal surfaces, potentially offering a new method to promote human mucosal tissue health. Overall, self‐assembled PAs with mucosal surface targeting capabilities represent a novel strategy that leverages both nanoscale self‐assembly and molecular targeting to promote human mucosal tissue health through enhanced delivery, protection, and bioactivity of peptides at mucosal sites. This approach addresses challenges of current treatments by enhancing mucosal healing, reducing inflammation, and potentially modulating the immune response locally at the affected sites.
