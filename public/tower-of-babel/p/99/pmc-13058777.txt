High Intrahepatic cccDNA in Resolved HBV Infection: Insights From Living Donor Liver Transplantation With Anti‐HBc‐Positive Grafts
Authors: Sung Kwan Bae, Nobuhisa Akamatsu, Akihiko Ichida, Yujiro Nishioka, Yoshinori Inagaki, Nozomi Miyake, Takeshi Takamoto, Yoshikuni Kawaguchi, Sumihito Tamura, Junichi Arita, Junichi Kaneko, Motoyuki Otsuka, Yasuhito Tanaka, Hiroshi Yotsuyanagi, Kyoji Moriya, Kiyoshi Hasegawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimIn hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)‐negative recipients with antibody to hepatitis B core antigen (anti‐HBc)‐positive liver grafts, hepatitis B virus (HBV) can be reactivated under post‐transplant immunosuppression. We recently reported a median intrahepatic covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) level of 238 copies/μg in HBsAg‐positive patients. Meanwhile, the potential levels of intrahepatic cccDNA in anti‐HBc‐positive grafts have never been focused on as a factor for HBV recurrence.MethodsAmong 168 patients who underwent living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) between 2018 and 2022, 4 HBsAg‐negative recipients with anti‐HBc‐positive grafts were consecutively enrolled in this prospective study. Intrahepatic cccDNA levels in donor grafts were measured based on an intraoperative liver biopsy and digital droplet polymerase chain reaction.ResultsIn all four donor grafts, intrahepatic cccDNA levels ranged from 5.2 to 408 copies/μg, and all were negative for the high‐sensitivity hepatitis B core‐related antigen. Three of four recipients experienced HBV reactivation 14–24 months after LDLT. Two patients developed hepatitis with high HBV DNA and HBsAg levels, escape mutations (G145R ± K141R) with genotype B or C were detected. Entecavir therapy achieved serological and virological clearance within 2–9 months. The fourth patient remained recurrence‐free during 36 months of follow‐up.ConclusionsEven among patients with resolved HBV infection, there are cases possessing intrahepatic cccDNA levels as high as or even higher than those in HBsAg‐positive patients. To resolve this critical pitfall in immunosuppressed patients, the development of serum markers that precisely reflect intrahepatic cccDNA levels will be essential for improving HBV prophylaxis strategies.Keywords: anti‐HBc, HBV reactivation, intrahepatic cccDNA, LDLT

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a major cause of chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) worldwide [1, 2]. Meanwhile, the shortage of organs for liver transplantation (LT) has been a critical global problem. Current efforts to overcome this organ shortage include the use of marginal liver grafts such as those from hepatitis B core antibody (anti‐HBc)‐positive donors.

However, in hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)‐negative recipients with anti‐HBc‐positive grafts, HBV may be reactivated due to post‐LT immunosuppressive therapy [3]. It is well known that the probability of HBV recurrence after LT from anti‐HBc‐positive donors depends on the HBV serological status of the recipient [4]. We previously reported that HBV‐naïve status (anti‐HBc‐negative and hepatitis B surface antibody [anti‐HBs]‐negative) of the recipients contributes to HBV recurrence after LT from anti‐HBc‐positive donors [5].

Meanwhile, potential HBV levels in anti‐HBc‐positive grafts have never been focused on as a factor of HBV recurrence. Even after HBsAg clearance, covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), the template for the transcription of viral RNAs, remains in hepatocytes [6, 7]. We recently reported that even in patients with resolved HBV infection, there are cases with high intrahepatic cccDNA levels, comparable to HBsAg‐positive cases [8, 9]. These findings also apply to anti‐HBc‐positive donors, a condition equivalent to resolved HBV infection.

In this study, we report a prospective case series of four HBsAg‐negative LDLT recipients with anti‐HBc‐positive grafts. Notably, in this study, intrahepatic cccDNA levels in donor grafts were measured based on an intraoperative liver biopsy and droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR).

Methods

Patients

This study was a prospective, single‐center, observational study conducted from April 2018 to March 2022 at the University of Tokyo Hospital. The inclusion criteria were HBsAg‐negative recipients who underwent LDLT from anti‐HBc‐positive donors. Patients with chronic liver disease complicated by HCV or HIV coinfection were excluded. During the study period, 168 consecutive LDLT procedures were performed at our center. Among them, 156 were HBsAg‐negative. Five patients received anti‐HBc‐positive grafts. After excluding one patient with an HCV co‐infected graft, four recipients were recruited for this study (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of the patients enrolled in the present study.

Informed consent was obtained from all the participating recipients and donors. The study protocol complied with the Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Tokyo Hospital (Project Number 11914).

Intraoperative Liver Biopsy and Cryopreservation of Donor Liver Tissue

For intrahepatic cccDNA quantification in anti‐HBc‐positive grafts, a 5‐mm square liver sample was obtained by an intraoperative liver biopsy at the time of anti‐HBc‐positive donor hepatectomy. The obtained samples were immediately cryopreserved at −80°C.

Quantification of Intrahepatic HBV cccDNA Levels

Intrahepatic cccDNA levels were measured by ddPCR as previously reported [10]. Briefly, total liver DNA treated with plasmid‐safe ATP‐dependent DNase (PSAD) (EPICENTRE, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) was used as the template input for ddPCR amplification. The target intrahepatic cccDNA was amplified using a C1000 touch Thermal Cycler (Bio‐Rad LABORATORIES, Hercules, California, USA). The sequences of the forward and reverse primers were 5′‐ACGGGGCGCACCTCTCTTTACGCGG‐3′ [nt: 1519–1543] and 5′‐CAAGGCACAGCTTGGAGGCTTGAAC‐3′ [nt: 1862–1886], respectively, and the probe 5′‐FAM‐AACGACCGACCTTGAGGCAT‐MGB‐3′. After amplification, positive and negative droplets were quantified using a QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio‐Rad) and the QuantaSoft software program (Version 1.7, Bio‐Rad). All intrahepatic HBV cccDNA values were normalized to the cell number assessed using an hRPP30 copy number variation assay (Bio‐Rad).

Immunoprophylaxis

Hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) monotherapy was administered as postoperative prophylaxis. HBIG was intravenously administered at 10,000 IU during the anhepatic phase. HBIG was administered once a month to maintain the HBsAb level above 200 IU/L during the first year and above 100 IU/L thereafter [11].

Immunosuppression Protocol

The details of the immunosuppression protocol are described elsewhere [12]. The post‐transplant immunosuppression regimen consisted of steroids and tacrolimus, both of which were gradually tapered. The targeted serum trough level of tacrolimus was 5 ng/mL, and methylprednisolone was prescribed at a dose of 0.05 mg/kg more than 1 year after LT.

Serological Monitoring

HBV recurrence was defined as the development of HBsAg and/or HBV DNA positivity after LT [4]. Standard biochemical tests of liver function were performed at each follow‐up visit. Measurements of HBsAg, anti‐HBs, and anti‐HBc were performed at the University of Tokyo Hospital using commercial chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) kits in an ARCHITECT ANALYSER i2000 (Abbott Japan Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The sensitivity of the HBsAg assay ranged from 0.05 to 250 IU/mL. Specimens with HBsAg values exceeding 250 IU/mL were diluted to 1:500 using a diluent recommended by the manufacturer, and the exact concentrations of the samples were measured. The sensitivity of the anti‐HBs assay ranged from 6.0 to 1000 mIU/mL. Anti‐HBc was measured using the CLIA method, in which samples with sample/cutoff (S/CO) values ≥ 1.0 were regarded as positive, while those with values < 1.0 were regarded as negative. HBV DNA levels were quantified using the COBAS TaqMan HBV Test v2.0 (Roche Diagnostics), which has a dynamic range of 2.1–9.0 log copies/mL (1.3–8.2 log IU/mL). Serum hepatitis B core‐related antigen (HBcrAg) levels were measured using a high‐sensitivity chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay (iTACT‐HBcrAg, Fujirebio Inc., Tokyo, Japan), which has a measurement range of approximately 2.1 to 7.0 log U/mL [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. These parameters were regularly evaluated every 4 weeks after LDLT. Once HBV recurrence occurred, treatment with nucleoside analogs was initiated. Patients were monitored every 4–12 weeks, and treatment was continued.

Direct Sequencing of HBV DNA

Direct sequencing of the coding region of HBs in HBV DNA was performed as follows. Viral DNA was extracted from 200 μL of patient sera using a QIAamp MinElute Virus Spin Kit (#57704, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). An approximately 1500 bp region coding for the HBs antigen was amplified by PCR using the primers 5′‐CCCTTGGACTCATAAGGTGGG‐3′ and 5′‐CGGTAAAGTACCCCAACATCC‐3′. After electrophoresis of the PCR product on an agarose gel, DNA was recovered using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (#28704, Qiagen). Direct sequencing was then conducted, and the obtained results were compared with the HBV genotype C consensus sequences (GenBank: AB246345).

Results

Patient Demographics

The patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. All patients were men, with a median age of 50 years (range, 37–63 years). The primary diseases associated with LT in these patients were alcoholic cirrhosis (n = 2), autoimmune hepatitis (AIH, n = 1), and citrullinemia type 2 (CTLN2). At the time of LT, all recipients were HBV‐naïve, except for one anti‐HBs‐positive patient. The median follow‐up period after LT was 41 months (range, 29–58 months).

Case 1

Patient 1 was a man in his 60s who underwent ABO‐incompatible LDLT for decompensated alcoholic cirrhosis (Table 1, Case 1). The recipient was HBV‐naïve. The donor was his wife, who was in her 40s, HBsAg‐negative, and anti‐HBc‐positive. The intrahepatic cccDNA level in the donor graft was 408 copies/μg. The patient tested negative for high‐sensitivity HBcrAg. Eighteen months after LT, the HBsAg level of the recipient changed from negative to positive (Figure 2a). During the subsequent clinical course, HBV DNA peaked at 7.3 log IU/mL, serum total bilirubin at 18.6 mg/dL, and ALT at 149 IU/L. A direct sequence analysis of the HBV DNA revealed an escape mutant (G145R, K141R mutation) and HBV genotype C. The serum total bilirubin and transaminase levels normalized within a few months after the initiation of entecavir treatment. HBsAg and HBV DNA were negative again after 5 and 9 months, respectively.

Case 2

Patient 2 was a man in his 50s who underwent LDLT for decompensated cirrhosis due to AIH and HCC (Table 1, Case 2). The recipient was HBsAg‐negative, anti‐HBc‐negative, and anti‐HBs‐positive. The patient had no history of HBV vaccination. The donor was his wife, who was in her 40s, HBsAg‐negative, and anti‐HBc‐positive. The intrahepatic cccDNA level in the donor graft was 9.4 copies/μg. The high‐sensitivity HBcrAg was negative. Twenty‐four months after LT, the HBsAg level of the recipient changed from negative to positive (Figure 2b). Over the subsequent course of treatment, HBV DNA peaked at 4.5 log IU/mL, serum total bilirubin at 1.7 mg/dL, and ALT at 230 IU/L. A direct sequence analysis of the HBV DNA revealed an escape mutant (G145R), with HBV genotype B. His serum total bilirubin and transaminase levels returned to normal within a few months after the initiation of entecavir. HBV DNA and HBsAg returned to negative after 6 months. Notably, at 15 months after the induction of entecavir, anti‐HBs increased to 789 mIU/mL, and entecavir could be discontinued. Since then, HBV reactivation has not yet been observed.

Case 3

Patient 3 was a man in his 40s who underwent LDLT for decompensated alcoholic cirrhosis (Table 1, Case 3). The recipient was HBV‐naïve. The donor was an older brother, who was in his 40s, HBsAg‐negative, and anti‐HBc‐positive. The intrahepatic cccDNA level in the donor graft was 10.0 copies/μg. High‐sensitivity HBcrAg was negative. Fourteen months after LT, the HBV DNA level of the recipient changed from negative to positive (Figure 2c). During the subsequent clinical course, HBV DNA peaked at 1.4 log IU/mL, serum total bilirubin at 1.2 mg/dL, and ALT at 57 IU/L, respectively. Owing to the low viral load, the HBs region could not be amplified by PCR, and escape mutations could not be identified. The HBV genotype was not determined. After entecavir was initiated, the patient's serum transaminase levels immediately returned to normal, and HBV DNA became undetectable after 2 months.

Case 4

Patient 4 was a man in his 30s who underwent auxiliary partial orthotopic LT using a left lobe graft for citrullinemia type 2 (Table 1, Case 4). The recipient was HBV‐naïve. The donor was his mother, who was in her 60s, HBsAg‐negative, and anti‐HBc‐positive. The intrahepatic cccDNA level in the donor graft was 5.2 copies/μg. High‐sensitivity HBcrAg was negative. After LT, the patient's refractory hyperammonemia significantly improved. The patient is currently being followed up for 36 months after LT, and there has been no HBV reactivation.

Discussion

It is well known that resolved HBV infections in anti‐HBc‐positive grafts can be reactivated in HBsAg‐negative recipients due to immunosuppression after LT. In a systematic review, the reactivation rate was reported as 28.2% in recipients who received no prophylaxis, 18.7% with HBIG monoprophylaxis, and 2.6% with lamivudine monoprophylaxis [4]. Even under HBIG prophylaxis, the probability of HBV recurrence depends on the HBV serological status of the recipient. In a previous report, we showed that HBV‐naïve recipients are at risk for HBV recurrence after LT [5]. However, intrahepatic cccDNA levels in anti‐HBc‐positive grafts have never been investigated as a factor of HBV recurrence. Although Caviglia et al. quantified intrahepatic cccDNA levels in anti‐HBc‐positive deceased donor livers, their study was cross‐sectional and did not prospectively assess intrahepatic cccDNA as a marker of HBV reactivation after LT [18]. In addition, as only HBsAg‐negative donor grafts were included in their study, the clinical significance of the quantified intrahepatic cccDNA levels remained uncertain in the absence of comparisons with HBsAg‐positive cases.

In this study, we measured intrahepatic cccDNA levels in living donor grafts by an intraoperative liver biopsy and ddPCR and prospectively investigated their association with post‐transplant HBV reactivation for the first time. As a result, the intrahepatic cccDNA levels in the four cases showed a considerable range, from 5.2 to 408 copies/μg. Furthermore, three of the four patients had HBV reactivation after LT (Cases 1–3). Of these, an escape mutant (G145R with or without the K141R mutation) was identified by HBV DNA sequencing in two patients (Cases 1 and 2).

In LT for HBsAg‐negative recipients with anti‐HBc‐positive grafts, the prophylactic administration of HBIG has been used in many countries; however, the reactivation rate remains high at nearly 20%. One reason for this high rate is the emergence of mutant HBV strains. Originally, HBV is known to have a high frequency of viral gene mutations because its replication process involves reverse transcription from RNA to DNA [19]. Furthermore, HBIG has been reported to promote mutations in the HBV S gene [20, 21]. In this study, two cases (Cases 1 and 2) developed G145R with or without K141R mutation. These mutations are clinically significant as they emerge as immune‐escape variants in response to immune pressure from HBV vaccination or HBIG [20]. Once such variants emerge, the effectiveness of HBIG is quite limited. Owing to this well‐recognized risk, prophylaxis with HBIG alone has not been recommended globally in recent years. Instead, prophylaxis with nucleoside analogs is now recommended, significantly improving reactivation rates to 2%–3% [22, 23]. In Japan, prophylaxis with nucleoside analogs is not yet covered by health insurance. Therefore, HBIG remains the only prophylactic treatment for HBV reactivation in LT from anti‐HBc‐positive donors.

In Case 1, the intrahepatic cccDNA level in the graft was 408 copies/μg, exceeding the median level reported for the HBsAg‐positive group (238 copies/μg) [9]. The influence of this high viral load on the emergence of the G145R and K141R mutations is still unclear. However, it was remarkable that an anti‐HBc‐positive donor graft with a latent high intrahepatic viral load was identified, which led to HBV reactivation.

Case 2 is noteworthy. Fifteen months after the introduction of entecavir for HBV reactivation, the anti‐HBs level spontaneously increased, and entecavir was successfully discontinued. Thus, acquisition of sufficient anti‐HBs can lead to the cessation of nucleoside analogs. In recent years, host genetic studies have progressed with regard to the reactivity to HBV vaccines [24, 25]. Further studies are needed for clinical application.

In Case 3, anti‐HBs levels declined rapidly despite the regular administration of adequate HBIG, resulting in early HBV reactivation at 14 months after LT. This clinical course suggests that an S‐gene mutation, such as G145R, may have already been present as a naturally occurring variant in the donor graft at the time of LT. However, the intrahepatic cccDNA level in the donor graft was only 10 copies/μg, which was insufficient for consistent PCR amplification. Thus, the presence of escape mutations in the S gene could not be confirmed. Although not definitive, this case suggests that naturally occurring mutants may contribute to early post‐LT reactivation. All three patients who developed HBV reactivation in this study showed a decline in anti‐HBs levels. This finding is consistent with previous reports [26], and from the perspective of viral mutations, may provide insights that are applicable beyond the context of LT.

Under the current guidelines for HBV reactivation in Japan, the basic policy for patients with resolved HBV infection is simply periodic monitoring of HBV markers during immunosuppressive therapy [27]. That is, nucleoside analog prophylaxis is not covered by health insurance. This differs from the global policy, in which nucleoside analog prophylaxis is recommended not only for HBsAg‐positive patients but also for those with resolved HBV infection who are at high risk of reactivation, such as in hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, rituximab‐based therapy, and LT using anti‐HBc–positive grafts [22, 23, 28]. In fact, in our previous study, the HBV reactivation rate after LT using anti‐HBc–positive donor grafts was as high as 17% (4 of 24 cases), despite HBIG prophylaxis [5]. In the present study, although the sample size was small, the reactivation rate was extremely high at 75% (3 of 4 cases). Therefore, nucleoside analog prophylaxis should be considered for health insurance coverage in Japan, at least for cases involving high‐risk immunosuppressive treatment.

In addition, we previously demonstrated that even among patients with resolved HBV infection, there are cases possessing intrahepatic cccDNA levels as high as or even higher than those in HBsAg‐positive patients [9]. In the present study, we confirmed that HBV reactivation actually occurred in a patient with resolved HBV infection who had high intrahepatic cccDNA levels (Case 1). For such “landmine” cases, the prophylactic administration of nucleoside analogs should be considered, as in HBsAg‐positive patients, even in the absence of intensive immunosuppression. This significant issue remains a pitfall in current clinical practice, as accurate intrahepatic cccDNA quantification requires both a liver biopsy and ddPCR, making it difficult to perform and not addressed in major international HBV guidelines [22, 23]. In the previous studies, we also demonstrated that the anti‐HBc antibody titer could serve as a useful serum marker for identifying cases with high intrahepatic cccDNA levels [8, 9]. However, the proposed cutoff anti‐HBc titer of 11.0 S/CO showed 50% sensitivity and 92.6% specificity, which was insufficient. In fact, in the present study, Case 1 (10.1 S/CO) could not be identified using this criterion. Notably, high‐sensitivity HBcrAg tests were negative in all cases. From this perspective, the further development of serum markers with improved sensitivity and specificity to better reflect intrahepatic cccDNA levels is warranted.

The present study had several limitations. Although this was a single‐center prospective study, the number of enrolled patients was lower than expected. Therefore, we are obliged to report it as a case series. In addition, due to the limited number of cases, we could not demonstrate a direct relationship between high intrahepatic cccDNA levels and HBV reactivation with S gene mutations. Furthermore, intrahepatic cccDNA can only be measured in one sample per donor to minimize damage to the donor liver. Therefore, it cannot be denied that this may have introduced a selection bias in the study.

However, this study is noteworthy in that it prospectively demonstrated, for the first time, the presence of latent high intrahepatic viral loads in anti‐HBc‐positive donor grafts and the occurrence of subsequent HBV reactivation. Although the sample size was limited to four cases, this finding, along with our previous reports [8, 9], may extend to all patients with resolved HBV infection receiving immunosuppressive therapy. This is particularly important from the perspective of the prophylactic administration of nucleoside analogs.

In conclusion, among patients with resolved HBV infection, there were cases with high intrahepatic cccDNA levels comparable to those in HBsAg‐positive cases. In such cases, the risk of HBV reactivation should be carefully considered under immunosuppression, as in HBsAg‐positive cases. To resolve this critical pitfall, further exploration and development of serum markers that precisely reflect intrahepatic cccDNA levels will be essential for improving HBV prophylaxis strategies.
