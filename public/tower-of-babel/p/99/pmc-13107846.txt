The association between executive function and eye–hand coordination in older adults: the partial mediation of attention, global cognition, and muscle strength
Authors: Tiecheng Wu, Shujing Lei, Jialin Chen, Minguang Yang, Hong Liu, Jingsong Wu, Youze He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEye‒hand coordination (EHC) integrates cognitive and motor processes and is important for daily functions in older adults. Poor EHC performance is often associated with a decline in cognitive function, namely, executive function and attention, as well as a decline in motor function. However, their relationship remains unclear.ObjectiveTo examine the associations among executive function, attention, global cognition, muscle strength, and EHC performance in elderly individuals.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 213 community-dwelling older adults (median age 71 years, IQR 65.5–76.0) underwent assessments of executive function (Shape Trail Test Part B, STT-B), attention (Digit Cancellation Test, DCT), global cognition (Montreal Cognitive Assessment, MoCA), EHC reaction time and accuracy (EHC-RT, EHC-ACC), right shoulder flexion strength (rSFS), and right hand grip strength (rGS). Mediation analyses were conducted via PROCESS Model 4 with 5,000 bootstrap resamples (95% CI).ResultsSTT-B score was associated with EHC-RT (ρ = 0.32, p < 0.001) and remained an independent predictor of EHC-RT (β = 0.17, p = 0.03) after adjusting for other variables in a linear regression model. Significant indirect effects of STT-B on EHC-RT were observed via DCT (β = 0.09, 95% CI [0.03, 0.17]; 23.50% of the total effect), MoCA (β = 0.18, 95% CI [0.08, 0.30]; 49.73%) and rSFS (β = 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.10]; 13.11%). Chain mediation analysis revealed a serial association from the STT-B via DCT and the MoCA to the EHC-RT (β = 0.03, 95% CI [0.00, 0.06]). Direct effect remained significant.ConclusionExecutive function was associated with EHC-RT, with attention, global cognition, and shoulder flexion strength acting as partial statistical mediators. Longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to test causal pathways.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registration Center, No. ChiCTR2300073086. Introduction

The aging of the global population has led to a marked increase in the incidence of cognitive decline, with approximately 15–20% of adults aged 65 years and older exhibiting measurable cognitive impairments in domains such as processing speed, attention, and executive control [1, 2].

As an integration of cognitive and motor functions, eye‒hand coordination (EHC) plays an essential role in daily activities (e.g., reaching, writing, and operating devices) and is susceptible to age-related decline [3]. EHC can be divided into the subabilities of visual‒motor integration, visual perception, and motor coordination, which collectively transform visual information into precise and timely motor actions [4]. A slow reaction time or reduced accuracy in EHC has been associated with increased fall risk, mobility limitations, and diminished autonomy in older adults [5]. Despite its importance, the underlying cognitive or motor mechanisms contributing to age-related EHC decline during aging remain poorly understood.

Emerging evidence suggests that executive function, which encompasses cognitive flexibility, inhibitory control, and working memory, plays a pivotal role in motor planning and visuomotor integration, thus potentially affecting EHC [6]. Functional neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that older adults recruit prefrontal cortices to a greater extent during precision motor and dual-task paradigms, suggesting compensatory activation to meet the cognitive demands of visuomotor control [7]. Moreover, deficits in executive function correlate with slowed bimanual coordination, impaired gait adaptability, and decreased fine motor precision in aging cohorts [8, 9].

However, in an EHC task such as driving, executive function does not operate in isolation. Tasks that require quick, goal-directed motor responses are dependent not only on executive function but also on the ability to sustain attention and process sensory information effectively [10]. Deficits in attention can lead to lapses in monitoring environmental cues or internal task goals, resulting in delayed reactions, missed targets, or poorly calibrated motor responses during continuous EHC activities [11]. Age-related declines in vigilance and attentional maintenance directly impair visual tracking accuracy and the ability to sustain precise motor control over time, thereby contributing substantially to visuomotor errors [12]. Recent research using naturalistic driving videos demonstrated that drivers aged 65 years and older exhibit reduced attentional capture and impaired hazard anticipation, highlighting the critical role of attentional processes in real-world EHC tasks such as driving [13]. However, research that directly studies the associations among executive function, attention and EHC task performance in older adults is lacking.

Bringing the mentioned cognitive functions together, an individual's global cognitive status represents the integrated functional capacity of the brain and provides a critical substrate for EHC. Reduced global cognition has been consistently linked to poorer performance across diverse EHC-involved tasks, including pointing accuracy, trajectory corrections, and complex multi-step object manipulations [14, 15]. This association likely arises because EHC engages distributed cortical and cerebellar networks integrating sensory processing, decision-making, memory, and motor execution[16] and because rapid, goal-directed motor responses depend not only on executive control but also critically on sustained attentional systems and the overall functional capacity of the cognitive system [17]. In addition to cognitive domains, adequate proximal muscle strength supports the stability and precision required for EHC, and its decline may further exacerbate the age-related slowing of visuomotor responses [18].

Although EHC is closely linked to executive function and attention, as well as motor function, their relationship remains unclear in the elderly population. We aim to examine the specific associations between motor function, executive function, attention and EHC in aging populations and to further elucidate the potentially interactive contributions.We hypothesize that lower executive function will be associated with poorer EHC performance in older adults, and that this association may be influenced by attention and motor function.

Method

Study design and participants

Participants were recruited by posters, telephone contact and community onsite visits across 5 neighborhoods in Fuzhou between October and December 2023. In total, 681 individuals were initially assessed for eligibility (Fig. 1). Of these, 442 were excluded prior to enrollment for the following reasons: not meeting inclusion criteria, declined participation, or unable to be contacted. A total of 239 participants (aged 60–80 years) were enrolled. During assessment, 26 participants were excluded due to incomplete data or withdrew, leaving 213 participants for the final analysis. See Fig. 1 for the detailed screening and inclusion flow. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Rehabilitation Hospital Affiliated with Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (No. 2023KY-013–02) and registered at the China Clinical Trial Registry (No. ChiCTR2300073086). Participant screening, enrollment, and analysis flowchart

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 60–80 years, with clear consciousness; (2) normal muscle strength (Manual Muscle Test score = 5); (3) right-handed (Edinburgh Handedness Inventory Chinese version, score ≥ + 40) [19]; (4) normal joint range of motion (The Functional Active Range of Motion Screen results within normal limits); (5) normal vision or corrected-to-normal vision with lenses and the absence of ocular diseases; and (6) provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe visual/hearing impairment, psychiatric disorders, or other conditions precluding study assessments; (2) history or diagnosis of diabetes, cardiovascular/cerebrovascular diseases, arthritis, Parkinson's disease, dementia, moderate to severe or recent traumatic brain injury, and severe psychosis; and (3) concurrent participation in other clinical trials that may confound outcome evaluation.

Measures

Neurocognitive assessment

Chinese Fuzhou version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)

The scale is widely used in the assessment of cognitive functions in elderly individuals, and the assessment content includes 8 aspects, namely, visuospatial function, naming, memory, attention, language fluency, abstract thinking, delayed recall, and orientation, with a total of 30 points. The higher the scores are, the better their cognitive function. The cutoff value for distinguishing normal cognitive function from cognitive impairment was < 26 points. The Chinese Fuzhou version of MoCA showed satisfactory test–retest reliability (1-week delay) values of 0.90 [20].

Shape Trail Test: Part B (STT-B)

The STT-B was used for the assessment of executive function. The participants were asked to trace a path between sequentially numbered points, alternating between circular and square nodes. To ensure task comprehension, an 8-item familiarization trial was conducted before each experimental segment. The completion time (in seconds) for actual trial were documented as the primary measure, higher STT-B indicates longer completion time, the test–retest reliability of STT-B is 0.88 [21, 22].

Digit cancellation test (DCT)

DCT used for the assessment of attention in this study. The test was administered via a matrix of 11 rows of randomized digits ranging from 1 to 9. The timed task (30 s) required participants to sequentially identify and strike through all target digits "4", with correct responses constituting the performance measure [23].

Eye–hand coordination

Eye‒hand coordination (EHC) assessment was conducted via a custom-developed software program running on a Windows 10 operating system (Microsoft Corporation Inc.) and installed on a touchscreen all-in-one device (OptiPlex 7470 AIO Series, Dell, USA), featuring a 4 × 4 grid of 16 squares for target presentation; touchscreen-based reaction time tasks could be reliably deployed in older subjects in prior studies [24]. The participants began each trial by placing their right hand on an external touchpad (T650, Logitech, China). A short auditory start cue is presented to set the readiness of participant followed by a 3-s countdown within the program at the beginning of each trial. A randomly illuminated square subsequently triggered the program to record the stimulus onset time (t1, in seconds). Upon the participant's touch on the screen, the response time (t2) was recorded, with the hand being required to return to the touchpad for a 1-s intertrial interval before the next illumination. Each session consisted of 20 trials, with quadrants balanced at five illuminations per quadrant and randomized sequences within blocks, ensuring methodological rigor. The device height was adjusted to align with the participants' eye level during standing assessments, and the research assistants instructed the participants to respond as quickly as possible while aiming for the central dot embedded in each square to maximize precision. The data were automatically captured for reaction time (RT) as the sum of all t2 minus t1 divided by 20. Accuracy was also recorded through spatial deviation measurements in millimeters (mm) between touch positions and target centers per trial. Hence, comprehensive metrics for both the temporal and accuracy data of EHC are provided.

Motor function

Hand grip strength: Right hand grip strength was tested via a manual dynamometer. Grip strength can reflect the overall strength and physical function of the human body [25]. The participants maintained an upright posture while seated, with their lower limbs resting on the ground. The participants exerted force for 3 to 5 s with the shoulder adducted and the elbow flexed at 90 degrees. Three tests are conducted, with an interval of 30 s between each test, and the average value is taken as the grip strength.

Shoulder flexion strength: Right shoulder flexion was tested via a Microfet 2 hand-held dynamometer (Hoggan Health Industries, Utah, USA). It is a critical movement during eye‒hand coordination tasks. During the test, the participants maintained a neutral shoulder position while standing. The dynamometer was placed at the distal end of the humerus, and the subject was instructed to perform maximal shoulder flexion for 3–5 s. The test was repeated three times with 30-s rest intervals between trials, and the final result was recorded as the average of the three measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via IBM Statistical Product Service Solutions (SPSS) v27.0 software. Continuous variables were first tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test, α = 0.05). Normally distributed data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (means ± SDs), whereas nonnormally distributed data are expressed as medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)]. For eye-hand coordination reaction time (EHC-RT), trials with anticipatory responses (< 200 ms) and prolonged lapses (> 3000 ms) were excluded prior to analysis to minimize the influence of outliers.

Education level was included as a covariate in all correlation, regression, and mediation analyses. For the mediation analysis, we first assessed associations among variables via Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation, as appropriate. To control for multiple testing, p-values from correlation analyses involving EHC performance and theoretically relevant candidate mediators were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Variables demonstrating significant correlations with EHC performance were further examined via multiple linear regression to identify independent predictors.

Potential indirect effects were then evaluated via the PROCESS macro for SPSS v27.0. In each model, STT-B served as the predictor, EHC-RT or EHC-ACC served as the dependent variable, and the remaining measures served as mediators. All paths were estimated via ordinary least-squares regression and tested via a nonparametric bootstrap procedure (5,000 resamples) to derive bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for effect estimates. Effects were deemed statistically significant when p values were less than 0.05 or when the bootstrapped 95% confidence interval (CI) did not include zero. For a further chain mediation model, the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance were calculated for all independent variables, with a VIF < 10 and tolerance > 0.1 indicating no severe multicollinearity, multicollinearity detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between key variables. A significant negative correlation was observed between the EHC-ACC and EHC-RT (ρ = −0.310, p < 0.001). In terms of EHC performance and other variables, EHC-ACC was significantly negatively correlated with only DCT (ρ = −0.289, p < 0.001). Regression analysis

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with EHC-RT and EHC-ACC, with STT-B, MoCA, DCT, rSFS, rGS, and years of education entered simultaneously as predictors. In the multivariable regression model, EHC-ACC was significantly associated only with DCT scores (β = −0.326, 95% CI [−0.270, −0.075]; see Supplementary Table S7). For the model with EHC-RT as the dependent variable, the overall model explained 30.94% of the variance in EHC-RT (R2 = 0.309; adjusted R2 = 0.289; N = 213). STT-B (β = 0.1736, p = 0.0316), DCT (β = −0.1800, p = 0.0267), and rSFS (β = 0.1891, p = 0.0107) were significantly associated with EHC-RT. In contrast, MoCA score (β = −0.1976, p = 0.1082), rGS (β = −0.0402, p = 0.5614), and years of education (β = 0.0057, p = 0.9459) were not significantly associated with EHC-RT (Table 2).

Chain mediation analysis

Chain mediation between executive function and eye–hand coordination

A chain mediation analysis was conducted to further explore the differential roles of attention and global cognition between executive function and eye-hand coordination. The total effect of STT-B on EHC-RT was significant (β = 0.366, 95%CI [0.24, 0.50]). After controlling for the mediators, the direct effect remained significant (β = 0.185, 95%CI [0.02, 0.34]).

The model revealed that the effects of STT-B were significantly mediated by both the DCT score (a1: β = −0.334, 95% CI [−0.45, −0.21]; b2: β = −0.173, 95%CI [−0.33, −0.01]) and the MoCA score (a2: β = −0.361, 95%CI [−0.44, − 0.28], c: β = −0.265, 95%CI [−0.50, −0.03]). The pathway effects are a1*b2: β = 0.058, 95%CI [0.00, 0.13]; a2*c: β = 0.096, 95%CI [0.01, 0.19], accounting for 15.85% and 26.23% of the total effect. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to focus on the associations between executive function, attention, global cognition, muscle strength and eye‒hand coordination (EHC) in an aging population. Our findings suggest that better performance on executive function and attention, as well as lower shoulder muscle strength, are independently associated with shorter EHC reaction times (EHC-RT). Our mediation analyses showed that performance of DCT, MoCA, and muscle strength statistically mediated the association between STT-B performance and EHC-RT, suggest the potential mediating role of attention and global cognition between executive function and EHC performance. Further a chain-mediation pattern involving both DCT and MoCA was also statistically significant. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted at the level of composite test performance rather than as evidence of isolated cognitive processes or definitive mechanistic pathways, given that STT-B, DCT, and MoCA reflect multiple overlapping cognitive, perceptual, and motor demands in older adults.

As an important function of daily living activities, EHC emerges from the interaction of the perceptual, cognitive and motor systems. Therefore, EHC performance is determined not only by the quality of visual information but also by cognitive processing (e.g., attention, prediction, decision-making) and motor function. Additionally, task characteristics such as speed-accuracy demands and sudden target perturbations markedly alter EHC execution strategies and performance [26, 27]. Multiple empirical studies and reviews have indicated that three core components, namely, visual perception, visual‒motor integration, and motor coordination, jointly determine the speed and accuracy of EHC [4, 26]. Moreover, in older adults, aging, the level of physical activity, and neurological disease affect the efficiency of these components, thereby affecting the temporal dynamics and precision of EHC [28, 29]. Hence, EHC arises from the dynamic coupling of visual perception, cognitive control, and motor output and shows an age-related decline in elderly individuals.

Among the cognitive tests assessed in the present study, STT-B showed a significant correlation with EHC reaction time. Consistent with the correlation findings, linear regression indicated that STT-B score remained an independent predictor of EHC-RT after adjusting for DCT, MoCA score, muscle strength and years of education, supporting a direct role of executive processes in EHC reaction time performance. However, performance on the STT-B reflects not only executive function but also processing speed, visual scanning, and visuomotor integration demands, which are known to contribute substantially to task completion time in older adults [21, 30]. These component processes overlap substantially with the perceptual-motor demands of EHC tasks, making STT-B particularly sensitive to EHC-related performance. Executive function includes task switching, the inhibition of irrelevant responses and rapid error correction, thus supports the online reprogramming of actions in response to changing visual information [31]. Accordingly, impairment in executive function is expected to prolong the EHC reaction time [32]. Previous work reported that executive functions contributed to EHC accuracy in children but not in adolescents or adults; as a result, children require greater cognitive resources to complete sensorimotor tasks successfully, but that study did not include older adults [33]. Our finding in an exclusively older cohort aligns with kinematic and trajectory analyses in older adults showing that specific executive subprocesses map onto observable visuomotor behaviors, such that deficits in executive function may contribute to impaired EHC performance [34]. Moreover, Lustig and colleagues tested 122 healthy adults via a virtual-reality-based paper-and-pencil tracking task and reported that higher executive/cognitive demands disrupt head-hand and eye-hand timing and coordination, effects that are often amplified in older participants, implicating executive resources in the maintenance of visuomotor synchrony [35].

There was also a significant association between DCT scores and EHC-RT, suggesting higher attention capacity may be associated with shorter EHC reaction times. Previous studies have indicated that selective attention to visual targets is critical for goal-directed EHC tasks and optimizes visuomotor processing by prioritizing task-relevant spatial information and suppressing irrelevant distractors [36]. This focused sampling stabilizes gaze, reduces the computational burden of sensorimotor transformation, and consequently shortens saccadic latencies [37]. By enhancing retinal-to-hand mapping, this process further supports predictive control, enabling rapid online corrections [38].

A previous study in older adults identified a subgroup with atypical visual tracking (deviations from the target line and disorganized gaze) that also demonstrated reduced attention test performance, suggesting a link between attentional decline and altered visual strategies [39]. Complementing these findings, Kővári and colleagues examined eye-movement and mouse-movement parameters in 19 participants completing the Psychology Experiment Building Language version of the Trail Making Test (TMT-PEBL) and reported that stronger visual attention was associated with fewer eye and mouse fixations and higher eye–hand coordination efficiency; moreover, the modulatory influence of attention on EHC was more pronounced in the more complex STT-B task [40]. Thus, declines in sustained or selective attention may force participants to adopt more disorganized visual strategies, such as more fixations and scatter in the gaze path, which in turn degrade EHC performance [41]. In our study, scores of DCT was independently associated with EHC-RT in the adjusted model, reflected the association between performance-based attention under time constraints and is also influenced by motor speed and visuospatial search strategies, rather than representing a pure measure of sustained attention. These findings are consistent with a possible role of attentional deficits in destabilizing gaze and reducing the efficiency of retinal-to-hand mapping, which may contribute to poorer EHC, and broader declines in global cognition could also be involved.

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is a cognitive assessment tool designed to capture overall cognitive status across multiple domains [42]. Because the MoCA includes visuospatial/visuo-constructional items (e.g., cube copy, clock drawing), higher MoCA scores have been associated with better performance on motor and visuomotor learning tasks, suggesting that a summary index of global cognition may relate to complex sensorimotor behaviors [43, 44]. Stronger global cognition may improve the accuracy of visual spatial localization and stabilize gaze, thereby providing more reliable visual information to guide hand movements [45]. In addition, higher global cognition can enhance visual perceptual accuracy and the efficient selection of task-relevant over irrelevant sensory information, which together support more effective, goal-directed visuomotor processing [46, 47]. During multimodal sensory integration, global cognition promotes the selective weighting and fusion of these sensory signals and thus may increase the fidelity of the sensory‒motor integration of EHC [48]. Similar patterns have been noted in clinical studies, where global cognitive decline has been associated with difficulties in sensory processing and motor planning, potentially leading to poorer EHC performance [49].

In our study, higher MoCA scores were associated with faster EHC reaction times, and MoCA emerged as a statistically significant indirect mediator in the mediation model even though it was not an independent predictor in the fully adjusted regression. This pattern may have couple interpretations. First, MoCA may index a distal or aggregate cognitive resource, such as cognitive reserve or overall processing efficiency, that contributes indirectly to EHC through its shared variance with executive and attentional task performance [50]. Second, MoCA may capture domain-general cognitive factors, including processing speed, visuospatial ability, and attention, that overlap with the perceptual and motor demands of EHC tasks, thereby producing statistical mediation without establishing a specific mechanistic pathway [51].

An unexpected positive association between shoulder flexion maximal strength and EHC reaction time was observed in our study, with greater muscle strength related to slower responses. This finding contrasts with other studies in aging populations in which low muscle strength was linked to slower reaction times [52]. Several explanations are plausible. Participants with greater strength may adopt slower, accuracy-focused strategies in precision visuomotor tasks since they are instructed to point to the center of the illuminated square as accurately as possible [53]. Moreover, age-related increases in joint stiffness and antagonist coactivation could prolong premovement muscle activity and delay movement initiation, even in individuals with high maximal voluntary contraction [54]. Finally, maximal voluntary contraction reflects peak capacity but does not capture the speed of force generation; measures such as the rate of force development and early neural drive may be more strongly associated with performance in EHC-RT [55]. Future analyses of the rate of force development, time-to-peak force, and premovement EMG may help clarify these mechanisms.

Our mediation analysis revealed that attention, global cognition, and right shoulder strength partially mediated the relationship between executive function and EHC reaction time. The identification of this partial mediation effect further highlights the multifaceted nature of the influence of executive function on EHC, which is beyond attention, global cognition, and muscle strength; executive function may also directly modulate motor responses through other cognitive or noncognitive factors [56]. In cognitive–motor integration frameworks, continuous synergy among the eyes, head and hands for sensory sampling and guidance is emphasized, and executive control provides top-down regulation, while sensory inputs supply continuous feedback to refine ongoing actions [57, 58]. Consequently, even when attentional and global cognitive resources are relatively preserved, executive control may retain a distinct contribution to coordinating motor behavior. These findings are consistent with previous results, which revealed that increased cognitive demand reduces EHC consistency, indicating impaired motor coordination under conditions of high cognitive load [35].

Further chain mediation analysis revealed a significant pathway from executive function to attention to global cognition and to EHC. This pathway extends single-mediation perspectives by showing a possible serial association in which attention and global cognition are linked in series between executive function and EHC. Efficient attentional allocation may improve sensory filtering, thereby facilitating greater cognitive performance [59]. A recent study of an aging population reported that sustained and selective attention are highly correlated with global cognition and can further predict functional task performance, such as visuomotor correction or gait speed [60]. Furthermore, a previous study indicated that attention serves as a mediating variable and plays a critical role in the relationship between cognitive decline and sensorimotor adaptation in older adults [61]. According to this interpretative model, improvements in attention could first enhance initial sensory filtering, which may be associated with better global cognitive functioning and, in turn, with improved visual‒motor coordination and thus shorter EHC-RT. However, it should be noted that the observed associations among these metrics likely reflect, to a considerable extent, their shared reliance on a common general speed factor. Indeed, both STT-B and DCT are timed tasks, and EHC-RT is inherently a reaction time-based measure; consequently, their intercorrelations may be driven by overlapping variance in processing speed. Although our analyses statistically controlled for years of education as a covariate, we acknowledge that residual confounding due to general processing speed cannot be entirely ruled out. We therefore recommend that future studies employ more refined methodological approaches to better disentangle the unique contributions of processing speed from those of higher-order cognitive and motor control mechanisms.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and as the findings were observed among older adults aged 60 to 80, caution is warranted when generalizing these results to the broader older adult population. Second, the application of strict medical and functional exclusion criteria, along with the inclusion of only right-handed participants, may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader older adult population. Third, the inclusion of cognitively healthy and highly selected participants may result in ceiling effects on the MoCA, thereby reducing its sensitivity to subtle inter-individual differences in cognition and limiting its ability to account for variability in EHC performance. Finally, the proposed mediation pathway is exploratory in nature-intended to generate hypotheses rather than confirm causal directions. It should be considered for the future research to employ longitudinal designs, experimental manipulations, or computational modeling approaches to systematically compare the relative fit of alternative mediation structures and more robustly infer underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

Our results suggest that eye-hand coordination performance is associated with both motor and cognitive functions and that better executive function and attention and less shoulder muscle strength are independent linear predictors of shorter EHC-RT. Our mediation models further demonstrated that the relationship between executive function and EHC-RT was partially mediated by attention, global cognition and shoulder strength. These findings suggest a potential intervention target for improving EHC among older adults, and future longitudinal or randomized studies are needed to confirm these implications.
