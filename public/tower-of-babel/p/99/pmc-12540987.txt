Influence of dispersed water in water in oil emulsions on oil recovery and pressure response in porous media
Authors: Hamidreza Eslami, Pourya Malmir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOil recovery from reservoirs containing emulsified crude is often hindered by elevated oil-phase viscosity, pore-throat blockage, and excessive pressure drops, all of which limit displacement efficiency. This study provides a systematic experimental assessment of water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion flow in porous media, with a focus on the coupled effects of dispersed water content, pressure dynamics, and temperature on oil recovery. Stable emulsions were prepared from crude oil sourced from a southwest Iranian reservoir using optimized salinity conditions (6000 ppm NaCl+10,000 ppm Na2SO4) to ensure long-term droplet stability. Controlled flooding tests were conducted in 2–3 D sand packs with dispersed water fractions of 0%, 10%, 20%, and 40%, while continuously monitoring pressure evolution before and after breakthrough. The results reveal a strong, non-linear relationship between dispersed water content and flow behavior. At low water fraction (10%), emulsions exhibited flow characteristics nearly identical to non-emulsified oil, maintaining low pressure fluctuations (~ 0.2 psi) and achieving the highest recovery at ambient temperature. At moderate fraction (20%), sweep efficiency improved but viscosity-induced resistance increased; elevated temperatures (up to 90 °C) mitigated this effect, reducing fluctuations and boosting recovery to 42.4%. At high fraction (40%), irreducible water saturation approached zero, but severe pore-throat blockage produced prolonged high-pressure peaks, reduced displacement efficiency, and slower post-injection pressure decay. Post-test analysis indicated that entrapped water droplets acted as micro-elastic elements, sustaining residual pressures (~ 8 psi after 1 h) and delaying full pressure stabilization. These findings demonstrate that while moderate water fractions combined with thermal optimization can enhance sweep coverage without compromising mobility, excessive dispersed water significantly degrades displacement performance. The study offers new mechanistic insight into W/O emulsion transport in high-permeability reservoirs and provides practical guidance for designing emulsion-flooding operations to balance recovery gains with operational stability.Keywords: W/O emulsion, Pressure drop, Plugging, Ultimate recovery, Irreducible water saturationSubject terms: Chemical engineering, Crude oil

Introduction

Enhanced oil recovery (EOR) has long been a critical component of petroleum production, particularly in reservoirs containing heavy or asphaltenic crudes where conventional water flooding is limited by poor sweep efficiency, viscous fingering, and early waterbreakthrough1–3. Among the many processes affecting displacement efficiency, the generation and flow of emulsions within porous media are of particular importance. Emulsions (dispersions of one immiscible liquid within another) are frequently formed during secondary recovery operations. Their role is paradoxical: on one hand, they can improve mobility control, increase sweep efficiency, and delay water breakthrough; on the other hand, they can create serious flow assurance challenges, including increased pressure drop, higher pumping costs, pipeline corrosion, separation difficulties, and even production losses2,4,5. This duality has made the study of emulsion behavior a recurring focus in EOR research over the last five decades.

Early investigations, such as those by McAuliffe6,7, demonstrated that oil–water emulsions could improve recovery efficiency by altering multiphase flow behavior in porous media. These studies provided some of the first experimental evidence that in-situ emulsification could be beneficial, particularly in mobility control. Later work built upon these findings, showing that the extent to which emulsions aid or hinder recovery depends strongly on their stability, rheology, and transport mechanisms1,3,8,9. With the growing complexity of reservoirs being developed today (many of which are heavy oil reservoirs with high water cuts) understanding the formation and behavior of emulsions has become increasingly urgent3,10,11.

Emulsions are thermodynamically unstable because the creation of a dispersed phase increases interfacial area and surface energy. Stabilization is therefore required, either through natural components present in crude oil or by external additives. In petroleum reservoirs, water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions are far more common than oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions, particularly in viscous or asphaltenic crudes, due to the predominance of oil-phase emulsifiers12–14.

Naturally occurring stabilizers in crude oils include Asphaltenes, resins, and waxes. Asphaltenes are the most important: their polyaromatic structures contain polar heteroatoms (e.g., nitrogen, sulfur, oxygen) that provide amphiphilic character, enabling adsorption at the oil–water interface14,15. Upon adsorption, Asphaltenes form rigid viscoelastic interfacial films that reduce interfacial tension and resist droplet coalescence. Resins play a complementary role, coating Asphaltenes to prevent aggregation and precipitation while enhancing interfacial elasticity13. Waxes, though less understood, are thought to strengthen emulsion stability by adsorbing at the interface or modifying the rheology of the interfacial film13,14.

External factors also exert strong control over emulsion formation. The aqueous phase composition (particularly salinity and ionic strength) can dramatically affect interfacial behavior. High salinity and the presence of divalent cations (Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺) compress electrostatic double layers, lowering repulsive forces between droplets and enhancing stability8,10,16,17. Conversely, low salinity conditions may destabilize emulsions, a principle leveraged in smart water injection strategies16. Fine particles such as clays, silica, or corrosion products can irreversibly adsorb at droplet surfaces to form Pickering emulsions, which are exceptionally stable against coalescence18,19.

Thus, emulsion formation in reservoirs is controlled by a complex interplay of natural emulsifiers, aqueous phase chemistry, reservoir temperature and pressure, and the presence of fine solids3,8,13,20. These factors vary across reservoirs, underscoring the need for reservoir-specific studies.

The stability of emulsions is generally explained through three primary mechanisms: (i) electrostatic stabilization, arising from repulsive interactions between charged droplet surfaces; (ii) steric stabilization, provided by interfacially adsorbed macromolecules such as Asphaltenes and resins that form protective layers; and (iii) particle stabilization, caused by solids that adsorb at droplet interfaces16,18–23. In crude oil systems, these mechanisms often act in concert, producing emulsions with lifetimes that range from minutes to years depending on conditions.

Reservoir temperature and pressure also play a central role. Zhang et al.13 demonstrated that at HPHT conditions, interfacial films become more rigid and resistant to deformation, thereby enhancing emulsion stability. Conversely, shear stresses in porous media may promote coalescence by rupturing interfacial films2,3. The balance between stabilizing forces and destabilizing hydrodynamic stresses determines whether emulsions will persist or coalesce under reservoir flow conditions.

Once formed, emulsions interact with porous media in complex ways. Radke’s deep-bed filtration theory4,24 provides a framework to describe droplet retention, identifying straining, where droplets larger than pore throats are physically trapped, and interception, where smaller droplets adsorb onto pore walls. Both processes contribute to permeability reduction, pressure buildup, and changes in relative permeability curves. Laboratory experiments have confirmed that W/O emulsions can cause strong pressure fluctuations during water flooding, particularly at low injection rates, and can significantly delay water breakthrough1,8,9. Recent studies using microfluidics25 and core flooding11,12 have provided pore-scale and core-scale insights, demonstrating that transport is sensitive to droplet size distribution, capillary number, wettability, and reservoir heterogeneity. These findings underscore the need for mechanistic models that couple pore-scale retention with field-scale displacement efficiency.

The rheological behavior of emulsions is another key determinant of their performance in EOR. W/O emulsions typically exhibit higher apparent viscosities than crude oils, while O/W emulsions generally reduce viscosity3,17. The viscosity of an emulsion depends strongly on dispersed-phase fraction: at low water contents, emulsions may behave as shear-thinning fluids, whereas at high water fractions they often exhibit viscoelastic or yield-stress behavior9,26,27.

This rheology directly affects the mobility ratio, a critical parameter in water flooding. Increased viscosity in W/O emulsions can improve sweep efficiency by suppressing viscous fingering5,26. However, excessive viscosity buildup increases injection pressures, reduces injectivity, and risks formation damage5,9. The balance between these competing effects is particularly important in high water-cut systems, where dispersed-phase volume fractions are large and non-Newtonian behavior dominates6,7,27–29.

Emulsions may be either naturally formed or deliberately induced. Naturally formed emulsions arise during water flooding as a result of crude oil composition and brine interactions16,20,21. Heavy oils with high Asphaltene and resin content are especially prone to stable W/O emulsification1,10. Induced emulsification, by contrast, is intentionally generated using surfactants, alkalis, or nanoparticles to enhance mobility control10,18,22,23,30,31. For example, ASP flooding combines emulsion stabilization with interfacial tension reduction to achieve improved recovery21,22. Smart water injection has also emerged as a promising strategy, exploiting ion tuning to alter interfacial properties and enhance emulsification10,16,17,30.

Recent work has extended these concepts. Zhang et al.11–13 systematically examined the role of salinity, injection rate, and solid particles in emulsion stability, while Liu et al.19 reviewed advances in understanding natural emulsifiers. These studies confirm the importance of both chemical and physical stabilization mechanisms, yet also highlight that most prior work has relied on surfactant-induced emulsions.

Despite significant progress, several gaps remain in the literature. First, the majority of studies focus on surfactant-induced systems26,30,31, whereas naturally stabilized emulsions, where Asphaltenes and resins dominate, remain less explored14,19. Second, while many studies examine emulsions at moderate water contents, the behavior of emulsions under high water-cut conditions is poorly understood. In such systems, viscosity effects may dominate, reducing recovery efficiency despite improved sweep6,7,27–29. Third, the coupled effects of salinity, injection rate, Temperature, and reservoir heterogeneity on naturally stabilized emulsions remain unresolved, despite their clear relevance to field operations5,10,11.

This study addresses these gaps by investigating the displacement behavior of naturally stabilized W/O emulsions from a southwest Iranian reservoir. Unlike many prior studies that relied on additives, emulsions in this work were stabilized solely by indigenous crude oil components under carefully controlled brine salinity and ionic strength. Flooding experiments were conducted at varying water fractions, and temperatures to evaluate the interplay of viscosity, emulsion concentration, and recovery efficiency. By focusing on high water-cut systems under reservoir-representative conditions, this study provides new insights into emulsion behavior in porous media and practical guidance for optimizing water flooding strategies in heavy and asphaltenic reservoirs.

Experimental material and methods

Brine, oil, emulsion properties

The crude oil used in this study was sourced from a producing reservoir in southwestern Iran. Field production data indicated a naturally high water cut of 8–26% by volume, which informed the selection of three representative dispersed water fractions (10%, 20%, and 40%) for water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion preparation. All emulsions were formulated without surfactants, with stability controlled by salinity of the dispersed water phase. The brine used both as the emulsion water phase and as the injection fluid during water flooding was a synthetic formulation containing 6000 ppm NaCl and 10,000 ppm Na2SO4 (Table 1).

Emulsion stability assessment

Long-term emulsion stability was essential for ensuring reproducible flooding experiments. Stability was optimized by adjusting the brine salinity to suppress coalescence and maintain uniform droplet dispersion for periods exceeding six months. Three criteria guided formulation: Droplet dispersion was verified by microscopic imaging (Microscopic uniformity). Emulsions remained stable over the full flooding duration (Long-term integrity). A single batch was used for multiple runs to maintain consistency (Sufficient volume).

Stability testing involved sampling from the top, middle, and bottom of emulsion containers at 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. Each sample was mounted between glass slides and imaged using a Dino microscope at 600 × magnification, resolving droplets as small as 5 µm. ImageJ software was used for droplet analysis. Images were converted to 8-bit format, calibrated with a 0.019 µm reference needle, and processed to automatically identify droplet diameters. Data from multiple fields of view were aggregated to generate droplet size distribution curves.

Sand pack preparation and properties

Flooding experiments were conducted using a laboratory sand pack in place of reservoir cores, which were too unconsolidated for handling. Reservoir sand was processed, mixed with 99% pure silica sand (< 100 µm) to adjust pore structure, and packed into a stainless-steel cylindrical holder. Distributors with 180-mesh steel screens were fitted at both ends to prevent sand migration, and 1 mm-spaced retaining rings were installed along the wall to minimize bypass flow. A schematic representation of the flooding apparatus is presented in Fig. 3, illustrating the arrangement of the sand pack, injection system, heating unit, and pressure measurement points.

Sand Pack was carried out vertically with vibration-assisted settling to ensure homogeneity. The Sand Pack was evacuated at 3 Pa for 10 h before brine saturation. Porosity and pore volume were determined gravimetrically, with more than 95% measurement accuracy. Absolute permeability was obtained by injecting brine at multiple flow rates and calculating from Darcy’s law using the linear pressure–flow rate relationship. The prepared sand pack was then mounted horizontally in a temperature-controlled furnace for flooding tests. Inlet and outlet pressures were continuously recorded. Flooding procedure and data collection

The flooding protocol followed a fixed sequence for all tests. First, the sand pack was saturated with brine to establish baseline pore volume and porosity. The pack was then flooded with non-emulsified oil until no water was produced, defining irreducible water saturation (Swi). Next, the selected emulsion (10%, 20%, or 40% water) was injected under controlled conditions, and the Swi value was determined again to evaluate the effect of emulsified oil. Water flooding was then performed using brine of identical salinity. Inlet and outlet pressures were recorded continuously, and effluent samples were collected at defined intervals before and after water breakthrough. These samples were analyzed for oil, water, and emulsion content, as well as for changes in emulsion stability during flow. Cumulative production data were used to calculate final oil recovery. Six flooding runs were conducted (Table 4), including additional temperature-sensitivity experiments where the 20% water emulsion was tested at 60 °C and 90 °C. This allowed evaluation of how elevated temperatures influence emulsion viscosity, droplet stability, and recovery efficiency.

Result and discussion

Emulsion stability analysis

Emulsions prepared using a magnetic stirrer (via gradual addition of saline water into oil with 30 min stirring and a 15 min final mixing step) demonstrated exceptional stability, persisting beyond 6 months (Fig. 4). Droplet size analysis using ImageJ (Fig. 5) showed minimal change over 48 h, confirming stability. Emulsions containing 6000 ppm NaCl and 10,000 ppm Na2SO4 exhibited the highest stability and were selected for flooding experiments (Fig. 6). Temperature effects were evaluated for 20% water emulsions at T = 25, 60, and 90 °C. Although higher temperatures slightly increased droplet coalescence, overall stability remained sufficient for flooding tests.

Final oil recovery and irreducible water content analysis

Final oil recovery and irreducible water saturation (Swi) are summarized in Fig. 7. Increasing the dispersed water fraction in the W/O emulsions significantly enhanced the emulsion viscosity, which improved sweep efficiency by promoting more uniform displacement fronts and reducing capillary bypassing, thereby lowering irreducible water. For SP4 (40% water), Swi after emulsion flooding was nearly zero, reflecting highly effective water displacement and excellent pore-scale coverage. Despite this, the final oil recovery for SP4 was lower than that observed for SP2 (10% water) and SP1 (0% water), suggesting that excessively high emulsion concentrations can increase flow resistance and induce pore-blocking. The presence of highly viscous water droplets likely led to fingering and non-uniform flow paths, which restricted oil mobilization from certain zones. SP2 exhibited the highest recovery at 25 °C, indicating that moderate emulsion concentrations are sufficient to enhance mobility control without causing significant flow hindrance. Notably, SP3 (20% water) demonstrated a strong positive response to increasing temperature, with recovery rising from 28.6% at 25 °C to 42.4% at 90 °C. This indicates that elevated temperatures reduce emulsion viscosity and interfacial resistance, facilitating more efficient displacement, opening previously unswept pathways, and mobilizing residual oil trapped in low-permeability zones. Overall, these results highlight a delicate balance between emulsion concentration, viscosity, and temperature: moderate emulsion content combined with thermal effects optimizes sweep efficiency, enhances oil displacement uniformity, and maximizes ultimate recovery, whereas excessive dispersed water fractions can hinder performance through pore blockage and flow instability.

(A) Final oil recovery, (B) Temperature effect, and (C) irreducible water content. Recovery decreases at high dispersed water content (SP4) due to pore blocking, while irreducible water decreases with increasing water fraction.

Pressure response analysis

In reservoirs where in-situ emulsions form, oil production after water flooding is often complicated by the presence of dispersed water droplets, which alter flow resistance and displacement efficiency. To investigate these effects, four emulsion-flooding tests (SP1–SP4) were conducted, and the pressure drop across the sand pack was continuously monitored during and after injection.

The pressure behavior of non-emulsified oil (SP1) and the 10% dispersed water emulsion (SP2) was nearly identical, exhibiting minimal fluctuations of approximately 0.2 psi (Fig. 8). This indicates that at low dispersed water fractions, the emulsion behaves similarly to pure oil, and the droplets do not significantly impede flow or alter viscous resistance. This observation is consistent with the high final recovery and relatively stable irreducible water content observed for SP2 (Figs. 7, 12), confirming that moderate emulsification does not negatively affect mobility control or sweep efficiency.

Injection pressure behavior during emulsion injection before breakthrough. Injection pressure increases with water content due to droplet entrapment and higher viscosity. In contrast, non-emulsified oil and 10% water emulsion exhibit similar, low-fluctuation behavior.

Temperature effect on pressure-drop after breakthrough and recovery. Elevated temperatures reduce post-breakthrough pressure peaks and enhance final oil recovery, mitigating pore blockage effects.

Increasing the dispersed water fraction to 20% (SP3) and 40% (SP4) introduced pronounced changes in pressure response. During emulsion injection, the pressure rose more sharply, and fluctuations increased to 3–5 psi (Figs. 8, 9). These fluctuations result from the interplay between increased emulsion viscosity and the entrapment of water droplets within pore throats. Pressure accumulates until the viscous forces are sufficient to deform the droplets and mobilize them, resulting in cyclical pressure rises and drops. This mechanism explains the observed prolonged pressure peaks, which gradually decay as droplets re-enter flow paths and the system approaches steady-state conditions (Fig. 10).

Early-time pressure behavior before breakthrough. Higher dispersed water fractions cause larger and more frequent pressure peaks.

Pressure behavior after breakthrough. 20% and 40% water emulsions show prolonged pressure peaks from in-situ emulsion formation and pathway blockage.

Before breakthrough, SP3 and SP4 exhibited an initial pressure increase during water injection, reflecting the capillary forces that must be overcome to initiate emulsion displacement. Once sufficient viscous force was applied, the emulsions began to move through the porous medium, leading to a gradual decline in pressure, although small-amplitude fluctuations persisted (~ 0.1 psi; Fig. 9). The magnitude of these fluctuations scaled with the dispersed water fraction, highlighting the significant impact of higher water content on flow resistance and the transient nature of pore-scale blocking phenomena (Figs. 11, 12).

Temperature effect on pressure-drop before breakthrough. Increasing temperature (T = 60 and 90 °C) reduces overall pressure and dampens fluctuations for 25% water emulsions, indicating decreased viscosity and improved flow.

Post-breakthrough analysis revealed that SP2 and SP1 maintained nearly constant pressures, with minor fluctuations (Fig. 10), whereas SP3 and SP4 displayed prolonged pressure peaks that decayed slowly over time. These peaks are attributed to the coalescence of emulsion droplets and the formation of in-situ emulsions, which generate temporary bridges within the pore network and restrict fluid movement. The presence of these bridges increases the reopening time of flow paths, delaying pressure stabilization and demonstrating how high dispersed water content can hinder displacement efficiency despite improving sweep coverage.

Notably, even after cessation of water injection, the sand packs exhibited elevated pressures. About 80% of the pressure difference dissipated within the first 30 min; however, a residual pressure of ~ 8 psi persisted after one hour (Fig. 13). This behavior is explained by the spring-like characteristics of dispersed water droplets, which act as micro-shock absorbers, dampening rapid pressure changes and prolonging the relaxation of the system. This phenomenon is particularly significant for SP4, where high droplet content creates sustained resistance even in the absence of flow.

Post-injection pressure stabilization. Residual pressure decreases slowly due to damping by dispersed water droplets acting as micro “shock absorbers.”

Integration with recovery and irreducible water data provides further insight into the interplay between emulsion concentration, pressure dynamics, and oil displacement. For SP2, low dispersed water fractions facilitated low-resistance flow, yielding high recovery and moderate reduction in irreducible water (Figs. 7, 12). For SP3, increased temperature reduced emulsion viscosity, enabling higher recovery (up to 42.4% at 90 °C; Fig. 12) despite transient pressure peaks, demonstrating that thermal effects can mitigate flow resistance while preserving the mobility control benefits of moderate emulsification. SP4, with the highest water content, achieved minimal irreducible water but suffered from reduced recovery due to pore-blocking and prolonged high-pressure conditions (Figs. 7, 10, 12), highlighting the trade-off between enhanced sweep efficiency and increased flow resistance.

Overall, these findings reveal a complex and interdependent relationship among emulsion viscosity, dispersed water fraction, pore-scale droplet dynamics, and temperature. Low to moderate water content emulsions improve sweep efficiency and maintain favorable pressure conditions, whereas high water fractions induce transient flow blockages, prolonged pressure peaks, and delayed stabilization. Understanding these pressure responses is critical for designing emulsion-flooding strategies in reservoirs prone to in-situ emulsification, allowing optimization of both recovery efficiency and operational stability (Fig. 7).

Emulsion water content analysis

During the emulsion injection and subsequent water flooding, the effluent emulsions were collected, and the water content was separated from the emulsion phase using centrifugation at 5000 rpm. The results, presented in Fig. 14, reveal distinct trends depending on the initial dispersed water fraction. For SP2 (10% water), the volume of separated water remained nearly constant throughout both emulsion injection and water flooding, indicating that the majority of the emulsion passed through the sand pack without significant droplet entrapment. This behavior aligns closely with the pressure response data (Figs. 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and 13) and final oil recovery results (Fig. 7A), confirming that low water fraction emulsions maintain effective mobility, avoid significant pore blocking, and produce negligible resistance in porous media with permeabilities on the order of 2–3 Darcy.

Water content in effluent emulsions during injection (B) and water flooding (C). SP2 shows minimal droplet retention, while SP3 and SP4 exhibit significant water entrapment in the porous medium.

In contrast, higher dispersed water fractions (SP3 at 20% and SP4 at 40%) displayed markedly different behavior. The centrifuged output showed that roughly 50% of the dispersed water droplets were retained within the porous medium, indicating substantial in-situ droplet entrapment. This entrapment is consistent with the prolonged pressure peaks observed during injection and post-breakthrough (Figs. 9, 10, 12, 13) and the reduced final oil recoveries for these higher water emulsions (Fig. 7A). The retained droplets effectively block pore throats and flow pathways, causing localized pressure buildup, intermittent fluctuations, and a reduction in overall displacement efficiency.

Moreover, these results suggest that in-situ emulsification can occur during water flooding. When non-emulsified oil is contacted by injected water, new emulsions form within the porous medium, increasing the effective size and retention of dispersed droplets. The resultant in-situ emulsions amplify pore-scale obstruction, enhance retention, and generate the observed pressure fluctuations, particularly for SP3 and SP4, where the combined effect of initial emulsion content and in-situ formation creates significant flow resistance.

Taken together, the water content analysis reinforces the conclusions drawn from the pressure and recovery studies: low water fraction emulsions (≤ 10%) flow efficiently with minimal plugging, while moderate to high water fractions (≥ 20%) increase droplet retention, generate significant pressure variations, and reduce recovery. These findings underscore the importance of controlling dispersed water content in emulsion flooding, especially in reservoirs susceptible to in-situ emulsion formation, to optimize sweep efficiency, manage pressure response, and maximize oil production.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the impact of dispersed water fraction, salinity, and temperature on the stability, flow behavior, and pressure response of water-in-oil emulsions in high-permeability porous media. The findings provide direct insight into the mechanisms controlling in-situ emulsion behavior and their implications for designing efficient flooding strategies in oil production.
