Operationalizing Decentralized Clinical Trials: Technology Insights from the Trials@Home RADIAL Proof‐of‐Concept Trial
Authors: Sten Hanke, Dimitrios Giannikopoulos, Bernhard Neumayer, Tea Vedenkannas, Robert Davey, Lampros Mpaltadoros, Brian Guthrie, Rebecca Jackson, Bart Lagerwaard, Mira Zuidgeest, the Trials@Home consortium
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While decentralized clinical trials (DCTs) hold great promise for improving trial accessibility and efficiency, the effective deployment of DCT elements requires robust technological infrastructure and careful system integration. Although several innovative technologies are now available for implementing DCT elements, most existing setups integrate single‐vendor solutions, which restrict the potential for tailored trial designs as well as seamless interoperability between different platforms and solutions. This paper presents operational learnings from the Trials@Home RADIAL proof‐of‐concept trial. RADIAL implemented a modular, multi‐vendor technology package. RADIAL adopted a deliberate strategy to avoid a monolithic “one‐vendor‐for‐all” solution, instead selecting technologies and integrating them only where it added clear value. Core systems—such as eConsent and Bluetooth glucometer—were fully integrated into the central platform, while other components were deliberately managed outside the core system. The aim was to implement and validate a multi‐vendor technology setup and generate learnings that would help DCT trialist in designing DCTs, especially in terms of technology selection and integration. Key challenges arose from Bring Your Own Device (BYOD) variability, immature device technologies, and infrastructure limitations at clinical sites—particularly affecting components like telemedicine. The results emphasize the significance of investing in participant support infrastructure, as well as early cross‐functional support, while automated, multichannel notifications seem to guide participant engagement. Finally, embedding compliance by planning early streamlines documentation through a clear governance model seemed to enhance agility and reduce burden.

Decentralized clinical trial (DCTs) approaches are reshaping the clinical research landscape through the integration of digital technologies and other operational operations that allow the centering of the trial around the participant. 1 Enabled by telemedicine, wearable sensors, remote eConsent platforms, electronic Patient‐Reported Outcome (ePRO)/electronic Clinical Outcome Assessment (eCOA) tools, and direct‐to‐patient services, DCTs offer the potential to reduce patient burden, expand access, and enhance data quality and operational efficiency. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5

Digital health technologies (DHTs) enable many new opportunities for clinical trials, particularly around continuous monitoring and remote assessment. DHTs range widely, including both software (e.g., mobile health and study apps) and hardware (e.g., wearable devices, sensors). 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 One key application is medication adherence monitoring, where DHTs can provide real‐time tracking and customized reminders, although studies show data quality challenges with retrospective diary entries and premature completions remaining common concerns. 10

By relocating trial‐related activities from traditional sites to participants’ homes or local care environments, DCTs seek to improve trial access, particularly for populations historically underserved by site‐based research. 11 , 12

Despite growing interest in DCTs, limited evidence is available on how best to design, deploy, and govern the technological ecosystems required to support them. Particularly lacking are operational case studies that document how multi‐vendor systems can be configured, validated, and managed effectively in a trial setting involving diverse geographies and regulatory environments. Furthermore, the implementation of DCTs is hindered by challenges, such as the heterogeneous digital infrastructure, variable participant digital literacy, regulatory ambiguity, and inconsistent technology integration across systems and vendors. 4 , 11 , 13 , 14 These challenges underscore the limitations of a one‐size‐fits‐all approach; instead, DCT technology designs must be tailored to the local regulatory, technological, and operational landscape as well as site and patient needs. 2 , 5 , 15 , 16

Although interest in DCTs has grown and numerous small‐scale pilots have been conducted, practical demonstrations at scale across multiple countries using a modular, risk‐managed approach remain limited in Europe. Most published studies to date focus on specific components—such as remote eConsent or wearable deployment—without exploring integrated operational ecosystems under regulatory oversight. 3 , 6 , 7 , 12 , 17 , 18

The RADIAL trial, conducted within the EU‐funded Trials@Home project, was initiated to fill this gap. Designed as a pan‐European, three‐arm, proof‐of‐concept trial comparing conventional, hybrid, and fully decentralized trial approaches, RADIAL aimed to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of a combination of eight decentralized trial elements, including a modular technology package. Specifically, the trial examined modular technology selection, risk‐based validation and deployment, participant onboarding and workflow coordination, post‐go‐live governance, and integration strategies across decentralized tools in a multi‐country setting.

The results and findings provide operational insights and lessons learned regarding technology selection, integration, and trial operations to guide the design and implementation of future decentralized and hybrid trials. Our approach focused on multi‐vendor hardware and software integrations to investigate both integration and interoperability challenges.

This paper is part of a series on the operational learnings from the RADIAL trial, a proof‐of‐concept trial (Box 1), where participants were enrolled in either a site‐based, hybrid, or fully decentralized arm in six European countries. This design allows for learnings on operational aspects of the different trial arms. This paper of the series presents the operationalization experience of the RADIAL trial, detailing the technology selection process, system architecture, phased deployment strategy, and post‐launch support model. It offers practical insights into implementing decentralized and hybrid trials across multiple countries, with emphasis on integration strategy, governance, and participant‐centered support. Study results themselves, as well as KPI achievements, will be published in a separate paper.

The RADIAL paper series.

This is a series of papers in which we share insights gathered during setup, regulatory submission, and conduct of the RADIAL proof‐of‐concept trial, the first clinical trial studying the feasibility and acceptability of decentralized trial approaches (DCTs). The papers will focus on:

1. Introduction into DCTs and the RADIAL trial.

2. Regulatory advice, interactions and approval.

3. Site selection and training for DCTs.

4. Recruiting and consenting decentralized trial participants.

5. Supply of investigational medicinal product and study materials to decentralized trial participants.

6. Technological solutions for DCTS.

For each of these topics, we discuss why the topic is important in DCTs, elaborate on how we set this up in the RADIAL trial, share our experiences and learnings, contrasted against what is known from literature, and provide recommendations for future trials.

Study design

RADIAL employed a unified protocol and a shared technology package to evaluate increasing levels of decentralization in clinical trial conduct. The study enrolled adults with Type II diabetes and used a commercially available insulin to enable safe testing of remote interventions without introducing investigational product complexity. Its three‐arm design enabled direct comparison between the Conventional and Hybrid approaches (randomized) (Part A) and a fully Remote approach (Part B). In the Conventional arm, activities followed traditional on‐site procedures.

The Hybrid arm introduced selected decentralized elements—such as home nurse visits and ePRO collection—while retaining some site visits. Research also from Reist et al. demonstrates that hybrid trials—combining remote and conventional approaches—allow researchers to leverage the advantages of both methodologies. 19

The Remote arm used a fully decentralized model, including remote consent, telehealth, direct‐to‐patient shipments, remote data capture, and virtual follow‐ups. The overall approach enabled a comprehensive assessment of the technological impact of decentralization within a single protocol. The study has been concluded with 2025‐01‐21. For a comprehensive overview of the study design including study methodology, study population, and rationale for RADIAL, the reader is referred to the first paper in this series “Bringing trial activities to participants – the Trials@Home RADIAL proof‐of‐concept trial investigating decentralization of trials”. 20 Details regarding the recruitment as well as the allocation in Part A and B of the trial can be found in the fourth paper in this series “Recruiting and Consenting Decentralized Clinical Trial Participants – learnings from the Trials@Home RADIAL proof‐of‐concept trial”. 21

The current paper provides a more detailed account of the technology selection process, the system architecture, phased deployment strategy, and post‐go‐live support model for the decentralized study elements during the study.

Technology selection and integration considerations

To ensure a structured, requirement‐driven technology selection process, the trial was decomposed into seven core building blocks: setup and design, recruitment and enrollment, intervention and follow‐up, data acquisition and processing, operations and coordination, participant engagement, and closeout and reporting. Operational activities were mapped to appropriate blocks, and subsequently a desk‐based scan was conducted to identify and catalog potential technologies. 18

A comprehensive set of general quality criteria was developed reflecting broader industry and regulatory standards.

The technology selection process began with an internal and external landscape analysis, including a RFI, to identify technologies suitable for supporting DCT setups. For functionalities not covered by internal consortium partners in alignment with RADIAL’s specific requirements, a public Request for Proposals (RFP) was issued to identify and contract external DCT solution providers. A total of 37 vendor submissions were received, covering 26 discrete activities. After a structured evaluation involving vendor self‐assessments, committee reviews, and live demonstrations, five vendors were selected—four external providers and one central platform vendor sourced from within the consortium. Where feasible, preference was given to vendors with prior validation history by consortium members, in order to reduce the validation and qualification burden.

The selection of the central platform vendor represented a distinct and critical step in the overall technology procurement process. Drawing on insights from the RFI and a broader technology scanning effort, two candidate platforms were identified based on their ability to meet technical specifications and financial considerations. Following an internal assessment process, a final selection was made. The chosen platform was designed to serve as the core integration hub, enabling seamless communication and data exchange across all connected systems and services.

To support the specific operational, regulatory, and user‐facing requirements of RADIAL, the platform required targeted extensions and adaptations. As the primary interface for participant interaction, a custom study app was designed, developed, and tested by the RADIAL team. This app was tightly integrated with the central platform, enabling secure and synchronized data exchange between participants and the study infrastructure (Figure 1 ).

Technology architecture and integration

From the outset, RADIAL adopted a modular, hub‐and‐spoke system architecture rather than relying on a single‐vendor solution. A central platform served as the integration and coordination layer across connected components (Figure 1 ). It comprised a central backend system, including the electronic data capture (EDC) environment, as well as a web‐based interface accessible to participants and other study stakeholders.

Data security concerns inherent in the hub‐and‐spoke architecture were systematically addressed during the design phase through a comprehensive Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) that evaluated all data flows and processing activities. Role‐based access controls applying principles of least privilege were implemented, ensuring each party could only access data necessary for their specific trial functions. Contractual agreements with all processors mandated compliance with GDPR and ISO 27001/2 security standards, while operational procedures enforced secure data transmission and storage protocols. The DPIA process identified minimal residual risks and confirmed that the multi‐layered security framework adequately protected participant data without compromising the operational benefits of the distributed architecture.

Integrated operational components

Recruitment and pre‐screening

A standalone study portal—serving as the public face of the study—provided information and directed potential participants to a pre‐screening questionnaire. The questionnaire was hosted within the core platform, facilitating creation of the full participant record upon site review and acceptance of the pre‐screener without the need for transcription or integration. Further details can be found in the fourth paper in this series “Recruiting and Consenting Decentralized Clinical Trial Participants – learnings from the Trials@Home RADIAL proof‐of‐concept trial”. 21

eConsent

RADIAL leveraged the core system’s built‐in eConsent module, eliminating the need for external integrations. This setup supported real‐time monitoring and centralized oversight, aligning with best practices for participant engagement and data management. A qualified electronic signature, compliant with eIDAS standards, was implemented for both participant and investigator signatures. Identity verification was handled via automated identification checks and facial recognition. For investigators, a digital wallet mechanism enabled the reuse of verified identity credentials across signature sessions, minimizing site burden. Signed consent forms were accessible for download by both sites and participants at any time.

Telemedicine

RADIAL implemented a hybrid telemedicine model designed for simplicity and consistency. Sessions were initiated directly from the core platform via a secure, SSO‐like link embedded as a “Join” button within participant‐facing forms. Sessions launched in a separate browser window, allowing participants continuous access to other study app features without disruption. Participants were registered automatically through integration with the RADIAL core platform, while investigators were added manually due to their lower volume. Similarly, site creation in the telemedicine platform was handled manually. Scheduling and reminders were managed centrally through the platform’s task engine, ensuring cross‐roles coordination. This lightweight integration balanced technical effort with user experience needs and proved sufficient to support remote consultations without introducing unnecessary system complexity.

Glucometer device

A commercially available Bluetooth‐enabled glucometer was provided to participants for self‐monitoring plasma glucose levels. The glucometer was fully integrated with the RADIAL mobile app—avoiding the need for separate apps and logins for participants. The device’s Bluetooth interface supported custom integration and validation using test kits, allowing reliable data transfer to the device and then to the backend via proprietary APIs.

Smart injector subsystem

In the fully remote and hybrid arms, participants captured dosing and timing data related to their daily basal insulin treatment using a Bluetooth‐enabled smart injector cap connected to a companion mobile app, enabling remote monitoring of treatment adherence. While full integration into the custom RADIAL app was considered, the device’s native app was retained due to its advanced functionality in data visualization and established workflows.

To balance usability and system coherence, RADIAL adopted a hybrid approach: Participants accessed the adherence app via a secure deferred deep link from the study app, eliminating the need for separate login. Participant users were automatically created in the adherence system’s backend via an API call from the core platform, while site user setup remained manual to minimize integration complexity.

Adherence data were transferred weekly to the core platform through proprietary web APIs. This batch transfer schedule was considered sufficient, in line with a risk‐based assessment of adherence monitoring needs. Real‐time access to adherence data via a dedicated web interface was available to site staff (Figure 2 ). Adherence algorithms that recognized suboptimal dosing patterns, titration variations, and manual data entries were summarized and generated weekly e‐mail notifications.

Operational components with no integration

Several RADIAL components were deliberately not integrated with the central study platform (Table 1 ), due to considerations of technical feasibility, optimal operational workflows, and vendor‐specific constraints. In some cases, integration would have introduced unnecessary complexity or added limited value, particularly where existing systems already supported reliable standalone operation.

The RTSM system was managed by a separate vendor and was not integrated into the main study infrastructure. While technically feasible to integrate, the logic was straightforward, and site staff could easily enter the allocation manually in the study platform. To reduce transcription risks, a simple electronic signature was required within the platform to confirm and validate the entry of arm allocation. Trial supply management was handled entirely outside the core system and did not intersect with participant‐level data flows. As such, integration offered little operational value.

The home nursing system was not integrated with the central study platform. Integration was evaluated across three areas: delegation management, nurse access to participant contact details, and source documentation. In all cases, existing vendor and site workflows were considered sufficient to meet regulatory and operational needs, making integration or substitution within the central platform unnecessary.

Similarly, the logistics vendor system was not integrated with the central study platform. Instead, study kit and Investigational Medicinal Product (IMP) delivery was managed through a dedicated “shipping” user role in the central study platform that granted vendors access only to participant contact details. This restricted access protected participant privacy while enabling accurate and timely shipments. Given the workflow’s simplicity and low frequency, manual transcription was accepted as an acceptable trade‐off.

As lab data volume was low, there was now technical integration with external systems for the central lab vendor. Instead, a lightweight barcode‐scanning feature was implemented in the study app, allowing participants to scan sample return labels. This linked each sample to the correct participant record in the study backend, ensuring accurate traceability without the overhead of full system integration.

Orchestration and workflow enablement

In addition to managing systems and data flow, RADIAL’s central platform functioned as a workflow engine—coordinating participant journeys, site activities, and study communications. The protocol’s schedule of activities was translated into logic tailored to each study arm, where activities were triggered either by specific study events or by predefined timelines. Notably, the workflow was tailored to each study arm, with specific activities and functionalities dependent on the assigned intervention. These workflows were mapped using swim lane models and executed via multichannel notifications (SMS, email, in‐app tasks), ensuring synchronized engagement across participants and site staff. The platform also supported participant‐driven event reporting: on‐demand eDiary forms allowed users to report medical events and medication changes, with dedicated companion fields enabling site staff to add professional interpretations and the assessment of the event. Structured alerts flagged serious events or missing confirmations, enabling timely follow‐up and maintaining safety oversight in decentralized settings. Centralizing these functions avoided the need for separate training or reporting systems, as the central platform supported all key workflows natively. This streamlined monitoring via real‐time dashboards and enabled timely intervention when participants disengaged.

Deployment, validation, and post‐go‐live management

RADIAL applied a structured risk‐based validation and governance model tailored to its modular, multi‐vendor setup. User Acceptance Testing (UAT) ensured that each component met functional, usability, and regulatory needs before go‐live. After deployment, weekly cross‐functional meetings tracked open issues and coordinated fixes. “Change requests” (planned system updates with user‐facing impact) and “bug fixes” (corrections of unintended system behavior) were evaluated and tested without disrupting study operations. In total, RADIAL implemented six vendor‐led backend updates, 13 user‐visible changes requiring full UAT, and three minor fixes—mainly to support onboarding, translations, and usability.

Technology support system

A dedicated technology support system, including a study‐tailored knowledge base (KB), managed by a specialized sub‐team of the RADIAL technology team, formed a central backbone of RADIAL’s technology support infrastructure. The team was familiar with the deployed technologies and equipped to assist users effectively. The support system provided centralized ticketing, multilingual support, and structured documentation. The KB served as a centralized repository for up‐to‐date guidance, study information, support videos, and more. In RADIAL, participants contacted the site for initial support. Site staff were supported by CRAs, who could provide help in the national language. When KB searches did not provide sufficient guidance to resolve an issue, CRAs or site staff could escalate issues through a centralized ticketing system, triggering expert review or live support if needed. These solutions were subsequently incorporated into the KB as FAQs or video tutorials to enhance future self‐service. While English was the default support language, native‐language staff were involved in live support calls when necessary, ensuring inclusivity and accessibility across all study sites. The technology support system (including the KB) was implemented using a single instance of the open‐source software and was not publicly accessible. Further operational details on the technology support system are available in a separate publication. 22

Technology implementation and deployment insights

Interoperability was a factor in the selection quality criteria applied in the vendor selection process for both software and hardware components. The hub‐and‐spoke architecture enabled coordination across multiple systems, but integration was often constrained by incompatible or poorly documented APIs, limited vendor flexibility, and lack of effective interoperability standards. A deliberate decision was made to incorporate components from multiple vendors to showcase the potential for modular system design and flexibility. While this approach allowed for greater adaptability and innovation, it also introduced significant challenges. A major limitation was the lack of widely adopted technical standards across current technologies, which complicated integration efforts. To mitigate the risk of incorporating immature or unproven technologies, care was taken during the selection process to include only components that had already been evaluated and tested in previous studies. This precaution aimed to ensure a certain level of reliability at the component level. End‐to‐end testing followed a risk‐based validation approach, with all critical workflows thoroughly tested to ensure safe and reliable system operation.

As previously discussed, the study design included the full integration of the glucometer with the central study app, while the smart injector system operated independently through its own data portal, patient‐facing application, and backend infrastructure. Feedback from patients and study sites indicated that a consolidated view of both insulin delivery from the smart injector system and glucose monitoring data from the glucometer would have been highly beneficial for usability and clinical insight. This highlights the potential value of deeper integration between digital health devices; however, it also underscores the associated trade‐offs—namely, increased technical complexity, higher integration efforts, and the risk of unforeseen issues arising from insufficiently tested combined systems.

Coordinating multiple external vendors introduced significant complexity in terms of communication and management and required robust oversight mechanisms. To manage service quality and streamline communication, RADIAL implemented a centralized support ticketing system that served as the interface between the study team and vendor‐specific helpdesks. Additionally, the decentralized elements introduced new operational dynamics, such as request for shipment triggers to the logistics vendor tied to participant contact data. In the early shipment requests, unclear instructions in this area led to delivery delays. These challenges and the broader operational insights from direct‐to‐participant logistics are discussed in detail in fifth paper of this series (“Supply of Study Materials and Investigational Medicinal Product to Participants – Insights from the Trials@Home RADIAL Proof‐Of‐Concept Trial”). 23

The Bring Your Own Device (BYOD) model used in RADIAL offered flexibility and reduced hardware costs but introduced significant real‐world variability in real‐world use. Device performance was influenced not only by differences in participant smartphones—such as model, operating system, and user and regional unit settings (e.g., mmol/L vs. mg/dL)—but also by the study devices themselves. Key hardware components, including the Bluetooth‐enabled smart insulin injector cap and the range of glucometers used, presented unforeseen challenges. The smart cap, in particular, demonstrated technical limitations and appeared insufficiently mature for deployment in a clinical trial setting. Similarly, the heterogeneity among glucometer models was not fully understood prior to trial initiation, and post‐deployment device control was limited. A more thorough pre‐study assessment of device variability and associated risks could have enabled targeted compatibility testing and prevented issues—such as incorrect display unit settings—that affected data capture and participant experience. As a result of both participant device and study device variability, connectivity problems emerged along two dimensions simultaneously, compounding the challenge. These issues increased participant burden and triggered a high volume of support needs, often requiring video‐based troubleshooting by site staff, CRAs, or RADIAL’s centralized technology support team. Some issues remained unresolved, prompting a mid‐study update to the eDiary to allow manual glucose entries. While this mitigated data loss, manual entry is inherently prone to error, emphasizing the need for robust digital solutions that not only accommodate BYOD variability but also incorporate well‐defined fallback mechanisms for when device integration fails.

RADIAL implemented a telemedicine solution semi‐integrated with the central study platform. This model supported over 53 sessions across 15 participants, with calls averaging ~ 10 minutes and totaling more than 8.7 hours of consultation time. Although ~ 49,9% of connection attempts failed—primarily due to system site‐level infrastructure limitations, not participant unavailability—this was not anticipated during testing. Nevertheless, the overall uptake and functionality were adequate to initiate the study and support its core operational needs. Despite extensive UAT, the telemedicine system’s integration challenges did not surface until real‐world use, highlighting the difficulty of replicating complex operational environments during pre‐deployment testing. These issues were compounded by local technical constraints at the clinical site, such as firewalls blocking video calls and limited internet bandwidth at some clinical sites. As a consequence, the integrated telemedicine solution was frequently bypassed in favor of alternative systems that were native to the study site. While this approach simplified workflows for the study teams, it led to limitations in the logging of essential metadata, such as the number, timing, and duration of teleconsultations. Moreover, the use of non‐integrated commercial tools may raise data protection and compliance risks, particularly in the absence of system validation, audit trails, or documentation of teleconsultation metadata. 24 , 25 These points have also been raised by Ma et al. who underlined the importance of internet connectivity requirements, secure data transmission in healthcare settings, and careful management of televisit recordings and storage requirements. 8

To support the technology‐related aspects of the RADIAL study, an elaborate support system was established, as described above. The RADIAL technology support system handled 170 user support tickets without needing escalation to formal system changes. Nearly half of these were related to participant access issues with the app and devices (44%), followed by general technical support (13%) and process clarifications (8%). Importantly, 7% of tickets led to updates in the multilingual KB, reinforcing the value of a closed‐loop support model.

The system’s effectiveness was limited by a fragmented communication chain from participants to sites, CRAs, and finally to centralized technology support. In 80% of cases, CRAs were bypassed with sites directly generating support tickets. Issues with third‐party technologies required further escalation to external vendor systems, causing delays particularly during on‐site activities like app installation or device connection. However, site staff and CRAs often lacked technical training for independent troubleshooting, raising questions about whether technical support should rely on site capacity or require centralized infrastructure for DCT elements.

In contrast to conventional trials—where most technology is centralized and under direct sponsor control—DCTs introduce additional complexity due to their modular, multi‐vendor setups and participant‐facing technologies. As such, change handling in RADIAL required coordination across third‐party systems, consideration of interoperability, and impact assessment on participant devices operating under varied hardware and software conditions. This highlights the need for robust, flexible change management protocols tailored to the unique demands of DCT environments.

LESSONS LEARNED AND DISCUSSIONS

While the study confirmed several known DCT challenges, it also uncovered new operational insights relevant to large‐scale implementation. The key learnings below offer practical guidance for future decentralized and hybrid trials. The learnings can be understood as a comprehensive summary reporting what worked well in the trial, which pitfalls were experienced and should be addressed in future studies, and a reflection on potential improvements—all contextualized within current state‐of‐the‐art literature.

Prioritize interoperability during vendor selection

Although specific quality criteria were applied during the technology selection process, interoperability challenges still emerged during the study—particularly in the integration of connected health devices. While prior studies have demonstrated that the integration of smart health technologies into clinical trial infrastructures is technically feasible and supported by various standards and frameworks, 26 , 27 , 28 including successful use cases in diabetes management, 29 , 30 , 31 our experience in RADIAL did not fully reflect this promise. Furthermore, we underline the findings of Chen et al. that multi‐modal data collection approaches integrating multiple wearable devices, eDiaries, and clinical outcome assessments provide comprehensive adherence monitoring, though this integration creates additional complexity in data standardization and interoperability, 6 especially when integrating solutions from different providers.

We found that technical feasibility alone was insufficient; it is equally critical to assess integration readiness, vendor flexibility, and a demonstrated track record of compatibility with EDC systems and APIs. In RADIAL, the lack of effective interoperability standards, limited API documentation, and inflexible vendor systems often required late‐stage workarounds. These added operational complexity and introduced potential risks to data integrity. These insights underscore the need to move beyond theoretical interoperability and ensure that selected technologies are practically and reliably integrable in real‐world, multi‐vendor trial settings—an area where RADIAL demonstrated that the interoperability of devices and components was only partially sufficient to meet the demands of a complex, decentralized study.

Adopt a modular, risk‐based deployment strategy

To manage complexity in multi‐vendor trials, technology components should be validated independently and, once functionally mature, integrated into the broader system and subsequently tested in the integrated setup. This phased, risk‐based approach enables timely rollout while maintaining regulatory compliance, even for components that cannot be fully validated in conventional test environments. In RADIAL, this strategy allowed technologies such as telemedicine to go live safely through staged validation and targeted governance oversight.

Use integration selectively

Not all systems require full integration. RADIAL’s “fit‐for‐purpose” approach tried to avoid over‐engineering by relying on manual processes, role‐based access, or lightweight connections in cases where data volume, risk, or participant interaction was low (e.g., logistics and RTSM).

RADIAL’s telemedicine setup, which operated via secure deep links, was only partially integrated. Despite this, it supported over 53 participant sessions and proved adequate for focused remote consultations, though it led to several technical and usability challenges during the trial, as reported earlier.

Light‐touch integration models can reduce technical complexity, lower validation overhead, and still meet operational and compliance requirements, but they need to be well tested in integration. At the same time, this model can maintain a user‐friendly experience and limit the need for more than one application for participants, which is also a preference according to EU guidelines (European Medicines Agency, 2022).

Plan for BYOD variability

BYOD strategies in clinical trials offer potential advantages over traditional data collection methods (e.g., study specific electronic or paper diaries). BYOD can improve participant compliance and user‐friendliness by allowing participants to use familiar technology. 32 , 33 It may also reduce costs associated with provisioning devices. 33 However, BYOD also presents challenges, including device heterogeneity in both hardware and software configurations, as well as potential exclusion of underrepresented populations with limited technology access. 32 While BYOD can enhance data integrity and accuracy, 34 regulatory guidance on how to use BYOD in clinical trials is still evolving. 32 Chen et al. value BYOD for increasing compliance and diversity (as participants use familia technology) but see it critical regarding inconsistent functionality, data availability, and quality across different devices. 6 Clinical trial sites generally find BYOD acceptable and appreciate its benefits, such as increased completion compliance and timely responses. 35

RADIAL’s experience with a BYOD approach revealed significant compatibility challenges. Like described, participants faced issues connecting Bluetooth‐enabled devices—particularly the smart insulin injector cap—to their personal smartphones, often due to outdated operating systems or incompatible phone models. Based on RADIAL’s experience, a more stringent BYOD strategy that is established upfront during protocol and technology planning could have mitigated many study issues. Use automated, multichannel notifications to guide participant engagement

Automated, multichannel notifications are increasingly used to enhance participant engagement in clinical trials. These systems can improve recruitment rates, with one study reporting an increase from 56% to 84% in investigator notifications for potentially eligible patients. 36 Such tools also support consistent, timely communication of protocol information to potential subjects, positively impacting study participation rates. 37 In RADIAL, task‐based alerts delivered via SMS, email, and in‐app notifications helped participants navigate the study schedule, complete forms on time, and attend virtual visits. These prompts were tailored by study arm and triggered by protocol‐defined milestones, enabling real‐time coordination across decentralized workflows. While not formally evaluated, this orchestration was intended to support participant navigation and adherence—aligned with previous findings highlighting their potential to reduce confusion and promote protocol compliance. 28 , 29 In recent studies, AI‐powered adherence support systems provide customized messaging for medication reminders, automated deadline alerts for study procedures, and predictive analytics to identify non‐adherent participants before protocol deviations occur. 6

Establish cross‐functional support infrastructure early

The literature on managing technology support in DCTs remains sparse, particularly when software and hardware components from different third‐party vendors are involved. The RADIAL study demonstrated that a centralized technology support system—combining a ticketing system and a continually updated KB—is essential for addressing the operational challenges of DCTs. 22 Also, Singh et al. noted the importance of technical support as technology use varies significantly among patients. 38

Multilingual onboarding materials, CRA support, and participant access to this centralized system enabled self‐training and reduced dropout risk, highlighting the value of integrating participant support early in the trial design.

Despite targeted training, site staff and CRAs often lacked the technical expertise required to independently resolve complex issues. This raises a critical question for future DCTs: should the burden of technical problem‐solving fall to site personnel, or should centralized, dedicated support teams be established? Based on RADIAL’s experience, we strongly recommend the latter. Centralized support not only alleviates the burden on sites but also ensures operational continuity and enhances participant safety.

Nevertheless, the effectiveness of RADIAL’s system was limited by a fragmented, multi‐step communication chain. This often caused delays in resolving issues. Direct participant support may offer efficiency gains and further reduce site burden, but it also presents challenges such as maintaining patient‐site relationships, addressing data privacy concerns, and managing language barriers.

To address these challenges, future trials should: Equip support teams to handle both participant and site‐level issues.Anticipate varying levels of digital literacy among site staff and design scalable escalation pathways.Ensure external vendor ticketing systems support coordinated troubleshooting and shared visibility across teams.Use knowledge base updates to document recurring issues and reduce long‐term support burden.Clearly define the scope and responsibilities of technical support early in the study design.

These insights from RADIAL underline the importance of planning for robust, scalable, and participant‐inclusive support systems as a core component of DCT implementation.

Embed compliance planning early to streamline documentation through flexible governance

RADIAL benefited from a clearly defined compliance plan. However, coordinating updates, documenting changes, and ensuring traceability across heterogeneous systems required rigorous governance and process discipline. While comprehensive compliance frameworks exist, studies highlight the difficulty of translating these into operational practice, particularly in trials requiring high degrees of customization, as is often the case with DCTs. 32 , 39 , 40 , 41 This challenge—clearly observed in RADIAL—is likely typical for DCT implementations and underscores the need for early planning, strong cross‐vendor coordination, and scalable governance structures as foundational elements of future DCT designs.

We recommend the use of a rigorous process for major updates, while addressing minor updates through lightweight bugfix and hotfix pathways. RADIAL’s governance model showed that this risk‐based strategy can sustain compliance and agility without overburdening teams.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE OUTLOOK

The RADIAL trial demonstrated that selected DCT components can be successfully operationalized and demonstrated feasibility under real‐world, multi‐country conditions.

RADIAL’s hub‐and‐spoke, multi‐vendor architecture enabled flexibility but introduced complexity in validation, testing, and maintenance. To support scalable modular designs, shared data models, interface standards, and early cross‐system testing should become standard practice. The BYOD approach, while enabling flexibility, introduced variability in device compatibility and user environments—highlighting the importance of technical screening, layered support, and pre‐built fallback workflows. 42

Crucially, decentralization should not shift operational burden onto participants and should ensure adequate support. Ensuring equitable access, clearly defined roles, and ethically grounded implementation will be essential to realizing the full potential of decentralized and hybrid trials.
