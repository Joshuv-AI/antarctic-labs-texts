Staged Bilateral Total Hip Arthroplasty in an Adolescent Patient With Multiple Epiphyseal Dysplasia and Bilateral Avascular Necrosis
Authors: Erik M. Hegeman, David B. Carmack, Matthew R. Schmitz, Donald N. Hope
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia is a rare congenital disorder characterized by irregular, delayed ossification at multiple epiphyses, typically in the lower extremity. In this arthroplasty in rare conditions case report, we present a 14-year-old female with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia and borderline acetabular dysplasia who developed progressive bilateral femoral head avascular necrosis. She presented with a worsening antalgic gait and bilateral hip pain starting at 10 years of age, which was refractory to nonoperative measures. She was treated with staged bilateral total hip arthroplasty when her triradiate cartilage was closed and is presenting with 3 years of clinical follow-up with excellent reported outcomes. The goal of this case presentation is to discuss the specific surgical challenges related to this patient population and highlight important considerations in the adolescent population undergoing total hip arthroplasty.

Introduction

Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED) is a heterogeneous congenital disorder characterized by irregular and delayed ossification in multiple epiphyses of long bones first described by Fairbank [1]. Its estimated prevalence is 9-16 cases per 100,000 births in the United States [2]. MED is caused by various autosomal dominant and recessive mutations. In general, the autosomal dominant forms, which are associated with mutations in collagen-related matrix proteins, typically present earlier in childhood with short stature, waddling gait, and precocious end-stage osteoarthritis and reported cases of avascular necrosis (AVN) [3,4]. This is due to highly disorganized endochondral ossification of the epiphysis, which may lead to lack of a subchondral supporting architecture. The autosomal recessive variant is associated with mutations in the sulfate transporter gene (SCL26A2) and typically presents in late adolescence or early adulthood with progressive osteoarthritis at multiple joints with noted involvement in the hands and feet with mild scoliosis [5]. Roughly 44% of those with the autosomal recessive form have birth defects including clubfoot, cleft plate, or clinodactyly, which differentiates it from the autosomal dominant form.

Patients who manifest symptoms in childhood typically present with early fatigue during long walks or play, decreased range of motion to the affected joint, pain, joint contractures, and mildly short stature with preserved truck height. Diagnosis of MED is made by radiographic evaluation of the affected joints and skeletal survey to discover all areas of pathology. Importantly, radiographic findings in MED are symmetric. This is important as differentiating MED from Legg-Calve-Perthes disease in the hip is paramount due to vastly different treatment algorithms [6]. Radiographic findings that are common in MED include small dysplastic proximal femoral epiphyses and resulting degrees of acetabular dysplasia with version abnormalities. The proximal femoral morphology usually includes a short and wide femoral neck in varus alignment. Hip flexion contractures are common. These anatomic factors make primary total hip arthroplasty (THA) a challenging reconstruction to optimize component positioning for achieving successful long-term outcomes and function in this young population. Complicating the already challenging anatomic features are possible prior hip surgical corrections such as hemiepiphysiodesis and valgus realigning osteotomies with hardware, which may add further complexity, surgical time, and considerations for THA implant selection. The patient provided written consent that her clinical information would be submitted for publication as she was 18 years old at the time of final follow-up. The aim of this manuscript is to provide a case example of THA in MED and provide a review of the available literature to help guide treatment in this unique patient population.

Case history

A 14-year-old female presented to the orthopaedic clinic with 4 years of bilateral hip pain and loss of function (Fig. 1). She has a history of MED and has undergone multiple lower extremity surgeries with valgus producing osteotomies to correct varus malalignment of the bilateral ankles and knees (Fig. 2). She reported no surgeries on her hips. Her pediatric orthopaedic surgeon obtained plain films and magnetic resonance imaging, which demonstrated bilateral hip AVN with significant subchondral collapse of the bilateral femoral heads and evidence of early osteoarthritic changes in the acetabuli (Fig. 3). She was referred for consideration of bilateral THA. On exam, she was neurovascularly intact with mild pain in her right hip and severe pain in her left hip with associated antalgic gait and difficulty ambulating even over small distances with assistive devices. She had bilateral positive Stinchfield tests and 100 degrees of passive hip flexion without flexion contractures (Fig. 4). After discussion about possible treatment options with her parents, they provided written consent to proceed with a left THA. The patient’s hip was templated prior to her surgical date. This allowed us to identify the need for small implant sizes and plan for restoration of leg lengths and offset. Given the borderline acetabular dysplasia that was seen on her radiographs, the decision was made to use a cone-style implant so the version could be more easily controlled intraoperatively. The patient first underwent a left THA via a posterior approach. Intraoperatively, there was noted increased acetabular and femoral neck anteversion with anterolateral acetabular dysplasia. Care was taken when reaming to not overream posteriorly given the relative anterior wall deficiency. A press fit 42 millimeter (mm) acetabular cup with highly cross-linked polyethylene (PE) liner was utilized with two 25 mm screws for additional fixation given the amount of lateral undercoverage. A cone-type femoral implant was utilized to address the increased femoral version. The goal was to place the stem in 20 degrees of anteversion. For the acetabular component, a combination of the transverse acetabular ligament, anterior wall contour, posterior wall contour and cup overhang, and fluoroscopic recreation of the patient’s standing anteroposterior pelvis were used to obtain and assess appropriate acetabular component anteversion as well as abduction. With the toes pointed straight upward, the combined version was assessed through evaluation of the relative head ball coverage/uncoverage anteriorly compared to that posteriorly. The femur was reamed to 13 mm, the smallest size available, and a 13 mm implant was placed. The procedure went without any complications and the patient was discharged home after physical therapy evaluation the following day. The patient was made weight-bearing as tolerated postoperatively. She was given posterior hip precautions to include no flexing the hip past 90 degrees, adducting the leg past neutral, or excessive internal rotation of the leg for the first 6 weeks after surgery. No abduction brace or pillow was utilized. She was counseled to avoid high-impact sporting activities. Postoperatively, the patient stated she had relief of all preoperative pain in her left hip with persistent debilitating right hip pain. The patient was then indicated, and her parents consented to proceed with right THA.

In a staged fashion, the patient underwent right THA 3 months later. Again, a posterior approach was used. Similar deformities were encountered, as in the index THA, which was approached in a similar fashion. The procedure went without any complications, and the patient was discharged home the next day after physical therapy evaluation (Fig. 5). At 3 years follow-up for both hips, there have been no postoperative complications, and the patient reported only mild pain in her left hip after long periods of activity and reported no complaints in her right hip (Fig. 6). She is overall very satisfied with her outcome thus far and has returned to university ambulating without assistive devices and not limited by hip pain.Figure 5Immediate postoperative AP pelvis after staged bilateral THA. Discussion

In this case, we present a 14-year-old female with MED with bilateral femoral head AVN who underwent bilateral total hip arthroplasty with 3 years of clinical follow-up. This case highlights the specific surgical challenges related to this patient population and important considerations in the adolescent populations undergoing THA.

There are very few reports of THA in the MED population. Pavone et al. reported on 7 patients who underwent bilateral THA using a short, conical, and tapered femoral stem and hemispherical acetabular component with an average of 6 years of follow-up [7]. Their population included MED patients who were in their 3rd decade of life who underwent THA due to progressive osteoarthritis. Overall, they concluded that THA to address MED can be very successful. They noted no intraoperative or early postoperative complications in their series. They had one case of aseptic loosening and proximal migration of the acetabular component at 3-year follow-up, which underwent revision. Additionally, they noted 2 cases of bone radiolucency in Charnley and DeLee zone 2 of the acetabular component. The femoral component presented one case of cortical hypertrophy in Gruen zone 5, 2 cases of radiolucency in zones 2 and 3, 3 cases in Gruen zone 4, and no case of calcar resorption. All patients reported significantly decreased pain and increased function after THA. They recommend that surgeons beware of the diaphyseal-metaphyseal mismatch with a narrow medullary canal and use of a narrow conical stem, which is ideal for its diaphyseal anchorage, rotational stability, and osseous integration [7,8].

Supporting these findings are the results from Lim et al., who performed modular cementless THA in 23 hips with MED [9]. The average age undergoing THA was 47.2 years (range 23-54). Overall, they reported an average increase in Harris hip scores (HHS) of 53.5 points from the mean preoperative HHS of 40.6 to the mean last postoperative visit HHS of 93.8 points. At final follow-up, patient-reported outcomes were excellent, 12 patients (22 hips, 95.7%) were not in pain, but 1 patient (1 hip, 4.3%) had moderate hip pain that was associated with a large osteolytic lesion at the greater trochanter. Additionally, 11 patients (21 hips, 91.3%) had no limp, and the remaining 2 patients (2 hips, 8.7%) had a slight limp, but no patient required any kind of walking aid. They concluded that the modern modular implant designs combined with refined surgical techniques offer promising outcomes to adult patients with MED and precocious end-stage osteoarthritis of the hip; however, long-term outcome studies are required.

Current controversies and future considerations

The adolescent and young adult patient undergoing THA is vastly different from the older adult patient undergoing THA for primary osteoarthritis, as the pediatric hip is more likely to have proximal femoral deformities and acetabular dysplasia, which can make a reconstruction more challenging. Some factors to consider when planning for THA in this population should include implant design, size of the implants, bearing surfaces utilized, severity of leg length discrepancies, severity of acetabular and femoral version abnormalities, and use of femoral osteotomies to achieve a hip with an anatomic center of rotation. In MED, it is common to encounter patients with short stature, which may necessitate small implants that limit offset options and head sizes further limiting reconstructive options. This was encountered in our patient. In the setting of limited implant options, it is paramount to optimize cup and femoral stem positioning by assessing the combined anteversion. This will confer stability in the setting of limited implant options. Utilizing a femoral osteotomy was considered but ultimately not indicated in this patient. The increased anteversion due to her MED was present, but she did not have a significant leg length discrepancy, which would place her at risk for excessive lengthening, in addition to requiring a derotation. Therefore, we addressed her increased anteversion with the use of a cone-style stem without an osteotomy.

Given this patient’s age, her implant will likely be used for a longer period of time compared to an older adult patient, calling into question the mechanical and design properties of current implants and bearing surfaces. We prefer the use of a ceramic head on a highly cross-linked PE liner. The new generation of highly cross-linked PE liners has improved wear characteristics with promising mid-term survivorship. [10] It is also reasonable to consider a ceramic-on-ceramic bearing surface, which has the best wear characteristics; however, recent data in older adults identified minimal differences in mid-term survivorship with highly cross-linked PE vs ceramic-on-ceramic suggesting both are potentially safe and have good long-term survivorship. [11]

A recent systematic review by Te Velde et al. looking at the results of THA in the teenage population highlights these differences [12]. In their review, they identified 16 relative heterogenous studies, which encompassed 2409 adolescent hips undergoing THA. The most common indication for THA was a pediatric hip disorder (1119, 46.9%), which included AVN, developmental dysplasia of the hip, Legg-Calve-Perthes disease, and slipped capital femoral epiphysis. Following this were causes of secondary arthritis (542, 21.9%) and other causes (366, 15.3%), such as trauma and systemic inflammatory diseases. Average HHS improvement in these patients was 39.2 points. Revision rates were highly linked to the time at which the THA was performed. Overall, they reported an 11.7% revision rate at 10 years follow-up, which is elevated from the older adult revision rate at 10 years, which is reported to be under 5% [13]. They noted a revision rate of 19.9% in those patients with THA performed prior to 2000 and 4.4% for THA performed after 2000. This difference is likely multifactorial due to the higher demands younger patients place on the implants, improved material properties and designs of later-generation implants, and better surgical techniques aimed at improving implant positioning. Future data and research is needed to evaluate how newer generation implants and use of technology such as robotic or computer-assisted devices can help improve implant longevity for this unique patient population.

Summary

THA can have successful outcomes in patients with MED with severe AVN and early osteoarthritic changes. It provides a reproducible procedure that can allow immediate weight bearing, pain control, and increased function in an adolescent population that has been living with severely limited hip function. It is important to understand the commonly seen acetabular and femoral deformities to plan the ideal implant placement to optimize implant longevity and function. Keys to successful outcomes include having cone or modular femoral stems to correct version and leg lengths and revision cups available to improve cup stability. Future studies and long-term outcome data on the use of robotic or computer-assisted THA in this population could be beneficial to assess its impact on implant longevity in this unique patient population.
