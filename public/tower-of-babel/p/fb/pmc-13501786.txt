The impact of public health services on the quality of life of migrants in China: an examination based on health records and health education
Authors: Qingjun Zhao, Yongmei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic health services accessible to urban migrants not only directly impact their health status, but may also significantly influence subjective well-being. However, existing research predominantly focuses on objective welfare aspects (e.g. disease prevention and health management), while largely neglecting its impact on subjective well-being domains such as quality of life (QoL). This study bridges this gap by extending the analytical scope of public health services (including health records and health education) from objective welfare to subjective well-being domains, thereby providing novel insights into the formation mechanisms of health equity pertaining to migrants’ QoL.MethodsCausal effect analysis was conducted using a cross-sectional dataset from the 2017 China Migrants Dynamic Survey (CMDS; valid sample size n = 154,586). Baseline estimates were generated using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, while the conditional mixed-process (CMP) estimator and omitted variable tests were employed to correct for endogeneity issues related to health records and health education. Additionally, the Karlson-Holm-Breen (KHB) decomposition method was used to explore mediating mechanisms.ResultsEstablishing health records (β = 0.0186, p < 0.01) and receiving health education (β = 0.0192, p < 0.01) significantly improved the QoL of migrant populations, and these findings held after correcting for endogeneity. Heterogeneity analysis revealed that these positive effects were more pronounced among older generations, females, urban-to-urban migrants, and inter-provincial migrants. Further KHB mediation analysis identified significant mediating roles for health habits, health behaviors, social interaction, and local identity, with local identity contributing the largest share of the mediating effect.ConclusionGovernment provision of equalized public health services to migrants can significantly enhance their QoL. This process is primarily realized through mediating mechanisms such as improved health habits, shaped health behaviors, expanded social interaction, and strengthened local identity Therefore, we recommend incorporating public health services into the top-level design of migrant social integration policies as a crucial means to achieve universal health coverage and promote social equity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27300-9.Keywords: Public Health Services, Quality of Life, Domestic Migrants, Health Record, Health Education

Background

Since the 1980s, accompanied by rapid industrialization and urbanization, China has undergone massive and sustained rural‑to‑urban migration and inter‑regional population flows. According to the Seventh National Population Census, the total number of migrants nationwide has reached 376 million, accounting for 26.6% of the total population, constituting the largest scale of population mobility worldwide. However, this large‑scale migration has markedly intensified health inequities, and its deep‑rooted institutional drivers are closely linked to the public‑service entitlement distribution pattern established by China’s long‑standing household registration (hukou) system.

China has operated a hukou system since 1958; a core feature of this system is the tight coupling of multiple basic public‑service entitlements—such as education, employment, social security, and health care—to an individual’s place of registration [1–3]. Specifically, under this arrangement, migrants who reside and work in destination areas for many years are unable to access local public health services on an equal footing with non-migrants because of hukou barriers. Moreover, being geographically distant from their place of registration prevents migrants from effectively utilizing the public health services provided in their place of origin, ultimately putting them in a situation where “both locations fail to provide adequate coverage” of health services [4]. Clearly, this hukou‑based welfare allocation mechanism not only restricts migrants’ access to and utilization of public health services, but also, in the context of massive population mobility, amplifies the pre‑existing health disadvantages they face, thereby accentuating health inequality.

To address this structural challenge, the Chinese government has successively introduced a series of policies aimed at gradually incorporating the migrant population into the coverage of public health services. In 2009, the State Council issued the “Opinions on Promoting the Gradual Equalization of Basic Public Health Services (BPHS),” which for the first time explicitly included migrants as beneficiaries of the national basic public health service program. Subsequently, a series of important measures have been implemented in succession. In 2013, the National Health Commission launched a pilot program for the equalization of BPHS for the migrant population. The 2016 “Healthy China 2030” Planning Outline explicitly called for the promotion of equalization of basic public services. The report of the 19th National Congress of the Communist Party of China further emphasized the need to build a high‑quality, efficient public health service system and to enhance migrants’ sense of “gain” in public services [5].

It is crucial to clarify that, within the context of this study, BPHS possesses a specific institutional connotation unique to China. Distinct from inpatient and specialized medical services primarily covered by social medical insurance, BPHS is fully subsidized by government finance and provided free of charge to the resident population. It focuses on establishing health records, health education, vaccination, infectious disease prevention and control, and follow-up management for chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes. At the implementation level, BPHS is primarily delivered through primary healthcare institutions, such as Community Health Service Centers and Township Health Centers. These institutions not only undertake public health functions in the traditional sense but also serve as the cornerstone of China’s primary healthcare system. They provide basic diagnostic and treatment services for common and frequently-occurring diseases to all residents, including the migrants, without hukou-based entry restrictions. Consequently, BPHS is not merely limited to health education or preventive projects; rather, it constitutes a comprehensive institutional arrangement for public health and primary care. Underpinned by the primary healthcare system, it integrates the functions of prevention, management, and basic medical treatment.

While the National Basic Public Health Service Program aims to cover the entire Chinese population, migrants have historically been structurally excluded from accessing these services in their host cities due to the hukou system. Therefore, the ‘Equalization of BPHS’ policy analyzed in this study is not about creating a new system for migrants, but rather about removing institutional barriers to ensure migrants can access these essential ‘health gatekeeper’ services on par with local registered residents. Taking the “National Basic Public Health Service Program,” launched in 2009 and further expanded in 2019, as an example, its scope has evolved from a limited set of initial items to encompass 28 domains, including health‑record establishment, infectious disease control, elderly health management, chronic disease management, and health education. Among these, health records and health education serve as foundational public services with universal applicability, aiming to gradually reduce health disparities caused by household registration through systematic and standardized service delivery, thereby improving the overall health of the population.

Through the policy of equalizing basic public health services, migrants are brought within the service coverage of Community Health Service Centers. This signifies not only access to cost-free preventive services but, more crucially, the establishment of a “Medical Home” in their destination cities—a point of access for primary medical care characterized by low barriers to entry. While this does not fully substitute for medical insurance designed to mitigate the risks of catastrophic illnesses, at the current stage, by providing convenient, affordable, or even free primary diagnosis, treatment, and health management, it substantially alleviates migrants’ daily anxieties regarding the “difficulty and high cost of accessing medical care.” Evidently, this initiative constitutes, in essence, a comprehensive and systematic health governance measure. It commits not only to disease prevention in the traditional sense but also achieves the organic integration of “health promotion” and “disease diagnosis and treatment” by fortifying primary healthcare services. It should be noted that, although positive progress has been made in various domains, the full implementation of the program continues to face numerous challenges stemming from practical factors such as the uneven distribution of regional medical resources, variations in local implementation capacity, and the intensifying aging of the population.

Compared with the vigorous promotion of policy design and implementation at the national level, the academic community still lacks sufficiently rigorous and in‑depth empirical research on the actual role of public health services in the lives of migrants. An important yet unanswered question of practical significance is: what exact role do public health services play in the QoL of migrant populations? To address this research gap, this paper utilizes data from the “China Migrant Dynamic Monitoring Survey” organized by the National Health Commission of China, constructing an explicit “quality‑of‑life” indicator to measure migrants’ subjective welfare. Focusing on two dimensions—establishment of health records and health education services—we assess the impact of public health services on migrants’ QoL and further explore the heterogeneity of these effects and their underlying mechanisms.

Compared with existing studies, this paper makes three main contributions: (1) From a research standpoint, it expands the scope of public health service impacts from traditional objective health indicators (e.g. morbidity, life expectancy) to the subjective domain, namely migrants’ overall QoL and subjective well‑being. It is the first systematic examination of how the goal of BPHS (health records and health education) progresses from “curing disease” to “living better,” offering a new analytical pathway for understanding the formation mechanisms of migrants’ health rights and QoL. (2) In terms of measurement methodology, focusing on the specific group of domestic migrants and their urban employment contexts, we innovatively construct a quality‑of‑life measurement framework from two perspectives: perceived urban belonging (subjective) and community participation behavior (objective). By integrating subjective experiences with objective practices, we identify and capture core aspects crucial to migrants’ daily life experiences, providing a more targeted and effective tool for precisely assessing migrants’ QoL. (3) Regarding mechanism identification, after first confirming a causal effect of public health services on migrants’ QoL, we systematically dissect the impact pathways across multiple dimensions—health habits, health behaviors, social interaction, and identity recognition. This analysis encompasses both objective behavioral changes and subjective identity construction, thereby furnishing robust empirical evidence and theoretical support for subsequent policy optimization and health‑promotion strategies.

Literature review and theoretical analysis

Literature review

As China’s urbanization deepens, the health issues of migrant populations have attracted increasing scholarly attention. However, migrants often have a structural disadvantage in accessing public health services in destination areas, and the level of public‑health resources they actually receive is generally lower than that of local hukou‑registered residents [6]. This hukou‑based service allocation mechanism not only constrains migrants’ access to BPHS but also, to some extent, limits the overall improvement of the group’s welfare, including QoL.

Existing research has focused extensively on the impact of public health services on the objective health outcomes of migrants, including disease prevention, health management, and healthcare service utilization [7–9]. Multiple empirical studies have shown that the accessibility and utilization of public health services directly affect the health status of migrants. For example, studies based on CMDS data have found that the equalization of public health services significantly improves the utilization of medical services by migrants and reduces health inequities [8]. Specifically, the implementation of BPHS indirectly improves the health behaviors and health outcomes of migrants by enhancing the accessibility of inpatient services, strengthening health education, and establishing health records [10]. Furthermore, health education plays a key role in improving the health literacy of migrants, while health records, as a continuous health management tool, significantly improve the overall health status and social integration of migrants [11].

As a key component of public health services, health records play an important role in systematically managing health information and supporting targeted disease prevention. Studies have found that the coverage rate of resident health records is closely related to the participation of migrants in health education, particularly in elderly migrant populations, where it significantly improves health status [12, 13]. Rural migrants who establish health records in their destination areas, participate in health education, and join local medical insurance systems experience positive impacts on their public health service utilization and health status [14, 15]. Health records indirectly enhance the long-term settlement intentions of migrants by improving health monitoring and service accessibility, suggesting a potential positive impact on their QoL [16]. However, existing research often positions health records as a health management tool and tends to overlook their effects on subjective well-being. Only a few studies suggest that health records may enhance QoL by increasing health security and social belonging [17, 18].

In terms of public health education, studies generally indicate that this effectively enhances the health awareness and behavioral patterns of migrants through lectures, promotional materials, and community consultations, with positive impacts on chronic disease prevention and healthcare service utilization [10, 19, 20]. A cross-sectional study of the migrants found that health education promotes healthcare service utilization, particularly in migrant populations, with the effect mediated by health literacy [21]. Other studies emphasize the bridging role of the family doctor contract services in connecting health education and health records, further confirming the benefits of public health services for the objective welfare of migrants [11].

Overall, the existing literature provides a valuable foundation for understanding the impact of public health services on migrant health, yet considerable research gaps remain to be explored. First, although health records and health education are regarded as pivotal policy instruments for promoting migrant health, their effects on migrants’ subjective well‑being have not been systematically evaluated, particularly lacking rigorous causal inference and mechanistic analyses. Second, the majority of studies continue to focus on objective health metrics, resulting in a relatively weak examination of how public health services influence the subjective dimension of migrants’ QoL, and the pathways of effect remain unclear. Consequently, it is essential—within the context of China’s institutional framework—to further elucidate the mechanisms by which public health services affect migrants’ subjective well‑being, with particular attention to the potential pathways and policy value of health records and health education in enhancing their QoL.

Theoretical analysis

QoL as a multidimensional complex construct, fundamentally reflects an individual’s subjective evaluation and cognitive synthesis of overall life conditions [22, 23]. Its multidimensionality does not imply that measurement instruments must encompass every conceivable dimension; rather, it permits and encourages researchers to identify, within specific populations and social contexts, the core dimensions that offer the greatest explanatory power and discriminative ability, thereby constructing context‑specific measurement models [23, 24]. As the QoL research field has progressed from early universal scales (e.g., WHOQOL‑100, SF‑36) toward disease‑specific, occupation‑specific, or culture‑specific instruments [25], measurement of QoL for particular vulnerable or marginal groups should further emphasize their unique structural constraints and core concerns.

Within China’s distinctive hukou system and dual urban‑rural structure, roughly 300 million rural‑registered domestic migrants who have worked, lived, and paid taxes in cities for extended periods nonetheless experience substantial disparities in housing, education, healthcare, and social security services compared with local residents due to institutional exclusion. This structural dilemma of “economic absorption coupled with social exclusion” endows migrants in China with a fundamentally different QoL profile from that of native urban dwellers [26, 27]. For local residents, employment income, health status, and leisure activities typically constitute the core variables of QoL. For migrants, however, while these factors remain foundational, they are not decisive; what truly determines whether they can “stay” and “live happily” is the extent to which they obtain identity recognition, social acceptance, and emotional belonging in the host city [28, 29].

First, perceived urban belonging constitutes the psychological core of QoL. For Chinese migrants, having left their familiar rural social networks, they often occupy a vulnerable “outsider” status within the city. Second, community participation behavior serves as a behavioral manifestation and integrative indicator of QoL. It signifies that migrant individuals transcend the mindset of “temporary worker” or “foreign guest,” adopting a “proprietor” stance to engage in urban public affairs. “Perceived urban belonging” and “social participation behavior” together form two pivotal dimensions for assessing the QoL of Chinese migrants in urban settings. The former reveals an internal psychological integration, serving as the subjective anchor of QoL. The latter demonstrates external behavioral integration capacity, providing an objective corroboration of QoL. This measurement framework, centered on sociocultural adaptation, not only aligns tightly with the core lived experiences of Chinese migrants but also offers a highly explanatory analytical tool for deepening understanding of their urban survival and development. Accordingly, this paper adopts “perceived urban belonging” and “social participation behavior” as the central constructs for measuring migrants’ QoL in their host cities.

Public health services primarily influence the QoL of migrants through the following pathways: first, encouraging healthy living. Through health education activities (e.g., community lectures, promotional materials, and new media platforms), migrants can systematically access and learn about health knowledge, understand the importance of good hygiene habits (e.g., balanced diet, regular exercise, disease prevention), thereby gradually improving their behavioral patterns, enhancing health literacy, and reducing health risks [30]. This positive transformation in knowledge and behavior can directly enhance their life satisfaction and active participation in social life.

Second, public health services improve the accessibility and utilization efficiency of health services [31]. On the one hand, through the establishment of health records and the provision of regular physical examinations and screening services, migrants are enabled to detect health issues earlier and adopt preventive and diagnostic measures more effectively [32]. On the other hand, the Community Health Service Centers, which underpin the BPHS, provide migrants with primary diagnostic and treatment services that function as a “safety net.” For many migrants who are excluded from the local medical insurance system, these institutions offer affordable channels for the treatment of common diseases. This tangible assurance of access to medical care, combined with the sense of control derived from health management, provides a solid physiological and material foundation for a high quality of life. Migrants transition from passive coping to active health management, thereby improving their overall health status and laying the physiological and psychological groundwork for a high quality of life.

Third, promoting social interaction. Government and social organizations conduct various health-related activities in communities, such as health lectures, free medical clinics, and psychological counseling, creating platforms for interaction between migrants and local residents and community workers. These activities not only expand the social support networks of migrants, enhancing their sense of social integration, but also help alleviate social isolation and psychological loneliness, thereby improving life satisfaction [33]. A more supportive social network may also encourage migrants to develop a willingness and behavior of giving back to the community and helping others, triggering social feedback behaviors.

Fourth, strengthening local identity. The equalization of public health services is a form of social empowerment, signifying the institutional recognition of migrants’ rights to social welfare, which helps to alleviate the “outsider” identity label [34, 35]. When migrants receive the same public health services as local residents, they may feel a sense of being accepted and treated equally, thereby enhancing their sense of belonging and identification with the city and society. This psychological integration and identification are key mechanisms in measuring the improvement of their QoL.

In summary, public health services promote the improvement of migrants’ QoL by improving hygiene habits, guiding healthy behaviors, expanding social interaction channels, and strengthening local identity, thereby promoting QoL from both cognitive and behavioral dimensions. Concurrently, the operation of these mechanisms is often contingent upon the institutional foundation of primary healthcare accessibility.

Methods

Data source

The data employed in this study are primarily derived from the CMDS. Conducted by the National Health Commission of China, the survey utilizes a stratified, multistage, probability‑proportional‑to‑size sampling design to comprehensively gather multidimensional data on migrants and their household members, including basic demographics, employment and income‑expenditure profiles, access to public services, migration and residence intentions, as well as social integration and mental health indicators. Because only respondents who have resided in the destination area for more than six months are eligible for public‑health‑service questions, this study excludes samples with a residence duration of less than six months. After applying these criteria, a final set of 154,586 valid samples is obtained.

Variables

The dependent variable is QoL. For Chinese migrants—particularly rural‑to‑urban or cross‑regional migrants—the core challenge to urban QoL does not lie in basic material subsistence (e.g., food, housing) but in attaining a sense of belonging, achieving social mobility, and establishing new social networks within an unfamiliar social environment. This fundamentally differs from the QoL dimensions emphasized by residents who remain in their place of origin. Accordingly, this paper measures migrants’ QoL in destination cities along two dimensions: perceived urban belonging and social participation behavior. The urban belonging perception index is derived from four items in the CMDS questionnaire that capture respondents’ lived experiences in the host city, whereas the social participation behavior index is based on a single CMDS item assessing respondents’ voluntary engagement in the host city’s social life (see Table 1).

This study employs the mean Euclidean distance method to comprehensively calculate the various indicators of migrant QoL and construct a total index [36]. This method treats the performance of migrants across multiple dimensions of QoL as points in a multi-dimensional space. The relative distance between these points and two predefined optimal and worst-case reference points is calculated to achieve a quantitative assessment of QoL. Specifically, the total index is determined by the forward distance to the worst point and the reverse distance to the optimal point, reflecting the extent to which an individual’s QoL approaches the ideal state. Compared to traditional comprehensive evaluation methods such as principal component analysis, entropy weight method, or equal-weighted linear weighting, the mean Euclidean distance method has the following three advantages:

First, perceived urban belonging (PUB) constitutes the psychological core of QoL. Chinese migrants detach from their familiar rural kin networks and often occupy a disadvantaged “outsider” status in the city. Consequently, affection for the city (Q1) and attention to local development (Q2) serve as primary indicators of emotional attachment and investment, forming the foundation of high‑quality urban life. Willingness to integrate (Q3) and perceived acceptance (Q4) directly address the most sensitive and pivotal component of migrants’ QoL—social integration. If migrants feel excluded or unable to integrate, their psychological well‑being and life satisfaction deteriorate markedly, regardless of satisfactory income or housing. Hence, these four items are not arbitrary “urban belonging” questions; they precisely capture the psychological core determinants of migrant urban life experiences within the Chinese context.

Second, community participation behavior (CPB). operationalizes QoL as a behavioral and integrative indicator. Among Chinese migrants, proactive involvement in activities such as blood donation and volunteering carries exceptional significance (Q5). This behavior transcends mere “helping others” and directly corresponds to the “practical dimension” of migrants’ QoL. It signifies that migrants move beyond a “temporary worker” or “outsider” mindset, adopting a “citizen‑owner” stance in urban public affairs. This constitutes an active identity construction and social integration process. Migrants who are able and willing to engage in such participation typically possess greater social capital, more stable living conditions, and a stronger self‑identification—direct manifestations and outcomes of high‑quality urban life. Accordingly, this item serves as a robust behavioral metric, effectively reflecting the degree of migrants’ integration within the urban social structure and providing strong objective evidence of their urban QoL.

By constructing a QoL index for migrants based on the aforementioned indicators, we can obtain a detailed understanding of migrants’ overall QoL.

First, this method better aligns with the theoretical property of the non-substitutability of multiple dimensions in QoL. Linear weighting methods implicitly assume that dimensions are fully substitutable, which is inconsistent with the independent and non-compensatory nature of QoL dimensions. The mean Euclidean distance method avoids linear cancellation between dimensions through geometric distance synthesis, making it more aligned with the multidimensional essence of QoL.

Second, this method has clearer benchmark orientation and policy interpretability. By clearly defining optimal and worst-case reference points, the assessment results not only have intuitive normative meaning but also provide clear policy directions for improvement (i.e., reducing the distance to the optimal state).

Third, this method is less sensitive to extreme values and distributional forms, making it more robust. Compared to principal component analysis and entropy weight methods that rely on variance structures or information entropy, the mean Euclidean distance method is less affected by skewed distributions or outliers, making it more suitable for the potentially unbalanced data structures in migrant populations.

Consequently, the average Euclidean distance method not only captures the multidimensional complexity of migrant QoL more rationally, but also provides a methodological foundation for subsequent mechanistic analyses of how public health services affect it. The independent variable is public health services. The establishment of health records and health education promotion are considered the foundation and focus of promoting the equalization of public health services. Based on data availability, this study selects health records and health education as proxy variables for migrant public health services. Specifically, the health record is measured by the CMDS question item “Has a resident health record been established for you locally?” with a value of 1 if the answer is “Yes, it has been established,” and 0 otherwise. Health education is measured by the CMDS question item “Have you received any of the following health education in the past year?” with a value of 1 if at least one of the nine options (e.g., occupational disease prevention, sexually transmitted disease prevention, reproductive health, etc.) was received, and 0 otherwise.

The mediating variables are hygiene habits, health behaviors, social interaction, and local identity. Specifically, hygiene habits are measured by the CMDS question item “My hygiene habits are the same as those of local residents,” with a value of 1 if the answer is “Yes,” and 0 otherwise. Health behaviors are measured by the CMDS question item “Did you seek medical care promptly when you had a cold or illness in the past year?” with a value of 1 if the answer is “Yes,” and 0 otherwise. Social interaction is measured by the CMDS question item “Do you spend your leisure time interacting with the most people locally?” with a value of 1 if the answer is “Yes,” and 0 otherwise. Local identity is measured by the CMDS question item “I feel like I am already a local resident,” with a value of 1 if the answer is “Yes,” and 0 otherwise.

Based on CMDS 2017 and drawing on approaches from existing literature, this study controlled for variables potentially affecting migrants’ QoL, including age, gender, marital status, education level, household registration, income level, health level, housing status, occupation, awareness of public health service programs, migration range, duration of residence, and intention for permanent migration. Additionally, to account for variations in city-level policies, this study controlled for city effects using dummy variables.

Additionally, there may be endogeneity issues between public health services (including health records and health education) and the QoL of migrants, primarily due to reverse causality and omitted variable bias. For example, higher-quality-of-life migrants may be more proactive in seeking public health services, leading to reverse causality. Additionally, unobserved individual characteristics (e.g., health awareness or family background) may simultaneously affect the access to public health services and QoL, leading to omitted variable bias. To address these issues, this study employs instrumental variable (IV) methods for robustness testing. The basic principle of the IV method is to use an exogenous variable IV) to “isolate” the exogenous part of the endogenous explanatory variable, thereby eliminating endogeneity bias [37, 38]. Specifically, the IV selected is whether the migrant’s city is listed as a priority city for public health service equalization. This variable is sourced from the National Health Commission’s policy documents and pilot lists (see Table A1), and it is a binary variable: 1 if the city is a priority city, and 0 otherwise. The selection of this variable is based on the following considerations:

First, the IV must satisfy the relevance condition. The national priority for public health service equalization policies has been implemented earlier, more extensively, and with more comprehensive services in pilot cities. If a migrant’s city is designated as a priority city, it is more likely to have access to higher-quality and more accessible public health services, such as more comprehensive health record management systems and broader health education programs, thereby significantly improving the actual public health services they receive. Therefore, this variable has a significant correlation with the endogenous explanatory variable (public health services).

Second, the IV must satisfy the exogeneity condition. Whether a city is selected as a priority city typically depends on macro-level factors such as administrative level, financial capacity, medical resource base, and policy implementation capability, rather than individual migrant QoL. This policy arrangement has strong exogeneity and top-down characteristics, and has no direct causal relationship with individual migrant outcomes such as life satisfaction and social feedback behavior, thus meeting the exogeneity requirement for IV.

In summary, the selected IV can influence the endogenous variable through its effect on the actual supply of public health services, and is unlikely to directly affect the QoL of individual migrants, thus meeting the basic assumptions of IV and can be used to mitigate potential endogeneity issues in the model.

Results

Baseline regression results

Table 3 presents the baseline regression results of public health services on migrant QoL. To assess the robustness of the findings, we employ stepwise regression estimation. The results indicate that, both in models containing only the core explanatory variables and in those that additionally incorporate control variables and city fixed effects, health records and health education exert a statistically significant positive effect on migrant QoL at the 1% level, demonstrating strong robustness of the estimates. Discussion

This study systematically evaluates the impact of public health services on the QoL of migrants and the underlying mechanisms, based on large-scale survey data of migrant populations. The empirical results indicate that the provision of health records and health education significantly improves the QoL of migrant populations. This conclusion remains robust after controlling for endogeneity and using various robustness tests. Compared with existing literature, this study not only confirms the positive impact of public service accessibility on the subjective well-being of migrants but also reveals the heterogeneity of effects and the underlying mechanisms, providing empirical evidence for the design of differentiated policies. The main findings are as follows:

The most important finding of this study is that public health services can significantly improve the QoL of migrants through health records and health education. This finding aligns with the goal of basic public service equality. It suggests that integrating migrants into the public health service system not only has health equity implications but also substantially improves their subjective well-being. Migrant populations often face health disadvantages due to high occupational risks, poor living environments, and limited access to health services [44]. The intervention of public health services helps to address deficiencies in health knowledge and service access, thereby improving overall QoL.

Furthermore, it is essential to distinguish the role of BPHS from that of medical insurance coverage for curative care. We acknowledge the critical point that access to medical treatment for acute illnesses is fundamental to health rights. A health record alone cannot replace the financial protection against catastrophic medical bills provided by health insurance. However, in the current transitional stage where full portability of medical insurance is still developing, the equalization of BPHS offers an irreplaceable value of ‘empowerment’ and ‘social inclusion.’ First, following the principle that ‘prevention is better than cure,’ free chronic disease management and maternal/child health care (as detailed in the pilot work plan) act as a vital safety net, reducing the risk of health deterioration for vulnerable migrants. Second, and central to our findings, is the psychological impact. For migrants often marginalized by the hukou system, the establishment of a health record serves as a form of institutional recognition. This signal of ‘being accepted’ significantly boosts their sense of belonging and subjective quality of life. Thus, while BPHS does not solve all medical accessibility issues, it represents a foundational step toward health equity and social integration.”

Heterogeneity analysis further reveals that the effects of public health services are more pronounced for older-generation migrants, female migrants, inter-city migrants, and inter-provincial migrants. This finding has important policy implications. Older-generation migrants often face more chronic health issues and functional limitations, and their dependence on health services is stronger, thus the marginal benefit of public health services is higher [45]. Female migrants often bear more family health responsibilities. Targeted health education and service support can significantly alleviate their health stress and promote overall family health levels [46]. Inter-city and inter-provincial migrants face stronger cultural and institutional barriers in new environments. Public health services not only provide health support but also help enhance their social identity and sense of belonging in the receiving areas [47, 48]. These findings suggest that public health resources should be allocated differentially to meet the needs of different subgroups to achieve more precise service coverage.

Mechanism analysis shows that public health services not only directly improve the QoL of migrants but also indirectly affect it through four mediating pathways: improving hygiene habits, promoting healthy behaviors, expanding social interactions, and enhancing local identity. Among these, the mediating effect of local identity is the largest, indicating that public health services not only provide health support but also have significant social psychological integration functions. This finding resonates with existing research on social integration and subjective well-being [49, 50], further confirming the key role of public services in promoting migrant psychological adaptation and social identity. The establishment of health education and health records not only conveys health knowledge but also enhances migrants’ trust and sense of belonging to the urban public service system, thereby improving their life satisfaction and willingness to integrate into the city [51].

It requires further clarification that the mediating pathways identified in this study—such as hygiene habits, health behaviors, social interactions, and identity recognition—do not imply that health education or behavioral changes can substitute for medical care itself. In fact, a crucial institutional premise for BPHS to exert a substantive impact on migrants’ quality of life lies in its reliance on Community Health Service Centers and Township Health Centers to provide accessible primary medical services to the migrants. This “minimum threshold for entering the urban medical system” inherently contributes to enhancing migrants’ sense of health security and life stability. Constrained by data availability and research design, this study was unable to quantitatively analyze primary medical service utilization as an independent mechanism. Instead, focusing on health records and health education—the two most universal and comparable policy instruments—we prioritized examining the socio-psychological pathways through which public health services impact migrants’ subjective quality of life.

Nevertheless, it is also necessary to note that there are significant gaps in the establishment of health records and participation in health education among the current migrant population. According to the CMDS 2017 data, 70% of migrants have not established health records, and 27% have not received health education. Therefore, at the policy level, in addition to expanding service coverage, greater emphasis should be placed on improving service accessibility, adaptability, and transparency to achieve a true shift from “having access to services” to “effective utilization.”

In conclusion, this study situates the measurement of QoL within the specific contexts of perceived urban belonging and community participation, which facilitates a nuanced analysis of migrants’ subjective perceptions and objective behaviors, yet it also entails two principal limitations. First, with respect to measurement dimensions, the present framework primarily concentrates on urban belonging perception and social participation, omitting other salient determinants of QoL such as job satisfaction, housing conditions, and health‑care security; future research should expand these dimensions to develop a more comprehensive assessment system. Second, at the data level, the paucity of long‑term longitudinal surveys on migrants in China constrains this study to cross‑sectional analysis, which (i) hampers the ability to capture potential dynamic adjustments between public health services and QoL, and (ii) precludes the use of panel models to control for time‑invariant individual heterogeneity, thereby potentially compromising the robustness of causal inference.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluates the causal effects and underlying mechanisms of two core public health services—health records and health education—on the QoL of migrants, based on CMDS 2017 data. After correcting for potential endogeneity using IV methods, the study finds that both establishing health records and receiving health education significantly improve the QoL of migrants. Mechanism analysis further reveals that improved hygiene habits, adoption of healthy behaviors, expanded social interactions, and strengthened local identity are important pathways through which public health services exert their effects. To further optimize policy effectiveness, it is recommended to promote the construction of cross-regional health information sharing platforms, implement personalized and targeted health education interventions, and enhance the accessibility and utilization efficiency of public health services for migrants, thereby comprehensively improving their QoL.

This study empirically demonstrates the dual value of public health services in promoting migrant health and enhancing subjective well-being. However, this does not suggest that health education or health behaviors can compensate for the structural inequalities resulting from the absence of medical insurance coverage. Conversely, against the reality that medical insurance portability for migrants has not yet been fully realized, basic public health services—anchored in community health service centers—offer migrants an accessible framework for primary healthcare and institutional care. On this basis, health records and health education generate significant additive effects on migrants’ subjective quality of life by improving behavioral patterns, fostering social interaction, and strengthening institutional identity. Consequently, basic public health services should be viewed as a foundational institutional arrangement—a starting point rather than an end point—for promoting health equity and social integration among the migrants. The key to future policy remains the further advancement of medical insurance integration and portability to effectively address major health risks requiring high-cost inpatient treatment.
