Riboflavin Salvage Supports Glycolysis in Borrelia burgdorferi Through Flavin‐Dependent NAD + Regeneration
Authors: Anna M Schulz, Grace E Easterling, Aaditya Balasubramaniyan, Mollie W Jewett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Borrelia burgdorferi (Bb), the etiological agent of Lyme disease, lacks the genes for de novo riboflavin biosynthesis and depends on host‐derived riboflavin to generate flavin cofactors FMN and FAD. Riboflavin salvage by Bb has demonstrated importance to infection and metabolic homeostasis, though the critical roles of flavin cofactors to central metabolism are not well defined. Here, we demonstrate that riboflavin availability directly controls glycolytic flux through regulation of intracellular redox balance. Disruption of riboflavin uptake by deletion of gene bb0318 encoding the putative ATPase component of the riboflavin transporter resulted in impaired glycolysis as indicated by reduced intracellular pyruvate and a perturbed NADH/NAD+ ratio driven by faulty NAD+ regeneration. We identified coenzyme A disulfide reductase (CoADR) as a key flavin‐dependent mediator of glycolytic flux. A Δcdr mutant mimicked the glycolytic dysregulation of the Δbb0318 mutant, but was not rescued by exogenous riboflavin, placing BbCoADR downstream of riboflavin salvage. Biochemical analyses of recombinant BbCoADR demonstrated FAD‐dependent NADH oxidase activity. Targeted mutagenesis of predicted FAD‐binding residues impaired cofactor binding and NADH oxidase activity, further confirming the dependence of BbCoADR on FAD. Together, this work reveals a direct link between riboflavin acquisition and core metabolic processes driven by BbCoADR‐mediated flavin‐dependent redox metabolism.Keywords: Borrelia burgdorferi , central metabolism, Lyme disease, NAD+ regeneration, riboflavin salvage

Introduction

Borrelia burgdorferi is the causative agent of Lyme disease, the leading bacterial vector‐borne disease globally. B. burgdorferi is host dependent and is maintained in an enzootic cycle between its tick vector, Ixodes scapularis , and reservoir hosts, such as rodents, birds, and reptiles. Humans are incidental hosts that can contract Lyme disease when bitten by an infected tick (Coburn et al. 2021).

B. burgdorferi harbors a reduced genome which lacks genes required for numerous biosynthetic and metabolic pathways (Casjens et al. 2000; Fraser et al. 1997). This spirochete's survival depends on its ability to scavenge required amino acids, nucleotides, vitamins, and cofactors from its host environments. One such critical nutrient not synthesized by B. burgdorferi is riboflavin, which is important for a variety of biological processes (Averianova et al. 2020). Riboflavin is the precursor molecule to flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) which serve as cofactors for many enzymes. Flavoproteins, proteins containing FMN or FAD cofactors, play crucial roles in metabolism, DNA repair, and redox homeostasis (Massey 2000).

We and others have established that B. burgdorferi genes bb0316‐bb0319 encode an ABC‐type transport system for riboflavin uptake (Deka et al. 2013; Muramatsu et al. 2021; Ramos et al. 2022). The transporter is encoded by four genes, bb0316‐bb0319, where genes bb0316 and bb0317 are putative permeases, bb0318 encodes a putative ATPase, and bb0319 encodes a riboflavin binding protein, suggesting the specificity of this transporter for riboflavin (Deka et al. 2013). This transport system plays a significant role in the ability of B. burgdorferi to salvage riboflavin, where attenuation of this transport system has been accomplished by mutation of solely the putative ATPase component (bb0318) (Ramos et al. 2022; Showman et al. 2016). Yet, influx of riboflavin is not completely eliminated in mutant strains lacking the transporter, suggesting the likelihood of an alternative riboflavin transport mechanism that is yet to be identified (Ramos et al. 2022; Muramatsu et al. 2021). Riboflavin salvage has been implicated in a variety of important processes in B. burgdorferi , including carbon metabolism (Ramos et al. 2022), defense against oxidative stress (Ramsey et al. 2017; Showman et al. 2016), and evasion of macrophage killing (Showman et al. 2016). Furthermore, B. burgdorferi riboflavin transport mutants are severely attenuated for infection in mice (Muramatsu et al. 2021; Showman et al. 2016) and unable to survive in feeding ticks (Ramos et al. 2022), yet demonstrate only a modest growth defect in vitro at 35°C. Previously, targeted comparative metabolomics demonstrated an accumulation of several glycolytic intermediates and a decrease in hexose sugars in the absence of maximal riboflavin uptake (Ramos et al. 2022). Although this broad analysis did not reveal significant riboflavin‐dependent changes in energy or redox balance (Ramos et al. 2022), spirochetes lacking the riboflavin transport system demonstrated severely attenuated growth in vitro at 23°C and in unfed ticks (Ramos et al. 2022), suggesting a substantial reduction in riboflavin uptake has severe bioenergetic consequences for B. burgdorferi . Altogether, these phenotypes suggest a significant role for riboflavin transport throughout the enzootic cycle of B. burgdorferi .

As a result of its highly reduced genome, B. burgdorferi is limited in its ability to generate ATP. B. burgdorferi encodes enzymes to complete the glycolytic pathway, but not for oxidative phosphorylation or the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Fraser et al. 1997; Casjens et al. 2000). B. burgdorferi can use several carbon sources to support glycolysis depending on its environment, allowing for metabolic flexibility and host adaptation (Fraser et al. 1997; Von Lackum and Stevenson 2005; Hoon‐Hanks et al. 2012). In the mammalian host, B. burgdorferi primarily uses glucose as its carbon source whereas in the tick vector, glycerol, chitobiose, and N‐acetyl glucosamine are thought to be the primary carbon sources (Caimano et al. 2016). Regardless of the initiating carbon source, glycolysis generates a net gain of two ATP molecules and ends in the terminal metabolite, pyruvate (Gherardini et al. 2010). Because B. burgdorferi does not encode the machinery for an electron transport chain, the only fate for pyruvate is conversion to lactate, which is essential for B. burgdorferi survival (Sze et al. 2025). Given the essential role of glycolysis for B. burgdorferi survival and importance of riboflavin salvage for metabolism, we sought to dissect the contributions of riboflavin and downstream effectors, FMN and FAD, to glycolysis and metabolic equilibrium. We have revealed the reliance of glycolysis on riboflavin salvage and subsequently the FAD‐dependence of an important enzyme, coenzyme A‐disulfide reductase (BbCoADR), that is critical for the maintenance of glycolytic flux.

Results

B. burgdorferi Glycolytic Success Depends on Flavin Availability

We previously determined that B. burgdorferi lacking bb0318 experience metabolic dysregulation (Ramos et al. 2022). Given the important and versatile roles played by riboflavin‐derived cofactors FMN and FAD (Massey 2000), we sought to determine the specific contributions of riboflavin salvage to B. burgdorferi metabolism. To elucidate any connection between riboflavin salvage disruption and glycolysis, we quantified intracellular pyruvate as a readout of glycolytic flux. Pyruvate was extracted from live spirochetes grown to mid‐logarithmic phase and quantified using a fluorescence‐based assay (Zhu et al. 2010). Intracellular pyruvate levels when grown in BSKII medium were significantly diminished in the Δbb0318 mutant compared to wild‐type spirochetes, indicating impaired glycolysis in the absence of optimal riboflavin salvage (Figure 1). The metabolic defect of the Δbb0318 mutant was restored to significantly greater than wild‐type levels in the bb0318+ complemented strain, likely due to high, constitutive expression of bb0318 under the control of the flagellar promoter on the Borrelia shuttle vector, pBSV2G flaB p (Jain et al. 2015; Ramos et al. 2022). Furthermore, while experimental evidence thus far has implied the importance of bb0318 as an ATPase for the riboflavin transport system (Ramos et al. 2022; Showman et al. 2016), we cannot rule out the possibility of its role as a multifunctional ATPase whose overexpression could have additional effects on the metabolism of the spirochete. We and others established that riboflavin, FMN, and FAD levels in Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi are reduced but remain detectable, suggesting the possibility of an alternative mechanism for riboflavin uptake (Ramos et al. 2022; Muramatsu et al. 2021). Furthermore, these findings suggest the possibility of increasing the intracellular flavin concentrations in the mutant with the addition of excess riboflavin to the growth medium, perhaps resulting in rescue of the metabolic defect. Consistent with this notion, supplementation of BSKII medium with exogenous riboflavin restored intracellular pyruvate levels of Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi to levels not significantly different from that of wild‐type B. burgdorferi grown without supplemental riboflavin (Figure 1), further suggesting the flavin‐dependence of this metabolic defect. Interestingly, the presence of excess riboflavin resulted in a significant reduction in pyruvate levels in wild‐type B. burgdorferi , suggesting the possibility of a deleterious effect of high intracellular flavin levels as has been reported for other bacteria (Aghilan et al. 2016). In contrast, however, the complemented bb0318 strain (bb0318+) exhibited consistent significantly elevated levels of pyruvate despite the presence of supplemental riboflavin.

Oxidative‐Reductive Balance of NAD in B. burgdorferi Is Regulated in a Flavin‐Dependent Manner

Redox balance is an important component of bacterial homeostasis that supports glycolytic flux (Foster and Moat 1980; Gazzaniga et al. 2009). In bacterial glycolysis, the levels of oxidized and reduced forms of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) play a significant role in the determination of glycolytic success (Foster and Moat 1980; Gazzaniga et al. 2009). In the conversion of glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate to 1,3‐bisphosphoglycerate, glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) uses NAD+ as an electron‐accepting cofactor, resulting in generation of NAD in its reduced form, NADH (Foster and Moat 1980; Gazzaniga et al. 2009). The reduced NAD must then be oxidized once again to NAD+ to continue to fuel glycolysis (Corona and Schwartz 2015). Thus, the balance between oxidized and reduced forms of NAD are quintessential to the functionality of the pathway. Following this model, we hypothesized that in B. burgdorferi , impaired glycolytic flux detected when riboflavin salvage was significantly diminished may have resulted from disruption of the NADH/NAD+ redox balance. To test this, we measured NADH and NAD+ levels using the NAD‐Glo assay (Promega), quantifying these dinucleotides in the B. burgdorferi strains grown in the presence and absence of excess riboflavin. Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi in BSKII medium alone exhibited a markedly altered NADH/NAD+ ratio, which upon supplementation of the growth medium with 125 μM riboflavin was restored to levels comparable to wild‐type B. burgdorferi grown in untreated medium (Figure 2A). The NADH/NAD+ ratios of wild‐type B. burgdorferi grown in BSKII plus riboflavin and the bb0318+ complemented strain in both the presence and absence of additional riboflavin demonstrated no statistical difference from wild‐type B. burgdorferi grown in untreated medium. Separation of this ratio into the two individual components indicated that NADH levels remain unchanged across all genotypes and growth conditions (Figure 2B). However, NAD+ levels varied across different genotypes and conditions (Figure 2C). Specifically, Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi demonstrated significantly attenuated levels of NAD+ in BSKII medium alone, which were restored to untreated wild‐type levels in the presence of excess riboflavin. Riboflavin supplementation had no effect on NAD+ levels in wild‐type B. burgdorferi . Similar to what was found for intracellular pyruvate levels, NAD+ levels in bb0318+ B. burgdorferi in both growth medium with and without supplemental riboflavin were significantly increased compared to wild‐type B. burgdorferi in untreated medium. Together these results suggest that the metabolic defect of Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi may result from the compromised activity of one or more enzymes responsible for oxidizing NADH to NAD+ along the glycolytic pathway due to the absence of maximal riboflavin salvage.

Gene bb0728 (cdr) Contributes to Glycolytic Success

Current understanding of B. burgdorferi metabolism suggests that there are at least three enzymatic activities in the glycolytic pathway that contribute to NAD+ regeneration from NADH. Coenzyme A disulfide reductase (BbCoADR/BB0728) catalyzes the NADH‐dependent reduction of CoA disulfide (Boylan et al. 2006). Lactate dehydrogenase (BbLDH/BB0087) catalyzes the NADH‐dependent conversion of pyruvate to lactate (Sze et al. 2025). The glycerol‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (BbGpsA/BB0368) is predicted to convert dihydroxyacetone phosphate to glycerol‐3‐phosphate in an NADH‐dependent manner (Gherardini et al. 2021; Corona and Schwartz 2015; Drecktrah et al. 2022). Furthermore, BbGpsA has been demonstrated to play a significant role in controlling NADH levels and maintaining NADH/NAD+ homeostasis (Drecktrah et al. 2022). A consequence of all three reactions is the oxidation of NADH to NAD+. Consistent with the phenotypes of B. burgdorferi riboflavin salvage mutants, B. burgdorferi lacking cdr (bb0728) exhibit reduced survival in fed ticks and are not infectious in mice (Eggers et al. 2011). Moreover, BbCoADR was predicted to be FAD‐dependent (Boylan et al. 2006), furthering our interest in its contributions to riboflavin salvage‐dependent glycolytic phenotypes. To examine the contributions of BbCoADR to glycolysis, we measured intracellular pyruvate and NADH/NAD+ levels in a Δcdr mutant (Eggers et al. 2011). Consistent with BbCoADR's known role in NADH oxidation (Boylan et al. 2006), the intracellular pyruvate levels (Figure 3A), NADH/NAD+ balance (Figure 3B) and NAD+ levels (Figure 3C) were disrupted in this mutant when grown in untreated BSKII medium as compared to the wild‐type strain grown under the same conditions, indicating impaired glycolysis. The cdr+ complemented strain (Eggers et al. 2011) demonstrated significantly increased intracellular pyruvate level in untreated growth medium compared to wild type (Figure 3A), likely as a result of cdr overexpression due to the increased copy number of the complementation plasmid, yet the NADH/NAD+ ratio was unchanged (Figure 3B). In contrast to Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi , we hypothesized that the glycolytic attenuation of Δcdr B. burgdorferi would not be restored with exogenous riboflavin, given our prediction that this enzyme may be the flavin‐dependent cause of disruption to glycolysis in a riboflavin transporter mutant. Indeed, supplementation with excess riboflavin did not result in restoration of wild‐type intracellular pyruvate levels or NADH/NAD+ balance, all of which remained notably dysregulated when compared with wild‐type B. burgdorferi grown without supplemental riboflavin (Figure 3). There was some variability in the metabolic phenotypes of the wild‐type and complemented strains in the presence of excess riboflavin, and these effects could likely be explained by accumulation of high levels of intracellular flavins. Together these data provided support for the notion that BbCoADR may be a flavin‐dependent enzyme that is crucial for NAD+ regeneration in B. burgdorferi.

cdr Transcript and BbCoADR Protein Levels Are Unaffected by Riboflavin Availability

To date, only one other B. burgdorferi protein, BbGlpD (BB0243), a glycerol 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase, has been experimentally identified as a flavoprotein (Ramos et al. 2022). In addition, we previously demonstrated that glpD transcript levels and BbGlpD protein levels are significantly reduced when riboflavin salvage is attenuated (Ramos et al. 2022). In the same manner, we sought to determine the effect(s) of riboflavin availability on cdr/BbCoADR expression. We assessed cdr transcript and BbCoADR protein abundance in wild‐type, Δbb0318, and bb0318+ B. burgdorferi grown with or without supplemental riboflavin. Neither cdr transcript (Figure 4A) nor encoded protein levels (Figure 4B) varied among all these genotypes and conditions, suggesting a lack of riboflavin‐mediated regulation at the level of transcription or translation.

NADH Oxidase Activity of BbCoADR Is FAD‐Dependent

The enzymatic activity of BbCoADR has been previously examined, confirming its function as a coenzyme A disulfide reductase and NADH oxidase capabilities (Boylan et al. 2006). To test whether BbCoADR is capable of FAD binding, we cloned and expressed recombinant BbCoADR as a glutathione S‐transferase fusion in E. coli and purified the protein using glutathione affinity chromatography (Figure S1). We examined FAD‐binding of recombinant BbCoADR protein via fluorescence spectroscopy. A fluorescence spectral scan was conducted by exciting recombinant BbCoADR at 450 nm and examining fluorescent peaks from 490 to 700 nm. FAD emits a fluorescent peak at approximately 520 nm upon excitation at 450 nm (Figure 5A). In agreement with this, recombinant BbCoADR emitted a fluorescent peak at ~520 nm, suggesting that the purified protein likely contained bound FAD cofactor (Figure 5A). In contrast, GST protein alone demonstrated little to no fluorescence at 520 nm (Figure 5A). Furthermore, we examined NADH oxidase activity in the presence and absence of FAD to examine the impacts of this cofactor on BbCoADR enzymatic activity. For these studies M9 minimal medium was used for recombinant BbCoADR production in E. coli to minimize exogenous flavin cofactor availability (Figure S2). We quantified NADH oxidation by purified recombinant BbCoADR in the presence and absence of FAD. Recombinant BbCoADR was supplied with CoA disulfide, NADH and 0, 10, or 100 μM FAD. Absorbance was measured at 340 nm over a time course of 15–45 min to monitor loss of NADH as a result of oxidation. The NADH oxidase activity of the enzyme was significantly improved in the presence of 100 μM FAD, supporting the hypothesis that the activity of BbCoADR is FAD‐dependent (Figure 5B).

BbCoADR Contains Several Conserved Residues Critical to Both FAD Binding and NADH Oxidation

Following confirmation of the FAD‐dependence of BbCoADR's NADH oxidase activity, we sought to elucidate the residues important for FAD‐binding. We employed a comparative alignment strategy to identify putative conserved structural and/or functional elements of BbCoADR. A sequence alignment of BbCoADR to Staphylococcus aureus CoADR (SaCoADR), the annotated CoADR proteins of two other species within the phylum Spirochaetes ( Borrelia miyamotoi and Treponema denticola ), and the NADH oxidase (Nox) of Clostridium difficile was performed (Figure 6A) to highlight conserved residues across these proteins of similar known or predicted function. Like CoADR enzymes, C. difficile Nox is a member of the Peroxide‐Oxidase‐Reductase (POR) ancestry group, as well as the Flavoprotein Disulfide Reductase (FDR) family but is evolutionarily divergent (Ojha et al. 2007; Argyrou and Blanchard 2004). This sequence was included in the analysis to identify evolutionarily conserved residues critical for FAD binding in BbCoADR. The crystal structure of SaCoADR has been solved previously and FAD binding regions of this enzyme defined (Mallett et al. 2006). The FAD binding domains in SaCoADR were found to correspond to BbCoADR amino acids Gly7‐Glu32 (FAD1), Thr271‐Asp281 (FAD2), and Tyr424 (FAD3) (Figure 6A). Strong sequence similarity across each of these domains was detected between the proteins analyzed, further implicating the significance of these regions. Five amino acids in FAD1, four amino acids in FAD2, and the tyrosine that constitutes FAD3 were identical in all the NADH‐oxidase enzymes included in the analysis (Figure 6A). To examine the biological relevance of these residues, amino acid substitutions were introduced into BbCoADR coding sequence by inverse PCR corresponding to conserved residues identified within the putative FAD binding regions (Figure 6A) and cloned into an expression vector for recombinant protein production in E. coli . Four amino acid substitutions, G7A, G12A, R21A, and E32A, were introduced into the first putative FAD‐binding region, resulting in the recombinant BbCoADR variant ΔFAD1. The second putative FAD‐binding region was targeted with amino acid substitutions, T271A, I276A, G280A, and D281A, resulting in the recombinant BbCoADR variant ΔFAD2. Finally, the substitution Y424A targeted the third predicted FAD‐binding site, generating the recombinant BbCoADR variant ΔFAD3. The compilation of all of these amino acid substitutions together resulted in the recombinant BbCoADR variant termed ΔFADTotal. To evaluate the ability of wild‐type BbCoADR and the BbCoADR variants to bind FAD, the recombinant proteins were expressed and purified from E. coli as described above (Figure S1). FAD binding by the purified recombinant BbCoADR variant proteins was analyzed by fluorescence spectroscopy and compared to that of wild‐type BbCoADR protein. The spectral peaks at 520 nm for all four recombinant BbCoADR variants were significantly diminished compared to recombinant wild‐type BbCoADR. These data suggested purified BbCoADR‐ΔFAD3 protein contained a reduced amount of bound FAD. Furthermore, the BbCoADR‐ΔFAD1, ‐ΔFAD2, and ‐ΔFADTotal proteins were found to be nearly void of any bound FAD (Figure 6B). The NADH‐oxidase capabilities of these recombinant BbCoADR variants were examined in the presence of 100 μM FAD, as described above. In agreement with a significant reduction in FAD binding capacity, all BbCoADR protein variants exhibited significantly diminished NAD+ generation, though BbCoADR‐ΔFAD3 appeared to retain some activity relative to variants ΔFAD1, ΔFAD2, and ΔFADTotal (Figure 6C). Altogether, these data identified key conserved amino acids in BbCoADR that are critical for FAD binding and NADH oxidase activity.

Discussion

The highly restricted metabolic capacity of B. burgdorferi necessitates extensive scavenging of essential nutrients from its hosts. B. burgdorferi lacks riboflavin biosynthetic pathways (Fraser et al. 1997; Massey 2000; Casjens et al. 2012) and thus relies on riboflavin uptake to supply the critical precursor of the cofactors FMN and FAD. B. burgdorferi encodes an ABC‐type transport system (BB0316‐BB0319) involved in the uptake of riboflavin (Muramatsu et al. 2021; Deka et al. 2013; Ramos et al. 2022). Given the metabolic dysregulation and infectivity defects observed in mutants lacking this transport system (Ramos et al. 2022; Showman et al. 2016; Muramatsu et al. 2021), we hypothesized that riboflavin salvage contributes to core metabolic pathways critical to the survival of these bacteria.

Glycolysis is the sole pathway for ATP generation in B. burgdorferi (Fraser et al. 1997; Casjens et al. 2012). Our data demonstrated significant disruption of glycolysis in a B. burgdorferi riboflavin transport mutant, as evidenced by reduced intracellular pyruvate levels and a dysregulated NADH/NAD+ ratio. The imbalance in the NADH/NAD+ ratio in Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi was attributed to significantly reduced NAD+ levels, consistent with the central role of NAD+ regeneration in B. burgdorferi for maintaining redox equilibrium to sustain glycolytic flux (Foster and Moat 1980; Gazzaniga et al. 2009; Fraser et al. 1997; Casjens et al. 2012). Both parameters of glycolytic flux in Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi were restored to untreated wild‐type levels upon supplementation of the growth medium with excess riboflavin, suggesting a role for one or more flavin‐dependent enzymes in these phenotypes. Though excess riboflavin appeared to exert modest deleterious effects on wild‐type B. burgdorferi , we speculate that these effects may result from intracellular flavin accumulation. Accordingly, comparisons between all genotypes and treatment groups were made relative to wild‐type B. burgdorferi grown in BSKII medium without supplemental riboflavin, as this represented the most biologically relevant baseline while still accounting for treatment‐dependent effects through two‐way ANOVA analysis. BbCoADR was identified and characterized as a novel flavoprotein whose FAD‐dependent activity is crucial for the generation of NAD+ to sustain ATP production through glycolysis.

Analysis of glycolytic phenotypes in the absence of the cdr gene revealed dysregulated glycolysis. Comparable to Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi, in growth medium without supplemental riboflavin, bacteria lacking cdr demonstrated reduced levels of intracellular pyruvate relative to the wild‐type and complemented strains. In contrast to B. burgdorferi lacking bb0318, Δcdr B. burgdorferi did not experience significant restoration of intracellular pyruvate levels upon supplementation with riboflavin. Similarly, NADH/NAD+ balance was disrupted in Δcdr B. burgdorferi, and NAD+ levels were significantly diminished when compared to the wild‐type and complemented strains. This phenotype was also not rescued with excess riboflavin supplementation, suggesting that BbCoADR is the primary flavoprotein important for maintaining glycolytic flux in B. burgdorferi. However, while not significant, there appeared to be a modest trend in improvement of pyruvate and NAD+ levels in Δcdr B. burgdorferi in the presence of additional riboflavin, raising the possibility of other flavin‐dependent enzymes that could also contribute support to glycolysis. Multiple pathways exist in B. burgdorferi for the regeneration of NAD+. One mechanism of NAD+ regeneration occurs during lactogenesis, when pyruvate is converted to lactate by a bifunctional lactate/pyruvate dehydrogenase encoded by bb0087 (ldh) (Wolfe 2005). This enzyme has been characterized, and its essentiality is supported by its requirement for in vitro growth (Sze et al. 2025). We have found that the activity of recombinant BbLDH is not FAD‐dependent (Figure S3), indicating a lack of interplay between riboflavin salvage and BbLDH. A second mechanism for NAD+ regeneration exists through the conversion from dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) to glycerol 3‐phosphate (G3P), catalyzed by BbGpsA (BB0368). The gpsA gene has been previously demonstrated to play a critical role in NADH/NAD+ balance (Drecktrah et al. 2022). Interestingly, gpsA expression was found to be increased in Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi (Ramos et al. 2022), suggesting the possibility of a compensatory mechanism to overcome the NAD+ deficiency in this mutant, but this effect was not sufficient to restore NAD+ regeneration to wild‐type levels. Finally, the nicotinamide salvage pathway is important for NAD+ production (Gazzaniga et al. 2009). A nicotinamidase has been identified in B. burgdorferi (BBE22/BbPncA), as well as the required enzymes for conversion of nicotinamide to NAD+ (Jewett et al. 2011; Fraser et al. 1997; Purser et al. 2003; Casjens et al. 2011, 2000). However, a nicotinamide uptake system has not yet been characterized. While BbGpsA and BbPncA are not predicted to bind flavin cofactors, this possibility cannot be excluded, and future studies will determine potential flavin‐dependence of these proteins.

In the absence of riboflavin salvage, B. burgdorferi was found to maintain wild‐type transcription and translation of cdr/BbCoADR. This suggested that flavin‐dependent regulation of this protein occurs post‐translationally through cofactor availability. Expression of gene cdr is known to be positively regulated by the alternative sigma factor RpoS and DNA/RNA‐binding protein BosR, both of which also control other stress and metabolism‐related genes (Raghunandanan et al. 2025; Grassmann et al. 2026). Given that riboflavin transport had seemingly no effect on cdr transcription or BbCoADR translation, the mechanism of its regulation likely proceeds independent of flavin availability. This contrasts with the regulation of previously identified flavoprotein, BbGlpD, whose mRNA and protein levels were both markedly decreased in Δbb0318 B. burgdorferi (Ramos et al. 2022). This cofactor‐dependent regulation could reflect mechanisms of energetic conservation, as GlpD has little to no activity in the absence of its FAD cofactor (Ramos et al. 2022). The continued production of BbCoADR, even in the absence of riboflavin, suggests the possibility of flavin‐independent activity or the use of an alternative electron carrier in riboflavin‐limiting environments.

The enzymatic activity of BbCoADR has been experimentally examined previously (Boylan et al. 2006; Eggers et al. 2011). We have herein demonstrated the FAD‐dependence of this enzyme by revealing a marked increase in BbCoADR activity in the presence of FAD. Residual activity of purified BbCoADR in samples without supplemental FAD likely resulted from trace FAD derived from the riboflavin biosynthetic capabilities of E. coli, which could supply cofactor to the recombinant protein during production. Furthermore, we characterized key FAD‐binding residues of this protein. Guided by comparative sequence alignment with structurally and functionally characterized homologs, we defined three putative FAD‐binding regions in BbCoADR and experimentally validated their importance through targeted mutagenesis of recombinant protein. The strong concordance between conserved residues and functional outcomes underscores the utility of leveraging evolutionary conservation to infer structure–function relationships in enzymes for which high‐resolution structural data is unavailable. Substitution of conserved residues within each predicted FAD‐binding region in BbCoADR resulted in a marked reduction or near‐complete loss of flavin binding and NADH oxidase activity, even in the presence of excess FAD. Notably, recombinant variants targeting the first and second binding regions (FAD1 and FAD2) exhibited almost complete abrogation of FAD binding and NADH oxidase activity, suggesting that these two domains form a core FAD‐binding pocket. In contrast, substitution of the third region (FAD3), led to a partial reduction in FAD binding and NADH oxidase activity, consistent with a peripheral or stabilizing role. These findings align with structural data from SaCoADR (Mallett et al. 2006), supporting a model in which BbCoADR employs a similarly distributed FAD binding interface.

Experimental demonstration of the flavin‐dependence of BbCoADR marks a significant discovery regarding flavin biology and its importance to the survival of B. burgdorferi . This work has established the importance of BbCoADR activity for NAD+ regeneration. BbCoADR is also critical for maintaining the optimal CoA/CoA‐disulfide ratio (Boylan et al. 2006; Eggers et al. 2011). CoA in B. burgdorferi is important for intracellular redox homeostasis. To protect against harmful effects of ROS, many organisms maintain stringent thiol‐disulfide homeostasis (Holmgren 1989; Holmgren et al. 2005; Imlay 2008). CoA is the most readily abundant low‐molecular weight thiol found in B. burgdorferi suggesting the likelihood of its importance to the maintenance of this thiol‐disulfide balance (Boylan et al. 2006). Given the importance of both riboflavin transport and BbCoADR to survival against ROS stress (Boylan et al. 2006; Eggers et al. 2011; Showman et al. 2016), it is likely that CoA deficiency in these strains is a strong contributor to this phenotype. B. burgdorferi lacking either bb0318 or cdr exhibit attenuated infectivity in mice (Showman et al. 2016; Eggers et al. 2011). Furthermore, both riboflavin salvage and BbCoADR activity have been found to be significantly important for survival of B. burgdorferi in feeding ticks, suggesting that FAD‐dependent redox balance is essential across the enzootic cycle (Eggers et al. 2011; Ramos et al. 2022). CoA in B. burgdorferi is also predicted to function as a coenzyme acyl‐carrier important for the mevalonate pathway toward biosynthesis of the peptidoglycan precursor undecaprenyl phosphate (Van Laar et al. 2012; Richards et al. 2015). The consequences of attenuated riboflavin salvage in the context of peptidoglycan maturation and cell wall synthesis have yet to be investigated.

In summary, this study expands the understanding of riboflavin utilization in B. burgdorferi, demonstrating that flavin cofactors sustain glycolysis through the activity of the FAD‐dependent enzyme BbCoADR. These findings reveal a mechanism linking riboflavin salvage to glycolytic function and redox homeostasis in B. burgdorferi , expanding our understanding of the importance of flavin‐dependent processes in the survival of this pathogen. The significance of riboflavin salvage to B. burgdorferi metabolism, survival, and infectivity highlights the potential that riboflavin transport may serve as a target for future B. burgdorferi specific therapeutic approaches for the treatment of Lyme disease.

Experimental Procedures

Bacterial Strains and Growth Conditions

B. burgdorferi strains were derived from clone B31 A3‐68 Δbbe02 (wild‐type) lacking cp9 and lp56, as well as the bbe02 gene from lp25 (Rego et al. 2011). B. burgdorferi lacking gene bb0318 (Δbb0318) and its in trans complement (bb0318+) carrying the pBSV2G shuttle vector with expression of bb0318 driven by the constitutive flagellar promoter, flaB p, were generated previously (Showman et al. 2016). The B. burgdorferi wild‐type (strain 297), Δcdr mutant (c309, cdr::ermC), and cdr+ complement (c1655, c309+pCE1735) strains were kind gifts from Melissa Caimano and Justin Radolf (Eggers et al. 2011). B. burgdorferi cultures were grown in liquid Barbour‐Stoenner‐Kelly II (BSKII) medium supplemented with gelatin and 6% rabbit serum (Barbour 1984). When appropriate, antibiotics kanamycin (200 μg/mL), streptomycin (50 μg/mL), gentamycin (40 μg/mL), and/or erythromycin (0.06 μg/mL) were added to cultures. Spirochetes were maintained at 35°C in tightly capped culture vials for regular cultivation. When indicated, supplementation with up to 125 μM riboflavin was accomplished from a 1 mM stock solution of riboflavin dissolved in water. This concentration of riboflavin is roughly estimated to be between 100 and 1000X greater than the concentration of riboflavin in undefined and complex BSKII medium. Equal volumes of water were added as the vehicle control to untreated cultures. E. coli strains DH5α and BL21 were grown in LB medium or M9 minimal medium. Ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and isopropyl β‐D‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; 1.0 mM) were added as needed.

Measurement of Intracellular Pyruvate

The assay for measurement of intracellular pyruvate was adapted from Zhu et al. (2010). Briefly, B. burgdorferi strains were grown in triplicate to mid‐logarithmic phase (3–5 × 107 Bb/mL) in BSKII in the presence or absence of 125 μM excess riboflavin and collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min. The cell pellets were washed twice with HN buffer (50 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) and metabolite extraction was performed by resuspending the pellets in ice cold 0.25 M HClO4 followed by 5‐min incubation on ice. The reactions were neutralized with 5 M K2CO3. The reactions were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min to separate cell debris from metabolite extract (supernatant). In a 96‐well opaque‐walled clear‐bottom plate, the supernatants were loaded in triplicate in wells containing reaction cocktail (100 mM potassium phosphate with 1 mM EDTA (pH 6.7), 1 mM magnesium chloride, 0.2 units/mL pyruvate oxidase, 50 μM amplex red, and 0.2 units/mL HRP). The reactions were incubated at ambient temperature for 30 min, followed by measurement of fluorescence at ex/em 535/590 nm using a BioTek Cytation 5. Statistical significance was assessed using two‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's posttest compared to wild‐type untreated (GraphPad Prism, version 10.6.1).

Measurement of NADH/NAD +

B. burgdorferi strains were grown in triplicate to mid‐logarithmic growth phase (3–5 × 107 Bb/mL) in 10 mL BSKII in the presence or absence of 125 μM riboflavin and collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min. Cell pellets were washed twice with HN buffer (50 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) followed by resuspension to equal densities in phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Cell pellets were lysed and assayed using the NAD/NADH‐Glo Assay Kit (Promega) according to manufacturer's instructions. The levels of NAD+ and NADH were measured independently by reading luminescence in a white‐walled 96‐well plate using a BioTek Cytation 5. Statistical significance was assessed using two‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's posttest compared to wild‐type untreated (GraphPad Prism, version 10.6.1).

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR

B. burgdorferi strains were grown in triplicate cultures to mid‐logarithmic growth phase (3–5 × 107 Bb/mL) in BSKII in the presence or absence of 125 μM riboflavin and collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min. Cell pellets were washed twice with HN buffer (50 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). RNA was extracted from all cultures using phenol‐chloroform extraction followed by ethanol precipitation, as described previously (Drecktrah et al. 2015). RNAs were then treated twice with DNase (Roche) to remove any contaminating DNA. Following DNase treatments, the iScript Select cDNA synthesis kit and random primers (BioRad) were used to generate cDNA samples from 1 μg of RNA. Reactions without reverse transcriptase were conducted in parallel. Quantitative PCR was performed using iQ SYBR green SuperMix (BioRad), 100 ng of cDNA per replicate, and primers for bb0728/cdr and housekeeping gene bb0131/recA (Table S1). The amounts of bb0728 and bb0131 transcripts were determined using a B. burgdorferi B31 genomic DNA standard curve (100 ng, 10 ng, 1 ng, 0.1 ng, and 0.01 ng) for each target. Relative bb0728 gene expression was assessed by normalization to copy numbers of recA for each genotype. Statistical significance was assessed using two‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's posttest compared to wild‐type untreated (GraphPad Prism, version 10.6.1).

SDS‐PAGE and Immunoblot

B. burgdorferi strains were grown to mid‐logarithmic growth phase (3–5 × 107 Bb/mL) in BSKII medium in the presence or absence of 125 μM riboflavin. Spirochetes were collected by centrifugation at 4000 x g for 10 min and cell pellets were washed twice with HN buffer (50 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). Cell pellets were then resuspended to a final density of 107 spirochetes/μl. Protein lysates were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Immunoblots were performed using rabbit anti‐CoADR (1:500) (Boylan et al. 2006) and mouse anti‐FlaB (1:200) (Barbour et al. 1986).

Cloning of bb0728/cdr

Gene bb0728/cdr was codon‐optimized for translation in E. coli and synthesized by GenScript. This sequence was PCR amplified using Phusion DNA Polymerase (Thermo Scientific) and primers 3118 and 3119 (Table S1). The DNA fragment and expression vector pGEX‐6p‐1 were digested with BamHI and SalI, ligated, and cloned in DH5α E. coli . The plasmid construct was verified by restriction digest and Sanger sequencing. The pGEX‐coADRopt plasmid was transformed into BL21 E. coli for protein production.

Generation of cdr Sequence Variants

Point mutations were introduced into cdr at nine different amino acid codons using inverse PCR to generate the following non‐synonymous changes in the translated sequence: G7A, G12A, R21A, E32A, T271A, I276A, G280A, D281A, and Y424A. Primer pairs were designed with overlapping regions (Table S1) to introduce point mutations to the codon‐optimized cdr sequence using Q5 DNA Polymerase (Thermo Scientific). Following amplification of each mutated DNA sequence, PCR products were purified (QIAGEN PCR Purification Kit) and DpnI‐treated to remove the template DNA. Mutations were added sequentially or at the same time depending on the distance between target nucleotides. Each mutation was verified by Sanger sequencing prior to the addition of the next. Four plasmid constructs were generated. Plasmid pGEX‐cdr‐ΔFAD1 contains mutations to generate amino acid substitutions G7A, G12A, R21A, and E32A. Plasmid pGEX‐cdr‐ΔFAD2 contains mutations to generate amino acid substitutions T271A, I276A, G280A, and D281A. Plasmid pGEX‐cdr‐ΔFAD3 contains mutations to generate amino acid substitution Y424A. To generate pGEX‐cdr‐ΔFADTotal, the mutations for the first four substitutions and the latter five substitutions were each introduced into two independent plasmid constructs. The two plasmids were digested with restriction enzymes SalI and EcoRI, and the appropriate fragment of each ligated together. All final plasmid constructs were reconfirmed by Sanger sequencing and transformed into BL21 E. coli for protein production.

Cloning of bb0087/ldh

Gene bb0087/ldh was PCR amplified from B. burgdorferi B31 genomic DNA using primers TC83 and TC84 (Table S1) and Phusion DNA Polymerase (Thermo Scientific). The DNA fragment and expression vector pGEX‐6p‐1 were digested with BamHI and XhoI, ligated, and cloned in DH5α E. coli . The plasmid construct was verified by restriction digest and Sanger sequencing. The pGEX‐ldh plasmid was transformed into BL21 E. coli for protein production.

Production and Purification of Recombinant Proteins

For protein production and purification, BL21 E. coli containing each protein expression plasmid or pGEX‐6p‐1 alone were grown in 500 mL of LB or M9 minimal media at 37°C to optical density at 600 nm of 0.6–0.8 and induced with 1 mM IPTG at 18°C for 16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C and the pellets resuspended in PBS and lysed by sonication. The soluble fraction was applied to a glutathione Sepharose column (Cytvia) following the manufacturer's instructions. For downstream enzymatic assays, the protein was then cleaved from its GST tag using Prescission Protease and its associated buffer for 16 h at 4°C.

Spectral Scan for FAD Binding

Purified recombinant proteins were generated from E. coli grown in LB medium. Fluorescence emission was measured using a Perkin‐Elmer LS‐55 Fluorescent Spectrometer. Data were collected using FL Win Lab software. Purified protein samples (4 μg) were excited by 450 nm light (2.5 nm bandwidth) and fluorescence emission spectra scanned from 490 nm to 700 nm at sampling intervals of 1 nm. FAD fluorescence emission was confirmed by detection of a peak at 510–525 nm for a 100 nM FAD solution.

NADH Oxidation Assay

Purified recombinant wild‐type or variant BbCoADR protein was buffer exchanged into NADH oxidation assay buffer (100 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.5 with 1 mM EDTA) using a 10 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter (Millipore Sigma). The coenzyme A disulfide reductase activity of each protein was monitored at 340 nm using a BioTek Cytation 5 Plate Reader, examining a decrease in absorbance at this wavelength resulting from the oxidation of NADH to NAD+. The reaction was carried out as described previously (Dolin 1957; Higuchi et al. 1993). Triplicate reactions were conducted with 5–10 μg purified protein, 100 μM Coenzyme A, and 100 μM NADH in the presence or absence of 10 or 100 μM FAD in assay buffer (100 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.5 with 1 mM EDTA). Upon addition of purified protein to initiate oxidation, reactions proceeded for 15–45 min at 25°C. The extinction coefficient of NADH has been reported previously as 6220 M−1 cm−1 at 340 nm (Poole and Claiborne 1988). Statistical significance was assessed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's posttest compared to ‐FAD or WT (GraphPad Prism, version 10.6.1).

Multiple Sequence Alignment

Full‐length amino acid sequences of NADH oxidase enzymes were aligned using Clustal Omega. Uniprot numbers: B. burgdorferi CoADR (O51670); B. miyamotoi CoADR (A0AAQ2WUP5); T. denticola CoADR (Q73RD6); S. aureus CoADR (O52582); C. difficile Nox (A0A0H3N9F4).
