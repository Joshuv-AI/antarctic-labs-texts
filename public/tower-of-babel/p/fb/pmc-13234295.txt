Chromosome-level genome assembly of the blackchin tilapia (Sarotherodon melanotheron)
Authors: Yuhan Niu, Kang Du, Hanyuan Zhang, Yujun Ao, Hesheng Xiao, Jun Qiang, Jianmeng Cao, Deshou Wang, Wenjing Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The blackchin tilapia (Sarotherodon melanotheron) is an aquaculture species in Southeast Asia, recognized for its adaptability to diverse environmental conditions. Genomic resources for this species remain limited, hindering progress in genetic research and breeding programs. In this study, we generated a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly of blackchin tilapia by utilizing Illumina short reads, ONT long reads, Hi-C, and RNA sequencing data. The final assembly spans 1,027.41 Mb (scaffold N50 = 38.87 Mb), with 93.42% of the assembly anchored to 22 chromosomes. Genome completeness, assessed using BUSCO, reached 99.50%. We predicted 27,896 protein-coding genes, of which 90.87% were functionally annotated. Repetitive elements accounted for approximately 42.62% of the genome. This chromosome-scale genome assembly provides a reference for comparative genomic and genetic analyses of blackchin tilapia, supporting investigations into the genetic basis of salinity tolerance and traits relevant to aquaculture breeding.

Background & Summary

Blackchin tilapia (Sarotherodon melanotheron, Rüppell 1852) is classified within the family Cichlidae, genus Sarotherodon, as a fast-breeding, highly dispersive invasive fish. Adult coloration is variable, with conspicuous black patches commonly present on the chin, flanks, and fin rays1,2. As the sister species of mango tilapia (S. galilaeus), blackchin tilapia is primarily distributed in brackish lagoons and estuarine areas of West Africa3. It can also colonize the middle and upper reaches of low-flow rivers and is widely present in freshwater ponds, lakes, and reservoirs4. Due to its aquaculture value, blackchin tilapia has been translocated to multiple countries in Asia, the United States and Europe5.

From a genomic and evolutionary perspective, blackchin tilapia is particularly intriguing due to its exceptional environmental adaptation. Physiological and ecological studies have characterized this species as an extremely euryhaline teleost6. Among bony fishes, only a few species can survive and reproduce successfully in hypersaline environments over extended periods, and blackchin tilapia is regarded as among the most salt tolerant representatives7. Survival has been documented at salinities approaching 60–70‰, corresponding to approximately 1.7–2.0 times the salinity of normal seawater (≈35‰), and successful reproduction has also been observed under such hypersaline conditions8,9. Beyond salinity tolerance, blackchin tilapia also exhibits substantial resilience to turbidity and pollution, as well as opportunistic feeding behavior that enables flexible dietary shifts in response to environmental variation, collectively contributing to its strong ecological adaptability10,11.

Blackchin tilapia has considerable economic importance in multiple countries, particularly in West Africa12,13. Within its native range, the species has long served as an important local food fish, is widely recognized for its aquaculture potential, and constitutes a dominant component of small-scale artisanal catches14,15. Although publicly available statistics on annual aquaculture production are lacking, rudimentary and semi-intensive culture systems and large-scale fry production techniques have been developed and implemented in most West African countries, and polyculture trials with other farmed species have been conducted16. Blackchin tilapia provides an important source of animal protein for the local populations and has contributed, to some extent, to increased economic returns. The remarkable environmental adaptability of blackchin tilapia facilitates its rapid occupation of available ecological niches in introduced regions. This expansion poses substantial threats to local biodiversity, while also giving rise to public health and broader ecological concerns17,18. For example, in polluted reaches of the Ogun River and Eleyele River in Nigeria, concentrations of several heavy metals, including lead, cadmium, and nickel, detected in blackchin tilapia tissues have approached or exceeded established food safety thresholds19. A survey of wild blackchin tilapia in the lake region of Benin reported an overall parasitism prevalence of 86.6%, with parasites identified from diverse taxonomic groups such as myxosporeans, monogeneans, trematodes, cestodes, nematodes, and copepods20. Therefore, alongside efforts to selectively breed blackchin tilapia strains for improved aquaculture performance, systematic monitoring of pollutant accumulation and pathogen-associated risks remains essential for ensuring aquaculture safety and long-term sustainability.

Although blackchin tilapia is of considerable importance in evolutionary biology and aquaculture, its genomic resources remain extremely limited compared with other cichlids. This scarcity of genomic information restricts comparative studies of tilapia chromosomal evolution and hinders both genetic breeding and the development of molecular tools for blackchin tilapia. Here, we generated a chromosome-level genome assembly by combining short reads produced by Illumina sequencing, long reads generated by Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data. The final assembly has a total length of 1,027.41 Mb, a scaffold N50 of 38.87 Mb, and comprises 22 chromosomes and 1,311 unplaced scaffolds. Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) analysis indicates high completeness, with 99.50% of genes complete (98.8% single-copy and 0.7% duplicated), 0.1% fragmented, and 0.4% missing. In total, 27,896 protein-coding genes were predicted. This high-quality genome assembly of blackchin tilapia not only provides essential resources for elucidating the genetic basis of salinity tolerance, but also establishes a solid genomic foundation for the selection of aquaculture traits and the breeding of superior strains.

Methods

Sample collection

A mature male blackchin tilapia was collected from Pearl River Fisheries Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Fisheries Science. Genomic DNA was extracted from muscle tissue using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were immediately frozen and subsequently stored in liquid nitrogen until use. DNA integrity and purity were assessed through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the concentration was measured using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Genome sequencing

For short-read sequencing, a 350 bp insert-size library was prepared from sheared DNA using the Illumina DNA Prep kit (Illumina, USA) and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA), generating 72.37 Gb of raw reads. For long-read sequencing, ONT libraries were constructed with the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK109) and sequenced on the PromethION system, producing 227 Gb of continuous long reads. Additionally, Hi-C libraries were prepared from approximately 1 g of blackchin tilapia muscle tissue using the GrandOmics Hi-C kit (DpnII digestion) and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform, resulting in 209.40 Gb of Hi-C data.

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

For comprehensive genome annotation, RNA was extracted from ten tissues (brain, gill, heart, head-kidney, kidney, liver, muscle, testis, spleen, and skin). RNA quality was evaluated using both a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA), cDNA libraries were prepared and sequenced in paired-end 150bp mode on the NovaSeq 6000 platform, yielding 69.01 Gb of transcriptomic data.

Genome survey

The k-mer frequency distribution was calculated from clean short reads using Jellyfish v2.3.021, followed by genome heterozygosity assessment with GenomeScope v2.0.122. Multiple k-mer lengths were tested, yielding genome size estimates ranging from 861.71 to 878.30 Mb. Genome assembly

ONT long reads were assembled into a draft genome using NextDenovo v2.5.223. The initial genome assembly was subsequently polished twice with ONT data using Racon v1.5.024, followed by two additional polishing rounds using short-read data with Polypolish v0.6.125. Haplotypic duplications were removed from the aligned bam file using Purge Haplotigs v1.1.326. Clean Hi-C reads were mapped to the draft genome using HiCUP v0.9.227 to generate valid read pairs. Subsequently, invalid pairs, unmapped reads, and PCR duplicates were removed. Next, ALLHiC v0.9.1428 was used to cluster contigs into chromosome-level groups, followed by sorting and orientation. Interaction data between contigs were converted into binary files for visualization using 3D-DNA v20100829 and Juicer v1.630. Visual corrections to the assembly were finalized using JuiceBox v2.17.0031, guided by observed chromosome interaction intensities. Ultimately, 93.42% of the assembled sequences were anchored to 22 chromosomes, with chromosome lengths ranging from 30.95 Mb to 126.28 Mb (Fig. 2). Repeat annotation

To comprehensively characterize the repetitive elements in the blackchin tilapia genome, de novo repeat prediction was conducted using RepeatModeler v2.0.732 to establish a species-specific repeat library. This customized library was subsequently merged with the RepBase database v2018102633. The combined library served as the input to RepeatMasker v4.2.134 for the identification and masking of repetitive sequences. Gene prediction and annotations

Protein-coding genes in the genome assembly of blackchin tilapia were predicted using a comprehensive strategy that combines de novo prediction, transcriptome-assisted annotation, and homology-based prediction. For de novo prediction, AUGUSTUS v3.5.035 and GlimmerHMM v3.0.436 were employed with default parameters. For transcriptome-assisted prediction, a total of 69.01 Gb of RNA-seq data were generated and used for genome-wide protein-coding gene prediction. The RNA-seq data were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.137. The resulting alignments were subsequently assembled into transcripts using StringTie v3.0.238. Transcript models were further refined using PASA v2.5.339, and coding sequences were predicted with TransDecoder v5.7.1 (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder). Homology-based prediction was generated by aligning protein sequences from five reference species (Oreochromis niloticus40, O. aureus41, O. mossambicus42, S. galilaeus43, and Oryzias latipes44) using Miniprot v0.1845 with stringent alignment criteria. Results were integrated using EVidenceModeler v2.1.046, resulting in a final consensus set of 27,896 high-confidence protein-coding genes, with an average gene length of 15,357 bp, an average coding sequence length of 1,493 bp, and a mean of 8.91 exons per gene. Functional annotation of the predicted protein sequences was conducted using Diamond v2.1.1347 against the SwissProt48, eggNOG49, and NR databases50, with an e-value cutoff of 1e-6. A total of 25,350 protein-coding genes were annotated, representing 90.87% of all inferred genes (Table 3). Data Records

The raw Illumina short reads, Hi-C data, ONT long reads, and multi-organ RNA-seq datasets from blackchin tilapia have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject PRJNA134852751 and BioSample SAMN5290416752. The SRA accession numbers are SRR3590124153 (Illumina short reads), SRR3590123654 (Hi-C data), SRR3590124055 (ONT long reads), and SRR3590122956, SRR3590123057, SRR3590123158, SRR3590123259, SRR3590123360, SRR3590123461, SRR3590123562, SRR3590123763, SRR3590123864, and SRR3590123965 (multi-organ RNA-seq). The final chromosome-level genome assembly has been deposited in the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC) under accession GWHHASP00000000.166 and in GenBank under accession JBSKFG00000000067. Gene annotations have been deposited in Figshare68.

Technical Validation

The accuracy of the blackchin tilapia genome assembly was evaluated by assessing its completeness with the conserved actinopterygii_odb12 gene set from BUSCO v5.4.369. The analysis showed high completeness of 99.50%. Specifically, 98.80% of genes were complete and single-copy, 0.70% were complete and duplicated, 0.10% were fragmented, and 0.40% were missing (Table 4). Next, short reads from a short-insert size library were aligned to the assembled genome using BWA v0.7.1927. The alignment rate was approximately 99.23%. The average sequencing depth was approximately 79.35×. Genome coverage reached approximately 97.07%. These comprehensive analyses validate the high quality of our genome assembly.
