Social marginalization and access to publicly funded home care in Ontario: a population-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Amina Jabbar, Glenda Babe, Aaron Jones, Meredith Vanstone, Kerry Kuluski, George Heckman, Andrew P Costa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults experiencing social marginalization may face inequitable access to publicly funded home care services. In Ontario, Canada, the Ontario Marginalization Index (ON-Marg) provides a multidimensional measure of area-level marginalization. This study examined whether ON-Marg dimensions were associated with access to publicly funded home care among community-dwelling adults aged 75 years and older.MethodsWe conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study using linked administrative health data from Ontario, Canada, for the year 2019. The cohort included individuals aged 75 + residing in the community (excluding those in long-term care). Home care access was stratified by ON-Marg dimensions: residential instability, material deprivation, dependency, and ethnic concentration. The primary outcomes were receipt of long-stay (≥ 90 days) and short-stay (< 90 days) home care aggregated at the community level (Aggregated Dissemination Areas -ADAs). Multivariable regression models adjusted for age, sex, hospitalization, and health complexity.ResultsAmong 1,071,899 older adults, 14% received long-stay home care and 9% received short-stay home care. Communities in the highest deprivation and ethnic concentration quintiles had a 1.60 and 2.77 percentage point greater probability, respectively, of receiving long-stay care compared to the least marginalized areas. In contrast, the most dependent communities had a 0.68 percentage point lower probability of long-stay access. For short-stay care, the highest instability quintile was associated with a 0.90 percentage point greater probability of access, while the highest ethnic concentration quintile was associated with a 2.26 percentage point lower probability. Regional analyses revealed considerable variation in these patterns across Ontario.ConclusionsAccess to home care in Ontario varies by dimension of marginalization. While some marginalized groups may have greater access—likely reflecting higher underlying need—populations with high dependency may face persistent barriers. Equity-focused reforms should consider how specific forms of marginalization affect not only need but also access and service delivery.Keywords: Home care, Marginalization, Older adults, Ontario, Equity, Access to care, Health services research, ON-Marg

Introduction

Equitable access to health care services is fundamental to achieving health equity for older adults, particularly in the context of aging in place. Publicly funded home care services are essential for supporting autonomy, preventing premature institutionalization, and maintaining quality of life for older adults. However, access to these services is not uniform across populations. Socioeconomic status, geographic location, language barriers, and systemic assumptions around informal caregiving can all shape whether and how older adults access home care [1–5].

Although Canada provides publicly funded home care as part of its health system, research increasingly demonstrates that disparities persist in how these services are used. The Ontario health system, in particular, has faced scrutiny for uneven delivery of home care services despite universal coverage principles [6]. Inequities may arise from structural and systemic factors rather than individual preferences or clinical need. Aspects of marginalization, such as socioeconomic status, gender, race and ethnicity, may both increase the need for home care and create barriers to accessing it [7, 8].

Andersen’s Behavioural Model and Access to Medical Care framework (1995) defines equitable access as affected by two major factors: 1. demographic characteristics and 2. need for health services [9]. Conceptual frameworks further emphasize the interplay between acceptability, affordability, awareness, and appropriateness as key components of access [8]. In Canada, for example, directly funded home care programs provide individuals with the funds with which they can arrange their home care services and are intended to give recipients more control. Paradoxically, these programs have been found to benefit those who are already more privileged, due to the complexity of the programs and reliance on informal support networks, such as family members, to advocate for access the home care services [6].

Globally, several studies have documented how health and social care systems fail to adequately serve older adults from marginalized groups. A recent systematic review noted that barriers to access stem from both demand-side issues (e.g., low health literacy, social exclusion, financial hardship) and supply-side factors (e.g., workforce shortages, geographic limitations, and cultural insensitivity) [10].

Despite this growing body of literature, few large-scale population-based studies have quantitatively examined how social marginalization influences access to publicly funded home care. One study using the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) dataset found that need—rather than gender or socioeconomic status—was the strongest determinant of formal care use, but also noted that informal care was disproportionately accessed by immigrants and socially advantaged groups [11]. These findings suggest that public home care may help mitigate certain disparities, yet gaps remain in understanding how marginalization affects access at the population level, particularly in Ontario.

In this study, we address this evidence gap by examining whether levels of marginalization—as measured by the Ontario Marginalization Index (ON-Marg)—are associated with access to publicly funded home care services among community-dwelling adults aged 75 and older in Ontario. Our analyses make two contributions to the existing literature; First, we provide analyses at the population-level. Second, we provide a focus specifically on publicly-funded home care services. We hypothesized that older adults living in more marginalized areas would have reduced access to publicly funded home care services, even after adjusting for demographic and clinical factors such as age, sex, health complexity, and hospitalization.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a population-based, retrospective cohort study using linked administrative health data from Ontario, Canada. The cohort included all individuals aged 75 years and older residing in community settings (i.e., not in long-term care homes) between January 1 and December 31, 2019. The study period was selected to capture patterns of home care utilization prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, minimizing the influence of pandemic-related disruptions.

Ontario provides home care services through its publicly funded health care system. During the selected study period, health care in Ontario was administered through 14 regions referred to as the Local Health Integration Networks (LHINs). Each of the LHINs were also responsible for coordinating public home care services within their catchments as a part of their portfolios. Each administrative region assessed the needs of potential home care recipients for specific services then subsequently coordinated the provision of that care through private service provider organizations [12]. Individuals and families with the financial means could pay for additional services to either augment what was publicly provided or opt out of the public administrative system entirely. Home services are varied in type and include personal support, nursing care, and therapies (occupational therapy, physiotherapy, speech language pathology, and dieticians) [13, 14].

Data sources

We accessed linked, de-identified data at ICES, a prescribed entity under Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act (PHIPA), which is allowed to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. Data sources included the Home Care Database (HCD), which captures all publicly funded home care service encounters; the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) Discharge Abstract Database and National Ambulatory Care Reporting System for hospital and emergency visits; and the Registered Persons Database for demographic information. Health complexity was assessed using the Resource Intensity Weight (RIW), a CIHI-derived measure based on health care utilization. Marginalization was measured using the 2016 Ontario Marginalization Index (ON-Marg), a validated area-based measure of social determinants of health, linked via postal code to Aggregated Dissemination Areas (ADAs). ADAs are a geographic-level of measurement; they are generally smaller than census tracts with a defined cluster of neighbourhood areas that have approximately 5,000–10,000 individuals [7, 15, 16].

ON-Marg was developed to capture social determinants of health and its 2016 iteration consists of four dimensions: residential instability, material deprivation, ethnic concentration, and dependency [7, 17]. Table 1 defines each of the ON-Marg dimensions as per the 2016 User Manual [7].

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was access to publicly funded home care services, defined as proportional receipt of either long-stay (≥ 90 days) or short-stay (< 90 days) home care at the ADA level during the study period. Long-stay care typically reflects ongoing support for chronic or progressive conditions, while short-stay care is often associated with acute recovery needs (e.g., post-surgical or rehabilitative episodes). Service duration (long-stay versus short-stay home care) was captured through billing and care plan data in the HCD.

Covariates

Covariates included demographic factors (age, sex), clinical characteristics (hospitalization within the calendar year [yes/no], and health complexity via RIW), and area-level marginalization (ON-Marg dimensions). Each were aggregated up to the ADA level as the ADA was the unit of analysis. These covariates were selected based on previous literature demonstrating their association with home care use [9, 18]. Our selection of demographic factors and clinical characteristics is also well supported by Andersen’s Behavioural Model and Medical Care Use framework in which equitable access is defined as a combination of demographic factors and well as need for health care; more specifically, both hospitalization within the last year and health care complexity are indicators for need [9]. RIW was used as a proxy measure of health complexity since the variable represents the relative resources used by a patient; the RIW increases with the consumption of resources [19].

Each of the four ON-Marg domains (residential instability, material deprivation, dependency, and ethnic concentration) were modeled as categorical variables using their respective standard quintile distributions, with quintile 1 indicating the lowest marginalization and quintile 5 the highest [7]. We opted to use quintiles instead of more aggregated groupings for several reasons. First, quintiles can help assess if trends are “U-shaped” as opposed to purely straight-line relationships. Second, the granularity of quintiles can help identify the thresholds at which effects start or accelerate. Third, quintiles offer more detail than binary splits or tertiles but are easier to interpret than deciles [20].

Prior to proceeding with the statistical analyses, we confirmed that there was no collinearity between the covariates.

Statistical analysis

We generated descriptive statistics to compare demographic and clinical characteristics between home care recipients and non-recipients. Multivariable linear regression models were used to examine the association between ON-Marg dimensions and the change in the proportion receiving long- or short-stay home care, adjusting for age, sex, hospitalization, and RIW; the first model was not regionally stratified by LHINs and the second model was regionally stratified by LHINs. We opted to do this to account for possible regional effects of home care provision given the LHINs functioned as administrative coordinators for services within their catchments.

To closely examine regional variations in associations, we conducted post-hoc analyses for eight of Ontario’s 14 administrative regions, selected to represent major urban catchment areas. All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide (version 8.3) within the ICES Remote Access Environment (RAE). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Missing data were minimal (< 1%) and were handled through listwise deletion.

Results

We included 1,071,899 community-dwelling individuals aged 75 years and older across Ontario. A total of 1529 ADAs in Ontario were included within our analysis, with an average of 695.35 older adults per ADA (SD = 364.55).

Among individuals in the sample, 14% (n = 148,923) received long-stay home care (defined as services provided for 90 days or more), and 9% (n = 94,914) received short-stay home care (defined as services provided for less than 90 days). Compared to non-recipients, individuals who received either form of home care were older, more likely to be female, had higher rates of hospitalization, and exhibited greater health complexity, as measured by their RIW. These patterns were consistent across both long- and short-stay home care users.

Descriptive characteristics

Table 2 presents the baseline characteristics of the study cohort stratified by home care status. Home care recipients had higher mean ages, greater hospitalization rates, and higher health complexity than non-recipients. Among long-stay home care recipients, 64.2% were female, compared to 56.2% among both short-stay recipients and those who did not receive home care. When stratified by quintiles of marginalization, long-stay recipients were more likely to reside in areas with higher levels of material deprivation and ethnic concentration. In contrast, individuals in areas characterized by higher dependency were less likely to receive long-stay care. For short-stay home care, patterns by marginalization dimensions were more variable, though a modest increase in access was observed in areas of greater residential instability.

Multivariable regression analysis

Access to long-stay home care

In multivariable regression models adjusting for age, sex, hospitalization, and health complexity, we observed distinct associations between dimensions of marginalization and access to long-stay home care. Communities in the second, third, fourth, and fifth quintiles of material deprivation had significantly greater access compared to those in the first deprivation quintile (Q2: β = 0.0079, p = 0.002; Q3: β = 0.0077, p = 0.004; Q4: β = 0.0068, p = 0.02; Q5: β = 0.0160, p < 0.0001). Specifically, the highest deprivation quintile (Q5) was associated with a 1.60 percentage point greater probability of long-stay home care access.

Ethnic concentration followed a similar trend. Relative to the least ethnically concentrated communities (Q1), the most concentrated areas (Q5) had a 2.77 percentage point greater probability of access to long-stay home care (β = 0.0277, p < 0.0001). Quintiles 3 and 4 also showed significantly increased access (Q3: β = 0.0087, p = 0.005; Q4: β = 0.0195, p < 0.0001). Only ethnic concentration Quintile 2 did not show significantly increased access (Q2: β = 0.0017, p = 0.53).

In contrast, higher dependency was associated with lower access to long-stay home care. The most dependent communities (Q5) had a 0.68 percentage point lower probability of receiving care compared to those in the least dependent areas (β = –0.0068, p = 0.04). The second, third, and fourth quintiles of dependency also demonstrated negative associations, though only Q2 reached statistical significance (Q2: β = –0.0064, p = 0.01; Q3: −0.0026, p = 0.34; Q4: β = −0.0047, p = 0.12).

Residential instability was not significantly associated with access to long-stay home care in any quintile. All coefficients were small and not statistically significant (Q2: β = 0.0011, p = 0.67; Q3: β = 0.0012, p = 0.67; Q4: −0/0038, p = 0.22; Q5: β = –0.0030, p = 0.34). These associations remained robust in stratified models by region, although some variability remained. Access to short-stay home care

For short-stay home care, the patterns of association with ON-Marg dimensions differed. Residential instability was positively associated with short-stay home care access. In the highest instability quintile (Q5), communities had a 0.90 percentage point greater probability of receiving short-stay home care compared to the most stable areas (β = 0.0090, p = 0.0004). Quintiles 2, 3, and 4 did not have significant associations with access to short-stay home care (Q2: β = 0.0012, p = 0.57; Q3: β = −0.0025, p = 0.28; Q4: β = 0.0039, p = 0.12).

Ethnic concentration, in contrast, was negatively associated with access to short-stay home care. The most ethnically concentrated communities (Q5) had a 2.26 percentage point lower probability of receiving short-stay services compared to the least concentrated (β = –0.0226, p < 0.0001). Quintiles 3 and 4 were also significantly associated with lower access (Q3: β = –0.0057, p = 0.02; Q4: β = –0.0110, p < 0.0001) while there was no association with Quintile 2 of ethnic concentration and access to short-stay home care (Q2: β = 0.0010, p = 0.67). Material deprivation and dependency did not demonstrate consistent or significant associations with short-stay access. These findings remained generally consistent in regionally stratified models, though some variation was observed in the direction and magnitude of effects. Regional variations in home care access

Post-hoc analyses by regions revealed substantial variation in the association between ON-Marg dimensions and home care access. For long-stay home care (Fig. 1), increased material deprivation was associated with greater access in the Toronto Central and Hamilton Niagara Haldimand Brant (HNHB) regions. For example, in the Toronto Central region, the most deprived communities (Q5) had up to a 1.81 percentage point greater probability of receiving long-stay home care compared to the least deprived (β = 0.0181, p < 0.0001).

Dependency was negatively associated with access to long-stay home care in the Central East and Champlain regions. In the Champlain region, the most dependent communities had a 0.93 percentage point lower probability of accessing care than those in the least dependent areas (β = –0.0093, p = 0.001). Ethnic concentration remained positively associated with long-stay home care in most regions. For instance, in the South West region, communities in the highest ethnic concentration quintile had a 2.01 percentage point greater probability of receiving long-stay home care compared to those in the lowest quintile (β = 0.0201, p < 0.0001).

For short-stay home care (Fig. 2), residential instability was consistently associated with increased access in several regions. In the Central West, Champlain, and South West regions, communities in the highest instability quintile had between 0.82 and 1.18 percentage point greater access than those in the lowest quintile (e.g., South West LHIN: β = 0.0082, p < 0.0001).

Increased ethnic concentration was negatively associated with access to short-stay home care in several regions, including Champlain, Mississauga Halton, and Central East regions. In Champlain, the most ethnically concentrated communities had a 0.74 percentage point lower probability of receiving short-stay home care compared to the least concentrated (β = –0.0074, p = 0.001). Material deprivation and dependency showed smaller and less consistent associations with short-stay home care access across regions. In some regions, these dimensions were weakly negatively associated with access (e.g., Deprivation Q4 in South West region: β = –0.0036, p = 0.02), while in others they were not statistically significant. Overall, these findings suggest that even where population-level associations exist, local service delivery models and policies may mediate access patterns in meaningful ways.

Discussion

This population-based study of over 1 million community-dwelling older adults in Ontario found that marginalization, as measured by the Ontario Marginalization Index (ON-Marg), is significantly associated with differential access to publicly funded home care. Specifically, we observed increased access to long-stay home care in areas with greater material deprivation and ethnic concentration, but reduced access in areas with higher economic dependency. These patterns remained robust after controlling for individual health complexity, hospitalization, age, and sex.

These findings challenge older public health models which often considered all aspects of marginalization to be static, monolith barriers to the access health care services. Both the early version of the Anderson Behavioural Model of Health Services Use (1968) and Penchansky and Thomas’ The “Five A’s” of Access (1981), for example, uniformly frame demographic factors such as low income, racialization, and female gender, as roadblocks to access [9, 21].

Theoretical shifts towards recognition of intersectionality have meant that researchers now more widely understand that different social identities have different impacts on public health outcomes, that their impacts are not simply additive, and that the intersections between different aspects of marginalization create different outcomes [22, 23]. Our findings reflect and support this theoretical shift. For example, some dimensions of marginalization—particularly material deprivation and ethnic concentration—may be associated with increased receipt of long-stay home care services, potentially reflecting targeted outreach, higher baseline need, or that wealthier patients are more likely to procure private care services. The negative association between dependency and access highlights a persistent gap that may be attributable to better availability of informal caregivers (therefore, less use of publicly available home care services) or, conversely, bureaucratic navigation barriers in more dependent populations. Unfortunately, the reasons for the negative association cannot be explained within the existing analysis.

Our results complement recent evidence from Vafaei et al. [11], who found that need, rather than sociodemographic characteristics like gender or income, primarily predicted receipt of formal home care in Canada. Our stratified analysis by region suggests that geographic variation may further modify how marginalization interacts with access. Eight of Ontario’s 14 administrative regions (LHINs) which were selected for our post-hoc analyses each represent major urban catchment areas and shared substantial similarities with respect to their 2019 demographics [24, 25]. Most of the regions with exception to two LHINs had populations of 1.2–1.5 million residents; Central West and South West LHINs were smaller at about 900,000 to 980,000 residents, respectively. Adults aged 65 and older represented about 15–19% of the populations in all of the selected regions. Five of the eight regions were highly racially diverse – 40–60% of their populations identified as non-white. The remaining three regions, Champlain, HNHB, and South West, were less diverse with 22%, 13%, and 5% of their residents identified as non-white, respectively. About 9–14% of their residents classified as low-income among seven of the selected eight regions; Only Toronto Central had a higher proportion of its residents classified as low-income at about 19% [24, 25].

Despite the substantial overlap among the LHINs, our analyses noted significant and varying differences in home care access across the administrative regions by marginalization and long-stay versus short-stay home care. Unfortunately, little is publicly available with regards to home care allocation decision making in each of the administrative regions. However, our analysis reinforces prior critiques of Ontario’s regionalized model of care delivery and its inconsistent implementation across catchment areas [6].

At the same time, our findings align with international literature documenting both structural and contextual barriers to care. Cabañero-Garcia et al. [10] highlight that older adults often encounter a constellation of access barriers—from economic hardship to the digital divide—that shape both formal and informal care utilization. Researchers such as Penchansky and Thomas (1981) and Carroll et al. (2022) further emphasize the multidimensional nature of access, pointing to concepts such as availability, affordability, acceptability, and awareness as critical mediators [8, 21]. Our findings reflect these themes and add population-level evidence from a Canadian context.

Our findings also question assumptions about family caregiving in racialized and immigrant communities. While some policymakers and providers presume that informal supports are more robust in these groups, our findings—consistent with Brotman’s [4] critique of the “mythology of the extended kin network”—indicate that formal services are, in fact, more frequently accessed in areas of high ethnic concentration, potentially due to increased need or fewer viable alternatives.

Taken together, our findings suggests that while Ontario’s publicly funded home care system may deliver services in a manner partially aligned with equity principles, disparities remain—particularly for populations characterized by high dependency. These patterns may reflect mismatches between system design and the lived realities of older adults in marginalized communities. For example, those with the greatest dependency may face challenges securing assessments, navigating bureaucratic processes, or accessing providers willing to serve complex clients.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study include the use of a large, population-based cohort, linkage of administrative and sociodemographic data, and the use of ON-Marg as a multidimensional measure of marginalization. However, our findings must be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, we used pre-pandemic 2019 data; patterns of access may have shifted post-COVID-19, particularly with the expansion of virtual services and increased caregiver strain.

Second, ON-Marg is an area-level measure and may not fully capture individual-level experiences of marginalization. In addition, our analysis used home care access data from 2019 but needed to rely on the 2016 ON-Marg index; some of the dimensions may have shifted within that three-year period. Third, our outcome measure captures access to care, not quality or adequacy of care.

Fourth, while our models controlled for key demographic and health complexity variables, unmeasured confounders—such as caregiver availability or language proficiency—may influence observed associations. Proxy variables, such as living arrangements and marital status were missing for a large proportion of the dataset and, as such, were not reliable. Similarly, our regional analysis only included those areas with large urban centres. As such, all of the northern regions of Ontario were excluded and our findings may not be generalizable to rural regions. Our findings may also not be generalizable to jurisdictions outside of Ontario where health system structures and home care eligibility criteria differ.

Implications for policy and practice

Our findings carry important implications for health system planners and policymakers. First, efforts to improve equity in home care must consider the nuanced and sometimes counterintuitive ways that marginalization shapes access. Second, targeted interventions may be needed to address low access among populations with high dependency—potentially including care navigation supports, mobile assessments, or alternative intake pathways. Third, more granular monitoring of access disparities—disaggregated by ON-Marg dimensions and geography—should be embedded into performance frameworks within Ontario’s health care system and other integrated care models.

Future research should examine the quality, intensity, and outcomes of care across marginalized groups. Similarly, we need to better understand how different aspects of marginalizations affect access. Access alone does not guarantee equity and greater insight is needed into how care is delivered, experienced, and sustained in communities facing structural disadvantage.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that social marginalization, as measured by the Ontario Marginalization Index, is significantly associated with access to publicly funded home care services for older adults in Ontario. While greater material deprivation and ethnic concentration were associated with increased access to long-stay home care—likely reflecting higher levels of need—older adults living in areas with high dependency were less likely to receive services. These findings suggest that the home care system may partially align with equity goals but fails to adequately serve all marginalized populations, particularly those facing compounded vulnerabilities.

Equity-focused planning must move beyond universal coverage to ensure responsive, accessible, and appropriate care for diverse older adult populations. Future research should examine care quality and outcomes across marginalization gradients and identify targeted strategies to reduce structural barriers—especially for individuals with high dependency or limited support networks.
