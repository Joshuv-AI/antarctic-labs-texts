Underground vs outdoor air in the “Wieliczka” Salt Mine health resort: particulate matter and selected gaseous pollutants
Authors: Patrycja Rogula-Kopiec, Magdalena Żurawka, Jan Stefan Bihałowicz, Wioletta Rogula-Kozłowska, Karolina Bralewska, Anna Smola-Dmochowska, Magdalena Kostrzon, Artur Badyda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The impact of particulate matter (PM) on respiratory health is a multifaceted issue, contingent on factors such as particle size and composition. The present study set out to compare the concentrations of size-resolved PM and selected gases (VOCs, CO, and NO2) in the underground treatment chamber in the “Wieliczka” Salt Mine (a UNESCO-listed tourist attraction and health resort, not an active industrial mining site), and in the adjacent outdoor environment. The study also assessed the variability of these concentrations with respect to occupancy. The results showed that the mean indoor-to-outdoor ratio (I/O ratio) for fine particles (PM1) was less than 1 (i.e., 0.623). In contrast, the mean I/O ratio for larger fractions (PM4–PMtot) was greater than 2, likely due to the mechanical resuspension of settled dust by visitors. VOC concentrations were particularly low in the underground environment; the mean I/O ratio was 0.063, and the mean I/O ratio for NO2 was also low (0.62), indicating limited infiltration of anthropogenic pollution. CO levels were moderately elevated indoors due to restricted ventilation but remained below the strictest applicable regulatory thresholds. Overall, the underground microclimate is characterised by internally generated aerosol and low levels of gaseous pollutants. The underground chamber was characterised by markedly lower concentrations of anthropogenic pollutants than the outdoor environment. Introduction

Particulate matter (PM) is a key air pollutant and an important determinant of human health. Consequently, it has become a major focus of research in epidemiology, toxicology, and environmental sciences (Anderson et al., 2012; Paul et al., 2022). Due to their minute size, PM particles can penetrate all regions of the respiratory system. There, they may induce inflammatory reactions, oxidative stress, ventilation disorders, and structural changes in lung tissue. These effects can extend to the cardiovascular system (Paul et al., 2022). Crucially, the depth of respiratory deposition is size-dependent: particles in the coarse size range (2.5–10 µm) are primarily deposited in the upper airways by inertial impaction, while fine (PM2.5) and submicrometere (PM1) fractions penetrate to the bronchioles and alveoli, exerting the most pronounced adverse effects (Anderson et al., 2012; WHO, 2021). This size dependency means that size-resolved characterisation of PM—spanning the full continuum from PM1 to total suspended particulate matter—is essential not only for accurate health risk assessment, but also for evaluating intentionally designed aerosol environments for therapeutic inhalation.

Among the natural components of atmospheric aerosols, sea-salt particles have received increasing scientific attention due to their significant contribution to PM10 and PM2.5 mass concentrations in coastal and inland regions alike (Lewis & Schwartz, 2010; Murphy et al., 2019). These particles are of marine origin and are generated primarily through the bursting of bubbles, evaporation, and splashing of saline water. They are predominantly composed of NaCl, along with MgCl₂, CaSO₄ and KCl, and differ in their hygroscopicity, optical properties, and chemical reactivity (Lewis & Schwartz, 2010). Sea-salt aerosols exhibit robust hygroscopic properties and undergo dynamic changes in size and phase in response to ambient relative humidity. This modifies their optical behaviour and atmospheric visibility (Liu et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2021). In polluted environments, sea-salt particles also act as reactive surfaces for acidic and oxidised gases, such as NOx, SO2, and HNO3, leading to chloride depletion and the formation of secondary sulphate and nitrate salts that may enrich particles with toxic elements (Murphy et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022). While there is a general consensus that sea-salt aerosol is generally less toxic than anthropogenic particulate matter, its atmospheric role and potential health impacts are more complex than previously assumed (Horowitz et al., 2020; Murphy et al., 2019).

Beyond their atmospheric significance, the physicochemical properties of salt aerosols have long been exploited for therapeutic purposes in spa environments and brine graduation towers. Salt aerosol is intentionally used for inhalation therapy, generating particles enriched in Na⁺, Cl⁻, Mg2⁺, Ca2⁺, and SO42⁻ that differ markedly from urban PM in composition and biological properties (Faracik, 2020a; W. Rogula-Kozłowska et al., 2022). Subterranean facilities, such as the “Wieliczka” Salt Mine health resort, represent a particularly distinctive microenvironment for speleotherapy, in which mineral particles dominate the size-segregated PM—predominantly NaCl with minor amounts of CaSO4—and contributions from trace elements are negligible (Wioletta Rogula-Kozłowska et al., 2016). The enclosed underground setting further limits the infiltration of anthropogenic pollutants. NaCl surfaces have been shown to scavenge reactive acidic gases, including HNO₃ and HCl, and outdoor pollution entering through ventilation shafts is progressively attenuated along the tunnel system (Salmon et al., 1996). Consequently, the subterranean spa environment at Wieliczka is considered to be one of the most pristine aerosol microenvironments in Poland (Bralewska et al., 2022).

A substantial body of clinical and experimental research has examined the effects of inhaled saline aerosols on respiratory function, particularly when used alongside standard treatments for chronic respiratory diseases such as asthma and COPD (Oleksa et al., 2022; Peddibhotla et al., 2024). Saline aerosols have mucolytic properties, reducing the viscosity of bronchial secretions and enhancing mucociliary clearance. This may alleviate symptoms such as coughing, dyspnoeal, and airway obstruction in certain patient groups (Peddibhotla et al., 2024). It is hypothesised that these effects are more pronounced in environments characterised by very low concentrations of anthropogenic pollutants and allergens, as this prevents the co-inhalation of pro-inflammatory agents that could otherwise exacerbate respiratory and cardiovascular outcomes (Konduracka & Rostoff, 2022; Peddibhotla et al., 2024). However, due to the limited number and heterogeneity of existing clinical trials, halotherapy and speleotherapy are currently regarded as complementary rather than primary therapeutic approaches (Oleksa et al., 2022; Peddibhotla et al., 2024).

It should be noted that exposure to salt mine conditions may pose a higher risk of adverse reactions for certain patient groups, including those with unstable asthma, significant circulatory failure, hyperthyroidism, or claustrophobia. These reactions may include increased coughing and increased bronchial secretion production, which are related to the physiological airway-clearance effect induced by salt aerosol. Exposure to high concentrations of saline aerosol may also cause temporary skin and conjunctival irritation, such as dryness, redness, and a burning sensation, as well as a slight increase in blood pressure, which may be related to the specific underground conditions. These symptoms are usually transient and do not lead to permanent health consequences (Czajka et al., 2006; Faracik, 2020b; Kamińska & Krusiec-Świdergoł, 2023; Ziemska et al., 2019). In terms of the aerosol’s properties, its granulometric composition is also important: to achieve a therapeutic effect in obstructive diseases, a predominance of particles with a diameter of 1–3 μm is desirable, whereas a larger proportion of the fine fraction (0.5–1 μm), which is deposited in the peripheral parts of the lungs, may be less beneficial (Śliwiński et al., 2014). Coarse-droplet aerosols mainly act in the upper respiratory tract, causing mucosal congestion and increased secretion through a mechanical effect. This is sometimes used to prepare for actual therapy (Achkar, 2015). It is also worth noting that hypertonic salt aerosols may induce bronchospasm in individuals with bronchial hyperreactivity during diagnostic testing, whereas this does not occur in healthy individuals (Crisan-Dabija et al., 2021). While available data suggest that serious complications associated with aerosol exposure in salt spas are uncommon, considering the aforementioned short-term, mostly mild side effects allows for a more balanced evaluation of the impact of this microclimate.

Despite the recognised therapeutic potential of underground salt environments, quantitative data directly comparing size-resolved PM fractions and gaseous pollutants between operational underground spa chambers and the adjacent outdoor environment remain scarce (Bralewska et al., 2022; Wioletta Rogula-Kozłowska et al., 2016). Without such data, it is not possible to determine the extent to which the distinctive aerosol composition of the underground microclimate differs quantitatively from ambient conditions, nor to assess how episodic increases in PM concentration driven by visitor occupancy modify the otherwise pristine therapeutic environment. A rigorous indoor-to-outdoor characterisation, accounting for the influence of human activity on aerosol dynamics, is therefore essential to substantiate the therapeutic rationale for speleotherapy and to inform evidence-based occupancy and ventilation management in subterranean spa facilities (Bralewska et al., 2022; Puławska et al., 2021; Salmon et al., 1996).

The objectives of this study are twofold: (i) to compare the concentrations of size-resolved particulate matter and selected gaseous pollutants in the 'Wieliczka' Salt Mine health resort with those in the nearby outdoor environment; and (ii) to quantify the differences in the PM fractions that are most relevant to respiratory deposition in the underground treatment chamber,1 using surface measurements to represent typical ambient conditions.

Methods

Measurement devices

Air quality investigations were conducted using a Palas AQ Guard 1200 online aerosol spectrometer equipped with a multigas sensor (Palas GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany) (Palas GmbH, 2024). These instruments are designed for the continuous monitoring of air quality parameters in both indoor and outdoor environments. They are based on optical aerosol spectrometry technology. The analysers operate by measuring the intensity of light scattered by airborne particles, enabling both quantitative and qualitative characterisation of particulate aerosols.

The devices allow determination of particle size distributions within an aerodynamic diameter range of 0.175–20 µm. High-resolution measurements are performed using 32 channels per particle-size decade, enabling detailed analysis of particulate matter fractions and precise determination of their mass and number concentrations at 1-min sampling intervals. This high measurement resolution is important for inhalation exposure assessments, especially in environments with dynamically changing aerosol emission sources.

In addition to particulate matter measurements, the AQ Guard 1200 analyser is equipped with integrated electrochemical and semiconductor sensors that enable the simultaneous monitoring of selected gaseous pollutants. Concentrations of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were measured as these are key indicators of emissions from everyday products, such as cosmetics, paints, varnishes, and cleaning agents. Additionally, carbon dioxide (CO₂) concentrations were monitored because CO₂ is a widely used indicator of ventilation efficiency and indoor air quality. Elevated CO₂ levels may indicate insufficient air exchange.

The device also monitored nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), a gas mainly generated in combustion processes that can infiltrate indoor environments from outdoor air and adversely affect respiratory health, up to 2000 µg/m3 with a resolution of < 1 µg/m3. Furthermore, the analyser measured carbon monoxide (CO) concentrations of up to 5000 µg/m3 with a resolution of less than 1 µg/m3. CO is a hazardous air pollutant that may result from incomplete fuel combustion and poses a direct threat to human health and life.

Using the integrated Palas AQ Guard 1200 measurement system allowed for the simultaneous, continuous, and high-resolution assessment of the aerosol and gaseous components of air quality. This formed the robust basis for a thorough analysis of inhalation exposure in the studied environment.

Characteristics of measurement points

The “Wieliczka” Salt Mine is a unique measurement site on both national and global scales. As a UNESCO-listed tourist attraction and health resort rather than an active industrial mining site, it enables studies of a specific underground environment that differs significantly from typical surface locations in microclimatic conditions, aerosol composition, and air exchange dynamics. Consequently, the facility is a particularly valuable location for assessing air quality and conducting comparative aerosol analyses between underground and outdoor environments.

Air pollution was measured concurrently at two measurement points, selected to facilitate evaluation of disparities between conditions within the mine and those in the external environment. Location of the sampling points is presented in Fig. 1. The initial measurement point was situated in the western section of the “Wessel Lake” salt chamber on the third level of the mine, approximately 135 m below ground level. The region's distinctive microclimatic conditions, characterised by stability and limited air exchange with the external environment, give rise to an aerosol with a specific composition. This aerosol is a product of the natural properties inherent in the salt rock formation. The Wessel Lake chamber was excavated from a deposit of typical green salt. The chamber measures approximately 66 m in length, 20 m in width, and 11–15 m in height. Compliance with regulations

In this study, the primary objective is to assess the air quality in the underground health resort against the requirements of the revised Ambient Air Quality Directive (AAQD 2024/2881) (EC 2024). It is noteworthy that subterranean facilities are rare, and most therapeutic spas in Europe operate as above-ground sanatoria in spa towns. In such locations, outdoor air quality is routinely evaluated against the AAQD framework. However, when therapeutic procedures are conducted in enclosed spaces specifically intended to improve patients' respiratory health, it is reasonable to require compliance with the highest legally binding standards. Consequently, the underground spa environment is being held to the target values that will become mandatory under AAQD 2024, effective from 2030 onward. Furthermore, the analysis aligns with the World Health Organization (WHO) air quality guidelines (WHO, 2021), which represent the most stringent evidence-based recommendations currently available, although they lack legal force. The evaluation aims to ascertain whether this atypical facility meets not only formal regulatory thresholds but also the most health-protective international benchmarks.

Results and discussion

The statistical parameters of particulate matter (PM) and selected gaseous pollutants, i.e., volatile organic compounds (VOCs), carbon monoxide (CO), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2), inside and outside the Wieliczka Salt Mine are presented in Fig. 2. A thorough examination of the data presented in this Figure reveals a conspicuous discrepancy in the concentrations of PM2.5 and larger particulate matter. The levels of these particles were found to be significantly higher indoors (underground) compared to outdoors. The discrepancy between PM10 and PMtot exceeds twofold. This relationship applies to both average and maximum values, as well as to the third quartile (Q3). The measurement campaign comprised 44,640 valid 1-min observations from 30.05.2025 8:01 to 30.06.2025 8:00, i.e., collected over 31 complete days. Data were averaged into right-closed hourly mean concentrations (n = 744, Fig. 2). The mean PM2.5 concentration in the subterranean environment during the campaign was 9 µg/m3, whereas the mean PM10 concentration was 32 µg/m3. The maximum observed daily PM2.5concentration was 20 µg/m3, i.e., around 80% of the daily limit set in the 2024 recast of the Ambient Air Quality Directive (EC 2024). For PM10, the maximum daily value was 94 µg/m3. It should be noted that the 2024 recast allows up to 18 exceedances per calendar year of the daily PM10 limit (45 µg/m3), so a single high value cannot be directly interpreted as non-compliance without full-year data. Due to the campaign’s limited temporal coverage (31 days), the mean concentrations cannot be directly compared with annual air quality standards. Nevertheless, the average PM2.5 of 9 µg/m3 is below the annual limit of 10 µg/m3 (EC 2024), indicating a limited contribution to the annual mean. However, full-year data would be required to confirm compliance with annual standards. In light of prior research on the composition of PM in the “Wieliczka” Salt Mine, it can be posited that the primary source of PM particles is the structural material that envelops the tunnel walls (Wioletta Rogula-Kozłowska et al., 2016; Wiszniewski, 2015). The influence of atmospheric air on the recorded spikes in PM concentrations can be excluded. Although air entering the mine from outside can contain anthropogenic pollutants, these are filtered out as the air flows through the ventilation tunnel system, leaving only trace concentrations (Wioletta Rogula-Kozłowska et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019).

This pattern demonstrates that coarser PM fractions exhibit increasingly right-skewed distributions, characterised by episodic high concentrations that substantially elevate the mean above the median, indicating episodic resuspension events driven by patient activity rather than continuous background emission (Bralewska et al., 2022).

The coefficients of variation (CV) for coarse PM fractions were exceptionally high indoors, exceeding 100% for PM10 (CV = 135%), PMtot (CV = 142%), and PM4 (CV = 122%), confirming pronounced temporal fluctuations, while outdoor for these fractions were between 43 and 51%. These results underscore the need for occupancy management and traffic control in underground therapeutic spaces to limit short-term episodes of elevated PM exposure that may exceed ambient air quality guidelines during peak visitor hours; low baseline concentrations between activity periods do not offset the potential health relevance of such episodic peaks. This is important because, although coarse particles are less penetrating than fine particles, they can cause irritation (e.g., conjunctival and mucous membrane irritation) after prolonged exposure or at relatively high momentary concentrations (Khaniabadi et al., 2017).

The results presented in Fig. 2 support the hypothesis proposed in the extant literature (Frączek et al., 2013; Puławska et al., 2021; Wioletta Rogula-Kozłowska et al., 2016) and extend it by demonstrating that the predominant mechanism of dust formation is likely resuspension of mineral particles caused by human movement. In addition, during business hours (06:00–18:00), a brine fountain is occasionally operated, which may further enhance local resuspension of salt particles in the device's immediate vicinity. An additional argument for the dominance of mechanically generated mineral dust is the low proportion of PM1 (mean = 3.6 µg/m3; 7.2% of PMtot), indicating a marginal contribution from fine particulate sources (Błaszczak et al., 2025; Mishra et al., 2024; Skiba et al., 2024). This suggests the absence of direct combustion-related emission sources, such as heating, smoking, or vehicle exhaust, within the treatment chamber.

In contrast to particulate matter, outdoor air exhibited significantly higher VOC concentrations (mean = 20 µg/m3; range 0.067–170 µg/m3) compared to the underground spa (mean = 0.44 µg/m3; range 0.00083–11 µg/m3). This distribution is consistent with the absence of typical indoor VOC sources in the salt mine environment, such as synthetic building materials, furniture, paints, varnishes, or cleaning products. Earlier studies of natural underground facilities (e.g., salt mines, caves) have similarly reported mineral and aerosol dominance with limited VOC emissions (Ijoma et al., 2025). Elevated outdoor VOC levels reflect contributions from vehicular traffic, solvent use, industrial coatings, and petrochemical facilities (Jiang et al., 2025).

CO concentrations in the underground chamber (mean = 2.8 mg/m3; range 2.7–3.3 mg/m3) were consistently higher than outdoor levels (mean = 1.3 mg/m3; range 0.42–2.0 mg/m3), despite the absence of combustion sources within the facility. This phenomenon, documented in subterranean environments (Menéndez et al. 2022; Novas et al., 2017), is attributable to restricted air exchange and the tendency for CO to accumulate in underground spaces through slow infiltration from surface air. The narrow concentration range (CV = 1.7%) and extremely low standard deviation (SD = 0.047 mg/m3) confirm the absence of episodic internal CO sources and indicate a stable background level maintained by ventilation rather than active emission. Importantly, all measured CO concentrations remained well below current EU regulatory limits (Directive 2008/50/EC: 8-h average limit = 10 mg/m3) and comply with the stricter, forthcoming standard (Directive 2024/2881/EU: daily average limit = 4 mg/m3). Nevertheless, the persistent elevation above outdoor air levels highlights the importance of adequate ventilation design in underground therapeutic facilities to prevent the gradual accumulation of even low-level gaseous pollutants.

NO2 concentrations in the underground environment exhibited remarkable stability (mean = 38 µg/m3; SD = 0.046 µg/m3). Measurements were conducted in a closed underground environment with no air exchange with the ambient atmosphere. Under such conditions, NO₂ concentrations are expected to remain stable, as there are no external emission sources or ventilation. The sensor resolution is < 1 µg/m3; the total observed range of NO₂ values (max–min 0.38 µg/m3) falls below this threshold, confirming that the apparent constancy reflects true environmental stability rather than sensor saturation or data processing artefacts. When accounting for the extended uncertainty due to resolution, the value is 38 ± 2 µg/m3. Indoor NO2 levels were substantially lower than outdoor concentrations (mean = 75 µg/m3; range 8.8–290 µg/m3), which varied strongly with traffic intensity and meteorological conditions, particularly wind speed and direction (Mucha et al., 2026). The absence of temporal variability indoors (CV < 1%) confirms that NO2 originates exclusively from slow diffusion of ambient air through ventilation shafts, with no internal generation.

Underground NO2 levels (38 µg/m3) approach but do not exceed the current EU annual mean limit (40 µg/m3; Directive 2008/50/EC) and remain below the 1-h limit (200 µg/m3, not to be exceeded more than 18 times per year). However, the recently adopted Directive 2024/2881/EU establishes more stringent standards: a maximum daily mean of 50 µg/m3 (≤ 18 exceedances/year) and an annual mean of 20 µg/m3. The underground environment currently meets these criteria due to temporal stability; continuous monitoring is recommended to ensure ongoing compliance and to assess whether seasonal variations in outdoor NO2 or changes in ventilation rates could lead to future exceedances, particularly given the facility's proximity to traffic emission sources.

It should be noted that the measurements described were conducted only during the warm months (May–June), so the results refer only to the non-heating season. While the results indicate that the mine's microclimate is stable and determined by internal factors, due to its depth and ventilation systems, allowing for the preliminary exclusion of seasonal variations, outdoor concentrations of PM, VOCs, CO, and NO₂ may be higher in winter. This is related to higher emissions of these pollutants from combustion processes during the heating season, poorer dispersion conditions of pollutants, and lower efficiency of photochemical transformations (Nidzgorska-Lencewicz & Czarnecka, 2015; Zabiegała et al., 2010; Żyromski et al., 2014). Therefore, future studies should be conducted in different seasons (heating vs non-heating) to strengthen the hypothesis that atmospheric air quality does not affect mine air quality, which is determined by internal sources and may contribute to favourable microclimatic conditions.

Analysis of temporal variability in PM and gas concentrations between business hours (Mon–Fri, 06–18, including preparation hours) and off-hours (nights and weekends) provides insight into how human activity influences underground air quality. All indoor PM fractions and nearly all investigated gases showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.001) between these two periods, confirming the impact of visitors and staff presence on the aerosol environment. During business hours, concentrations of all PM fractions in the underground chamber increased markedly compared to off-hours. The magnitude of this increase was strongly size-dependent, with the coarsest fractions showing the greatest relative change: PM10 increased from 14 to 63 µg/m3 (4.5-fold increase), PM4 from 9.1 to 34 µg/m3 (3.7-fold), PM2.5 from 5.3 to 15 µg/m3 (2.8-fold), while PM1 showed the smallest difference (3.1–4.4 µg/m3, 1.4-fold). Such size-dependent patterns are characteristic of mechanical resuspension of settled dust by foot traffic and air movement, rather than primary emission from a localised source (Bralewska et al., 2022). The predominance of coarse particles (PM10–PM4) further supports the interpretation that visitor activity mobilises previously deposited particles from chamber surfaces and pathways. In contrast, outdoor PM concentrations remained relatively stable across the same time periods (PM10: 13–14 µg/m3; PM2.5: 8.1 µg/m3; PM1: 6.5–6.7 µg/m3), with only minor statistically significant differences (Table 1). This temporal stability outdoors confirms that diurnal changes in ambient air quality do not drive the observed indoor variability; rather, fluctuations in occupancy and activity within the chamber do. VOC concentrations in the underground environment remained extremely low regardless of occupancy, with mean values of 0.64 µg/m3 during business hours and 0.33 µg/m3 during off-hours—both substantially lower than outdoor levels (~ 20 µg/m3). The corresponding I/O ratios (0.032 and 0.017, respectively) reflect the near absence of typical VOC emission sources in the salt chamber, such as synthetic materials, cleaning agents, or combustion processes. This characteristic is consistent with previous reports on speleotherapeutic environments (Rogula-Kozłowska et al., 2016) and is often cited as a factor potentially contributing to improved respiratory function in patients. Outdoor VOC concentrations, by contrast, are primarily attributable to vehicular emissions and secondary atmospheric formation (In’T Veld et al., 2024).

CO concentrations exhibited an inverse pattern compared to outdoor air, with I/O ratios consistently above 2 (2.33 during business hours; 1.93 during off-hours). However, absolute CO levels remained low and stable (~ 2.7–2.8 µg/m3 indoors vs ~ 1.2–1.4 µg/m3 outdoors), indicating the absence of active combustion sources within the chamber. The elevated I/O ratios are more likely attributable to restricted air exchange and gradual accumulation of CO from natural background processes or minor infiltration from outdoor air, rather than to local emissions. Importantly, no statistically significant difference in indoor CO was observed between business and off-hours (p < 0.001 for temporal difference, but absolute change was minimal), suggesting that human activity does not introduce additional CO sources into the chamber.

NO2 concentrations in the underground chamber remained lower than outdoors in both time periods, yielding I/O ratios below 1 (0.42 during business hours; 0.58 during off-hours). Indoor NO2 levels remained constant at approximately 38 µg/m3, while outdoor concentrations varied significantly between business hours (91 µg/m3) and off-hours (65 µg/m3, p < 0.001), reflecting reduced vehicular traffic during nights and weekends. The absence of an increase in indoor NO2 during visitor hours confirms that the underground environment is largely insulated from traffic-related gaseous pollution. The observed indoor NO2 is likely attributable to slow infiltration of outdoor air through ventilation shafts, as previously suggested by (Salmon et al., 1996).

The I/O ratios of 1-h average PM concentrations are presented in Fig. 3. While the median I/O ratios remain relatively constant across particle fractions, the mean values increase with increasing particle size. For PM1, both the median and mean I/O ratios remained below 1.0, suggesting that indoor sources of fine particles were limited, and that outdoor air quality therefore directly influenced indoor PM1 concentrations. As particle diameter increased, a second peak in the I/O ratio distribution emerged, with a local maximum for PMtot around I/O = 7. The hourly analysis of I/O ratios in Fig. 4 indicates that elevated I/O values occurred primarily during business hours, whereas values below 1.0 occurred primarily during off-hours. This diurnal variability suggests that the observed pattern was associated not only with periodic indoor activities in the treatment chamber, including human movement and resuspension processes, but also with temporal changes in outdoor concentrations affecting the denominator of the I/O ratio. Concurrently, as the aerodynamic diameter increased, a systematic increase in average I/O values was observed, from 0.624 for PM1 to 2.91 for PMtot. The substantial differences between median and mean values, particularly for PM4, PM10, and PMtot, indicate strongly right-skewed distributions and sporadic but intense episodes of elevated indoor concentrations attributable to human presence and activity. These findings suggest that coarse particles are more susceptible to accumulation under underground conditions characterised by limited air exchange, stable microclimatic conditions, and reduced flow dynamics. Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas with a higher density than that of air, which accumulates in lower-level spaces. Ventilation plays a pivotal role in the elimination of carbon monoxide. The results of statistical comparisons between business and off-hours indicate that concentrations are higher in both periods. Consequently, both the average I/O and the median exceed 2, as illustrated in Fig. 6. However, the maximum concentrations of CO in the indoor air (i.e., 3.3 mg/m3 from Fig. 2) did not exceed the permissible standards for either outdoor or indoor air. The distribution's analysis reveals a marked asymmetry, characterised by a protracted right tail that corresponds to incidental, elevated values of the I/O index, reaching several units. This distribution pattern may suggest periodic accumulation of carbon monoxide in the facility, potentially related to activities within the health resort or to limited air-exchange efficiency during certain periods (Baldermann et al., 2024; Gren et al., 2022; Menéndez et al. 2022). Analysis of temporal variability showed that periods of increased I/O ratios coincided not only with slightly elevated indoor concentrations but also with simultaneous decreases in outdoor CO concentrations. Since the indoor concentration (numerator of the I/O ratio) remained relatively stable (coefficient of variation CV = 1.4%, Fig. 2), the outdoor concentration (denominator) is responsible for the changes in the I/O values. As the denominator decreased, the resulting I/O values increased substantially. This decrease in daytime outdoor CO concentrations may be associated with photochemical processes, atmospheric mixing conditions, or other meteorological factors that influence urban background concentrations (Ehhalt, 1999; Harrison, 2018). Furthermore, nitrogen dioxide concentrations inside the mine were found to be lower than outside, with most measurements indicating they were about 60% of outside levels (Fig. 7). The distribution of ratios was symmetric, with the mean nearly equal to the median (not visible in Fig. 7 due to the logarithmic x-axis). Considering the mine's surroundings, nitrogen dioxide air pollution is likely related to road transport emissions—the relatively low concentrations of this compound in the mine result in I/O ratios below 1. Previous studies on the relationship between indoor and outdoor NO2 concentrations have shown that, in buildings without significant indoor emission sources, indoor concentrations are primarily determined by outdoor air infiltration and ventilation parameters. The I/O ratio for NO2 close to 1 is characteristic of buildings with an effective ventilation system and no indoor emissions. This is determined by several factors, including rapid air exchange and the compound's physicochemical properties (Banasiewicz & Janicka, 2025; Salonen et al., 2019). The observed diurnal variability of the I/O ratio appears to be driven primarily by fluctuations in outdoor concentrations rather than by changes in indoor NO2 levels. During daytime hours, decreases in I/O ratios coincided with increases in outdoor NO2 concentrations, likely associated with intensified traffic activity and urban emission patterns (Carslaw, 2005; Harrison, 2018). Since indoor concentrations remained relatively stable (coefficient of variation CV = 0.1%, Fig. 2), while outdoor concentrations (the denominator of the I/O ratio) increased during these periods, the resulting I/O values decreased. These findings indicate that the underground environment effectively limits NO2 penetration into the facility interior. At the same time, temporal variability in the I/O ratio is influenced by external urban emission dynamics and ventilation-related transport processes (McManus & Haddad, 2019).
