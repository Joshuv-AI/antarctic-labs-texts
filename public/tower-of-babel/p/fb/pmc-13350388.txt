Fatal Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis After Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Replacement
Authors: Samuel Rustem Panday, Mehdi Eskandari, Alexandros Papachristidis, Rupert Simpson, Tsz Ho Chan, Philip MacCarthy, Mary N Sheppard, Jonathan Byrne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) is an emerging therapy for severe tricuspid regurgitation with an evolving complication profile. Fatal prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) following orthotopic TTVR appears exceptionally rare, with detailed pathologic description through specialist cardiovascular autopsy remaining limited.Case SummaryA 65-year-old man with severe functional tricuspid regurgitation and persistent atrial fibrillation underwent TTVR. The following day, he developed complete heart block requiring pacemaker implantation. He remained well at 1 and 3 months, with improving symptoms and unremarkable bloods. At 6 months, he developed nonspecific viral-like symptoms over 48 hours culminating in death at home. Routine postmortem examination attributed death to coronary artery disease. Specialist cardiovascular pathology demonstrated PVE with large vegetations, atrioventricular nodal obliteration, left atrial communication enabling systemic septic embolization, diffuse coronary microembolization, and widespread left ventricular microabscesses.DiscussionFatal PVE after TTVR may present with nonspecific prodromal symptoms. History of Presentation

A 65-year-old man with severe functional tricuspid regurgitation was referred for transcatheter intervention. He described progressive lethargy and exertional breathlessness. He was NYHA functional class II-III with grade 2 peripheral edema. Clinical Frailty Scale was 4. Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Overall and Clinical Summary Scores were 60 and 70.

Initial transesophageal echocardiography had demonstrated anatomy borderline for transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, with combined atrial and ventricular components limiting feasibility. Despite ongoing guideline-directed medical therapy, his symptoms progressed and repeat imaging demonstrated widening of the coaptation gap to 10 mm, increased leaflet tethering, and worsening right ventricular dilatation and dysfunction. He was discussed at the heart team meeting and deemed unsuitable for transcatheter edge-to-edge repair and high risk for surgery (TRI-SCORE 5); transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) was preferred. He was enrolled in the TARGET study, a prospective, multicentre, single-arm study evaluating the safety and performance of the Cardiovalve TR replacement system.1

Past Medical History

Past medical history included permanent atrial fibrillation, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease stage 3b-4, and iron-deficiency anemia. A previous episode of left ventricular systolic impairment attributed to alcohol excess and atrial arrhythmia had recovered on guideline-directed therapy, with cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging showing no scar.

Preprocedural cardiac computed tomography demonstrated heavily calcified but nonobstructive (25% stenosis) triple-vessel coronary artery disease, incidental hepatic congestion with cirrhosis, and small-volume ascites. Maintenance medications comprised sacubitril-valsartan 49/51 mg twice daily, spironolactone 25 mg, dapagliflozin 10 mg, furosemide 80 mg and 40 mg daily, and rivaroxaban 15 mg. There was no history of prior cardiac surgery, endocarditis, intravenous drug use, indwelling central venous catheters, or recent dental procedures.

Physical Examination

Heart rate was irregularly irregular at 55 to 65 beats/min with a pansystolic murmur at the left lower sternal edge. The jugular venous pressure was elevated with giant c-v waves, and there was grade 2 peripheral edema. There were no stigmata of infective endocarditis and no peripheral intravenous or central venous access.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a moderately dilated left ventricle with mildly impaired systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction 45% to 50% by core laboratory), severely dilated right ventricle with reduced systolic function (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion 1.2 cm, fractional area change [FAC] 22%, right ventricular S’ 8.3 cm/s), biatrial dilatation, and a dilated tricuspid annulus (4.6 cm) with torrential central tricuspid regurgitation and a 10-mm coaptation gap on transesophageal imaging (Figure 1A).

Preprocedural cardiac computed tomography demonstrated a tricuspid annulus of 45 × 52 mm with an annular area of 18 cm2 and perimeter 151 mm, approved by the core laboratory screening committee for an X-Large Cardiovalve device. Right heart catheterization during initial workup demonstrated elevated right atrial pressure (mean 18 mm Hg, v-wave 21), pulmonary artery pressure 46/18/29 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 17 mm Hg, cardiac output 4.2 L/min with reduced cardiac index 1.86 L/min/m2 (Fick), and pulmonary vascular resistance 3.3 Wood units.

Formal risk stratification using the TRI-SCORE2 yielded 5 points: right heart failure signs (2 points), left ventricular ejection fraction <60% (1 point), and moderate-to-severe right ventricular dysfunction (2 points).

Preprocedural laboratory assessment showed hemoglobin 103 g/L, creatinine 183 μmol/L, bilirubin 14 μmol/L, alanine aminotransferase 144 U/L, and normal inflammatory markers.

Management

The patient underwent structured preprocedural optimization comprising intravenous diuresis, iron repletion, and glycemic control. The 10-mm coaptation gap was an anatomic finding not amenable to preload reduction.

The procedure was performed via right femoral venous access under transesophageal echocardiographic and fluoroscopic guidance (Figures 1C and 1D). The Cardiovalve prosthesis was successfully deployed with no paravalvular leak and mild residual transvalvular regurgitation.

The following day the patient developed complete heart block, a recognized complication of TTVR, and a permanent pacemaker was implanted the same day.3 Predischarge echocardiography demonstrated a well-seated prosthesis with trace residual regurgitation and a mean gradient of 4 mm Hg (Figure 1B). Anticoagulation was transitioned from rivaroxaban to warfarin per study protocol, and he was discharged on day 6.

Follow-Up

At 1-month review, the patient reported marked symptomatic improvement and had returned to work. NYHA functional class improved to II and Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire scores improved (Overall Summary 60-71; Clinical Summary 70-84). Right ventricular FAC improved to 28% with preserved free-wall longitudinal strain (−13.1%); tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion declined to 1.0 cm, a pattern recognized after transcatheter tricuspid interventions reflecting altered longitudinal annular motion rather than true contractile deterioration. Left ventricular systolic function was similar to baseline.

At 3-month review, he reported continued improvement. There was no history of recent dental procedures, skin injury, or other potential source of bacteremia. Inflammatory markers including C-reactive protein and white cell count were normal.

Approximately 48 hours before his death, the patient developed nonspecific viral-like symptoms, including fatigue and malaise. He did not seek medical advice. The following morning, he told his daughter that he still felt unwell, but again did not seek medical attention. At approximately 07:00 the next day, he again contacted his daughter to report persistent symptoms and called in sick to work, which he had not previously done during his recovery. He denied fever, chest pain, or breathlessness. A few hours later, he failed to answer further calls, and his daughter found him deceased at home at approximately 11:00. The total symptomatic interval was approximately 48 hours.

Differential Diagnosis

A general practitioner certified the death as congestive heart failure. The implanting team challenged this given the preceding clinical stability, and a coroner's postmortem was requested.

The initial postmortem identified coronary artery disease as the presumptive cause of death. Although atherosclerotic disease was present, there was no evidence of acute plaque rupture, intraluminal thrombus, or regional myocardial infarction, and the heart was referred to a specialist cardiovascular pathology unit.

Specialist assessment demonstrated florid prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Macroscopic inspection revealed large friable vegetations along the leaflet free edges and coaptation surfaces (Figure 2A). Histological sections demonstrated numerous discrete microabscesses throughout the left ventricle, with central suppurative necrosis, dense neutrophilic infiltration, and bacterial colonies (Figure 2B). Infection extended from the annular interface into the atrioventricular conduction region, causing extensive destruction of the atrioventricular node. The process tracked through this region to establish communication with the left atrium, providing a conduit for systemic septic embolization including diffuse coronary microembolization. Pacemaker interrogation demonstrated no malignant arrhythmia, making a primary arrhythmic mechanism unlikely.

Discussion

TTVR is an emerging therapy for severe tricuspid regurgitation. The EVOQUE system (Edwards Lifesciences) is approved for clinical use; other orthotopic devices remain under evaluation.1,4, 5, 6

To our knowledge, fatal PVE following orthotopic TTVR has not been previously documented in detail; device-related infection has been described after transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, which represents a fundamentally different intervention and substrate.7

Atrioventricular conduction disturbance is a recognized feature of invasive aortic valve endocarditis, reflecting spread into periannular tissue.8,9 Here, prosthetic infection extended into the atrioventricular conduction region with destruction of the atrioventricular node and established communication with the left atrium, enabling systemic embolization. Diffuse coronary microembolization with widespread left ventricular microabscess formation likely explains the rapid fatal course.

The clinical course also warrants consideration. No indwelling vascular access was present, and no dental work, invasive procedure, or skin breach was identified, although no source is identified in a substantial minority of cases. The patient had chronic multisystem dysfunction in the context of severe tricuspid regurgitation, including congestive hepatopathy, anemia, and chronic kidney disease, a profile that may have impaired host defenses. Normal inflammatory markers at the 3-month review and the rapid terminal course argue against early postoperative contamination and suggest seeding occurred in the months following. His terminal illness evolved over approximately 48 hours with progressive nonspecific malaise; fever was not documented as he was not clinically assessed during this period. The clinical course is compatible with evolving bacteremia culminating in fulminant septic cardiogenic shock once the left-sided circulation was seeded. No source of bacteremia was identified.

Routine death certification and postmortem may be insufficient after recently implanted transcatheter devices. Death was initially attributed to heart failure, then to coronary artery disease; only specialist cardiovascular pathology established the true mechanism.10 This aligns with recommendations that unexplained sudden death after cardiac device implantation should prompt specialist cardiac autopsy.11,12

Whether orthotopic TTVR confers distinctive susceptibility to PVE remains speculative. The pulmonary circulation functions as a vascular filter for infected embolic material and contemporary TTVR systems incorporate substantial prosthetic frames where biofilm formation can facilitate bacterial persistence.13, 14, 15 A single case cannot establish whether such factors translate into clinically meaningful risk. Limitations include absence of blood cultures and inability to identify a causative organism following tissue fixation.

Conclusions

We report a case of fatal PVE following orthotopic TTVR. Death was consistent with fulminant sepsis, with diffuse coronary septic microembolization causing widespread myocardial microabscesses and acute hemodynamic collapse. The diagnosis was established only through specialist cardiovascular pathology, underscoring the need for vigilance for endocarditis after transcatheter valve intervention and the value of specialist cardiac autopsy in unexplained death.
