Rational attenuation of duck plague virus by targeting the ICP4 DNA-binding domain generates a safe and efficacious live vaccine candidate
Authors: Ying Wu, Yumei He, Chenjia Wang, Ruofei An, JiaMing Yang, Shu Chen, Xiaotong Zhou, Haiyan Yu, Luyao Yang, Mingshu Wang, Shun Chen, Renyong Jia, Mafeng Liu, Qiao Yang, Bin Tian, Xumin Ou, Juan Huang, Di Sun, Dekang Zhu, XinXin Zhao, Shaqiu Zhang, Yu He, Zhen Wu, Anchun Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Duck plague (DP) is an acute, highly lethal disease of waterfowl caused by duck plague virus (DPV). Viral latency and reactivation pose persistent threats, and current vaccines lack DIVA capability while risking insufficient immunogenicity or reversion to virulence. We targeted the DPV ICP4 DNA-binding domain (DBD) for site-specific mutagenesis and systematically evaluated the mutants in vitro and in vivo. Structural modeling and sequence alignment guided alanine substitution at five conserved α-helical residues (L694, Q711, S718, L719, Y723). Dual-luciferase assays showed Y723A and L719A most strongly suppressed viral promoter activity; Q711A and S718A had moderate effects. Five recombinant mutants were generated via Red recombination; L694A, Q711A, and S718A remained stable over 10 passages. Q711A displayed the greatest attenuation, with markedly reduced viral gene expression, RNA polymerase II occupancy, and replication compartment formation. In ducklings, Q711A and S718A were highly attenuated, achieving 100% survival with minimal clinical signs at high-dose inoculation. Both elicited neutralizing antibody titers comparable to a live vaccine and conferred complete protection against 100 LD₅₀ DPV, with reduced organ viral loads and pathology. L694A showed insufficient attenuation. Thus, the ICP4 DBD is critical for DPV transcription and replication, and Q711A combines robust attenuation, genetic stability, and full immunogenicity as a promising next-generation DIVA vaccine candidate.

Introduction

Duck plague (DP) is an acute and highly lethal infectious disease of waterfowl caused by duck plague virus (DPV) (Wu and Wilcox, 1990; Dhama et al., 2017; Tian, et al., 2026). Since its first identification in the Netherlands in 1923 and subsequent report in Guangdong Province, China in 1957, DP has become widely distributed across many regions, causing substantial economic losses due to its high transmissibility and pathogenicity(Wu, et al., 2012; Dhama, et al., 2017; Gao, et al., 2022). DPV spreads through both horizontal and vertical transmission, with domestic ducks and wild waterfowl capable of bidirectional virus exchange. Persistent viral shedding in feces and oral secretions contributes to environmental contamination, facilitating viral spread via water and aerosols (Dhama, et al., 2017; Gao, et al., 2022).

Currently, no specific antiviral therapy exists for DP, and prevention relies primarily on vaccination. Commercial vaccines include attenuated and inactivated formulations(Dhama, et al., 2017). Attenuated vaccines, derived from serial passage in chicken embryos, induce protective immunity within 7–10 days and confer protection for over six months. Inactivated vaccines, produced by physical or chemical viral inactivation, exhibit lower immunogenicity. However, neither vaccine allows differentiation of infected from vaccinated animals (DIVA), which complicates disease control programs. Moreover, most attenuated vaccines are empirically derived with undefined genetic backgrounds, posing a risk of virulence reversion, while inactivated vaccines have a limited protective duration(Dhama, et al., 2017). Consequently, the development of novel vaccines, including recombinant, DNA, and genetically engineered marker vaccines, has become a research priority. Progress has been made with gene-targeted DPV vaccines, such as gC/gE double-gene deletion strains (Ruan, et al., 2022), ICP27 single-gene deletion mutants(Wu, et al., 2023), and ICP8 single-gene mutants(Wu, et al., 2026), which demonstrate improved safety and protective efficacy in vivo. Nonetheless, safe, effective, and DIVA-compatible DPV vaccines remain urgently needed.

DPV, an Alphaherpesvirinae member, is an enveloped spherical DNA virus (120–180 nm) comprising core, capsid, tegument, and envelope(Yuan, et al., 2005). Its 158–162 kb genome contains a unique long (UL) and short (US) region flanked by inverted repeats (IR and TR), encoding 78 open reading frames(Chen, et al., 2013). Viral gene expression follows a temporal cascade of immediate-early (IE), early (E), and late (L) phases. IE genes, including ICP4, activate E and L gene expression; E genes facilitate viral DNA replication, and L genes encode structural components essential for progeny production(Whitley and Roizman, 2001; Liu, et al., 2015).

ICP4, an immediate-early gene within the internal repeat sequences, functions as a transcriptional regulator, activating early and late genes while autorepressing itself(DeLuca and Schaffer, 1985, 1987; Gu, et al., 1993). Its DNA-binding domain (DBD) mediates specific DNA interaction and recruits RNA polymerase II (RNAPII), coordinating the formation of the preinitiation complex with host transcription factors such as TFIID, TFIIB, and Mediator(Smith, et al., 1993; Zabierowski and Deluca, 2008; Sampath and Deluca, 2008; Lester and DeLuca, 2011; Wagner and DeLuca, 2013). Homologs of ICP4 in other herpesviruses, including EBV Zta/Rta, HCMV IE1/IE2, and VZV IE62, are also essential for viral replication(Felser, et al., 1988; Disney and Everett, 1990; Xu, et al., 2001; Castillo and Kowalik, 2002; Zhang, et al., 2003; Kenney and Mertz, 2014). ICP4’s DBD not only regulates transcription but also contributes to the formation of viral replication compartments (VRCs) via liquid-liquid phase separation, a process crucial for efficient viral replication(Hidalgo and Gonzalez, 2019; Charman and Weitzman, 2020; Seyffert, et al., 2021).

Although ICP4’s structural and functional roles are well-characterized in other Alphaherpesvirinae, its precise mechanisms in DPV and potential as a vaccine target remain unclear. We hypothesize that targeted site-directed mutagenesis of the ICP4 DBD can attenuate the virus and provide a genetically stable marker suitable for DIVA strategies.

In this study, we systematically screened key ICP4 DBD residues via sequence alignment and structural prediction, followed by alanine substitution mutagenesis to evaluate their effects on transcriptional activity using dual-luciferase reporter assays. Recombinant and revertant DPV mutants were constructed via Red recombination and characterized in vitro for replication, gene expression, RNAPII recruitment (ChIP-qPCR), and VRC formation (immunofluorescence). Finally, in vivo studies in ducklings assessed pathogenicity, immunogenicity, and protective efficacy. This work aimed to identify genetically stable, strongly attenuated DPV candidates with DIVA potential, and to elucidate the transcriptional mechanisms underlying their attenuation.

Materials and methods

Virus and cell culture

Primary duck embryo fibroblasts (DEFs) were obtained from nine-day-old duck embryos provided by Chengdu Grimaud Breeding Company.The culture conditions consisted of DMEM containing 10% newborn bovine serum (NBS, Gibco), incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ in a humidified chamber. The wild-type virus used was the duck plague virus (DPV) strain CHv (GenBank: JQ647509.1). To prepare viral stocks, DEFs were infected, and virus titers were calculated as TCID₅₀ following the Reed–Muench. The same culture system was used to generate and expand ICP4 mutants.

Antibodies and reagents

The following primary antibodies were used: anti-FLAG (ABmart, M20008M; Proteintech, 66008-4-Ig), anti-TBP (Proteintech, 22006-1-AP), anti-Rpb1 CTD (4H8) (Cell Signaling Technology, 2629s), anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 66031-1-Ig), anti-β-actin (ABmart, AC026), rabbit control IgG (ABclonal, AC005), and in-house polyclonal antibodies against ICP4, ICP8, ICP22, VP16, and UL47. The following secondary antibodies were used: goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) conjugated to HRP (Proteintech, SA00001-2), goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) conjugated to HRP (Proteintech, SA00001-1), goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A11008), and goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) labeled with Alexa Fluor 568 (Invitrogen, A11004).

Other reagents and their sources were as follows: protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, HY-K02), an ultrasonic chromatin immunoprecipitation kit (ABclonal, RK20258), a viral DNA extraction kit (Magen, 3191), ClonExpress II One-Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C112-01), Clarity Western ECL luminescent substrate (YEASEN, 36208ES76), Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (TransGen, TR202-02), RNA-easy extraction reagent (Vazyme, R701-01), and qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (LABLEAD, R0202-02).

Structural modeling

Sequence data for duck plague virus (DPV) ICP4 and related homologous proteins were obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Sequences were aligned using DNAMAN software (version 8.0; Lynnon Biosoft). The SWISS-MODEL platform (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) was used to build homology-based three-dimensional structural models. PyMOL (version 3.0.3; Schrödinger, LLC) was used to generate all molecular graphics.

Plasmid construction

The ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C112-01), site-directed mutagenesis was performed to introduce specific mutations within the ICP4 target region. Dual-luciferase reporter assay

BHK-21 cells were transfected with three plasmids: an expression construct for wild-type ICP4 or a DBD mutant, a firefly luciferase reporter driven by a viral promoter, and a Renilla luciferase internal standard. After a 48-hour incubation, luciferase signals were read using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910). Transactivation activity was calculated as the firefly to Renilla luminescence ratio.

Generation of ICP4 mutants and in vitro stability assay

Using the GS1783-pBAC—CHv-ΔminiF-ΔICP4 bacterial strain (Wu, et al., 2023). we generated multiple recombinant DPV strains carrying mutations in ICP4. Positive clones were identified by PCR and followed by sequencing. After plasmid extraction, the recombinant plasmids were introduced into DEF cells to rescue recombinant viruses. Due to the repetitive nature of the mini-F cassette, it is spontaneously lost during serial passaging. The revertant virus was recovered following the same protocol. To evaluate the genetic stability of the ICP4 mutant, DEF cells underwent ten rounds of serial passage. Viral DNA was isolated every five passages, and the intended mutation was confirmed by PCR and sequencing (Shanghai Sangon Biotech).

Virus growth kinetics

To examine replication kinetics, DEF cells were exposed to either wild-type or ICP4 mutant DPV at an MOI of 0.01. At 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, both culture supernatants and cell lysates were harvested for subsequent measurement of viral titers and genome copy numbers. Viral titers were determined by the TCID50 assay following the Reed–Muench method (Thakur and Fezio, 1981). Genomic copy numbers of both wild-type and mutant strains were quantified via a TaqMan qPCR targeting the UL30 gene (Guo, et al., 2009), using a standard curve defined as y = −3.3715x + 43.233.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay

Immunofluorescence microscopy was used to examine viral replication compartment (VRC) formation in DEF cells following infection. Cells cultured on coverslips placed in 24-well plates were infected with either wild-type or ICP4 mutant DPV at an MOI of 1. At 24 h, cells were fixed overnight at 4°C using 4% paraformaldehyde, rinsed with PBST, and then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100. Subsequently, cells were incubated first with an anti-ICP4 antibody, followed by a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody, and finally counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured with a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

DEF cells were inoculated with wild-type DPV CHv or an ICP4-deficient mutant (MOI = 0.01) to assess viral transcriptional kinetics over time. RNA was harvested at 24 and 36 hpi using RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, R701-01), followed by first-strand cDNA synthesis with the SuperScript III system (Invitrogen, 18080-051). qPCR was employed to quantify the transcripts of six viral genes (ICP4, ICP22, ICP8, UL30, UL47, and UL19), with relative expression calculated by the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Data were normalized to 18S rRNA as an internal reference.

Western blotting

DEFs grown in 24-well plates were exposed to wild-type or mutant DPV at an MOI of 1. After 48 h, both infected and mock-infected cells were collected and lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein extracts were separated by 8% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Following blockade with 5% non-fat milk in PBST, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies recognizing ICP22, ICP8, ICP4, UL47, or β-actin. After a 1-hour incubation at 37°C with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, signals were revealed with Clarity Western ECL substrate (YEASEN, 36208ES76). Densitometric analysis was carried out using ImageJ.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR (ChIP-qPCR)

RNAPII promoter occupancy during infection with wild-type or ICP4 DBD mutant DPV was assessed using ChIP with an ultrasonic kit (Abclonal, RK20258). DEF cells (10⁷) were infected at MOI = 2 and harvested at 12 hpi. After 15 min of crosslinking with 1% formaldehyde on ice, chromatin was sonicated to <1 kb (confirmed by gel electrophoresis). Input (5%) was reserved, and the rest was incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-RNAPII or control IgG. Complexes were captured with protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, HY-K02). Following decrosslinking and DNA purification, viral promoters were amplified by qPCR with specific primers. Enrichment was normalized to input and expressed as fold change over control IgG.

In vivo virulence assessment

Fourteen-day-old Peking ducks (n = 14 per group) received intramuscular injections of 1 mL MEM containing either 10⁵ or 10⁶ TCID₅₀ of wild-type DPV CHv or three DBD mutants (L694A, Q711A, S718A). Control birds were given MEM only. Mortality, body weight changes, and rectal temperatures (measured every 48 hours) were monitored on a daily basis. At three days post-infection, four ducks from each group were sacrificed, and their liver, spleen, and thymus were harvested for viral load measurement and histopathological examination. Organ indices were derived as the ratio of organ weight to body weight. Tissue samples were homogenized in PBS, followed by DNA extraction using DNAiso (Takara, 9770A). Viral copy numbers per milligram of tissue were quantified via UL30-targeted qPCR. For shedding evaluation, cloacal swabs taken at 3 and 5 days post-infection were processed using a viral DNA extraction kit (Magen, 3191).

In vivo immunogenicity assessment

Four groups (n = 5) of twenty 14-day-old ducks received 10⁶ TCID₅₀ of either the Q711A or S718A ICP4-DBD mutant, or one of two vaccine doses; controls received MEM alone. Neutralizing antibody titers were determined from serum (2 mL/duck) sampled weekly from the jugular vein during weeks 1–6 post-immunization.

Challenge protection assay

A total of forty 14-day-old ducks were divided into three vaccinated groups (n = 10 each) and one unvaccinated control group (n = 10). Birds in the vaccinated groups received 10⁶ TCID₅₀ of either the Q711A mutant, the S718A mutant, or a commercial vaccine. Fourteen days later, all ducks were challenged with 100 LD₅₀ of virulent DPV CHv. Following challenge, body weight, rectal temperature, and mortality were monitored. Cloacal swabs were collected on days 3, 5, 7, and 10 to assess viral shedding. Ducks that died were subjected to necropsy; surviving ducks were euthanized on day 10 for tissue collection. Tissue samples were processed for histopathological analysis using H&E staining (Wuhan Servicebio). Viral shedding and clearance were evaluated according to the procedures described above.

Virus neutralization assay

Serum samples were collected weekly over a period of 6 weeks from ducks vaccinated with ICP4 mutants or a commercial vaccine. Blood was kept at 37°C for 2 hours and then placed at 4°C overnight to allow serum separation. After heat inactivation at 56°C for 1 hour, sera were serially diluted two-fold (ranging from 1:2 to 1:256). Each dilution (450 μL) was combined with an equal volume of DPV CHv (200 TCID₅₀), incubated at 37°C for 2 hours, and then added to DEF monolayers in 96-well plates. Controls included virus alone (100 TCID₅₀) and a low-dose virus control (0.1 TCID₅₀). Following incubation at 37°C for 7–10 days, cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed under a microscope, and the 50% neutralization titer was calculated using the Reed–Muench method based on the severity of CPE.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to analyze data from ≥3 biological replicates, with results shown as mean ± SD. Group differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. Significance levels are denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Rational design and in vitro validation of ICP4 DNA-binding domain (DBD) mutants

Sequence alignment of DPV ICP4 with other alphaherpesviruses using DNAMAN revealed that the DBD is highly conserved (Fig. 1A). Tertiary structure prediction via Swiss-Model indicated that the DPV ICP4 DBD adopts a typical helix-turn-helix (HTH) fold (Fig. 1B). The recombinant DBD protein was expressed in a prokaryotic system using the pET-28a-His-DBD plasmid and subsequently purified. To assess DNA-binding capacity, a 20 μL incubation mixture of DBD protein and double-stranded DNA (dsDNA, corresponding to the ICP4 promoter sequence) was prepared and analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. As shown in Fig. 1C, incubation with the DBD protein caused a significant retardation in dsDNA migration, confirming the predicted dsDNA-binding activity of the DPV ICP4 DBD.

Eukaryotic expression plasmids carrying individual ICP4 DBD mutations were constructed (Fig. 1D). Western blot analysis confirmed that all mutant proteins were expressed at the expected levels, with minimal variation among them (Fig. 1E). To evaluate the functional impact of these mutations on viral promoter activity, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. As shown in Fig. 1F, deletion of the entire DBD region significantly reduced promoter activity, confirming its essential role in transcriptional activation. Among the point mutants, Y723A and L719A exerted the strongest inhibitory effect on promoter activity, whereas Q711A and S718A exhibited moderate repression. The L694A mutation had the least impact. Notably, ICP4 is known to autorepress its own transcription; deletion of the DBD partially relieved this repression, resulting in a modest recovery of ICP4 promoter activity.

Generation and in vitro characterization of recombinant ICP4 DBD mutant viruses

To investigate the effects of key ICP4 DBD mutations on DPV, five corresponding mutant viruses were constructed using our previously established DPV reverse genetics system. Among these, three mutants (ICP4-L694A, ICP4-Q711A, and ICP4-S718A) and their respective revertant viruses were successfully rescued (Fig. S1A-B). The three mutant viruses (ICP4-L694A, ICP4-Q711A, ICP4-S718A) were serially propagated in duck embryo fibroblasts (DEFs), and viral DNA was extracted from the culture supernatants at passages 1, 5, and 10. Sequencing analysis confirmed that all introduced mutations were retained across passages, demonstrating the genetic stability of these mutants in vitro (Fig. S2).

To assess the impact of ICP4 DBD mutations on viral replication, we evaluated viral growth kinetics, gene expression, and viral replication compartment (VRC) formation. Multi-step growth curves revealed that the Q711A mutant exhibited the most pronounced replication defect, with significantly lower viral titers than the wild-type (WT) virus at 24 and 48 h post-infection (h.p.i). The S718A mutant also displayed reduced replication at 24 h.p.i., while the L694A mutant showed minimal differences compared with WT (Fig. 2A). Discussion

The ICP4 DBD Functional domain as a core regulator of viral gene expression

ICP4 is a critical transcriptional regulator in alphaherpesviruses, orchestrating the temporal expression of viral genes. In HSV-1, ICP4 comprises four functional domains: the N-terminal transactivation domain (NTA), DNA-binding domain (DBD), nuclear localization signal (NLS), and C-terminal transactivation domain (CTA)(DeLuca, et al., 1985; Paterson and Everett, 1988; Tunnicliffe, et al., 2017). Among these, the DBD is highly conserved across alphaherpesviruses, whereas other domains exhibit lower conservation. The DBD adopts a canonical helix-turn-helix motif capable of specifically recognizing conserved RTCGTCNNYNYSG sequences in viral genomes, a binding mode highly conserved across the genus (Wu and Wilcox, 1990; Pizer, et al., 1991; Tunnicliffe, et al., 2017). Previous studies demonstrated that DPV ICP4 exhibits a similar domain architecture to HSV-1 ICP4, repressing its own transcription while activating early and late viral genes (Yang, et al., 2026).

Our sequence alignment and structural modeling analyses confirmed that the DPV ICP4 DBD is highly conserved and retains a helix-turn-helix structure analogous to HSV-1, suggesting a similar mechanistic role in transcription regulation. In HSV-1, the DBD mediates transcriptional repression by specifically binding conserved sequences, recruiting TFIID and forming the tripartite ICP4 complex (TPC), whereas in transcriptional activation, the DBD engages non-conserved promoter sequences to facilitate RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) recruitment (Smith, et al., 1993; Leopardi, et al., 1995; Lester and DeLuca, 2011; Wagner and DeLuca, 2013). Consistently, in DPV, deletion of the DBD or alanine substitutions at five conserved residues (L694, S718, Q711, L719, Y723) significantly impaired promoter activity, with inhibition ranked as Y723A > L719A > Q711A > S718A > L694A. ChIP assays further confirmed that Q711A disrupts RNAPII recruitment, indicating a conserved role of the ICP4 DBD in transcription activation across alphaherpes viruses.

Furthermore, viral replication compartments (VRCs), the subnuclear foci where viral transcription and DNA replication occur, require coordinated assembly of viral proteins and regulatory factors (Wang, et al., 2013; Schmid, et al., 2014; Hidalgo and Gonzalez, 2019; Charman and Weitzman, 2020). In HSV-1, ICP4 is a key VRC component, and DBD deletion severely impairs VRC formation (Seyffert, et al., 2021). Our study demonstrated that Q711A and S718A mutations reduce viral promoter activity and VRC formation, likely by limiting the availability of viral transcription and structural proteins necessary for compartment assembly. This interplay suggests that the DBD integrates transcriptional regulation with VRC assembly efficiency to orchestrate viral gene expression.

Collectively, the DPV ICP4 DBD serves as a central hub regulating viral transcription via its conserved structure, promoter activity modulation, RNAPII recruitment, and VRC formation, highlighting its essential role in viral gene expression.

Molecular basis of replication defects in mutant viruses

The Q711A and S718A mutants exhibited marked replication defects in vitro. Both viral titers and genome copy numbers were significantly reduced compared to wild-type CHv, with Q711A showing the most severe impairment. Mechanistically, these defects are attributable to disrupted transcriptional regulation and compromised VRC assembly.

ICP4 facilitates transcription initiation by binding viral promoters and recruiting RNAPII and other transcription factors to form the pre-initiation complex (PIC) (Lester and DeLuca, 2011; Gruffat, et al., 2016). Our results demonstrated that Q711A and S718A mutations significantly reduce promoter activity, including that of the ICP8 promoter, a key replication-associated gene. Consequently, mRNA levels of early and late genes, such as ICP8 and UL30, were markedly decreased, impeding viral DNA synthesis. EdU labeling further revealed reduced co-localization of ICP4 with nascent DNA in Q711A-infected cells, indicating impaired DNA-binding and PIC assembly, which may trigger a cascade of transcriptional and replicative inhibition.

VRC assembly is similarly affected, as ICP8 and UL30 are essential scaffolds for compartment formation, recruiting viral and host proteins to replication foci (McNamee, et al., 2000; Uprichard and Knipe, 2003; Packard and Dembowski, 2021). Suppression of ICP8 and UL30 expression by Q711A compromises VRC integrity, further perturbing the spatial and molecular environment required for efficient viral replication.

In vivo attenuation and safety assessment

In vivo, the three ICP4 DBD mutants exhibited differential pathogenicity. L694A caused notable virulence, with 70% mortality at 10⁶ TCID₅₀, whereas Q711A and S718A displayed significantly reduced pathogenicity. Q711A showed minimal viral shedding and no fever, reflecting superior safety. The attenuation of Q711A is likely mediated by the combined effects of impaired transcription and defective VRC formation, leading to substantial suppression of viral gene expression and replication. Compared to previously reported NTA mutants (Yang, et al., 2026), Q711A exhibits improved safety, with reduced fever and approximately tenfold lower viral shedding, highlighting its potential as a vaccine candidate.

The molecular stability of Q711A was validated through ten serial passages and high-throughput sequencing, demonstrating no reversion or loss of the mutation, ensuring reliable molecular marking. The Q711A mutation can serve as a DIVA (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) marker, facilitating differentiation between vaccinated and naturally infected birds, thereby supporting disease monitoring and control strategies.

Prospects and limitations

The Q711A mutant represents a promising marker vaccine candidate, combining high safety with potential for robust immunogenicity. However, several questions remain. Mechanistically, whether Q711A directly disrupts ICP4-RNAPII interactions or affects cooperation with other transcription factors (e.g., TBP, TFIIA) warrants further investigation. Additionally, the impact of Q711A on latent infection, viral persistence in neural tissues, and long-term immune durability has yet to be explored.

From an application standpoint, long-term immunogenicity, shedding dynamics, and large-scale production feasibility remain key considerations. Optimization of vaccine formulation and manufacturing protocols will be necessary to ensure cost-effective, scalable deployment without compromising stability or efficacy.

In summary, targeted mutation of the ICP4 DBD at Q711 generates a safe and highly attenuated DPV strain, CHv-ICP4-MutQ711A, capable of eliciting strong humoral immunity and complete protection against virulent challenge. The Q711A mutation provides a stable molecular marker, enabling DIVA strategies. This study provides a foundation for the development of next-generation DPV vaccines, with clear advantages in safety, immunogenicity, and molecular traceability.
