Construction of a ferroptosis-related prognostic signature and identification of SRSF9 as a key biomarker in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Jian Wang, Yixi Wei, Jia Zhang, Wenshu Chai, Yubin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFerroptosis is a distinct form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, which plays crucial roles in tumor biology and therapeutic response. However, the prognostic significance of ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to construct and validate a ferroptosis-related prognostic model for LUAD by integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from GSE127465 were integrated with bulk transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas lung adenocarcinoma cohort (TCGA-LUAD). FRGs were identified through single-cell gene set scoring with the AUCell algorithm and differential expression analysis. A least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-Cox regression model was constructed and externally validated using the GSE72094 and GSE68465 cohorts. Associations between the model, genomic features, and the tumor immune microenvironment were systematically analyzed. The functional role of SRSF9 was verified through in vitro experiments.ResultsA total of 93 candidate genes significantly associated with ferroptosis activity were identified, and an 8-gene prognostic signature (PEBP1, TXN, TIMP1, SRSF9, CFL1, OTUB1, FURIN, and PTPN6) was constructed. The signature demonstrated robust prognostic performance across validation cohorts, with time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.65 to 0.74. High-risk patients exhibited elevated tumor mutation burden (TMB) and distinct immune infiltration patterns. Consensus clustering analysis classified patients into two ferroptosis-related molecular subtypes with significantly different prognoses. Functional experiments demonstrated that SRSF9 knockdown inhibited LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Moreover, SRSF9 knockdown downregulated glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and SLC7A11, increased lipid peroxidation and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, and the resulting cell viability loss was rescued by ferrostatin-1, indicating that SRSF9 knockdown induces ferroptosis in LUAD cells.ConclusionsThe ferroptosis-related prognostic signature established in this study may facilitate risk stratification in LUAD patients. SRSF9 may regulate ferroptosis-related pathways through modulation of GPX4 and SLC7A11, and functional experiments further confirmed that SRSF9 knockdown induces ferroptosis in LUAD cells, highlighting SRSF9 as a candidate therapeutic target that warrants further investigation.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), ferroptosis, prognostic model, SRSF9, tumor microenvironment

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study integrates single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data to construct a ferroptosis-related prognostic signature and suggests SRSF9 as a potential regulator of ferroptosis-related pathways in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD).

• High-risk patients exhibited elevated tumor mutation burden (TMB), an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and poor prognosis.

• SRSF9 knockdown inhibited LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, accompanied by decreased glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and SLC7A11 expression.

What is known and what is new?

• Ferroptosis plays critical roles in tumorigenesis and therapeutic response, but comprehensive ferroptosis-related prognostic signatures for LUAD integrating single-cell data are limited.

• This study provides an analytical framework integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data to identify ferroptosis-related prognostic markers, and suggests SRSF9 as a potential regulator of ferroptosis-related pathways in LUAD.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The ferroptosis-related signature may facilitate risk stratification for LUAD patients, potentially guiding treatment decisions such as chemotherapy selection for high-risk patients. Prospective clinical validation is needed before clinical implementation. Additionally, the mechanistic link between SRSF9 and ferroptosis warrants further investigation.

Introduction

Background

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) representing the most prevalent histological subtype, accounting for approximately 40% of all lung cancer cases (1,2). Despite significant advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapy, prognosis for LUAD patients remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 15–20%. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve clinical outcomes.

Among the emerging mechanisms implicated in cancer biology, ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, which is morphologically and mechanistically distinct from apoptosis and necrosis (3-5). Several key regulators have been identified, including glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11/xCT), which protect cells from ferroptosis by reducing lipid peroxides and maintaining intracellular cystine levels, respectively (6,7). Beyond the GPX4-GSH axis, the FSP1-CoQ10 system and GCH1-BH4 pathway have also been confirmed to participate in negative regulation of ferroptosis (8,9). In cancer therapy, inducing ferroptosis has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome drug resistance, as drug-resistant tumor cells often exhibit high sensitivity to ferroptosis, and targeting GPX4 or SLC7A11 can effectively reverse resistance to various chemotherapeutic agents (8). Furthermore, complex interactions exist between ferroptosis and immunotherapy: IFN-γ secreted by CD8+ T cells can enhance tumor cell sensitivity to ferroptosis by downregulating SLC7A11 expression, while damage-associated molecular patterns released by ferroptotic cells can activate antitumor immune responses (9). In LUAD, multiple ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) exhibit significant differential expression between tumor and normal tissues and are closely associated with patient prognosis (10). However, the associations between FRG expression profiles and LUAD molecular subtypes, immune microenvironment characteristics, and therapeutic responses remain to be systematically elucidated.

Given the complexity and heterogeneity of the LUAD tumor microenvironment, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) offers a powerful approach to address these challenges. scRNA-seq has revolutionized our understanding of tumor heterogeneity and the tumor microenvironment by enabling genome-wide analysis at single-cell resolution (11,12). By integrating scRNA-seq data with bulk transcriptomic data, researchers can identify cell type-specific gene signatures with enhanced biological relevance. However, previous ferroptosis-related prognostic models in LUAD have primarily relied on bulk transcriptomic data (13,14), which may not fully capture cellular heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment. The Cancer Genome Atlas lung adenocarcinoma cohort (TCGA-LUAD) (15) provides a well-characterized bulk transcriptomic resource for such analyses. Notably, SRSF9 is a member of the serine/arginine-rich (SR) protein family, which plays essential roles in alternative splicing regulation, and SR proteins have been implicated in tumorigenesis across multiple cancer types (16,17). However, whether SRSF9 is involved in ferroptosis regulation in LUAD remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to construct and validate a ferroptosis-related prognostic signature for LUAD by integrating scRNA-seq data and the TCGA-LUAD bulk transcriptomic data, and to explore the association between this signature and the tumor immune microenvironment, as well as the functional role of SRSF9 in LUAD. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and TRIPOD reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0248/rc).

Methods

Data sources and preprocessing

scRNA-seq data of LUAD tumor samples were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE127465) (18). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Bulk RNA-seq expression data (HTSeq-Counts) and clinical information were downloaded from the TCGA-GDC Data Portal (524 tumors and 58 normal samples). Two external validation cohorts, GSE72094 (n=442) (19) and GSE68465 (n=462) (20), were also obtained from GEO. After excluding patients with incomplete survival data or overall survival (OS) of less than one month, 490 patients were retained in the TCGA-LUAD cohort as the training set for prognostic model construction, while 386 and 439 patients from the GSE72094 and GSE68465 cohorts served as independent external validation sets. Baseline demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of the three bulk cohorts are summarized in Table S1. An additional independent LUAD scRNA-seq dataset (GSE123902) (21) was obtained from the GEO database as an external single-cell validation cohort. FRGs were compiled from the FerrDb database (22).

For the TCGA-LUAD training cohort, HTSeq-Counts data were converted to transcripts per million (TPM), followed by log2(TPM + 1) transformation. For the GSE72094 and GSE68465 external validation cohorts, probe IDs were mapped to gene symbols according to the respective platform annotations, and expression values were averaged when multiple probes corresponded to the same gene. The raw expression data were log2 transformed and quantile normalized using the limma R package.

Single-cell data processing and cell annotation

The raw count matrix of GSE127465 was processed using the Seurat R package (version 4.3.0) (23). Quality control was performed to remove low-quality cells. Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 5,000 detected genes, fewer than 200 or more than 30,000 unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, mitochondrial gene content ≥20%, or hemoglobin gene content ≥5% were excluded.

After quality control, the data were normalized using the LogNormalize method (scale factor =10,000). Highly variable genes were identified using the “vst” method in Seurat. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted, and the significant principal components were selected based on the elbow plot. A shared nearest neighbor graph was constructed using the selected PCs, and clustering was performed using the FindClusters function (resolution =1.0). The t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) algorithm was applied for dimensionality reduction and visualization.

Cell type annotation was based on canonical marker genes: T cells (CD3D, CD3E), B cells (BANK1, CD79A), neutrophils (CXCL8, ADGRG3, FCGR3B), plasma cells (JCHAIN, IGHG1), endothelial cells (VWF, PECAM1), smooth muscle cells (MYLK, ACTA2), fibroblasts (PDGFRB, PDGFRA, LUM, DCN, FAP), myeloid cells (LYZ, CD68, FABP4), epithelial cells (CDH1, KRT19, EPCAM), and mast cells (GATA2, KIT). The annotation results were validated through dot plot visualization of marker gene expression across all clusters.

Single-cell ferroptosis activity assessment and key gene screening

FRGs from the FerrDb database (including driver, suppressor, and marker genes) were intersected with the GSE127465 expression matrix, and the AUCell algorithm was used to calculate the ferroptosis gene set area under the curve (AUC) score for each cell as an indicator of ferroptosis pathway activity (24). Spearman correlation coefficients between each gene’s expression level and the AUCell score were calculated, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied (FDR <0.05), and the top 100 genes with the highest correlation coefficients were selected as candidates. Cells were then divided into high and low ferroptosis activity groups based on the median AUCell score, and differentially expressed genes between the two groups were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (FDR <0.05). The intersection of the top 100 candidate genes and the upregulated differentially expressed genes in the high AUCell group yielded 93 key genes for subsequent analysis. The same pipeline was independently applied to GSE123902 to verify the cross-cohort robustness of the screened genes.

Functional enrichment analysis

The 93 key genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis using the clusterProfiler R package based on over-representation analysis (ORA) (25). Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, with FDR <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Bulk transcriptome prognostic model construction and validation

In the TCGA-LUAD training cohort, the prognostic model was constructed using the 93 key genes as candidates. First, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to screen genes significantly associated with OS (P<0.05). Then, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-Cox regression analysis with 10-fold cross-validation was applied to further reduce overfitting, and the optimal penalty parameter was determined to select the candidate gene set (26). Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression combined with Akaike information criterion (AIC)-based bidirectional stepwise regression was employed to determine the optimal gene combination with the minimum AIC value. The fixed risk score formula and cutoff value were directly applied to the external validation cohorts (GSE72094 and GSE68465). Model performance was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank tests and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS prediction.

Genomic feature analysis

Based on TCGA-LUAD somatic mutation annotation files (MAF format), mutation profile analysis was performed using maftools (27). Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was estimated as the total number of nonsynonymous somatic mutations per megabase of coding region. Differences in TMB between high- and low-risk groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the association between risk group and dichotomized TMB status was assessed using the chi-square test.

Immune microenvironment analysis

Multiple immune deconvolution algorithms, including Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT), Microenvironment Cell Populations (MCP)-counter, Estimating the Proportion of Immune and Cancer cells (EPIC), Quantification of the Tumor Immune contexture (quanTIseq), and Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumours using Expression data (ESTIMATE), were used to compare immune cell infiltration differences between high- and low-risk groups (28-31). ESTIMATE was used to calculate StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore (32). Differences in immune cell infiltration and stromal/immune scores between high- and low-risk groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with P values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method within each deconvolution algorithm (FDR <0.05).

Immunotherapy response prediction and clinical utility assessment

Immunophenoscore (IPS) was calculated based on The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) (33); the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) model was applied to predict immunotherapy response (34); and the prognostic signature was evaluated in the IMvigor210 cohort for cross-cancer reference validation (35). IPSs between high- and low-risk groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The association between risk group and TIDE-predicted response was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. Survival differences in the IMvigor210 cohort were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves with the log-rank test.

Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association of risk score and clinicopathological features [age, gender, clinical stage, tumor (T) stage, node (N) stage, and metastasis (M) stage] with OS. Variables with P<0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the nomogram construction. A nomogram integrating risk score with T, N, and M stages was constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, with internal validation using bootstrap resampling (B =1,000) and calibration curves.

Consensus clustering analysis

Consensus clustering analysis was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus R package (36) based on the expression profiles of the 8 signature genes to identify ferroptosis-related molecular subtypes. A total of 1,000 iterations with 80% sample resampling were performed. The optimal number of clusters was determined by evaluating the consensus matrix heatmap and the consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves.

SRSF9 expression characterization and cell-cell communication analysis

To characterize the expression and prognostic significance of SRSF9, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to compare OS between SRSF9 high- and low-expression groups in the TCGA-LUAD cohort, using the median expression value as the cutoff. Differential expression of the 8 signature genes between tumor and adjacent normal tissues was also evaluated. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify SRSF9-associated biological pathways, with FDR <0.05 considered statistically significant (37).

SRSF9 expression distribution was analyzed across cell types in the scRNA-seq data. SRSF9 exhibited higher expression in epithelial cells compared with other cell types. Therefore, epithelial cells were divided into SRSF9⁺Epi and SRSF9⁻Epi subpopulations for subsequent analysis. The CellChat R package was used to construct ligand-receptor interaction networks using the Secreted Signaling subdatabase, comparing communication numbers, intensity, and enriched signaling pathways between the two epithelial cell subpopulations (38). CellChat analysis was independently repeated on GSE123902 using identical parameters to assess reproducibility of SRSF9-related communication findings.

Drug sensitivity prediction

The oncoPredict R package was used based on the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer 2 (GDSC2) database for drug sensitivity prediction, with criteria of Wilcoxon rank-sum test FDR <0.05 for half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) differences between high- and low-risk groups and lower IC50 in the high-risk group to screen potentially sensitive drugs for high-risk patients (39).

Cell culture

The human normal bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B and LUAD cell lines H1299 and PC9 were purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). The LUAD A549 cell line was obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). BEAS-2B cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Servicebio, G4515) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Servicebio, G8003) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Servicebio, G4003). A549, H1299, and PC9 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Servicebio, G4535) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

siRNA transfection

Three specific siRNAs targeting human SRSF9 (siSRSF9-1, siSRSF9-2, siSRSF9-3) were designed and synthesized by Hippo Biotechnology, with non-targeting siRNA as negative control (siNC). Transfection was performed using CALNP™ RNAi transfection reagent (D-Nano, DN001) at a final concentration of 50 nM following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected upon reaching approximately 60–70% confluence. Fresh complete medium was replaced 24 h post-transfection. Cells were harvested 48 hours post-transfection for western blot validation and functional experiments.

siRNA sequences were as follows: siSRSF9-1, sense: 5'-GCUGAUGUGCAGAAGGAUG-dTdT-3', antisense: 5'-CAUCCUUCUGCACAUCAGC-dTdT-3'; siSRSF9-2, sense: 5'-GAAGGAUCACAUGCGAGAA-dTdT-3', antisense: 5'-UUCUCGCAUGUGAUCCUUC-dTdT-3'; siSRSF9-3, sense: 5'-GCGAGAUCGAGCUCAAGAA-dTdT-3', antisense: 5'-UUCUUGAGCUCGAUCUCGC-dTdT-3'.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed as previously described (40). Cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China; P0100) supplemented with protease inhibitor (Xinliyzhihe, Changsha, China; AC0201), phosphatase inhibitor (Xinliyzhihe, AC0218), and PMSF (Xinliyzhihe, AC0244), incubated on ice, and centrifuged to collect supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; P0012S). Twenty micrograms of total protein per lane were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to 0.45 µm PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA; IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with rapid blocking solution (Epizyme, Shanghai, China; PS108P) for 15 min at room temperature.

Primary antibodies were diluted in TBST and incubated with membranes overnight at 4 ℃: anti-SRSF9 at 1:1,000 (CUSABIO, Wuhan, China; CSB-PA00214A0Rb), anti-GPX4 at 1:1,000 (Wanleibio, Shenyang, China; WL05406), anti-SLC7A11/xCT at 1:1,000 (UPBio, Hangzhou, China; YP-Ab-17133), and anti-β-actin at 1:5,000 (Servicebio, GB15003). HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody at 1:10,000 (Servicebio, GB23303) was incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL hypersensitive detection reagent (BGBiotech, Chongqing, China; BG0015) and images were acquired on a Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin. All Western blot experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates (n≥3).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) proliferation assay

Transfected A549 and H1299 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5×103 cells per well with five replicates per group. CCK-8 reagent (Abbkine, Wuhan, China; BMU106) was added at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm after incubation. Results were normalized to 0 hour or negative control group. Experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

Migration assays were performed using 24-well Transwell chambers with 8 µm pore polycarbonate membrane inserts. Cells were serum-starved for 12 hours, then resuspended in serum-free medium. Two hundred microliters of cell suspension (5×104 cells/well) was added to the upper chamber, with 600 µL complete medium containing 10% FBS in the lower chamber as chemoattractant. After 24 hours at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently removed with cotton swabs. Cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15–20 min, and washed with PBS. Five non-overlapping fields were randomly selected and counted at 200× magnification by a blinded observer. Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and normalized to control group.

For invasion assays, inserts were pre-coated with Matrix-Gel basement membrane matrix (Beyotime, C0372) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded at 200 µL (1×105 cells/well) in the upper chamber, with 600 µL complete medium containing 10% FBS in the lower chamber as chemoattractant. After 36-hour incubation at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2, subsequent fixation, staining, and counting steps were performed as described for migration assay. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to 90–100% confluence. A straight wound was created perpendicular to the plate surface using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Cells were washed twice with PBS to remove detached cells and changed to serum-free or low-serum (0–1% FBS) medium with corresponding treatments. Images were captured at 0 and 24 hours under an inverted phase-contrast microscope with a 4× objective (total magnification ×40) at the same or random positions (at least 3 fields per well). Wound area was measured using ImageJ (Wound Healing Tool plugin), and wound closure rate was calculated as (A0 − At)/A0 × 100%. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Lipid peroxidation and cell death rescue assays

To verify ferroptosis induction by SRSF9 knockdown, three assays were performed in A549 and H1299 cells. For lipid peroxidation detection, siNC- or siSRSF9-3-transfected cells were incubated with 5 µM C11-BODIPY 581/591 (Beyotime, S0043S) in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 ℃, washed with PBS, and imaged by fluorescence microscopy; the green (oxidized)/red (reduced) fluorescence ratio was quantified using ImageJ. For malondialdehyde (MDA) measurement and ferroptosis rescue, four groups were established in both cell lines: siNC, siNC + Fer-1, siSRSF9-3, and siSRSF9-3 + Fer-1, with Fer-1 (MCE, Shanghai, China; HY-100579) applied at 1 µM 24 h post-transfection. MDA levels were measured with a commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China; A003-4-1) and normalized to total protein by BCA assay. Cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, C0038) 24 h after Fer-1 treatment and normalized to the siNC group. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were assessed using Student’s t-test, and among multiple groups using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficient. For bioinformatics analyses involving multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Time-dependent AUC values and the nomogram C-index were internally validated using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated using the percentile method. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bioinformatics analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2); experimental data analysis and graphing were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2).

Results

Identification of FRGs from scRNA-seq

To construct a single-cell atlas of the LUAD tumor microenvironment, we performed quality control, dimensionality reduction, and clustering analysis on the GSE127465 dataset (18). Quality control metrics before and after filtering are shown in violin plots (Figure 1A). Dot plot analysis based on canonical marker genes verified the accuracy of cell type annotation (Figure 1B). Unsupervised clustering identified 22 cell clusters, which were subsequently annotated into 10 major cell types based on canonical marker gene expression, including T cells, B cells, plasma cells, myeloid cells, neutrophils, mast cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and smooth muscle cells, with clear separation between cell clusters (Figure 1C,1D). Feature plots showed the expression of 10 representative marker genes in corresponding cell types (Figure 1E).

Ferroptosis activity scores were calculated for each cell using the AUCell algorithm based on the FerrDb ferroptosis gene set, with gradient coloring revealing heterogeneous distribution of ferroptosis activity across different cell types and subpopulations (Figure 2A). Cells were dichotomized into high and low ferroptosis activity groups based on median AUCell score, showing distinct spatial distribution patterns on t-SNE (Figure 2B).

To screen ferroptosis-related key genes, we first calculated Spearman correlation between each gene’s expression level and AUCell score, selecting the top 100 genes with the highest correlation as candidates (Figure 2C); simultaneously, differential expression analysis was performed between high and low ferroptosis activity groups. The intersection of the top 100 correlated genes and upregulated differentially expressed genes (FDR <0.05) in the high AUCell group yielded 93 ferroptosis-related candidate genes. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these 93 genes were mainly enriched in KEGG pathways including Ferroptosis, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and Glutathione metabolism; GO-BP analysis showed enrichment in oxidative stress response and reactive oxygen species metabolism (all FDR <0.05, Figure 2D,2E), confirming that the screened genes are closely related to ferroptosis regulation. To assess the cross-cohort robustness of our single-cell findings, we further analyzed an independent LUAD scRNA-seq cohort (GSE123902, 17,741 cells from 8 primary LUAD tumor samples) using the same analytical pipeline. Of the 93 candidate FRGs identified in GSE127465, 74 (79.6%) were independently recapitulated in GSE123902 (Figure S1A-S1D).

Construction and validation of the prognostic model

In the TCGA-LUAD training cohort (15), a prognostic risk model was constructed using the 93 ferroptosis-related candidate genes as input. First, univariate Cox regression identified 17 genes significantly associated with OS (P<0.05). Subsequently, LASSO-Cox regression was applied for variable selection, with 10-fold cross-validation determining the optimal penalty parameter λ (Figure 3A,3B). Genes selected by LASSO were included in multivariate Cox regression, combined with AIC bidirectional stepwise regression, to determine an 8-gene prognostic signature: PEBP1, TXN, TIMP1, SRSF9, CFL1, OTUB1, FURIN, and PTPN6 (Figure 3C).

(B) Ten-fold cross-validation for tuning parameter selection. (C) Forest plot of multivariate Cox regression analysis for the 8-gene signature. (D) Kaplan-Meier survival curves and time-dependent ROC curves in the TCGA-LUAD training cohort. (E, F) Validation of the prognostic signature in GSE72094 (E) and GSE68465 (F) cohorts. *, P<0.05; **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001. AIC, Akaike information criterion; AUC, area under the curve; CI, confidence interval; LASSO, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator; LUAD, lung adenocarcinoma; ROC, receiver operating characteristic; TCGA, The Cancer Genome Atlas.

The risk score formula was: riskScore = −0.434 × PEBP1 + 0.228 × TXN + 0.189 × TIMP1 + 0.178 × SRSF9 + 0.186 × CFL1 + 0.176 × OTUB1 + 0.293 × FURIN − 0.301 × PTPN6.

Using the training set median risk score as the cutoff, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that the high-risk group had significantly worse OS than the low-risk group (log-rank P<0.001); time-dependent ROC curves showed AUC values of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.66–0.81), 0.71 (95% CI: 0.65–0.78), and 0.73 (95% CI: 0.67–0.79) for 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS prediction in the TCGA training cohort (Figure 3D).

To validate the robustness of the model, we performed validation in two independent external cohorts (19,20). In the GSE72094 cohort, applying the fixed risk score formula and cutoff, significant OS differences were observed between high- and low-risk groups (log-rank P<0.001), with AUC values of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.62–0.79), 0.70 (95% CI: 0.63–0.77), and 0.68 (95% CI: 0.59–0.77) for 1-, 2-, and 3-year prediction (Figure 3E). Similar significant survival stratification was observed in the GSE68465 cohort (log-rank P=0.005), with AUC values of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.58–0.75), 0.65 (95% CI: 0.59–0.71), and 0.67 (95% CI: 0.61–0.72) for 1-, 2-, and 3-year prediction (Figure 3F). These results demonstrate that the ferroptosis-related prognostic signature exhibits robust predictive performance across multiple cohorts.

Genomic characteristics of risk groups

To explore genomic differences between high- and low-risk groups, we performed systematic analysis based on TCGA-LUAD somatic mutation data. Oncoplot waterfall diagrams displayed the mutation landscape of both groups (Figure 4A,4B). The genes with the highest mutation frequencies in both groups were TP53, TTN, MUC16, CSMD3, and RYR2, but mutation rates for all genes were significantly higher in the high-risk group. The difference in TP53 mutation frequency was most pronounced (61.7% vs. 40.2%). Additionally, KRAS mutations were more enriched in the high-risk group (34.6% vs. 25.7%), while EGFR mutations were more prevalent in the low-risk group (15.4% vs. 9.6%), consistent with clinical observations of better prognosis in EGFR-mutant LUAD (2).

TMB analysis showed significantly higher TMB in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Figure 4C). Using median TMB as the cutoff, the proportion of high-TMB samples was significantly higher in the high-risk group (Figure 4D).

Immune microenvironment, immunotherapy-related indicators, and nomogram construction

To explore the relationship between ferroptosis-related risk score and the tumor immune microenvironment, we compared immune cell infiltration characteristics between high- and low-risk groups using multiple immune deconvolution algorithms (CIBERSORT, MCP-counter, quanTIseq, EPIC, and ESTIMATE) (Figure 5A). Results showed that the high-risk group exhibited an overall immunosuppressive microenvironment. Multiple algorithms consistently showed significant decreases in key antitumor immune effector cells in the high-risk group, including CD8⁺ T cells (EPIC: FDR <0.001; MCP-counter: FDR =0.010), B cells (MCP-counter: FDR <0.001; quanTIseq: FDR <0.001), myeloid dendritic cells (MCP-counter: FDR <0.001), and cytotoxic lymphocytes (MCP-counter: FDR = 0.027). Conversely, M0 macrophages (CIBERSORT: FDR <0.001) and neutrophils (CIBERSORT: FDR <0.001) were increased in the high-risk group, and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs: EPIC: FDR <0.001) were significantly elevated, suggesting enrichment of immunosuppressive and stromal remodeling components.

To explore the potential generalizability of the ferroptosis-related signature, we performed an exploratory analysis in the IMvigor210 cohort, an independent urothelial carcinoma immunotherapy cohort (35). Despite differences in cancer type, the risk model retained prognostic value. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that the high-risk group had significantly shorter OS than the low-risk group (log-rank P=0.006, Figure 5B).

Furthermore, we evaluated immunotherapy response potential using the TIDE online tool (34). TIDE analysis showed that the predicted immunotherapy response rate in the low-risk group (49.4%) was significantly higher than in the high-risk group (26.5%) [Fisher’s exact test, P<0.001, odds ratio (OR) =2.70], indicating that high-risk patients may exhibit higher predicted immune evasion potential, with a possible differential benefit from immunotherapy (Figure 5C).

Pearson correlation analysis between risk score and tumor microenvironment scores showed that risk score was significantly negatively correlated with immune score (r=−0.310, P=2.22×10−12), ESTIMATE Score (r=−0.257, P=8.34×10−9), and stromal score (r=−0.156, P=5.12×10−4), while positively correlated with tumor purity (r=0.263, P=3.39×10−9), suggesting that high-risk patients may exhibit an immunologically cold tumor phenotype (Figure 5D).

IPS analysis based on the TCIA database (33) revealed significantly higher scores in the low-risk group across all four immunotherapy regimens (Figure 5E), suggesting a potentially enhanced immunotherapy response in low-risk patients.

To enhance clinical utility, we first evaluated the association of risk score and clinicopathological features with OS in LUAD patients. Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that risk score [hazard ratio (HR) =2.72, 95% CI: 2.16–3.42, P<0.001], clinical stage (HR =2.71, 95% CI: 1.98–3.72, P<0.001), T stage (HR =2.30, 95% CI: 1.55–3.41, P<0.001), N stage (HR =2.69, 95% CI: 1.99–3.64, P<0.001), and M stage (HR =2.23, 95% CI: 1.30–3.82, P=0.004) were significant predictors of OS, while gender (HR =1.08, 95% CI: 0.80–1.45, P=0.61) and age (HR =1.00, 95% CI: 0.71–1.40, P=0.99) showed no significant association with OS (Figure S2A).

Based on univariate analysis results, we integrated risk score with T, N, and M stages to construct a nomogram for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probability in LUAD patients (Figure 5F). Bootstrap resampling (B =1,000) internal validation showed good predictive performance, with a bias-corrected C-index of 0.733 (95% CI: 0.697–0.769). Calibration curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates showed good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed values (Figure 5G), indicating high predictive accuracy for clinical personalized prognostic assessment.

Additionally, consensus clustering analysis was performed based on the expression profiles of the 8 signature genes to identify ferroptosis-related molecular subtypes. The consensus matrix heatmap and consensus CDF curves supported the optimal classification of patients into two subtypes (Figure S2B, S2C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly different OS between subtypes C1 and C2 (Figure S2D). PCA and t-SNE analyses further confirmed clear separation between the two molecular subtypes (Figure S2E,S2F).

Cell-cell communication and drug sensitivity prediction

To further explore the role of SRSF9 in the tumor microenvironment, we analyzed SRSF9 expression distribution across cell types. Results showed that SRSF9 was predominantly expressed in epithelial cells compared with other cell types (Figure S3A). Therefore, we divided epithelial cells into SRSF9⁺Epi and SRSF9⁻Epi subpopulations based on SRSF9 expression status for subsequent analysis.

CellChat cell communication analysis showed that SRSF9⁺Epi cells exhibited enhanced communication activity, showing higher overall communication strength compared to SRSF9⁻Epi cells (Figure 6A,6B).

Further analysis of ligand-receptor interactions showed that when SRSF9⁺Epi served as signal source, they mainly communicated with endothelial cells and myeloid cells (Figure 6C). When serving as signal target, SRSF9⁺Epi cells also exhibited stronger signal receiving capacity (Figure 6D). Signaling pathway analysis showed enrichment of inflammation and angiogenesis-related pathways including MIF, CXCL, VEGF, and MK in cell communication. These results suggest that SRSF9⁺ epithelial cells have enhanced proliferative activity and cell-cell communication capacity, while exhibiting immune evasion characteristics, potentially promoting tumor progression through tumor microenvironment remodeling. Independent CellChat analysis in GSE123902 confirmed that SRSF9⁺Epi cells exhibited greater communication number and strength than SRSF9⁻Epi cells. Four key pathways (MIF, MK, CXCL, VEGF) were consistently enriched across cohorts. Myeloid cells and endothelial cells remained the principal communication targets of SRSF9⁺Epi cells in both cohorts (Figure S3B-S3E).

Additionally, we performed drug sensitivity prediction using the oncoPredict R package based on the GDSC2 database. Violin plots showed that the predicted IC50 was significantly lower in the high-risk group for 10 drugs (all FDR <0.05), including docetaxel, paclitaxel, and vinorelbine (Figure 6E), suggesting that high-risk patients may be more sensitive to these drugs.

Functional validation of SRSF9 in LUAD cells

Prior to functional validation, we examined SRSF9 expression characteristics and prognostic significance in TCGA-LUAD. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that high SRSF9 expression was significantly associated with poor OS (P=0.01, Figure S3F). Expression analysis showed that SRSF9 was significantly differentially expressed between tumor and normal tissues in the TCGA-LUAD cohort (Figure S3G). GSEA was further performed based on SRSF9-correlated genes in TCGA-LUAD (37) (Figure S3H).

Given that SRSF9 is a key component of the prognostic signature, we further validated its functional role in LUAD using in vitro assays. Western blot analysis showed that, compared with normal bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B), SRSF9 expression was significantly upregulated in LUAD cell lines (A549, H1299, and PC9) (P<0.05; Figure 7A). To explore the function of SRSF9, three specific siRNAs were designed for knockdown experiments. Western blotting confirmed that all three siRNAs effectively reduced SRSF9 protein levels in H1299 cells (Figure 7B) and A549 cells (Figure 7C).

We then performed a series of functional assays to evaluate the effects of SRSF9 knockdown. CCK-8 assays showed significantly reduced proliferative capacity after SRSF9 knockdown (Figure 7D). Transwell migration/invasion assays demonstrated that SRSF9 knockdown significantly decreased cell migration and invasion compared with the control group (P<0.05; Figure 7E). Wound healing assays similarly demonstrated impaired migratory ability (Figure 7F).

We further examined key ferroptosis-related regulators and found that GPX4 and SLC7A11 protein expression was significantly downregulated after SRSF9 knockdown in A549 cells (Figure 7G) and H1299 cells (Figure 7H, P<0.05), suggesting a potential association between SRSF9 and ferroptosis-related pathways. Collectively, these results suggest a tumor-promoting role of SRSF9 in LUAD, as its knockdown inhibits cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Lipid peroxidation and cell death rescue assays

To directly demonstrate that SRSF9 knockdown induces ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation, MDA levels, and Fer-1 rescue assays were performed in A549 and H1299 cells. C11-BODIPY 581/591 staining showed a significant increase in the green/red fluorescence ratio in si3-transfected cells compared with siNC controls in both cell lines (P<0.05; Figure 8A-8D), indicative of elevated lipid peroxidation. MDA levels were similarly elevated following SRSF9 knockdown and were restored upon Fer-1 treatment (P<0.05; Figure 8E,8F). Furthermore, the reduction in cell viability induced by SRSF9 knockdown was significantly rescued by Fer-1 (P<0.05; Figure 8G-8H), whereas Fer-1 had no significant effect on siNC cells. Collectively, the C11-BODIPY, MDA, and ferrostatin-1 rescue assays provide direct phenotypic evidence that SRSF9 knockdown induces ferroptosis-associated cell death in LUAD cells, consistent with the observed downregulation of GPX4 and SLC7A11.

Discussion

Ferroptosis is a distinct form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and its role in tumorigenesis, progression, and therapeutic response has garnered increasing attention (3,4). In LUAD, multiple FRGs exhibit significant differential expression between tumor and normal lung tissues and are closely associated with patient prognosis (10). However, previous studies have predominantly relied on bulk transcriptomic data, which cannot capture the heterogeneity of ferroptosis activity across distinct cell types within the tumor microenvironment. The advent of scRNA-seq technology has enabled the exploration of heterogeneous gene expression profiles at single-cell resolution, providing novel perspectives for in-depth characterization of the molecular features of tumor-infiltrating cells and the identification of cell-type-specific gene signatures with greater biological significance (11,12). Given the complexity and heterogeneity of the LUAD tumor microenvironment, we sought to integrate scRNA-seq data to evaluate ferroptosis activity at the single-cell level, thereby screening candidate genes with cell-type-specific functional relevance that cannot be captured through bulk RNA-seq analysis alone. In this study, we constructed a novel 8-gene prognostic signature based on FRGs and validated its prognostic value in two independent external cohorts and clinical subgroups. A nomogram integrating the risk score with tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging demonstrated favorable predictive accuracy, and consensus clustering analysis further identified two ferroptosis-related molecular subtypes with significantly different prognoses. Furthermore, we explored the associations of this signature with genomic features and the tumor immune microenvironment, and validated the functional role of the key gene SRSF9 in LUAD through in vitro experiments.

We systematically analyzed the genomic characteristics and immune cell infiltration patterns within the LUAD tumor microenvironment. At the genomic level, high-risk patients exhibited a higher TMB and TP53 mutation frequency, whereas EGFR mutations were more prevalent in the low-risk group, consistent with the clinical observation that EGFR-mutant LUAD is associated with a more favorable prognosis (2). Aberrant p53 has been shown to promote immune evasion, leading to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment (41), which may partially explain the molecular basis of the immunosuppressive state observed in the high-risk group. Analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment using multiple immune deconvolution algorithms revealed significant differences in the composition of tumor-infiltrating immune cells between the two risk groups. Most notably, key anti-tumor effector cells, including CD8⁺ T cells, B cells, and myeloid dendritic cells, were significantly reduced in the high-risk group, whereas M0 macrophages, neutrophils, and cancer-associated fibroblasts were increased. Macrophages within the tumor microenvironment are prone to polarization toward an immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophage phenotype, and their elevated abundance has been associated with poor prognosis (42). The risk score was significantly negatively correlated with the immune score and the ESTIMATE score, while positively correlated with tumor purity, suggesting that high-risk patients exhibit an immune-cold tumor phenotype.

Immunotherapy and pharmacotherapy represent effective anti-cancer strategies. TIDE analysis (34) demonstrated that the predicted immunotherapy response rate was significantly higher in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group, and the IPS (33) was consistently higher in the low-risk group across all four immunotherapy regimens evaluated. Meanwhile, exploratory analysis using the IMvigor210 immunotherapy cohort (35) indicated that the risk model retained a degree of prognostic value. In addition, drug sensitivity prediction revealed that the high-risk group exhibited greater predicted sensitivity to several chemotherapeutic agents, including docetaxel, paclitaxel, and vinorelbine. These findings suggest that, although high-risk patients may derive limited benefit from immunotherapy, they could potentially benefit from chemotherapy. Although these findings may offer preliminary insights for personalized treatment, the predictions are entirely computational and require prospective experimental and clinical validation before any translational application.

The prognostic signature constructed in this study comprises eight genes (PEBP1, TXN, TIMP1, CFL1, OTUB1, FURIN, PTPN6, and SRSF9), which are involved in diverse cancer-related biological processes. PEBP1 is a well-established positive regulator of ferroptosis that forms a complex with 15-lipoxygenase to catalyze the peroxidation of polyunsaturated phosphatidylethanolamines, thereby triggering ferroptosis (43), consistent with its role as a protective factor in this model. TXN encodes thioredoxin, a compensatory antioxidant that sustains tumor cell survival upon glutathione system inhibition; simultaneous targeting of both antioxidant systems has been shown to enhance ferroptosis sensitivity (44-46). OTUB1 stabilizes SLC7A11 protein levels through deubiquitination, thereby suppressing ferroptosis; its knockout leads to SLC7A11 degradation and reduced glutathione levels (47,48). TIMP1, a tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases aberrantly overexpressed in cancer-associated fibroblasts, has been demonstrated to promote LUAD tumor progression through CD63 (49), mediate chemoresistance via IL-6 induction in NSCLC (50), and serve as a potential biomarker for early detection and prognosis of lung cancer (51). CFL1 modulates actin cytoskeletal dynamics to facilitate cancer cell migration and invasion (52), and its overexpression in NSCLC is significantly associated with poor prognosis and drug resistance (53). FURIN is a proprotein convertase that activates multiple pro-oncogenic substrates (54) and promotes tumor invasion and metastasis through the RAF/MEK signaling pathway (55); pan-cancer analyses have further confirmed its prognostic and immunological relevance (56). PTPN6 (SHP-1) is a protein tyrosine phosphatase that negatively regulates immune receptor signaling (57) and may improve prognosis by enhancing anti-tumor immune responses (58).

In this study, we further performed experimental validation of SRSF9. SRSF9 belongs to the SR protein family, whose members have been extensively reported to exert oncogenic functions across multiple tumor types through alternative splicing regulation, cell proliferation, and apoptosis (16,17). We found that SRSF9 was significantly upregulated in LUAD cell lines compared with normal bronchial epithelial cells, and that high SRSF9 expression was associated with poorer prognosis. Functional experiments demonstrated that SRSF9 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells, supporting a tumor-promoting role of SRSF9 in LUAD. CellChat cell-cell communication analysis (38) suggested that SRSF9⁺ epithelial cells may exhibit enhanced communication activity. They primarily interacted with endothelial cells and myeloid cells through inflammation- and angiogenesis-related pathways, including MIF, CXCL, VEGF, and MK. These findings suggest that SRSF9⁺ epithelial cells may promote tumor progression by remodeling the tumor microenvironment. At the molecular level, SRSF9 knockdown significantly downregulated both GPX4 and SLC7A11, two core negative regulators of ferroptosis (6,7). GPX4 reduces lipid peroxides, while SLC7A11 is the essential subunit of the cystine/glutamate antiporter System Xc⁻. Consistent with this molecular effect, SRSF9 knockdown induced elevated lipid peroxidation and MDA accumulation, and the resulting cell viability loss was specifically reversed by ferrostatin-1, a selective ferroptosis inhibitor. Together, these results provide direct phenotypic evidence that SRSF9 knockdown induces ferroptosis in LUAD cells. The concurrent decrease in both protein levels suggests that SRSF9 may protect tumor cells from ferroptosis by maintaining GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, which warrants further investigation as a candidate therapeutic target in LUAD. Taken together, these findings indicate that SRSF9 may promote LUAD progression through both direct oncogenic effects and modulation of key ferroptosis pathways.

There are several limitations in this study. First, the precise molecular mechanism by which SRSF9 regulates GPX4 and SLC7A11 remains to be elucidated. Second, although our single-cell findings were supported by an external scRNA-seq validation cohort, further validation in larger independent cohorts and functional experiments is warranted. Third, the three bulk cohorts demonstrated heterogeneity in clinicopathological characteristics, including differences in tumor stage distribution, smoking history, and the availability and completeness of treatment-related information, which may influence the cross-cohort generalizability of the prognostic signature. Finally, the immunotherapy response predictions and drug sensitivity analyses in this study were exploratory in nature, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Further experimental and prospective clinical validation is warranted before any translational application. Nonetheless, this study provides an analytical framework for integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data to investigate ferroptosis-related prognostic biomarkers, and SRSF9, as a potential ferroptosis regulator and therapeutic target in LUAD, warrants further in-depth investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, by integrating scRNA-seq and bulk transcriptomic data, this study constructed and validated an 8-gene prognostic signature based on FRGs. This signature demonstrates potential prognostic value in evaluating clinical outcomes of LUAD patients and reveals associations between ferroptosis-related features and the tumor immune microenvironment as well as therapeutic responses. Furthermore, our findings suggest that SRSF9 may play a role in ferroptosis regulation in LUAD by maintaining the expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11, and may represent a candidate therapeutic target. However, further experimental validation and prospective clinical studies are required to confirm its clinical relevance and therapeutic potential.
