Off-label use of dapagliflozin in a dog with recurrent calcium oxalate urolithiasis: a case report
Authors: Jay Pakhawala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalcium oxalate (CaOx) urolithiasis is a common and recurrent condition in dogs, typically managed with dietary modification, thiazide diuretics, and potassium citrate. In human medicine, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors such as dapagliflozin are increasingly used to manage diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease and have been shown to alter urinary factors associated with nephrolithiasis. Their effects in veterinary patients, however, remain uncharacterized. This case report describes the novel off-label use of dapagliflozin in a dog with refractory CaOx cystolithiasis despite standard preventive therapies.Case presentationAn 8-year-old neutered male Chihuahua with a history of recurrent CaOx urolithiasis experienced multiple episodes of stone recurrence despite adherence to a therapeutic stone-prevention diet, hydrochlorothiazide, and potassium citrate. Dapagliflozin was initiated off-label as adjunctive therapy to evaluate its potential impact on urinary calcium excretion. Pre- and post-treatment urinalyses and urine calcium-to-creatinine ratios were compared. Following initiation, the dog developed glucosuria, acidic urine, and increased urine specific gravity, while urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratios remained elevated. The medication was discontinued after 12 days due to concerns about underhydration.ConclusionsShort-term SGLT2 inhibition combined with a thiazide diuretic did not reduce hypercalciuria and may increase lithogenic risk if fluid intake is insufficient to offset the osmotic diuresis. This case underscores the importance of hydration monitoring and highlights potential pharmacologic interactions when using SGLT2 inhibitors off-label in dogs. Further controlled studies are needed to elucidate their safety and therapeutic role in managing CaOx urolithiasis in veterinary patients.Keywords: Sodium glucose cotransporter 2, Calcium oxalate urolithiasis, Urolith, Hydrochlorothiazide, Dapagliflozin, SGLT-2 inhibitor

Background

Calcium oxalate (CaOx) urolithiasis is common in both humans and dogs and often associated with hypercalciuria [1]. In dogs, CaOx stones have a high recurrence rate, with approximately 50% of affected dogs developing a recurrence within three years despite dietary management [2]. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, initially developed for glycemic control in type 2 diabetic patients, are now approved for a variety of clinical indications in human medicine including heart failure and chronic kidney disease [3]. In a rat model, SGLT2 inhibitors reduced renal inflammation and urinary excretion of oxalate [4]. Additionally, SGLT2 inhibitor therapy increases excretion of urinary citrate, a known inhibitor of CaOx crystal formation, in nondiabetic humans with calcium and uric acid kidney stones [5]. However, if fluid intake is insufficient, SGLT2 inhibitors can lead to dehydration and volume depletion [6], which in turn concentrates solutes and increases the risk for stone formation [7].

Case presentation

This report describes the case of an 8-year-old male neutered Chihuahua that was managed for calcium oxalate cystolithiasis with dapagliflozin. The dog was initially presented to the primary care veterinarian for an annual wellness examination. Physical examination revealed a grade III/VI heart murmur but was otherwise unremarkable. Complete blood count (CBC) and serum biochemistry profiles (CHEM) were performed which revealed an elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) at 237 U/L (laboratory reference interval 5-160 U/L), but no other abnormalities were noted. Urinalysis revealed a pH of 6.0, a urine specific gravity (USG) of 1.035 and the presence of calcium oxalate crystalluria (6–20 per high power field). The urine culture was negative. Abdominal radiographs were subsequently performed, and multiple cystoliths were visible. The dog then underwent a cystotomy with the primary care veterinarian where three calculi were retrieved ranging from 1.5 to 2 cm in size. Urolith analysis confirmed that all three calculi were composed of calcium oxalate. After the cystotomy, the dog was prescribed a therapeutic urinary diet for calcium oxalate stone prevention (Hill’s c/d urinary care), hydrochlorothiazide (2 mg/kg PO q12 hours), and potassium citrate (75 mg/kg PO q12 hours). The dog was reportedly maintained exclusively on the prescribed therapeutic diet with no known dietary indiscretion. Water was available ad libitum; however, daily water intake was not quantitatively measured. Eight months after the cystotomy, routine screening radiographs at the primary care veterinarian revealed recurrence of cystoliths. The urinalysis performed at that time did not show the presence of any crystalluria and the urine pH was 7.0 with a USG of 1.015; all other urinalysis parameters were normal. No clinical signs attributable to hypercortisolism were ever reported by the owners; however, a low dose dexamethasone suppression test was performed with results within the laboratory reference intervals. The dog was referred to a specialty hospital for stone removal via a percutaneous cystolithotomy (PCCL).

At the specialty hospital, an echocardiogram was performed which revealed degenerative mitral valve disease stage B1. No abnormalities were detected on the CBC, while the CHEM showed persistence of the ALP elevation (225U/L). Urinalysis revealed a urine pH of 7.5 while the urine specific gravity was 1.016. No crystalluria was observed; and the urine culture was negative. An ionized calcium level was performed on a blood gas analyzer (I-STAT Alinity) and was within the laboratory reference interval. Repeat abdominal radiographs showed multiple urinary bladder calculi with the largest measuring 0.4 cm. The PCCL procedure was performed the following week without any complications. Four calculi were retrieved, and stone analysis confirmed them all to be calcium oxalate.

Nine months after the PCCL procedure, routine screening through the primary care veterinarian via radiography again revealed the presence of multiple pinpoint calculi in the urinary bladder, with the largest measuring 1.5 mm. The dog then presented again to the specialty hospital. The dog was reported to be completely asymptomatic at home, and physical examination was static to prior. The previously elevated ALP had resolved, and the rest of the bloodwork was unremarkable. Urinalysis revealed a pH of 6.5 with calcium oxalate crystalluria (6–20 per high power field). The urine specific gravity was 1.018. An abdominal ultrasound performed by a board-certified radiologist revealed mild diffused hepatopathy, bilateral chronic nephropathy with pinpoint nephroliths and miniscule cystic calculi. Given the small size of the cystic calculi, a voiding urohydropropulsion was performed, and 20 stones were retrieved. Stone analysis confirmed them all to be calcium oxalate.

Due to the patient’s multiple recurrences of calcium oxalate urolithiasis, alternative therapies were sought. Two weeks after the VUH procedure dapagliflozin was prescribed (2.5 mg q24 hours) in addition to the hydrochlorothiazide and potassium citrate therapy. Prior to starting dapagliflozin, urinalysis was performed which revealed a pH of 7.0 and a urine specific gravity of 1.015. No crystalluria was noted. Additionally, urine calcium and creatinine concentrations were measured on midstream voided urine samples (IDEXX Laboratories) at 5 different time points (three morning fasted samples between 7AM-8AM and two evening non-fasted samples between 6PM-8PM) on the days prior to starting dapagliflozin administration. Samples were collected under routine clinical conditions and were not fully standardized. A urine calcium-tocreatinine ratio (UCaCr) was performed on all these samples (Table 1). A fasted morning urine sample and non-fasted evening sample was then obtained on days 9,10 and 11 after starting dapagliflozin, and a UCaCr was performed on the samples (Table 1). Results of the pre-treatment and post-treatment UCaCr, urine calcium, and urine creatinine were compared with Wilcoxon rank sum tests (Fig. 1), and it was discovered that the patient remained hypercalciuric for the duration that dapagliflozin was administered with a decrease in urine creatinine concentration but without a statistically significant change in urine calcium concentration or UCaCr. Additionally, urinalysis on day 11 revealed that the urine pH was 5.0, with a significant glucosuria (1000 mg/dL, 3+), urine specific gravity of 1.060 and the presence of calcium oxalate crystals (6–20/high power field). History provided by the owner revealed that while being administered dapagliflozin, the patient was polyuric and polydipsic, but no other significant observations were noted. During the treatment period, hydration status was assessed through serial physical examinations, body weight measurements, and urinalysis, including urine specific gravity. Water intake was not quantitatively measured but was assessed only through owner observations. No changes were made to the patient’s diet or concurrent medications during the treatment period. Physical examination revealed tacky mucous membranes while the body weight was decreased by 0.2 kg (3.3 kg from 3.5 kg prior to dapagliflozin therapy). In conjunction with marked glucosuria and urine specific gravity of 1.060, these findings raised concern for underhydration during dapagliflozin therapy. The drug was subsequently stopped on day 12. A complete blood count and serum biochemistry were performed at the primary care veterinarian one week after stopping dapagliflozin and was unremarkable. Urinalysis one week after discontinuation of dapagliflozin revealed a pH of 6.5, urine specific gravity of 1.019 and no crystalluria.

Discussion and Conclusions

Current managemental strategies for calcium oxalate (CaOx) urolithiasis in dogs focus primarily on dietary and medical interventions aimed at reducing urinary supersaturation of calcium and oxalate [8–9]. One study highlighted that decreasing urine specific gravity from 1.034 to 1.024 reduced CaOx saturation by 34% [10]. Additionally, hydrochlorothiazide is also recommended as it decreases urine calcium excretion [9].

SGLT2 inhibitors reduce proximal tubular glucose and sodium reabsorption, resulting in osmotic diuresis and increased distal sodium delivery. These changes may alter renal calcium handling and thereby effect calcium oxalate stone formation. In the present case, dapagliflozin was selected as a novel adjunctive therapy due to persistent recurrence despite standard interventions, with the aim of modulating urinary solute handling and reducing hypercalciuria.

In the present case, no benefit was observed during therapy with dapagliflozin in a dog with recurrent CaOx urolithiasis. While a decrease in urine creatinine concentration occurred, consistent with the known osmotic diuretic effect of this drug class, no change was apparent in the urine calcium or UCaCr. Human studies have demonstrated that SGLT-2 inhibitors may reduce urinary risk factors associated with nephrolithiasis in diabetic patients [11–12]. A recent trial of empagliflozin in nondiabetic humans with calcium and uric acid nephrolithiasis similarly found that it did not decrease urine calcium concentrations or the relative supersaturation of CaOx [5]. SGLT2 inhibitors promote glucosuria and natriuresis, leading to increased urine volume and subsequently decreased urinary solute concentrations [13–14]. If the effect of the drug were limited solely to water diuresis, a proportional decrease in the concentration of other urinary analytes, including calcium, would be expected. However, the absence of a similar reduction in urinary calcium concentration suggests that absolute calcium excretion remained stable or even increased. This dissociation supports the hypothesis that SGLT2 inhibition may induce hypercalciuria by impairing proximal tubular calcium reabsorption, as has been described in human and rodent models [15–16]. A confounding factor with the present case is that the dog was receiving a thiazide diuretic concurrently with the SGLT2 inhibitor, and the enhanced sodium delivery to the distal convoluted tubule might have reduced effectiveness of the thiazide. This interaction may have altered distal tubular sodium and calcium handling, potentially attenuating the expected hypocalciuric effects of thiazide therapy.

The recurrent calcium oxalate urolithiasis observed in this case is most consistent with idiopathic hypercalciuria, which is a well-recognized driver of calcium oxalate stone formation in dogs. Despite implementation of standard preventive strategies, including dietary modification, thiazide therapy, and potassium citrate, recurrence persisted, suggesting either incomplete therapeutic response or underlying physiologic drivers of calcium excretion. Although urine pH was variably within acceptable ranges, calcium oxalate formation is less dependent on urine pH and more strongly influenced by urinary calcium concentration and supersaturation. Additionally, intermittent increases in urine concentration may have further contributed to a persistently lithogenic urinary environment.

In addition to the lack of observed benefit, the decreased body weight and increased urine specific gravity compared to the degree of glucosuria during SGLT2 inhibitor administration were consistent with underhydration rather than appropriate diuresis. This finding implies that the expected dilutional effect of osmotic diuresis may have been blunted by inadequate water intake and compensatory fluid conservation. Consequently, urinary calcium may have remained concentrated, sustaining or increasing the risk of calcium oxalate supersaturation. The concurrent thiazide therapy was likely a major contributor to this finding. While hypovolemia can occur with SGLT2 inhibitor therapy alone, the risk is relatively low [6]. However, combined therapy with a SGLT2 inhibitor and hydrochlorothiazide significantly increases the effects on urine sodium excretion and urine volume in humans with type 2 diabetes mellitus [17]. These findings underscore the importance of ensuring adequate hydration when SGLT2 inhibitors are used in stone-forming patients, particularly those receiving thiazides, as osmotic diuresis alone may not be sufficient to mitigate the lithogenic potential if water intake is suboptimal.

This case report has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, as a single-patient observation, the findings cannot be generalized to the wider canine population without further study. Spot urine samples were collected, which are known to have large intraindividual variability in several measurements, including in calcium-to-creatinine ratios [18]. Additionally, the patient remained on concurrent therapies—namely hydrochlorothiazide and potassium citrate—which may have influenced urinary calcium handling and confounded the interpretation of the SGLT2 inhibitor’s isolated effects. Hydration status was inferred from urine specific gravity, clinical signs, and physical examination findings; however, objective measures such as quantitative water intake were not recorded. Additionally, detailed information regarding dietary compliance, water intake, and urinary habits was not objectively quantified. These factors are known to significantly influence urine concentration and calcium oxalate supersaturation and may have contributed to the patient’s recurrent urolithiasis. The duration of dapagliflozin administration was relatively short (12 days), which limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding its effects on urinary calcium excretion and long-term lithogenic risk. Finally, urinary citrate concentrations and calcium oxalate relative supersaturation indices were not assessed, which would have provided a more complete understanding of the lithogenic environment.

While SGLT2 inhibitors hold theoretical promise in modifying urinary risk factors for nephrolithiasis, the findings of this single-case report should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Adequate hydration appears to be critical to mitigating the potential lithogenic risks of SGLT2-induced osmotic diuresis; however, achieving consistent water intake can be challenging in veterinary patients due to both patient factors and limitations in owner compliance. Since the mechanisms underlying the cardio- and nephroprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors remain incompletely characterized in veterinary medicine, caution is warranted when considering their off-label use in stone-forming patients. Further research is warranted to determine whether these agents may have a role in specific subpopulations or under more controlled hydration conditions in veterinary medicine.

Euthanasia/sacrifice methods

No animals were euthanized or sacrificed for the purposes of this study. All procedures were performed in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of animals. All procedures (voiding urohydropropulsion and percutaneous cystolithotomy) were performed under general anesthesia using routine veterinary anesthetic protocols. The patient was premedicated with methadone (0.3 mg/kg IV, once), induced with lidocaine (2 mg/kg IV, once), midazolam (0.2 mg/kg IV, once), and alfaxalone (up to 2 mg/kg IV, titrated to effect), and anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane in oxygen. The patient remained fully anesthetized and unconscious throughout all surgical procedures, and appropriate monitoring and supportive care were provided.
