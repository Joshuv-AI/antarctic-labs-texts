From trigger to response: an ecological momentary study of intrusive memory sequences in PTSD and complex PTSD
Authors: Lina Dietiker, Amelie Zacher, Charlotta Rühlmann, Boris B Quednow, Birgit Kleim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Intrusive trauma memories are a hallmark symptom of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and contribute substantially to its persistence. However, the sequential unfolding of intrusions, from pre-intrusion states to post-intrusion responses, remains insufficiently understood.Method: In a pre-registered Ecological Momentary Assessment study, we examined 553 intrusions reported by 51 individuals diagnosed with PTSD or complex PTSD (cPTSD), capturing key features of the intrusion sequence, including content, modality, triggers, and responses.Results: Results showed high within-person variability across intrusion features, underscoring the dynamic and context-sensitive nature of intrusions and the fluctuating regulatory demands imposed on patients in daily life. Further, emotions demonstrated a pivotal role in the intrusion sequence, functioning as trigger, sensory modality or response. Multilevel analyses revealed that anger prior to an intrusion predicted greater subsequent distress (B = 0.11, p = .041) and heightened perceived loss of control (B = 0.20, p = .001). PTSD and cPTSD subgroups did not differ in terms of trigger types, modalities, or response patterns.Discussion: Together, these findings highlight the real-world burden of intrusive memories and the central role of emotions, such as anger, in intrusion-related distress. This emphasizes the need for interventions targeting not only intrusions themselves, but also their emotional and physiological precursors and consequences, through strategies that promote adaptive regulation in both PTSD and cPTSD.KEYWORDS: Intrusion, intrusive memory, PTSD, complex post-traumatic stress disorder, ecological momentary assessment

Introduction

Intrusive memories are involuntary, vivid memories of a specific event (Ehlers et al., 2004) frequently occurring after trauma and constitute a hallmark symptom of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). As they tend to be among the first symptoms to emerge after trauma they may play a central role in the network of other PTSD symptoms (Bryant et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2024). Theoretically, intrusions are conceptualized to be a consequence of stress-induced alterations in trauma memory consolidation, resulting in poorly integrated memories, rendering them more susceptible to triggers in everyday life, sustaining the development and persistence of PTSD symptoms (Brewin et al., 2010; Brewin & Holmes, 2003; Ehlers & Clark, 2000). They can be highly disruptive in patients’ lives, strongly impact affective states (Berntsen, 2001), i.e. longer-lasting mood states (Ekman, 1992), and evoke helplessness, fear, anger, shame, and guilt (Kleim et al., 2013). Furthermore, it has been suggested that spontaneous intrusive memories may exert a stabilizing effect on the trauma memory itself by attenuating their natural fading over time. Specifically, intrusions may not merely be a consequence of insufficiently integrated trauma memories, but also contribute to the persistence of the intrusive memory content in its form, for instance, by repeatedly reactivating specific aspects of the memory that may then be preferentially reconsolidated and strengthened (Herz et al., 2020; Kindt & Elsey, 2023).

Though research has yet to establish a clear picture of the moment-to-moment sequence of intrusions, including their frequency, triggers, modalities, characteristics, and responses (Kleindienst et al., 2017). Intrusive memories are most commonly described as visual memories and images (e.g. Ehlers et al., 2004; Iyadurai et al., 2019), while also involving other sensory modalities such as emotions, bodily sensations and auditory impressions. Prospective research indicates that intrusion characteristics predict PTSD symptom severity more accurately than intrusion frequency (Michael et al., 2005). In this context, Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) has provided valuable insights into daily fluctuations of intrusions and their context. Findings on intrusion frequency measured with EMA, however, vary across different samples, patient settings, and sampling schemes (Kleim et al., 2013; Kleindienst et al., 2017; Massazza et al., 2022; Priebe et al., 2013). However, intrusive trauma-memory characteristics differ between trauma-exposed individuals and those with diagnosed PTSD (e.g. Kleim et al., 2013; Reed et al., 2024), underscoring the need for research in diagnosed PTSD populations despite methodological challenges (e.g. participant burden) that often result in smaller samples sizes compared to trauma exposed samples (e.g. Dornbach-Bender et al., 2020; Kleim et al., 2013). Further studies provide insight into intrusion phenomenology and its associations with other symptoms (e.g. Porcheret et al., 2020; Schenker et al., 2023) underscoring that frequency alone provides only a limited perspective. Notably, a stronger feeling of ‘nowness’ is associated with emotional responses, such as fear, helplessness, guilt, and shame (Kleim et al., 2013). These findings highlight that, although often brief, intrusions may initiate enduring cascades of responses and symptoms. Yet, the precise sequences linking their characteristics, contextual triggers, and the associated emotional, physiological, and cognitive–behavioural responses remain poorly understood, despite their clear relevance for PTSD and complex PTSD (cPTSD) (Brewin et al., 2025). The latter, introduced in ICD-11, extends PTSD by including additional symptoms such as emotion dysregulation, negative self-concept, and interpersonal difficulties (World Health Organization, 2022). Yet, research comparing intrusions occurring in PTSD and cPTSD, respectively, remains scarce.

The current pre-registered study therefore aimed to (1) characterize the frequency and sequence of intrusive memories in individuals with PTSD or cPTSD in daily life, including their content, sensory modalities, triggers, responses, and memory characteristics (intrusiveness, vividness, distress, fear, loss of control). As part of this first research question, exploratory analyses were conducted to examine potential differences in these intrusion features between the PTSD and cPTSD subgroups. Beyond this, we investigated (2) within-person variability in intrusion characteristics (vividness, distress, fear, and perceived loss of control). Further, (3) we examined associations between contextual factors (social contact, affective state, and substance use) and intrusive memory characteristics, and (4) investigated associations between intrusion frequency and demographic variables, as well as between symptom severity and intrusion characteristics. We hypothesized that higher symptom severity would be related to higher intrusive memory frequency and higher ratings of intrusiveness, vividness, distress, fear, and feeling of loss of control.

Materials and methods

Preregistration

The present study design and research questions were pre-registered: https://osf.io/vge6q/. The statistical analysis plan for this manuscript was developed and time-stamped after inspecting preliminary data patterns, but before formal hypothesis testing. Data sets (with exclusion of intrusive memory content due to sensitivity of data) and analysis code are available on the Open Science Framework and can be accessed at https://osf.io/z43en/. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Philosophical Faculty of the University of Zurich (Nr. 22.2.19). The authors assert that all procedures contributing to this work comply with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. The authors report there are no competing interests to declare.

Participants

Fifty-four participants with intrusive trauma memories in the context of classic or complex PTSD symptomatology were recruited via social media advertisements, websites, and local psychiatric treatment facilities between April 2022 and April 2023.

Inclusion criteria required participants to (1) be aged between 18 and 60 years;(2) own a smartphone; (3) have sufficient proficiency in German; and (4) fulfil DSM-5 PTSD criteria cluster A (trauma exposure), B (re-experiencing), F (< 1 month duration), G (functional impairment), and H (exclusion of other causes), as well as at least two criteria clusters from C (avoidance), D (negative thoughts or feelings), or E (trauma-related arousal) to ensure fulfilment of diagnostic criteria for subthreshold, full or complex PTSD (most commonly used definition of subthreshold PTSD by Blanchard et al. (1996) as fulfilment of diagnostic criteria A, B, F, G, H and at least 2 out of C-E). Exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism spectrum disorder, and substance use disorder (except for mild alcohol or cannabis use disorder or mild to moderate nicotine use disorder) according to DSM-5. Of the 54 participants screened, two did not fulfil diagnostic criteria for subthreshold PTSD, and one fulfilled diagnostic criteria for a cocaine use disorder. Thus, data from N = 51 participants were included in the analysis.

Procedure

This longitudinal study consisted of a baseline face-to-face laboratory visit followed by 14 days of EMA data collection (see Figure 1). At baseline, participants provided informed consent, completed questionnaires, and were interviewed for demographic information as well as clinical diagnostics carried out by trained graduate level interviewers, supervised by clinical psychologists. The SEMA3 application was installed on participants’ personal smartphones, with instructions provided alongside a demo version of the questionnaires (Koval et al., 2019).

The EMA assessment included two different sampling schemes. Participants were asked to complete an event-based questionnaire (adapted from Hackmann et al., 2004) whenever an intrusion occurred or as soon as possible afterward. Additionally, they received time-based notifications twice a day to assess social contact quality, their affective state, and substance intake, with a 3-hour response window. Participants were debriefed after study completion and compensated with CHF 100, with an additional CHF 20 if compliance in time-based questionnaires exceeded 75%.

Intrusive memory assessment

We adapted the intrusion questionnaire (Hackmann et al., 2004) to measure via EMA intrusion-preceding affective state (tiredness, feeling of safety, relaxation, comfort and anger, rated from 1-10), intrusion triggers (multiple-choice), sensory modalities (multiple-choice), content (key words), characteristics (vividness, intrusiveness, fear, distress, and loss of control, rated from 1-10), and emotional, physiological and cognitive–behavioural responses (multiple-choice). Multiple-choice questions came with additional voluntary open text fields, which were subsequently categorized into existing answer options by two raters. For this study, content of intrusive memories was translated from German to English using DeepL machine translation with the ‘deeplr’ package (Zumbach & Bauer, 2023).

Clinical variables

For psychiatric diagnostics the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 – Clinical Version (SCID-5-CV) was employed to assess DSM-5 diagnoses (Beesdo-Baum et al., 2019; First et al., 2016). PTSD symptoms were assessed with the Clinician Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5) (Müller-Engelmann et al., 2020; Weathers et al., 2018). Three questions were added manually to assess self-organization disturbances to identify fulfilment of cPTSD (Maercker et al., 2022). Participants were assigned to the PTSD subgroup if they had either classic PTSD (fulfilment of all diagnostic criteria) or if they had subthreshold PTSD (definition by Blanchard et al. (1996)). The cPTSD subgroup included participants who fulfilled all diagnostic criteria of PTSD as well as the three required criteria for cPTSD according to ICD-11. PTSD symptoms were also assessed with the self-report PTSD-checklist (PCL-5) including the revised Life Events Checklist (LEC-5, screening of criterion A) for DSM-5 (Blevins et al., 2015; Krüger-Gottschalk et al., 2017). Detailed information on the aforementioned questionnaires, along with descriptions of additional questionnaires employed, can be found in Supplement S13.

Data analysis

We conducted all analyses in R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2023). The occurrence of triggers, modalities and responses was calculated as percentage per person. Intrusive memory content was analysed using a word cloud, with punctuation, numbers and stop words removed and words lemmatized using the ‘tm’ (Feinerer et al., 2024) ‘textstemm’ (Rinker, 2018) and ‘wordcloud2’ (Lang & Chien, 2018) packages. Words describing the modality instead of content (e.g. flashback) were filtered out.

For all multilevel models (MLMs), we first estimated null models, and all predictors were person-mean-centred. We fitted MLMs by restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML) and t-tests with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom approximations using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). Model assumptions were tested. The first MLMs (MLM1) predicted intrusive memory characteristics from preceding affective state assessed in the same intrusion entry (see Table S1).

We used time-lagged MLMs to estimate the associations between context factors during the timeframe between two time-based entries (9–14 h) – and the intrusive memory characteristics of the intrusion occurring within that timeframe, see Figure 1B. Predictor variables included affective state at the start of the timeframe (assessed in the initial time-based entry) and social contact and substance use during the same timeframe (assessed with the time-based entry at the end of the timeframe). Data reordering ensured meaningful regression of variables collected at different timepoints but referring to the same timeframe. We only considered the 491 intrusion entries with respective prior time-based entries for this analysis. We estimated one time-lagged MLM per intrusive memory characteristic (see MLM2 in Table S1). The final EMA dataset consisted of all time- and event-based assessments including incomplete entries.

We used linear regression models (LM) to estimate the association between demographic variables and intrusion frequency as well as between symptom severity and intrusive memory characteristics.

For our explorative investigation of group differences between the PTSD and cPTSD subgroups, we used GLMs (models specified in supplementary Table S1).

Results

Descriptives and compliance

The sample was mostly female and on average 30 years old with moderate PTSD symptom severity (CAPS-5 score M = 30.2, SD = 12.4) and on average 2.5 comorbid diagnoses (including nicotine use disorder). Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Participants filled out a total of 1777 EMA entries of which 1224 were time-based entries, where compliance varied between 31% and 100%. A total of 553 event-based intrusions were reported (M = 10.84, SD = 8.47, range 1–39). Intrusion frequency was significantly higher in the cPTSD group, where 255 intrusions were reported from 18 participants compared to 298 intrusions from 33 participants in the PTSD group (n = 18 participants fulfilled criteria for full PTSD, n = 15 for subthreshold PTSD). Most intrusion entries (68%) were reported within 60 min after intrusion occurrence.

Intrusion content

Participants’ intrusion descriptions most frequently included the words violence and fear as illustrated in the word cloud in Figure 2A. Most frequent additional words describing intrusions were event, helplessness, head and injury in the PTSD subgroup (Figure 2(B)) and father, mother, child and abuse in the cPTSD subgroup (Figure 2(C)), highlighting both shared and distinct content features of intrusive memories in PTSD and cPTSD.

Triggers, modality and responses to intrusions

Most frequent triggers in the overall group were place/environment/situation (23.5% of intrusions per participant on average), conversations (22.7%) and thoughts/emotions (20.4%). Sensory modalities in which intrusive memories were perceived mostly comprised emotions (55.6%), bodily sensations (43.8%) and movie-like (43.9%) or fragmented (33.9%) visual memories. Emotional responses were mainly reported as helplessness (53.1%), fear (45.8%), and anger (26.3%). Physiological responses were most often indicated as agitation (62.9%), tension (60.0%), and racing heart (29.8%). Most often applied cognitive–behavioural responses, or associated coping strategies, included distraction (50.2%), suppression (31.0%), and rumination (25.4%). Occurrence of triggers, modalities and responses for both subgroups are depicted in Figure 3.

There were no significant subgroup differences in the averaged percentages of trigger types, modalities, emotional, physiological or cognitive–behavioural responses (all p-values greater .05, see Figure S5 in the supplement for coefficient plots of the estimated effects of predictors).

Co-occurrences of cognitive–behavioural responses to intrusive memories are depicted in Figure S6 in the supplement. The coping strategy most often combined with others was self-harm (most frequently co-occurring with rumination or suppression), with even higher co-occurring rates observed in the cPTSD subgroup. For memory triggers, modalities, emotional and physiological responses, co-occurrence patterns did not differ between the two subgroups (see Figures S7-S10 in supplement, S11 & S12 for co-occurrence of all intrusion variables for the PTSD and cPTSD group).

Intrusion characteristics

Intrusions were, on average, reported as highly intrusive, vivid, distress and with a sense of fear and loss of control. Notably, there was substantial variance both between–, but also within-person over time (see Table 1). Associations between context and intrusion characteristics

MLM including pre-intrusion states assessed within the same intrusion entry showed that higher levels of tiredness immediately prior to an intrusive memory was significantly associated with greater intrusiveness (B = 0.06, p = .049) and fear (B = 0.08, p = .037). Higher levels of anger immediately before an intrusion were significantly associated with increased fear (B = 0.11, p = .021), distress (B = 0.15, p < .001), and feeling of loss of control (B = 0.15, p = .001). Higher feeling of safety directly before an intrusion was significantly associated with lower feeling of loss of control (B = −0.14, p = .025). A total of 513 intrusion entries were included in these models; due to one missing distress rating, the model including distress as the outcome comprised 512 intrusion entries.

MLM including time-lagged associations between social contact, substance use and affective state within the same timeframe (up to 12 h) as intrusion occurrence showed a significant positive association between anger reported in the last time-based entry before intrusion occurrence and increased distress (B = 0.11, p = .041) as well as increased loss of control (B = 0.20, p = .001) (see Table 3), with 376 intrusion entries included in these models, as only those observations had preceding time-based entries and data on all variables required for the analysis (due to one missing distress rating, the multilevel model including distress rating as the outcome comprised 375 intrusion entries). Fixed effects in our models accounted for 1–3% of variance, these effects were statistically significant across hundreds of intrusion observations and point to meaningful, clinically relevant associations between pre-intrusion states and intrusion characteristics. The full models, including random effects, explained 30-47% of the variance, see Tables 2 and 3.

Symptom severity and intrusions

Higher age (β = 0.48, p < .001) and more depressive symptoms (β = 0.29, p = .041) were significantly associated with increased intrusion frequency (coefficients plot in Figure S3 in the supplement). PTSD symptom severity was not associated with more intrusiveness, vividness, distress, fear or feeling of loss of control (coefficients plot in Figure S4 in the supplement).

Discussion

In this pre-registered EMA-study, we provide a fine-grained, real-world understanding of trauma-related intrusion sequences, including their frequency, content, triggers, sensory modaities and responses, in individuals with PTSD and, critically, extend daily life intrusive memory research to complex PTSD. The frequency of intrusions in our sample aligned with prevision EMA studies in PTSD samples (Kleim et al., 2013; Kleindienst et al., 2017). Compared with the PTSD subgroup, individuals with cPTSD reported more frequent intrusions, a greater number of traumatic events, higher scores across CTQ-subscales, higher levels of dissociation and lower overall global functioning levels. This pattern is consistent with the broader and more pervasive symptom burden typically associated with cPTSD and underscores the cumulative and multifaceted impact of complex trauma on daily psychological functioning (Brewin et al., 2025).

While both subgroups most frequently used the words violence and fear to describe the content of their intrusions, more fine-grained differences emerged beyond these shared core themes. In the PTSD subgroup, intrusion content tended to remain closely anchored to the traumatic event itself (e.g. event, head, injury) and related emotions (e.g. helplessness, anger), suggesting a relatively circumscribed, incident-focused representation. Individuals with cPTSD more often used words referring to persons and close relationships (e.g. father, mother, child), pointing to a more interpersonal and relational embedding of intrusive memories. This pattern could reflect the developmental and relational context in which cPTSD commonly arises – namely prolonged, interpersonal trauma, often occurring in early life (Karatzias et al., 2017). In line with this interpretation, the cPTSD subgroup also showed higher levels of childhood trauma across CTQ subscales, suggesting that the social and relational nature of their trauma experience may be reflected in the phenomenology of later intrusions.

Participants were not always able to identify specific triggers for their intrusions. When triggers were identified, both external (e.g. environment, conversations) and internal cues (e.g. thoughts/emotions) were reported, with the type of trigger differing between one intrusion and another. Prior research suggested visual triggers are more common after single-incident trauma whereas acoustic triggers are associated with prolonged trauma (Müller et al., 2016). Although heterogeneity of trauma types in our sample precluded a direct test of this hypothesis, we found no significant differences in trigger type occurrence between PTSD and cPTSD subgroups. Both subgroups showed high within-person variability in triggers, and also in sensory modalities through which intrusions were experienced. Participants most often reported emotions, frequently combined with body perceptions and visual imagery (movie-like and fragmented), supporting the conceptualization of intrusions as sensory-perceptual representations (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). In contrast to previous studies, visual images were only the second most frequent sensory modality intrusions were perceived in (Ehlers et al., 2004; Michael et al., 2005). This may reflect methodological differences. Specifically, EMA may reduce memory-related biases such as the picture superiority effect – the tendency for images to be remembered more accurately and vividly than verbal or other non-visual information (Nelson et al., 1976). To accurately interpret the observed variations in intrusion modality distribution, further research on methodological influences of EMA is necessary (Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2009). Taken together, the high within-person variability of intrusion triggers and sensory modalities indicates that situational context may play a greater role in shaping responses to trauma reminders (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Also, reactions to intrusions varied highly within-person. Emotional responses alternated between helplessness, fear, anger, shame, and guilt, consistent with previous EMA findings (e.g. Kleim et al., 2013) and were often accompanied by signs of physiological arousal, such as agitation, tension, racing heart, freezing, trembling, restlessness, and sweating. Physiological arousal in PTSD not only predicts overall symptomatology but is particularly associated with intrusion frequency (Ruggero et al., 2021; Shepherd & Wild, 2014), suggesting that arousal following an intrusion may increase the likelihood of subsequent intrusions. Future research should explore potentially self-reinforcing cascades by investigating the temporal dynamics and associations between physiological and other symptoms and further assess whether physiological reactivity to intrusive memories can serve as a biomarker of heightened vulnerability or risk for chronic symptoms, and whether it can help identify individuals most likely to benefit from targeted interventions.

Effectively coping with intrusions requires regulation of shifting emotional and physiological demands, a task made more challenging by the well-documented emotion regulation difficulties in PTSD (Seligowski et al., 2015). In the current study, participants most frequently used distraction, suppression and rumination to cope with intrusive memories, consistent with previously reported emotion regulation strategies in PTSD (O’Brien et al., 2023). These strategies are generally considered maladaptive given their associations with greater PTSD symptom severity prospectively (e.g. Bardeen et al., 2013), within the same day (e.g. Short et al., 2018), and specific links to higher intrusion frequency and distress (Canty et al., 2024). While the circumstances under which certain strategies become dysfunctional are not always clear, flexibility in their use appears to be critical, underscoring the clinical relevance of day-to-day variability in coping strategies (Bonanno & Burton, 2013). Existing evidence in PTSD samples on limited flexibility in use of coping strategies across occasions (Fine et al., 2021) is extended by our findings, which reveal that even within a single occasion individuals with PTSD tend to rely on one coping strategy per intrusion rather than switching or combining strategies, thereby underscoring limited coping flexibility as a potential maintenance factor in PTSD. Notably, self-harm – although reported by only a small subset of participants – often co-occurred with other maladaptive strategies such as suppression and rumination in both PTSD and cPTSD, aligning with previous research (e.g. Richmond et al., 2017; Voon et al., 2014). Strengthening patients’ ability to flexibly select, combine and apply context-fitting coping strategies - and, for many individuals, first building a broader repertoire of such strategies – in response to intrusions could help mitigate distress and interrupt the negative feedback loop between emotional reactivity, maladaptive coping, and symptom exacerbation (e.g. Schmitz et al., 2025). Future studies should further disentangle the physiological and emotional dynamics in the intrusion sequence, as these processes may hold valuable clinical insights for refining emotion-focused interventions and tailoring treatments to symptom patterns over time.

A comprehensive understanding of the dynamic picture of intrusions also requires consideration of contextual factors. Our MLM results highlight the substantial role of individual differences between participants, with conditional R² values indicating that 30–47% of the variance in intrusion characteristics was accounted for by both fixed and random effects. While the variance explained by fixed effects alone was modest (1–3%), these effects were statistically significant and clinically meaningful.

Specifically, higher rated feelings of safety prior to an intrusion were associated with a reduced sense of loss of control, suggesting potential clinical value of strategies that foster a subjective sense of safety to help buffer the distressing impact of intrusions. Higher self-reported tiredness before an intrusion was associated with greater intrusiveness and fear. Tiredness has been associated with reduced cognitive control, which can impair the suppression of intrusions, which may not only influence their frequency but also intrusiveness and associated fear (Bomyea et al., 2012; Van Schie & Anderson, 2017). Addressing intrusions should therefore go beyond promoting adaptive coping skills to include the management of tiredness, implementation of energy-conservation strategies, and treating potential sleep disturbances.

Pre-intrusion anger emerged as another important factor, showing consistent associations with distress, fear and loss of control, both within the same intrusion entry and in time-lagged analyses. Anger has been repeatedly linked to both development and persistence of PTSD (Olatunji et al., 2010; Sullivan et al., 2018) as well as negative trauma-focused psychotherapy outcome (Speckens et al., 2006). In our study, anger not only predicted intrusion-related distress, fear and loss of control but also emerged as a common emotional response to intrusions, highlighting the need to explicitly address anger and its regulation in trauma-focused treatments. More broadly, associations between pre-intrusion affective states and intrusion characteristics may inform symptom-specific, personalized interventions with affect assessment and regulation as central elements. The emotional complexity surrounding intrusions calls for trauma-focused approaches that cover a wide range of emotions and incorporate strategies for emotional awareness and regulation (Cloitre, 2025). Notably, the lack of significant differences in coping strategies between participants with PTSD and those with cPTSD suggests that treatments aimed at strengthening adaptive coping in PTSD could also provide a solid foundation for addressing cPTSD (Karatzias et al., 2019).

While anger stood out as a key predictor, other potential influences were less evident. Neither substance use (reported in 40% of time-based entries included in these MLMs), social contact quality, nor other affective states were associated with intrusion characteristics in the time-lagged models. Given that the sample excluded individuals with substance use disorders, substance use observed in this study likely reflects non-dependent or subclinical use rather than chronic patterns. Either these predictors may not have that long-lasting temporal trajectories (Piccirillo & Rodebaugh, 2022) or the lack of predictive power may be a consequence of the increased noise between assessments more spread over time, even in highly frequent assessment forms as EMA (Kleindienst et al., 2017). Additional research is needed on the timing of intrusive memories and affective states to better understand their temporal dynamics.

Contrary to our hypothesis intrusive memory characteristics were not associated with PTSD symptom severity. One possible explanation is that these constructs operate at different levels: while symptom severity captures a broader, aggregated clinical phenotype, intrusion features were assessed in daily life, thus reflecting more context-dependent, momentary processes that may not always be linearly related to global symptom burden. Relatedly, we observed variability in situational triggers, emotional states, or regulatory responses that may obscure cross-sectional associations, even if such relationships emerge over time or within individuals. Differences in sample, e.g. trauma type, chronicity, comorbidity, may further contribute to some of the inconsistencies with prior findings. Intrusion frequency, however, was positively correlated with age and depressive symptom severity. Age-related declines in memory suppression (Anderson et al., 2011) or a higher number of traumatic events in older participants (τ = 0.267, p = .009) may increase likelihood of intrusions in older individuals. The association with the depression score might reflect transdiagnostic mechanisms such as reduced cognitive control or rumination (Brewin et al., 2010; Newby & Moulds, 2012). Future research should employ transdiagnostic designs to clarify the mechanisms underlying intrusion sequences across diagnostic categories.

Our study is not without limitations. First, while EMA allows for a fine-grained tracking of inter- and intraindividual fluctuations in intrusive memory sequences (Chun, 2016), it poses some methodological challenges such as reactivity as time-based sampling may increase intrusion frequency (Kleindienst et al., 2017). In our study, we used event-based sampling for intrusions in combination with time-based sampling. Compliance calculated from the time-based entries was 83%, which is exceptionally high for clinical samples (Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2009). Second, while our sample was representative of a clinical PTSD population in terms of symptom burden and comorbidities, we only included participants who experienced at least two intrusions per week, hence our findings might not be representative of (c)PTSD populations with minimal re-experiencing symptoms. Third, our sample is mostly white, female and of a rather young age, generalization of our results must thus be drawn with caution. Fourth, although our sample is relatively large for EMA studies in clinical PTSD populations, the modest variance explained by fixed effects may partly reflect limited statistical power. The substantial conditional R² highlights the importance of modelling individual differences but also underscores the need for future research to identify their sources. Larger clinical samples are needed to clarify the contributions of pre-intrusion states, social contact, and substance use beyond these individual differences, and to ensure that statistical significance is robust. Comparisons between PTSD and cPTSD were exploratory, unbalanced, and relatively small, warranting replication. Nonetheless, this study provides a detailed, preliminary examination of intrusive memory sequences in clinical PTSD and cPTSD, offering a foundation for future research.

Our findings highlight the context-dependent and highly dynamic nature of intrusion sequences in daily lives of individuals with PTSD and cPTSD, as well as their impact on emotional, physiological and cognitive–behavioural processes. Importantly, established first-line PTSD treatments, such as Cognitive Processing Therapy & DBT-PTBS, have consistently been shown to reduce the frequency of intrusive memories (e.g. Schmitz et al., 2025). Building on these effective approaches, more fine-grained understanding of intrusion dynamics may offer opportunities to further optimize these treatments by identifying when, how and for whom intrusions are most likely to occur and escalate, as well as refine psychoeducational resources. In this context, EMA provides a unique opportunity for individuals to gain insight into their own intrusion patterns and may itself hold therapeutic potential, as emerging evidence suggests that monitoring intrusive memories can reduce their frequency (Pollmann et al., 2024). Further, emotional processes emerged as a near constant component of intrusion sequences, suggesting the presence of self-reinforcing cascades that may amplify distress over time. This underscores the importance of interventions that not only reduce overall occurrence of intrusions, but also foster the flexible, context-sensitive regulation of emotional and physiological responses when intrusions occur. Finally, EMA protocols could be extended with ecological momentary interventions targeting intrusion-related affective states, such as anger, with the aim of disrupting dynamic cycles that sustain and exacerbate intrusion-related distress in everyday life (Pavlacic et al., 2025).
