Contrary effects of soil moisture-atmosphere feedback on dry and humid heatwaves
Authors: Sisi Chen, Peng Ji, Shanshui Yuan, Qibo Xu, Chunhui Lu, Jianyun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough soil moisture-atmosphere feedback (SAF) has been widely recognized as a key driver of temperature extremes, its distinct impacts on dry and humid heatwaves remain insufficiently understood, largely due to previous studies focusing predominantly on dry heatwaves alone. In this study, we combine SAF perturbation experiments from CMIP6 with partial differential analysis to construct a global map of SAF’s influence on both humid and dry heatwaves during 1985-2014 and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Unlike the homogeneously amplifying effect of SAF on dry heatwaves, SAF reduces the duration and severity of humid heatwaves by 20-40% in low-to-mid latitude regions with strong soil moisture-atmosphere coupling. In contrast, it increases their duration and severity by ≥50% in high latitude regions. These spatially divergent responses are primarily attributed to the competition between SAF-induced thermal warming and moisture depletion, which respectively raise or lower wet-bulb temperatures. Our findings reveal a dual regulatory mechanism by which SAF influences heat extremes through temperature-humidity coupling, providing scientific insights for region-specific adaptation strategies to both dry and humid heatwaves.Subject terms: Attribution, Hydrology, Atmospheric dynamics

Introduction

Heatwaves are among the most devastating natural hazards, frequently causing crop yield reduction, plant mortality, and economic losses, posing severe threats to both ecosystems and socio-economic development1,2. The deadliest heatwave in this century alone resulted in tens of thousands of deaths3. In the context of global warming, extreme weather events have become increasingly frequent and intense, with extreme high-temperature events showing particularly pronounced intensification4. This has spurred growing scientific interest in the mechanisms driving heatwave evolution.

Anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions are well recognized as the primary global driver of escalating heatwave risks, as the enhanced greenhouse effect raises global temperatures and increases the probability of extreme heat events5. However, one core and significant question is the extent to which land surface moisture and temperature conditions interactively influence the evolution of heat extremes6–9. While the role of land-atmosphere feedback in modulating heat extremes is being established, research has focused predominantly on dry heatwaves, characterized solely by extreme temperature anomalies. In contrast, humid heatwaves, which involve concurrent anomalies in both temperature and humidity, have received relatively less attention, despite their profound societal impacts10. Crucially, humid heatwaves pose a more severe threats to human health than dry ones, as elevated humidity severely impairs thermoregulation, resulting in substantially higher heat-related mortality rates10–12.

Land-atmosphere feedbacks are increasingly recognized as the local intensifiers of hydrometeorological extremes. As soil moisture declines, reduced evapotranspiration shifts surface energy partitioning toward sensible heat, thereby raising near-surface air temperatures. Concurrently, diminished evapotranspiration reduces atmospheric humidity, which further enhances soil drying by increasing evaporative demand. This positive soil moisture-atmosphere feedback (SAF) loop substantially amplifies both magnitude and duration of heatwaves, accounting for 25% to over 50% of historical increases in dry heatwave occurrence6–9,13–15. Under ongoing global warming, the continued depletion of soil moisture is projected to further intensify SAF, thereby aggravating the frequency, duration, and intensity of dry heatwave events6,16–18.

In contrast, the interaction between soil moisture and humid heatwaves is complicated by shifts in energy partitioning between latent and sensible heat fluxes. While soil drying amplifies extreme temperatures through enhanced sensible heating, the associated reduction in evaporation can simultaneously suppress air humidity—potentially mitigating humid heatwave conditions. For example, a recent study demonstrated a negative correlation between soil moisture and dry heatwaves across nearly the entire contiguous United States, in contrast to a positive correlation for humid heatwaves19. Given these opposing effects—where reduced soil moisture both intensifies temperatures (positive feedback) and lowers relative humidity (negative feedback)—the net influence of SAF on humid heatwaves under climate change is substantially more complex than its influence on dry heatwaves. In addition, emerging evidence reveals divergent responses of humid and dry heatwaves to global warming, with significantly different climate sensitivities10,20. A systematic comparative assessment of SAF’s impacts on these two types of heatwaves is therefore imperative to elucidate their distinct physical mechanisms, ultimately informing effective and targeted climate adaptation strategies.

Here, using simulations from the Land Feedback Model Intercomparison Project with prescribed Land Conditions (LFMIP-pdLC) and the historical experiments from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project phase 6 (CMIP6), we present a global-scale assessment of SAF’s role in shaping heatwaves during the historical period. We aim to address two key scientific issues: 1) What is the role of SAF in modulating dry and humid heatwaves? 2) What are the differences in the patterns and the physical mechanisms behind?

Results

Historical changes in heatwaves and the reliability of CMIP6 models

Dry and humid heatwave events are defined as periods of three or more consecutive days during which daily mean dry-bulb or wet-bulb temperatures exceed their respective 90th percentile thresholds21,22 (Methods). The ERA5 reanalysis data reveal coherent spatial patterns in the historical (1951–2014) mean annual total duration (TDUR) and total severity (TSEV) of both dry and humid heatwaves (Supplementary Fig. S1). Specifically, TDUR is substantially longer in tropical and subtropical regions, including eastern Africa, northern Australia, and India, while TSEV is more pronounced in high-latitude areas, including northeastern Canada and Siberia. These spatial patterns are well captured by the multi-model ensemble mean of CMIP6 historical simulations, with spatial correlation coefficients ranging from 0.67 to 0.96 and relative absolute errors between 9% and 17% (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Figure 1 illustrates the changes in TDUR and TSEV of heatwaves during 1985–2014 relative to the baseline period of 1951–1980. The reanalysis dataset reveals a global increase in TDUR for humid heatwaves, with the most pronounced trends observed in tropical regions, including northern South America, central Africa, and the Asian Maritime Continent (Fig. 1a). Similarly, TSEV shows a globally increasing trend, but the largest increases are concentrated in high-latitude regions (60-90°N), followed by the tropics (–20°S-20°N) (Fig. 1b). In comparison to humid heatwaves, dry heatwaves exhibit similar spatial patterns of change, but with a smaller increase in TDUR and a more substantial rise in TSEV. The multi-model ensemble mean of CMIP6 historical simulations demonstrates the capability to reasonably simulate the temporal evolution of TDUR for both humid and dry heatwaves (Fig. 1e, g), with spatial correlation coefficients ranging from 0.66 to 0.77. The simulated changes in TSEV show a spatial correlation of approximately 0.5 with ERA5 reanalysis data, and the global mean relative simulation errors remain below 7% (Fig. 1f, h). The CMIP6 models continue to reasonably capture the changing trends and magnitudes of both heatwave types at regional scales (Supplementary Fig. S2), with the spatial correlation coefficients increasing to 0.60-0.89 and mean relative absolute error predominantly below 27%. However, possibly due to the coarse resolution and biases in surface energy partitioning23, relatively larger simulation errors are found in some coastal and high-latitude regions, such as the Caribbean, northeastern Brazil, and northern Europe (Supplementary Fig. S2). Despite some uncertainties at local scales, the ensemble mean of the CMIP6-ALL experiment exhibits strong capability in reproducing the historical increasing trends and spatial variations of both humid and dry heatwaves during 1985–2014.

SAF-induced changes in humid and dry heatwaves

Figure 2 depicts the influence of SAF on humid heatwave changes during 1985–2014, estimated from the differences between the CMIP6-ALL and CMIP6-pdLC experiments (Methods). SAF substantially reduces the annual total duration and severity of humid heatwaves by 10–20 days and 15-20 K, respectively, across low latitudes (–20°S-20°N), southern mid-latitudes (20–60°S), southwestern North America (20°N-40°N, 90°W-125°W) and central and south Europe (20°N–60°N, 10°W-70°E) (Fig. 2a, b). Compared to the combined influence of other factors (e.g., climate warming, as estimated by the CMIP6-pdLC experiment), SAF’s reduction effect alleviates the increasing trends of humid heatwaves by 10-40%, with particularly pronounced values in Europe, Australia, and North Africa (Fig. 2c, d). In contrast, SAF increases the annual total duration and severity by 5–10 days and 15–20 K, respectively, in northern high-latitudes (60–90°N) regions (e.g., Alaska, northern Asia, and northern Europe), therefore amplifying the increasing trends of humid heatwaves by 10-50%. Above results hold consistent at regional scales across both the 26 IPCC SREX regions (Supplementary Fig. S3) and latitudinally defined zones (low-, mid-, and high-latitudes) (Supplementary Fig. S4). The opposing effects of SAF in low-to-mid latitudes and high latitudes also contribute to a smoothing of its global-scale impacts on humid heatwave changes, as the global mean impacts of SAF on TDUR and TSEV are amount to only -0.82 days and -0.26 K, respectively. This indicates that SAF plays a critical role in shaping the spatial distributions of humid heatwave changes, rather than substantially altering the global mean magnitude.

In contrast, SAF generally enhances the duration and severity of dry heatwaves (Supplementary Fig. S5a, b). The impact of SAF exceeds 1.5 times of the combined influence of other factors over subtropical regions, suggesting that SAF is the dominant driver of dry heatwave changes (Supplementary Fig. S5c, d). Previous studies have also shown that SAF increases the risk of global warming and extreme temperatures globally and they attributed SAF’s strong influence in subtropical regions to the strong coupling strength between soil moisture and the atmosphere6,8,9.

Here we defined the SAF coupling index as the correlation between monthly soil moisture and surface specific humidity multiplied by the standard deviation of surface specific humidity (Methods)24. Therefore, a higher coupling index suggests a stronger change in surface specific humidity induced by a unit change in soil moisture. Hotspots of strong SAF coupling are identified in South Asia, Southeast Asia, central North America, eastern South America, northern and southern Africa, and Australia (Fig. 3a). These patterns are consistent across seasons with those in ERA5 reanalysis when using the same coupling index (Supplementary Figs. S6, S7), and align with previous studies using other coupling indices25,26. The spatial variation in SAF’s effects on humid and dry heatwaves across 26 IPCC SREX regions is found to be significantly correlated with the coupling index. Specifically, SAF tends to increase the annual total duration and severity of humid heatwaves in regions where the coupling index is low (<0.2), but decrease them as the index increase beyond 0.4. In contrast, the stronger the coupling index, the greater the SAF-induced increase in total duration and severity of dry heatwaves.

A reduction in soil moisture, often initiated by precipitation deficits, can efficiently suppress evaporative cooling, leading to higher surface temperatures and enhanced atmospheric heating8,9. This initiates a positive feedback loop, in which drier soils further intensify heatwave conditions by increasing sensible heat flux and reducing latent heat flux. Additionally, reduced soil moisture inhibits cloud formation and precipitation, thereby exacerbating the persistence and intensity of dry heatwaves6. These mechanisms collectively explain why soil moisture-atmosphere coupling tends to increase both the duration and severity of dry heatwaves, particularly in regions where evaporation is sensitive to soil moisture deficits. However, they fail to explain the effect of SAF on the humid heatwave changes, potentially because the wet-bulb temperature reflects both thermal and moisture conditions.

Mechanisms for the impact of SAF on humid and dry heatwave changes

We recalculated the duration and severity of heatwaves in the CMIP6-ALL simulations after removing SAF-induced mean-state changes in daily wet-bulb temperature (Methods), in order to quantify the influences of SAF-driven shifts in mean wet-bulb temperature against changes in wet-bulb temperature variability. Figure 4a, b demonstrate that heatwave changes attributable to adjustments in mean wet-bulb temperature closely resemble the patterns observed in Fig. 2a, b. In contrast, changes in wet-bulb temperature variability generally increase the duration or severity of humid heatwaves across the tropical and subtropical regions (Fig. 4c, d). The SAF-driven shifts in mean daily wet-bulb temperature during 1985-2014 also closely aligns with SAF-induced changes in TDUR and TSEV of humid heatwaves with spatial correlation coefficients ranging from 0.6 to 0.75. These findings suggest that, SAF spatial varying effect on humid heatwaves is primarily attributed to alterations in the mean state of wet-bulb temperature, rather than by altering its variability. Similarly, for dry heatwaves, SAF influences primarily through shifts in the mean state of dry-bulb temperature, though both mean-state and variability changes act in concert to increase heatwave duration and intensity globally (Supplementary Fig. S8), resulting in a net positive influence of SAF observed in Supplementary Fig. S5.

We further evaluated the role of the mean state by comparing daily temperature distributions between the ALL and LFMIP-pdLC experiments (Supplementary Note 1). The spatial structure of SAF-induced changes in the probability of hot humid days and hot dry days closely consistent with SAF’s impacts changes in actual heatwaves (spatial correlation coefficients = 0.78–0.87; Figs. S9a, b, 2a, b, and S5a, b). A statistical attribution shows that shifts in the location parameter (mean) explain >70% of this change in most regions, robust across models (Supplementary Figs. S9c, d, g, h), while the scale parameter (variability) plays a minor role (Supplementary Fig. S9e, f). These results independently corroborate that SAF exerts distinct influences on dry and humid heatwaves primarily via its modification of the mean states of dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperature, respectively. Therefore, the divergent responses can be mechanistically understood and are directly linked to SAF’s modulation of these mean conditions.

SAF-induced dry-bulb warming is typically attributed to the “warmer climate-drier soil” feedback whereby climate warming exacerbates the precipitation-evapotranspiration deficit, leading to soil drying that further amplifies warming via increased sensible heat (and consequently reduced evapotranspiration) and surface solar radiation6,9,27,28. However, this climatological-scale feedback alone cannot fully account for the widespread increasing of dry heatwave, as pronounced soil moisture declines are largely confined to subtropical regions during 1985-2014 (Supplementary Fig. S10). Crucially, at subseasonal to interannual scales, soil moisture variability in response to positive temperature anomalies can also trigger a “warmer-drier” feedback29. This feedback exacerbates negative anomalies in soil moisture and latent heat flux while concurrently amplifying the positive anomalies in sensible heat flux (Fig. 5a, c, e), thereby explaining the stronger temperature anomaly observed when SAF is accounted for (Fig. 5g). Similar patterns are observed for dry spells (Supplementary Fig. S11). In contrast, during cold days, although soil moisture variability tends to produce positive soil moisture anomalies, its impact on surface energy fluxes is negligible (Fig. 5b, d, f, h), likely because evapotranspiration is energy-limited during these periods. This asymmetric impact of soil moisture variability between warm and cold periods results in a net positive effect on dry-bulb temperature. Moreover, the amplitude of soil moisture anomalies driven by variability generally exceeds that associated with the long-term trend (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. S10). Consequently, even in regions where soil moisture exhibits a wetting trend over time, the warming effect induced by soil moisture variability can dominate over any cooling tendency arising from the trend itself, thereby still contributing to net warming of dry-bulb temperature.

The influence of SAF on wet-bulb temperature shifts is a combined effect of SAF-induced changes in dry-bulb temperature and relative humidity. We used the SAF-induced changes in dry-bulb temperature and relative humidity at climatological scales (Fig. 6b, c) to estimate mean SAF-induced changes in daily wet-bulb temperature based on a partial difference function (Methods). Although some uncertainties remain due to the nonlinear relationship between wet-bulb temperature and dry-bulb temperature (and relative humidity), the estimation method demonstrates robust skill in reproducing the sign of SAF-induced wet-bulb temperature changes, capturing spatial agreement with reference values across 85% of global land areas for the multi-model ensemble mean (Fig. 6a vs. 6d). Importantly, this capability extends to individual CMIP6 models, with 88–95% of global land grids showing consistent sign agreement between estimated and simulated anomalies (Supplementary Fig. S12). The estimation process is visually illustrated in the isopleths diagram of wet-bulb temperature (Fig. 6e). For example, in the regions such as western Australia and Central Europe, the positive effect of SAF-induced dry-bulb temperature warming on wet-bulb temperature is weaker than the negative effect of SAF-induced drying in relative humidity, resulting in a decrease in wet-bulb temperature (from the red circle to the yellow triangle in Fig. 6e). In high latitude regions, like northern Asia, SAF does not cause strong drying in relative humidity, therefore, the dry-bulb temperature warming dominates SAF’s influence on wet-bulb temperature.

The SAF-driven warming will reduce relative humidity under constant water-vapor pressure according to the Clausius-Clapeyron relation (Supplementary Fig. S13a). Across mid- to low-latitude regions, the pronounced SAF-induced suppression of evapotranspiration depletes near-surface moisture supply (Supplementary Fig. S14a), causing declining trends in specific humidity (Supplementary Fig. S14b) and amplifying thermodynamic drying (Supplementary Fig. S13b). The combined effect of elevated temperature and reduced moisture drives a marked decline in relative humidity (Fig. 6c). Critically, in these regions the drying influence of SAF outweighs its warming effect, leading to lower wet-bulb temperatures and a corresponding decrease in humid heatwaves. By contrast, in high-latitude regions where evapotranspiration is energy-limited, atmospheric warming can stimulate additional evapotranspiration that partly counteracts the thermodynamic drying from higher temperatures (Supplementary Fig. S13b). Here, the warming effect of SAF dominates, producing a net increase in wet-bulb temperature and contributing to more frequent humid heatwaves.

Discussion

Although SAF is well known to amplify dry heatwaves, our results reveal its regionally dependent influence on humid heatwaves with suppression of their frequency and severity in low-to-mid latitudes but intensification in high-latitude regions. This spatially heterogeneous effect primarily results from SAF’s modulation of the mean wet-bulb temperature, rather than its variability. In low-to-mid latitude regions with strong soil moisture-atmosphere coupling, SAF’s cooling effect on wet-bulb temperature (mediated through reduced surface relative humidity) dominates its concurrent warming effect (via temperature increase). The opposite occurs at high latitudes, where SAF’s warming effect dominates over cooling.

The influence of SAF on terrestrial thermal and hydrological processes has become a central focus of climate research focus in recent years7,30–32. While previous studies have primarily examined its role in dry heatwaves, this study extends the literature by quantifying SAF’s effects on humid heatwaves, which involve the coupling of both temperature and humidity and more directly linked to human thermoregulation and health outcomes. Our findings deepen the mechanistic understanding of SAF’s dual role in extreme heat events via temperature-humidity coupling, and highlight the need for refined climate adaptation strategies that consider the latitude-dependent impacts of SAF on humid and dry heatwaves.

Although this study focuses on the annual total duration and severity of the humid heatwaves, the spatially variable effects of SAF are observed across seasons (Supplementary Fig. S15). In addition, the results remain robust when using a more complex algorithm for estimating wet-bulb temperature33, or removing the seasonal cycle of temperatures before calculating the 90th percentile34, or applying a 95th percentile threshold for identifying humid heatwaves (Supplementary Fig. S16). Results based on alternative heat stress metrics, including the discomfort index, Oxford index, and equivalent temperature, closely align with those from the wet-bulb temperature-based index (Supplementary Note 2 and Figs. S17a–f). Conversely, metrics that assign greater weight to dry-bulb temperature, such as the heat index35, exhibit spatial patterns that increasingly resemble those of dry-bulb temperature as their sensitivity to temperature increases (Supplementary Figs. S17g, h). These systematic contrasts across metrics reveal that changes in humid heatwaves are governed by the competitive interplay between SAF-induced warming and drying effects.

Our approach to decomposing heatwave changes into mean shifts and variability changes of temperature follows an additive assumption commonly adopted in previous studies36–38. While this assumption enables a tractable separation of the two components, we acknowledge that it simplifies potential nonlinear interactions between mean and variability under sustained warming. Recent work suggests that changes in the temperature mean state may also modulate temperature variability in a warming future39, which could introduce uncertainty in the exact attribution of extremes. Nevertheless, changes in temperature variability in historical period are much smaller that in mean state40, suggesting their interaction can not strong enough to influence the robustness of the role of mean state changes. Additionally, we tested the sensitivity of our results to the heatwave definition threshold (e.g., 95th versus 99th percentiles) and the method used to estimate warming signals (e.g., linear versus third-order polynomials fittings), finding the result remains robust across these methodological choices (Supplementary Fig. S18).

It should be clarified that the primary focus of our study is the climatological-scale effect of SAF on humid heatwaves. At this scale, SAF substantially reduces the mean wet-bulb temperature across low-latitude and subtropical regions, leading to an overall suppression of humid heatwave occurrence (Fig. 4). This climatological cooling effect has been largely overlooked in previous studies, which instead emphasize the event-scale role of SAF in triggering humid heatwaves through enhanced evapotranspiration following precipitation in strongly coupled regions41–43. Importantly, these event-based findings are not contradictory to our results. As shown in Fig. 4c, when the mean wet-bulb temperature is held constant, SAF enhances wet-bulb temperature variability, resulting in more frequent humid heatwave days, particularly over India and equatorial Africa. However, our results further demonstrate that, at the climatological scale, the suppressing effect of SAF on the mean state of wet-bulb temperature outweighs its enhancing effect on variability, thereby reducing humid heatwave occurrence overall. This implies that, although individual humid heatwaves can still be promoted by event-scale wet anomalies in precipitation and soil moisture, the background atmospheric drying induced by SAF raises the threshold for their initiation. How such climatological shifts in mean wet-bulb temperature interact with event-scale triggering mechanisms of SAF warrants further investigation.

We also acknowledge that quantitative estimates of SAF’s role are subject to model structural uncertainties. For example, considerable inter-model spread persists in certain high-latitude and coastal regions (especially northern Canada and northern Europe), where the sign and magnitude of SAF-induced changes vary significantly across the ensemble. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in model physics, resolution, and the representation of land-atmosphere coupling processes over complex terrain or under snow-influenced regimes. Moreover, uncertainties in physical parameterizations, particularly for convection and boundary-layer processes, may introduce coherent biases in simulated heat and moisture budgets44. This issue is especially pronounced in the wet tropics, where observational constraints on land-atmosphere interactions remain sparse, and models exhibit large uncertainties. Nevertheless, the overall direction and spatial pattern of SAF’s impact on humid heatwaves are credible for several reasons. First, CMIP6 models reproduce SAF-related processes reasonably well, as seen in our evaluation (Supplementary Figs. S6, S7) and in prior literature6,45. Second, the spatial structure of SAF’s effect on humid heatwaves is consistent across models despite differing physics, pointing to a physical signal rather than a model artifact (Supplementary Fig. S6). Third, our conclusions are robust to varying heatwave definitions and analytical approaches (Supplementary Figs. S16, S17, S18). To further constrain remaining uncertainties, future work should combine multi-model, high-resolution, convection-permitting simulation with expanded observational networks, especially in data-sparse regions where land-atmosphere interactions are critical.

The SAF inherently integrates multiple co-varying factors over the historical period, including internal climate variability, climate change, and land-cover changes. For instance, the intensity and pattern of SAF may themselves evolve under a warming climate6,15, while land-cover modifications can alter surface drying and locally enhance or weaken coupling strength46. However, SAF can in turn modulate regional climate change and influence vegetation and land-surface conditions, forming a tightly coupled system. Isolating the specific contribution of any single factor (e.g., climate change versus vegetation dynamics) to the overall SAF signal lies beyond the scope of the present study and would require carefully designed sensitivity experiments and novel methodological frameworks.

Methods

Data

The fifth-generation ECMWF reanalysis (ERA5) is used as the reference dataset due to its good performance and assimilation of extensive station and satellite observations worldwide47. The original spatial resolution of the ERA5 dataset is 0.25°×0.25°, and it was remapped to 1°×1° by bilinear interpolation. Hourly relative humidity in ERA5 was calculated from the hourly near-surface temperature, surface pressure, and specific humidity48. Daily mean temperature and relative humidity were computed from the hourly values.

Daily mean temperature and relative humidity from the CMIP6 historical (ALL) experiment during 1951-2014 and the LFMIP-pdLC experiment during 1980-2014 were collected. The CMIP6 ALL experiment includes all external forcings and simulates the real-world climate system, making it suitable for comparison with observations49. The LFMIP-pdLC experiment begins in 1980 with the initial conditions from the ALL experiment in the same year50. In addition, LFMIP-pdLC experiment shares the same settings with ALL experiment except that the soil moisture was prescribed to a climatological mean state during 1980-2014. Five CMIP6 models, including CNRM-CM6-1, EC-Earth3, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC6, and MPI-ESM1-2-LR, were selected because they provide daily outputs for both experiments. We used the r1i1p1f1 realization consistently across all CMIP6 models, except for CNRM-CM6-1, for which r1i1p1f2 was used due to the unavailability of r1i1p1f1.

Definition of dry and humid heatwaves

There is no universal definition of a heatwave, but most operational definitions characterize it as a period of abnormally high temperatures relative to the local climate and time of year, often based on percentile thresholds (e.g., exceeding the 90th percentile)53. Such a relative threshold-based method is more generalizable to large regional scales or different climate divisions, compared to the absolute threshold approach54,55. A dry hot day was defined when the daily mean dry-bulb temperature exceeds its corresponding 90th percentile, while a dry heatwave event was identified when at least three consecutive dry hot days occurred. The 90th percentile thresholds were calculated using a 15-day moving window centered on each calendar day across the 1951–1980 baseline period, yielding 450 reference values (15 days × 30 years) for each temperature metric. Heatwave duration refers to the total days in an event, while severity is calculated as the accumulated temperature exceedance above daily thresholds during the event period. Mean annual total duration and total severity represent the accumulated duration and severity of all the heatwave events within a given year.

The definition of humid heatwave events follows that of dry heatwaves, except that wet-bulb temperature, a key indicator of humid heat stress, is used in place of air temperature10,20,56.

Quantifying the SAF’s impact on historical heatwave changes

For a given heatwave metric M (e.g. annual total duration), let M¯ALLbl and M¯ALLhist denote its mean values in the ALL experiment during the baseline (1951-1980) and historical (1985-2014) periods, respectively, and let M¯pdLChist represent the corresponding mean in the LFMIP-pdLC experiment during 1985-2014. The total observed change in the historical period is therefore ΔMtotal=M¯ALLhist−M¯ALLbl, which includes the combined influence of all factors (e.g., external forcing, internal variability, and SAF). The change in the absence of SAF is ΔMnoSAF=M¯pdLChist−M¯ALLbl. Hence, the effect of SAF is defined as ΔMSAF=ΔMtotal−ΔMnoSAF, and its relative contribution is calculated as (ΔMSAF/ΔMnoSAF)×100%.

Because both the sign and magnitude of changes vary across models, ΔMSAF and its relative contribution are first computed for each individual model, and the multi-model ensemble mean is then derived from those individual estimates. A signal is considered statistically significant when at least four of the five models agree on the sign of the ensemble-mean result6,31,45.

SAF impacts on heatwaves: mean state vs. variability effects

The distinct heatwave characteristics between the ALL and LFMIP-pdLC experiments (1980-2014) stem from SAF-induced modifications in both temperature the mean state (warming or cooling) and its variability. Following previous research5, we fitted separate second-order polynomials to the monthly mean temperature series (1981–2014) from the ALL and LFMIP-pdLC experiments at each grid cell to capture the warming/cooling signal. Their difference, which varies by year and month, is considered as the SAF-induced component of warming/cooling and was subtracted from the daily temperature record of the ALL experiment. The different heatwave metrics between this adjusted ALL series and the original LFMIP-pdLC experiment, reflects SAF-induced variability effects, encompassing interannual, seasonal, intraseasonal, and diurnal components. Consequently, the residual component of SAF-induced heatwave changes represents the effect of mean temperature shifts. Similar methods have been widely used to examine the relative roles of mean shifts versus variability changes climate extremes36–38,57,58, although approaches to estimating the mean shifts vary (e.g., using polynomial fittings of different orders).
