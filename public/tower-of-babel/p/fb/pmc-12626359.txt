Decoding Multiple Keloids: A Gateway to Diagnosing Rubinstein-Taybi Syndrome
Authors: Neha C. Jangid, Ramesh Poriya
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome (RSTS; MIM 180849) is a rare disorder characterized by a range of congenital anomalies, including broad thumbs and halluces, facial dysmorphism, short stature and varying degrees of intellectual disability. Mutations in the CREBBP and EP300 genes have been identified in 50–60% and 5–8% of cases, respectively.[1] Many cutaneous abnormalities are associated with RSTS, and multiple spontaneous keloids are one of them.

A 16-year-old female, born of a non-consanguineous marriage, presented with multiple raised lesions over the trunk for 3 years. The lesions were gradually progressive and appeared spontaneously without any history of preceding trauma, pustular lesions, injections, or burns. There was no similar history in any of the family members. On cutaneous examination, there were multiple (more than 10) discrete keloidal nodules present over her shoulders, upper back and chest ranging in size from 1 cm2 to 6 cm2 along with keratosis pilaris [Figure 1]. The patient had distinct facial features [Figure 2a] along with broad short halluces and broad thumbs with preaxial (radial) polydactyly [Figure 2b]. On general physical examination she was obese, had short stature and scoliosis of cervicodorsal spine with convexity towards right side. The patient had a high refractive error in both eyes, for which she wore high power spectacles. Her birth history was uneventful with full term normal vaginal delivery, but at age of 3 months, she was diagnosed with left hydroureteronephrosis, left ureterocele, and right ectopic kidney, for which cystoscopy and fulguration were performed. Her MRI spine revealed segmentation anomaly with block vertebrae at the C6 to D6 level, with partial sacrococcygeal agenesis. A low lying cord ending at the L3 vertebral level and mild disc bulges in the cervical spine were reported. The rest of the neurological, audiological, intraoral and cardiological examinations, including a 2D-echocardiogram were normal. Skin biopsy from a lesion on the upper back revealed increased number of thick collagen fibers arranged in bundles in the reticular dermis, which was consistent with the histopathology of a keloid [Figure 3]. Genetic analysis could not be done in this patient due to lack of financial resources. Based on the history, clinical examination, and investigation, a diagnosis of RSTS was made.

Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome (RSTS) is a rare syndrome, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 100,000 to 125,000 live births. Mutations in either cyclic AMP–regulated enhancer binding protein (CREBBP) in chromosomal region 16p13.3 [Type 1] or E1A binding protein p300 (EP300) on chromosome 22 [Type 2], have been suggested as causes of RSTS.[2] The gene functions as a transcriptional co-activator and plays roles in the regulation of cell growth, cellular differentiation, tumor suppression, and long-term memory.[3] This condition has a variable degree of phenotypic expression, and almost all mutations occur de novo, with only a few cases of autosomal-dominant transmission.[1] RSTS was first described by Michail et al. in 1957, and later a case series of seven cases was reported by Rubinstein and Taybi in 1963. It is generally characterized by specific physical characteristics, including broad thumbs, broad halluces, dysmorphic facial features, and short stature. Additional features can include eye abnormalities, heart and kidney defects, dental problems and obesity [Table 1]. Dermatological manifestations most frequently described include hirsutism and capillary malformations. Other unusual manifestations encountered in RSTS include keloids, thick and high-arched eyebrows, abnormal dermatoglyphics, supernumerary nipples, keratosis pilaris, and atopic eczema.[4] The frequency of keloid formation in RSTS is not well known, with estimates varying from 5% to 57%.[5] Spontaneous multiple keloids are also reported in the Goeminne syndrome, which is characterized by torticollis, cryptorchidism, renal dysplasia, and multiple nevi. In addition to RSTS, keloids are also described in Pachydermoperiostosis, Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, Turner’s syndrome, and Noonan’s syndrome.[6] Diagnosis of RSTS is usually clinical and mainly depends on the presence of the classical features of the syndrome. Treatment is mainly supportive, tailored to the problem of patient’s specific issues, and requires a multidisciplinary approach. Outcomes of treating multiple keloids are frequently less than ideal.

Characteristics of Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome

We report a case of 16-year-old female of Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome with broad thumb, polydactyly, presence of multiple keloids, keratosis pilaris, distinctive facial features, renal abnormalities and scoliotic deformity. There is a paucity of Indian literature describing this entity, and diagnosis is often delayed due to non-familiarity with the classical features of the syndrome. Our patient differs from the classically described cases of RSTS in terms of horizontal palpebral fissures compared to the typical antimongoloid slant, normal intellect compared to poor intellect with mental retardation, and the presence of multiple keloids, which is an unusual and generally unrecognized minor characteristic of Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome. Hence, in any patient presenting with spontaneous multiple keloids possibility of RSTS should be kept in mind.
