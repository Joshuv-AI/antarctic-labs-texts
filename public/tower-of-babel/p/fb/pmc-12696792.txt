Low‐Surface‐Energy Capped Hydrogel Micropillar Arrays for Transparent Superhydrophobic Antifogging Surfaces
Authors: Hyeongjeong Kim, Hyomin Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransparent substrates widely used in applications such as optical displays, solar cells, and medical devices are highly susceptible to fogging and contamination, often degrading their visual clarity. While various antifogging approaches have been explored, achieving sustained transparency across diverse environments through a simple and scalable design remains a critical challenge in surface engineering. Herein, a structurally simple, low‐surface‐energy polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) capped hydrogel micropillar arrays comprising poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA) are introduced to achieve transparent superhydrophobic antifogging surfaces. The exposed sidewalls of hydrogel micropillars effectively absorb water vapor, while the low‐surface‐energy caps on the top offer superhydrophobicity with a high water contact angle (>160°). It is demonstrated that this design effectively prevents fogging and exhibits self‐cleaning properties to maintain high optical transparency (>84%), offering a scalable and effective solution for transparent substrates operating in diverse optical and environmental applications.Keywords: antifogging, hydrogel, optical transparency, self‐cleaning, superhydrophobic

Introduction

Transparent substrates are essential components in a wide range of optical applications, including automotive sensors, optical displays, solar cells, and medical devices. However, their optical performance is often compromised by environmental factors such as fogging[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] and surface contamination.[ 6 ] In particular, fogging occurs when water vapor condenses on a cold surface, forming discrete droplets that scatter incident light and degrade visual clarity.[ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ] These issues can severely reduce device functionality, highlighting the need for multifunctional surface coatings that combine optical transparency, antifogging, and self‐cleaning.

Conventional antifogging strategies typically utilize hydrophilic materials, which promote filmwise condensation of water vapor.[ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] While the formation of a water film instead of droplets on the surface minimizes light scattering and preserves optical transparency, many conventional hydrophilic surfaces suffer from water‐holding capacity and tend to distort or haze under prolonged exposure.[ 18 , 19 ] Alternatively, hydrogels have attracted attention as effective antifogging materials due to their surface hydrophilicity as well as hygroscopicity, the ability to effectively absorb water vapor.[ 20 , 21 , 22 ] However, when applied as continuous films on rigid substrates like glass, hydrogels are prone to delamination due to volumetric swelling mismatch under wet conditions.[ 23 ] Moreover, their high‐surface‐energy often leads to surface contamination that is difficult to remove, compromising the long‐term optical clarity.[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 ]

To address these limitations, superhydrophobic (often referred to as dry‐style antifogging) surfaces have been explored.[ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] These designs incorporate low‐surface‐energy materials and micro/nanostructured roughness to repel water droplets in a Cassie–Baxter state. The resulting high water contact angle and low hysteresis enable effective removal of condensed water droplets as well as contaminants through the self‐cleaning mechanism. Nevertheless, such dry‐style antifogging surfaces often fail during the early stages of condensation, as scattered sub‐micron fog droplets remain pinned on the surface, degrading the optical clarity until coalescence occurs.[ 32 , 33 , 34 ]

Recently, hybrid strategies known as wet‐style superhydrophobic antifogging surfaces have emerged to overcome these limitations.[ 35 ] This design synergistically combines hydrophilic materials capable of absorbing water vapor with superhydrophobic surface features for water droplet repellency. For instance, a multilayer coating consisting of perfluoropolyether (PFPE) micropillars combined with a polymer‐silica nanocomposite was reported, achieving both antifogging and self‐cleaning functionalities through a complex Layer‐by‐Layer (LbL) and two‐step lithography process. In a separate work, a monolithic, flexible antifogging tapes were reported where inkjet‐printing was employed to pattern poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA) hydrogels in between polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) micropillars to impart enhanced mechanical robustness as well as fog resistance even under mechanical deformation.[ 36 ] However, both designs require multistep fabrication procedures, including two‐step lithography or inkjet printing of hydrogel patterns into microhole arrays, which limits scalability and optical clarity due to structural complexity.

In this work, we introduce a simple yet effective strategy for preparing wet‐style superhydrophobic antifogging surfaces with enhanced optical transparency. This design is based on regularly patterned hydrogel micropillar arrays directly fabricated on a 3‐(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (TMSPMA)‐treated glass substrate using a single‐step ultraviolet (UV) lithography process. Here, PEGDA was selected as the hydrogel precursor for its UV‐photopatterning compatibility, high hygroscopicity supporting vapor uptake, and well‐established biocompatibility. Subsequently, a thin low‐surface‐energy PDMS (19–21 mJ m−2)[ 37 ] is selectively transferred to the top of each pillar. The sidewalls of the hydrogel micropillar array serve as hygroscopic reservoirs, effectively absorbing water vapor and suppressing fog formation, while the low‐surface‐energy PDMS caps on the top allow water droplets to remain in the Cassie–Baxter state to exhibit superhydrophobicity as well as self‐cleaning property. We demonstrate that these discrete hydrogel micropillars more effectively dissipate swelling‐induced stress in water compared to a continuous hydrogel film, while simultaneously functioning as a vapor‐absorbing array, with each pillar acting as a localized sink that draws in surrounding moisture. The periodic micropillar arrangement with optimal diameter minimizes optical scattering, maintaining transmittance above 84% in the visible range (400 nm≤ λ≤ 700 nm). As our strategy synergistically combines the absorption‐based antifogging mechanism of hydrogels with the repellency of low‐surface‐energy caps in a structurally simple format, we envision that it offers a simple and scalable approach to prepare transparent superhydrophobic antifogging surfaces for diverse optical and environmental applications.

Results and Discussion

Design and Optimization of the Hydrogel Micropillar Arrays

To achieve transparent antifogging surfaces under humid conditions, a hydrogel micropillar array composed of PEGDA was designed and fabricated, as schematically illustrated in Figure 1a. Here, each hydrogel pillar functions as a hygroscopic reservoir that locally absorbs water vapor into the hydrogel matrix, thereby suppressing the nucleation and growth of discrete water droplets on the surface that can scatter incident light. This columnar geometry was selected because it maximizes sidewall exposure for vapor uptake and allows straightforward one‐step UV photopatterning.

D and P each refer to pillar diameter and pitch, respectively. Scale bar represents 100 µm. c) Schematic illustration of the effect of pillar diameter on condensation behavior, as well as the high magnification, top‐view optical microscope image of hydrogel micropillar array (D/P = 108) under fogging conditions. The micropillar arrays were fabricated with a fixed pitch (P) of 250 µm. Upper‐row images were obtained under ambient conditions (26 ± 0.5 °C, 60 ± 3% relative humidity (RH)). Lower‐row images were taken 10 s after transfer from a −19 °C freezer to ambient conditions. All scale bars represent 100 µm.

To verify whether the individual hydrogel micropillars can effectively absorb water vapor to suppress nucleation and growth of surface‐induced water droplets under humid conditions, we first fabricated a hydrogel micropillar array with diameter (D) of 27 µm, center‐to‐center spacing (pitch, P) of 250, and 120 µm height using a single‐step UV lithography process, as shown in the side‐view electron micrograph of Figure 1b. We find that placing the hydrogel micropillar array (D/P = 0.108) in a −19 °C freezer for 2 h, followed by exposure to ambient conditions (26 ± 0.5 °C, 60 ± 3% relative humidity (RH)), also known as the cold‐fog transition test,[ 35 , 36 ] leads to the formation of a distinct vapor depletion zone, radially extending from the center of each pillar, where the surface remains devoid of microdroplets as shown in Figure 1c. This central region indicates that water absorption into the hydrogel micropillars is dominant, while the surrounding area is characterized by full coverage of discrete water droplets, indicative of prevailing heterogeneous nucleation and growth on the surface. This clear spatial contrast underscores a competitive interplay between absorption and nucleation‐driven condensation, governed by local water vapor transport dynamics.

To determine whether pillar geometry affects the extent of the depletion zone, we prepared sets of hydrogel micropillar arrays with varying diameters (D = 27, 43, and 67 µm), while keeping the pitch and height constant at 250 and 120 µm, respectively. A continuous hydrogel film with the same composition and thickness was also prepared as a control for visual comparison. Top‐view optical micrographs of these surfaces under ambient conditions confirm the uniformity of both the hydrogel film and the micropillar arrays and show an increasing solid fraction with increasing pillar diameter (Figure 1d). Similarly, the influence of pillar diameter on the condensation behavior was examined by undergoing the identical cold‐fog transition test. As the control hydrogel film uniformly covers the entire glass substrate, it effectively absorbs water vapor without forming any microdroplets, indicating efficient water vapor absorption under fogging conditions. However, optical micrographs under fogging conditions for micropillar arrays clearly show a diameter‐dependent condensation behavior. Depletion zone increases as the D / P ratio increases from 0.108, 0.172, to 0.268, respectively. Notably, for the 67 µm micropillars (D/P = 0.268), the array remained clear visibility throughout the entire sample without any tiny droplet formation to exhibit antifogging property (Figure 1d; Movies S1–S3, Supporting Information). This trend is attributed to the increase and overlap of the vapor depletion zone radius surrounding each hydrogel micropillar. As the pillar diameter increases, the individual depletion zones expand laterally, leading to greater spatial overlap, and this overlap enhances the uniformity and rate of vapor absorption, reducing the possibility for water nucleation to occur in the inter‐pillar regions. In contrast, smaller pillars offer limited vapor absorption area and generate isolated depletion zones, allowing residual water vapor to condense and form light‐scattering droplets in the interstitial regions. To additionally assess the influence of ambient humidity, we compared cold‐fog transitions under identical pre‐cooling (−19 °C, 2 h) followed by exposure to a less humid environment (29 ± 0.5 °C, 37 ± 3% RH). Under this drier condition, lateral overlap of depletion zones started to appear at a smaller threshold (D/P = 0.128), whereas under the more‐humid condition (26 ± 0.5 °C, 60 ± 3% RH), the onset shifted to a larger diameter (D/P = 0.172), as shown in Figure 1d and Figure S1a (Supporting Information). For the same pillar diameter of 32 µm (D/P = 0.128), the humid case produced isolated depletion zones that allowed condensation in the interpillar regions, while the drier case exhibited partial overlap with an expanded depletion‐zone radius (Figure S1b, Supporting Information). These results indicate that higher humidity reduces the depletion‐zone radius, requiring larger pillar diameters for overlap, whereas lower humidity increases the radius, allowing overlap at smaller diameters and thereby supporting improved antifogging performance (Movies S4–S7, Supporting Information).

To further evaluate whether micropillar height significantly influences the extent of the vapor depletion zone, we fabricated a hydrogel micropillar array with a fixed diameter of 40 µm and pitch of 250 µm but different heights of 40 and 120 µm, representing a 3 fold difference in height, on a single substrate (Figure S2a,b, Supporting Information). Following the cold‐fog transition test, both configurations showed comparable depletion zone profiles, as observed from the fogging pattern (Figure S2c, Supporting Information). This result suggests that, under the current geometrical and environmental conditions, the height of the hydrogel micropillars plays a negligible role in vapor absorption dynamics, further highlighting that lateral vapor transport and depletion zone overlap, which are primarily governed by micropillar diameter and spacing, are the dominant factors in fog suppression. As a result, maximizing the antifogging performance requires tuning the pillar diameter to provide sufficient overlap of the vapor depletion zones to achieve complete coverage of the glass substrate. However, it should be noted that increasing the pillar diameter inherently reduces optical transmittance due to a higher solid fraction (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Thus, while larger pillars enhance antifogging performance by promoting overlap of the depletion zones, this improvement comes at the expense of optical clarity. Taking this trade‐off into account, the pillar array with a diameter of 67 µm (D/P = 0.268) was identified as the optimal design, as it most effectively suppressed fog formation while retaining the highest level of transparency among the pillar geometries.

Mechanical Stability of the Hydrogel Micropillar Array

Although the hydrogel micropillar array with a diameter of 67 µm (D/P = 0.268) exhibits the highest level of transparency among the tested pillar structures and effectively prevents fog formation through efficient water vapor absorption, repeated exposure to humid conditions induces substantial swelling‐related stress, which may eventually lead to adhesion failure. In fact, mechanical stability is essential for the practical implementation of functional surface coatings, especially those exposed to humid or submerged environments. To ensure strong bonding between the hydrogel micropillars and the glass substrate, we pretreated the glass substrate with TMSPMA, a silane coupling agent that introduces methacrylate groups on the glass surface for subsequent covalent bonding with the acrylate termini of hydrogel precursor, PEGDA, during UV polymerization, as schematically illustrated in Figure 2a. Indeed, a comparison of the adhesion strength of PEGDA hydrogel films on untreated and TMSPMA‐treated glass revealed a ≈3.8 fold increase, from 356 to 1354 kPa, thereby validating the effectiveness of this treatment (Figure S4, Supporting Information).

TMSPMA functionalization promotes covalent bonding between the glass substrate and the hydrogel, enhancing adhesion. b–e) Side‐view optical microscope images comparing the adhesion stability of the continuous hydrogel film and independent hydrogel micropillar arrays on untreated and TMSPMA‐treated glass substrates after water immersion and drying. Hydrogel film on b) untreated glass and c) TMSPMA‐treated glass. Hydrogel micropillar array on d) untreated glass and e) TMSPMA‐treated glass. In b‐d), the hydrogel structures detached from the substrate after water immersion, as indicated by red dashed lines marking their original positions. In contrast, in e), the hydrogel micropillars remained firmly adhered to the TMSPMA‐treated glass, demonstrating improved stability under swelling conditions. All scale bars represent 100 µm.

To evaluate their suitability for long‐term use under humid conditions, we conducted a mechanical stability test on both the continuous hydrogel film and the optimal micropillar array (D/P = 0.268), fabricated with identical thickness and height (120 µm). Their behavior upon swelling was monitored after water immersion to assess adhesion to the rigid glass substrate. We found that on untreated glass substrates, both the hydrogel film and the micropillar arrays detached within 5 min of water immersion, indicating interfacial failure caused by swelling‐induced stresses as shown in Figure 2b–d. In the case of micropillar arrays, a significant number of pillars collapsed even before water immersion when no covalent anchoring sites were provided, demonstrating poor structural stability (Figure S5, Supporting Information).

On the other hand, when silane groups capable of forming covalent bonds with the hydrogel network were introduced by functionalizing the glass substrates with TMSPMA, a distinct improvement in adhesion behavior was observed. However, even with the chemical bonding provided by the TMSPMA treatment, the continuous hydrogel film detached from the glass substrate within 15 min of water immersion (Figure 2c). This failure is attributed to the accumulation of swelling‐induced stress across the large, continuous contact area between the hydrogel film and the rigid glass substrate. Uniform expansion of the film generates substantial lateral stresses at the interface, which eventually overcame the covalent bonds and led to delamination. In contrast, the discrete geometry of the micropillars localizes swelling‐induced deformation, effectively preventing the propagation of interfacial stress across the surface. Each pillar accommodates volumetric expansion independently, thereby avoiding the buildup of macroscopic stresses. As a result, the hydrogel micropillar array on the TMSPMA‐treated glass remained intact even after 1 h of water immersion, demonstrating superior interfacial stability enabled by covalent bonding formed via radical‐mediated polymerization between methacrylate and acrylate groups upon UV irradiation (Figure 2e). These results indicate that, under equivalent chemical bonding conditions, geometric configuration plays a crucial role in determining the mechanical durability of the hydrogel‐based structures. Therefore, despite the higher level of transparency offered by the hydrogel film (Figure S3, Supporting Information), the hydrogel micropillar array was selected as the optimal design for water vapor‐absorbing antifogging surfaces, as it ensures both functional performance and long‐term mechanical stability under humid conditions.

Selective Low‐Surface‐Energy Capping and Optical Characterization

To impart superhydrophobic properties to the hydrogel micropillar arrays, low‐surface‐energy features were selectively introduced at the tips of the micropillars, as schematically illustrated in Figure 3a. Briefly, after the hydrogel micropillar array was patterned on the TMSPMA‐treated glass substrates using UV photolithography, low‐surface‐energy (19–21 mJ m−2)[ 37 ] PDMS precursor was selectively deposited onto the pillar tips using a spin‐coating transfer technique.[ 38 , 39 ] To achieve this, uncured PDMS precursor was spin‐coated onto a donor substrate to a thickness of ≈10 µm, and the hydrogel micropillar array was gently stamped onto the coated surface, enabling selective transfer of the PDMS layer to the pillar top without bridging the interstitial regions. Indeed, additional incorporation of red fluorescent dye (Nile red) into the PDMS precursor, followed by transfer and thermal curing, confirms that the process preserves the hydrogel sidewalls essential for effective water vapor absorption. This is evidenced by the spatial confinement of the fluorescent signal to the tops of the micropillars (Figure 3b). Additional side‐view scanning electron microscope (SEM) image as well as elemental mapping further reveals the sharp interface between the PDMS cap and the hydrogel micropillar body, with no detectable overflow of PDMS into the lateral or basal regions (Figure 3c; Figure S6, Supporting Information).

a) Schematics illustrating the fabrication process of the hydrogel micropillar array followed by selective low‐surface‐energy capping with PDMS elastomer. b) Fluorescence microscope image showing selective PDMS capping at the tips of the hydrogel micropillars, confirmed through red fluorescent dye (Nile red) incorporation into the PDMS precursor. Scale bar represents 100 µm. c) Side‐view electron microscope image of the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array. Scale bar represents 100 µm. d) Optical transmittance spectra comparing the hydrogel micropillar array (black) and the low‐surface‐energy capped analog (red).

This structural configuration, featuring a low‐surface‐energy coating exclusively on the pillar tops while leaving the sidewalls fully exposed, is essential for simultaneously achieving superhydrophobicity and antifogging functionality. The exposed hydrogel sidewalls serve as accessible pathways for water vapor absorption, enabling diffusion of water molecules into the hydrogel matrix. Meanwhile, macroscopic water droplets deposited on the pillar tops remain in the Cassie–Baxter state, thereby exhibiting superhydrophobic behavior.

To first determine the effect of low‐surface‐energy capping on the optical performance of the hydrogel micropillar array, we measured the transmission spectra of the array before and after selective capping (Figure 3d). Both structures exhibited high transmittance in the visible range (400 nm≤ λ≤ 700 nm), with average values exceeding 84%. The negligible difference between the spectra indicates that the thin hemispherical PDMS caps do not significantly affect optical performance. Overall, these results demonstrate that the presented strategy enables multifunctionality to be achieved without significantly compromising structural simplicity or optical performance.

Antifogging and Self‐Cleaning Performance of Low‐Surface‐Energy Capped Hydrogel Micropillar Array

To demonstrate the utility of the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array as a superhydrophobic antifogging surface, we first compared its antifogging behavior with that of a bare glass substrate under fogging conditions. We note that since the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar arrays were patterned only on one side of the glass substrate, the opposite side was coated with a continuous hydrogel film to prevent interference with visual observations and transmission measurements during comparison. When the samples were cooled in a −19 °C freezer for 2 h and subsequently exposed to ambient conditions (24 ± 0.5 °C, 56 ± 3% RH), the bare glass exhibited rapid fogging, characterized by the formation of micron‐sized water droplets that scatter light and thus significantly reduce visibility (Figure 4a). In contrast, the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array remained optically clear with no visible fog accumulation (Figure 4b). Close examination of these surfaces conducted 10 s after transfer from a −19 °C freezer to ambient conditions (26 ± 0.5 °C, 60 ± 3% RH) reveals that the bare glass surface is fully covered with condensed droplets, whereas the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array shows no evidence of droplet formation, even at the microscale (Figure 4c,d; Movie S8, Supporting Information). These results confirm that the micropillar array design effectively suppresses droplet nucleation and growth by leveraging the hygroscopic hydrogel sidewalls, which absorb water vapor before it can condense on the surface. To quantitatively evaluate the optical performance, we measured the average transmittance of the samples in the visible light spectrum (400 nm≤ λ≤ 700 nm) before and after cold‐fog transition test (Figure 4e); both samples were cooled in a −21 °C freezer for 2 h and subsequently exposed to ambient air (29 ± 0.5 °C, 42 ± 3% RH). While the bare glass exhibited a marked decrease in transmittance to ≈46%, the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array maintained high transparency (>82%) even under fogging conditions. Comparable high transparency was also observed for uncapped hydrogel micropillar arrays under identical fogging conditions, confirming that vapor uptake through the hydrogel sidewalls is the dominant antifogging mechanism (Figure S7, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the capped hydrogel micropillar arrays preserved their antifogging functionality over 10 repeated cold‐fog cycles, consistently maintaining an optical transmittance of ≈82% (Figure S8, Supporting Information). These results clearly demonstrate the effectiveness of the hydrogel micropillar architecture in preventing visual obstruction caused by fog formation in humid environments.

Next, the surface wettability of the hydrogel micropillar arrays was characterized by measuring the advancing (θadv) and receding (θrec) contact angles using sessile water droplets. The uncapped hydrogel micropillar array exhibited a significantly low receding contact angle of 14°, with a large contact angle hysteresis (≈85°), which is attributed to strong pinning of the water droplet on the hygroscopic hydrogel surface (Figure 5a,b). As a result, the droplets adhered to the surface and failed to roll‐off, even under tilting conditions. In contrast, the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array exhibited superhydrophobic behavior with an advancing contact angle of 166° and a receding contact angle of 131°. This marked reduction in contact angle hysteresis is attributed to the low‐surface‐energy caps, which minimize liquid‐solid contact and promote a stable Cassie–Baxter state (Figure 5a–c). Moreover, the superhydrophobicity was consistently preserved even after ten repeated wet–dry cycles, with advancing and receding contact angles remaining within experimental variation (Figure S9, Supporting Information). In line with these results, the roll‐off angle for a 15 µL water droplet was as low as 16°, demonstrating facile droplet sliding and robust water‐shedding capability (Figure S10, Supporting Information).

a) Plot comparing the water advancing (θadv) and receding (θrec) contact angle of the hydrogel micropillar array and low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array (n = 3). Photographs of the 15 µL water droplet deposited on the b) hydrogel micropillar array and c) low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array. Scale bars represent 1 mm. d) Photographs showing the self‐cleaning performance of the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array. Scale bar represents 1 cm.

As the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array provides enhanced droplet mobility, enabling dynamic functions such as self‐cleaning, where rolling droplets can remove contaminants from the surface with minimal energy input, we examined its self‐cleaning performance by scattering model contaminants (sand, 20–30 mesh) onto a sample tilted at 30°, followed by gently rolling a water droplet over the surface. We observe efficient removal of surface contaminants along the droplet path, leaving a clean trail behind (Figure 5d; Movie S9, Supporting Information). The particles were removed without droplet pinning, demonstrating that the surface maintains low adhesion even under particle loading. Overall, these results confirm that the selective low‐surface‐energy capping strategy introduced in this work imparts both water droplet repellency and dynamic droplet mobility, while simultaneously preserving the underlying hydrogel functionality necessary for fog suppression.

Conclusion

We developed a structurally simple, transparent superhydrophobic antifogging surface by integrating hydrogel micropillar arrays with selectively capped low‐surface‐energy material. Compared with bare glass, which lost transparency to ≈46% upon cold‐fog transition, our design maintained significantly higher optical clarity of >82% across the visible spectrum, thereby demonstrating robust antifogging capability. Unlike conventional hydrophilic coatings that often achieve higher baseline transparency but are prone to surface contamination and droplet pinning, our design spatially decouples water vapor absorption and water droplet repellency by exposing the hydrogel sidewalls for vapor uptake, while confining low‐surface‐energy PDMS exclusively to the pillar tops to maintain superhydrophobicity. This unique architecture enables rapid fog suppression, high droplet mobility for self‐cleaning, and excellent optical transparency (>84%), all without relying on complex multilayer assemblies or inkjet‐based patterning, thereby significantly simplifying the fabrication process. As a future direction, while the present work employed a 4‐inch photomask in a laboratory setting, scalability can be further advanced through larger photomasks or photo‐roll lithography (PRL) for large‐area patterning.[ 40 ] Following such patterning, the low‐surface‐energy cap could be applied at scale using stamping from a large donor substrate or inkjet printing[ 36 ] to selectively coat only the pillar tops, providing practical pathways toward cost‐effective, large‐area manufacturing. We anticipate that this transparent, self‐cleaning, and fog‐resistant coating strategy will offer broad utility in optoelectronic devices, smart windows, and next‐generation optical display platforms that require reliable performance under humid and fog‐prone environments.

Experimental Section

Materials

Poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA, Mw = 700 g mol−1) monomer, 2‐hydroxy‐2‐methylpropiophenone (Darocur 1173), 3‐(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (TMSPMA), toluene, trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), and Nile red were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Korea). Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184) was purchased from Dow Corning. Sand (20–30 mesh) was purchased from JUNSEI. Glass slides (76 × 26 × 1 mm) were purchased from Marienfeld.

Fabrication of the TMSPMA‐Treated Glass

The fabrication process began with the surface modification of the glass substrates to ensure robust adhesion between the hydrogel and the glass surface. Glass slides were first plasma‐cleaned to activate the surface and then immersed in a 50 mM solution of TMSPMA in toluene containing 0.1 vol% TFA as a catalyst. The slides were left in this solution for 18 h at room temperature to achieve methacrylate functionalization. After silanization, the substrates were rinsed thoroughly with toluene and dried, followed by annealing at 75 °C for at least 1 h.

Fabrication of the Low‐Surface‐Energy Capped Hydrogel Micropillar Array

Hydrogel micropillar arrays were fabricated via photolithography using PEGDA as the base material. A prepolymer solution containing PEGDA and 1 wt.% photoinitiator (Darocur 1173) was drop‐casted onto the TMSPMA‐treated glass substrate. A photomask with circular patterns was placed in contact with the substrate, and the sample was exposed to UV light (365 nm, 4.6 mW cm−2) through the mask to initiate crosslinking. After exposure, the unreacted prepolymer was removed by rinsing, leaving behind a regularly patterned array of PEGDA hydrogel micropillars (spacer height = 120 µm). For selective capping of the pillar tops with PDMS, a spin‐coating transfer method was employed. A mixture of PDMS precursor and curing agent (Sylgard 184, 10:1 weight ratio) was spin‐coated onto a silicon wafer at 9500 rpm for 60 s to obtain a uniform thin film of ≈10 µm thickness. The hydrogel micropillar array was gently brought into contact with the PDMS‐coated wafer and then carefully detached. During this stamping step, a thin PDMS layer was selectively transferred only to the tops of the hydrogel micropillars. The sample was then cured at 70 °C for 1 h to fully crosslink the PDMS caps.

Cold‐Fog Transition Test

The cold‐fog transition test was performed by incubating the samples in a −19 °C freezer for 2 h and subsequently transferring to ambient lab conditions.

Cyclic Antifogging Test

To assess the durability, 10 cycles of antifogging tests were performed on the low‐surface‐energy capped hydrogel micropillar array. The samples were initially incubated in a −19 °C freezer for 2 h before being transferred to laboratory ambient conditions for testing.

Measurement of Adhesion Strength

A universal test machine (INSTRON) was used for measuring the adhesion strength. The adhesion strength was measured upon detachment from all samples with the same hydrogel diameter (1 cm).

Other Characterization Methods

Electron micrographs were obtained using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM‐7800F Prime, JEOL Ltd.). Contact angles were measured by using a goniometer in which the advancing and receding contact angle values are acquired at three different locations for each sample (SmartDrop, FemtoBiomed Inc.).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Excel 2016) and Origin software (Origin Lab, Origin Pro 9.0). All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviations (SD). The error bars represent SD.
