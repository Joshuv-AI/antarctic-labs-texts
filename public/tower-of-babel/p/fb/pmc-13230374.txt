Getting In and Staying In? The Relative Contributions of Entering and Exiting Employment to Early Labour Market Trajectories of Migrants’ Daughters Versus Native Women
Authors: Julie Maes, Jonas Wood, Karel Neels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn most European countries, women’s labour force participation differs strongly by migration background. As employment trajectories unfold as a result of the interplay of employment entries and exits, employment gaps might be rooted in differential risks of entering and/or exiting employment. However, available research mainly addresses differences by migration background in employment rates or the initial school-to-work transition, thereby obscuring the underlying dynamic process through which repeated employment entries and exits accumulate and shape diverging employment trajectories. Therefore, this study uses longitudinal microdata on women’s employment trajectories in tandem with innovative multistate hazard and microsimulation models to quantify the relative importance of differential risks of entering and exiting employment as dynamics shaping differences in early employment by women’s migration background in Belgium. Our results indicate that differences in early employment between migrants’ daughters and women without a migration background are mainly driven by lower probabilities of entering employment. Except for second generation Southern European women (for whom the employment difference with native women is also limited), the barriers for employment entry clearly outweigh the barriers for employment stability. This is particularly true for 1.5 generation women who migrated after the age of 12, as a sizeable share never enters employment during the first four years after graduation. Hence, our results indicate that particularly policies aimed at reducing barriers to employment entry could substantially enhance the early employment of migrants’ daughters in Belgium.Keywords: Employment trajectories, Migrants’ descendants, Multistate hazard models, Microsimulation, Belgium

Introduction

In most Western and Northern European countries, women’s labour force participation differs strongly by migration background, with particularly women of non-European origin exhibiting low employment levels. Although the employment gap is most pronounced among first generation non-European migrants, their children also display lower aggregate-level employment rates (Heath et al., 2008; OECD, 2023a, 2023c; Piton & Rycx, 2020) and are less likely to experience stable employment trajectories as young adults (Maes et al., 2019; Nielsen et al., 2003; Zwysen & Longhi, 2018). Given the long-lasting implications of early labour market experiences for individuals’ further career development (Cockx & Picchio, 2013), and the need for higher employment rates in young adults in the light of population ageing and labour shortages, it is crucial to understand how such differential labour market trajectories for migrants’ descendants unfold during women’s early careers. Notwithstanding a large body of literature proposing and testing various potential explanations for lower employment rates and less favourable early career trajectories for daughters of migrants versus women without a migration background (hereafter, “natives”) (e.g. Corluy, 2014; Maes et al., 2019; Piton & Rycx, 2020; Zwysen & Longhi, 2018), we argue that it is empirically and conceptually useful to decompose employment into employment entries and employment exits. In contrast to research focussing on aggregate-level employment rates or individual-level employment, unable to identify the degree to which subgroup differences arise from differential risks of entering employment or retaining employment, a quantification of the relative importance of risks of employment entry and exit for the daughters of migrants vice versa native women will enhance our understanding of how lower employment in migrants’ daughters unfolds. We are the first to answer the question “To which degree are differential labour supply and early employment experiences of migrants’ daughters versus native women explained by differential risks of entering and/or exiting employment?”. In order to do so, this study presents a dynamic micro approach – combining hazard models of employment entry and exit with microsimulations. As such, the framework presented in this study has the potential to influence future research regarding specific determinants of employment, by explicitly distinguishing employment entries and exits, and by simulating the relative weight of employment entries and exits as underlying processes behind the individual-level accumulation of employment experience and group-level employment rates.

Since individual employment trajectories unfold as a result of the dynamic interplay between employment entries and exits, a lower employment rate among migrants’ daughters compared to native women might arise because of lower risks to enter the labour force, but also differential probabilities to retain a job and accumulate stable employment experience. Unfortunately, to date, it remains unclear whether the overall lower employment rates among migrants’ daughters are primarily driven by lower probabilities of entering (first) employment or by higher risks of exiting employment for two reasons. First, a large body of research relies on cross-sectional data and addresses differences by migration background in employment rates or compares labour market positions at specific time points after graduation (Baert et al., 2016; Belfi et al., 2022; Piton & Rycx, 2020; Zwysen & Longhi, 2018). This focus on labour market positions masks the underlying dynamic process of entering and exiting employment and does therefore not allow to disentangle the relative contribution of each process. Second, although several studies use longitudinal data and methods to examine early-career transitions among school-leavers with different migration backgrounds, they typically address only one fragment of the early career, such as entering the first job following graduation. Taken together, available evidence for Western and Northern European countries indicates that school-leavers with a non-European background take longer to enter a first job and are less likely to enter stable employment compared to their native counterparts (Baert et al., 2016; Belfi et al., 2022; Kalter & Kogan, 2006; Lillehagen & Birkelund, 2022; Maes et al., 2019; Nielsen et al., 2003; Zwysen & Longhi, 2018). Some studies subsequently consider the duration of first employment after graduation (Maes et al., 2019; Nielsen et al., 2003) and show that non-European origin school-leavers are also more likely to exit their first employment after short durations. However, these studies provide no insight on how differences by migration background in employment entries and exits accumulate and shape diverging employment trajectories at the beginning of their professional careers. The lack of studies analysing repeated transitions into and out of employment within an integrated framework implies that we currently lack a quantification of the relative contributions of differential risks of entering and exiting employment to differences in the early labour force participation of migrants’ descendants and natives without a migration background. Unpacking whether differential labour market trajectories are mainly related to differential probabilities of entering or exiting employment provides detailed input for scholars to understand, and policy-makers to remediate more cumbersome employment trajectories for vulnerable population subgroups, as it will indicate to which degree solutions are to be sought in barriers to access employment, or factors affecting employment retention.

To unravel the relative contribution of differential risks of entering and exiting employment to the labour supply and early work experience of migrants’ daughters, this study proposes innovative multistate hazard and microsimulation models. In a first step, multistate hazard models are used to estimate transition probabilities into and out of employment among women with different migration backgrounds. In contrast to studies addressing employment positions rather than the underlying transitions, this more detailed and dynamic approach is important to understand diverging labour market trajectories by migration background as employment entry and exit are idiosyncratic processes shaped by distinct mechanisms (cf. Section 3), the study of which is likely to benefit from our approach. In a second step, the estimations of these hazard models are used to simulate group-specific employment trajectories to compare group-level employment rates and individual-level accumulation of work experience. By performing counterfactual scenarios in which (first) entry or exit risks of native women are assigned to the daughters of migrants, we can quantify to what extent differences in employment rates and accumulation of work experience are related to differences in the probability of (I) entering employment, (II) finding a first job or (III) retaining employment.

The Belgian case study is well-suited to study our research question, and of international interest to scholars and policy-makers for three reasons. First, similar to other Western-European countries (e.g., the Netherlands, Germany, France), Belgium is an old immigration country, with large migrant communities rooted in post-WWII labour migration from Southern Europe (mainly Italy), Turkey and Morocco (Van Mol & de Valk, 2016). As a consequence, the Southern European, Turkish and Moroccan communities in Belgium include large groups of descendants of migrants. Second, in comparison to other European countries, Belgium exhibited relatively wide gaps in labour market outcomes between migrants’ descendants and natives without a migration background throughout the 2005–2016 period addressed in this study (Eurostat, 2014). This situation has been interpreted in terms of strong labour market regularisation, with high employment protection and high hiring and firing costs, which is at risk of strengthening gaps between the so-called insiders and outsiders of the labour market (Andersen, 2012; Doerflinger et al., 2020; FOD WASO & CGKR, 2013). Third, the administrative data at hand is longitudinal which allows to study employment entry and exit, whilst the scale of data allows to study population heterogeneity in a detailed manner. We distinguish between the group of Southern European origin and the two largest non-European origin groups in Belgium (i.e. Turkish and Maghreb origin group) and differentiate school-leavers of the second generation (born in Belgium) and the 1.5 generation (migrated before the age of 18). While Belgium’s specific institutional and labour market context may limit the generalisability of our findings, it provides a valuable setting to gain insights that may be less visible in contexts with smaller gaps in labour market outcomes by migration background.

The Belgian Context

Turkish, Moroccan and Southern European Communities

Belgium is an old immigration country, with a substantial share of the population having a migration background. Apart from neighbouring countries, the largest foreign origin groups in Belgium originate from Southern Europe (mainly Italy), Maghreb countries (with the overwhelming majority originating from Morocco) and Turkey. As a result of their long migration history, these origin groups also consist of a large second generation (FOD WASO & UNIA, 2022). Although these origin groups were initially recruited in the context of labour migration after the Second World War, they differ considerably regarding their subsequent patterns of migration.

The presence of Turkish and Moroccan origin groups in Belgium can be traced back to the large-scale migration of guest workers from 1964 onwards who were recruited to address labour shortages in sectors such as industry, mining and construction (Reniers, 1999; Van Mol & de Valk, 2016). Turkish and Moroccan guest workers were predominantly recruited from low-educated rural areas, resulting in a very selective profile of non-European guest workers in Belgium. Since their stay in Belgium was considered to be temporary, there were very few civic integration and language programmes available at the time (Höhne, 2013). With the passing of time, however, many Turkish and Moroccan guest workers decided to settle permanently in Belgium and to bring their spouses and family members over from their respective origin countries in anticipation of or following the “migration stop” related to the oil crises in the early 1970s. Due to restrictive migration policies towards non-European migrants, family reunification and formation have become and remain major migration channels for Turkish and Moroccan origin groups (FOD WASO & UNIA, 2019). The specific settlement patterns of Turkish and Moroccan labour migrants resulted in so called “transplanted communities” that maintain strong bonds with the communities in the region of origin and facilitate transnational marriages and new migrations (Surkyn & Reniers, 1997). As a result of these three migration waves (i.e. the initial recruitment of labour migrants, subsequent family reunification and finally marriage migration), second generation Turkish and Moroccan school-leavers in the period considered in this study (2005–2016) consist partly of the children of post-WWII guest workers, but mainly of the children resulting from subsequent family migration. As a result of these “transplanted communities”, Turkish and Moroccan school-leavers who migrated to Belgium as a child (i.e. the 1.5 generation) have a similar socio-economic background as the second generation.

Southern European origin school-leavers in this paper consist not only of the children and grandchildren from the initial guest workers, but also of the children from more recent Southern European migrants. In contrast to Turkish and Moroccan origin groups, the migration history of Southern Europeans resulted in a more heterogenous origin group in terms of socio-economic background. The recruitment of guest workers from Southern Europe already started in 1946 as a result of agreements with Italy to send workers to the Belgian mines (Myria, 2016). The vast majority of the Italian guest workers originated from poor regions, mostly in Southern Italy (Levi, 1953; Morelli, 1988). After the mine disaster of Marcinelle in 1956, the Belgian government started to recruit guest workers in Spain, Portugal and Greece. Since Southern Europeans could move within Europe without legal restrictions since the 1960 s and due to economic growth in their origin countries during that period, there was a large extent of return migration among Southern European guest workers. Compared to the predominantly male and low-educated migration flows after WWII, more recent Southern European migrants are characterised by a more divers profile in terms of their socio-economic position and gender, and display a mainly urban background (Myria, 2016). Moreover, the close link between migration and family formation that is typical for Turkish and Moroccan origin groups is absent among Southern European origin groups given their free mobility within Europe.

The Belgian Labour Market

Compared to other OECD countries, the Belgian labour market is characterised by high employment protection, relatively high minimum wages and high unemployment benefits (Eurostat, 2025; McGowan et al., 2020). These institutional features contribute to a pronounced labour market segmentation with stark differentials in labour market opportunities between insiders on the one hand who enjoy better working conditions and higher job security and outsiders on the other hand who tend to be either not employed, or employed in secondary labour market segments with less stable and lower paid jobs (Andersen, 2012; Doerflinger et al., 2020). Moreover, while secondary sectors with lower educational requirements and wages are more accessible, mobility to primary labour market segments with well-paid stable jobs is more difficult. This low degree of labour market flexibility and the relatively high minimum wages also affect employers’ recruitment policies (e.g. being risk averse in recruitment decisions). Furthermore, the (administrative) distinction between unemployment and inactivity is blurred in the Belgian context. During the period under study, unemployment benefits were not limited in time and several non-employed groups, such as social-assistance recipients, are obliged to register with the Public Employment Service in Flanders. As a result, a considerable share of registered jobseekers are individuals who are not actively searching for work (EURES, 2025; McGowan et al., 2020).

Particularly non-European origin groups such as those with a Turkish or Maghreb background are considered outsiders on the Belgian labour market, exhibiting low employment levels and being overrepresented in lower segments of the labour market (i.e. temporary contracts and employment sectors with low wages and irregular working hours) (FOD WASO & UNIA, 2022; Piton & Rycx, 2020). Southern European origin groups hold an intermediate position between the labour market positions of Belgians without a migration background on the one hand and Turkish and Maghreb origin groups on the other hand. While the first generation shows the largest differences in labour market outcomes with the Belgian majority population, the differences remain substantial among the second generation (Piton & Rycx, 2020). Unfortunately, information on the labour market outcomes of the 1.5 generation remains limited. Moreover, the gender gap in employment is significantly larger among Turkish and Maghreb origin groups compared to Belgians without a migration background (FOD WASO & UNIA, 2022). Compared to other European countries, Belgium exhibits one of the largest employment gaps between native and second generation non-European women (Eurostat, 2014). As in other European countries facing accelerated population ageing and shrinking working age populations, the successful labour force integration of the large and growing share of women with a (non-European) migration background is considered increasingly important to cover welfare state costs (e.g. pensions or health care expenditure).

Theoretical Framework

Employment from a Multistate Perspective

From a multistate perspective, women’s employment trajectories can be understood as movements between origin and destination states, most fundamentally the transition from non-employment to employment (entry) and from employment back to non-employment (exit). These transitions generate the flows into and out of the stock of employment, and the interplay of these flows determines both group-level employment rates at any point in time and individual-level accumulation of work experience over the life course.

Employment entry is a central process shaping early-career trajectories. A crucial initial transition upon graduation is the first move from non-employment to employment, as the probability of first entry determines whether young women enter the labour force at all. Lower risks of first entry imply that a larger share remains in the origin state of non-employment for longer—or indefinitely—thereby generating substantial gaps in early-career employment rates and work experience accumulation between subgroups (e.g. migrants’ descendants versus natives). Beyond this initial transition, however, differences in entry dynamics more generally also matter: variation in the probability of entry determines how many individuals flow into employment, whereas variation in the timing of entry shapes how long individuals remain in non-employment before securing a (next) job. Even if two subgroups eventually exhibit similar proportions of young adults who have entered employment, later average timing of entry among one group produces systematically shorter employment spells and thus both lower accumulated work experience at the individual level and lower employment rates at the macro level. Conversely, earlier entries increase the inflow into the employment stock, raising employment rates and lengthening average work histories. Once employed, individuals are at risk of exiting back into non-employment. These exit flows determine the retention of employment and the stability of work histories. Lower exit risks—i.e. greater employment retention—keep individuals in the stock of employment for longer durations, while higher exit risks shorten employment spells and reduce work experience accumulation. As with entries, timing matters as early or frequent exits reduce overall time spent in employment, whereas delayed exits produce more stable early careers.

We argue that it is theoretically meaningful to re-consider and specify how well-established determinants of employment might operate differently in their presumable effect on employment entry and exit. The factors that facilitate or hinder employment entry do not necessarily coincide with the factors affecting employment exit (Blossfeld & Rohwer, 2002; Khoudja & Platt, 2018). This study aims to quantify the relative importance of differential employment entry and exit as dynamics underlying lower employment rates and accumulated work experience in daughters of migrants versus natives, and to present a dynamic hazard modelling and microsimulation framework. Consequently, the assessment of potential determinants of differential entry and exit risks – which would require identification strategies that convincingly approximate causal effect estimation – lies beyond the scope of this article. However, in order to theoretically justify our multistate conceptualisation of gaps in employment rates and early work experience between migrants’ daughters and natives, we illustrate the importance of explicitly distinguishing employment entry and exit, using well-established theories of employment. It should be noted that this is not intended to be an exhaustive overview of the determinants of employment. Whereas most existing studies apply these theoretical frameworks to explain differences in employment status, we argue that conceptually distinguishing employment entry and exit allows to more precisely specify how the theoretical mechanisms operate similarly or differently between employment entry and employment exit.

Mechanisms Shaping Employment Entry

Human Capital

Human capital theory posits that human capital increases marginal productivity, making job candidates with a higher degree of human capital more attractive to employers (Becker, 2009). Since school-leavers have limited, if any, work experience, their educational level is considered an important determinant of their human capital, especially for their first entry into employment after graduation. As individuals accumulate work experience, this experience can eventually become a more important factor when entering subsequent employment spells than the level of education initially obtained. Empirical evidence shows that highly educated school leavers (ISCED 5–6) have a shorter unemployment spell before finding a first job than lower educated school leavers (Baert et al., 2016; Lillehagen & Birkelund, 2022). In this respect, differences by migration background in the probability of entering (first) employment are expected to be partly related to variation in educational credentials. Research consistently shows that migrants’ descendants - particularly those of non-European origin and the 1.5 generation - continue to display lower levels of educational attainment compared to natives without a migration background (FOD WASO & UNIA, 2022; Maes et al., 2019; OECD, 2023c). Moreover, they are typically enrolled in fields of education which are associated with weaker employment prospects such as textile, food and secretariat, which further affects their likelihood of entering employment after graduation (FOD WASO & UNIA, 2022). However, research has also shown that substantial differences in employment outcomes by migration background remain after controlling for differences in educational credentials (Corluy, 2014; Maes et al., 2019).

Apart from education, proficiency in the host country’s language has been identified as a crucial factor of human capital to enter employment, although the relevance of host country language skills depends on the sector and type of job (Baert et al., 2016; Corluy, 2014; Esser, 2006). Especially in contexts such as Flanders where the overwhelming majority of vacancies require (very) good Dutch language proficiency, limited language skills can be an important barrier to employment entry for individuals with a migration background (OECD, 2023c). In this respect, studies for Belgium indicate that Maghreb and particularly Turkish origin youngsters generally have lower Dutch language skills than natives without a migration background (Adam & Torrekens, 2015; Maes et al., 2020). As younger children learn new languages with greater ease and success than adolescents, Dutch language proficiency of the 1.5 generation is expected to be lower than that of the second generation, especially in case they migrated at older ages (Maes et al., 2019, 2020).

Social Networks

Another important theoretical framework for employment entry is the social capital theory, which highlights the importance of social networks and the resources embedded within these networks to enter employment (Granovetter, 1974). Available research indicates that particularly the characteristics of individuals in one’s network influence job opportunities (Gracia et al., 2016; Verhaeghe et al., 2013; Zuccotti, 2015). On the one hand, social networks can affect employment entry directly, for instance by providing job search advice, information about job openings and direct referrals and recommendations. On the other hand, social networks may influence employment entry indirectly by shaping educational outcomes, language skills and work-family attitudes, which in turn affect employment entry themselves. Besides the broader social network, partners’ (relative) resources (e.g. income) may affect women’s employment (re-)entry by shaping the economic necessity to work and their comparative advantages and negotiation positions in couples’ role specialisation (Becker, 1991).

Following social capital theory, the probability of entering (first) employment is likely to differ by migration background as a result of differential social networks. Due to the specific migration history of their parents, particularly Turkish and Maghreb origin school-leavers have been disproportionately raised in working-class and low-income families by low educated parents with limited Dutch language skills (Djait, 2014; Verhaeghe et al., 2013, 2015). Moreover, studies indicate that the social networks of Turkish and Maghreb groups typically consist for a large part of co-ethnics (Maes et al., 2020), which can provide job opportunities within their own community (ethnic enclaves), but may offer fewer job opportunities in primary labour market segments compared to networks that mainly consist of individuals without a migration background.

Work-Family Attitudes

Women’s employment entry may further differ by migration background as a result of differential work-family attitudes. Women’s work-family attitudes may vary by migration background due to differential socialisation processes resulting from exposure to norms and role models (de Valk, 2008; De Valk & Liefbroer, 2007; Holland & de Valk, 2017; Idema & Phalet, 2007). Migrants’ daughters may find themselves in an intermediate position between two normative contexts: although they are (largely) socialised within the norms of the majority population through their school environment and the broader society, their parents, family networks, and the wider migrant community may stimulate the norms from their countries of origin (Idema & Phalet, 2007; Zuccotti & Platt, 2017). As such, women who prefer to prioritise caregiving roles might limit their investment in education and employment and start family formation at an early age, reducing the probability of (re-)entering employment at the beginning of the career (Bass, 2015; Khoudja & Fleischmann, 2015; Khoudja & Platt, 2018).

It is important to note that women’s work-family attitudes, labour force participation and family dynamics are strongly interrelated. While work-family attitudes shape labour market outcomes, limited labour market opportunities may also foster traditional work-family attitudes (Wood, 2024). As migrants’ daughters, especially of Turkish and Maghreb origin, are generally characterised by lower labour market opportunities (cf. Section 2.2), they may become demotivated and consider family formation as an alternative career (i.e. taking up full-time carer role as a more stable alternative to uncertain paid work as a source of identity), resulting in family formation at an early age (Wood et al., 2017; Wood & Neels, 2017; Elloukmani & Ou-Salah, 2018; Friedman et al., 1994). Hence, as a result of (anticipated) reduced labour force participation after family formation, migrants’ daughters may be less likely to enter the labour force after graduation or to re-enter employment after having children compared to native women.

Institutional Support to Combine Work and Family

For women with (young) children, employment entry is also expected to be influenced by the availability and design of work-family reconciliation policies as it affects the opportunity costs of childrearing (Oppenheimer, 1994). Especially when women have limited access to affordable and flexible formal childcare, childcare responsibilities may be an important barrier to enter employment. In countries such as Belgium that are characterised by supply shortages in formal childcare and long waiting lists (European Commission, 2014; Vandenbroeck et al., 2008) where access to formal childcare and parental leave is strongly conditioned on stable employment, differences by migration background in labour market opportunities by design entail differences in institutional support to combine work and family life (Biegel et al., 2021; Kil et al., 2018; Marynissen et al., 2021; Wood et al., 2023; Wood, 2025; Maes et al., 2023). As particularly women of Turkish or Maghreb origin are characterized by younger ages at marriage and childbearing than native women (Corijn & Lodewijckx, 2009) and overrepresented in temporary contracts and employment sectors with low wages and irregular working hours (FOD WASO & UNIA, 2022), differential access to work-family reconciliation policies can shape distinct employment entries at the beginning of the career.

Discrimination

A final mechanism that may contribute to lower employment entry among women with a migration background, particularly of non-European origin, is discrimination during the hiring process. There is consistent evidence for Belgium that certain origin groups, such as Turkish and Moroccan groups, are less likely to be invited for job interviews (Baert et al. 2013; Lippens et al. 2023a; Lippens et al., 2023b) and experience discrimination on the labour market (Maes et al., 2020). Different types of discrimination can be distinguished. On the one hand, taste-based discrimination implies that employers refuse to hire persons of a certain background because of their origin, religion or skin colour (Becker, 2010). On the other hand, statistical discrimination occurs when employers use the limited observable information available to infer unobservable attributes of job candidates. For instance, employers may perceive candidates with long or recurrent periods of non-employment (especially shortly after graduation) to be less motivated. Moreover, especially in labour market contexts with high employment protection and minimum wages such as Belgium, employers may be more cautious with hiring decisions when they lack of full information and be more likely to base their decisions on stereotypes associated with specific groups. In this respect, employers are likely to use migration background as an indicator of individuals’ productivity due to a general lack of full information regarding job candidates. Besides the direct impact of discrimination on employment access, it may also indirectly shape differences by migration background. Experiences and expectations of discrimination are likely to shape the (perceived) labour market prospects of migrants’ daughters, which can in turn reduce motivation and educational investments and foster traditional work-family attitudes and early family formation (De Valk & Liefbroer, 2007; Elloukmani & Ou-Salah, 2018; Friedman et al., 1994).

Mechanisms Shaping Employment Exit

Labour Market Segmentation

Labour market segmentation is expected to contribute to differences by migration background in employment exit risks. Due to differences in human capital and social networks, as well as exposure to discrimination, migrants’ daughters (particularly of non-European origin) are more likely than women without a migration background to enter lower labour market segments with temporary contracts and/or in occupations and economic branches that are particularly vulnerable during economic restructuring (FOD WASO & UNIA, 2022; Gesthuizen & Wolbers, 2010; Kogan, 2006). Employment in these segments generally entails higher risks of involuntary exit, as jobs are more sensitive to fluctuations in labour demand and offer fewer opportunities for internal mobility or skill development. Moreover, technological change is more likely to reduce workers’ marginal productivity in low-skilled sectors, further increasing the likelihood of job loss. In contrast, women without a migration background are more often employed in higher-skilled, primary labour market sectors, where the risks of involuntary exit are lower due to greater job stability and opportunities for skill development.

Institutional Support to Combine Work with Other Life Domains

Following micro-economic theories of utility maximisation, individuals are assumed to exit employment in case the (financial) returns from employment are insufficient to compensate for the costs of employment. These opportunity costs are strongly influenced by the institutional context such as the availability and accessibility of work-family reconciliation policies (Oppenheimer, 1994). In this respect, migrants’ daughters (especially of non-European origin and the 1.5 generation) may be more likely to exit employment compared to women without a migration background as a result of lower net income gains from employment. In countries such as Belgium where access to work-family reconciliation policies (e.g. childcare support or flexible work arrangements) is conditioned on earlier work experience or employment sector, lower labour market opportunities for non-European origin women may exacerbate difficulties in combining work and family and in turn entail higher risks of leaving employment (Marynissen et al., 2021; Vandenbroeck et al., 2008).

Changing Work-Family Attitudes

Finally, individuals’ work-family attitudes may be adjusted over the life course and particularly change after live events that entail new social roles and expectations (and social penalties in case of deviations of particular expectations) (West & Zimmerman, 1987). In this view, research indicates that the transition to parenthood is associated with the emergence of more traditional gender role attitudes among both men and women (Baxter et al., 2015). This implies that the likelihood of leaving employment may be affected by changing attitudes and role expectations regarding labour force participation after the transition to parenthood. In this view, changes in (gender role) expectations may contribute to differential employment exit risks by migration background. For instance, research among the Turkish and Moroccan second generation in Belgium suggests that once women are married, and particularly when they have children, it is no longer considered desirable to work within certain subsegments of the Turkish or Moroccan community (Wood, 2024; Adam & Torrekens, 2015).

Data and Methods

Data and Sample

This paper uses data from the Flemish administrative panel on Migration, Integration and Activation (MIA Panel) covering the period 2005–2016 (Wood & Neels, 2020), which provides longitudinal microdata from the Belgian Social Security Registers (CBSS/KSZ). The MIA Panel was developed in 2017–2019 in the context of a research project focussing on the labour market integration of the children of Southern European and non-European migrants and provides information on a sample of (i) individuals without a migration background (i.e. natives), (ii) individuals of Southern European origin1 and (iii) individuals of non-European origin2 aged 18–65 and legally residing in Flanders on the 1 st of January 2005. Natives are defined as individuals who have had the Belgian nationality since birth and have two parents with the Belgian nationality at birth. An individual is considered to be of migrant origin when the person him/herself or one of their parents has a nationality at birth that is not Belgian. Individuals with a migration background who are not born in Belgium and migrated before the age of 18 are defined as the 1.5 generation and individuals with a migration background who are born in Belgium are defined as the second generation. Sampled individuals are followed until (i) the age of 65, (ii) emigration or death, or (iii) the end of the observation period on the 31 December 2016. To maintain cross-sectional representativeness, supplementary annual samples of 18-year olds were drawn to guarantee the presence of the youngest age group in the data throughout the observation period. For each observation year, household members of sampled individuals on the first of January are also included in the data. Despite limitations of the MIA Panel in terms of sample sizes, the observation window of sampled individuals (i.e. no information before the age of 18 or after the age of 65) and the specific countries of origin, it provides longitudinal information on labour market positions of individuals on a quarterly basis, which allows examining transitions between employment positions and taking the duration of (non-)employment spells into account. Moreover, the MIA Panel is disproportionately stratified by age and migration background, with an overrepresentation of both younger age groups (i.e. 18–35 year olds) and individuals with a Southern European and non-European migration background3. This disproportional stratification by migration background provides sufficiently large sample sizes to study the employment outcomes of the children of Southern European and non-European migrants.

For this study, we select women without a migration background and Turkish and Maghreb origin women who graduated in Flanders between 2005 and 2016. Women are followed from the quarter in which they graduate, which is identified based on their entitlement to child benefits4, until the end of the observation period. The resulting analytical sample consists of 5931 women (1373 women without a migration background, 1390 Southern European women of the second generation, 1309 Maghreb women of the second generation, 1125 Turkish women of the second generation, 217 Southern European women of the 1.5 generation, 264 Maghreb women of the 1.5 generation and 253 Turkish women of the 1.5 generation), generating 117,802 person-quarters of observation.

Methods

Modelling Transitions into and Out of Employment

In a first step, we estimate the probabilities of making a transition into employment and out of employment (i.e. becoming unemployed or inactive). As entry and exit probabilities are expected to differ by migration background, they are estimated for each group separately (i.e. women without a migration background, second generation Southern European women, second generation Maghreb women, second generation Turkish women, Southern European women of the 1.5 generation, Maghreb women of the 1.5 generation and Turkish women of the 1.5 generation). Over the observation period, multiple spells of (non-)employment can be observed upon graduation and individuals can make a transition into or out of employment multiple times. For each group, we estimate two discrete-time hazard models using a complementary log-log link function to estimate the (continuous-time) hazard of making a transition in quarter () for an individual woman in the origin state. Microsimulation of Early Labour Market Trajectories

While the estimated hazards of entering and exiting employment identify differences by migration background in transition risks, they do – as such – not show how these risks accumulate and shape early employment trajectories in terms of employment stability and the duration of (non-)employment spells. To capture these dynamic processes, we use the estimated group-specific hazards to simulate labour market trajectories for 10,000,000 women over the first four years after graduation. For each quarter since graduation, the hazard models generate the conditional probability of making a transition, given a woman’s origin state (employed or not employed), the time spent in the spell and the order of the spell. This probability is subsequently compared to a random value drawn from a uniform distribution to determine whether a transition takes place in the quarter considered. This microsimulation approach allows us to reconstruct how differential entry and exit risks accumulate into diverging employment trajectories. The group-specific labour market trajectories that are generated by the microsimulations are then aggregated to assess the average number of quarters in which women were employed during the first four years since graduation and the employment rate in the fourth year after graduation.

To examine the relative contribution of transitions into and out of employment as the dynamics underlying differences by migration background in employment rates and the accumulation of work experience, four simulations are performed (cf. Table 1). First, we simulate women’s labour market trajectories by migration background, using the estimated group-specific employment entry and exit probabilities. These base simulations will be used as a reference point to compare simulated trajectories of counterfactual simulations. In the second simulation, the estimated probabilities for entering employment of native women are assigned to all groups, while retaining the group-specific probabilities for employment exit. Hence, this simulation assumes similar access to employment across groups, whereas differences in employment retention persist. Third, to better understand the relative importance of entering employment for the first time after graduation, we only assign the entry probabilities of native women into the first employment spell to all groups, while each group retains its own specific probabilities for entering high-order employment spells and for exiting employment. This allows us isolate the impact of the initial school-to-work transition on group-level employment rates and the accumulation of work experience. Fourth, we assign the probabilities for leaving employment of native women to all groups, while each group retains its own specific entry probabilities. As such, this simulation still considers potential differences in accessing employment across groups, but assumes similar employment stability afterwards. By comparing the number of quarters in which women were employed during the first four years since graduation, as well as employment rates in the fourth year after graduation across the base simulation and these three counterfactual simulations, we can quantify to what extent differences in early employment among migrants’ daughters compared to women without a migration background are related to lower chances of entering (first) employment (e.g. due to differences in human capital, social networks, work-family attitudes, access to work-family reconciliation policies or discrimination) and to what extent they are associated with a higher risk of leaving employment (e.g. due to entry into differential labour market segments, changing work-family attitudes after family formation or differential access to work-family reconciliation policies) (cf. Section 3). As such, the simulations enable us to assess to what extent early employment gaps would persist if migrants’ daughters had the same opportunities to enter or remain in employment as women without a migration background.

Descriptive Results

Figure 1 displays the proportion of women who are employed and not employed over the first four years since graduation for each group, by the order of their (non-)employment spell (i.e. results of the base simulation). The information on the order of (non-)employment spells visualises the dynamic process of labour market mobility: among the non-employed, we can identify the share who have never entered employment upon graduation (i.e. those remaining in non-employment spell 1) versus those who have returned to non-employment after one (non-employment spell 2) or more periods of employment (i.e. higher-order non-employment spells). Similarly, among the employed, we can assess the share who remained continuously employed after their initial school-to-work transition (i.e. employed spell 1) versus those who have re-entered employment after one or more periods of non-employment.

Figure 1a shows that women without a migration background are characterised by a relatively stable and continuous attachment to the labour market. About 65% of women without a migration background has entered employment in the first quarter after graduation and 55% is still employed in their first employment spell four years after graduation. In the fourth year after graduation, about 88% of women without a migration background is employed and 12% is not employed, but only 5% has never been employed during these four years. In contrast, women with a migration background, especially of Turkish and Maghreb origin and of the 1.5 generation, exhibit a lower and less stable labour market participation. Regarding the second generation, 9% of Southern European women and about 12% of Turkish and Maghreb women has never entered employment by the fourth year after graduation and respectively 43% and 29% is still employed in their first employment spell. Regarding the 1.5 generation, around 20%−25% has never entered employment in four years after graduation and only 21%, 23% and 32% of Turkish, Maghreb and Southern European women respectively remains in their first employment spell by the fourth year after graduation.

Figure 2 shows for each group how many quarters they have on average been employed during the first four years since graduation. Whereas the differences between groups are relatively limited in the first two years after graduation, the gap widens over time. Four years after graduation, women without a migration background have on average been employed for 13 out of 16 quarters. Moreover, 36% of women without a migration background is employed for all 16 quarters after graduation and 45% for 12 to 15 quarters, which highlights the stability of their early employment trajectories (cf. Figure 8 in Appendix). Second generation Southern European women closely follow native women and are on average employed for 12 out of 16 quarters. The difference with native women is more pronounced among second generation Turkish and Maghreb women, who are on average employed for 10 of the 16 quarters. Women of the 1.5 generation show the lowest number of quarters in employment four years after graduation (only 8–9 quarters), which is largely driven by a high proportion of women who never enter employment (ranging from 20% to 25%). However, once entered the labour market, migrant origin women are characterised by a lower labour market attachment: 25% of second generation Southern European women, 15% of second generation Turkish and Maghreb women and only 8% of the 1.5 generation is employed for all 16 quarters following graduation (cf. Figure 8 in Appendix). Hence, these findings highlight not only the challenges migrant origin women (especially of Turkish and Maghreb origin and the 1.5 generation) face in entering the labour market but also in remaining employed, leading to more fragmented employment trajectories at the beginning of their career compared to women without a migration background.

The Relative Importance of Differential Transitions into and Out of Employment

Our model estimates indicate that both the probability to enter and exit employment differ strongly by migration background (cf. Figures 6 and 7 in Appendix). Women with a migration background – particularly of Turkish and Maghreb origin and of the 1.5 generation - are not only less likely than women without a migration background to (re-)enter employment, they are also more likely to exit employment. In order to quantify the relative contribution of these differential hazards of entering and exiting employment to differences in employment rates and the accumulation of work experience for the different groups studied, we use the base simulation with group-specific employment entry and exit probabilities as a reference point and compare the time spent in employment during the first four years since graduation and employment rates in the fourth year after graduation across the base simulation and three counterfactual simulations. This approach will provide empirical insight into whether the lower labour supply and early employment experiences of migrants’ daughters compared to women without a migration background is mainly driven by differences in the probability to enter (first) employment (e.g. due to differences in human capital, social networks, work-family attitudes, access to institutional support or discrimination) or by differences in the probability to exit employment (e.g. due to entry into differential labour market segments, changing work-family attitudes after family formation or differential access to work-family reconciliation policies) (cf. Section 3.2 and 3.3).

Regarding the accumulation of employment experience, the results of the base simulation in Fig. 3 show that women without a migration background are on average employed for 82% of the time during the first four years after graduation (i.e. on average 13 of the 16 quarters as indicated in Fig. 2). Second generation Southern European women are on average employed for 73% of the time and second generation Turkish and Maghreb women for 62% of the time. Women of the 1.5 generation display the lowest time spent in employment during the first four years after graduation: Southern European, Turkish and Maghreb women of the 1.5 generation are on average employed for only 57%, 54% and 49% of the time respectively. In order to illustrate the contribution of differences in entering employment in explaining the difference with women without a migration background in early employment, we perform a first counterfactual simulation in which migrant origin women are assigned the hazards of employment entry of women without a migration background. In this counterfactual simulation (i.e. “entry BE” simulation), migrant origin women - particularly of the 1.5 generation and of Turkish and Maghreb origin - show an increase in the proportion of employed quarters: the time spent in employment increases to 72% among Turkish women of the 1.5 generation and up to 78% among second generation Southern European women. This indicates that the lower probabilities of entering employment among migrant origin women compared to women without a migration background substantially contribute to the differences in early employment across groups. However, since migrant origin women are still more likely to exit employment than women without a migration background, their average time spent in employment remains lower. In this respect, Figure 8 in Appendix shows that while there is an increasing share of migrant origin women who are employed for 12–15 quarters in the first four years since graduation in the “entry BE” simulation compared to the base simulation (ranging from 47% to 53%), they still lag behind women without a migration background in terms of being employed for all 16 quarters (ranging from 15% among Turkish and Maghreb women of the 1.5 generation to 28% among second generation Southern European women versus 37% among women without a migration background). Furthermore, improving employment entry of migrant origin women also substantially improves their group-level employment rates. When migrant origin women display the same probabilities to enter employment as native women, employment rates in the fourth quarter after graduation increase to 80%−84% among the second generation and to 72%−77% among the 1.5 generation (cf. Figure 4).

To isolate the relative importance of differences by migration background in the first entry into employment, the “FIRST entry BE” simulation only assigns the entry probabilities of native women into the first employment spell to all groups, while each group retains its own specific probabilities for entering high-order employment spells. For Southern European women (both second and 1.5 generation), the results closely mirror those of the full “entry BE” simulation, indicating that their early employment disadvantage is largely due to a difficult first entry into employment. In contrast, for Turkish and Maghreb origin women, both the time spent in employment and employment rates in the fourth quarter after graduation increase less strongly than in the full “entry BE” simulation. This shows that although the lower probability of entering a first employment spell after graduation among Turkish and Maghreb origin women compared to women without a migration background contributes for a large part to the differences in early employment, difficulties in re-entering employment after leaving the first employment spell also depress their early employment experiences and employment rates. In this respect, Figure 8 in Appendix indicates that a relatively large share remains employed for less than 8 quarters (around 16–18%) in this counterfactual simulation.

Finally, in the third counterfactual simulation, in which migrant origin women are assigned the probabilities of leaving employment of native women, but retain their own (lower) probabilities of entering employment (i.e. the “exit BE” simulation), their average time spent in employment and employment rates also increase compared to the base simulation. For second generation Southern European women, the increase in the time spent in employment and in the employment rate in the fourth year after graduation is similar in magnitude compared to the “entry BE” simulation. However, for the other groups, and particularly for the 1.5 generation, improving employment stability entails a smaller increase in the time spent in employment and in employment rates compared to the “entry BE” simulation. Figure 8 in Appendix shows that although there is an increasing share of women of the 1.5 generation employed for all 16 quarters compared to the base simulation (about 21%−24%), a substantial share still never enters employment during the first four years since graduation. Furthermore, for second generation Turkish and Maghreb origin women, our results indicate that their lower likelihood of remaining in employment is equally important as their lower likelihood of entering a first employment spell after graduation for the difference with native women in the accumulation of employment experiences.

In sum, these findings indicate that differences in employment rates and the accumulation of work experience between migrants’ daughters and women without a migration background are mainly driven by lower probabilities of entering employment. Except for second generation Southern European women (for whom the difference with native women is also limited), the barriers for employment entry clearly outweigh the barriers for employment stability. This is particularly true for the 1.5 generation, as a sizeable share never enters employment during the first four years after graduation. Additional analyses separating Turkish and Maghreb women of the 1.5 generation by age at migration reveal that these patterns are concentrated among those who migrated after age 12, whereas those who migrated to Belgium before the age of 12 display trajectories more similar to the second generation (cf. Figure 9 in appendix).

The Relative Contribution of Differential Entry and Exit Risks by Level of Education

Finally, since compositional differences in educational attainment are frequently considered a key explanation for lower employment rates among migrants’ descendants, we examine whether the relative contribution of differential entry and exit risks for differences by migration background in early employment varies across educational levels. Based on information from the Learning and Experience Certificates Database (LED), which collects data on qualification certificates obtained in Flemish educational institutions, we distinguish between women who have at most a secondary education degree (ISCED-levels 0–4), women who have obtained a tertiary degree (ISCED 5–6) and women for whom the educational level is not known based on this database. Table 3 in appendix shows the composition of the origin groups in terms of educational level and illustrates that the share of highly educated women is considerably lower among Southern European, Turkish, and Maghreb origin groups than among women without a migration background6. In our sample, there is also a large share of women with an unknown educational level (ranging from 25% among native women to 67% among Southern European women of the 1.5 generation). The relativively large share of women with an unknown educational level among the 1.5 generation, especially among women who migrated after the age of 12, suggests that a substantial proportion of women of the 1.5 generation did not obtain a secondary or tertiary degree in Flanders. Moreover, since 70% of women with an unknown educational level are observed from the age of 18 or 19 onwards, this further indicates that they are unlikely to have completed a tertiary degree, given the minimum duration of such programmes.

Discussion

In most Western and Northern European countries, the daughters of migrants - especially those of non-European origin - exhibit lower employment rates than women without a migration background (OECD, 2023b) and are less likely to secure stable employment from the beginning of their career (Maes et al., 2019; Nielsen et al., 2003). Since early labour market experiences have long-lasting implications for individuals’ further career development (Cockx & Picchio, 2013), understanding how these differences unfold during the early career is relevant at both the societal level (e.g. increasing employment rates in view of accelerated population ageing and shrinking working-age populations) and the individual level (e.g. social protection and poverty reduction). Yet, available research on the early employment of migrants’ descendants has predominantly focussed on aggregate-level employment rates at particular time points after graduation (Baert et al., 2016; Belfi et al., 2022; Zwysen & Longhi, 2018) or on the initial school-to-work transition (Lillehagen & Birkelund, 2022; Maes et al., 2019; Nielsen et al., 2003), thereby obscuring the underlying dynamic process through which repeated employment entries and exits accumulate and shape diverging employment trajectories. As a result, it remains unclear whether employment disparities by migration background primarily arise from differential entry into first and later jobs, or from sooner exists from work among those having a job. This is unfortunate as a quantification of the relative importance of risks of differential entry and exit risks to differences in women’s early employment provides important input for policy-makers since the mechanisms that facilitate or hinder employment entry differ from the factors affecting employment exit. Hence, in order to enhance the labour supply and early employment experience of migrants’ daughters, an important first step is to understand whether solutions are mainly to be sought in barriers to access employment, or factors affecting employment retention. Therefore, the aim of this study is to answer the question: “To which degree are differential labour supply and early employment experiences of migrants’ daughters versus native women explained by differential risks of entering and/or exiting employment?” To this end, this study uses longitudinal microdata for Belgium on women’s employment trajectories and proposes innovative multistate hazard and microsimulation models.

Consistent with previous research (Maes et al., 2019; OECD, 2023c; Piton & Rycx, 2020), our results show that the daughters of migrants, particularly of Turkish or Maghreb origin and of the 1.5 generation, exhibit a lower and less stable labour market attachment compared to women without a migration background. Whereas the employment rate in the fourth year after graduation amounts to 88% among native women, it amounts to 61% to 73% among second generation women and to 49% to 57% for women of the 1.5 generation. Regarding the accumulation of employment experience, we find that women without a migration background are on average employed for 82% of the time during the first four years after graduation, whereas Southern European, Turkish and Maghreb women are on average employed for 73% (second generation Southern European) to 49% of the time (Maghreb women of the 1.5 generation). By comparing employment rates in the fourth year after graduation and the time spent in employment during the first four years since graduation across counterfactual scenarios, our results indicate that differences in early employment between migrants’ descendants and women without a migration background are mainly driven by lower probabilities of entering employment. Except for second generation Southern European women (for whom the difference with native women is also limited), the barriers for employment entry clearly outweigh the barriers for employment stability. This is particularly true for women who migrated after the age of 12, as a sizeable share never enters employment during the first four years after graduation. Moreover, our results indicate that a tertiary degree entails a better access to employment as differences in early employment with native women are substantially smaller among highly educated women compared to the lower educated group and not related to differences in accessing a first job after graduation. However, there are still differences in the time spent in employment during the first four years after graduation among highly educated women, which are related to the higher risk of second generation Turkish and Maghreb women of exiting employment. This is in line with previous studies indicating that the returns of educational credentials are lower for migrant origin groups compared to majority populations and that differences in educational levels and language skills can only partially explain differences in employment rates and entering stable employment (Corluy, 2014; Maes et al., 2019). This suggests that a tertiary degree entails a better access to employment for second generation Turkish and Maghreb women, but not necessarily access to the primary labour market segment with a higher degree of stability.

Hence, our findings indicate that the mechanisms shaping employment entry play a more important role than the mechanisms shaping employment exit for explaining differences in early employment between women with and without a migration background in the Belgian (Flemish) context, especially for Turkish and Maghreb origin women, the 1.5 generation and lower educated women. As discussed in Section 3, the mechanisms influencing employment entry are strongly interrelated and many of these factors (such as educational attainment, work-family attitudes and access to work-family reconciliation policies) are to some extent shaped by women’s (perceived) labour market opportunities. In this respect, we argue that especially policies aimed at reducing stereotypes and discrimination are crucial to improve early employment of migrants’ daughters. A substantial body of research documents persistent hiring discrimination against certain origin groups such as Turkish and Maghreb groups in Belgium (Baert et al., 2013; Lippens et al., 2023b). Such selective hiring practices have been related to structural features of the Belgian labour market (e.g. high employment protection and relatively high minimum wages) that may make employers more risk-averse. Moreover, evidence for Flanders indicates that context matters, as there is less discrimination under conditions of labour market tightness and differs depending on the size of the organization and the type of jobs (Baert et al. 2013; Lippens et al. 2023a; Lippens et al., 2023b). Hiring discrimination not only shapes employment entries of migrants’ daughters directly, but also indirectly. Experiences and expectations of discrimination are likely to shape the (perceived) labour market prospects of migrants’ daughters, which can in turn reduce motivation and educational investments and foster traditional work-family attitudes and early family formation (Elloukmani & Ou-Salah, 2018; Friedman et al., 1994). In this respect, reducing discrimination constitutes a crucial precondition for improving labour market opportunities and enabling long-term shifts in educational outcomes and work-family attitudes among migrants’ daughters. Moreover, in the Belgian insider/outsider context, work-family reconciliation policies that primarily support parents who are firmly established in the labour market imply by design a differential access for native and migrant origin women, since they face different opportunity structures from the onset of their career. As migrants’ daughters are more likely than native women to be/become employed in unstable jobs with irregular or flexible working hours and/or low incomes, they may face more practical and/or financial difficulties to combine family formation with labour force participation, which reinforces their precarious employment positions (Marynissen et al., 2021; Vandenbroeck et al., 2008). In this respect, universal access to flexible and affordable family policies would therefore be especially beneficial for women with weaker labour market opportunities, including many migrants’ daughters. Furthermore, if access to primary labour market segments would become less selective by migration background, the resources embedded in the networks of women with and without a migration background would also become more similar.

Finally, five avenues for future research can be mentioned. First, as exit probabilities are to some extent related to the specific labour market segment into which individuals enter, an interesting path for future studies would be to take into account the labour market segment in which individuals enrol when they enter employment. As such, future hazard and microsimulation models of labour market trajectories could further differentiate employment in terms of contract type, full-time vs. part-time work or sector of activity as these may mediate exit risks and differences between population subgroups. Second, as this paper focussed on differences by migration background in early employment rates and accumulated employment experience, we considered transitions between spells of employment and non-employment. In the Belgian context during the period under study, a further distinction between unemployment and inactivity is empirically less meaningful as a substantial share of registered jobseekers are not actively searching for work (cf. Section 2.2) (EURES, 2025; McGowan et al., 2020).Nevertheless, in contexts where unemployed and inactive individuals constitute more clearly distinct groups - such as in countries with time-limited unemployment benefits - future research could extend our hazard and microsimulation models to include three states: employment, unemployment, and inactivity. In such settings, differentiating unemployment from inactivity may be meaningful as becoming/being inactive could reflect a more voluntary withdrawal from the labour market compared to becoming/being unemployed, which in turn affects individuals’ employment entry and exit risks. Third, an interesting path for future research would be to adopt the proposed dynamic approach of this study to examine (subgroup variation in) how employment entries and exits are influenced by factors such as childbearing and/or economic cycles. As such, multistate hazard and microsimulation models could be adopted to assess whether and to what extent transition into and out of employment before the birth of a child or an economic recession differ from the entry and exit patterns afterwards. Fourth, a fruitful avenue for future research would be to explore transitions between jobs and employers that occur within a given employment spell. Particularly changes between employers may be an important indication of stable labour market attachment, especially in the Belgian context where eligibility criteria for some policies (e.g. parental leave) are conditioned on continuity with the same employer. Fifth and finally, it would be beneficial if future research could address whether similar patterns regarding the relative importance of differential entry and exit risks are found in other contexts. Belgium’s strongly regulated labour market with high employment protection and relatively high minimum wages, may amplify differences by migration background in entry probabilities. In countries with more flexible labour markets (e.g. Denmark) or countries with lower minimum wages (e.g. USA or UK), turnover might be higher and employment trajectories less stable, differences in exit probabilities might play a more important role in shaping early employment outcomes of migrants’ daughters (Kogan, 2006).
