Production of 5‑Hydroxymethylfurfural from Glucose under Hydrothermal Conditions
Authors: Haojie Tian, Sichan Li, Mengli Zhou, Tingyu Zhu, Xinyu Liu, Zhimin Wu, Qiying Liu, Jianchun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
5-Hydroxymethylfurfural (5-HMF) is an important biomass-based platform molecule with various commercial applications, and its green, efficient production is the key for value-added applications of lignocellulosic biomass. This work studied the preparation of 5-HMF from glucose using autogenic H+ and OH– as the catalysts under hydrothermal conditions without additional catalysts. The effects of reaction temperature, time, pressure, and glucose concentration on the 5-HMF yield were discussed by single-factor experiments. The results show that the highest yield of 5-HMF is 70.2% and the selectivity is 80% at 180 °C for 6 h. The kinetics for glucose and fructose conversions was investigated in detail. It was found that the processes of glucose and fructose conversion to 5-HMF conform to the first-order reaction rate equation, showing the activation energies for 5-HMF formation of 94.5 and 89.6 kJ/mol, respectively. Moreover, 77% of the furfural yield could also be obtained by using xylose as the feedstock. This work offers an alternative, versatile, and eco-friendly strategy for furanic compound production from lignocellulosic biomass and sugar derivatives.

Introduction

Fossil resources such as petroleum, coal, and natural gas have played an indispensable role in social development. However, considering the overuse of such fossil resources brought about a series of problems involving global warming and air pollution. People are constantly trying to find new alternatives to replace the existing fossil resources to compass above-mentioned problems. , Lignocellulosic biomass as a renewable carbon-neutral resource in nature showed priorities of huge storages, wide distributions and low cost, etc., which has been paid more and more attention all over the world. Using biomass-based carbohydrates instead of fossil raw materials to produce platform compounds has also become one of the hot spots and key directions in the research field of biomass applications. , Among the platform compounds, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (5-HMF) contains hydroxymethyl, furan ring, and aldehyde groups in its molecular structure, which has high reactivity for preparing a series of high-value-added chemicals and biofuel precursors via diversified conversion pathways. , Therefore, the efficient production of 5-HMF from biomass and the derivatives is of scientific and practical importance for achieving biomass valorization.

In recent years, studies have shown that hexoses (glucose and fructose, etc.) are the main raw material for preparing 5-HMF. − Dumesic and co-workers conducted experiments using a biphasic solvent system and SBA-15-functionalized propylsulfonic acid as the heterogeneous catalyst. A moderate 5-HMF yield of 49% was obtained with a 66% conversion at a reaction temperature of 180 °C. Tucker et al. also used this type of porous silica catalysts for realizing the selectivity of 5-HMF of up to 60% in the mixed solvent of THF/H2O at 130 °C. Wang et al. investigated the kinetics of glucose/fructose dehydration to 5-HMF. For glucose dehydration, the reported activation energy was 71.8–161 kJ/mol. While for fructose dehydration, the lower activation energy was in the range of 42.2–65.9 kJ/mol. It is noted that the activation energy for the dehydration of glucose to 5-HMF (160.9 kJ/mol) was much higher than humins formation (23.5 kJ/mol), resulting in the open challenge for producing 5-HMF with high yields. Tang et al. evaluated the kinetics for the conversion of glucose over the AlCl3 catalyst in NaCl–H2O/THF and H2O/THF biphasic solvents as well as water medium at 130–160 °C. Glucose can be obtained by hydrolysis of lignocellulose, which has the advantages of abundant reserves and low price. It is considered to be a more potential and ideal raw material based on the above-mentioned priorities. Therefore, producing 5-HMF from glucose is favored by more and more researchers. , Unfortunately, because of the stable pyran ring in the glucose structure, the conversion of glucose into 5-HMF is more complicated, facing challenges such as low yields and side reactions. To achieve high 5-HMF yields from glucose and even raw lignocellulosic biomass, diversified solvents (water contained biphase, ionic liquids, and polar nonproton organic solvents) and acidic catalysts involving solid/mineral acids and acidic salts were developed; , however, catalyst performance and the waste emissions induced by liquid acids still pose challenges for the large-scale production of 5-HMF.

The conversion of glucose to 5-HMF involves a multistep reaction involving the isomerization of glucose to fructose followed by dehydration of fructose to 5-HMF, − in which the synergistic effect of Lewis acid/Bronsted acid sites is the key. At present, a variety of homogeneous (inorganic acids, soluble metal salts, etc.) and heterogeneous (metal oxides, molecular sieves, metal-organic framework compounds, etc.) acid catalysts have been developed. , Unfortunately, these catalysts have disadvantages such as high cost, poor hydrothermal stability, and difficulty to recover for homogeneous catalysts. For organic solvents, nonproton organic solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and dimethylformamide (DMF) could effectively inhibit the degradation of formed 5-HMF. − However, the separation of 5-HMF from these solvents is rather difficult and requires an intensive energy input. Ionic liquids as a solvent can get a high yield of 5-HMF, − but their preparation process is also complex, expensive, and difficult to recover, which is not conducive to industrial application. Comparatively, water is an ideal solvent that is environmentally friendly, cheap, and readily available, which is the primary solvent used in the early studies. However, 5-HMF is unstable in acidic aqueous environments and is prone to further decomposition to produce byproducts such as formic acid and levulinic acid (LA), along with the formation of humins. , The ideal 5-HMF production process requires the efficient conversion of biomass to 5-HMF (such as glucose and fructose) in green solvent (such as water) with a simple and safe catalyst or without catalyst.

High-temperature liquid water (HTLW) represents a quintessential hydrothermal medium, defined as compressed liquid water maintained between 180 and 350 °C, which can simultaneously act as acid and base catalysts (due to H3O+ and OH– formation at high-temperature ionization) to replace the above catalysts. It can also replace a toxic ionic liquid or organic solvent as the reaction medium. Using water as a solvent can effectively avoid the further decomposition of 5-HMF in the acidic environment. This is because the ionization constants of HTLW are hundreds or even thousands of times as compared to the normal water, which remarkably rises the [H3O+] and [OH–] concentrations to act as acid and base sites for catalyzing 5-HMF production from biomass. This in situ-formed sites avoid the addition of exotic acid–base catalysts and thus the separation as well as the acid and alkali neutralization processing, etc. − Therefore, taking advantage of the excellent solubility and acid–base catalysis of HTLW can promote the conversion of glucose and fructose to 5-HMF under hydrothermal conditions while suppressing the formation of side products like humins induced by feedstock and further 5-HMF conversions. , Table S1 compares the physical properties among supercritical, high-temperature liquid, and normal temperature water. , It has carried out much research on the biomass reaction in HTLW at home and abroad. In the work of Ramli et al., LA was produced from fructose in liquid water at high temperatures without any additional catalyst. It was found that the decomposition of fructose in liquid water at high temperature was quite complete, among which the yield of the intermediate product 5-HMF could reach 50%, but the yield of the target product LA was very low (less than 4%). This is due to the insufficient acid amounts of liquid water at high temperatures. Sasaki et al. − focused on the decomposition mechanism of biomass in HTLW, specifically examining the reaction kinetics of cellulose, cellobiose, and glucose at 320–350 °C. However, their study did not thoroughly analyze the significant variations in the glucose hydrolysis rate and product distribution near the critical temperature, nor did it systematically evaluate the role of the system’s acid–base properties in the glucose conversion pathway.

This work used the excellent solubility and acid–base catalysis of HTLW for the selective 5-HMF production from glucose and other feedstocks. The reaction conditions are optimized, and kinetic studies for glucose and fructose conversions were carried out to provide theoretical guidance for the industrialization of the process.

Experiment

Materials

Fructose (≥99%), 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (≥97%), cellulose (GR), xylan (≥90%), and acetic acid (≥99.7%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.; LA (≥99%) and xylose (≥99%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd.; furfural (≥99%), formic acid (AR), H2SO4, and NaOH were purchased from Tianjin Damao Chemical Reagent Factory; glucose (AR) was purchased from Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory. All reagents were not further processed before use.

Experimental Method

The experiment was carried out in a sealed 50 mL autoclave with stirring (MS-50-316L, Anhui Kemi Machinery Technology Co., Ltd., Hefei, China). In a typical experiment, 0.125–6.0 g of glucose and 20 mL of deionized water were charged into the reactor. After that, a certain amount of nitrogen was charged into the sealed reactor to exclude the air residue. Finally, the autoclave was filled with pure N2 to a desired pressure of 0–5 MPa. The reaction was conducted in the temperature range of 170–210 °C for 0–12 h at 800 rpm. After each reaction, the reactor was naturally cooled to room temperature and the reaction solution was collected in a plastic bottle for further analysis.

Product Analysis

Before the liquid phase analysis, the products were filtered by a 0.22 μm aqueous filter. The qualitative analysis of liquid phase products was performed by GC–MS (Agilent, USA, INNOWAX column). The liquid phase products were quantitatively analyzed on a HPLC instrument with an SH1011 column (the size is 8.0 × 300 mm, and the diameter is 6 μm). The column temperature is set at 50 °C, using 0.05 mmol H2SO4 as the mobile phase. Glucose, fructose, LA, and others were detected by a 2414RI detector, and 5-HMF was detected by a 2489UV/Vis detector. We diluted all liquid phase samples a certain number of times using deionized water before measurement.

Results and Discussion

Composition of Products

Under hydrothermal conditions, the target product from glucose conversion is 5-HMF, together with the side ones involving fructose intermediate by isomerization of glucose, furfural by C–C splitting and acetic acid, LA, and formic acid via 5-HMF rehydration, etc.

As shown in Figure S1, the main component of the gaseous products is N2 with the other possibly produced gas components of less than 1% under all temperature and time conditions, indicating that the gaseous products from glucose conversion in these current hydrothermal conditions are negligible. Both GC–MS and HPLC results confirmed that the main product of glucose and fructose conversions is 5-HMF with the minor above-mentioned byproducts and a few unidentified compounds under different reaction conditions (Figures S2–S5).

Effect of Reaction Temperature on Glucose Dehydration to 5-HMF

The reaction temperature is a key factor affecting the reaction. The glucose conversion and yield of 5-HMF changed with the reaction temperature. As shown in Figure , when the reaction was carried out at 170 °C, the glucose conversion was 21.05% and the yield of 5-HMF was 14.94%. Under hydrothermal conditions at 180–190 °C, glucose undergoes rapid isomerization to form fructose, the critical intermediate for 5-HMF production. However, when the temperature exceeds 180 °C, the conversion kinetics of fructose are significantly enhanced, leading to its rapid depletion during the mid-to-late reaction stages. This phenomenon exhibits strong agreement with kinetic results derived from the Arrhenius equation (Figure ), indicating a competitive regulatory mechanism between fructose generation and consumption pathways under elevated temperatures. When the reaction was carried out at 210 °C; however, the glucose conversion increased to 78.70%, and the yield of 5-HMF rapidly increased to 56.10%, obtaining a selectivity of 71.3%. It can be seen that the conversion of glucose and the yield of 5-HMF are greatly affected by the reaction temperatures. One reason is that with the increase in temperature, the increased ionization constant of water leads to higher H+ and OH– concentrations in water, which plays a catalytic role in the formation of 5-HMF from glucose conversion via multisteps. ,, However, when the reaction temperature exceeded 180 °C, the selectivity of 5-HMF decreased and the side products increased slightly, showing that the higher temperatures accelerated the formation of byproducts. The amounts of other products were minimal, almost negligible. Fructose was obtained as the intermediate, but the yield was less than 5%. Increasing the reaction temperature promotes the conversion of glucose into 5-HMF and also promotes the formation of byproducts.

Effect of Reaction Time on Glucose Dehydration to 5-HMF

We investigated the product distribution at different reaction times, as shown in Figure . The conversion of glucose and the yield of 5-HMF were 21.30% and 17.04%, respectively, at 30 min. With the reaction time increased, the conversion of glucose and yield of 5-HMF increased, and the highest yield of 5-HMF was 64.50% with a 70.10% glucose conversion at 3 h of reaction, showing a high selectivity of 92%. The conversion of glucose and the yield of 5-HMF sharply increased in the first 3 h. Further extending the reaction time, the glucose conversion continued to increase to more than 90%, and the yield of 5-HMF slightly increased to the highest of 71% at 9 h, showing the reduced selectivity due to side reactions. At 12 h, both of them showed a downward trend and 5-HMF declined more obviously. It is preliminarily believed that the obvious decrease of 5-HMF at a longer reaction time is due to further polymerization or decomposition reaction of 5-HMF and thus accelerates the consumption of 5-HMF. It is obvious that a suitable reaction time is the key to obtaining high yield and selectivity of 5-HMF via suppressing the unwanted side products.

Influence of Reaction Pressure on Glucose Dehydration to 5-HMF

Reaction pressure is also an important factor in the reaction. The conversion of glucose and the yield of 5-HMF vary with the reaction pressure, as shown in Figure . The reaction pressure exhibited a negligible influence on the catalytic process. When the N2 pressure was elevated from 1 to 4 MPa, the product selectivity remained stable at approximately 80%. Meanwhile, the glucose conversion efficiency and 5-HMF yield demonstrated statistically insignificant fluctuations within this pressure range. When the reaction pressure was 3 MPa, the glucose conversion was 87.77% and the highest yield of 5-HMF was 71%.

Influence of Substrate Concentration on Glucose Dehydration to 5-HMF

The effect of glucose conversion and the 5-HMF yield on the initial concentrations of glucose is shown in Figure . It was observed that as the glucose concentration increased beyond 1.25 wt %, the obvious coking phenomenon was found, and a lot of insoluble black rot was found in the reaction solution. The above phenomenon well explains that the yield and selectivity of 5-HMF decrease significantly with the increase of the initial glucose concentrations. Consistent with the results reported in the literature, the polymerization reaction of the unreacted substrate with 5-HMF reduced the yield of 5-HMF. It is noted that LA was significantly increased under higher glucose concentrations due to the 5-HMF rehydration side reaction, which is another reason for obtaining the reduced 5-HMF.

Under a glucose concentration of 5 wt %, the effect of reaction temperature was investigated in Figures S6 and S7. As the temperature increased, the conversion of glucose and the yield of 5-HMF both rose correspondingly. Prolonging the reaction time also promoted the yield of 5-HMF to some extent. Under a process temperature of 180 °C, the selectivity of 5-HMF reached 91.47%. However, further increasing the glucose concentration led to an improved conversion but a decreased yield of 5-HMF due to the formation of more byproducts and severe charring. The reaction with fructose as the substrate showed similar trends as compared to those of glucose feedstock and also exhibited charring at high concentrations (Figures S8–S10).

In conclusion, by using in situ-generated H+ and OH– from HTLW and optimizing the reaction conditions, we obtained the highest 71% of 5-HMF yield with a high selectivity of 92% from glucose under hydrothermal conditions at 3 MPa N2 and 180 °C for 6 h.

Kinetic Analysis

During the production of 5-HMF from glucose, a small amount of fructose can be detected as an intermediate. To better understand the formation of 5-HMF, we carried out a study of the kinetics of preparing 5-HMF with glucose and fructose in liquid water at high temperatures. The trends for glucose and fructose conversions and the corresponding 5-HMF yields with the reaction time at different temperatures are shown in Figure A–D. At a relatively low temperature of 160 °C, as the reaction time extended, the yield of glucose’s primary product, fructose, initially increased. However, these primary products readily decompose into other secondary products, particularly under acidic conditions. When the temperature is elevated to 200 °C, the product yields exhibit varying trends before decreasing with further prolongation of the reaction time. Meanwhile, the yield of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (5-HMF) increased with the extension of the reaction time. Ultimately, the experimental results indicate that the optimal conditions are at a temperature of 180 °C for a duration of 6 h. It was found that the conversion of glucose and fructose and the yield of 5-HMF were significantly affected by the reaction temperature and time, showing good positive correlation trends.

By fitting the linear equation of ln­(k) on 1000/RT by the least-squares method, the prefinger factor is 1.15 × 1010 h–1 and 8.83 × 109 h–1, respectively. The experimental results showed that the activation energy of glucose preparation 5-HMF was 5.3% higher than fructose preparation 5-HMF, which is consistent with the results in the literature. , The higher activation energy for glucose conversion is due to its stable pyran structure as compared to the furan structure of fructose and more steps for 5-HMF formation.

The thermodynamic parameters of the reaction, evaluated based on the activation complex theory, , show a very good fit to the experimental data, with an R 2 value of >0.99. The relatively high value of the activation enthalpy (ΔH ‡) for the conversion of glucose to 5-HMF (∼90.8 kJ/mol) indicates that a relatively high energy is required to break the stable pyranose–glucose ring. The negative ΔS ‡(ΔS ‡ < 0) implies that the transition-state structure is more ordered than the ground-state reactants, thus resulting in a negative activation entropy. Meanwhile, the positive ΔH ‡ value (ΔH ‡ > 0) indicates that the process is endothermic, meaning that an external heat source is needed to increase the energy level and convert the reactants into the transition state. The obtained positive ΔG ‡ value indicates that the reaction is nonspontaneous (Table S3).

Catalytic Mechanism

Considering that organic acids like formic acid, LA, and acetic acid were produced during 5-HMF production from glucose conversion, to clarify the role of these acids in glucose conversion, we additionally introduced the possibly produced organic acids (formic acid, acetic acid, LA) in the reaction, and the results are shown in Figure . Obviously, the glucose conversion and product distribution are similar to the pristine one without any acid introduction, all of which show a similar 5-HMF yield of about 35%. Although the added organic acids influenced the distribution of byproducts, they did not significantly enhance the yield of 5-HMF from glucose under our hydrothermal conditions. This suggests that the acidity provided by these organic acids was not the primary driver for the conversion of glucose to 5-HMF in this specific reaction system.

To exclude the influence of trace metal ions released from the stainless steel autoclave, we carried out ICP–AES tests for the aqueous solution after the reaction. As shown in Figure , the typical metals like Fe, Mo, Sn, Ni, and Cd were detected but the very trace amounts could be ignored, which ruled out the possibility that the metals released from the autoclave played a catalytic role in the reaction.

To further understand the catalytic role of HTLW in the conversion of glucose to 5-HMF, we prepared aqueous solutions with different pH values for experimentation by introducing different amounts of H2SO4 or NaOH. The experimental results are shown in Figure . We found that both the conversion of glucose and the color of the reaction solution were significantly influenced by the pH value of the solution. In acidic environments (pH < 7), the glucose conversion increased as the pH value decreased (acidity increased). After reacting for 30 min in a water solution with pH = 2, the reaction solution turned brown, and the glucose conversion increased from the original 21.3% (at pH = 7, where the reaction solution was light yellow) to 47.5%. Additionally, the yield of 5-HMF was 33%, and a 4% yield of LA was obtained, indicating that 5-HMF is unstable in this acidic aqueous solutions with pH = 2 and further rehydrate to produce the side products such as LA and humins, etc. On the other hand, in basic environments (pH > 7), the glucose conversion significantly increased as the pH value increased (basicity increased). At pH = 13, the glucose conversion reached as high as 98.9%, and the reaction solution turned dark brown with severe coking, achieving a 23.1% yield of lactic acid without detecting the target product 5-HMF. Interestingly, we found that both weakly acidic (3 ≤ pH < 7) and weakly basic (7 < pH ≤ 11) environments were conducive to the isomerization of glucose to fructose, resulting in satisfactory yields of fructose. Specifically, in a weakly acidic environment of pH = 4, the yield of fructose reached 19% and the 5-HMF yield was 14%. In a weakly basic environment of pH = 10 and 11, both yields of fructose were also about 19%. Obviously, the acidic or basic environments are favorable for the isomerization of glucose to fructose. At the neutral condition (pH 7), despite the glucose conversion and 5-HMF yield being low at a short reaction time of 0.5 h, the high selectivity of 90% demonstrated that the spontaneous H+ and OH– from water ionization act as the synergistic acid and base catalytic sites for promoting glucose isomerization and the subsequent fructose dehydration to obtain 5-HMF, respectively, enhancing the integrated conversion and suppressing side product formation.

Bronsted acid or base are beneficial for the isomerization of glucose to fructose, but they inhibit the dehydration of fructose to produce 5-HMF, thereby obtaining a higher yield of fructose and promoting further decomposition of 5-HMF or the conversion of glucose to lactic acid as well as other side products, which is not conducive to the formation of 5-HMF. Typically, the mechanism for producing 5-HMF from glucose can be divided into two sequential steps: (1) glucose is isomerized to fructose catalyzed by Bronsted acid or base, and then fructose loses three molecules of water under the action of Bronsted acid (H+) to produce 5-HMF; , (2) glucose first loses a molecule of water under the action of Bronsted acid to form anhydroglucose, which then loses two more molecules of water to produce 5-HMF. However, during the reaction process, black insoluble humins and soluble polymers are easily produced, and 5-HMF further rehydrates to produce byproducts such as LA and FA. Among them, the isomerization of glucose under acidic conditions is the key step in determining the yield and selectivity of 5-HMF. It is believed that glucose first reaches a balance between α-glucose and β-glucose through mutarotation and then forms enol intermediates through H+ shift and protonation, which are isomerized to fructose. , The second step of fructose dehydration to produce 5-HMF survives two routes: the first one is a cyclic route through fructofuranose intermediates (acyclic intermediates), where, under acidic conditions, fructose first loses a molecule of water to form fructofuranose ring intermediates, which then continue to lose two molecules of water to form 5-HMF. The second one involves an acyclic route through enol intermediates (cyclic intermediates), where, under acidic conditions, fructose does not form cyclic intermediates but directly loses three molecules of water in a straight chain form to form 5-HMF. ,

Based on the above discussion, we speculate that the equivalent H+ and/or OH– from HTLW dissociation is responsible for the isomerization of glucose to fructose intermediate, after which three water molecules are further removed to obtain the final 5-HMF under the catalysis of H+ sites. Due to the limited H+ and OH– sites during the reaction, the rehydration of 5-HMF and other side reactions were suppressed to maintain its high yield of 70%. For the preparation of furan derivatives from carbohydrate feedstocks, the use of HTLW with dissociated acids (H+) and bases (OH–) is generally effective. As shown in Table S2, apart from xylose and glucose being detected at the low cellulose conversion due to its recalcitrant nature and proper C–C splitting (for the formation of xylose), using disaccharide sucrose, polymeric xylan, and monosaccharide xylose as the feedstock, significant 5-HMF and furfural could be produced at high conversions. Importantly, starting from xylose, a high furfural yield of 77% can be obtained. These findings demonstrated that the HTLW method is diversified for converting carbohydrates for the production of furanic platforms.

Conclusions

We designed a green way to produce 5-HMF, and we studied the reaction process of preparing 5-HMF by dehydration of glucose under hydrothermal conditions. The single-factor analysis was used to determine the best experimental conditions for producing 5-HMF from glucose. The yield of 5-HMF was 70.2%, and the selectivity was 80% when 1.25% glucose was reacted in a 3 MPa nitrogen atmosphere at 180 °C for 6 h. The HTLW could effectively promote the conversion of glucose and fructose to 5-HMF via the in situ-produced H+ and OH– sites from water ionization to enhance the sequential glucose isomerization and fructose dehydration. This method is also highly efficient for xylose conversion to furfural, obtaining a high yield of 77%. This strategy using spontaneously autogenic H+ and OH– from water for furanic platform formation proved the green and efficient utilization of lignocellulosic biomass and its derivatives.
