Efficacy of mental and physical training on blood pressure and resting heart rate among physically inactive young adults with elevated blood pressure: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Imtiyaz Ali Mir, Syeda Humayra, Anil T. John, Cai Hui Wan, Xue Ying Toh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Physically inactive adults with elevated blood pressure (BP) are at an increased risk of developing hypertension and cardiovascular comorbidity. Lifestyle-based non-pharmacological interventions are therefore essential. While mindfulness meditation and aerobic exercise independently improve BP, limited evidence exists on their combined effect—mental and physical training (MAPT). This study examined the effectiveness of MAPT on systolic BP (SBP), diastolic BP (DBP), and resting heart rate (RHR) among young adults with elevated BP. In a single-blinded randomized controlled trial, 58 young adults with elevated BP were allocated to 4 groups: mental training (MT, n = 14; mindfulness-based stress reduction), physical training (PT, n = 15; aerobic exercise at 40–60% heart rate reserve), MAPT (n = 15; combined MT and PT), and an active control group following the DASH diet (n = 14). Interventions were conducted 3 times weekly for 6-weeks. Outcomes were measured at baseline and post-intervention. Data were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA to examine time, group, and group-by-time interaction effects. Participants had a mean age of 20.2 ± 1.7 years and BMI of 22.4 ± 1.8; 43% reported a family history of hypertension. Significant SBP reductions were observed in MT (p = 0.002; ηp2 = 0.17), PT (p = 0.003; ηp2 = 0.15), and MAPT (p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.67), with MAPT demonstrating the largest effect. DBP decreased significantly in MT (p = 0.006; ηp2 = 0.13), PT (p = 0.002; ηp2 = 0.17), and MAPT (p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.31), though no significant group-by-time interaction was found. RHR declined significantly in PT (p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.28) and MAPT (p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.56). MAPT showed greater SBP reduction than MT (p = 0.04, 95% CI = − 9.62 to − 0.18) and DASH (p = 0.05, 95% CI = − 9.42 to 0.02), and greater RHR reduction than DASH (p = 0.03, 95% CI = − 14.08 to − 0.60). Integrating mindfulness with aerobic exercise improves SBP and RHR, though no significant differences emerged for DBP. MAPT appears to be a novel and potentially beneficial non-pharmacological intervention for managing BP and RHR in young adults with elevated BP.Trial registration This study was registered prospectively (09/05/2022) on clinicaltrials.gov with the registration no. NCT05533268 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05533268).

Introduction

Elevated blood pressure (BP) is defined as an office-measured systolic BP (SPB) between 120–139 mmHg and/or a diastolic BP (DBP) between 70–89 mmHg1. These values exceed the optimal BP range yet remain below the threshold for a clinical diagnosis of hypertension (HTN), representing a pre-hypertensive state associated with an increased risk of progression to overt HTN and cardiovascular morbidity2. Worldwide, the prevalence of elevated BP is increasing at an alarming rate, representing a significant public health concern and affecting 25% to 50% of the global population3. Malaysia mirrors these trends, with 41–46% of its population being affected, particularly young male adults4,5. Elevated BP induces significant abnormalities in the cardiovascular system, and its relationship with cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) is well documented in literature6. The risk of CVDs rise exponentially with an increase in SBP of 20 mmHg or DBP of 10 mmHg7. Consequently, individuals with elevated BP exhibit a twofold greater likelihood of developing CVDs compared to those with normal BP8. Among the various contributors to the rise of elevated BP, physical inactivity emerged as a dominant and modifiable risk factor9. It is a widespread phenomenon, impacting 27.5–31.3% of the adults globally10,11. Physical inactivity is a key contributor to HTN, CVD, stroke, and other non-communicable diseases (NCDs), responsible for over 5 million deaths annually9. In Malaysia, more than 37% of the adults are classified as physically inactive12. Alarmingly, the prevalence of physical inactivity is even higher among young adults, ranging between 41.4 and 66.4%13,14. In addition to physical inactivity, autonomic imbalance particularly in the form of elevated resting heart rate (RHR) has been identified as a significant contributor to increased BP and the development of cardiovascular dysfunction15,16. Research demonstrates a positive correlation between elevated RHR and increased sympathetic nervous system activity, coupled with reduced parasympathetic tone, resulting in high vascular resistance and cardiac workload, factors that accelerate the progression from elevated BP to established HTN16,17. It has been further reported that individuals with a higher RHR (≥ 90 beats/minute) demonstrate an increased risk of all-cause mortality18.

Elevated BP is a precursor of HTN and warrants significant lifestyle modifications and non-pharmacological management to prevent its progression to HTN19,20. Lifestyle changes, especially regular physical training (PT), has proven to reduce SBP/DBP and RHR through improved endothelial function, autonomic regulation, and cardiac efficiency21. Moderate to vigorous PT can lower SBP by 7.5 mmHg and DBP by 4.4 mmHg, significantly decreasing CVD risk and mortality21. Mental training (MT), encompassing interventions like mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) presents a low-cost, accessible approach that has shown beneficial effects on autonomic regulation22. MBSR is a “non-judgmental, present-moment awareness of physical sensations, emotions and thoughts,” and involves objectively observing sensory input and mental phenomena, then responding skilfully to these stimuli23. A growing body of evidence strongly supports the positive impact of MT, confirming MT’s efficacy in lowering BP across populations with elevated BP and HTN24,25.

Globally, elevated BP and physical inactivity poses significant public health challenges and demands urgent attention3,11. Although the individual benefits of PT and MT in managing BP have been previously studied, there remains a notable paucity of research exploring the synergistic or cumulative effects of mental and physical training (MAPT) interventions when applied in combination, particularly among physically inactive young adults with elevated BP. No study to date has systematically investigated the combined impact of PT and MT on BP and RHR, representing a critical knowledge gap. Given the distinct but potentially convergent pathways through which PT and MT exert their effects, their integration may offer enhanced benefits. Addressing this gap could facilitate the development of holistic, non-pharmacological strategies to mitigate the early progression of HTN. We hypothesized that the MAPT intervention would produce greater reductions in BP and RHR compared to MT alone, PT alone, and the DASH diet among physically inactive young adults with elevated BP.

Methods

Study design and participant recruitment

This single-blinded randomized controlled trial, in which outcome assessors were blinded was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (Fig. 1) and was prospectively registered at the clinicaltrials.gov (ID: NCT05533268). The research was carried out at physio centre and research lab, Universiti Tunku Abdul Rahman (UTAR), Sungai Long Campus in Kajang, Selangor, Malaysia. Participants were reached through social media platforms, emails, and poster advertisements for voluntary participation and recruited using a purposive sampling method. Participant recruitment commenced on 11 July 2022 and continued through 16 December 2022. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1) for a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (within–between interaction), with 4 parallel groups and 2 measurement time points (baseline and post-intervention). The calculation assumed 90% statistical power, a two-sided α of 0.05 and a moderate interaction effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.30). This effect size was selected to reflect a clinically meaningful group-by-time interaction while avoiding overestimation, and is consistent with effect sizes commonly reported in lifestyle-based BP intervention trials in young adults in the absence of prior data on integrated MAPT interventions. The estimated required sample was 44 participants; allowing for 30% attrition, the final target sample size was 58. Recruited participants were randomly assigned into 3 experimental (MT, PT, MAPT) and 1 active control groups (DASH) using a computer-generated block randomization sequence (block size of 4, allocation ratio 1:1:1:1) to ensure an unbiased and truly random assignment of participants. Allocation concealment was then implemented to prevent foreknowledge of group assignments and minimize the risk of selection bias. Allocation concealment was strictly managed through the use of sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes (SNOSE). To ensure the integrity of the randomization process, these were prepared by an external researcher and remained sealed until baseline data collection was completed, thereby reducing potential selection bias. Due to the behavioural nature of the interventions, participant blinding was not feasible. However, outcome assessors remained blinded to participant recruitment, randomization, group allocation and intervention delivery throughout data collection to minimize measurement and detection bias.Fig. 1CONSORT diagram. The inclusion criteria in this study were physically inactive young male adults (males were exclusively selected due to their 3–4 times higher prevalence of elevated BP compared to females)26,27, aged 18–25 years with a normal body mass index (BMI = 18.5–24.9), having an SBP between 120–139 mmHg and/or DBP between 70–89 mmHg, without any hearing abnormalities, and willing to participate in this study. Participants were excluded if they had any kind of cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, neurological disorders, or any medical condition limiting aerobic activity. Those who were on antihypertensive or BP-affecting medications, had hearing impairments, or were practicing relaxation techniques such as yoga or meditation were also excluded. Initial screening was conducted using the physical activity readiness questionnaire (PAR-Q +) to ensure medical fitness and readiness of participants for physical activity (PA); while the international physical activity questionnaire short form (IPAQ) was utilised to determine their PA levels. Individuals with a cut-off of ≥ 600 metabolic equivalent of task minutes/week score were considered physically active and hence excluded from the study28. Interim analyses were prospectively specified and conducted according to predefined statistical stopping boundaries for efficacy, safety, or futility. No early stopping criteria were met during the trial.

Interventions

Mental training (MT)

The MT implemented in this study was based on MBSR approach by Jon Kabat-Zinn29. All the participants in the MT group were required to listen to the audio of Guided MT for 30-min, 3 times/week for 6-weeks in a sitting position. MT was carried out as a group therapy and incorporated breathing meditation and body scanning. During breathing meditation, participants were instructed to feel the air move in and out of the lungs without changing the rate and depth of breath. Participants were guided to direct their attention to abdomen and feel the abdomen rise when breathing in and feel it fall when breathing out in a rhythmic fashion. This was followed by body scanning, in which participants were guided to focus on various body parts to become aware of the feelings or any kind of sensation (tingling, moisture, cold, warmth, itch etc.) non-judgementally. This was done by feeling these sensations in the toes first and slowly moving up to feet, legs, thighs, abdomen, chest, back, arms, shoulders, neck and finally to head.

Physical Training (PT)

PT group performed moderate intensity aerobic exercise on a treadmill for 30-min, 3 times/week for 6-weeks. Before beginning the first exercise session, each participant’s maximum heart rate (HRmax) was calculated using the HUNT formula [HRmax = 211-(0.64*age)] which is reported to be a better predictor of HRmax than the original Åstrand age prediction30. After predicting the HRmax, the exercise HR at a given percentage (40–60% HR reserve [HRR]) of training intensity was calculated for each exercise session using the Karvonen formula: Exercise HR = [40–60% HRR] + RHR, where HRR = HRmax–RHR. Participants began with a 5–10-min warm-up session followed by 30-min of continuous running on treadmill (Pro Action G6700KER) without inclination, which was followed by 5–10-min of cooldown by slowing down the speed of the treadmill and walking at their own comfortable pace before ending the exercise session. The dynamic stretching protocol as a warm-up targeted 6 major lower-limb muscle groups (hamstrings, quadriceps, gluteus, psoas, adductors and calf). Participants performed each muscle stretch independently, holding the stretch for 10–15 s and repeating it 3–4 times on both legs. They were instructed to stretch only to the point of mild discomfort and avoid any painful stretching. To ensure participants remained within the target intensity of 40–60% HRR, exercise HR was continuously monitored using the treadmill’s built-in sensor and a pulse oximeter (GIMA OXY-5). The absolute and relative indications for termination of the exercise session according to American College of Sports Medicine guidelines were strictly followed31.

Mental and physical training (MAPT)

MAPT group engaged in both MT (MBSR) and PT (moderate intensity aerobic exercise). The MAPT intervention implemented was similar to one used by Shors et al., (2018), where investigators used mindfulness for 30-min followed by 30-min of moderate-intensity aerobic training32. Precisely, 3 MAPT sessions were conducted every week for a period of 6-weeks. First participants engaged in listening to the audio of MBSR for 30-min followed by 30-min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (excluding the warm-up and cooldown) at their 40–60% of HRR on a treadmill (Pro action G6700KER). A 15-min break was provided at the end of MT sessions, before participants engaged in PT. To avoid any discrepancies in the intervention, participants in the MAPT group followed the exact same training program and procedures as executed for the MT group and PT group. Participants in all 4 groups were instructed to refrain from engaging in any additional PA or structured relaxation training outside the allocated intervention throughout the study period.

Dietary approaches to stop hypertension (DASH)

The DASH group served as the active control group and were provided with a detailed handout on the DASH diet (Fig. 2), which recommended increased intake of vegetables, fruits, and low-fat dairy, along with moderate consumption of poultry, fish, whole grains, and nuts, while limiting sodium intake to less than 2 g per day33. Participants were required to attend the research facility once per week, with supplementary weekly phone calls conducted to reinforce adherence to the dietary protocol. Adherence to the DASH diet was assessed throughout the study period using self-reported dietary logs to estimate participant compliance. None of the participants across all 4 groups reported any harmful or unintended effects resulting from the intervention.Fig. 2DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) Guidelines.

Outcome measures

All outcome measures were assessed in accordance with the standard protocols recommended by the Ministry of Health Malaysia and the American Heart Association, ensuring participants abstained from smoking, food intake, caffeine, or exercise at least 30-min prior to BP assessment34,35. SBP and DBP were designated as the primary endpoints, as they directly reflect the main objectives of the intervention in relation to BP control. RHR, on the other hand, was evaluated as a secondary endpoint, providing additional insight into the cardiovascular adaptations associated with the intervention. Participants were seated for 5-min in a relaxed posture before BP and RHR were measured using the electronic Omron HEM-7122-Z and GIMA OXY-5 devices respectively. Omron HEM-7122-Z model is validated by European Society of Hypertension and is proven for its reliability and accuracy, whereas GIMA OXY-5 is a highly accurate and calibrated pulse oximeter, which determines the HR accurately36,37. BP was recorded twice from both arms, with 1–2 min interval, and the average of 2 readings from the arm with the higher BP was used, minimizing misclassification due to inter-arm differences. RHR was simultaneously recorded, and an average of 2 readings were obtained. Measurements were conducted between 9:00 AM and 10:00 AM to reduce circadian variability, and all assessments were performed at baseline and after 6-weeks of intervention.

Data analysis

All the data obtained was recorded into Microsoft Office Excel 2019 and subsequently analysed using IBM SPSS version 24.0. The demographic data computed for descriptive analysis were age, BMI and family history of HTN in all the 4 groups. Frequency and percentage were used to analyse the qualitative data such as the percentage of participants with family history of HTN. For inferential analysis, Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess the normality of demographic data. To examine if there were any significant differences in dependent variables (SBP, DBP and RHR) at baseline, a one-way ANOVA test was run. Finally, for within and between group differences at end-point, two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted using 2 factors, time and group to determine the main effect of time within each group and the interaction effect between the 2 factors followed by a post-hoc pairwise comparison to evaluate which groups significantly differed from one another. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise error rate and reduce type I error across multiple comparisons. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta-squared (ηp2) to determine the magnitude of observed effects, with values interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14). In both ANOVA analyses, the assumption of homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test of equality. Additionally, for the two-way repeated measures ANOVA, the assumption of sphericity was examined using Mauchly’s Test. When sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied; otherwise, unadjusted results were reported. The significance level for all tests was set at p ≤ 0.05. All analyses were conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle, including all participants as randomized, irrespective of adherence or withdrawal. A total of 4 participants discontinued the study; 1 from the MAPT group (ankle sprain), 1 from the PT group (family reasons) and 2 from the DASH group (lost to follow-up). Missing outcome data for these 4 participants were addressed using multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption, with results pooled across imputations and complete-case analyses performed as a sensitivity check.

Figure 3 illustrates that MAPT intervention produced the most substantial reduction in SBP, with mean values decreasing from 129.7 ± 5.2 mmHg at baseline to 116.9 ± 7.2 mmHg at end-point (p ˂ 0.001), with a larger effect size (ηp2 = 0.67). The MT group also exhibited a significant decline in SBP, from 130.3 ± 6.0 mmHg to 126.1 ± 4.1 mmHg (p = 0.002). Similarly, participants in the PT group experienced a significant reduction, with SBP decreasing from 128.5 ± 4.3 mmHg to 124.6 ± 5.2 mmHg (p = 0.003). In contrast, the DASH diet group showed only a modest and statistically non-significant reduction in SBP (128.9 ± 4.4 mmHg vs. 127.1 ± 4.6 mmHg; p = 0.176) within the study duration.

The within-group comparisons presented in Fig. 4 indicate statistically significant reductions in DBP among participants in the MT and PT interventions. Specifically, the MT group demonstrated a decrease in DBP from 79.8 ± 4.2 mmHg at baseline to 76.9 ± 4.5 mmHg post-intervention (p = 0.006). Similarly, the PT group exhibited a significant reduction from 80.4 ± 5.3 mmHg to 77.2 ± 4.8 mmHg (p = 0.002). The greatest improvement was observed in the MAPT group, where DBP decreased from 78.6 ± 4.6 mmHg to 73.8 ± 5.7 mmHg (p ˂ 0.001), indicating a larger effect size (ηp2 = 0.31). Conversely, the DASH group demonstrated a statistically non-significant reduction in DBP over the study period (78.4 ± 6.0 mmHg vs. 77.2 ± 6.3 mmHg; p = 0.224).

From baseline to end-point, statistically significant reductions in RHR were observed in the PT and MAPT groups (Fig. 5). In the PT group, mean RHR decreased from 80.7 ± 7.4 to 73.3 ± 7.9 beats per minute (p ˂ 0.001). An even greater reduction was observed in the MAPT group, with RHR decreasing from 83.2 ± 8.6 to 69.9 ± 7.8 beats per minute (p ˂ 0.001), indicating a large within-group effect (ηp2 = 0.56). The findings in Fig. 5 therefore suggest meaningful physiological improvements in autonomic or cardiovascular regulation within these 2 intervention arms. However, no statistically significant changes were detected in the MT group (79.1 ± 5.8 vs. 76.3 ± 4.8 beats per minute; p = 0.09) or the DASH group (83.1 ± 6.9 vs. 84.7 ± 8.3 beats per minute; p = 0.327). These findings underscore the comparatively limited effectiveness of MT and the DASH diet in reducing RHR over the intervention period.

Effect of intervention over time on SBP, DBP, and RHR between groups

MAPT demonstrated greater reductions in SBP compared with MT (p = 0.04) and DASH (p = 0.05), suggesting a comparatively stronger antihypertensive effect of this intervention. Discussion

This study emphasized the effectiveness of MAPT in controlling BP and RHR among physically inactive young adults with elevated BP. Although mindfulness meditation and aerobic training respectively have been reported to be associated with improving BP, but no previous studies has explored the combined effect of these approaches. Conclusively, our study findings reveal that integrating MAPT intervention produced a significantly greater reduction in SBP and improvement in RHR rather than implementing the interventions exclusively. These results underscore the importance of incorporating MAPT in managing BP and its effectiveness in improving cardiovascular health markers in physically inactive young adults with elevated BP. MT fosters a mindset of acceptance and non-judgment, which can make it easier to engage with and persist through potentially uncomfortable or challenging experiences38. In parallel, PT can boost feelings of achievement and motivation, further reinforcing the desire to continue both the exercise and meditation practices39. Additionally, MT and PT practices both share the same underlying neuropsychological mechanisms40,41. Hence, practicing these approaches concurrently can amplify the cognitive and emotional benefits, and create a profound lasting impact on overall well-being. The combined MAPT intervention may therefore yield a synergistic effect by improving exercise adherence and amplifying neuropsychological and physiological benefits40,42.

In previous research, MAPT has shown notable benefits across diverse patient populations, including individuals with major depression43, cancer survivors44, Parkinson’s disease45, chronic lower back pain46, multiple sclerosis47, and age-related cognitive decline48. Nevertheless, its impact on cardiovascular markers like SBP, DBP, and RHR in physically inactive population with elevated BP still remains unexplored. Previously, a study investigating the effects of a single session of mindful PA on HR, BP, and anxiety in healthy adults found that moderate to vigorous PA alone or combined with mindfulness produced similar physiological responses42. SBP remained unchanged across all groups, while DBP increased significantly. HR escalated in the PA and combined groups but declined in the mindfulness-only group from pre-test to post-test. Whereas, anxiety decreased most significantly in the mindfulness condition, showing a notable time-by-condition interaction42. In contrast, the current study demonstrated a significant main effect of time on SBP and DBP across the MT, PT, and MAPT groups from baseline to post-test, as well as a significant reduction in RHR in the PT and MAPT groups. A significant time-by-condition interaction was observed for SBP, indicating greater reductions in the MAPT group compared to the MT and DASH groups, though not significantly different from the PT group. Similarly, for RHR, significant time-by-condition interactions were found between MAPT and DASH, and PT and DASH groups, but not across other groups. However, no significant interaction effects were observed for DBP across any of the groups. Although the study was powered to detect moderate group-by-time effects, it may have been underpowered to detect smaller between-group differences for DBP.

The MAPT significantly reduced SBP (− 12.8 mmHg) and modestly decreased DBP (− 4.4 mmHg), reflecting its efficacy over the other experimental arms. Given that an increase in SBP of 20 mmHg and DBP of 10 mmHg doubles the CVD risk7,8, while a reduction of SBP by 10 mmHg and DBP by 5 mmHg could potentially lead to a 20% decrease in adverse CVD events49, even the modest BP reductions are clinically meaningful. SBP and DBP are regulated by distinct haemodynamic and neurovascular mechanisms. SBP is primarily influenced by cardiac output, large arterial compliance, and sympathetic nervous system activity, whereas DBP is more closely associated with peripheral vascular resistance and arteriolar tone50,51. This differential response between SBP and DBP is physiologically plausible and has been consistently reported in exercise and lifestyle-based intervention studies52,53. Interventions such as mindfulness and aerobic exercise are known to exert early effects on autonomic balance through reduction in sympathetic activity and enhancement in parasympathetic modulation. These adaptations preferentially improve large artery compliance and reduce peak ventricular ejection pressure, leading to more immediate and pronounced reductions in SBP54,55. In contrast, meaningful reduction in DBP often require longer-term structural adaptations within the peripheral vasculature, including improvement in endothelial function, reduction in arteriolar stiffness and sustained increase in nitric oxide bioavailability56,57. The 6-weeks intervention period employed in the present study is therefore likely to have captured predominantly early neural and autonomic adaptations rather than slower-developing vascular structural changes. This observation is consistent with evidence from prior meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials indicating that reduction in DBP tend to emerge later than reduction in SBP, particularly when intervention durations are shorter than 8–12 weeks52,58. Baseline BP characteristics of the study population may have further contributed to the attenuated DBP response. Participants were young physically inactive adults, many of whom presented with DBP values closer to the normal range at baseline compared to SBP. When baseline DBP levels are near normal, the potential for further reduction is limited due to a physiological floor effect56,59. In contrast, SBP demonstrates greater variability and sensitivity to lifestyle-related factors such as stress, autonomic imbalance and physical inactivity, rendering it more responsive to short-term interventions in younger populations60. Additionally, the MT component may have preferentially influenced SBP through stress reduction and autonomic modulation, mechanisms that primarily reduce cardiac output and sympathetic activity. Consequently, SBP reduction tend to occur earlier, whereas meaningful DBP reduction may require longer intervention durations or greater training intensity54,61.

MAPT participants demonstrated the greatest reduction in RHR (− 13.3 beats/min) compared with the other groups. The findings align with prior studies showing that aerobic exercise and mindfulness independently reduce RHR62,63, and that longer-duration programs are typically more effective64. RHR reduction is attributed to increased vagal tone, reduced sympathetic activity, and possible changes in intrinsic cardiac rhythm. These changes reflect improved autonomic balance, with parasympathetic reactivation dominating early recovery15,16,18. Stress reduction via mindfulness may further contribute by lowering the levels of cortisol, which typically elevates HR through sympathetic activation. Given that stress is prevalent among young adults, the MAPT protocol’s stress-alleviating effects plausibly played a key role in improving RHR65.

Although MAPT demonstrated the largest mean reductions in SBP and RHR, pairwise comparisons (Table 3) indicate that PT alone was not significantly different from MAPT for several outcomes. Importantly, the apparent superiority of MAPT in Table 2 reflects within-group changes and does not establish the non-inferiority of PT in direct comparison with MAPT. Moreover, MAPT involved a longer intervention duration (60 min) relative to MT or PT alone (30 min), raising the possibility that the greater reductions in SBP and RHR may partly reflect a dose–response effect rather than a true synergistic benefit of combining the MT and PT. Therefore, it remains plausible that extending PT to 60 min could yield improvements comparable to MAPT, or conversely, that reducing MAPT to 30 min (e.g., 15-min MT + 15-min PT) might attenuate its observed effect. Future trials should adopt time-matched designs to determine whether MAPT confers additive benefits beyond those attributable to intervention intensity and duration.

Limitations and recommendations

Due to time constraint, intervention duration of this study was only 6-weeks. That could be one of the reasons, why DBP did not show any significant difference between groups. Using longer intervention durations, particularly 8-weeks or more, is crucial for future studies aimed at observing significant changes in BP, specifically in DBP. This extended time frame allows for appropriate physiological adaptations and more reliable findings, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of the MAPT intervention in lowering BP.

Participants in this study were recruited from a single institution, which may have introduced sampling bias and consequently restricted the extent to which the findings can be generalized to broader populations. Future multicentre studies involving diverse populations are recommended to enhance the external validity.

A methodological limitation of this trial was the unequal intervention dose across groups. Participants in the MT and PT groups received 30-min sessions, whereas those in the MAPT group received a combined 60-min intervention per session. This imbalance introduces a potential confounding influence of total training volume, thereby restricting the interpretability of between-group comparisons. Therefore, future trials should employ time-matched control interventions to isolate mechanistic contributions. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution and considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating.

While participants in the active control group received a detailed debriefing at the beginning of the study, along with weekly meetings and reminder phone calls to encourage adherence to the DASH dietary guidelines, continuous monitoring and observation were not feasible. Based on the self-reported dietary logs, which are inherently subject to recall and reporting bias, 57% of participants did not adhere to the prescribed intervention. Accordingly, the lack of BP reduction in the DASH group should not be interpreted as evidence against the efficacy of the DASH diet per se, but rather reflects real-world adherence limitations in unsupervised dietary interventions. Hence, future studies should focus on continuous supervision, education, and monitoring which are essential to ensure that participants follow the DASH diet appropriately, ultimately achieving its intended BP-lowering effects.

Furthermore, future studies should incorporate long-term follow-up to assess the sustained benefits of MAPT in terms of reducing BP and RHR. Long-term follow-up allow researchers to determine if the intervention’s effects are maintained over time. Short-term improvements due to MAPT may not necessarily equate to lasting benefits. Long-term follow-up may also help identify whether the observed changes in BP and RHR remain stable or diminish after the intervention concludes.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that MAPT was associated with greater improvements in SBP and RHR compared with individual training protocols and the DASH diet. While previous research has primarily examined the independent effects of mindfulness or aerobic training on BP, this study uniquely explored their integrated impact and was associated with greater cardiovascular benefits for SBP and RHR, though DBP did not change significantly. The combined practice of mindfulness and aerobic exercise may foster a positive feedback mechanism that enhances engagement and optimizes the benefits of both interventions.

In conclusion, MAPT appears to be an effective intervention for reducing SBP and improving RHR in physically inactive young adults with elevated BP. As a novel non-pharmacological approach, MAPT may contribute to BP regulation, RHR reduction, and potentially prevent the risk of CVDs in adult population with elevated BP.
