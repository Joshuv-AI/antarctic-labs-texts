Opioids for management of endometriosis associated pain: balancing effectiveness and quality of life
Authors: Yael Roza Douak, Nir Cohen, Ilan Matok, Tal Saar, Uri Dior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify risk factors for opioid use and assess the effect of opioids on women with endometriosis.DesignA retrospective cohort study.SettingA tertiary medical center.ParticipantsWomen with confirmed or suspected endometriosis aged 18–45 years.MethodsRecruited participants completed a questionnaire including baseline demographic and general health data, a Brief Pain Inventory (BPI-Heb) and a Visual Analogue Scale assessing Endometriosis associated pain symptoms. Women using opioids were compared to women not using opioids. Women treated with opioids further completed a Visual Analogue Scale quesionniare of pain symptoms after using opioids, a questionnaire assessing side effects, and the Opioid Risk Tool for the assessment of addiction risk.ResultsNinety-eight patients were included in the study. Of them, 49 were opioid users, and 49 were non-opioid users. One unit increase in overall pain was associated with a 28% increased risk of opioid use [OR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.1–1.5, p = .007]. Use of hormonal therapy and smoking also increased opioid use risk [OR = 6.16, 95% CI 1.7–22.5, p = 0.006, OR = 4.36, 95% CI 1.2–16.1, p = .027, respectively]. Within the opioid users group, all pain symptoms and quality of life domains were significantly improved, with the most significant improvement demonstrated in dysmenorrhea and sleeping quality (median change: -4.5 (p < .001) and 5 (p < .001) points, respectively). While there were no life-threatening side effects, 94% of women using opioids experienced at least one side effect and 33% had moderate to high risk of addiction.ConclusionIn our study, the risk of opioid use among women with endometriosis was increased for women with hormonal therapy, smoking and higher pain severity. While opioids were associated with high rates of side effects, they were effective pain relievers and overall quality of life was improved. More research is needed to delineate better the role and risks of opioids for the treatment of endometriosis associated pain.Keywords: Endometriosis- associated pain, Quality of life, Risk of addiction

Introduction

Endometriosis, affecting 10% of reproductive age women [1], is associated with pelvic pain and infertility and may severely affect quality of life [2–4].

Management of endometriosis-associated pain (EAP) poses a challenge with no one analgetic consistently showing high rates of success [5]. Pain management options encompass a range of analgesics, hormonal treatments, various alternative therapies, and laparoscopic surgery for excision or ablation of endometriosis lesions [6]. Assessing the effectiveness of pharmacologic treatment for EAP is challending and approximately 30% of women with endometriosis develop chronic pain that remains unresponsive to conventional treatment [7].

Scarce data exists on the efficacy of analgesics in general and opioids in particular on EAP. Very few studies have assessed the impact of opioids in treating EAP, with most of them primarily focusing on treatment safety [8–10].

While shown to be effective pain relievers in many pain conditions [11], opioids are associated with significant side effects such as physical dependence, increased sensitivity to pain, reduced level of attention, hallucinations, and hypothermia [12]. Though uncommon, opioids may also cause life threatening respiratory depression [13]. In addition,opioids carry risks of abuse, addiction, and overdose, which can result in death [14]. Therefore, the long-term use of opioids, particularly for the treatment of non-cancer chronic pain, is controversial [15].

As the role of opioids in the treatment of EAP is understudied, we sought to assess the effectiveness of opioids on pain symptoms and quality of life of women with endometriosis. We also aimed to evaluate the side effects rate and opioid addiction risk in these women.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Women attending an endometriosis clinic in a tertiary medical center in Jerusalem, Israel, between August 2022 and February 2023 were recruited to the study.

Included were women aged 18–45 years with confirmed or suspected endometriosis. Each patient attending our clinic undergoes comprehensive symptom questioning, including dysmenorrhoea, dyspareunia, dyschezia, dysuria and non-menstrual pelvic pain and an endometriosis-directed pelvic examination. An endometriosis-dedicated ultrasound is carried out when physical examination raises suspicion of deep endometriosis. Diagnosis was based on laparoscopic surgery, ultrasound or clinical diagnosis. If surgery was not performed and imaging studies did not reveal deep or ovarian endometriosis, the diagnosis was clinical and was defined as persistent and/or worsening pelvic pain, with other endometriosis associated symptoms [16]. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation, fibromyalgia and inability to complete the study questionnaire.

Aim and outcomes

Our primary aim was to identify risk factors for opioid use among endometriosis patients and to assess the effect of opioids on EAP and quality of life. Secondary outcomes included evaluation of the rate and types of adverse effects and assessment of the risk of addiction among women with endometriosis who use opioids.

Data collection

Recruited participants completed a questionnaire including: (1) Baseline demographic and general health data; (2) a Brief Pain Inventory translated into Hebrew (BPI-Heb) including the following items: questions on pain levels, utilization of medications or other treatments to alleviate pain and interference of pain with various domains of quality of life (including general activity, mood, sleeping, walking, relationships, working and pleasures of life) [17]; and (3) A Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) of the following: overall pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, dysuria, dyschezia, and non-menstrual pelvic pain (NMPP). Women who reported the use of opioids furthermore completed the following questionnaires: (1) BPI-Heb after using opioids; (2) VAS after using opioids; (3) Questionnaire to assess opioids side effects: constipation, nausea, vomiting, itching, dizziness, lethargy, agitation, anxiety, depression, and respiratory depression; and (4) The Opioid Risk Tool (ORT) for the assessment of the addiction risk (at the time of evaluation rather than risk assessed prior to opioid initiation) [18].

To assess the risk of opioid use, we first compared baseline characteristics and pain symptoms between women who used opioids and women who did not use opioids. We then compared the baseline effectiveness of non-opioid analgesics between both groups.

Frequency, type, and dosage of opioids were assessed within the opioid group. We then compared quality of life domains and EAP symptoms before and after using opioids. Finally, we assessed the frequency and type of side effects, as well as the risk of opioid addiction within the opioid users group.

Participants completed the questionnaires during routine clinic visits. Pain severity and quality-of-life measures prior to opioid initiation were retrospectively reported by participants at the time of questionnaire completion, based on their recollection of symptoms before beginning opioid treatment. Participants were also asked to report their symptoms after using opioids. Therefore, the assessment of symptom improvement reflects patient-reported perceived change rather than prospectively recorded pre-treatment measurements.

Statistical analysis

Based on previous studies, it was determined that a change of 1.5 points on the VAS score would be considered a clinically significant change [19–24]. Therefore, to reject the null hypotheses of no benefit of opioid use in women with endometriosis, at a significance level (α) of 5% and a power of 80%, the calculated sample size was 48 patients in the study group (women using opioids) and 48 patients in the control group (women not using opioids).

Baseline characteristics of women from both groups (opioid and non-opioid users) were compared with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and with the student’s t-test for continuous variables. Continuous variables were described using either mean with standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables or median with interquartile range (IQR) for variables with non-normal distribution. Multivariate logistic regression models were constructed to identify independent risk factors associated with the risk of using opioids. Odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed. Variables included were selected based on clinical relevance and potential association with opioid use as well as variables that differed between groups in univariate comparisons. Results were considered significant at a p-value of less than 0.05 (2-tailed). Within the opioid users group, the Wilcoxon test was used to compare quality of life and endometriosis associated pain domains before and after using opioids. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software version 25.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (HMO-22-0305), and each study participant signed a consent form.

Results

Study participants

Ninety-eight patients were included in the study. Of them, 49 were opioid users, and 49 were non-opioid users. Table 1 shows the participants’ baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. Smoking and use of hormonal therapy were more common among the opioid users group, as compared to the non-opioid users group (smoking: 37.8% versus 18.6%, p = 0.046; hormonal therapy: 69.4% versus 20.4%, p = 0.003). Opioid users were more likely to undergo laparoscopic surgery for the treatment of endometriosis, as compared to non-opioid users (70.6% versus 20.4%, p = 0.003).

Histopathologically confirmed superficial endometriosis was present in 30.6% (n = 15) of the opioid group and in 14.3% (n = 7) of the non-opioids group, p = 0.053. Clinically suspected endometriosis was present in 40.8% (n = 20) of the opioid users group and in 46.9% (n = 23) of the non-opioid users group, p = 0.541.

Deep endometriosis was diagnosed in 18.4% (n = 9) of the opioid group and 10.2% (n = 5) of the control group, p = 0.248. Ovarian endometriosis was diagnosed in 10.5% (n = 5) of the opioid group and 28.6% (n = 14) of the non-opioid group, p = 0.021. Mixed forms of endometriosis were characterized by the dominant form. Age, body mass index, parity, and medical comorbidities did not differ between groups.

Outcomes

Paracetamol was used by 91.8% (n = 45) of opioid users and by 71.4% (n = 35) of the non-opioid users, p = 0.009. Non-Steroidal Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) were used by 91.8% (n = 45) of opioid users and 81.6% (n = 40) of the non-opioid group, p = 0.14. Dipyrone was used by 75.5% (n = 37) and 55.1% (n = 27) of the opioid and non-opioid users, respectively, p = 0.03. Use of medical cannabis was reported by 44.9% (n = 22) and 8.2% (n = 4) of the opioid and non-opioid users, respectively, p < 0.001.

A multivariate model including age, hormonal therapy, smoking, pain comorbidities, use of NSAIDs, use of Dipyrone, and overall pain intensity has shown that hormonal therapy, smoking and worse overall pain were associated with risk of opioid use with an odds ratio [OR] of 1.3 [95% CI 1.1- 1.5, p = 0.007] per one unit increase in overall pain, an OR of 4.4 [95% CI 1.2–16.1] for smoking and an OR of 6.2 [95% CI 1.7–22.5, p = 0.006] for the use of hormonal therapy, as shown in Table 2.

Opioid users

Of the opioid users, 68.9% (n = 31) used Tramadol, 15.6% (n = 7) used Codeine, and 15.6% (n = 7) used Oxycodone. Two types of opioids were used simultaneously by 24.5% (n = 12) of patients. Among Tramadol users, 90.3% (n = 28) used a combined tramadol and paracetamol formulation.

The median time of opioid use was 1.5 years (IQR 0.50–4.25). Six women (13.1%) reported the use of opioids at least once daily, 9 (19.6%) reported weekly use, and 23 (50.0%) reported using opioids at least once a month. Three women (6.1%) have not answered this question, and 8 (16.3%) have reported inconsistent frequency of opioid use.

Among opioid users, the use of opioids was associated with a significant reduction in all EAP domains. The most prominent effects were observed in dysmenorrhea, NMPP and dysuria with median changes of −4.5 points (VAS) for dysmenorrhea (IQR: 8.0–10.0 before, 2.0–5.0 after, p-value < 0.001), −4 points for non-menstrual pelvic pain (IQR: 6.0–8.0 before, 1.8–5.0 after, p-value < 0.001) and −3.8 points for dysuria (IQR: 5.0–7.0 before, 1.0–5.0 after, p-value 0.004) (Fig. 1A).

All quality-of-life domains, as collected by the BPI-HEB questionnaire, were reported to be significantly improved after treatment with opioids. The most prominent effects were observed in influence of pain on sleeping quality and pleasures of life with median chages of −5 points (VAS) for the influence of pain on sleeping quality (IQR: 3.0–9.0 before, 0.0–5.0 after, p-value < 0.001) and −4 poinsts for pleasures of life (IQR: 5.0–9.0 before, 2.0–8.0 after, p-value < 0.001) (Fig. 1B).

Opioid-related side effects were reported by 94% (n = 46) of opioid users and are detailed in Fig. 2. The most common side effects were lethargy and nausea; 32.7% (n = 16) reported 1–2 side effects, 36.7% (n = 18) reported 3–4 side effects, and 24.5% (n = 12) reported five or more opioid-related side effects or. No life-threatening adverse events were reported.

Discussion

Our study shows an association between pain severity, smoking and hormonal therapy and the likelihood of opioid use in women with endometriosis. Within the opioid users group, significant improvements were observed in all endometriosis associated pain symptoms and quality of life domains.

A retrospective study [10] reported that women with endometriosis were more likely to have a prescription for opioids and that their prescriptions are with higher doses than those of women without endometriosis. However, the study did not investigate the actual use or effectiveness of opioids. Indeed, another study has shown that patients were prescribed 50% more opioids than actually used [9].

Current literature lacks studies specifically addressing risk factors for opioid use among women suffering EAP. Our findings of elevated pain levels and higher prevalence of hormonal therapy and use of non-opioids among women using opioids are unsurprising given that women experiencing greater pain are more likely to resort to opioids after exhausting conventional treatment options. This association may partly reflect confounding by indication, as patients with more severe symptoms are more likely to receive opioid therapy. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously and not considered causal. In addition, pain mechanisms of endometriosis are not exclusive to estrogen production, therefore hormonal therapy alone may not suffice to alleviate EAP symtpoms [25]. Smoking is often used as a coping mechanism for pain management [26]. Smoking has been associated with increased pain sensitivity and a higher prevalence of chronic pain conditions, possibly through inflammatory pathways and neurobiological mechanisms affecting pain perception. These mechanisms may contribute to greater analgesic use, including opioids, among smokers with chronic pain conditions. Given that opioid users experienced higher pain levels, our finding that smoking is a predictor of opioid use is expected.

Similarly to other studies assessing the use of opioids in various pain syndromes, a relatively high rate of adverse effects was observed in our study among opioid users.

An adverse effect rate of nearly 70%, mostly nausea, somnolence, and constipation, was reported in a study assessing the long-term efficacy and safety of tramadol tablets for treating chronic lower back pain [24]. This is still lower than the adverse effect rate in our study, possibly due to the complex nature of EAP that is often associated with multiple pain conditions as well as with central sensitization [25]. Of note and similar to our study, a recent cohort study assessing the effect of chronic opioid use in women with endometriosis reported 34% constipation, 19.6% dizziness, and 8.2% falls among opioid users [8].

In our study, despite the frequent occurrence of opioid-related adverse events, quality of life was reported to improve after the use of opioids. The observed improvements should be interpreted as patient-reported perceived changes rather than causal treatment effects. These findings may also reflect placebo effects or the natural fluctuation of chronic pain symptoms. In addition, many patients continued using other analgesics or treatments concurrently, which may have contributed to symptom improvement.This aligns with the previously mentioned study which showed similar benefits in lower back pain patients, despite a 68.9% adverse effect rate [24]. Similarly, two recent meta-analyses have shown that as compared to placebo, opioids were associated with improvement in physical functioning and sleep quality despite high adverse effect rates [27]. The improvement in quality of life with opioids despite the high rate of adverse effects in our study may be explained by the dominant effect of pain symptoms on quality of life of women with endometriosis as well as by the relatively infrequent use of opioids in our cohort, with 50% of patients using opioids once weekly or less.

Addiction is a main concern with the use of opioids. Despite relatively infrequent use, one-third of opioid users in our study were at moderate to high risk for opioid addiction. This rate is at the higher end of the addiction rate reported by a previously published systematic review [28] assessing rates of opioid misuse, abuse, and addiction in patients with chronic pain. However, this systematic review has reported outcomes of both men and women, and while they have reported the actual addiction rate, we have examined the risk of future addiction as per the ORT.

Lending support to our results, the above mentioned cohort study found an increased risk for chronic opioid use, opioid dependence, and opioid overdose in endometriosis patients, as compared to women without endometriosis [8]. Current international guidelines for chronic non-cancer pain recommend cautious and limited use of opioids, generally reserving them for patients with refractory pain after failure of first-line therapies and emphasizing careful monitoring and risk assessment [15]. Our findings of frequent adverse effects and a substantial proportion of patients at moderate-to-high risk of misuse further support the need for careful patient selection and close monitoring when opioids are considered for endometriosis-associated pain.

Opioids should therefore be considered only as a secondary or rescue therapy for carefully selected patients with refractory pain, after failure of standard medical, hormonal, and when appropriate, surgical treatments. Importantly, intermittent or episodic use for severe pain flares may carry a lower risk profile than chronic opioid therapy, which is associated with higher risks of dependence and adverse effects. When opioids are considered, their use should ideally occur within a structured prescribing framework, including risk stratification, clear treatment goals, duration limits, and multidisciplinary pain management approaches.

Our finding of improvement of main pain domains, particularly overall pain, with opioid use is consistent with two meta-analyses [27, 29] which described an improvement in pain intensity with opioids compared to placebo in patients suffering non-cancerous pain conditions. Importantly, these meta-analyses have not included women with endometriosis.

Strengths and limitations

Analgesics for endometriosis-associated pain is an understudied topic in endometriosis research. Most previous studies on opioid use included males and females of all age ranges with minimal inclusion or focus on endometriosis patients. To our knowledge, our study is one of the first studies assessing risk of opioid use and the effectiveness of opioids for endometriosis patients.

However, our study has several limitations. Baseline pain and quality-of-life scores prior to opioid initiation were retrospectively reported by participants. As a result, the study may be susceptible to recall bias, which could influence the magnitude of perceived symptom improvement after opioid use. Additionally, because baseline measurements were not prospectively collected before opioid initiation, the observed improvements should be interpreted as patient-reported perceived changes rather than as definitive evidence of treatment effectiveness. However, it is unlikely that the recall bias rate, if present, differed between the opioid and non-opioid users group.

As pain questionnaires for the non-opioid users group have not assessed pain levels after the use of non-opioid analgesics, we were unable to compare the improvement in pain intensity and quality of life between the opioid and non-opioid user groups.

Additionally, this was a single-center study conducted in a tertiary referral clinic, which may introduce referral bias toward patients with more severe disease. The relatively modest sample size may also limit statistical power and the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, prescribing practices and access to pain management strategies may vary between healthcare systems, which may limit the external validity of these results.

In addition, the relatively modest sample size and number of events may limit the stability of the multivariable regression model. Although the number of predictors included in the model was restricted to minimize overfitting, residual instability cannot be excluded. Furthermore, potential correlations between variables related to disease severity, such as pain intensity, hormonal therapy use, and prior surgical treatment, may introduce collinearity, which could influence the estimated associations.

Conclusions

In our study, the likelihood of using opioids among women with endometriosis was elevated for women with hormonal therapy, smoking and higher pain severity. Opioids were effective pain relievers for EAP. While the overall quality of life was improved, the risk of opioid abuse was relatively high, highlighting the importance of exercising caution when prescribing opioids to individuals with endometriosis. More research is needed to delineate better the role and risks of opioids for the treatment of EAP.
