NFYA-mediated promotion of castration-resistant prostate cancer progression through EGR4 regulation
Authors: Guijiang Sun, Yi Shao, Qianwang Ma, Shengju Song, Yutong Chen, Yang Li, Yue Gao, Haitao Wang, Zhiqun Shang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research investigates the intricate involvement of nuclear Transcription Factor Y Subunit Alpha (NFYA) in the advancement of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) and its consequential impact on early Growth Response 4 (EGR4) expression. NFYA demonstrates a significant elevation in CRPC tissues and cell lines, displaying robust upregulation in metastatic prostate cancer (mPCa) samples, closely associated with the Gleason score. Immunohistochemistry validates heightened nuclear staining of NFYA in CRPC patients, highlighting its crucial role in the progression of advanced prostate cancer. Silencing NFYA through siRNA in androgen-independent cell lines markedly impedes cell growth and migration, emphasizing NFYA’s pivotal role in promoting CRPC behavior. RNA-seq analysis identifies EGR4 as a downstream target of NFYA, with both genes consistently upregulated in CRPC. Validating this finding, heightened expression of EGR4 is observed in CRPC samples. In vivo studies utilizing a mouse model demonstrate that NFYA silencing substantially inhibits LNCaP-AI/22RV1shNFYA xenograft tumor growth, accompanied by reduced expression of EGR4 and Ki67. This comprehensive study reveals the multifaceted role of NFYA in CRPC progression, elucidates its downstream impact on EGR4, and underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting NFYA to inhibit CRPC growth in vivo. These findings contribute valuable insights into potential therapeutic strategies for managing CRPC.Keywords: NFYA, EGR4, Castration resistant prostate cancer, Progression

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa), predominantly afflicting males over the age of 50, is one of the most prevalent malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in this demographic [1]. Within the spectrum of PCa, CRPC emerges as a formidable clinical challenge. CRPC, evolving from hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, is characterized by its relentless progression despite conventional therapeutic strategies such as surgical castration or androgen-deprivation therapy [2, 3]. This advanced stage of PCa bifurcates into two distinct categories: nonmetastatic CRPC (nmCRPC), where metastases remain undetected, and metastatic CRPC (mCRPC), both of which are associated with a significantly elevated mortality risk compared to earlier stages of the disease [4–6]. The transition from hormone-sensitive to castration-resistant prostate cancer represents a critical juncture in the disease trajectory, underscoring the necessity for improved strategies in early treatment. Although research continues, the detailed molecular mechanisms driving CRPC development remain unclear, representing a significant gap in our understanding and a key area for further study.

Nuclear transcription factor Y (NF-Y), a trimeric protein complex ubiquitous across eukaryotic species, consists of three subunits: NFYA, NFYB, and NFYC [7]. This complex, also recognized as the CCAAT-binding factor (CBF), is instrumental in initiating gene transcription by engaging with the CCAAT box in gene promoter regions [8]. In the context of PCa, NF-Y has been identified as a critical factor in the molecular pathways that govern the disease’s progression from benign epithelial states to localized and hormone-refractory metastatic forms [9]. The NF-Y binding site, specifically the CCAAT box, is notably overrepresented in the promoters of genes that are upregulated in several cancer types, including breast, prostate, gastric adenocarcinoma, and lung squamous cell carcinomas, correlating with a poorer prognosis [10–14]. Elevated expression levels of the NFYA subunit have been particularly noted in these malignancies. Despite these findings, the precise role of NFYA in the advancement of CRPC, along with the detailed molecular mechanisms involved, remains an area of active investigation.

The Early Growth Response (EGR) gene family, comprising EGR1, EGR2, EGR3, and EGR4, is integral to various biological processes and has garnered significant attention in oncological research [15]. Located on chromosomes 5q31, 10q21, 8p21, and 2p13 respectively, these genes, particularly EGR4 (also known as NGFI-C or pAT133), have been implicated in the pathogenesis of several cancers [15, 16]. Elevated expression of EGR4, for instance, has been documented in diverse cancer types, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), where it is strongly associated with enhanced tumor proliferation [17]. Additionally, EGR4 has been implicated in the progression of prostate cancer. In prostate cancer, EGR4 expression has been linked to hormone receptor status and metastasis, suggesting its involvement in tumor aggressiveness and metastatic potential [15]. However, the specific mechanisms by which EGR4 promotes the progression of prostate cancer remain unclear.

Therefore, in CRPC, exploring the regulatory relationship between EGR4 and NFYA is a novel and promising area of research. Our research indicates a significant upregulation of NFYA expression in prostate cancer, which appears to correlate with the progression of CRPC. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the modulation of EGR4 expression by NFYA is a crucial mechanism driving the progression of CRPC, highlighting NFYA’s significant role in the pathophysiology of this aggressive form of prostate cancer.

Materials and methods

RNA-Seq analysis

In this study, we used 22RV1 cells transfected with NFYA shRNA and scramble shRNA for RNA sequencing. To assess RNA quantity and quality, we employed the NanoDrop ND-1000. We also checked RNA integrity using standard denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis. We generated sequencing libraries from 1 to 2 µg of total RNA. This was done using the KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit from Illumina, based in San Diego, CA, USA. We followed the manufacturer’s instructions closely. The first step was to enrich mRNA. For this, we used the NEBNext® Poly (A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module. After purifying the RNA, we fragmented it into small pieces. This fragmentation used divalent cations and was done under elevated temperature. Next, we reverse-transcribed the cleaved RNA fragments. This process created the first strand of cDNA. We used reverse transcriptase and random primers for this. Following that, we synthesized the second strand of cDNA. This step involved DNA polymerase I and RNase H.We then added a single ‘A’ base to these cDNA fragments. This was followed by the ligation of an adapter. After ligation, we purified the products. We then used polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to enrich them. This process helped us create the final cDNA library. To assess the enrichment and size distribution of our libraries, we used the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. We carried out hybridization and cluster generation on a cBot unit. The sequencing was done using the Illumina HiSeq 4000 sequencer. This machine performed single-end sequencing, producing reads that were 50 bp long. Finally, we treated the raw reads with pre-treatment steps. These steps included filtering and quality control (QC). We then mapped the sequencing reads against the human reference genome version 19. For this mapping, we used the TopHat algorithm version 2.0.9 and Ensembl gene annotations version GRCh37.65. After aligning the sequencing reads to the human genome, we quantified the expression levels of genes. This quantification was based on Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) values [18], calculated using the Ballgown software [19]. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the samples, we applied the DEGseq package. This analysis compared transcript levels based on FPKM values (foldchange ≥ 1, P-value ≤ 0.05). NFYA siRNA1 target sequence: GAGCAGUAUACAGCAAACATT, siRNA2 target sequence: GACAAGGUCAAACCAUCAUTT, NFYA shRNA target sequence: GAGCAGUAUACAGCAAACATT.

Analysis of public datasets

In this study, we analyzed two prominent prostate cancer datasets: the Prostate Adenocarcinoma (PRAD) dataset from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [20] and the Prostate Cancer Transcriptome Atlas (PCTA) [21]. For comparison with normal tissue, we sourced normal prostate tissue samples from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database [22, 23]. We acquired both mRNA expression profiles and clinical data from the GTEx, PCTA, and PRAD-TCGA datasets. The PCTA dataset encompasses 1321 clinical specimens, offering a comprehensive overview of transcriptomic variations in prostate cancer. The PRAD dataset, formally known as the Prostate Adenocarcinoma dataset, comprises 497 prostate cancer samples with complete data sets. This inclusion of diverse and extensive datasets allows for a robust comparative analysis of gene expression patterns in prostate adenocarcinoma.

Cell culture and cell lines

LNCaP, 22RV1, and C4-2 cells used in this study were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). We maintained these cells in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), which was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin, both obtained from Gibco. To create an androgen-deprived environment, parental LNCaP cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% charcoal-stripped FBS, sourced from Biological Industries (BI, Cromwell, CT, USA). This specific medium aids in minimizing androgen influence. The LNCaP-AI (androgen-independent) cell line was developed by subjecting parental LNCaP cells to long-term culture under these androgen-deprived conditions. This process was undertaken to study the progression of prostate cancer in low-androgen environments, reflecting a common clinical scenario in advanced prostate cancer treatment.

Cell proliferation, colony formation and invasion

We assessed cell proliferation using the MTT assay, as per the protocol provided by Sigma (USA). This method is consistent with the procedures described in previous studies [24]. To evaluate colony formation, approximately 1000 cells were seeded per well in six-well plates for both the experimental (trail set) and control sets. Each set was prepared in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. After two weeks of incubation, the formed colonies were stained with 0.5% crystal violet. We then proceeded with imaging and counting the colonies to assess growth.

The invasion capabilities of the cells were examined using the transwell invasion assay. This assay was conducted in accordance with methodologies detailed in prior research [25].

Tissue specimens and immunohistochemistry

Prostate tissue specimens utilized in this study were derived from surgical interventions in patients diagnosed with prostate cancer. We then subjected them to immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. These procedures followed standard diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining protocols for chromogenic detection of IHC targets. For quantitative assessment, each tissue specimen was assigned a score based on the staining intensity observed in the epithelial compartment. The scoring criteria were as follows: no staining (−), low positive (+), positive (++), and high positive (+++). To ensure consistency and accuracy, all stained specimens underwent review and calibration. This process adhered strictly to the protocols established in our previous studies. The primary antibodies used as follow: NFYA (abcam cat: ab139402), EGR4 (Bioss cat: bs-14522R), Ki67 (abcam cat: ab15580). The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all participating patients, ensuring adherence to ethical guidelines for biomedical research.

Animal studies

The animal studies conducted were approved by the Tianjin Institute of Urology, Tianjin, China. This ensured adherence to ethical standards for animal research. We procured 20 male BALB/c nude mice, aged 7 weeks, from SPF Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China. The mice were acclimatized under controlled conditions before the commencement of the experiments. We divided the mice into two main groups: control and trial sets, each containing two sub-groups (LNCaP-AI and 22RV1) with 5 mice in each. Subcutaneous tumor growth assays were initiated using LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 cells. Two weeks post-cell inoculation, the control set (both LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 sub-groups) received lentiviruses carrying control shRNA. In contrast, the trial set (both LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 sub-groups) was injected with lentiviruses carrying NFYA shRNA. Tumor growth was monitored on a weekly basis. Measurements were taken externally from the mouse skin. The investigator, blinded to group allocation, measured the tumor dimensions. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume (V) = 1/2 × larger diameter × (smaller diameter)2. After a two-week observation period, we assessed and documented the differences in tumor sizes between the groups. All mice were euthanized using cervical dislocation method. Subsequently, the tumors were harvested following standard, institutionally approved procedures. All harvested tumor samples were fixed in paraffin and prepared for subsequent immunohistochemistry analysis.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the cell samples utilizing TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and its concentration was quantified through ultraviolet absorbance at 260 nm. This RNA served as a template for first-strand cDNA synthesis, conducted using the Reverse Transcription System (Roche) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the synthesized cDNA was subjected to quantitative PCR analysis. This process employed the Applied Biosystems 7900 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific) in conjunction with the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche), adhering to the guidelines provided by the manufacturers. GAPDH was incorporated as an internal control to ensure data normalization. The relative expression levels of the target RNAs were calculated utilizing the comparative Ct (threshold cycle) method. Detailed primer sequences used as follow: NFYA, forward 5′- ACCCTCCAGGTAGTCCAAGG − 3′ and reverse 5′- TTGCTGCAAACCCTGTGTTC − 3′; EGR4, forward 5′- TCGTCAAGTCCACTGAAGGC − 3′ and reverse 5′- CTCAAGAAGTCGCCTGCTCC − 3′; GAPDH, forward 5′- CGGAGTCAACGGATTTGGTC − 3′ and reverse 5′- TCGCCCCACTTGATTTTGGA − 3′.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, 89,901). We quantified the protein concentration, ensuring approximately 50 µg of total protein was used for each sample. The proteins were then separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Following electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. To prevent non-specific binding, the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h. After being washed three times with Tris-buffered saline and Tween 20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following another series of three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at a 1:10,000 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. After washing off the secondary antibody with TBST, the membranes were prepared for chemiluminescent detection. Developing solutions A and B (Immobilon Western, Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate, Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) were mixed in the appropriate ratio. This mixture was then applied to the areas of the PVDF membrane corresponding to the molecular weight bands of interest. Finally, the membranes were placed in an exposure machine for the detection of protein bands. The primary antibodies used as follow: NFYA (abcam cat: ab139402), EGR4 (Bioss cat: bs-14522R), GAPDH (Abways, Catalog: AB0037), β-actin (abcam cat: ab8226).

Statistics

Statistical analyses in this study were performed using R software, version 4.2.3, and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Independent Student t-test, ranksums-test and ANOVA were applied for comparison. Data Representation and Significance Thresholds: The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were conducted in triplicate with independent repetitions and subjected to statistical analysis. We established significance levels as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, and ****p ≤ 0.0001. These thresholds were applied to determine the statistical significance of differences observed between groups.

Result

NFYA exhibits elevated expression levels in both tissues and cell lines associated with CRPC

A number of recent studies implicated a role of NFYA in transcriptional regulation of key cancer genes [26–29]. In our investigation into NFYA’s role in advanced prostate cancer progression, we analyzed NFYA expression patterns in clinical prostatic tumors using PCTA and TCGA data (Fig. 1A, B). Our findings revealed a significant increase in NFYA mRNA levels in metastatic PCa samples, correlating notably with the Gleason score. This upregulation was much higher compared to non-metastatic PCa or benign tissues, indicating a potential link between NFYA expression and prostate cancer aggressiveness. Further examination of NFYA expression in prostate cancer tissue via Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining showed intense nuclear staining in CRPC patients (Fig. 1C, D), contrasting with weaker staining in localized PCa and absence in benign tissues. Consistency across mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1E, F) was observed. Elevated NFYA levels were particularly evident in CRPC cell lines (LNCaP-AI and 22Rv1), contrasting sharply with hormone-sensitive PCa cell line (LNCaP). These findings strongly suggest NFYA upregulation in CRPC tumors, potentially serving as a biomarker for disease progression.

NFYA enhances the proliferation and migration abilities of CRPC cells

The heightened expression of NFYA in androgen-independent LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 cell lines, compared to androgen-sensitive LNCaP, prompted an in-depth investigation into NFYA’s biological role and its potential positive contribution to CRPC progression. To elucidate its functional implications, we established NFYA siRNA-transduced LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 cell lines and conducted assays to evaluate the impact of NFYA silencing on CRPC cell growth and migration in vitro (Fig. 2A). NFYA knockdown resulted in a significant inhibition of cell growth in both LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 cells, highlighting NFYA’s role in promoting CRPC growth (Fig. 2B). This underscores NFYA’s contribution to the heightened proliferative capacity seen in castration-resistant prostate cancer. Consistent findings were observed in the clonogenic assay, with NFYA-knockdown cells showing a reduction in colony number and size compared to controls, indicating NFYA’s role in facilitating colony formation (Fig. 2C). Cell migration assays further supported these findings, demonstrating decreased migration capacity upon NFYA silencing in both LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 cells (Fig. 2D, E). Furthermore, we established LNCaP cell lines overexpressing NFYA using lentiviral plasmids (Fig. 2F, G). MTT and Transwell assays revealed that NFYA overexpression significantly enhanced proliferation and metastasis capabilities in LNCaP cells (Fig. 2H, I). These results collectively underscore NFYA’s pivotal role in driving both the growth and migration of CRPC cells in vitro.

NFYA serves as an activator of the downstream gene EGR4 in PCa

In our quest to elucidate the biological function and specific mechanism of NFYA in CRPC progression, we utilized RNA-seq analysis and established stable NFYA shRNA-transduced LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 cell lines (Fig. 3A, B). Heatmap analysis of the 22RV1shNFYA RNA-seq data, combined with insights from the TCGA database, revealed the EGR4 gene as a downstream target of NFYA, particularly in the 22RV1 cell line (Fig. 3C-E). This discovery provided insights into the molecular pathways influenced by NFYA in CRPC. Numerous studies suggest that EGR4 can drive tumor progression and acts as an oncogene [17, 30, 31]. To validate whether NFYA positively regulates EGR4, we performed QPCR and western blot assays. The results from both assays confirm that NFYA indeed upregulates the expression of EGR4 (Fig. 3F, G).

EGR4 is highly expressed in CRPC and promotes the progression of CRPC

Analysis of RNA-seq data from the TCGA and GTEx database revealed elevated expression of EGR4 in prostate cancer tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 4A). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of BPH, ADPC, and CRPC tissues further demonstrated significantly higher levels of EGR4 expression in CRPC tissues compared to BPH and ADPC (Fig. 4B). Additionally, western blot experiments showed elevated expression of EGR4 in CRPC cell lines LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 (Fig. 4C). Stable knockdown of EGR4 using lentiviral transduction techniques was established in LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 cells (Fig. 4D, E), leading to a significant inhibition of cell growth in both cell lines (Fig. 4F). To elucidate EGR4’s role as a key target gene of NFYA, rescue experiments were conducted. Knocking down EGR4 in NFYA-overexpressing LNCaP cells resulted in a significant inhibition of cell growth (Fig. 4G), suggesting that NFYA promotes LNCaP cell growth through EGR4. These findings collectively underscore the pronounced upregulation of EGR4 in CRPC tumors and its potential role in advanced prostate cancer progression orchestrated by NFYA.

In a mouse model, the growth of PCa is effectively inhibited by silencing NFYA

In our investigation into the therapeutic potential of silencing NFYA in CRPC, we employed an in vivo animal model to assess its impact on established tumors. LNCaP-AI and 22RV1 cells transduced with NFYA shRNA, along with control cells, were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c nude mice mixed with Matrigel. The results from the animal model strongly supported the inhibitory effect of NFYA silencing on LNCaP-AI/22RV1shNFYA xenograft tumor growth (Fig. 5A, E). The growth curve revealed a significant reduction in castration-resistant tumor growth upon NFYA silencing, indicating a potential therapeutic avenue for controlling CRPC progression (Fig. 5B, C, F, G). Importantly, analysis of tumor xenografts showed a substantial decrease in EGR4 and Ki67 expression in LNCaP-AI/22RV1shNFYA compared to the control groups (Fig. 5D, H). This reduction suggests a downstream effect of NFYA silencing, further supporting its inhibitory role in castration-resistant prostate cancer growth in vivo. In conclusion, these in vivo findings highlight the therapeutic efficacy of silencing NFYA in suppressing the growth of established CRPC tumors and provide valuable insights into potential targeted therapeutic strategies against castration-resistant prostate cancer.

Discussion

Previous studies implicated a role of NFYA in multiple cancers [12, 32, 33]. Okada et al. reported that NFYA promotes the malignant behavior of triple-negative breast cancer in mice through the regulation of lipid metabolism [34]. Garipov et al. discovered that NF-YA underlies EZH2 upregulation and is essential for the proliferation of human epithelial ovarian cancer cells [35]. Cao et al. identified NFYA as a diffuse subtype-specific prognostic factor in gastric cancer [33]. The findings of this study provide comprehensive insights into the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying the progression of CRPC with a particular focus on the interplay between NFYA and EGR4.

Firstly, our results confirm the heightened expression of NFYA in CRPC tissues and cell lines, aligning with previous studies suggesting its pivotal role in advanced prostate cancer. The observed correlation between elevated NFYA mRNA levels and the Gleason score in metastatic PCa samples from publicly available databases further emphasizes the clinical relevance of NFYA in CRPC progression. Clinical correlation between NFYA and cancer progression was also reported in a number of cancers [36]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining results demonstrated a notable nuclear enrichment of NFYA in CRPC patients, contrasting with weak or undetectable staining in localized PCa and benign tissues. Silvia reported that higher expression and altered splicing signature of NF-YA are associated to PCa aggressiveness [12]. This nuclear localization hints at potential transcriptional regulatory functions of NFYA in the progression of advanced prostate cancer. Silencing NFYA through siRNA transduction in androgen-independent cell lines (LNCaP-AI and 22RV1) yielded significant reductions in cell growth and migration capacities. These results not only support NFYA’s growth-promoting role in CRPC, consistent with its upregulation in CRPC cell lines, but also highlight its involvement in enhancing the migratory capabilities of these cells. The concordance of results across MTT, clonogenic, and migration assays strengthens the robustness of our findings.

Intriguingly, RNA-seq analysis identified EGR4 as a downstream target of NFYA in the 22RV1 cell line. Gong reported that ERG4 promotes cholangiocarcinoma cell growth [37]. The coordinated upregulation of NFYA and EGR4 in CRPC, validated through analysis of clinical samples, further supports the existence of an NFYA-EGR4 regulatory axis in prostate cancer progression. QPCR and western blot analyses provided additional confirmation of the coordinated expression changes in NFYA and EGR4.Immunohistochemistry staining for EGR4 in clinical samples exhibited intensified nuclear staining in CRPC patients, aligning with the upregulation observed in the RNA-seq analysis. This consistent pattern strengthens the argument for NFYA’s positive regulation of EGR4 in CRPC. In vivo studies using a mouse model demonstrated that silencing NFYA significantly blocked LNCaP-AI/22RV1shNFYA xenograft tumor growth, further corroborating the inhibitory effect of NFYA silencing on CRPC growth observed in vitro. The reduced expression of EGR4 and Ki67 in NFYA-silenced tumor xenografts indicates potential downstream molecular changes responsible for the observed growth inhibition.

This study’s integrated approach, combining clinical data analysis, in vitro experiments, and in vivo modeling, provides a holistic understanding of the NFYA-EGR4 regulatory axis in CRPC. The identified molecular interactions present potential therapeutic targets for future interventions in advanced prostate cancer. Targeting NFYA or disrupting the NFYA-EGR4 axis may prove beneficial in mitigating CRPC progression, offering avenues for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. However, further research is warranted to delve deeper into the specific mechanisms underlying NFYA-mediated EGR4 regulation and to evaluate the translational potential of targeting these factors in a clinical setting.
