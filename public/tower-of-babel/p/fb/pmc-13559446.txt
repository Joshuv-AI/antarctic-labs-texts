Current evidence and emerging therapeutic strategies for non-exercise interventions in sarcopenia associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a narrative review
Authors: Renjun Huang, Ligong Chen, Fang Tian, Yuanfang Xie, Yuexin Zhang, Fanjie Lin, Zili Zhang, Wenju Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveSkeletal muscle provides the foundation for respiration and physical activity and plays an essential role in human survival. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)-associated sarcopenia is highly prevalent and significantly affects exercise tolerance, quality of life, and long-term prognosis. Unlike irreversible airway damage, skeletal muscle dysfunction remains potentially treatable. Pulmonary rehabilitation remains the cornerstone intervention; however, its application is substantially limited during acute exacerbations and in patients with severe disease. This review aims to summarize current evidence on non-exercise interventions for COPD-associated sarcopenia.MethodsTo retrieve literature related to non-exercise therapeutic strategies for COPD-associated sarcopenia, a keyword search was performed, which covered PubMed and Web of Science databases for publications from January 1, 2009 to March 31, 2026.Key Content and FindingsThis review categorizes non-exercise interventions for COPD-associated sarcopenia into three evidence tiers. Category 1 (clinical evidence from COPD studies): butyrate improves handgrip strength, gait speed, and short physical performance battery (SPPB); whey protein with vitamin D enhances muscle strength and performance; heliox increases exercise endurance; RJx-01 is currently under phase II investigation with results pending; myostatin inhibitors increase lean mass without functional gain. Category 2 (preclinical evidence from COPD models): hydrogen therapy, glycyrrhizin, and Jinshui Liujun decoction mitigate muscle wasting via anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and multi-target metabolic pathways. Category 3 (translational strategies from other wasting disorders): selective androgen receptor modulators (SARMs), growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15) antibody, and cell/gene therapy, all of which have demonstrated efficacy in cancer cachexia or age-related sarcopenia, represent promising directions for future research in COPD.ConclusionsDespite the current paucity of direct clinical evidence for non-exercise therapies in COPD-related sarcopenia, intensive ongoing investigations are catalyzing a pivotal shift in the field. By translating preclinical breakthroughs into targeted interventions, these advancements are paving the way for a new era of multifaceted therapeutic strategies, ultimately aiming to mitigate muscle dysfunction and improve outcomes in patients with COPD.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder rather than a condition confined to the airways, with extrapulmonary manifestations contributing substantially to long-term morbidity and reduced quality of life (1,2). Among these manifestations, skeletal muscle dysfunction has emerged as one of the most clinically relevant because it directly influences exercise tolerance, symptom burden, hospitalization risk, and survival (3,4).

Sarcopenia is currently recognized as a progressive skeletal muscle disorder characterized by reduced muscle strength, low muscle mass or quality, and impaired physical performance, with muscle strength serving as the principal diagnostic criterion in contemporary consensus definitions (3). Loss of muscle mass and strength in COPD is multifactorial and cannot be explained solely by reduced physical activity. Persistent systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, chronic hypoxemia, endocrine dysregulation, nutritional insufficiency, and recurrent exacerbations all contribute to a catabolic environment that progressively impairs muscle homeostasis (5-8). In many patients, these mechanisms coexist with anabolic resistance, meaning that physiological stimuli that normally maintain muscle protein synthesis become less effective over time (9-11).

Although pulmonary rehabilitation remains the cornerstone of non-pharmacological management for COPD-related muscle dysfunction, its clinical impact is often limited by poor adherence, advanced respiratory limitation, or comorbid frailty (12,13). This has led to increasing interest in adjunctive strategies capable of targeting biological pathways involved in muscle loss, particularly in individuals who are unable to tolerate sustained exercise-based intervention.

In recent years, several non-exercise approaches have attracted attention as potential therapeutic options. Nutritional interventions such as whey protein combined with vitamin D aim to restore anabolic responsiveness (14), whereas microbiota-derived metabolites including butyrate may influence inflammatory and mitochondrial pathways through the gut-lung-muscle axis (15,16). At the same time, emerging pharmacological and gas-based therapies—including heliox, RJx-01, myostatin inhibition, and molecular hydrogen—have been investigated for their capacity to improve muscle performance through distinct physiological mechanisms (17-22).

Beyond these strategies, several therapeutic concepts that have shown efficacy in other wasting disorders may also hold translational potential for COPD-associated sarcopenia. Selective androgen receptor modulators (SARMs), growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15) neutralization, mesenchymal stem cell-based therapies, engineered exosome delivery systems, and gene-editing approaches such as CRISPR-Cas9 are being explored as next-generation anabolic or regenerative interventions. Traditional Chinese medicine has also attracted attention because of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and mitochondrial regulatory effects (23). In parallel, tissue engineering and biomaterial-based muscle reconstruction strategies are gradually entering translational research, expanding the therapeutic landscape for severe muscle loss (24).

Given the expanding mechanistic understanding of COPD-related sarcopenia, a broader evidence-based evaluation of non-exercise interventions is needed. This review summarizes current progress according to the hierarchy of available evidence, focusing on butyrate, whey protein combined with vitamin D, heliox, RJx-01, myostatin inhibitors, and hydrogen therapy, while also discussing emerging therapeutic directions derived from other catabolic diseases, with the aim of providing updated perspectives for future clinical management and translational research.

Despite growing interest, the available evidence remains heterogeneous, with substantial differences in study design, intervention duration, patient selection, and outcome definition. Whether gains in muscle mass reliably translate into measurable functional benefit remains an open question in the field (25).

This review examines selected non-exercise interventions for COPD-associated sarcopenia, with particular emphasis on mechanistic rationale, available clinical observations, and unresolved questions relevant to future therapeutic development. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1097/rc).

Methods

A literature search was conducted to identify studies published between January 1, 2009 and March 31, 2026 that were relevant to non-exercise interventions for COPD-associated sarcopenia. PubMed and Web of Science were searched using the terms “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease”, “COPD”, “sarcopenia”, “muscle wasting”, “skeletal muscle dysfunction”, “nutritional supplementation”, “whey protein”, “butyrate”, “hydrogen therapy”, “heliox”, “selective androgen receptor modulators”, “bimagrumab”, “myostatin”, “extracellular vesicles”, “mesenchymal stem cells”, and “traditional Chinese medicine”, alone or in combination.

Priority was given to studies reporting clinical evidence, mechanistic insights, and translational relevance related to skeletal muscle dysfunction in COPD. Selection was limited to peer-reviewed original research articles and high-quality reviews focusing on nutritional, pharmacological, and emerging non-exercise therapeutic strategies. Relevant experimental studies were also included to illustrate underlying mechanisms. The search strategy is summarized in Table 1.

Butyrate

Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid generated through bacterial fermentation of dietary fiber in the colon, has recently attracted attention as a potential modulator of skeletal muscle metabolism in chronic disease (21). Interest in this molecule has increased alongside recognition of the gut-lung-muscle axis, a conceptual framework suggesting that intestinal microbial alterations may contribute to systemic inflammation and peripheral muscle dysfunction in COPD (29,30).

Beyond its established role as an energy substrate for colonocytes, butyrate acts as a signaling molecule capable of influencing inflammatory and metabolic pathways relevant to muscle preservation (21,31,32). Experimental studies have shown that butyrate inhibits histone deacetylase activity, modulates gene transcription, and suppresses nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling, thereby reducing production of pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6 (21,31,33-35). Because chronic low-grade inflammation is closely linked to accelerated proteolysis in COPD, these effects may be particularly relevant in limiting catabolic drive (21).

At the muscular level, preclinical evidence suggests that butyrate may support mitochondrial homeostasis and improve oxidative metabolism (21,35,36). Animal studies have reported enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, preservation of muscle fiber morphology, and reduced activation of proteolytic pathways following butyrate supplementation (37,38). Additional observations indicate improved insulin sensitivity and upregulation of genes involved in myogenic repair, suggesting a broader metabolic effect beyond anti-inflammatory action alone (39).

Potential benefits may also arise indirectly through modulation of intestinal barrier function and microbial composition (33,38,40,41). Reduced abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria has been described in patients with COPD, particularly during acute exacerbations, raising the possibility that impaired endogenous butyrate production contributes to systemic inflammatory amplification (21).

Direct clinical evidence has emerged from a randomized controlled trial by Qaisar et al., in which 12 weeks of butyrate supplementation improved handgrip strength, gait speed, and physical performance in COPD patients, accompanied by reductions in markers of intestinal permeability, systemic inflammation, and neuromuscular junction degradation (all P<0.05) (21). These findings provide initial randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence supporting butyrate as a non-exercise intervention for COPD-related sarcopenia, although the study was limited to male patients and a 12-week follow-up. Thus, the generalizability of these findings to female patients with COPD-associated sarcopenia remains to be established.

Taken together, butyrate represents a promising non-exercise intervention for COPD-related sarcopenia supported by initial RCT evidence. Its potential value lies in the ability to simultaneously influence inflammation, mitochondrial regulation, and metabolic signaling—three processes that are central to muscle decline in COPD. Further studies are needed to confirm long-term efficacy, optimal dosing, and applicability across different patient subgroups.

Whey protein combined with vitamin D

Nutritional support remains one of the most clinically accessible adjunctive strategies for COPD-associated sarcopenia, particularly in patients with reduced dietary intake, elevated resting energy expenditure, and persistent anabolic resistance (26,42). Among available nutritional interventions, the combination of whey protein and vitamin D has attracted particular interest because both components target biological pathways directly involved in muscle maintenance (43).

Whey protein is a rapidly absorbed, leucine-rich protein source with strong anabolic potential (44). Its high essential amino acid content, especially leucine, promotes activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin pathway and stimulates postprandial muscle protein synthesis (45). In COPD, however, chronic inflammation and metabolic stress may blunt this anabolic response, which partly explains why targeted protein supplementation is considered more relevant than simple caloric support alone.

Vitamin D provides complementary effects that extend beyond mineral metabolism. Skeletal muscle expresses vitamin D receptors, and inadequate vitamin D status has been associated with reduced muscle strength, impaired postural control, and increased vulnerability to physical decline (46). Deficiency is common in COPD, largely because of limited outdoor activity, nutritional compromise, and chronic inflammatory burden (47). A 2025 randomized controlled trial in 163 COPD patients reported that 16 weeks of whey protein plus vitamin D significantly improved handgrip strength, gait speed, and physical performance, accompanied by reductions in plasma C-terminal agrin fragment-22 (CAF22) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (all P<0.05) (26).

The rationale for combining these two interventions lies in their potentially complementary biological actions: whey protein provides the amino acid substrate required for muscle protein synthesis, whereas vitamin D may support myocyte differentiation, mitochondrial function, and anti-inflammatory regulation (48). Together, they may partially counteract the anabolic resistance characteristic of chronic disease.

Nevertheless, interpretation of current evidence remains complicated by substantial heterogeneity in trial design, including differences in protein dose, vitamin D replacement strategy, intervention duration, baseline nutritional deficiency, and rehabilitation intensity. In patients with advanced COPD and marked systemic catabolism, nutritional supplementation alone may produce only limited functional benefit (26).

Overall, whey protein combined with vitamin D remains a practical supportive strategy rather than a definitive therapeutic intervention (26). Better-designed randomized studies are still needed to identify which patient subgroups derive the greatest benefit and how supplementation can be integrated most effectively into broader sarcopenia management.

Heliox

Heliox, a low-density mixture of helium and oxygen, has long been explored as a supportive intervention in obstructive airway disease because of its capacity to reduce airflow resistance under conditions of expiratory limitation (49,50). In clinical practice, mixtures containing 70% helium with 30% oxygen or 80% helium with 20% oxygen are most commonly used (51). Owing to the markedly lower density of helium compared with nitrogen, heliox promotes more laminar gas flow within narrowed airways and reduces turbulent resistance without compromising oxygen transport.

In COPD, where expiratory flow limitation and dynamic hyperinflation impose substantial mechanical loading on the respiratory system, this physical property has particular physiological relevance. Respiratory muscles—especially the diaphragm—consume a disproportionate share of total oxygen delivery during exertion, which may divert perfusion away from locomotor muscles and accelerate peripheral fatigue. By lowering the work of breathing, heliox has been proposed as a means of redistributing oxygen delivery toward skeletal muscle and improving exercise tolerance under ventilatory stress.

Early physiological studies reported reductions in dyspnea and prolonged endurance time during constant-load exercise when heliox was administered during rehabilitation sessions (19,22,27). These findings supported the hypothesis that unloading respiratory muscles could indirectly preserve peripheral muscle performance, particularly in patients with severe ventilatory limitation (27,50,52).

However, subsequent clinical results have been less consistent. In a randomized controlled trial published in 2022, median endurance improvement following heliox-assisted exercise training reached 52.5 seconds, compared with 114 seconds in the room-air group, without a statistically significant between-group difference (53). These findings suggest that physiological benefit does not necessarily translate into superior rehabilitation outcomes under routine clinical conditions.

Interpretation of the evidence is further limited by the age and heterogeneity of the available literature. Much of the frequently cited supportive evidence was generated between 2009 and 2014, whereas more recent studies remain relatively scarce. A 2025 review likewise concluded that the overall certainty of benefit remains modest (50).

Importantly, heliox acts primarily as a ventilatory adjunct rather than a direct anabolic intervention. Although it may facilitate exercise participation in selected patients, current evidence does not demonstrate consistent improvement in core sarcopenia-related endpoints such as muscle mass, muscle strength, or sustained physical function. In addition, the need for dedicated gas delivery systems limits widespread clinical implementation.

RJx-01

RJx-01 is a combination therapy comprising metformin and galantamine, developed on the premise that simultaneous modulation of metabolic and inflammatory pathways may offer broader protection against muscle decline than single-target intervention (20). The rationale for this combination lies in the complementary biological actions of its two components: metformin activates adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase and promotes mitochondrial homeostasis, whereas galantamine exerts anti-inflammatory effects through cholinergic signaling and attenuation of oxidative stress.

Preclinical work has provided the principal basis for clinical interest in RJx-01. In aged experimental models, treatment was associated with preservation of muscle mass, improved muscle strength, enhanced neuromuscular junction integrity, and partial restoration of mitochondrial morphology (20). Additional findings suggested reduced denervation-related injury and attenuation of systemic inflammatory activation, indicating that the intervention may influence several mechanisms relevant to sarcopenia simultaneously.

Early translational evidence has also been encouraging. In a phase I study involving healthy older men exposed to immobilization-induced muscle loss, RJx-01 was associated with improvements in muscle strength, fatigue resistance, and selected functional measures. Although these participants did not have COPD, the findings provided preliminary support for further investigation in populations characterized by accelerated catabolic stress.

Clinical evaluation in COPD has now entered an early interventional phase. In May 2025, the first participant was enrolled in the phase IIa randomized double-blind placebo-controlled MINT-COPD trial conducted through the National Institute for Health and Social Care Research (NIHR) Leicester Biomedical Research Centre (28). The study is designed to recruit 130 hospitalized patients with acute exacerbations of COPD, who will receive daily RJx-01 or placebo for 168 days (54). Primary outcomes focus on safety and tolerability, while exploratory endpoints include muscle strength, lean mass, fatigue, physical performance, and quality of life. Completion is anticipated in 2028.

RJx-01 is therefore among the few pharmacological candidates currently progressing beyond proof-of-concept for COPD-associated sarcopenia. Its therapeutic relevance, however, remains provisional until clinical efficacy and long-term safety are confirmed in disease-specific populations.

Myostatin inhibitors

Myostatin is a key negative regulator of skeletal muscle growth within the transforming growth factor-β superfamily and exerts its biological effects primarily through Smad2/3 signaling, thereby suppressing muscle protein synthesis and limiting satellite cell activation (55,56). In COPD, chronic inflammation, hypoxemia, reduced physical activity, and sustained catabolic signaling may all contribute to increased myostatin activity, creating conditions that favor progressive muscle wasting and impaired regenerative capacity (57).

Elevated myostatin expression has been documented in quadriceps muscle from COPD patients with peripheral muscle weakness, supporting its relevance in disease-related muscle dysfunction (25). Persistent activation of this pathway may inhibit satellite cell proliferation and interfere with normal repair processes, thereby contributing not only to reduced muscle mass but also to diminished contractile adaptation (58).

Among currently investigated therapeutic strategies, bimagrumab has received the greatest clinical attention. Bimagrumab is a monoclonal antibody targeting activin type II receptors and thereby blocks signaling mediated not only by myostatin but also by related ligands involved in muscle catabolism (25).

In a phase II randomized placebo-controlled trial involving COPD patients with reduced skeletal muscle mass, bimagrumab produced a significant increase in quadriceps muscle volume and total lean body mass over 16 weeks, confirming clear anabolic activity at the structural level. Muscle volume increased as early as week 4, with peak gains approaching 7.8% by week 16 relative to placebo (25).

Functional outcomes, however, were less convincing. No statistically significant improvement was observed in 6-minute walk distance, despite measurable gains in muscle mass. Mild adverse events, including muscle spasms, diarrhea, and dermatological symptoms, were reported, although overall tolerability remained acceptable (25).

The dissociation between structural gain and functional response is particularly relevant in COPD. Exercise limitation in this population is determined not only by peripheral muscle quantity but also by ventilatory reserve, respiratory muscle loading, cardiovascular interaction, and neuromuscular efficiency. Consequently, pharmacologically increasing muscle volume alone is unlikely to substitute for the multidimensional benefits of pulmonary rehabilitation (25).

A similar pattern has been observed in older adults with primary sarcopenia, where phase II studies of bimagrumab also demonstrated increased lean mass but only modest functional improvement, largely confined to subgroups with lower baseline walking speed. These findings suggest that therapeutic response may depend partly on baseline functional reserve and disease context. Compared with butyrate and nutritional supplementation, pharmacological interventions currently remain at an earlier stage of clinical development (Table 2). Preclinical evidence from COPD models

Hydrogen therapy

Molecular hydrogen (H2) has recently attracted growing attention as a potential therapeutic adjunct in disorders characterized by oxidative stress and chronic inflammation (18). Unlike conventional antioxidants with broad redox effects, hydrogen appears to selectively neutralize highly reactive cytotoxic species—particularly hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and peroxynitrite (ONOO−)—while largely preserving superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide, both of which remain important for physiological intracellular signaling (59). This selective profile has generated interest because it may reduce oxidative injury without substantially disrupting redox-dependent cellular adaptation.

In skeletal muscle, hydrogen has shown protective effects across several experimental settings. Studies in exercise physiology have reported that inhaled hydrogen and hydrogen-rich water can attenuate acute muscle injury, reduce post-exertional oxidative burden, and lower circulating inflammatory markers. In animal models of muscular dystrophy, hydrogen-rich water has been associated with improved locomotor performance, lower plasma creatine kinase concentrations, and reduced histological evidence of muscle fiber degeneration, suggesting both structural and functional benefit (60).

Potential relevance in COPD extends beyond peripheral muscle protection. Hydrogen has been shown to modulate inflammatory signaling through multiple pathways, including activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and suppression of NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activity (61). In cigarette smoke-induced experimental COPD models, hydrogen inhalation attenuated airway inflammation and partially limited functional deterioration, supporting a possible dual effect on pulmonary and systemic oxidative injury (59).

These mechanistic observations are particularly relevant because oxidative stress is considered a major contributor to COPD-associated sarcopenia, where excessive reactive oxygen species promote mitochondrial dysfunction, accelerate proteolysis, and impair muscle regenerative capacity (56). By influencing both oxidative and inflammatory pathways, hydrogen may theoretically target several processes involved in muscle decline simultaneously.

However, direct clinical evidence remains scarce. Most currently available studies derive either from animal models or from non-COPD populations, and intervention protocols differ substantially with respect to delivery route, concentration, and treatment duration across inhaled hydrogen, hydrogen-rich water, and hydrogen-containing saline.

Hydrogen therefore remains a promising but exploratory intervention. Adequately powered randomized trials focusing on muscle mass, muscle strength, and functional outcomes will be required before its role in COPD-associated sarcopenia can be more clearly defined.

Glycyrrhizin

High-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is a damage-associated molecular pattern protein that is released into the extracellular space in response to cellular injury or stress, where it contributes to activation of innate immune signaling (62). A recent study by Xiong provided the first evidence implicating HMGB1-mediated pyroptosis in COPD-associated skeletal muscle atrophy (63). Mechanistic experiments showed that cigarette smoke extract upregulated HMGB1 expression and subsequently activated the HMGB1/toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-NLRP3-gasdermin D (GSDMD)-caspase-1 signaling cascade, leading to pyroptotic cell death, increased release of interleukin-1β and interleukin-18, and aggravated myotube atrophy (64).

At the clinical level, circulating HMGB1 concentrations were significantly higher in patients with COPD than in healthy nonsmokers and were inversely associated with mid-thigh circumference and body mass index. In C2C12 myotubes, exposure to cigarette smoke extract reproduced activation of the same pyroptotic pathway. Consistent findings were observed in cigarette smoke-induced murine COPD models, in which skeletal muscle HMGB1 expression increased in parallel with upregulation of TLR4 and downstream pyroptosis-related mediators (63).

Pharmacological inhibition with Glycyrrhizin, a direct HMGB1 inhibitor, partially reversed smoke-induced muscle atrophy and functional impairment in vivo, while also reducing skeletal muscle expression of TLR4 and pyroptosis-associated factors (63).

Taken together, HMGB1/TLR4-NLRP3-GSDMD-caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis appears to represent a critical inflammatory mechanism contributing to COPD-related skeletal muscle wasting. Although glycyrrhizin has demonstrated therapeutic potential in preclinical models, current evidence remains limited to experimental studies, and its clinical relevance in COPD has yet to be established. Future work should focus on the development of pathway-specific inhibitors and on translational studies evaluating their safety and efficacy in human disease.

Jinshui Liujun decoction combined with Morinda officinalis

Jingyue Quanshu-derived Jinshui Liujun decoction is a traditional herbal formula composed primarily of aged tangerine peel, processed pinellia, poria, licorice, angelica, and prepared rehmannia, and has long been used in the clinical management of chronic respiratory disorders, including COPD. Morinda officinalis, a kidney-tonifying medicinal herb traditionally used to strengthen musculoskeletal function, has recently been investigated in combination with this formula for its potential protective effects against COPD-associated skeletal muscle wasting. Network pharmacology analyses suggest that the principal targets of this herbal combination are enriched in pathways related to apoptosis, phosphorylation, and inflammatory regulation, with the Nrf2/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) signaling axis emerging as a potentially important mechanistic mediator (65).

A recent experimental study by Lin employed a lipopolysaccharide plus cigarette smoke exposure model to establish COPD in mice and evaluated the effects of Jinshui Liujun decoction combined with Morinda officinalis on skeletal muscle atrophy. Compared with untreated model animals, mice receiving herbal intervention showed significant improvement in skeletal muscle mass (P<0.001), accompanied by lower serum malondialdehyde concentrations (P<0.001), reduced tumor necrosis factor-α levels (P<0.001), and increased superoxide dismutase activity (P<0.05). Ultrastructural examination by transmission electron microscopy further demonstrated partial restoration of mitochondrial morphology and a reduction in intramyocellular lipid droplet accumulation (23).

At the mechanistic level, treatment significantly increased both gene and protein expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 (both P<0.05), supporting activation of endogenous antioxidant defense pathways (23).

Taken together, Jinshui Liujun decoction combined with Morinda officinalis appears to attenuate COPD-induced skeletal muscle atrophy through modulation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, providing experimental support for the potential role of traditional herbal medicine in COPD-associated sarcopenia. However, available evidence remains limited to preclinical models, and its therapeutic relevance in human COPD has yet to be established. Future high-quality randomized trials will be required to clarify both efficacy and safety in clinical settings. Translational strategies derived from other muscle-wasting disorders

The following approaches have shown promise in other chronic wasting conditions and may be translationally relevant to COPD-associated sarcopenia. Direct evidence in COPD populations, however, remains limited. Accordingly, their discussion is more concise, though each is briefly expanded below to highlight its scientific rationale.

Sarcopenia is a common complication across multiple chronic diseases, including liver cirrhosis, cancer cachexia, and age-related frailty. Although the initiating causes differ, several central pathological features—particularly chronic inflammation, anabolic resistance, and accelerated proteolysis—closely resemble those observed in COPD-associated sarcopenia, suggesting that non-exercise interventions effective in other wasting disorders may provide important translational insights for COPD-specific management (55,66-68).

Among pharmacological approaches, SARMs such as enobosarm and GSK2881078 have shown the capacity to increase lean body mass in phase II studies involving cancer cachexia and older adults with sarcopenia. These agents bind selectively to the androgen receptor and induce conformational changes that preferentially recruit coactivators to promote anabolic gene transcription in skeletal muscle, while exerting weaker activation in other tissues (69,70). Of particular relevance, a phase II trial of GSK2881078 specifically enrolled patients with COPD and demonstrated improvements in leg strength and lean body mass in male participants, although patient-reported outcomes remained unchanged. The principal safety findings included reversible reductions in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and transient elevations in hepatic transaminases, indicating that SARMs may offer anabolic benefit in COPD while leaving uncertainty regarding functional relevance (70). In addition, the predominance of male participants in this trial limits the generalizability of these findings to female patients. Notably, the observation that SARMs increase lean mass without consistent functional improvement parallels the experience with myostatin inhibitors, suggesting that anabolic agents alone may be insufficient to reverse the multidimensional nature of muscle impairment in COPD.

Neutralization of GDF-15 represents another promising anti-catabolic strategy. GDF-15, a member of the transforming growth factor-β superfamily, is released in response to inflammatory and cellular stress signals and has been closely linked to body weight loss and skeletal muscle wasting. In colorectal cancer, elevated baseline GDF-15 concentrations have been associated with poorer survival, whereas larger reductions in circulating GDF-15 were more frequently observed in individuals showing reversal of sarcopenic features (71,72). Preclinical studies further suggest that antibody-mediated inhibition of GDF-15 signaling can partially restore muscle mass, supporting this pathway as a candidate for future investigation in COPD (73). Unlike SARMs, which primarily target anabolic pathways, GDF-15 inhibition targets upstream inflammatory and stress signaling pathways that are closely linked to the pathophysiology of COPD—such as the NF-κB pathway, the NLRP3 inflammasome, and mitochondrial stress responses—a feature that makes it a promising direction with considerable translational potential.

Nutritional interventions also provide relevant translational evidence. Supplementation with branched-chain amino acids and β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate has shown beneficial effects on body composition and muscle strength in older sedentary or immobilized populations, particularly when baseline anabolic responsiveness is preserved (74,75). As a bioactive metabolite of leucine, β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate promotes muscle protein synthesis and inhibits protein breakdown, thereby helping to preserve muscle mass and function under conditions of physical inactivity. These findings are relevant to COPD patients who are often physically inactive due to dyspnea and fatigue. However, direct evidence in COPD populations remains limited, and the optimal dosing, timing, and combination with other nutritional approaches have not been systematically evaluated.

At the frontier of regenerative therapy, mesenchymal stem cells have attracted attention because of their immunomodulatory and paracrine properties, with experimental studies suggesting a capacity to support muscle regeneration and limit inflammatory injury (76). Mechanistically, mesenchymal stem cells secrete a range of bioactive factors—including growth factors, anti-inflammatory cytokines, and extracellular vesicles—that collectively modulate the local inflammatory milieu and create a pro-regenerative niche for resident muscle stem cells. However, clinical translation remains constrained by phenotypic heterogeneity, limited post-transplant survival, and uncertainty regarding long-term engraftment. Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived myogenic progenitors and adult satellite cells are also being explored as alternative cellular platforms (77). In parallel, CRISPR-Cas9 has evolved into a versatile platform for sequence-specific gene modulation. Beyond permanent genome editing, catalytically inactive CRISPR (CRISPRi) or CRISPR activator (CRISPRa) enables tunable regulation of gene expression without introducing double-strand breaks (78). This technological shift is conceptually attractive for COPD-associated sarcopenia, where muscle wasting is driven by acquired epigenetic alterations rather than a single genetic defect. Recent work has identified histone deacetylase 9 (HDAC9) as a key epigenetic regulator in this context: HDAC9 expression is elevated in skeletal muscle after cigarette smoke exposure, and HDAC9 knockout in a COPD mouse model attenuated muscle atrophy and enhanced satellite cell differentiation (79). These observations raise the possibility of using CRISPRi to suppress HDAC9 as a therapeutic strategy. While direct evidence in COPD models is still lacking, the convergence of target identification and platform maturation provides a rationale for future exploration. Meanwhile, engineered extracellular vesicles are emerging as cell-free delivery systems capable of transporting pro-regenerative proteins or microRNAs, offering a potential strategy for modulating muscle metabolism without the complexities associated with live cell therapies (80-82). An ongoing phase I/IIa study is evaluating adeno-associated virus serotype 9-mediated follistatin gene transfer for age-related muscle loss, and phase II clinical development of PLH-2301, an orally administered investigational therapy for sarcopenia, is also underway (83,84). Collectively, these regenerative and gene-based approaches remain at an early stage of development, but they share a common rationale—addressing muscle wasting at the cellular or molecular level rather than through systemic pharmacology—which may offer advantages in targeting local muscle pathology in COPD.

In the field of tissue engineering, biomaterial scaffolds and biomimetic matrices have been developed to reproduce key features of the native skeletal muscle extracellular microenvironment, thereby supporting cell adhesion, proliferation, and myogenic differentiation (85). These scaffolds provide structural support and biochemical cues that mimic the natural extracellular matrix, guiding the organization of muscle precursor cells into aligned myotubes and facilitating vascularization. When combined with three-dimensional bioprinting, these platforms have enabled construction of vascularized muscle grafts, offering a potential strategy for large-volume muscle replacement in traumatic or degenerative conditions (24). While the clinical application of tissue engineering in COPD-associated sarcopenia remains distant—given that muscle wasting in COPD is generally less severe than traumatic muscle loss—these technological advances provide a foundation for future exploration, particularly in patients with extreme muscle depletion or end-stage disease. Moreover, insights from tissue engineering, such as the importance of extracellular matrix mechanics and topographical cues for myogenesis, may inform the design of biomaterial-based delivery systems for other muscle-targeted therapies.

Taken together, these strategies have demonstrated varying degrees of efficacy across cirrhosis, cancer cachexia, and age-related sarcopenia. Because COPD shares several central catabolic mechanisms with other chronic wasting conditions, further investigation in COPD-specific populations appears justified. Priority should be given to validating candidates such as SARMs and GDF-15-neutralizing therapies, while also exploring the feasibility of regenerative approaches in COPD-associated sarcopenia. Conclusions

COPD-related sarcopenia is an important extrapulmonary complication that significantly affects exercise tolerance, quality of life, and long-term prognosis in patients. Its development is driven by multiple interconnected pathways, including chronic systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disturbances in protein metabolism, while also being influenced by reduced physical activity, chronic hypoxic exposure, and long-term pharmacological treatment.

This review systematically summarizes the progress of non-exercise interventions for COPD-related sarcopenia according to the hierarchy of available clinical evidence. Among current strategies, butyrate and whey protein combined with vitamin D are supported by randomized controlled trials conducted in COPD patients, with direct assessment of core sarcopenia-related outcomes such as handgrip strength, gait speed, and short physical performance battery (SPPB) scores, representing the highest level of evidence currently available for non-exercise interventions. Heliox is also supported by randomized controlled trial evidence in COPD populations; however, because its primary outcomes mainly focus on exercise endurance rather than core sarcopenia indicators, its level of evidence is relatively lower. Myostatin inhibition with Bimagrumab has completed phase II evaluation, demonstrating that increasing muscle mass alone does not guarantee functional benefit-a finding that underscores the importance of including both structural and functional endpoints in future trials. RJx-01 remains under phase II investigation with results pending. Evidence for hydrogen therapy, HMGB1 inhibition with glycyrrhizin, and Jinshui Liujun decoction currently derives mainly from animal models or cellular studies in COPD and therefore remains at a preclinical or exploratory stage. In addition, this review discusses several therapeutic approaches that have shown efficacy in other wasting disorders, including liver cirrhosis, cancer cachexia, and age-related sarcopenia, such as SARMs, GDF-15 neutralizing antibodies, cell-based therapies, gene editing, and tissue engineering, all of which may have translational potential in COPD-specific populations.

Overall, current research on non-exercise interventions for COPD-related sarcopenia still faces several common limitations. The number of high-quality randomized controlled trials remains limited, intervention protocols are insufficiently standardized, and many studies have not used muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance as primary endpoints. Long-term follow-up data are also lacking. In addition, direct comparative studies between different interventions remain scarce, and the potential synergistic effects of combined therapeutic strategies have not yet been clearly established.

Based on these considerations, future research should focus on several priorities. First, larger randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up periods should be conducted, specifically including patients of different sexes and varying disease severity. Second, a standardized core outcome set for sarcopenia should be established to improve comparability across studies. Third, the interaction between pharmacological interventions and pulmonary rehabilitation should be explored in order to facilitate the translation of structural improvements into functional benefits. Fourth, biomarker-based stratification should be developed to identify patients most likely to benefit and to promote individualized therapeutic strategies.

With a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying COPD-related sarcopenia and the accumulation of higher-quality clinical evidence, non-exercise interventions are expected to play an increasingly important role in the comprehensive management of COPD patients.
