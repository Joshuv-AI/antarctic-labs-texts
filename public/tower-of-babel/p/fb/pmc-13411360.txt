Development of Sub‐50 nm Core–Shell Silica Nanoparticles With Controlled In Vivo Behavior for 19F Magnetic Resonance Imaging
Authors: Yue Wu, Masafumi Minoshima, Kazuya Kikuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT 19F magnetic resonance imaging (19F MRI) enables quantitative and background‐free detection, but its inherently low sensitivity demands probes with high fluorine density and favorable pharmacokinetics. Perfluorocarbon (PFC)‐encapsulated nanoparticles are a promising class of 19F MRI probes that demonstrate high 19F MR sensitivity. However, their relatively large size (usually over 70 nm) results in slow hepatic clearance and limited tumor accumulation. Herein, we developed sub‐50 nm perfluoro‐15‐crown‐5‐ether (PFCE)‐loaded core–shell silica nanoparticles (s‐FLAME), which combine high 19F signal intensity with reduced size distribution. The established one‐pot double‐emulsion‐based synthesis shortened the silica shell formation time, suppressed droplet coalescence, and kinetically fixed PFCE emulsions at smaller sizes, resulting in the preparation of uniform core–shell nanoparticles (s‐FLAME‐OH) with a core diameter of 30 nm—significantly smaller than previously reported liquid‐core systems. A co‐condensation approach yielded carboxyl‐functionalized nanoparticles (s‐FLAME‐COOH), which reduced cytotoxicity and improved colloidal stability. The resulting nanoparticles exhibited strong, concentration‐dependent 19F MRI signals. In vivo 19F MRI revealed accelerated hepatic clearance and tumor‐associated signals, indicating size‐dependent biodistribution. This study establishes a versatile strategy for tuning the size and surface chemistry of silica‐based 19F MRI nanoprobes to achieve controlled in vivo behaviors, providing a promising platform for future molecular imaging applications.

Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is one of the most powerful noninvasive diagnostic techniques in clinical medicine owing to its excellent spatial resolution, deep tissue penetration, and lack of ionizing radiation [1, 2]. Conventional proton (1H) MRI, however, suffers from limited quantitative capability because of variable background signals arising from endogenous water and fat [2]. In contrast, fluorine‐19 (19F) MRI offers background‐free and quantitative imaging, as 19F is almost absent in biological tissues, and its signal intensity is directly proportional to the local fluorine concentration [3, 4, 5]. These features make 19F MRI an attractive modality for cell tracking, molecular imaging, and pharmacokinetic studies [6]. Nevertheless, the inherently low sensitivity of 19F detection necessitates the development of probes with high fluorine density and excellent biocompatibility.

Various materials have been explored as 19F MRI probes, including small‐molecule‐based 19F MRI probes [7], perfluorocarbon (PFC)‐based 19F MRI probes [8, 9, 10], polymeric nanoparticles and dendrimers [11], molecular clusters [12], and inorganic fluorinated nanoparticles [13, 14]. Among these, perfluorocarbon‐based 19F MRI probes are widely used because of their high fluorine density, high MR sensitivity, and commercial availability [5]. Perfluorocarbon‐encapsulated nanoparticles are generally prepared using nanoemulsion templating to maintain liquid perfluorocarbons in the core, which enables a high amount of 19F encapsulation while overcoming the solubility issue [5]. Such nanoemulsion systems often fall within the size range of ≥ 100 nm because of the intrinsic nature of nanoemulsions [5].

A few methods have been applied, including the use of polymers [9] and silica [15] to stabilize the nanoemulsion size. Silica‐based nanostructures have emerged as particularly promising platforms because of their chemical stability, tunable porosity, and versatile surface engineering. Previously, our group developed a perfluoro‐15‐crown‐5‐ether (PFCE)‐encapsulated core–shell silica nanoparticle, termed FLAME (FLuorine Accumulated silica nanoparticle for MRI contrast Enhancement), which exhibits a liquid perfluorocarbon core surrounded by a dense silica shell [15]. This design allows for high fluorine loading and strong, quantifiable MRI signals, enabling in vivo activatable imaging [16, 17, 18, 19]. However, conventional FLAME nanoparticles typically possess core diameters over 70 nm and hydrodynamic diameters of approximately 150 nm, which is consistent with those of the original nanoemulsions. The large size of these perfluorocarbon‐based nanoparticles leads to slow hepatic clearance and limited tumor accumulation [15, 20]. These issues restrict their potential for translational applications, where both efficient signal generation and rapid elimination are required for clinical safety.

The in vivo fate of nanoparticles is largely governed by their size and surface properties [21, 22]. Nanoparticles larger than 100 nm are prone to sequestration by the mononuclear phagocyte system, resulting in prolonged hepatic retention, while smaller particles (< 50 nm) can escape hepatic filtration more readily and undergo faster hepatobiliary or renal clearance [23, 24]. Furthermore, surface chemistry strongly influences the stability of nanoparticles and their interactions with biological components. Hydrophilic and negatively charged surfaces can reduce nonspecific adsorption and improve dispersion in physiological media. For 19F MRI probes, balancing the particle size, surface chemistry, and fluorine content is critical for achieving high signal intensity and favorable pharmacokinetics simultaneously. However, reducing the size of liquid core–shell nanoparticles remains challenging. Emulsion‐based synthesis typically requires prolonged hydrolysis and condensation of silane precursors on unstable PFC droplets, during which Ostwald ripening and droplet coalescence often occur. These processes make it difficult to maintain a uniform morphology and high encapsulation efficiency when targeting sub‐50‐nm particles [8, 9, 10].

In this study, we aimed to develop a smaller and biocompatible version of PFC‐encapsulated core–shell silica nanoparticles as 19F MRI probes. We redesigned the synthesis process to generate ultrasmall core–shell silica nanoparticles (s‐FLAME) through a one‐pot double‐emulsion‐based method. This synthesis method significantly shortened the silica shell formation time, suppressed droplet coalescence, and kinetically fixed PFCE emulsions at smaller sizes. This method enabled the preparation of much smaller and more uniform nanoparticles than those achievable by the conventional FLAME process, which often results in particle coalescence and size growth. Furthermore, we adopted a co‐condensation method [25, 26] to introduce carboxyl functional groups during nanoparticle formation, which achieved simultaneous surface modification. The decrease in nanoparticle size accelerated hepatic clearance without compromising the fluorine encapsulation or MRI sensitivity. Our findings highlight a straightforward and scalable design strategy for tuning the size and surface chemistry of fluorinated nanoprobes to achieve improved biocompatibility and clearance, while maintaining strong imaging performance.

Results and Discussion

Development Strategy for Smaller Nanoparticles

Conventional FLAME synthesis, which relies on prolonged emulsion‐templated sol‐gel processes, typically results in particle size growth. Although we have attempted several optimizations, such as changing different phospholipids and silica precursors to reduce the size, the effects were minimal (see Supplementary Information and Figures S1–S3 for details). To overcome the intrinsic constraints of the emulsion‐templated route, we introduced a conceptually distinct one‐pot synthesis (Figure 1a) that eliminates the pre‐formation of PFCE nanoemulsions. Because PFCs constitute a third phase immiscible with both water and hydrocarbons, we hypothesized that direct mixing of PFCE with aqueous and silica‐precursor phases could lead to the formation of a PFC–silane double‐phase emulsion in which the PFCE phase is transiently encapsulated within a TEOS‐rich layer (Figure 1b). This unique PFC–silane double‐phase structure, although typically considered highly unstable, was directly observed by cryogenic electron microscopy (Figure 1d). Notably, the inner phase of the double‐phase nanoemulsion exhibited a secondary double‐emulsion structure characterized by an additional droplet‐like core within the perfluorocarbon phase. A similar structural feature was observed in our previous study on conventional FLAME nanoparticles using cryo‐EM [27] as well as the prepared ultrasmall nanoparticles (Figure S4). We speculate that this internal core corresponds to a small amount of water that is transiently entrapped within the perfluorocarbon phase during the emulsification process. Such nanoscale water inclusions are known to occur under dynamic emulsification conditions [28].

This double‐emulsion structure enables the in situ nucleation, hydrolyzation, and condensation of the silica precursor, directly resulting in a core‐shell particle structure with liquid PFC (in this case, PFCE) inside a silica shell (Figure 1c). Compared to the conventional synthesis method of FLAME, this process significantly shortened the formation time of silica coating, thus significantly suppressing the process of coalescence and enabling the “fix” of some PFCE nanoemulsions at smaller sizes.

Synthesis and Characterization of s‐FLAME‐OH

The emulsification procedure was performed using a modified protocol by Doan‐Nguyen et al. [30], who prepared small silica nanocapsules via controlled Ostwald ripening in an oil‐in‐water system. While their method serves as a practical reference, our fluorocarbon–silane triphasic synthesis operates through a distinct mechanism that stabilizes immiscible PFCE droplets into ultrasmall core–shell nanoparticles.

To identify suitable components for this synthesis, we evaluated different silica precursors and surfactants (Figure 2, Table S1). Three silica precursors—tetramethyl orthosilicate (TMOS), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), and tetrapropyl orthosilicate (TPOS)—were selected based on their decreasing hydrolysis rates [28, 31]. Three surfactants were examined: cetyltrimethylammonium chloride (CTAC) or cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), a single‐chain surfactant commonly used in silica nanoparticle synthesis; and 1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (DSPC), a double‐chain phospholipid typically employed in FLAME preparation [15].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to assess the formation of nanoparticles. First, the influence of the silica precursor was investigated using CTAC as a surfactant. The TEM images of the nanoparticles prepared with TMOS showed silica fragments without a PFCE core, suggesting that the rapid hydrolysis of TMOS hindered stable micelle formation. In contrast, the nanoparticles synthesized with TPOS displayed incomplete encapsulation of the PFCE core, likely due to its slow hydrolysis. TEOS, which has an intermediate hydrolysis rate, produced uniform nanoparticles with well‐defined core–shell structures and was therefore selected as the optimal precursor (Figure 2a). Subsequently, when TEOS was used as the silica precursor, the nanoparticles synthesized with CTAB and CTAC exhibited distinct core–shell morphologies, whereas those prepared with DSPC were irregular and poorly defined, indicating that DSPC was unsuitable for this one‐pot approach (Figure 2b). Finally, the effect of the reactant ratio on the particle morphology was examined (Figure S5). Based on these observations, the conditions for the one‐pot synthesis of s‐FLAME‐OH were optimized.

The one‐pot synthesis produced a mixture of nanoparticles of different sizes (Figure S6). Although some large silica nanoparticles were observed, smaller nanoparticles with diameters of approximately 30 nm were also formed (Figure S6b). To enrich the ultrasmall nanoparticle population, we performed post‐synthesis size separation. Post‐synthesis size separation is widely applied for nanomaterial species that are difficult to simply obtain by synthesis [32, 33, 34]. In our study, we performed centrifugation at 14 000 × g for 30 min to separate these fractions. The larger nanoparticles sedimented, whereas the ultrasmall nanoparticles remained in the supernatant owing to their lower density. To remove the cytotoxic surfactant CTAC, sequential extraction and dialysis were performed (Figure S6a). The supernatant containing the ultrasmall nanoparticles was first extracted with an aqueous acetic acid solution and subsequently dialyzed with deionized water. The removal efficiency of CTAC was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy, which showed that more than 99% of CTAC was eliminated, indicating the effective purification of the nanoparticle dispersion (Figure S6c). Compared to conventional liquid‐core shell silica nanoparticle syntheses, which typically require multiple sequential steps including emulsion formation, silica shell condensation, and post‐synthetic functionalization, this one‐pot approach significantly simplifies the overall process and can be completed within approximately 4 days.

The purified nanoparticles were characterized to confirm their morphology, size distribution, and fluorine content (Figure 3). TEM revealed uniform spherical nanoparticles with well‐defined core–shell structures and an average core diameter of approximately 30 nm (Figure 3a). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed a hydrodynamic diameter of 34 nm with a narrow distribution (polydispersity index < 0.1), indicating colloidal uniformity (Figure 2b). The ζ‐potential of −3.9 mV suggests a nearly neutral surface charge, consistent with the presence of terminal silanol groups on the silica surface [35]. The 19F NMR spectrum of the nanoparticles exhibited a single sharp resonance identical to that of bulk PFCE, confirming successful encapsulation without chemical degradation (Figure 2c). The synthesis was repeated in at least four independent batches, showing good batch‐to‐batch reproducibility in terms of particle size distribution, ζ‐potential, and PFCE loading (Figure S7). The longitudinal relaxation time (T 1) and transverse relaxation time (T 2) were 547 and 463 ms, respectively (Table S2). Furthermore, phantom 19F MRI measurements demonstrated a strong and linear increase in the signal intensity as a function of nanoparticle concentration, represented by the PFCE content. Linear regression fitting analysis confirmed the quantitative imaging capability of s‐FLAME‐OH (Figure 2d–f). The overall MRI signal intensity was comparable to that of conventional FLAME nanoparticles and s‐FLAME‐OH nanoparticles even exhibited a modestly higher SNR compared to FLAME. This difference may be associated with variations in dispersion stability between the two systems. Larger nanoparticles such as FLAME are more susceptible to changes in dispersion state over time, which may reduce the effective 19F signal intensity. In contrast, the smaller s‐FLAME‐OH nanoparticles exhibit improved dispersion stability, contributing to more consistent signal detection. Collectively, these results indicate that the one‐pot synthesis yields ultrasmall, monodisperse core–shell nanoparticles with stable fluorine encapsulation suitable for subsequent biological evaluation.

The cytotoxicity of the s‐FLAME‐OH nanoparticles was assessed using HeLa cells (Figure S8). A pronounced concentration‐dependent decrease in cell viability was observed, indicating that the unmodified nanoparticles exhibited significant cytotoxicity. Such toxicity is commonly attributed to the generation of reactive oxygen species and disruption of cell membranes caused by surface silanol groups, which can strongly interact with and destabilize cellular components. Unmodified silica nanoparticles possess abundant terminal silanol (Si–OH) groups that can form hydrogen‐bond with membrane phospholipids and proteins, leading to oxidative stress, loss of membrane integrity, and apoptosis. The magnitude of this effect increases with higher specific surface area and stronger positive surface charge, both of which enhance cellular adsorption and internalization. Therefore, reducing the density and reactivity of surface silanol groups is essential to improve the biocompatibility of silica‐based nanomaterials [36, 37, 38].

Development and Characterization of s‐FLAME‐COOH

In this co‐condensation approach, 5‐(triethoxysilyl)pentanoic acid, a carboxyl‐functionalized silane with a slower hydrolysis rate, was mixed with TEOS during one‐pot synthesis (Figure 4a). The two precursors co‐condensed at the nanoparticle surface, forming silica shells bearing terminal carboxylic acid groups and yielding carboxyl‐modified nanoparticles, denoted as s‐FLAME‐COOH. This strategy enabled the direct formation of uniformly functionalized and well‐dispersed nanoparticles without requiring additional surface treatment.

The morphology and physicochemical properties of s‐FLAME‐COOH were characterized and compared with those of s‐FLAME‐OH (Figure 4b–g). TEM revealed that s‐FLAME‐COOH retained a clear core–shell architecture but exhibited a more spherical and uniform morphology than s‐FLAME‐OH, with an average particle size of approximately 30 nm (Figure 4b). The increased sphericity of s‐FLAME‐COOH may be attributed to changes in the interfacial condensation dynamics induced by the carboxyl‐functionalized silane, which stabilized the micellar structure during shell formation. DLS analysis showed an increased hydrodynamic diameter of 59 nm with a relatively narrow distribution (polydispersity index < 0.2) (Figure 4c). This hydrodynamic diameter of s‐FLAME‐COOH is larger than the core size observed by TEM, which can be attributed to the presence of surface charges and the associated electrical double layer, as well as interfacial hydration effects in aqueous solution. The ζ‐potential decreased markedly from −3.9 to −70.7 mV after carboxylation, confirming the introduction of negatively charged carboxyl groups on the nanoparticle surface (Figure 4d). This result corresponds to the surface‐carboxylated nanoparticles in the previous study (see Figure S9 for comparison between FLAME and s‐FLAME). The 19F NMR spectrum displayed a single sharp resonance identical to that of s‐FLAME‐OH (Figure 4e), indicating that the chemical state of PFCE did not change after modification. The synthesis was repeated in at least three independent batches, showing good batch‐to‐batch reproducibility in terms of the particle size distribution, ζ‐potential, and PFCE loading (Figure S10). The longitudinal relaxation time (T 1) and transverse relaxation time (T 2) of s‐FLAME‐COOH were 658 and 551 ms, respectively (Table S2). Phantom 19F MRI measurements showed comparable signal intensities and concentration‐dependent SNR responses between s‐FLAME‐COOH and s‐FLAME‐OH. Linear regression fitting analysis further demonstrated that the co‐condensation process preserved both fluorine loading and imaging capability (Figure 4f,g). Collectively, these results confirm that the co‐condensation strategy successfully produced uniform carboxyl‐functionalized nanoparticles with improved shape regularity and surface charge characteristics. This strategy is also highly efficient, which enables carboxyl‐functionalization without requiring an additional post‐synthetic modification step.

The cytotoxicity of s‐FLAME‐COOH was assessed using the same WST assay protocol described for s‐FLAME‐OH (Figure S11). In contrast to the pronounced concentration‐dependent cytotoxicity observed for s‐FLAME‐OH, s‐FLAME‐COOH exhibited minimal cytotoxicity across the tested concentration range. This strong improvement is attributed to surface carboxylation, which reduces the number of reactive silanol groups and suppresses their interactions with cellular membranes. These results confirm that the co‐condensation strategy effectively enhances the biocompatibility of the nanoparticles, making s‐FLAME‐COOH suitable for in vivo studies. To evaluate nanoparticle stability under biologically relevant conditions, the behavior of s‐FLAME‐COOH in serum was then investigated. 19F NMR measurements in serum showed sharp spectral peaks, and the transverse relaxation time (T 2) remained essentially unchanged compared to those measured in aqueous solution (Figure S12), indicating that the 19F MRI readout is minimally affected and that the PFCE core remains structurally intact in serum.

In Vivo 19F MRI and Biodistribution of s‐FLAME‐COOH Nanoparticles

To evaluate the in vivo behavior of s‐FLAME‐COOH, 19F MRI was first performed on Jcl‐ICR mice after intravenous administration of the nanoparticles (Figure 5a,b). A 100 µL dispersion of s‐FLAME‐COOH with a PFCE concentration of 10 mM was injected into a healthy mouse, and 19F MRI measurements were conducted at multiple time points post‐injection. Immediately after administration, strong 19F signals were observed in the liver, indicating predominant hepatic uptake of the nanoparticles. Interestingly, the signal intensity increased from Day 1 to 2, which is likely attributed to the prolonged circulation of s‐FLAME‐COOH, resulting in delayed peak liver accumulation at approximately 24 h after injection. Subsequently, the hepatic signal gradually decreased, and only weak residual signals were detected after approximately 2 weeks, suggesting efficient elimination through the hepatobiliary route. This phenomenon was observed consistently in three animals (Figure 5c, Figure S13), indicating reproducibility in vivo behavior.

In contrast, conventional FLAME nanoparticles exhibited persistent liver signals under identical imaging conditions [20, 29]. These findings demonstrate that the reduced size of nanoparticles markedly accelerates the hepatic clearance of silica‐based 19F MRI probes while maintaining their imaging performance, enabling longitudinal imaging with a minimal residual background signal.

The faster hepatic clearance of s‐FLAME‐COOH is likely attributed to its smaller size and improved dispersibility compared with conventional FLAME nanoparticles. The ultrasmall core–shell structure (∼30 nm core, ∼60 nm hydrodynamic diameter) enables more efficient transport through hepatic sinusoids and subsequent hepatobiliary excretion. In addition, the highly negative surface charge of s‐FLAME‐COOH promotes colloidal stability in physiological environments, preventing aggregation, which could otherwise lead to prolonged retention in the liver. These physicochemical characteristics account for the distinct temporal 19F MRI profile of s‐FLAME‐COOH, characterized by delayed peak accumulation and rapid signal decay, highlighting the critical influence of nanoparticle size and surface properties on in vivo clearance behavior.

To investigate potential tumor accumulation, 19F MRI was conducted using BALB/c‐nu/nu mice bearing subcutaneous tumors established from Colon‐26 murine colon carcinoma cells after intravenous administration of s‐FLAME‐COOH (Figure 5d, Figures S14 and S15). 19F signals were detected at both the liver and tumor sites 24 h post‐injection, suggesting nanoparticle localization in the tumor region. This behavior was observed in at least three mice. However, the tumor‐associated signals observed in the present study varied across animals, suggesting that tumor accumulation of s‐FLAME‐COOH is limited and influenced by complex in vivo factors. Interactions with serum components, such as protein adsorption, may alter the effective particle size and biological identity of the nanoparticles, thereby affecting their biodistribution and tumor accumulation behavior. Further surface engineering, such as PEGylation or the incorporation of targeting ligands, may improve delivery efficiency and reproducibility. These results demonstrate that s‐FLAME‐COOH combines accelerated in vivo clearance with detectable passive tumor accumulation, highlighting its potential for tumor imaging and delivery applications.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a new generation of sub‐50 nm core–shell silica nanoparticles for 19F MRI that achieve both high signal intensity and improved in vivo clearance. A one‐pot double‐emulsion‐based synthesis was established, which significantly shortened the silica shell formation time, suppressed droplet coalescence, and kinetically “froze” PFCE emulsions at smaller sizes, enabling the fabrication of ultrasmall and uniform nanoparticles. This strategy enabled the formation of sub‐50 nm PFCE‐encapsulated silica nanoparticles (s‐FLAME‐OH) with a controlled size and stable fluorine loading. Subsequent surface carboxylation via a co‐condensation approach produced uniformly functionalized and biocompatible s‐FLAME‐COOH nanoparticles without post‐synthetic treatment. The resulting nanoparticles exhibited a well‐defined spherical morphology, a high 19F MRI signal comparable to that of conventional FLAME, and markedly improved hepatic clearance in vivo.

Tumor‐associated signals were detected but remained limited, indicating that passive tumor accumulation of s‐FLAME‐COOH is modest under the present conditions. Further optimization through surface engineering, such as PEGylation or the incorporation of targeting ligands, is expected to enhance delivery efficiency and reproducibility. Despite this limitation, s‐FLAME‐COOH demonstrates a favorable combination of accelerated in vivo clearance and detectable tumor‐associated signal, supporting its potential for tumor imaging and delivery applications.

Overall, this work establishes a generalizable design principle for ultrasmall, biocompatible fluorine nanoprobes with efficient clearance and tunable biological behavior. We demonstrate non‐toxic, sub‐50 nm PFCE‐loaded silica nanoparticles that retain a strong and quantifiable 19F MRI signal, enabling real‐time in vivo tracking. This combination of high fluorine payload and a sub‐50 nm, rigid silica carrier represents a technically non‐trivial advance for biomedical imaging.
