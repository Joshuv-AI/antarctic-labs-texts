Fosl1 is a transcriptional effector of BRAFV600E-driven intestinal tumorigenesis
Authors: Zakia Alam, Rebecca Nightingale, Analia Lesmana, Cheng Liu, Laura J Jenkins, Mark F Richardson, Lawrence Croft, Ian Y Luk, Camilla M Reehorst, Fiona Chionh, Natalia Vukelic, Faiza Basheer, Eugene Tulchinsky, Joshua Badshah, Troy Dumenil, Latifa Bakiri, Erwin F Wagner, Niall C Tebbutt, Vicki Whitehall, John M Mariadason, Amardeep S Dhillon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe serrated neoplasia pathway is an alternate route to colorectal cancer (CRC) development where BRAFV600E is the most common initiating genetic alteration. BRAFV600E-driven tumorigenesis requires gene expression changes mediated by activation of the ERK MAPK signaling pathway. However, the key effectors of this process are elusive. Here, we identify the ERK-regulated transcription factor Fosl1, one such effector. We show that Fosl1 is dispensable for the initiation of BRAFV600E-driven serrated neoplasia in mice but promotes progression of the disease by regulating the expression of genes involved in inflammation, immunity, cell cycle control, fetal-like programming, and gastric metaplasia. Notably, transgenic Fosl1 expression alone was sufficient to induce tumors with a BRAFV600E-like serrated morphology and transcriptional profile. These findings reveal a mechanism through which oncogenic BRAF-driven ERK signaling reprograms transcription to drive serrated neoplasia.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Molecular mechanism of gene regulation, Cancer

Introduction

Most colorectal cancers (CRCs) arise via an adenoma-carcinoma sequence initiated by genetic lesions that activate the Wnt signaling pathway.1 However, up to 30% of CRCs develop via the less characterized serrated neoplasia pathway, where the most common initiating genetic alteration is a valine to glutamate substitution at codon 600 of the BRAF gene.2 The resulting BRAFV600E protein drives persistent activation of the ERK signaling pathway, a central regulator of gene expression programs governing key cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and survival.3

Serrated tumors have the poorest prognosis among CRCs.4 They develop mainly in the proximal colon, tend to be mucinous and poorly differentiated, and metastasize to the peritoneum and distant lymph nodes.2 They also often display the CpG island methylator phenotype and high rates of microsatellite instability, with microsatellite stable cases having poorer outcomes.5 Serrated CRCs arise from sessile serrated lesions (SSLs), which can lie dormant for many years before becoming dysplastic (SSLs with dysplasia, SSLD) and rapidly progressing to CRC.6,7,8 The mechanisms underlying progression of SSLs/SSLDs to cancers are not well understood, making predictions of which lesions are likely to progress into cancers a problem. Furthermore, because they appear flat, SSLs and SSLDs are often not detected during colonoscopy-based screening and are thus a significant source of interval cancers. Recent work indicates that unlike conventional adenomas that arise in stem cells, serrated CRCs develop from differentiated cells via a metaplastic process involving activation of gastric and fetal intestinal genes.9,10,11,12 This metaplasia is thought to arise due to immune responses and inflammation driven by microbial dysbiosis.

In addition to gene expression changes linked with metaplasia, BRAFV600E-driven serrated neoplasia involves a multitude of other transcriptional changes that impact various processes critical for tumor development and progression.11,13,14,15 However, the molecular effectors that couple BRAFV600E-ERK signaling with these transcriptional changes are not known. Identifying these proteins is important to gain a deeper insight into the mechanisms of BRAFV600E-driven serrated neoplasia and uncover potential strategies to inhibit the pathway by restricting its oncogenic transcriptional output.

Activator protein-1 (AP-1) is a transcription factor that consists of dimers formed by proteins with bZIP domains, among which members of the FOS and JUN families are most strongly linked with the ERK pathway.16,17 In this study, we identified FOSL1 as the main FOS or JUN isoform specifically associated with BRAF mutations in human CRCs and mouse intestinal tumors. Through intestinal-specific knockin, genetic deletion, and transgenic approaches, we show that Fosl1 is critical for the progression but not initiation of mutant Braf-driven serrated neoplasia in mice. These effects involved Fosl1 regulation of mutant Braf-induced transcriptional programs impacting tumor inflammation, immunity, cell cycle progression, fetal-like programming, and gastric metaplasia. We also found that about 20% of animals expressing a Fosl1 transgene alone developed intestinal tumors. These tumors bore striking resemblance to the histological and transcriptional profile of Braf mutant tumors. These findings thus reveal a key mechanism of oncogenic BRAF-driven transcriptional reprogramming in serrated neoplasia.

Results

FOSL1 is the main FOS/JUN AP-1 component associated with BRAF mutations in CRC

We first examined the TCGA colorectal adenocarcinoma dataset18 to determine if BRAF mutations are associated with altered expression of specific FOS or JUN isoforms in CRC. Among 3 transcripts (FOSL1, FOSL2, and JUN) displaying significantly altered expression, increased FOSL1 abundance was most strongly associated with BRAF mutation status (Figures 1A and S1). This association was also evident in CRC cell lines,19 where FOSL1 was the only FOS or JUN isoform significantly enriched in BRAF mutant compared to BRAF wild-type lines (Figures 1B and S2), which was confirmed by immunoblotting (Figure 1C). Finally, analysis of human colonic polyp gene expression data20 revealed elevated FOSL1 and JUNB levels in sessile serrated adenomas/polyps (where BRAF mutations are common) but not conventional adenomatous polyps (where BRAF mutations are rare) (Figures 1D and S3).

We next assessed Fos and Jun isoform abundance in a mouse model13 where conditional knockin of BrafV637E (equivalent to human BRAFV600E) using tamoxifen-inducible VillinCreERT2 induces rapid intestinal hyperplasia that progresses into murine serrated adenomas (mSAs) and carcinomas (Figure 1E). Consistent with our findings in human CRC, Fosl1 was the only Fos or Jun isoform specifically enriched in BrafV637E intestinal adenomas and carcinomas compared to BrafV637E hyperplastic crypts and the normal intestinal epithelium (Figures 1F and S4). This was confirmed by immunostaining for the ERK-phosphorylated Fosl1 protein, which was detected in 30%–40% of tumor cell nuclei but not in adjacent Braf-mutant hyperplastic crypts or in the normal intestinal epithelium (Figure 1G).

Fosl1 deficiency attenuates progression of BrafV637E-induced serrated neoplasia

Oncogene-driven ERK signaling has been shown to induce FOSL1 expression in CRC cell lines by activating transcription of the FOSL1 gene and through ERK-mediated phosphorylation of serine-265, which prevents proteosomal degradation of the FOSL1 protein.21,22,23,24 In mice, Fosl1 is dispensable for colitis-associated intestinal cancer induced by azoxymethane (AOM)/dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) treatment,25 but its role of Fosl1 in other subsets of CRC is unknown. To establish the role of Fosl1 during development and progression of BrafV637E-induced serrated neoplasia, we crossed BrafV637E/+;Vil-CreERT2/+ mice (denoted B) with Fosl1-floxed mice26 (denoted Ffl/fl) and treated their offspring with tamoxifen to induce Cre-mediated recombination. BF−/− and BF+/+ mice developed similar numbers of intestinal lesions when examined at 10–12 months post-Cre activation but did not display any health concerns (Figures 2A and 2B). However, BF−/− lesions were significantly smaller (Figure 2C) and contained fewer proliferative (Ki67-positive) cells (Figures 2D and 2E). Histologically, all lesions displayed hyperplasia, but the incidence of mSA was about 36% lower in lesions from BF−/− mice (7/11) compared to BF+/+ mice (11/11, p < 0.05, one-sided Fisher’s exact t test) (Figure 2F). Thus, Fosl1 regulates progression but not initiation of BrafV637E-induced serrated neoplasia.

Fosl1 regulates a subset of BrafV637E-induced transcriptional programs

To understand how Fosl1 regulates BrafV637E-driven intestinal neoplasia progression, we profiled the transcriptomes of BF+/+ and BF−/− adenomas using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using transcriptional hallmarks curated in the Molecular Signature database (MSigDB) revealed that Fosl1 deletion reduced the enrichment scores of a subset of hallmarks enriched in BF+/+ adenomas compared to control intestinal tissue (Figures 3A, S5, and S6). These transcriptional signatures contained genes involved in the regulation of cell cycle progression (Myc targets, E2F targets, G2M checkpoint), immune response (interferon response, allograft rejection), and inflammation (inflammatory response, IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, TNFA signaling via NFKB). Fosl1 deficiency did not result in significant changes in levels of other AP-1 family member transcripts in the adenomas (Figure S7).

Recent studies indicate that serrated CRCs develop via a metaplastic process wherein immune responses and inflammation induced by microbial dysbiosis promote activation of gastric and fetal intestinal genes.9,10,11,12 Compared to normal intestinal tissue, BF+/+ adenomas showed enrichment of a fetal intestinal gene signature and higher transcript levels of several genes normally expressed in gastric surface mucous, mucous neck, pit, and foveolar cells27,28 (Figures 3A–3D, S5, and S6). The levels of these gastric genes and enrichment of fetal intestinal genes were significantly reduced in BF−/− adenomas (Figures 3B–3D). These findings indicate that Fosl1 regulates metaplastic changes associated with progression of oncogenic Braf-driven serrated neoplasia.

Transgenic Fosl1 expression accelerates BrafV637E-driven intestinal neoplasia

Although intestinal hyperplasia was evident in our mice shortly after BrafV637E activation, tumors only began to emerge after 8 months. As Fosl1 was expressed in these tumors but not in adjacent hyperplastic crypts (Figures 1F and 1G), we wondered if the disease would progress quicker if Fosl1 expression was induced shortly after activating BrafV637E. To test this, BF+/+ mice were crossed with animals carrying a doxycycline-inducible FLAG-tagged Fosl1 transgene that can be expressed in a tissue-specific manner26,29 (denoted FTg). Shortly after inducing Cre-mediated recombination of mutant Braf, BFTg animals were fed a doxycycline-laced (denoted BFTg-ON mice) or control diet (denoted BFTg-OFF mice). By 8 months post-transgene activation, most BFTg-ON animals displayed symptoms of ill health, including abdominal swelling (60% of mice), weight loss (70% of mice), anemia (20% of mice), and severe hair loss (10% of mice). Due to these issues, 90% of BFTg-ON mice had to be euthanized by 13 months (Figure 4A). In contrast, BFTg-OFF mice first displayed symptoms of ill health requiring euthanasia from 13 months post-BrafV637E activation, with 60% of animals surviving over 18 months. Unexpectedly, while BFTg-OFF mice developed tumors only in the proximal duodenum, BFTg-ON animals also developed tumors in the distal duodenum (60% of mice) and jejunum (20% of mice) (Figure 4B). Confirming the proper functioning of the model, levels of Flag-Fosl1 mRNA and phospho-Fosl1 protein were significantly higher in BFTg-ON compared to BFTg-OFF tumors (Figure S8).

In addition to developing in more distal regions of the small intestine, BFTg-ON mice developed more and larger tumors than BFTg-OFF mice (Figures 4C and 4D). BFTg-ON tumors also contained a higher proportion of proliferative (Ki67-positive) cells compared to BFTg-OFF tumors (Figures 4E and 4F). Histological assessment revealed that despite being harvested earlier (8–13 months) than BFTg-OFF tumors (13–18 months), a higher proportion of BFTg-ON tumors were carcinomas (60% vs. 27%, Figure 4G). However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.19, Fisher’s exact test). Notably, BFTg-ON carcinomas displayed higher stromal content and invasion into the basement membrane than BFTg-OFF carcinomas (Figure 4H). These findings indicate that early induction of Fosl1 cooperates with BrafV637E to accelerate tumor growth and serrated neoplasia progression.

Transgenic Fosl1 expression induces BrafV637E-like intestinal tumors

To determine the extent to which intestinal-specific Fosl1 expression alone could recapitulate the effects of BrafV637E, we crossed Villin-CreERT2 mice with FTg mice. After tamoxifen treatment, animals were fed control or doxycycline-laced chow and monitored for up to 18 months. At 13 months post-transgene induction, one FTg-ON mouse became ill (abdominal distension, anemia, weight loss) and had to be euthanized. It had developed a tumor in the jejunum that occluded the intestine. Upon culling the remaining cohort at 18 months, 2 more animals were found to have developed intestinal adenomas. Histological analysis revealed that all these lesions had a morphology almost identical to BF+/+ adenomas (Figure 5A).

We next used RNA-seq to compare the transcriptomes of the FTg-ON adenomas to BF+/+ adenomas and intestinal epithelial tissue from control mice aged 18 months. Principal-component analysis (PCA) showed that the transcriptional profiles of FTg-ON and BF+/+ adenomas were more closely clustered compared to the distance from the control intestinal epithelium (Figure 5B). Among 4,940 genes upregulated and 3,973 downregulated in FTg-ON adenomas compared to control epithelial tissue, 3,808 (61%) were similarly upregulated and 3,085 (64%) downregulated in BF+/+ adenomas (Figure 5C). Accordingly, GSEA analysis revealed concordance in the enrichment profiles of MSigDB hallmark transcriptional signatures and the fetal intestinal signature in BF+/+ and FTg-ON adenomas compared to normal intestinal epithelial tissue (Figures 5D and 5E). As observed in BF+/+ adenomas, genes associated with gastric metaplasia were also significantly upregulated in FTg-ON adenomas (Figure 5E). These findings indicate that Fosl1 alone is sufficient to induce serrated adenomas, albeit less efficiently than BrafV637E and that Fosl1- and BrafV637E-initiated adenomas are highly similar at both the histological and molecular levels.

Discussion

The ERK pathway plays a central role in orchestrating transcriptional changes involved in oncogenic BRAF-driven tumorigenesis, but our understanding of how this occurs is limited. Here, we show that the AP-1 family transcription factor FOSL1 is critical for coupling oncogenic BRAF-ERK signaling with gene expression programs driving serrated neoplasia progression.

BRAFV600E-initiated serrated neoplasia in mice develops after a long latency, which is reduced upon concomitant inactivation of key drivers of intestinal differentiation (e.g., Cdx2, Smad4, and Alk5).11,12,30 Disease progression in these models correlates with intense ERK activation in tumors but not hyperplastic crypts,14 mirroring the profile of Fosl1 expression we observed. These findings imply that the mechanisms restricting ERK activation upon Braf oncogene activation (e.g., upregulation of negative feedback regulators) must be overcome for tumorigenesis to occur. We reasoned that the impacts of these mechanisms could be bypassed by enforced activation of key effectors mediating the downstream effects of the ERK pathway. Consistent with Fosl1 playing such a role, transgenic expression of Fosl1 shortly after BrafV637E activation resulted in a more advanced disease phenotype within a shorter time frame.

A somewhat unexpected finding was that expression of a Fosl1 transgene alone was sufficient to induce tumors in about 20% of mice. However, their lower incidence and longer latency compared to tumors induced by BrafV637E, along with the negligible impact of Fosl1 deletion on BrafV637E-induced tumor numbers, implies that Fosl1 has poor tumor initiating capacity. Nevertheless, our finding that Fosl1 transgene-expressing tumors closely resembled the histological and molecular profiles of BrafV637E-induced serrated tumors underscores a key role for Fosl1 in regulating the tumorigenesis via the serrated neoplasia pathway.

While not essential for Braf637E-induced intestinal tumor initiation, Fosl1-deficient tumors were smaller, contained fewer proliferative cells, and displayed attenuated progression from hyperplastic to serrated lesions. These phenotypic changes were associated with differential activation of a specific subset of Braf637E-induced transcriptional programs, including genes linked with mitotic checkpoints and cell cycle control, whose reduced enrichment is consistent with the lower proliferation rates of Fosl1-deficient tumors. Notably, similar impacts on the mitotic machinery were also observed when Fosl1 was deleted in Kras-driven mouse models of lung and pancreatic adenocarcinomas.31

Inflammation driven by immune responses to mucosal damage has been proposed to be a key feature of the microenvironment in which serrated CRCs evolve.9 These processes are thought to drive a metaplastic process not observed in conventional adenomas, characterized by the activation of gastric genes and YAP/TEAD-driven activation of fetal intestinal programs.9 Our findings indicate that an important mechanism whereby Fosl1 regulates BrafV637E-driven intestinal tumorigenesis is by modulating metaplasia, as Fosl1 deficiency dampened the activation of inflammatory, immune, fetal intestinal, and gastric metaplasia programs. In the context of fetal intestinal programs, our findings suggest that Fosl1 is a key component of AP-1 complexes that cooperate with YAP/TEAD32,33,34,35,36 to drive their activation.

In summary, our study highlights the value of delineating transcription effector networks to gain deeper insight into the mechanisms of oncogenic BRAF-ERK driven tumorigenesis. Our findings implicating FOSL1 as a driver of transcriptional programs promoting serrated neoplasia progression suggest that its expression could be used to identify serrated polyps that are likely to develop into cancers. They also suggest that approaches to target FOSL1 and/or the transcriptional programs it regulates could limit the pro-malignant effects of mutant BRAF-ERK signaling in CRC.

Limitations of the study

While our bulk RNA-seq analysis identifies tumor-intrinsic Fosl1 expression as a key modulator of inflammation and immune responses associated with progression of BRAFV600E-driven serrated neoplasia, the specific host cell types in the tumor microenvironment underlying these responses remain to be determined. Further work is also needed to identify direct Fosl1 target genes critical for progression of BRAF mutant intestinal adenomas. Finally, while we observed that transgenic Fosl1 expression cooperates with mutant BRAF to increased tumor number and size, it is unclear whether this reflects accelerated tumor development or enhanced progression. Serial tumor monitoring in mice expressing only mutant BRAF versus both mutant BRAF and the Fosl1 transgene could help resolve this. Alternatively, given its inducibility using doxycycline, another approach would be to allow BRAF mutant tumors to first emerge before activating transgenic Fosl1 expression.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with approvals from the animal ethics committees at LaTrobe University (AEC16-58, AEC16-94) and Austin Health (A2015–05310, A2018-05591) and conformed to the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes. Mice were housed in open-top boxes with basic enrichment and provided chow and drinking water ad libitum. Villin-CreERT2/+ mice,41 BrafV637E/+ mice,13 and mice bearing floxed42 or tetracycline-switchable29 Fosl1 alleles have been described previously. Male and female mice were randomly assigned to control or experimental cohorts. During experiments, animals were weighed and monitored at least once per week to the experimental or ethical endpoint. To induce Cre recombinase-mediated gene deletion, 6-week old mice were treated with 5 mg of tamoxifen (Sigma Aldrich, USA) diluted in sunflower seed oil from Helianthus annus (Sigma Aldrich, USA) by oral gavage for 2 consecutive days. Control mice were given the same dose regimen of sunflower oil solution. To induce the tetracycline-switchable Fosl1 transgene, mice were fed chow containing doxycycline at a concentration of 2000 mg/kg (Speciality Feeds Australia).

Cell culture conditions

CRC cell lines used in this study were maintained in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in DMEM-F12, supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (v/v), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (all from ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Cell lines were authenticated using the GenePrint 10 System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and routinely tested for mycoplasma status using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland).

Method details

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissues were prepared by incubating tissue placed in histology cassettes in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Australian Biostain) for 48 h followed by 80% ethanol. Samples were then processed and paraffin embedded (Department of Anatomical Pathology, Austin Hospital, Australia). For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, 4 μm sections were cut onto SuperFrost slides (Fisher Scientific, Australia) using a Leica Biosystems RM2245 Semi-Automated Rotary Microtome (Leica Biosystems, Australia). Slides were de-paraffinised and rehydrated through serial xylene and ethanol washes, rinsed in RO water and stained in filtered Mayer’s haematoxylin (Amber Scientific) for 60 s before washing in tap water. Slides were then placed in Scott’s tap water for 60 s, rinsed in tap water and counterstained in acidified aqueous eosin Y solution (Sigma Aldrich, USA) for 2–5 min before being dehydrated through serial ethanol and xylene washes and mounted with DPX (Sigma Aldrich, USA). Images from H&E−stained sections were captured using the Aperio AT2 Slide Scanner (Leica Biosystems, USA).

For immunohistochemical staining, FFPE sections were deparaffinised, rehydrated, and treated with 3% H2O2 (Science Supply Associates, Australia) for 20 min at room temperature. For antigen retrieval, the slides were incubated in citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate pH 6.0, 0.05% Tween) for 30 min at 100°C. After washing with Tris-buffered saline (pH 7.6) containing 0.05% Tween (TBST), the slides were blocked in 3% normal goat serum for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-Ki67 Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-14520) or anti-phospo-FOSL1 (Cell Signaling Technologies; Cat# 5841) antibodies. The slides were then washed with TBST and incubated with Dako envision anti-rabbit labeled polymer-HRP (Dako, USA) secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, washed with TBST, then incubated with 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (Dako, USA) until signal was detected. The slides were then counterstained with haematoxylin and mounted with coverslips using DPX (Sigma Aldrich, USA). Stained slides were scanned on an Aperio AT2 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems, USA) and image analysis was performed using the Aperio ImageScope software (v12.4.0.5043) or HALO image analysis software (Indica Labs. Version 3.4).

Intestinal epithelial cell collection

To isolate intestinal epithelial cells, the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum and ileum) and colon were opened longitudinally, washed in warm PBS and incubated in pre-warmed buffer 1 (PBS 93%, 0.5 M EDTA 3% and RNA secure 4%, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C on a shaker for 10 min. After vortexing the samples, the smooth muscle was removed and the epithelial cells pelleted by centrifugation at 1400g for 5 min at 4°C. The pellets were washed in Buffer 2 (PBS containing 4% RNA secure), centrifuged at 17,000 g for 1 min, and snap frozen.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA Lysis and Extraction buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 89900). Thirty micrograms of total protein from each cell line were resolved in precast 4%–12% Bis-Tris gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were then probed with anti-FOSL1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-28310) and anti-α-tubulin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-32293) antibodies.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR analysis

RNA was extracted from snap-frozen intestinal epithelial cells and tumors using the ReliaPrep Tissue Miniprep System (Promega). RNA concentration and quality was determined using a Nanodrop ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies, USA). cDNA was generated from 1000 ng of RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression levels were analyzed by qPCR using the ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Each reaction contained 0.75 μL each of 500 nM forward and reverse primer, 1 μL of cDNA (1:10 dilution), 2.5 μL of 2x PowerSYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fischer Scientific). Samples were amplified for 95°C for 10 min followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. Primer sequences used for qRT-PCR analysis are provided in Table S1 mRNA expression levels were calculated using the ΔCt method and normalised to levels of Tbp transcripts.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

RNA was isolated from intestinal epithelial cells and tumors using the AllPrep DNA/RNA/Protein Mini Kit (Qiagen) and its concentration and quality assessed using the TapeStation D1K TapeScreen assay (Agilent). cDNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit (v2) (Illumina Inc, San Diego, USA) or the stranded RNA library preparation kit with rRNA depletion (Illumina) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (100 paired end single reads). Sequence reads were quality filtered using fastp and mapped to the Mus musculus genome (GRCm38.99) using two pass mapping in STAR.43 Reads counts were aggregated to gene counts using STAR in built quantification. Read counts were converted to log2CPMs, quantile-normalised and precision weighted with the limma voom function. Log2CPM values were then converted to log2RPKM (reads per kilo exonic bases per million mapped reads) values. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using GSEA v4.1.0 software from the Broad institute.44 The data was analyzed against the MSigDB “HALLMARK” gene set signatures45 and a fetal intestine signature.46

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 (GraphPad Software, Fay Avenue, California, USA). All data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. A two-tailed Student’s T-test or Mann-Whitney test was used to determine statistical significance between two groups.
