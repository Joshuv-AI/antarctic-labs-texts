FH Genetic Testing in Preventive Cardiology: Unpacking Challenges and Enablers Beyond Cost
Authors: Chaitanyasre Lenin, Frederick H. F. Chan, Phoebe X. H. Lim, Gregory Ang, Ashna Nastar, Magdalena Daccord, Emma Print, Sharon Pek, Tavintharan Subramaniam, Konstadina Griva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ImportanceFamilial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is a common genetic condition associated with premature cardiovascular disease. Genetic testing enables definitive diagnosis and cascade screening, yet its uptake remains limited even in cost-free settings. As Singapore implements a national subsidized genetic testing service for FH, understanding persisting barriers to testing is critical to improving service design and implementation.ObjectiveThis qualitative study examined individual-level barriers and facilitators of genetic testing (GT) uptake and assessed experiences with genetic testing services to inform optimization of services.DesignOnline semi-structured interviews explored perceptions of FH, decision-making regarding GT, and experiences with GT services.Setting24 participants recruited from tertiary care and community settings, from August to October 2024, including 11 individuals who had undergone FH GT and 13 who declined or remained undecided.MeasuresBraun and Clarke’s thematic analysis was applied as a framework to identify major themes related to testing decisions and service experiences, supplemented by team-based coding to examine convergence and divergence across tested and untested subgroups.ResultsBoth tested and untested individuals recognized the personal, familial, and clinical value of GT. Shared facilitators included GT as a tool for definitive diagnosis and identifying family risk, lifestyle motivation, and justification for pursuing medical therapy for their high cholesterol; yet, uptake was undermined by several psychosocial and systemic barriers. Prevalent in both groups was skepticism about the added clinical value of results—especially among individuals already on statins or asymptomatic. Untested participants reported additional distinct concerns related to potential harms (i.e., psychological toll of knowing, social stigma and discrimination, institutional discrimination relating to employment and insurance, and limited information on the testing process and data privacy. Participants who underwent testing reported overall positive service experiences but noted challenges within pre-test procedures (documenting family history, inefficient appointment systems), and post-testing standard of care (long wait for results, heavily technical test result reports).Conclusions and relevanceEven with cost coverage, psychosocial and system-level barriers limit the uptake of FH genetic testing. To realize the full potential of genetic screening in cardiovascular care, implementation strategies must go beyond cost elimination to include patient education, structured care pathways, and sustained provider support. These findings have implications for integrating precision medicine into routine cardiovascular prevention globally.

Introduction

Financial barriers continue to limit access to optimized genetic testing pathways (GT), prompting several countries to introduce subsidies and to integrate screening services into primary care to improve uptake [1, 2].

Affecting approximately 1 in 313 individuals globally, FH is the most common genetic cause of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [3, 4]. Individuals with FH are at increased risk of premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, including coronary artery disease and, to a lesser extent, ischemic stroke, due to lifelong elevated LDL cholesterol. Clinical diagnosis is generally based on family history, and LDL cholesterol levels, although genetic testing is the only effective method to identify pathogenic variants in APOB, LDLR, PCSK9, and, more rarely, LDLRAP1 genes [5–9]. Management differs from multifactorial hypercholesterolemia: people with FH often require earlier initiation of statins, additional lipid-lowering therapies, and cascade testing of relatives to reduce lifetime cardiovascular risk. GT for FH is highly accurate, cost-effective, and enables cascade testing of close relatives, conferring broader preventative benefits [10].

Cost-free and well-structured national programs have been shown to increase screening uptake at the population level, as seen in Norway and Northern Ireland [11]. In Singapore, healthcare costs are partially offset by Medisave scheme, a national medical saving system that reduced out-of-pocket payment for eligible healthcare and preventative services. Building on Medisave, recent national initiatives emphasise preventative and precision medicine at population level. Healthier SG, launched in 2023, strengthens preventative care and chronic disease management in primary care. The National Familial Hypercholesterolaemia (FH) Genetic Testing Programme, launched in 2025 provides subsidized genetic testing and counselling for FH to improve early diagnosis and premature heart disease amongst at-risk. Under this program, Singapore Citizens and Permanent Residents pay anywhere between $18-$87 for FH GT and associated genetic counselling services compared to $764 without subsidy [12].

By introducing subsidies as part of National FH Testing Program, Singapore aims to bolster accessibility within the public healthcare system, and support timely identification, surveillance, and management of FH.

Even when low cost, and/or cost-free testing is offered to individuals suspected of FH, uptake remains low globally, indicating that financial support alone may not be sufficient to promoting testing [13]. Prior studies in FH and hereditary cancers have identified key barriers—such as negative perceptions of testing, lack of governmental protections from genetic discrimination, insufficient or limited access to information, and low confidence in clinical care—as determinants of testing intentions [14, 15]. These findings underscore a real gap in understanding determinants beyond cost in the optimization of GT services, specifically in the context of FH.

Recognizing this gap, especially at this critical juncture of National FH testing program in Singapore, we conducted a qualitative study aimed at examining the unique facilitators and barriers affecting individual decision-making on FH GT in individuals making the decision to undergo FH genetic testing. We explored their experiences and perceptions related to FH, their views on FH-specific genetic testing, and their perspectives on peri- and post-testing services provided. Participants included those who accepted, declined, or remained undecided about FH testing, all of whom met the criteria for a clinical FH diagnosis.

As one of the first few studies to focus on FH GT specifically, this work provides recommendations for interventions that can inform the optimisation of Singapore’s subsidised FH GT pathway and offer guidance to other health systems implementing similar cost-sensitive FH genetic testing pathways.

Methods

Study setting and participants

This study is based in Singapore, a multiethnic city-state with a well-developed public healthcare system and expanding national initiatives in precision medicine. During the study period, FH genetic testing was accessed through multiple pathways, including public-sector pilot programs and private care. As a result, subsidized testing was not uniform: some participants received no-cost testing, whereas others incurred out-of-pocket expenses.

Participants were recruited from both tertiary care and community settings to participate in online, semi-structured interviews examining experiences related to decision-making about genetic testing for FH. Eligible participants were English-speaking adults aged 21 to 99 years who met at least one of the following criteria: (1) clinical diagnosis or suspected diagnosis of FH defined as meeting criteria for possible FH as per Dutch Lipid Clinic Network Criteria for FH (REF) [12]; (2) prior FH genetic testing, regardless of results; (3) a family member who had undergone FH genetic testing, regardless of results; or (4) referral for FH genetic testing following clinical assessment in primary or tertiary care setting but who had not pursued testing, including those who were undecided or had declined testing. Those recruited from tertiary care settings were referred from monthly outpatient endocrinology clinics specializing in lipid disorders at Alexandra Hospital, National University Hospital, and Khoo Teck Puat Hospital, Singapore. Clinic participants were assessed using the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network Criteria for FH (REF) [12]. Individuals with scores ≥ 3, classified as possible FH (3–5), probable FH (6–8), or definite FH (> 8) were counselled by their attending Specialist on the possible diagnosis and FG genetic testing [16]. They were then referred for genetic testing as part of a clinical implementation pilot program. Those attending the clinical implementation program received an in-person pre-test genetic counselling session from trained FH Clinical Research coordinators, who delivered standardized information regarding FH genetic testing. Post-test counselling of genetic results and follow up care plan was completed by research coordinators and specialists trained in offering patients testing-related counselling.

Community participants recruited via a public online forum (‘Telegram’) met the same eligibility criteria as clinical participants and were receiving care for elevated cholesterol/suspected FH in primary or tertiary care settings. However they were not necessarily enrolled in specialist care or the clinical implementation trial that provided subsidized genetic testing and the standardized pretest genetic counselling. While these community participants received information about FH and genetic testing from their care teams, they did not have access the formal counselling sessions provided to those recruited from the clinical implamentation pilot. This recruitment strategy aimed to capture diverse experiences across individuals with FH or suspected FH, including those who pursued genetic testing, those who declined testing or remained undecided, and additionally, those who lacked access to standardized genetic counselling, to better understand barriers and facilitators related to genetic testing decision-making for FH.

The target sample size was approximately 20 to 30 participants, based on purposive sampling and the principle of thematic saturation (i.e., the point at which no new themes emerge). Costs for FH GT during the study window were fully subsidised as part of clinical implementation pilot (2022–2024) prior to launch of National FH Genetic Testing program (see acknowledgement).

Study design

A semi-structured interview guide suitable for both one-on-one and small focus groups (< 4 participants) was developed based on relevant empirical evidence including a systematic review [14] and studies in Singapore on genetic/cascade testing for hereditary cancer [17, 18]. The interview guide was piloted with members of the public (N = 5 community members) for clarity and relevance. The final interview guide focused on beliefs about FH and high cholesterol, attitudes toward FH genetic testing and screening, decision making processes, and preferences for care pathways. Participants who had undergone FH GT were asked additional questions on their experiences with GT services (pre-, during and post-test) offered by the national healthcare system. Untested individuals were not separately asked additional questions as the general interview topics were deemed to adequately capture considerations relevant to decision-making for FH GT. Participants were given the option to participate in either individual or small group interviews (on-site or online) but all opted for individual online interviews.

Study implementation

Interviews were scheduled at the participants’ convenience. Following written informed consent, interviews were conducted via a secure online videoconferencing platform (Zoom), lasted between 30 and 60 min, and were carried out in English. Upon completion of the interview, participants received a reimbursement of 30 Singapore Dollars (SGD). All interviews were video- and audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim removing any personal identifiers. Participants were categorized into two groups for analysis: (1) individuals who have undergone genetic testing for FH (i.e., “tested”), and (2) individuals (suspected or referred for FH or with family member(s) tested) who have not been tested for FH (i.e., “untested”).

Data analysis

Data analysis adopted the Braun and Clarke’s framework for thematic analysis [19, 20]. All transcripts underwent a multi-stage coding process conducted by four independent coders (CL, GA, PL, FC). The process involved familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, identification and refinement of themes, and development of thematic descriptions. An inductive coding approach was applied. Coders independently developed codes from transcripts to determine major themes across both tested and untested groups. Discrepancies (if any) were reconciled through discussions under the supervision of KG. Initial codes were organized into subthemes, which were further categorized into overarching themes. The process was iterative, with regular review of codes, subthemes, and themes against the original recordings to ensure clarity and relevance in line with Braun and Clarke’s (2023) post-positivist paradigm.

Separate codebooks were generated for “tested” and “untested” groups and examined for convergence and divergence of themes. Common themes across groups were merged, while themes unique to specific subgroups were retained and reported separately. All decisions were made through interpretive discussion and any remaining discrepancies were resolved collaboratively by CL and KG to ensure subgroup-specific experiences were accurately represented in the data.

Study ethics

The study was approved by the Domain Specific Review Board (DSRB), under the F2 Domain (DSRB 2023/00983). This is the designated ethics committee for medical and clinical research in Singapore, as participants were recruited from records at general hospitals offering familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) testing. Approval from the NTU Institutional Review Board (IRB) was also obtained to include participants recruited from the community. Research integrity was maintained by ensuring that all clinical collaborators were only involved in the initial referral process of potential participants, and all identifiable information by participants were anonymized.

Patient and public involvement

Team members from Familial Hypercholesteremia Europe (MD, EE, EP), served as community advisors and offered critical input on all aspects of study including data interpretation and dissemination.

Barriers and facilitators of decision to test or not

Analyses indicated convergence between the tested and untested participants with regards to value/benefit of testing for self especially when not already under specialist FH care. They also had shared concerns: scepticism about the value of testing when one perceived themselves as healthy and symptom-free (i.e., lack of cardiovascular adverse events and/or stroke onset) or those already on medical treatment for hyperlipidaemia. Untested participants expressed additional distinct concerns about the potential harms related to GT – not shared by those who opted to test (see Fig. 1; Tables 2 and 3).

Barriers

Facilitators

Participants in both groups identified several facilitators that could encourage uptake of GT for FH. These facilitators were largely about the perceived benefits of GT especially when not already on statin treatment and included the following subthemes: GT for definitive FH diagnosis; motivation to maintain a healthy lifestyle; GT to justify need for medication, and GT offering family value.

Tested participants emphasized that GT could provide a definitive diagnosis and clarify a genetic root cause for having elevated cholesterol levels: “I think getting genetic testing is helpful because I think I can get the formal diagnosis whether I have FH or not (FH09, tested).”.

Participants also discussed the positive impact of FH GT on their motivation to improve their lifestyle and health-conscious decisions: “Yeah, we got cut down on the fried food. Then like do more exercise. So like follow like more healthy lifestyle… After I got diagnosed this thing, I make some lifestyle changes (FH09, tested).” Strikingly, even when results were negative, participants remained adherent to a health-conscious lifestyle to address any modifiable health risks. Tested negative for FH, another individual (FH12) reflected: “So for me, I still take note of the FH. So I will choose my food choices more carefully”. Respondents also noted that GT offered clinical justification for need for pharmacological intervention, particularly in young adults who might otherwise be hesitant to initiate statin therapy: “After knowing that, oh, it’s actually genetics, then you start on medication because before that you always be thinking, yeah, I’m a bit too young to be eating statins for the rest of your life. So you always try to do other changes like exercise or dieting to try to not be on medication. But after knowing that, oh, OK, so it’s actually genetic, then you have to be on medication to keep it under control. So I think that’s the most helpful part (FH03, tested).”.

Participants also discussed how GT informs familial risk and planning, particularly in understanding the hereditary nature of FH, leveraging the test as a tool for risk assessment and timely treatment for relatives: “I wanted to know if it was a problem that would be hereditary because that would allow me to help the people around me (FH11, tested).” This also involved planning testing for their children: “I also wanted to know if I have a gene such that in the future, when the kids are a little older, I could also possibly send them for testing just to find out if it was something that is genetic (FH14, tested).”

Barriers to GT

Both tested and untested subgroups identified a range of psychological and perceptual barriers that influenced their decision-making regarding GT for FH.

Skepticism about the value of FH GT

Both tested and untested groups expressed skepticism regarding the additional value of undergoing a genetic test to confirm their FH status. This was particularly evident among individuals who were asymptomatic, already on lipid-lowering therapy, or adhering to a healthy lifestyle. Participants were not convinced that obtaining genetic confirmation of FH would offer any meaningful benefit as it did not alter clinical management for high cholesterol nor change health outcomes for those already taking good care of themselves.

Particularly, individuals who perceived themselves as healthy, lacking related cardiovascular and/or stroke symptoms, felt no urgency or necessity to confirm their FH status. A participant in the tested group (FH03) noted, “But if you look at it now, like right now, it feels like there is nothing wrong with me, I’m very healthy, I’m young.” A participant from the untested group (FH17) added: “We don’t really see the need in like going for any more checkups, like doctor checkups or testing or finding other solutions through the Western method if it’s not causing any symptomatic ailment.”

For participants already on treatment, such as statins or regular lipid monitoring, GT did not confer additional value. Given that they were already receiving medical therapy for high cholesterol, GT was unnecessary as it would not impact or alter current treatment. A tested participant (FH14) shared the reason why her relative was unwilling to go for testing: “And then because she’s also on medication, she goes for the six-monthly checkups similar to me at a polyclinic as well. So to her, she didn’t feel that there’s any benefit in testing to find out whether she had the gene or not.”

Potential harms of testing

Concerns about potential harms of genetic testing – which emerge in other context of hereditary conditions for GT—were voiced exclusively by untested participants and included: potential discrimination, social stigma, and the psychological toll of knowing. A prevailing concern was fear of discrimination—particularly in employment, education, and insurance. Several participants worried that a confirmed FH diagnosis could trigger institutional scrutiny or negatively impact future opportunities (i.e., education, employment). “So I think nowadays on one hand I’m also a bit paranoid to find out…if let’s say they find out, then this will affect employment, this will affect my student status. And then it will always lead to further inquiry like maybe the school or the company will ask, oh, so you have FH, is this something, is this a health concern? And then it will affect my standing and my status also (FH22, untested).”

Insurance-related concerns were also prevalent. Participants feared being denied coverage or incurring higher premiums due to their genetic status: “I think pre-existing conditions…you can’t buy insurance anymore, or like they’ll increase your coverage or something (FH23, untested).”

Beyond institutional consequences, participants discussed social and emotional burdens. Participants anticipated social stigma and discomfort in social settings related to lifestyle modification associated with FH. Dietary restriction was seen as potentially alienating in social settings, burdening others or result in being singled out. “I guess there is a social aspect to it in a sense. And because you go and eat as a group, then like being someone who has some limitations is not as nice… because the group has to cater to that person or, you know, and sometimes there’s just some jokes made (FH18, untested).” For many of the untested participants, however, the most powerful deterrent to getting tested was the anticipated psychological toll from test results. Participants feared that a confirmed diagnosis would introduce anxiety and a sense of inevitability, especially given the need for lifelong lifestyle changes. Rather than feeling empowered by knowledge, some described the possibility of becoming consumed by it: “You don’t know rather than you know. After that (GT) you may suffer mental issues because you already know and then you keep remembering it and then it becomes a mental issue. So some people choose not to know because by knowing it, another set of issues will come out because you start to worry (FH04, untested).”

Participants described how the psychological toll could extend into broader life choices. One participant noted that knowing their FH status would restrict their lifestyle and severely limit their pursuit of personal or professional goals, rendering them unable to live freely and fully: “I’m not sure if knowing that status currently adds to my life, how does it add value to me? Because it might add an additional level of stress. And then it just makes me like more restrictive in my diet, or more cautious in making life decisions like taking risks. Like do I want to work harder for that in that sense? Do I want to take up another side hustle or project in that sense? Like I’m not sure if knowing this status is beneficial in a sense (FH18, untested).”

Others articulated a cascade of worry and behavioural restrictions, where a genetic diagnosis could trigger hypervigilance or feelings of abnormality: “And it kind of creates this hypochondriac kind of impact where like the person starts to think, oh, I totally cannot do this right now. Like if my alcohol… enzyme…LDH something, if it’s way too low…I cannot hang out with my friends anymore when they go for drinks. It kind of creates that kind of psychological impact (FH17, untested).”

Limited information

A critical barrier to GT noted only amongst untested individuals was the absence of clear, publicly available information regarding the FH testing services. Many participants expressed a lack of clarity surrounding testing logistics, including where it could be done, the available clinical pathways (i.e. specialist service, or obtaining referrals from primary care) and the associated costs: “I’m not sure where it can be done and how much it can cost. Like for my friend, she did it at her private specialist doctor. So I’m not sure if you need a specialist doctor or you can just go get tested at any polyclinic, or any GP clinic or that you need a special request, meaning…you need to go through a specialist…to get tested for it…Yeah, I’m not sure what is the pathway to get tested (FH18, untested).”

Untested individuals also noted having no information on the potential benefits and risks of GT. As such, many voiced concerns about not knowing the associated benefits and risks, underscoring the need for more accessible and comprehensive information to encouraged informed decision making: “Like are there any side effects or risks associated with the test? How will the test benefit me or like what can I get from this test?… I’m saying…what kind of information can I get from this test? Are there any risks or side effects that that are involved in undertaking this test? How long does it take?” (FH23, untested).

Concerns about data management

Participants were concerned about the long-term confidentiality of their genetic information and were unsure about how their test results would be stored and shared. Without clear information on data privacy and protection, stories of past data breaches intensified apprehension. One untested participant (FH07) commented: “But yeah, general thing is how private will the information be? Because I don’t know about now, but I think years ago there were concerns that some of this kind of information, although they say it won’t be shared, but it ultimately was shared with like insurers and these kinds of parties. So yeah, you know, not too comfortable with that kind of thing happening.”

Closely linked to data privacy were participants’ uncertainty about the confidentiality of their test results, and whether they were required to disclose them during insurance applications. Individuals expressed a prevailing moral dilemma between knowing their positive results, but not knowing if they may confidently withhold this information from insurance providers. As one participant who had undergone testing (FH14) explained: “But like now if I have to apply for an insurance application, for example, and then there are questions that ask you about, you know, your health, right? And then you’re like, OK, it’s anonymous. So do I put no or do I put yes? And like, you know, and I start having those moral questions because ... those like, do you have any conditions?… And like technically I do, but like technically it’s anonymous. And then you have that moral dilemma. Should I put yes or no? Because, you know, putting yes, obviously would preclude you to certain benefits of the insurance, but putting no sort of seems like lying.”

Pre-testing

Participants described several challenges they encountered before undergoing GT. One recurring issue was the inconvenient locations of testing centres. “I think the location plays a part as they might be from neighborhood polyclinic and then they are thrown to a different part which is further away from them. So they might not be able to show up (FH01, tested).”

Many individuals also felt unprepared for the extensive pre-testing consultation, particularly with respect to detailed family history documentation. One participant (FH11, tested) recalled: “The only thing was the drawing of the family tree part was rather long and arduous and I didn’t have all the information, so I also could not provide a very accurate picture.” Individuals also reported that it was difficult to complete the family tree during the pre-testing consultation: “The checking of family history, that one because it was done in same, after the same consultation. So of course, I didn’t know everything about my other family members, including all the external family members, so it was quite difficult to fill out (FH02, tested).”

In addition, participants expressed their frustration with the inefficient appointment system. The processes of obtaining subsidies and scheduling appointments were described as cumbersome and time-consuming. “They told me if I go to see the specialist doctor, it will charge me private prices, then I say ‘oh, then how to get the subsidies?’ Then they asked me to go back to the polyclinic to see the doctor. Then after the doctor write referral letter, then I can get the things subsidized. Think it took like around one month to get the appointment with the specialist? Around one month (FH09, tested).” Participants who were referred to specialists for FH genetic testing reported positive experiences with the pre-test consultation process. They described the information provided as clear, comprehensive, and reassuring—instilling confidence in their decision to proceed with testing. As one participant shared: “My case, cos they refer me to specialists who knew a lot about the condition and they were able to explain to me quite thoroughly what it was about, what all can be transmitted, what are the risks and also how it affects your cholesterol levels in general. I think, yeah, having specialists who had the time to sit down and like thoroughly explain what the condition was about was quite helpful (FH08, tested).”

During testing

In contrast to pre-testing, participants generally had a rather straightforward and hassle-free testing experience, revealing that the test itself was the most seamless part of the process. Participants articulated consistently that the test is a simple blood test: “It’s very straightforward, just blood test and that’s it (FH03, tested).”

Post-testing

Many participants who had undergone GT expressed dissatisfaction with the quality of post-testing support.

They noted three key challenges in the post-testing process: prolonged delays in receiving results, test reports that were overly technical and difficult to interpret, and the absence of tailored post-test counselling to guide next steps in health management.

Testing was easy but getting the test results entailed long waiting times. As one simply noted, “The result took too long” (FH13, tested).

Participants also described post-test reports as overly technical and inaccessible for a lay person. They highlighted the lack of clarity in the reporting format, the use of dense medical terminology without adequate explanations that made comprehension challenging and created uncertainty about next steps.

One participant explained. “The report they gave me was very complicated and full of technical jargon. It wasn’t very useful for the average person—it looked like a medical report, with terms like polymerase chain reaction. Without proper explanation, it’s very easy to be confused about what the result actually means (FH08, tested).

Another (FH12, tested) added the lack of in-depth explanations in interpreting results for broader lifestyle and treatment decisions: “Actually the genetic testing is just a simple testing that I signed the consent. After that it’s just yes or no, yes, you have, no, you don’t have. But they don’t go in depth because mine was just a research study and they only can give incidental finding of my blood test result”.

Discussion

While the clinical utility of GT for FH has been well documented [21] and national initiatives and subsidies have improved accessibility, real-world uptake is approximated to be modest, with screening being performed only on a minority of patients with clinically suspected FH. Available data suggest that fewer than 10% and (as little as 1%) underwent GT for FH in the United States as of 2014 [22]. The lack of more recent and comprehensive data measuring real-world uptake of FH GT a indicates the lack of empirically-driven national initiatives targeting preventative approaches to FH. Our study reveals that preventative health measures for FH GT requires that health systems prioritise patient-centered advocacy to support patient understanding of the consequences of living with FH. While many studies in the context of other hereditary conditions have outlined barriers that speak to the complexity of decision-making for genetic testing, our qualitative study explored the unique barriers that come with deciding to undergo a test specifically for FH. By comparing and integrating perspectives between individuals who had undergone FH genetic testing and those who had declined or remained undecided, this study examines different perceptions that individuals at risk for FH have about the necessity of a genetic test alongside the psychological, interpersonal, and institutional factors that shape testing decisions. Despite the availability of subsidised clinical testing pathways, our study revealed an overarching ambivalence –defined as the absence of a strong compulsion– to obtain a genetic test to confirm FH, regardless of whether individuals ultimately decided to test. Overall, perceptions about the lack of value and necessity to get an FH genetic test was noted, and complex perceptual factors affected confidence in FH GT. Particularly, both tested and untested participants expressed sentiments about high cholesterol as a common and less serious condition (i.e., compared to other genetic conditions such as hereditary cancer) that can be managed if healthy, and perceived no added value in getting genetic confirmation if already receiving statin therapy. With a lack of knowledge about the consequences of FH, individuals strongly articulated psychological, social, and systemic friction points that reveal that individuals perceived greater costs to testing than benefits.

Consistent with previous FH studies [14, 23] perceived value emerged as a strong facilitator for testing decisions. Participants across both tested and untested groups valued GT for its diagnostic accuracy, its potential to motivate lifestyle changes, and inform the initiation of pharmacological treatment decisions—particularly among younger adults hesitant about long-term statin use, a finding that is underexplored in prior studies. For these individuals, GT justified statin treatment necessity and increased their willingness to start a medication regimen. Even when tested negative, receiving test results encouraged personal responsibility and sustained engagement in health-promoting behaviours. Notably, this reveals that knowledge of one’s genetic aetiology motivates behavioural change targeting exercise and diet to reduce modifiable cardiovascular risk, suggesting that the value of obtaining results should be emphasized not just in relation to managing treatment plans, but additionally in relation to patient autonomy in managing their own health goals despite the nature of one’s FH test results. Participants also highlighted the familial implications of testing, describing GT as a means of safeguarding family members’ health, reflecting that knowledge on FH status can empower family planning. The relatively young mean age of participants (34 years) reflects individuals at different stages of life who perceived FH GT as offering wider protective benefits for family planning, indicating that FH GT may have broader acceptability and uptake when framed within a wider preventative health context [14, 24, 25].

However, not all participants viewed FH GT as offering clinical value. Both tested and untested individuals expressed skepticism toward GT particularly in the absence of other associated cardiovascular symptoms or when already on lipid-lowering therapy. In such cases, GT was often seen as redundant or unlikely to influence management and care plans, revealing a disconnect between the biomedical conception of the risks conferred by FH mutations and widespread conceptions of FH as a non-urgent medical issue. For these individuals, “feeling healthy” or “already being treated” diminished the perceived necessity for genetic confirmation [24, 26–30]. Importantly, this skepticism persisted even after testing, suggesting a need for better education on the health risks of FH. Communication strategies must explicitly outline how GT adds value to standard care (routine lipid profiling and pharmacological treatment) and offer insights on the additional benefits beyond treatment such as improved risk stratification and cascade testing for family members [21, 31–33].

GT may carry unintended and often overlooked psychosocial consequences. Among participants who had not undergone testing, concerns about potential psychological, social, and institutional harms emerged as major deterrents. Fears of discrimination—particularly in relation to insurance and employment—along with social stigma and anticipated psychological distress from receiving a genetic diagnosis were frequently cited, even in the context of financial subsidies. Fear of future insurability has been consistently reported as a barrier to GT across various hereditary conditions [14, 15, 34, 47]. In the context of FH GT participants described a moral dilemma and uncertainty about disclosure of FH genetic results to insurers reflecting limited public understanding or awareness about of how FH test results are treated in underwriting. Unlike hereditary cancer, for which insurance and employment protections are more widely recognised, FH occupies a more ambiguous position in policies and public awareness.

In Singapore the moratorium on FH genetic testing and insurance was amended in 2025 to limit the use of predictive genetic test results, including those for FH obtained through the National Program, in underwriting insurance policies [12]. The moratorium provides full protection for FH testing conducted within the designated framework. However, the moratorium does not prevent insurers from asking patients to disclose their FH status, raising questions about what constitutes adequate protection for individuals living with hereditary conditions. These gaps underscore the need for policy makers to provide clear updates on the protections offered to empower individuals accessing services such as genetic testing.

Others described the emotional burden of receiving a genetic diagnosis and worried about how it might affect their identity and daily life decisions. For some, not knowing their genetic status was an active choice to avoid anticipatory stress or perceived lifestyle restrictions. This “burden of knowing” highlights the dual nature of genetic information—empowering for some individuals, but distressing for others. These findings raise important questions about psychosocial readiness and societal infrastructure to support the broader implementation of precision medicine in Singapore and other settings. Addressing these concerns—particularly around genetic privacy, discrimination safeguards, and mental health support—will be essential to building public trust and ensuring ethical, equitable access to genomic care which is pivotal to the success of national-scale genomics efforts.

Gaps in information, particularly outside specialist centres, amplify concerns about the need for genetic testing. Many individuals referred for FH genetic testing reported uncertainty about logistics, risks, and the confidentiality of results. Low public awareness about FH and limited communication beyond specialist settings, especially in primary care settings where most referrals originate, were identified as key barriers. This underscores the need for broader public education to drive genetic literary measures at the frontline of healthcare services.

Targeted information should clearly communicate the purpose, process, and protections involved in genetic testing—especially in legal contexts where awareness and understanding of safeguards against genetic discrimination may be limited [34, 35]. Raising awareness and building public trust will require a combination of coherent national policies, transparent referral pathways, robust data protection frameworks [36], and dedicated educational interventions, particularly as health systems increasingly adopt routine genomic screening to advance population-level disease prevention.

Study findings offer several important insights for improving the delivery and clinical implementation of genetic testing for FH across specialist lipid and cardiovascular settings. First, there is a need to provide specialized training for primary and secondary care providers to ensure wider access to clear, evidence-based information about FH genetic testing across all settings, including offline informational resources and online chatbots to promote empowered patient engagement [37–41]. Clinical encounters should be streamlined for efficiency, with family history documentation conducted pre-consultation through online, readable family-tree templates, reducing patient burden during consultations. Consultations can then focus on targeted education to address knowledge gaps and patientconcerns whilst highlighting the clinical utility of FH genetic testing, even among individuals already receiving lipid-lowering therapy or symptom free [42, 43]. Providers should receive multidisciplinary training led by genetic counsellors, cardiovascular, and endocrine specialists, emphasizing the role of GT in improving diagnostic certainty, informing long-term pharmacologic decisions, and facilitating cascade testing among at-risk relatives to strengthen preventaive cardiovascular care [17, 44–48]. Finally, public awareness campaigns and expanding financial support are also crucial to improve access and uptake of testing across different socioeconomic groups.

Second, concerns about potential psychological, social, and insurance-related harms must be proactively addressed during pre-test counselling, using clear, empathetic communication about data privacy protections and the broader preventive value of testing for individuals and families. In Singapore, a growing program links clinical referrals for FH genetic testing with dedicated genetic counsellors and genetic counselling assistants, who provide structured pre-and post-test support to address these complex concerns pertaining to FH GT. The genetic counselling program follows a standardized protocol to address patient concerns and also additionally guides probands who test positive to invite their family members for cascade testing via a forwarding form, protecting patient privacy and ensuring efficient scaling of preventative care. As the program expands, pre and post testing counselling should be evaluated for efficacy to further strengthen communication and improve patient experiences within the subsidized FH GT pathway. Third, logistical barriers—such as limited testing sites, unclear procedures, and inaccessible result formats—should be mitigated through more integrated, patient-centered service models, potentially involving primary care or digital health platforms.

Finally, broader public awareness campaigns and policy measures, including anti-discrimination legislation and clearer protections for genetic information, will be critical to fostering public trust and supporting equitable access to genomic medicine. Policy makers and healthcare systems should collaborate to create accessible, publicly available information about protections in place against discrimination whilst clarifying the circumstances under which genetic test results should be disclosed to insurance providers.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Participants were drawn from a single national context with a sample disproportionately comprising Chinese, well-educated, and single individuals, which may limit the generalisability to other health care systems with different demographics and health infrastructures. Social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses, particularly among those who opted for FH testing. Recruitment took place during the early implementation phase (clinical pilot), when access pathways and subsidy coverage for FH genetic testing varied across settings; while this may limit uniform interpretation of cost-related experiences, it also provides insight into real-world decision-making during the program rollout. While genetic testing studies have often considered genetic and cascade testing in tandem, our work focusses solely on factors impacting individual decision-making to know their own genetic risk, limiting transferability to cascade testing interventions. These personal decisions however have implications for cascade testing when understanding reasons individuals delay or decline testing, resulting in missed opportunities for cascade screening and early intervention among family members. Future research using qualitative and quantitative approaches with larger and more diverse populations is needed to validate and extend our study findings and to inform scalable interventions. Nonetheless, the purposive sampling and inclusion of both tested and untested individuals from two diverse settings, namely, Singapore’s multi-ethnic population and publicly funded GT programs, support the credibility of our findings, and provide transferrable insights for healthcare systems adopting genetic testing into a broader cardiovascular prevention paradigm.
