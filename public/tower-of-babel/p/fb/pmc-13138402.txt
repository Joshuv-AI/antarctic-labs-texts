Persistent Gender Differences in Medicare Practice and Payments to Orthopedic Surgeons
Authors: Pooja Dhupati, Megan L. Anderson, Joyce Zhuang, Jasmine K.M. Lopez, Sunny H. Vansdadia, Conrad Li, Brooke Halpin, Lola B. Chambless, Shelley S. Noland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Gender-based reimbursement disparities in orthopedic surgery remain poorly understood, particularly when accounting for practice composition and setting. The purpose of this study was to compare and evaluate gender-based differences in Medicare reimbursement and practice patterns among female and male orthopedic surgeons.Methods:A cross sectional analysis of public Medicare data from 2013 to 2021 was performed. Outcomes included annual Medicare payments, submitted charges, total services, service categories, and beneficiaries treated by orthopedic surgeons who bill Medicare. Univariate and multivariable analyses quantified differences in payment, practice volume and composition, geographic adjustment, and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT)-matched reimbursements.Results:A total of 29,357 orthopedic surgeons (27,442 men and 1,919 women) billed Medicare fee-for-service during the study period. Female surgeons, on average, submitted 48.3% fewer total charges and received 44.1% lower annual Medicare payments per surgeon compared with male surgeons. These differences persisted after multivariable regression adjusted for practice metrics and excluded top and bottom earners. Female surgeons also billed fewer services, treated fewer beneficiaries, and submitted fewer unique CPT codes. However, payment disparities persisted even after matching surgeons by volume of services, beneficiaries, and years in practice. Male surgeons performed higher volumes of all 10 highest-paying orthopedic CPT codes. In CPT matched analyses, female surgeons received lower reimbursement per service than male surgeons performing identical procedures.Conclusion:These findings suggest that the observed gender-based disparities in orthopedic surgery Medicare reimbursement cannot be explained by differences in volume, procedural mix, or geographic practice cost. Instead, they likely reflect a combination of structural and behavioral mechanisms described in prior literature that may influence how access to clinical opportunities, referrals, and reimbursement practices unfold in surgical practices. Variations in coding practices may represent an important focus for future investigation and targeted educational initiatives.Level of Evidence:Level III, Retrospective Cohort Study. Introduction

Gender diversity has increased across most medical and surgical fields,1 yet orthopedic surgery remains among the least gender diverse specialties, with women comprising only 7.4% of the workforce2,3. Women are also underrepresented in leadership roles3-5 and are significantly more likely to leave the field earlier in their careers6,7. In a national analysis of Medicare-enrolled orthopedic surgeons, women had nearly threefold higher odds of early-career attrition within the first 10 years of practice compared with men.7

Multiple studies have demonstrated that female orthopedic surgeons bill fewer services, submit fewer unique Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes, and receive lower total Medicare payments compared with male surgeons8-13. However, most existing analyses have been limited to narrow procedure groups and have not accounted for practice setting or geographic payment variation.14-16

Medicare claims represent a substantial portion of orthopedic practice for roughly three-quarters of US orthopedic surgeons billing Medicare17. Medicare data therefore provide a meaningful and detailed source for examining gender-related differences in reimbursement across diverse practice settings for orthopedic surgeons who bill Medicare. Research in neurosurgery and other surgical subspecialties have demonstrated that gender-based disparities in Medicare billing and payments persist even after adjusting for case volume, experience, geographic cost variation, and when comparing equivalent CPT codes18,19. A comprehensive assessment that considers service-level reimbursement, geographic adjustments, and practice setting is needed to clarify the drivers of persistent gender-based payment differences.

The purpose of this study was to compare practice metrics and Medicare reimbursement between female and male orthopedic surgeons and to explore factors associated with gender-based differences in reimbursement. It was hypothesized that female orthopedic surgeons would receive lower overall Medicare payments and submit fewer high-value procedures than their male colleagues, even after accounting for practice volume, years in practice, and service mix.

Materials and Methods

Data

This cross-sectional study analyzed publicly available Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Medicare Fee-for-Service (FFS) Provider Utilization and Payment Data from 2013 to 2021.20,21 Medicare Advantage, bundled-payment models, and non-FFS encounter data are not included. Provider Summary files, organized by National Provider Identifier (NPI), included surgeon demographics, service counts, charges, payments and practice setting. Additional files summarized services by CPT codes and locality. Years in practice were derived from graduation year20. This study did not require Institutional Review Board review.

Study Cohort

All orthopedic surgeons submitting ≥11 annual Medicare FFS services during the study period were included. Surgeon gender was based on self-reporting. The CMS “Hand Surgery” designation includes surgeons from multiple specialties22. Providers in this category were cross-referenced with NPI taxonomy codes to confirm orthopedic training20. Information about other orthopedic subspecialties were not available. The data set used for CPT level analysis reports data for services for which an individual provider submitted at least 11 identically coded services in a given calendar year21.

Variables of Interest

Primary outcomes were annual total submitted charges, total payments, mean charge per service, and mean payment per service, adjusted to 2021 dollar values using the Consumer Price Index23. Geographic Practice Cost Indices (GPCIs) were obtained from the 2021 CMS Physician Fee Schedule24. Additional variables included number of services, unique CPT codes, beneficiaries, and place of service (as defined by CMS to include office-based and freestanding sites of care)25.

Statistical Analysis

Group differences were compared using t-tests, chi-square tests, or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests where appropriate. Linear regression evaluated time trends, and multivariable regression modeled associations between gender and payments while adjusting for practice volume and years in practice. Sensitivity analyses excluded the top and bottom 2.5% of earners18 and analyzed a cohort matched by years in practice, beneficiaries, and services. CPT codes were grouped by the Restructured Berenson-Eggers Type of Service Classification System26 to compare service composition. Charges and payments were evaluated for the top 20 most common CPT codes and stratified by facility vs nonfacility setting. Analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Orthopedic Surgeon Cohort Receiving CMS Payments Between 2013 and 2021

A total of 29,357 orthopedic surgeons were included, of whom 27,442 (94.3%) were men and 1,919 (5.7%) were women. Annual female surgeon participation increased over 40%, from 1,068 in 2013 to 1,499 in 2021. Female orthopedic surgeons were more than twice as likely as male surgeons to be categorized as hand surgeons (10.7 vs. 5.0%) and had a more recent median graduation year (2004 vs. 1997).

Differences in Charges, Payment, and Practice Volume

Female surgeons submitted 49.4% fewer annual charges compared with male surgeons (mean [SE], $386,389.34 [$3,684.22] vs $770,592.17 [$2,043.06]; p < 0.001) and received 45.5% less in annual payments (mean [SE], $87,054.36 [$775.94] vs. $159,848.86 [$331.61]; p < 0.001) (Figs. 1-A and 1-B). These differences persisted after excluding top and bottom 2.5% earners (Figs. 1-C and 1-D).

Overall Medicare charges and payments by gender.

Female surgeons also billed fewer services (682 [interquartile range (IQR), 254-1,536] vs. 1,401 [IQR, 581-2,776]; p < 0.001), treated fewer beneficiaries (177 [IQR, 86-298] vs. 290 [IQR, 157-454]; p < 0.001), and submitted fewer unique CPT codes (56 [IQR, 33-80] vs. 68 [IQR, 44-94]; p < 0.001) (Table I; eFigure 1 in Supplement). The mean charge per service did not differ significantly ($584.27 vs $594.34; p = 0.20), whereas the mean payment per service was slightly higher for female surgeons ($112.10 vs. $109.41; p = 0.001) (Table I).

CPT codes were grouped into reimbursement quartiles using national mean Medicare-allowed amounts (Table III). Male surgeons submitted a greater proportion of services in both the highest (2.22% vs. 1.85%; p < 0.001) and lowest quartiles (46.58% vs. 41.29%; p < 0.001) (Table III). Distributions across facility and nonfacility settings were similar between genders.

Male surgeons performed more services categorized as imaging (31.76% vs. 25.55%; p < 0.001) and procedures (27.31% vs. 25.11%; p < 0.001), whereas female surgeons billed a larger share of evaluation and management (E&M) services (33.39% vs 25.57%; p < 0.001) (Table III). The 20 most common procedural services were identified, and their corresponding Medicare national allowed payments were compared in facility and nonfacility settings (eTable 2 in Supplement).

Female surgeons received lower total reimbursement for these procedures in both facility ($7,668.07 vs. $8,247.51) and nonfacility settings ($4,725.02 vs. $6,274.04). After adjusting for service volume, payments remained lower for female surgeons in both facility ($3,291 vs. $3,495 per 1% of volume) and nonfacility settings ($533 vs. $787 per 1%) (eTable 2 in Supplement).

Male surgeons performed higher volumes of all 5 most highly reimbursed orthopedic procedures (eFigure 3 in Supplement), including total knee arthroplasty (CPT 27447; $1,191.90), endoscopic rotator cuff repair (CPT 29827; $1,135.06), hip arthroplasty (CPT 27130; $1,112.55), femoral fracture repair (CPT 27245; $1,003.01), and shoulder prosthesis repair (CPT 23472; $980.34) (eTable 2 in Supplement).

Matched CPT Analysis

The detailed data set used for CPT level analysis reports data for services for which an individual provider submitted at least 11 identically coded services in a given calendar year. All procedural services were matched by CPT code. For identical codes, female surgeons submitted lower charges (−$1,101.06; p < 0.001) and received lower payments per service (−$155.71; p < 0.001) (Table IV). Among the 10 CPT codes with the largest payment discrepancies, female surgeons were reimbursed at 20.8%- 70.3% of the amounts received by male surgeons (Table IV). These differences persisted when restricted to 2021 payments only (-$134.52; p < 0.001). While geographic distribution varied between genders (eFigure 4, eTable 3 in Supplement), mean GPCI values were nearly identical (1.024 vs. 1.022). Even after removing geographic adjustments entirely, female surgeons received significantly lower standardized Medicare payments ($86,983.61 [$773.82] vs. $161, 299 [$330.92]; p < 0.001) (Table I).

Discussion

This national analysis demonstrates that across nearly a decade, female orthopedic surgeons treated fewer beneficiaries, submitted fewer services, billed fewer unique CPT codes, and received substantially lower annual Medicare payments compared with male orthopedic surgeons billing to Medicare. These differences persisted after multivariable adjustment and in matched cohorts. Female surgeons also received lower payments for identical CPT codes and performed a smaller share of the highest-reimbursed procedures. Together, these findings demonstrate persistent gender-based reimbursement disparities among orthopedic surgeons within this data set.

These results contrast with findings by Lutnick et al., who reported gender parity on a per-service basis and interpreted this as evidence of equity13. Their analysis relied on aggregated provider-level averages across heterogeneous CPT codes, which can obscure meaningful differences in procedure composition. For example, HCPCS code G0283 (unattended electrical stimulation, $10.12) accounted for 2% of the top 10 services billed by male orthopedic surgeons but did not appear in the top 10 for female surgeons. High-volume, low-reimbursement codes may dilute mean per-service payments and help explain the modest per-service differences observed in Table I.

Highly reimbursed procedures, including total knee arthroplasty16, rotator cuff repair, and hip arthroplasty14, comprised a larger share of male surgeons' procedural volume. Service composition also differed: E&M services represented a nearly 9% higher proportion of female orthopedic surgeons’ total billing, a pattern similarly observed among female neurosurgeons and other surgical subspecialties18,19. Since the extent of underrepresentation of women varies by subspecialty, subspecialty driven practice differences may contribute to these trends. The impact of this variable underrepresentation cannot be isolated in this data set.

Female orthopedic surgeons also had fewer unique Medicare beneficiaries compared with their male counterparts. This may relate to prior findings that female physicians tend to spend more time per encounter27-29 and are expected to address more psychosocial concerns and engage in more empathic communication30-32. It may also reflect referral patterns, as prior studies show that, on average, female surgeons receive fewer new patient referrals, perform lower-complexity cases, and are less likely to be referred by male physicians despite comparable training and availability31-33. These factors suggest that systemic underemployment may limit procedural opportunity30,33,34. Recent evidence further demonstrates that female surgeons spend significantly more time in the electronic health record, receive higher message volumes, and log more after-hours documentation time than male surgeons, even after adjusting for workload, underscoring differences in the distribution of clinical and administrative labor.35

Geographic variation did not account for observed payment gaps. CMS applies GPCIs at ZIP-code levels to adjust reimbursement through 122 distinct locality based multipliers. Unlike prior work that approximated GPCI effects using Census regions14-16, this analysis utilized true GPCIs and showed nearly identical values between genders, with payment gaps persisting even after geographic adjustments were removed as standardized Medicare payments.

The absence of meaningful geographic differences implies the remaining variability among identical CPT codes may stem from differences in billing and coding practices. Prior research indicates that female surgeons submit fewer CPT codes per case and may be less likely to apply modifier 22, even when clinical complexity or case duration would support its use33. A tendency to undercode or avoid billing for added complexity may reflect variation in training, mentorship, transparency around billing expectations, and exposure to coding education during residency8,18. For example, Mayers et al. found that modifier 22 usage in distal radius fracture repair was more closely tied to practice patterns and surgeon demographics than to fracture or patient complexity.36

Behavioral differences may reflect broader socialized norms around self-advocacy and compensation.8,31,36 Female surgeons have been shown to hold lower salary expectations and report greater discomfort with negotiation, even early in training37,38. These patterns carry lasting financial consequences: among academic hand surgeons, gender-based differences in annual salary have been shown to compound to a $2.0 to $2.6 million gap in lifetime earnings8-10. These workforce disparities may be associated with differences in procedural opportunity, compensation structure, and access to institutional support31,32,39. Future study is required to evaluate how procedural opportunity, coding education, and practice infrastructure relate to reimbursement patterns over time and whether interventions during training could influence observed disparities in both reimbursement and career attrition18.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, Medicare Fee-for-Service data do not capture physician employment models, institutional compensation structures, part time status, or subspecialty training, all of which influence total earnings. Therefore, observed differences should be interpreted as associations within Medicare FFS billing data and may be partly attributable to unmeasured practice characteristics. Inclusion is restricted to physicians billing at least 11 services, which may underrepresent infrequently performed, high-complexity procedures, or physicians who limit their number of Medicare patients. Pediatric orthopedics, the most common fellowship among female orthopedic surgeons, is largely absent from Medicare data. As a result, differences in services or beneficiaries may partly reflect differences in patient populations. Part-time status, speculated to be more common among women, also cannot be assessed with the current dataset40. However, these factors do not explain the persistent reimbursement disparities observed for identical CPT codes or within matched cohorts (Table III). This data set also lacks information on add-on or modifier codes18,19. Variation in individual coding practices and reliance on professional coding infrastructure may also contribute to observed variation but cannot be directly evaluated with available CMS data.

Despite these limitations, Medicare FFS data provide standardized billing information within a nationally uniform reimbursement system. These findings are relevant given the substantial proportion of orthopedic surgeons who participate in Medicare and the program's influence on private payer reimbursement41,42.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that the observed gender-based disparities in orthopedic surgery reimbursement cannot be explained by differences in volume, procedural mix, or geographic practice cost. Instead, they likely reflect a combination of structural and behavioral mechanisms that influence how clinical opportunities, referrals, and reimbursement practices unfold in everyday surgical practice. Variations in coding practices may represent an important focus for future investigation and targeted educational initiatives.
