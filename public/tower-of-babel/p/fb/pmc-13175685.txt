Survival Outcomes of Gamma Knife Radiosurgery in EGFR-Mutant Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer Patients With 1–4 Versus 5–10 Brain Metastases: A Vietnamese Study
Authors: Duc Linh Tran, Duc Lien Nguyen, Van Ba Nguyen, Thanh Duong Phan, Se-Hyuk Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the targeted therapy era, the indication for stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) has expanded to include patients with multiple brain metastases (BMs). This study aimed to compare treatment outcomes in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with 1–4 versus 5–10 BMs, all treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and upfront Gamma Knife radiosurgery.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 74 consecutive EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients with 1–10 synchronous BMs treated with first-line EGFR-TKIs and upfront Gamma Knife SRS at Vietnam National Cancer Hospital from 2021 to 2024. Patients were divided into 1–4 BMs (n=39) and 5–10 BMs (n=35) groups. Primary endpoints were intracranial progression-free survival (iPFS) and overall survival (OS).ResultsBaseline characteristics were balanced between the two groups. Median iPFS was not reached in the 1–4 BMs group and 19 months in the 5–10 BMs group (hazard ratio [HR] 1.06, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.95–1.17; p=0.31). Median OS was not reached in the 1–4 BMs group and was 23 months in the 5–10 BMs group (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.91–1.16; p=0.41). Multivariate analysis revealed extracranial response (HR 4.30, p<0.01 for iPFS; HR 7.29, p<0.01 for OS) and presence of extracranial metastases (HR 3.20, p=0.01 for iPFS) as the only independent prognostic factors; number of BMs was not prognostic. Radionecrosis occurred in 6.8%, of which 2.7% were symptomatic.ConclusionIn EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients receiving TKIs and upfront Gamma Knife radiosurgery, the survival time in patients with 5–10 BMs was comparable to that with 1–4 BMs. The number of BMs should not be regarded as a contraindication for SRS in this subgroup.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is the most common cancer globally, with over 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths reported in 2022 [1]. The brain is a common site of metastasis, and brain metastases (BMs) significantly impair patients’ quality of life and survival. It is estimated that up to 40% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients develop BMs at the time of diagnosis [2]. This incidence is increasingly rising due to improvements in systemic therapies and routine surveillance with MRI. For NSCLC patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have significantly improved prognosis, with a median overall survival (OS) ranging from 19 to 38.6 months [345]. However, the efficacy of TKIs on BMs is limited due to the blood–brain barrier. Among local treatment modalities for BMs, stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) offers advantages over whole-brain radiotherapy or surgery and has been used for decades. Several early studies demonstrated that patients with ≥5 BMs had worse OS after receiving SRS than those with 1–4 BMs; consequently, SRS has been primarily indicated for patients with 1–4 BMs [678]. Recent studies have reported promising outcomes for SRS in patients with more than 5 BMs [91011]. The American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) 2022 guideline recommends prioritizing SRS for patients with 5–10 BMs if feasible systemic treatment options are available [12]. Despite this shift, data specifically evaluating Gamma Knife SRS combined with TKIs in patients with 5–10 BMs remain scarce, particularly in Southeast Asian populations. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed data from NSCLC patients with BMs treated at the Vietnam National Cancer Hospital to compare clinical outcomes following Gamma Knife radiosurgery and TKI therapy between those with 1–4 BMs and those with 5–10 BMs. Furthermore, we also evaluated factors associated with survival in this patient cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient population

We conducted a retrospective study of patients with advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC who were initially diagnosed with BMs at Vietnam National Cancer Hospital (Hanoi, Vietnam) between January 2021 and June 2024. This study followed the STROBE reporting guidelines. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) pathological confirmation of NSCLC; 2) presence of an EGFR mutation; 3) presence of 1 to 10 BMs on MRI; 4) Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) ≥70; and 5) treatment with first-line TKIs and upfront Gamma Knife radiosurgery. The exclusion criteria were: 1) history of prior brain surgery or radiotherapy; 2) previous lung surgery or concurrent chemoradiotherapy; 3) presence of other severe chronic comorbidities; 4) incomplete medical records; and 5) patients who abandoned treatment or were lost to follow-up. The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Gamma Knife radiosurgery and EGFR-TKI treatment

SRS was performed using the Gamma Knife Perfexion system (Elekta AB). Patients underwent simulation MRI with contrast enhancement, using a 1.5-T GE scanner with 1-mm slice thickness, which included T1-weighted, gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted, and T2-weighted sequences. Treatment planning was conducted with Gamma Plan software version 10.1.1 (Elekta AB). For the dose scheme, we followed the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) 90-05 protocol. Specifically, the prescribed dose was determined based on the maximum diameter of the BMs: 20–24 Gy for metastases <20 mm, 18–20 Gy for metastases 20–30 mm, and 15–18 Gy for metastases >30–40 mm. The TKIs used in this study included the first generation (gefitinib and erlotinib), the second generation (afatinib), and the third generation (osimertinib) agents.

Data collection and follow-up

The collected baseline characteristics included age, sex, symptoms related to BMs, KPS, status of extracranial metastases, the number and total volume of BMs, EGFR mutation type, and the specific TKI agent used. Key dates recorded were the Gamma Knife treatment date, the TKI initiation date, the date of intracranial progression, and the date of death or last follow-up.

Clinical and imaging follow-ups were typically conducted at 3 months after SRS and subsequently every 3 to 6 months, or when clinically indicated. The extracranial response was evaluated by CT scans using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. According to these criteria, response to systemic therapy was defined as achieving a partial response or better. The intracranial response was assessed by contrast-enhanced MRI according to the Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology Brain Metastases (RANO-BM) criteria. Intracranial progression-free survival (iPFS) was defined as the time from SRS to the identification of any intracranial disease progression or death from any cause. OS was defined as the time from SRS to death from any cause, with data censored for patients who were alive at the last follow-up. Radionecrosis was assessed and graded according to the International Stereotactic Radiosurgery Society Recommendations [13].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp.). Group characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. The t-test was used to compare normally distributed continuous variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Survival rates were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Prognostic factors associated with iPFS and OS were identified through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Council of 103 Military Hospital under Certificate No. 28/2021/CNChT-HĐĐĐ, dated January 6, 2023. The data used in this study were collected and analyzed with the approval of the Vietnam National Cancer Hospital.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

At the data cut-off, 74 patients were eligible for this study, stratified into two groups: 39 patients with 1–4 BMs and 35 with 5–10 BMs. The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 60 years (interquartile range, 53–66), with no significant differences observed between the groups regarding age, sex, or neurological symptoms. The 5–10 BMs group exhibited trends toward a lower KPS (70–80: 62.9% vs. 43.6%) and a higher rate of extracranial metastases (82.9% vs. 64.1%); however, these differences were not statistically significant (p=0.109 and p=0.115, respectively). Notably, despite a significant difference in the number of BMs, the total intracranial tumor volume was comparable between the groups (7.39 cm3 vs. 6.07 cm3, p=0.551). This suggests that the 1–4 BM group was characterized by fewer but larger lesions, while the 5–10 BMs group comprised more numerous, smaller metastases. Furthermore, the distributions of EGFR mutation types and the specific EGFR TKIs administered were well-balanced across the cohorts.

Response evaluation

The extracranial disease response rate was comparable between the two groups. Regarding the intracranial response, the 5–10 BMs group showed a slightly higher complete response rate (51.4% vs. 43.6%); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.180). During the study period, 36 patients experienced intracranial progression, with the vast majority (88.9%) developing new lesions, and the pattern of progression did not differ significantly between the groups.

Survival outcome

The median follow-up duration was 17 months (range, 5 to 38 months). At the last follow-up, 36 patients had experienced intracranial progression, and 29 deaths had occurred. The median iPFS for all patients was 22 months (95% CI, 17.45–26.55). The cumulative iPFS rates at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months were 94.6%, 77.9%, 57.2%, and 40.4%, respectively. The median OS for all patients was not reached, the cumulative OS rates at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months were 97.3%, 83.8%, 69.6%, and 57.6%, respectively.

Fig. 2 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves for iPFS and OS of the 1–4 BMs and 5–10 BMs groups. The median iPFS was not reached in the 1–4 BMs group and was 19 months (95% CI, 15.86–22.15) in the 5–10 BMs group. Similarly, the median OS was not reached for the 1–4 BMs group, while it was 23 months (95% CI, 16.7–23.9) for the 5–10 BMs group. However, no significant differences in iPFS or OS were observed between the two groups, with HR 1.06 (95% CI, 0.95–1.17; p=0.31) and HR 1.03 (95% CI, 0.91–1.16; p=0.41), respectively.

Prognostic factors for iPFS identified by univariate and multivariate analyses are shown in Table 2. The multivariate analysis demonstrated that the presence of extracranial metastases (p=0.01) and extracranial response (p<0.01) were two independent factors significantly associated with iPFS. For OS, Table 3 shows that extracranial response remained a statistically significant prognostic factor.

SRS-related adverse events

Radionecrosis occurred in 5 of 74 patients (6.8%) after SRS. Among these, two were symptomatic (one with grade 2 treated with steroids, and one with grade 3 treated with steroids combined with bevacizumab), and three were asymptomatic. The time to onset of radionecrosis ranged from 6 to 12 months. The incidence of radionecrosis did not differ between patients with 1–4 BMs and those with 5–10 BMs (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

It is well recognized that the number of BMs reflects the burden of cancer in the brain. A higher number of metastases is associated with a greater risk of microscopic parenchymal BM and a higher likelihood of progression after radiosurgery. Previous studies have shown that the OS of patients with more than 5 BMs is worse than that of those with 1–4 BMs [345]. In recent years, with advancements in systemic therapies such as TKIs or immunotherapy, some studies have reported that OS of patients in the group with 5–10 BMs is not worse than that in the group with 1–4 BMs. In a 2018 retrospective study by Knoll et al. [10] involving 243 patients with various BMs treated with radiosurgery, the results showed no statistically significant difference in OS between the group with 1–4 BMs and the group with ≥5 BMs (HR: 1.40, 95% CI: 0.84–2.31, p=0.195). In a 2018 retrospective study of 784 patients with NSCLC and BMs, Shuto et al. [14] reported no significant difference in the incidence of new BMs (HR: 1.216, 95% CI: 0.951–1.555, p=0.118) or in OS between the group with 2–4 BMs and the group with 5–10 BMs (HR: 1.037, 95% CI: 0.842–1.277, p=0.73). In a 2022 study by Zhao et al. [15] on 74 cases treated with radiosurgery, the authors compared the group with 1–4 BMs to the group with 5–10 BMs and found no significant difference in either iPFS or OS.

The Kaplan–Meier analysis in our study revealed no significant difference in iPFS or OS between the 5–10 BMs group and the 1–4 BMs group (Fig. 2). Furthermore, univariate and multivariate analyses of factors affecting survival time in this study also indicated that the number of BMs was not a significant factor influencing iPFS or OS (Tables 2 and 3). Instead, the status of extracranial metastases and extracranial response were two independent prognostic factors for iPFS. Meanwhile, extracranial response was a significant factor affecting OS. These results are consistent with recent studies suggesting that iPFS and OS depend more on the systemic disease burden and systemic disease control than on intracranial factors [10151617]. Furthermore, studies by Knoll et al. [10] and Zhao et al. [15] found a correlation between the number of BMs and the extracranial disease burden as well as the degree of systemic disease control.

Whether the number of BMs directly affects survival time or merely reflects systemic disease burden and treatment response remains uncertain. In this study, we observed that the majority of patients experienced intracranial progression only after the extracranial tumor had developed resistance to TKIs, and most of these progressions were new brain metastatic lesions (88.9%). This result aligns with a 2019 study by Yomo et al. [18] on 608 NSCLC patients with BMs treated with Gamma Knife radiosurgery. The authors compared 238 patients receiving TKI therapy with 370 patients receiving other systemic therapies. The results showed that the TKI-treated group had longer intracranial control time but a higher rate of new brain metastatic recurrence compared to the group receiving systemic chemotherapy (HR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.12–1.89, p=0.005). Compared to chemotherapy, TKIs have better penetration of the blood–brain barrier. The combination of TKIs and radiosurgery has a synergistic effect, enhancing intracranial control efficacy, which may explain the similar iPFS observed between the 1–4 and 5–10 BMs groups. Specifically, radiosurgery directly targets the BMs to destroy macroscopic lesions, while radiation also helps disrupt the blood–brain barrier, increasing the permeability of TKIs into the brain parenchyma. Meanwhile, TKI therapy is effective against potential microscopic metastatic lesions and controls systemic disease, thereby limiting the emergence of new BMs.

According to medical literature, BMs have always been considered a poor prognostic factor for OS. Therefore, when intracranial disease control is improved, the number of BMs may not significantly impact OS. Clinically, TKI drugs have been proven to significantly improve survival outcomes in NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations, both in those without and those with BMs. The study by Yomo and colleagues [1819] showed that patients treated with radiosurgery and TKIs had better outcomes than those treated with radiosurgery combined with chemotherapy. Some retrospective studies even indicate that patients treated with TKIs and brain radiotherapy have OS outcomes not inferior to those of TKI studies in patients without BMs, particularly for patients treated with osimertinib [1617202122].

Compared to earlier generations, osimertinib (a third-generation TKI) has been proven superior in controlling BMs as well as prolonging OS. In this study, we observed a similar trend. Univariate analysis showed that the group using osimertinib had a trend towards improved iPFS and OS compared to the group receiving first- and second-generation TKIs, with HRs of 0.68 and 0.57, respectively. However, these results did not reach statistical significance (p>0.05) (Tables 2 and 3). The lack of statistical significance could stem from inherent limitations of this subgroup analysis. The small sample size (only 19/74 patients using osimertinib), combined with the short follow-up duration (median 17 months, range: 5–38 months), resulted in underpowered statistical analysis. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility of a real benefit of osimertinib on iPFS and OS within this combined treatment model. Notably, the favorable trend for third-generation TKIs that we observed is entirely consistent with robust evidence from large clinical trials such as FLAURA and AURA3, which have confirmed the superior efficacy of osimertinib in NSCLC patients with BMs [523]. This finding provides hypothesis-generating data suggesting that the advantage of osimertinib may still be maintained in a regimen combined with radiosurgery. To definitively confirm this, future studies with larger sample sizes, prospectively designed and adequately powered to directly compare the efficacy of each TKI generation in the context of multimodality therapy, are highly necessary.

This study has several important limitations that must be considered when interpreting its findings. It is a retrospective, single-center analysis with a relatively small sample size and short follow-up duration. These factors collectively limit the statistical power, introduce potential for selection bias and data incompleteness, and restrict the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Furthermore, the small sample size precluded a meaningful subgroup analysis stratified by EGFR-TKI generation—a clinically relevant distinction given the well-documented superior intracranial efficacy of third-generation TKIs compared to earlier generations. Consequently, while a favorable trend was observed for osimertinib, the analysis was underpowered to detect statistically significant differences in outcomes. Despite these constraints, to our knowledge, this is the first investigation within a Vietnamese population to evaluate clinical outcomes for NSCLC patients harboring 5–10 BMs treated with a combination of TKIs and Gamma Knife radiosurgery, and our preliminary findings suggest this approach may yield favorable outcomes even in this patient subset with a higher intracranial tumor burden.

In conclusion, in the era of targeted therapy, the clinically relevant distinction between patients presenting with 1–4 versus 5–10 BMs warrants particular attention. The analysis demonstrates that a higher intracranial disease burden is not associated with inferior iPFS or OS. Notably, extracranial response emerged as a powerful and independent prognostic factor for both iPFS and OS—an observation of considerable clinical significance. Collectively, these findings provide evidence supporting the use of upfront SRS in EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients with up to 10 BMs and underscore the fundamental role of systemic disease control in shaping outcomes.
