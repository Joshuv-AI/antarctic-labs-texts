Chidamide‑augmented BEAM conditioning and maintenance in first-remission T‑cell lymphoma: a single‑arm phase 2 experience
Authors: Yi-Ge Shen, Si-Yuan Chen, Rong-Ji Mu, Hui-Ying Li, Li Wang, Shu Cheng, Peng-Peng Xu, Zhong Zheng, Lin-Di Zhao, Hui-Juan Zhong, Ming-Ci Cai, Yao-Hui Huang, Di Fu, Yu-Jia Huo, Wei Tang, Meng-Meng Ji
Published: 2026

Introduction

T‑cell lymphoma (TCL) is a rare subset of non‑Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), accounting for 5–10% of cases in Western countries and 15–20% in Asia [1]. TCL encompasses multiple pathological subtypes, including nodal T‑follicular helper cell lymphoma, angioimmunoblastic‑type (nTFHL‑AI), peripheral T‑cell lymphoma‑not otherwise specified (PTCL‑NOS), and anaplastic large‑cell lymphoma (ALCL) [2]. First‑line CHOP‑based regimens yield unsatisfactory outcomes [2]. Autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) is a standard consolidation strategy for TCL in first remission, yet the 2‑year progression‑free survival (PFS) with BEAM conditioning is only 52.9% [3]. Chidamide, an oral HDAC1/2/3/10 inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy in relapsed/refractory TCL [4]. Preclinical studies suggest synergistic effects with chemotherapeutic agents such as cytarabine and etoposide [5]. However, no study has systematically integrated chidamide into the BEAM (carmustine, etoposide, cytarabine, and melphalan) backbone to improve transplant outcomes.

With this intent, we conducted a single‑arm, phase 2 trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the Chi‑BEAM regimen (chidamide added to BEAM) followed by ASCT and chidamide maintenance in patients with TCL achieving first remission after frontline chemotherapy.

Patients and methods

This single‑arm, phase 2 trial (NCT05367856) was conducted at Ruijin Hospital, China. Written informed consent and ethics approval were obtained per the Declaration of Helsinki.

Eligibility included TCL (excluding IPI 0‑1 ALK + ALCL), age 18–65 years, and complete remission (CR) or partial remission (PR) after first‑line chemotherapy. Patients with prior transplant, other malignancies within 5 years, uncontrolled infections, HBsAg/HCV/HIV positivity (HBV/HCV seropositivity allowed if DNA/RNA negative), left ventricular ejection fraction ≤ 50%, pregnancy, or severe organ dysfunction were excluded.

All patients received Chi‑BEAM (chidamide 30 mg on days –7, –4, –1, +3; carmustine day –7; etoposide and cytarabine days –6 to –3; melphalan day –2) followed by ASCT (day 0). Chidamide maintenance (30 mg twice weekly) begins 2 months after transplantation and continues for at least 2 years.

The primary endpoint was 2‑year PFS, defined as the proportion of patients who were alive and free from disease progression at 2 years after enrollment. Secondary endpoints included OS, CR rate, hematopoietic reconstruction, and adverse events. Neutrophil engraftment was defined as the first of three consecutive days with neutrophil counts ≥ 0.5 × 10⁹/L, and platelet engraftment as the first of seven consecutive days with platelets ≥ 20 × 10⁹/L without transfusion. Assuming 81% 2‑year PFS with Chi‑BEAM versus 52.9% historical, 23 patients were required (90% power, one-sided α = 0.025). Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan‑Meier method, and comparisons between groups were performed with the log‑rank test. p < 0.05 was significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26.0, and a two‑sided p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between June 15, 2022, and June 15, 2023, a total of 26 patients were screened, of whom 2 withdrew consent and 1 experienced mobilization failure. 23 patients were enrolled and proceeded to transplantation. The intention‑to‑treat population (ITTP) of 23 patients included: PTCL‑NOS (n = 6; 26.1%), ALK‑negative ALCL (n = 6; 26.1%), and nTFHL‑AI (n = 11; 47.8%); 56.5% of patients were men, and the median age was 49 years (range, 22–65). Regarding disease characteristics, 95.7% of the patients presented with advanced stage disease, 87.0% with elevated serum lactate dehydrogenase, and 47.8% with B symptoms (Supplementary Table 1).

At the time of analysis, all 23 enrolled patients were alive. The median follow‑up of surviving patients was 24.6 months (range, 18.2 to 38.7 months). In the ITTP, the 2‑year PFS and OS rates were 95.7% (95% CI, 72.9–99.4) and 100.0% (95% CI, 100.0–100.0), respectively (Fig. 1a, b). When analyzed by pathological subtyping, the 2‑year PFS rates were 100% (95% CI, 100.0–100.0) in ALCL, 100% (95% CI, 100.0–100.0) in PTCL‑NOS, and 90.9% (95% CI, 50.8–98.7) in nTFHL‑AI, with a 2‑year OS of 100% across all these subtypes (Fig. 1c, d).

At 3 months post‑transplantation, 17 (73.9%) patients had achieved CR and 5 (21.7%) had achieved PR. Subsequently, one patient (4.3%) experienced relapse or disease progression at 4.4 months post‑transplantation. Clonality analysis confirmed the same malignant clone as at initial diagnosis. This case, characterized by high‑risk mutations in TET2, RHOA, and IDH2 and classified as the T1 subtype [6], likely contributed to the poor response to transplantation. No transplant‑related mortality was reported until the last follow‑up.

The median time for neutrophil engraftment was 10 days, and for platelet engraftment was 16 days. The post‑transplant period was characterized by significant cytopenias, with a high incidence of febrile neutropenia (82.6%, 19/23), which had a median duration of 4 days; the median duration of severe (Grade 4) neutropenia and thrombocytopenia was 8 and 10 days, respectively. Supportive care requirements were notable for platelet transfusions (median 4 units), while red blood cell transfusions were minimal (median 0.5 units), consistent with the low incidence of severe anemia (13%, 3/23; Supplementary Table 2).

Grades 3‑4 hematologic toxicities were seen in 100% of patients, whereas grades 3‑4 non‑hematologic toxicities were recorded in 4 patients (17.4%), with infectious complications as the most frequent adverse event (n = 3; 13.0%), followed by hepatic (n = 2; 8.7%), vomiting (n = 1; 4.3%), and mucositis (n = 1; 4.3%). No patients died due to adverse events (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

ASCT is a standard consolidation strategy for TCL in first remission, supported by prospective and retrospective studies [3, 7, 8]. The BEAM regimen remains the most widely used conditioning backbone, but a substantial proportion of patients relapse post‑ASCT, underscoring the need for optimization [3, 8].

Chidamide, an oral HDAC1/2/3/10 inhibitor approved in China for TCL, exerts anti‑tumor effects through cell cycle arrest, apoptosis induction, and chemosensitization [4, 9]. Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that chidamide exerts anti‑tumor activity in hematologic malignancies via G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and chemosensitization [5, 10]. Thus, incorporating chidamide into the BEAM conditioning regimen is a rational approach to improve transplant outcomes.

Previous chidamide‑containing conditioning regimens (Chi‑CGB, Chi‑BEAC) have shown promising results in high‑risk lymphomas [11, 12], but no study has optimized the classic BEAM backbone. Our trial directly addresses this gap. In 23 transplantation‑eligible TCL patients, the Chi‑BEAM regimen achieved a 2‑year PFS of 95.7% and OS of 100% at a median follow‑up of 24.6 months, with consistent efficacy across ALCL, PTCL‑NOS, and nTFHL‑AI subtypes. Compared with historical BEAM data [3] and contemporary registry studies [7, 8], Chi‑BEAM confers a substantial survival advantage, providing first clinical evidence that adding chidamide to BEAM is feasible and improves outcomes.

The single relapsed patient carried a molecularly high‑risk signature (TET2, RHOA, IDH2 mutations, T1 subtype) [6], suggesting that even with Chi‑BEAM, certain genomic profiles may escape disease control. This underscores the need for integrating molecular risk stratification into transplant decisions and exploring novel post‑transplant maintenance strategies beyond chidamide.

Limitations include the single‑arm design and small sample size, which preclude definitive conclusions on the magnitude of benefit attributable to chidamide. The overrepresentation of AITL (47.8%) in our study cohort constitutes a limitation, likely attributable to enhanced diagnostic capabilities and referral bias at our institution. Moreover, TCL rarity limits large‑scale studies. Potential toxicities of combining chidamide with intensive chemotherapy require further monitoring, and longer follow‑up is needed to assess late relapse and long‑term survival.

In conclusion, the Chi‑BEAM regimen followed by ASCT and chidamide maintenance is a promising, well‑tolerated conditioning strategy for TCL patients in first remission. It warrants rigorous evaluation in larger randomized controlled trials.
