Synthesis of aniline from dinitrogen and benzene mediated by magnesium oxide supported sodium hydride
Authors: Shixiong Zhang, Yongli Cai, Hong Wen, Liang Liu, Lei Yu, Peng Zhang, Jianping Guo, Ping Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe activation and valorization of inert molecules (e.g., dinitrogen (N2), alkanes, and alkenes) for the synthesis of nitrogen-containing organic compounds have long been a highly sought-after goal in chemistry. However, it remains a formidable challenge, stemming from the inherent chemical inertness of the robust N ≡ N and C-H bonds, as well as the competitive adsorption and activation of reactants. Consequently, examples of direct C-N bond formation using N2 and alkanes/alkenes as feedstocks remain exceptionally scarce. Herein, we report that sodium hydride supported on magnesium oxide (NaH/MgO) possesses unique multiple reactive sites, which enable the conversion of N2 and unactivated arenes and facilitate C-N bond formation. The synergistic interplay between sodium, magnesium, and hydride species at the NaH/MgO interface plays a pivotal role in the reduction of N2 to NHx species. These reactive NHx intermediates then deprotonate the aryl C-H bond, attack the alkali-interacted aryl ring, and drive the formation of sodium anilide on the surface. Subsequent protonation of sodium anilide yields aniline with high selectivity (>90%). This work demonstrates the feasibility of transforming N2 and simple arenes into key nitrogen-containing organic compounds via a solid surface-mediated process, thereby opening ample room for developing heterogeneous catalysts for the transformation of N2 and organic substrates.Subject terms: Chemistry, Chemical synthesis, Heterogeneous catalysis

Introduction

The direct transformation of N2 into value-added nitrogen-containing products poses a formidable scientific challenge because of its inert nature. At present, almost all organic nitrogen-containing compounds are synthesized using ammonia or amine as the nitrogen source. The well-established industrial ammonia synthesis process (Haber-Bosch process) is energy intensive and of high carbon footprint. In recent years, on one hand, the development of more efficient catalysts for ammonia synthesis and alternative N2 fixation methods such as electrochemical, photochemical, plasma-catalytic, and chemical looping routes have been received much attention1–6. On the other hand, the direct transformation of N2 into organic nitrogen-containing compounds including amines, nitriles, alkaloids, etc., has also piqued significant interest7–12. Although heterogeneous, homogeneous, and biological catalysts are known for their roles in catalytic nitrogen fixation to ammonia, the (catalytic) synthesis of more complex nitrogen-containing organic molecules has far less precedent.

The pioneering research efforts regarding the conversion of N2 into organic compounds have largely concentrated on molecular transition metal complexes because of their versatile and well-defined compositions and structures, flexible chemical environments, as well as diversified coordination modes for both N2 and organic substrates8,13–19. Although there are some cases of the coupling of N2 with aryllithium or aryl halides, mediated by transition metals, to yield N-containing compounds20–23, research on the reactions of N2 with arenes without prior functionalization remains scarce. Holland et al. reported an iron β-diketiminate complex that mediates the coupling of N2 and arenes to yield aniline derivatives24. The key C-N bond formation step occurs after partial silylation of coordinated N2 on the Fe site. Tian et al. reported a plasma-enabled, catalyst-free approach to the direct coupling of benzene and N2 into arylamines at the plasma-liquid interface, leveraging the high-energy environment for N2 activation25. Very recently, Wei and Xi et al. reported a chromium-mediated synthetic cycle for the direct conversion of N2 into multi-substituted hydrazines via aryl migration, here termed the Xi Cycle, thereby representing a landmark advance in N2 functionalization26. The synthesis of organic nitrogen-containing compounds from N2 and inert hydrocarbon sources on a solid surface, hitherto, has rarely been reported.

Metal hydrides, existing in diverse structural and compositional forms, have played crucial roles in many fundamental chemical transformations27–30. It has recently been demonstrated that alkali and alkaline earth metal hydrides, including lithium hydride (LiH), barium hydride (BaH2), and sodium hydride (NaH), can enable N2 fixation and subsequent hydrogenation to form N-H bonds, driven by thermal energy, plasma, or ultraviolet light illumination31–35. Beyond N2 activation, hydride species (H‾) have also been shown to mediate the activation of inert C-H bonds, such as that in benzene, under mild conditions36–38. Taken together, these insights suggest the potential of hydrides to enable the synergistic activation of N≡N and C-H bonds, thereby facilitating direct C-N bond formation. Herein, we demonstrate the direct construction of C-N bond from inert N2 and unactivated arenes mediated by sodium hydride supported on MgO (Fig. 1). This transformation proceeds through three key steps: N2 fixation via mechanochemistry, C-N bond formation under thermochemical conditions, and protonation or hydrogenation to yield aniline. Anilines are obtained as dominant products with high selectivity (>90%). Notably, the one-pot synthesis of nitrogen-containing organic compounds from N2 and simple arenes can also be achieved under heating at 200 °C. Different from molecular transition metal complexes reported previously, this study reveals the intriguing role of alkali (and alkaline earth) metal hydrides in the activation of N≡N and C-H bonds, along with the formation of the C-N bond. Interestingly, the introduction of iron species exerts a pronounced promotional effect on N≡N bond activation and subsequent C-N bond construction. This work could create substantial opportunities for the advancement of heterogeneous materials aimed at converting N2 and hydrocarbon sources into valuable organic nitrogen-containing compounds.

Results

N2 fixation by NaH/MgO

While neat NaH has been reported to fix a negligible amount of N species under normal thermal conditions33, we found that when dispersed on the MgO support, it is capable to fix N2. Firstly, we investigated thermal N2 fixation by NaH/MgO, which was prepared by ball-milling a mixture of NaH (10 wt%) and MgO powders in a commercial tungsten carbide (WC) jar. The N2-temperature-programmed reaction (N2-TPR) profile of NaH/MgO shows H2 formation upon heating under a N2 flow, with a signal intensity significantly higher than that in Ar-temperature-programmed decomposition reaction (Ar-TPD) (Supplementary Fig. S1a), indicating N2 reduction by the hydride material31,39. In contrast, control experiments confirmed that bare MgO produced no detectable H2 signal (Supplementary Fig. S1b). Moreover, the NaH/MgO sample collected after heating in an N2 atmosphere exhibits the significant N-H bond stretching vibrations at 3263 and 3217 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. S2), confirming the formation of surface NHx (x = 1, 2) species. These results show that the ball-milled NaH/MgO can activate N2 and promote its hydrogenation to form N-H bonds.

To simplify precursor preparation for N2 fixation, we explored a streamlined, one-pot process using readily available Na metal pellets and MgO powder under H2 and N2 pressures to afford the N-fixed samples. That is, Na and MgO were co-fed and ball milled in a WC jar with variable N2/H2 pressures, affording a solid product (denoted as Na-N-H/MgO). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Supplementary Fig. S3), high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image, and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping (Fig. 2a) showed that Na species is evenly dispersed on the MgO support at the (sub)nanometer scale. Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFT) spectra (Fig. 2b) showed several bands around 3277, 3244, and 3220 cm−1 which are assigned to stretching vibrations of NHx (x = 1 and 2) species, a clear indication of N≡N bond cleavage followed by hydrogenation to N-H bonds. Isotope experiments using 15N2 and D2 also confirmed that these bands can be assigned to the stretching vibrations of NHx (Supplementary Fig. S4). Noted that the observed vibrational modes closely resemble those of NaNH2 and Mg(NH2)2 albeit with slight shifts, suggesting that the NHx species could be bonded with both Na and Mg atoms on the surface. Time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) with negative mode (Fig. 2c) detected cluster ions containing NHx species (NH‾, NaNH‾, NaNH2‾, and MgNH2‾), confirming NHx formation and N bonding with Na and/or Mg.

The amount of fixed nitrogen over Na-N-H/MgO was further quantified via hydrolysis with water vapor, where protonation of the fixed nitrogen yielded NH3 as the sole N-containing product (Supplementary Fig. S5); for example, after ball milling under a N2(15 bar)-H2(15 bar) atmosphere, the amount of fixed nitrogen was determined to be 0.35 mmol g−1 (Supplementary Fig. S6b). Additionally, we observed that the adventitious Fe introduced from the milling jar material significantly affects the amount of nitrogen fixed during ball milling. As shown in Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. S6a, the amount of fixed nitrogen reaches 1.2 mmol g−1 in a stainless-steel jar, ~3 times that in a WC jar. The XRD pattern reveals the presence of Fe nanoparticles after ball-milling of Na and MgO in a stainless-steel jar (Supplementary Fig. S7). It is well-known that Fe metal is catalytically active for ammonia synthesis from N2 and H2. We therefore propose iron species play an important role in this system, and accordingly prepared the nitrogen-fixed material comprising Na/MgO and Fe (denoted as Na-N-H/MgO-Fe). The amount of fixed N increases with increasing N2 partial pressure, with a maximum of 2.17 mmol g−1 achieved over the Na/MgO-Fe sample under N2(25 bar)-H2(10 bar) conditions (Fig. 2d). These results demonstrate the efficacy of one-pot mechanochemical methods in boosting N2 activation and conversion by NaH/MgO.

Coupling of fixed nitrogen species with benzene

We then examined the reaction of fixed NHx species with benzene to see whether C-H bond activation and C-N bond formation could take place. The Na-N-H/MgO-Fe samples were reacted with benzene at 200 °C for 3 h. The solid products were partially dissolved in DMSO, and the resulting solution was analyzed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) (Fig. 3a). Distinct up-field chemical shifts were observed at 5.35, 5.77, and 6.37 ppm, compared to typical aniline signals (6.48, 6.55, and 6.99 ppm). Similar proton resonance was observed in sodium anilide (C6H5-NHNa)40,41 (Supplementary Fig. S8), which was synthesized via the reaction of NaH with aniline. These results indicate the formation of sodium anilide salt species on the surface of Na-N-H/MgO-Fe. Moreover, the negative ToF-SIMS spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S9) showed the formation of a series of C-N bond-containing species such as C6H5NNa‾ and C6H5N‾, etc., further verifying the formation of anilide salts on Na-N-H/MgO.

The resulting anilide salts were further protonated with aqueous solutions, and the organic products were extracted into organic solvents. GC-MS analysis identified aniline as the predominant nitrogen-containing product, accompanied by the formation of other N-containing compounds including phenylenediamine, aminobiphenyl, and benzidine, as well as the homocoupling side product biphenyl (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Notably, Na-N-H/MgO sample synthesized in a WC jar (without Fe) also produced aniline via reaction with benzene followed by protonation, albeit with a lower yield of 1.41 μmol g−1 (Fig. 3b). This result substantiates that Na-N-H/MgO is intrinsically capable of mediating C-H bond activation and C-N bond formation in the absence of transition metals. In contrast, the reference Fe-N-MgO sample without NaH failed to produce aniline upon reaction with benzene, underscoring the critical role of the NaH/MgO interface in activating the arene C-H bond and enabling C-N bond formation. Remarkably, when Fe was present, the Na-N-H/MgO achieved an aniline yield of 214 μmol g−1 at 200 °C (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table S1). The specific prominent role of iron or other transition metals will be investigated in a separate study.

The reaction of Na-N-H/MgO-Fe samples with benzene was conducted at different temperatures (Fig. 3c, d, and Supplementary Fig. S11). Figure 3c reveals a volcano-shaped correlation between the amount of fixed nitrogen and the reaction temperature, with the maximum at 200 °C. Lower conversion at higher temperatures may be attributed to the decomposition of NHx or decomposition/conversion of anilide salts. Supplementary Fig. S11b shows the product distribution after reactions conducted at different temperatures. Notably, the selectivity for total N-containing products was higher than 95% below 200 °C (Supplementary Fig. S11a). More interestingly, the selectivity of aniline among N-containing products exceeded 90% typically below 180 °C (Fig. 3d), whereas aniline selectivity declined at higher temperatures, suggesting over-reactions between benzene and the solid surface. Through the optimization of reaction temperature, ball milling time, and loading of Na, a maximum conversion (ca. 26.3%) of fixed N in the 10 wt% Na/MgO sample was achieved at 200 °C (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table S1). The yields of aniline and total N-containing compounds were 214 and 310 μmol g−1, respectively. Furthermore, to verify aniline originated from N2 rather than nitrogen contaminates, Na-15N-H/MgO-Fe was synthesized using 15N2, and then reacted with benzene under the same conditions. GC-MS analysis confirmed 15N-labeled aniline as the main product (Fig. 3e), confirming N2 as the nitrogen source.

While hydrolysis offers a straightforward route to convert anilide salts to aniline, it results in the concomitant hydration of Na/MgO, leading to loss of reactivity. This prompted us to explore hydrogenation of anilide salts to produce aniline and regenerate NaH/MgO, potentially completing the synthetic cycle. To verify this, neat sodium anilide was synthesized and subjected to hydrogenation under a H2 flow. As shown in Fig. 3f, sodium anilide undergoes the desorption of unreacted aniline at lower temperature (ca. 80 °C), followed by hydrogenation to release additional aniline upon heating to 275 °C. In contrast, Ar-TPD confirmed only the lower-temperature aniline desorption signal, validating the hydrogenation process (Supplementary Fig. S12). Similarly, the surface-bonded sodium anilide species on Na/MgO can also be hydrogenated to aniline, albeit with lower signal intensity. No aniline formation was observed under argon atmosphere shown in Supplementary Fig. S13. These observations highlight the potential to close the chemical cycle via hydrogenation treatment, warranting further investigation to fully explore this process.

Conversion of N2 and benzene

Building on the successful stepwise synthesis of surface anilide, we further investigated the feasibility of one-pot C-N bond formation. NaH/MgO-Fe samples were employed as the mediator, over which benzene and N2 were introduced at specific pressures in a sealed system at 200 °C (Supplementary Fig. S14). Aniline was obtained after protonation treatment, albeit with relatively low yield of 109 μmol g−1 under 20 bar N2 pressure, attributed to competitive adsorption of benzene and N2 on NaH/MgO-Fe. Additionally, the one-step reaction was further evaluated in a flow reactor, where N2 served as both the reactant gas and the carrier gas to bring benzene vapor to the NaH/MgO-Fe. Aniline was formed after reaction at 200 °C under ambient pressure, followed by hydrolysis treatment (Supplementary Fig. S15). Overall, the results present a pioneering protocol for the direct formation of C-N bonds from N2 and benzene, in particular providing a viable strategy for the one-step transformation of N2 and arenes into nitrogen-containing organic compounds.

Mechanistic studies

The reaction mechanism of Na/MgO for the activation of N2 and C6H6, as well as the subsequent C-N bond formation was investigated by DFT calculations. Since neither NaH nor MgO alone functions for this process, the synergy between NaH and MgO is crucial for activating H2, N2, and C6H6 molecules, as well as for facilitating C-N bond formation. TEM and ToF-SIMS results revealed the presence of sub-nanosized sodium cluster (Na1-Na5) species (Supplementary Fig. S16). DFT calculation results further indicated that a Na4 cluster model featuring a quasi-square planar geometry exhibits the optimal interfacial binding energy, significantly superior to that of the Na1-Na3 and Na5 clusters (Supplementary Fig. S17). Accordingly, the Na4/MgO(100) model was selected for the subsequent mechanistic study (Supplementary Fig. S18).

On the Na4/MgO model, H2 undergoes heterolytic dissociation into Na-H-Mg and O-H species, with an energy barrier of merely 0.22 eV. N2 preferentially adsorbs in a side-on configuration at the Na-Mg-Na triangular site, with the N-N bond elongated to 1.17 Å. Subsequently, the hydride at the Na-H-Mg ensemble transfers to the adsorbed N2, producing the *N2H intermediate by 1.45 eV via a reductive protonation pathway. Migration of surface *OH species to the Na-Mg site to regenerate Na-H-Mg is exothermic; this replenishment of hydride facilitates the subsequent hydrogenation of *N2H to *N2H2, ultimately forming the surface species Na-NH2-Mg and Na-N-Mg. These findings are supported by experimental results from DRIFTS and ToF-SIMS.

After N2 fixation at the Na-H-Mg interface, benzene preferentially adsorbs at surface Na sites via cation-π interaction, adopting an η6 configuration. Deprotonation of benzene occurs upon attack by *N species, generating a surface-stabilized absorbed phenyl species on the Na-cluster surface (denoted as Na-C6H5*) and Na-NH-Mg species. This step is significantly exothermic (ΔE = −1.00 eV) with a moderate activation energy barrier (Ea = 0.36 eV), indicating favorable kinetics. Subsequently, surface *C6H5 was attacked by the nucleophilic *NH2 at the interface, yielding *C6H5NH2. Subsequently, *C6H5NH2 preferentially interacts with Na, accompanied by hydrogen transfer, to yield thermodynamically stable C6H5NH-Na and OH species in strongly exothermic step (−0.87 eV).

Scope of arenes

The successful synthesis of aniline from N2 and benzene inspired us to investigate C-N bond formation in reactions of N2 with a series of aromatic substrates bearing various functional groups using the Na/MgO sample. As shown in Fig. 4, a range of aniline derivatives was obtained under the general reaction conditions. Monoalkylated arenes reacted with surface-bound NHx species to afford the corresponding amination products (2–11) in moderate to good yields. Meta-substituted N-containing products predominate in these reactions, in contrast to conventional synthetic routes where substitution typically occurs at the ortho- and para- positions, often guided by directing groups. For example, m-toluidine (2) was the predominant product from toluene with a yield of 108 μmol g−1, with trace amounts of p-toluidine (Supplementary Fig. S19). Arenes bearing ethyl, propyl and butyl groups similarly gave exclusively meta-substituted amines (3–9) in appreciable yields, although their overall fixed-nitrogen conversions were lower than those observed with benzene. The preference for reaction at electron-deficient positions implies a process involving nucleophilic attack by NHx species.

Substrate scope of arene amination with N2 mediated by Na-N-H/MgO-Fe.

Yields of anilines are provided in the figure, with values in parentheses denoting the conversion of fixed nitrogen. General reaction conditions are as followed: 200 μL or 100 mg of the substrate, 50 mg Na-N-H/MgO, 200 °C, 3 h.

For bulkier arenes, 3-aminobiphenyl and 3-(2-phenylethyl) benzenamine (10 and 11) were obtained in yields of 161 μmol g−1 and 81 μmol g−1, respectively. For dialkyl-substituted substrates, m-xylene was converted to 3,5-dimethylaniline (12) with a relatively low yield of 22 μmol g−1. That is attributed to electron-donating methyl groups enhancing the electron density of the aromatic ring, thereby weakening its reactivity. In contrast, m-terphenyl afforded 3-amino-m-terphenyl (14) in a higher yield of 93 μmol g−1. Despite lower yields, 1-aminofluorene (13) (13 μmol g−1) and 4-amino-p-terphenyl (15) (12 μmol g−1) were also successfully synthesized. The amination method was also compatible with polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. In these cases, naphthalene underwent amination at the β-position (16). Similarly, both anthracene and phenanthrene underwent amination at β-sites (17 and 18), in contrast to the typical electrophilic substitution patterns. Quinoline was also effectively transformed into aminoquinoline (19) with a yield of 98 μmol g-1, consistent with the Chichibabin reaction pathway. Reactions of halogenated benzenes with Na-N-H/MgO yielded aniline (20a-23a), diphenylamine (20b-23b), and triphenylamine (20c-23c) instead of the corresponding haloanilines, suggesting a dehalogenative amination process analogous to the Buchwald-Hartwig cross-coupling42. Additionally, the surface anilide salts can react with chlorotrimethylsilane (TMSCl) to afford silylated aniline derivates (Supplementary Fig. S20).

Discussion

Given the scarcity of cases of N2 conversion to N-containing organic compounds, the reaction sequence reported herein is particularly relevant in the context of N2 functionalization using a solid mediator. Our results have demonstrated that NaH/MgO is capable of activating both N≡N and C-H bond of benzene, and enabling C-N bond formation. Moreover, the incorporation of iron not only promote the N2 fixation but also facilitate the C-N bond construction. This work offers an alternative heterogeneous pathway for the formation of the C-N bond from N2 and benzene, going beyond the use of molecular metal complexes. It should be noted that the synthesis of anilines using NaH/MgO as a mediator does not proceed in a catalytic manner. Future work could focus on achieving the catalytic synthesis of aniline from N2 and benzene, which may be realized by modifying the composition and structure of the NaH/MgO catalyst or introducing various transition metal dopants. Such modifications would destabilize the anilide species—the rate-limiting step in the catalytic cycle for aniline synthesis from N2 and benzene.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

Sodium block (99.7%) was purchased from Aladdin. MgO with a high specific surface area (ca. 170 m2 g−1) was synthesized according to a literature report43. Benzene (99.7%) was purchased from Aladdin and then was dried by 3 Å zeolite before use. N2 (99.999%) and H2 (99.999%) were purchased from Dalian Special Gas Co., Ltd. Ar (99.999%) was purchased from Dalian Junfeng Gases Co. Ltd. 15N2 (98%) was purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories. All materials were handled inside a glove box filled with purified argon (O2, H2O < 1 ppm), owing to the air- and moisture-sensitivity of the reagents and samples.

Materials synthesis

Fabrication of Na/MgO

Na/MgO with a Na loading of 10 wt% was prepared as follows. The MgO was pretreated under an argon flow at 580 °C for 8 h. Subsequently, metallic sodium (55.6 mg) and MgO (500 mg) were mixed and ball milled in either a tungsten carbide jar or stainless-steel jar at 200 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 12 h.

Fabrication of Na-N-H/MgO

Metallic sodium (55.6 mg) and MgO (500 mg) were mixed and ball milled in a tungsten carbide jar under a N2/H2 atmosphere at 200 rpm for 12 h. The obtained sample was denoted as Na-N-H/MgO.

Fabrication of Na-N-H/MgO-Fe

Metallic sodium (55.6 mg) and MgO (500 mg) were mixed and ball milled in a stainless-steel jar under a N2/H2 atmosphere at 200 rpm for 12 h. The obtained sample was denoted as Na-N-H/MgO-Fe.

Fabrication of Fe-N/MgO

MgO (500 mg) was ball milled in a stainless-steel jar under a N2/H2 atmosphere at 200 rpm for 12 h. The obtained sample was denoted as Fe-N/MgO.

Fabrication of NaH/MgO

NaH/MgO with a NaH loading of 10 wt% was also synthesized by ball milling NaH (44.4 mg) and MgO (400 mg) in a tungsten carbide jar at 200 rpm for 8 h.

After ball milling, the solid products were collected and stored in an Ar-filled glove box.

Quantification of fixed-N on Na-N-H/MgO samples

The active nitrogen species on Na-N-H/MgO were converted to ammonia by hydrolysis treatment and quantified using a conductivity meter. Water vapor carried by argon was introduced into the reaction tube to react with the Na-N-H/MgO. Then the exhaust gas was bubbled through dilute sulfuric acid, and the change of the conductivity meter was tracked to determine the mounts of ammonia discharge31,44,45, corresponding to the amount of nitrogen fixation of the sample.

General procedure of the reaction between Na-N-H/MgO and benzene

The reaction of Na-N-H/MgO with benzene was conducted in a home-made stainless-steel sealed reactor with a quartz lining. In a typical experiment, 50 mg of Na-N-H/MgO and 200 μL of benzene were added into the reactor in a glove box filled with argon. Then the reactor was heated to the desired temperature for several hours (such as at 200 °C for 3 h). After reaction, the solid-liquid mixtures were collected for further characterization.

One-pot procedure for aniline synthesis

The one-pot reaction of benzene and N2 over Na/MgO was conducted in a custom-designed stainless-steel sealed reactor with quartz lining. The Na/MgO was loaded into the quartz tube, while benzene was introduced into the reactor outside the tube, and N2 pressurized to desired values. Once the reaction temperature reached 200 °C, gaseous benzene and N2 were co-converted over Na/MgO. After reaction, the solid residues were hydrolyzed by an NH4Cl aqueous solution followed by extraction with ethyl acetate. The liquid products were then collected and analyzed by GC-MS.

In addition, in the flow system, benzene vapor, carried by N2 was co-fed to Na/MgO at 200 °C under ambient pressure. After reaction, the mixtures were treated and analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) as described above.

Quantitative analysis of aniline

50 mg of the solid products was hydrolyzed using 2 mL saturated ammonium chloride solution at room temperature, followed by extraction with 2 mL of ethyl acetate. Notably, aniline and other N-containing organics were typically obtained as the sole products after the demetalation treatment. Quantitative analysis of anilines was performed using gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID) employing dodecane as the internal standard. Characterizations

Power X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded on a PANalytical X’pert diffractometer in a home-made sample cell sealed by KAPTON film to avoid air and moisture contamination.

Diffuse reflectance infrared fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) measurements were conducted on a Brucker Tensor II unit in diffuse reflection mode with a scan resolution of 4 cm−1 and an accumulation of 32 scans each time.

Solution NMR spectroscopy was performed on a Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz NMR spectrometer (9.4 T) at room temperature. Solution 1H NMR were conducted with DMSO as the deuterated reagent.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping measurements were carried out on a JEOL JEM-ARM 300 F field-emission electron microscope operated at 200 kV acceleration voltage.

Time-of-Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) profiles were obtained by using a ToF-SIMS 5–100 (Ion-ToF GmbH) instrument in positive and negative mode, which was equipped with Ar-gas cluster sputtering ion source (5 keV) and Bi3+ ion beam as the analysis beam (30 keV). The analyzed area and the sputtered area were 100*100 μm2 and 300*300 μm2, respectively.
