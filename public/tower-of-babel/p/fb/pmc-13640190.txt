Medicolegal autopsy reconstruction of a medication-related adverse event trajectory in a psychiatric inpatient with diabetes: a case report
Authors: Hiroshi Tsutsumi, Ako Sasao, Kyoko Hirata, Ryota Hiraiwa, Yuki Ohtsu, Satoko Kimura, Rie Sano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse drug events are common in psychiatric inpatient care because polypharmacy is frequent and metabolic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus are prevalent. When nonspecific symptoms and reduced food intake are not promptly managed, clinically significant metabolic deterioration may be missed. We report a medicolegal autopsy case in which a review of nursing records, a postmortem investigation, and toxicology helped reconstruct a medication-related adverse event trajectory in a psychiatric inpatient with diabetes.Case presentationA 47-year-old Japanese woman of Asian ethnicity with bipolar disorder, insomnia, and a 12-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus was hospitalized in a psychiatric ward where medications were centrally managed. No blood tests were performed during hospitalization. From 8 days before death, nursing records documented frequent nonspecific symptoms and recurrent hypoglycemia. Her oral intake decreased from the evening of 2 days before death and became minimal after breakfast 1 day before death. She was found unresponsive and was pronounced dead, and the cause of death could not be determined clinically, including after postmortem computed tomography at another hospital. A medicolegal autopsy showed food material in the airway and bronchioles, with congested and edematous lungs, suggesting aspiration-related asphyxia. A femoral venous blood sample showed a metformin concentration of 46 mg/L and trihexyphenidyl concentration of 0.45 mg/L. The vitreous glucose concentration was markedly low at 0.33 mmol/L (6 mg/dL) in the left eye and 0.39 mmol/L (7 mg/dL) in the right eye. Our findings suggest that a medicolegal autopsy and postmortem toxicology can help reconstruct clinically relevant adverse event pathways when an inpatient death remains unexplained.

Background

Adverse drug events and medication errors are common among psychiatric inpatients, in whom polypharmacy is frequent and clinical monitoring may be challenging [1, 2]. In addition, people with severe mental illness have a substantially higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus than the general population [3]. Therefore, antidiabetic therapy is common in psychiatric settings.

During acute illness or reduced oral intake, the risk of medication-related metabolic deterioration may increase, particularly when diabetes treatment is continued without timely reassessment [4]. Sick day medication guidance emphasizes temporary withholding of selected agents, including metformin and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, together with intensified monitoring during dehydration, vomiting, or markedly reduced food intake [5, 6]. However, in psychiatric inpatient care, early metabolic deterioration may be difficult to recognize when symptoms are nonspecific.

We report a clinically unexplained inpatient death in which a review of nursing records, a medicolegal autopsy, postmortem chemistry, and toxicology helped reconstruct a plausible medication-related adverse event trajectory. The case is clinically informative because it shows how reduced food intake, recurrent hypoglycemia, ongoing antidiabetic therapy, and aspiration-related findings can be considered together when reconstructing the pathway leading to death in a psychiatric inpatient with diabetes.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 47-year-old Japanese woman of Asian ethnicity with bipolar disorder and insomnia had a 12-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus without documented nephropathy, retinopathy, or neuropathy. Two months before her death, laboratory tests showed the following: creatinine concentration, 50 µmol/L (0.57 mg/dL); estimated glomerular filtration rate, 87.8 mL/minute/1.73 m2; hemoglobin A1c value, 49 mmol/mol (6.6%); albumin concentration, 33 g/L (3.3 g/dL); white blood cell count, 9.4 × 10⁹/L (9,400/µL); red blood cell count, 4.48 × 1012/L (4.48 × 10⁶/µL); aspartate aminotransferase concentration, 0.317 µkat/L (19 U/L); alanine aminotransferase concentration, 0.300 µkat/L (18 U/L); and gamma-glutamyl transferase concentration, 0.517 µkat/L (31 U/L).

The patient was admitted to a psychiatric hospital for the first time. She had been treated for approximately 2 years as an outpatient by an internist under a diagnosis of depression, and bipolar disorder was diagnosed at approximately the time of psychiatric admission.

Medication management and regimen

Medication was centrally managed by the ward. Antidiabetic medications and other internal medicine medications prescribed at another hospital were continued during this psychiatric hospitalization. According to the nursing records, scheduled oral medications were taken as usual until after breakfast 1 day before death. No blood tests were performed during hospitalization, and the reason for this lack of tests was not documented in the available medical records. Consequently, no inpatient renal function data were available. Whether a sick day protocol was in place or applied during this admission could not be determined from the available records. Prodromal symptoms and glucosee trend

From 8 days before the patient’s death, the nursing records documented frequent nonspecific symptoms, including malaise, nausea, dizziness or unsteadiness, incoherent conversation, vomiting, pallor, and marked unsteadiness. Serial capillary glucose measurements showed values < 3.9 mmol/L (< 70 mg/dL) 8 and 2 days before death, and hypoglycemic values became frequent from 1 day before death (Fig. 1). Food intake and insulin administration

The patient’s meal intake decreased beginning in the evening 2 days before death. From breakfast 1 day before death onward, she was unable to eat most meals. The nursing record documented the last administration of insulin aspart (8 units) at 18:30 hours 4 days before death and the last administration of insulin degludec (10 units after waking) at 06:30 hours 2 days before death, when the capillary glucose concentration was 3.5 mmol/L (63 mg/dL). No record indicated intentional overadministration.

Death, referral for medicolegal autopsy, postmortem interval, and sampling

The patient was found unresponsive and was pronounced dead at 06:13 hours on the day of death. Postmortem computed tomography was performed at another hospital, but the cause of death could not be determined. Therefore, a medicolegal autopsy was performed under the Act on the Investigation of Cause of Death and Identification of Bodies Handled by the Police and Related Agencies in Japan. The interval from confirmation of death to the start of the autopsy was approximately 50 hours. Femoral venous blood was collected at autopsy, stored at 4 °C, and analyzed for toxicology.

Autopsy findings

The decedent’s height was 167 cm and weight was 85.9 kg at the autopsy. Food material was present in the airway, and the lungs were congested and edematous (right, 654 g; left, 608 g). The gastric mucosa was congested and contained approximately 200 mL of gastric contents, without visible tablet residue. The liver showed fatty change (1,498 g), and the kidneys were grossly unremarkable.

Histopathology identified food residue within the bronchioles, which suggested aspiration-related asphyxia. Additional microscopic findings included hepatic steatosis, pancreatic islet hyalinosis, and glomerulosclerosis. Baseline swallowing function, the functional status, and neurological abnormalities were not described in the available records. However, a regular diet was provided during hospitalization, and no aspiration episodes were documented before the terminal event.

Toxicology

Toxicological screening indicated intoxication with metformin and trihexyphenidyl, and a quantitative analysis was performed following the method described by Sasao et al. [13]. Briefly, samples were diluted 5- and tenfold with distilled water and extracted using the Micro Volume QuEChERS Kit™ (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). A 5 µL aliquot of the extracted sample was injected into a liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry system for analysis. Blank swine blood was used as the calibration matrix, and metformin and trihexyphenidyl concentrations of calibration samples were prepared at 0, 1.25, 2.5, and 5 µg/mL and at 0, 0.0625, 0.125, and 0.25 µg/mL, respectively. Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry was performed using a Shimadzu LCMS-8040 instrument with an electrospray ionization and Phenomenex Kinetex column (2.1 mm diameter × 100 mm length, 2.6 µm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). The mobile phase was as follows: 10 mM ammonium formate water and 0.1% formic acid for solvent A, and 10 mM ammonium formate methanol and 0.1% formic acid for solvent B. Solvent B was increased from 5 to 95% against solvent A over 10 minutes and held for 5 minutes. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/minute. A quantitative analysis was performed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry in the multiple reaction monitoring mode using the following transitions: metformin, m/z 130.1 → 60.0; trihexyphenidyl, m/z 302.2 → 98.2; and diazepam-d5 (internal standard), m/z 290.15 → 154.05. The metformin concentration in femoral venous blood was 46 mg/L. The trihexyphenidyl concentration in femoral venous blood was 0.45 mg/L.

Targeted postmortem chemistry

The hemoglobin A1c value, measured in National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program units, was 6.1% at the hospital that performed postmortem computed tomography. Acetone concentrations were 67.3 mg/L in cardiac blood, 100.6 mg/L in femoral venous blood, 54.3 mg/L in urine, and 80.5 mg/L in gastric contents. Vitreous humor glucose concentrations were 0.33 mmol/L (6 mg/dL) in the left eye and 0.39 mmol/L (7 mg/dL) in the right eye. No additional targeted postmortem chemistry data, such as vitreous lactate, β-hydroxybutyrate, or electrolytes, were available.

Discussion and conclusions

This medicolegal autopsy case is best interpreted as a complex medication-related adverse event trajectory in a psychiatric inpatient with diabetes, rather than as proof of fatal intoxication from a single drug. Nursing records documented frequent nonspecific symptoms beginning 8 days before death, a clear decline in oral intake beginning 2 days before death, and recurrent hypoglycemia during the terminal period (Fig. 1). An autopsy and histopathology identified food material in the airway and bronchioles, indicating aspiration-related asphyxia as the proximate mechanism of death. Postmortem toxicology showed clinically significant exposure to metformin and trihexyphenidyl, and vitreous glucose concentrations were markedly low, which suggested severe metabolic deterioration [9].

The hypoglycemia in this case was likely multifactorial. The reduced food intake and continued antidiabetic therapy were likely important contributors, and the residual effect of insulin degludec remained plausible because of the last documented administration 2 days before death. Metformin may also have contributed to the hypoglycemia, particularly if dehydration or acute kidney injury developed during hospitalization, although inpatient renal function data were unavailable [10, 11]. In contrast, trihexyphenidyl was not thought to be a direct cause of hypoglycemia or aspiration, but it may have contributed indirectly through an anticholinergic burden or impairment of alertness [12]. Therefore, the postmortem toxicology findings should be interpreted as supporting contributory drug exposure rather than establishing sole causality.

Alternative metabolic mechanisms of the hypoglycemia cannot be fully excluded. Postmortem lactate, pH, and β-hydroxybutyrate data were unavailable. Therefore, we could not differentiate metformin-associated lactic acidosis from starvation ketosis, diabetic ketoacidosis, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor-associated ketoacidosis, or other catabolic states. The elevated acetone concentrations suggested a ketotic state but were not specific [7, 8]. In addition, because no blood tests were performed during hospitalization and the reason for this was not documented, the mechanism and timing of metabolic deterioration could not be assessed directly.

This case also illustrates the role of medicolegal investigation in clinically unexplained inpatient deaths. In this patient, clinical evaluation, including postmortem computed tomography at another hospital, did not determine the cause of death. A review of nursing records, autopsy findings, postmortem chemistry, and toxicology enabled reconstruction of a plausible pathway leading to death. Such reconstruction may help identify clinically relevant prevention targets even when antemortem laboratory data are lacking.

This report has several limitations. First, no inpatient blood tests or terminal antemortem laboratory data were available, including renal function, the acid–base status, lactate, electrolytes, and ketone bodies. Second, detailed documentation of meal quantities and whether scheduled insulin doses were withheld because of reduced intake or low glucose values was not available beyond the nursing records. Third, baseline swallowing function, the functional status, and neurological abnormalities were not documented, although a regular diet had been provided and no aspiration episodes had been recorded before the terminal event. Finally, interpretation of postmortem drug concentrations is inherently limited, particularly after an approximately 50 hours postmortem interval, and should be integrated with the clinical context and autopsy findings.

In conclusion, this case suggests a clinically important medication-related adverse event trajectory in a psychiatric inpatient with diabetes, in whom reduced food intake, recurrent hypoglycemia, ongoing antidiabetic therapy, and possible contribution of metformin exposure preceded terminal aspiration-related asphyxia. These findings indicate the importance of prompt treatment when psychiatric inpatients with diabetes develop nonspecific symptoms and reduced food intake, together with timely reassessment of antidiabetic therapy and sick day management. Additionally, a medicolegal autopsy can clarify plausible pathways to death and inform prevention strategies when the cause of inpatient death remains clinically unexplained.
