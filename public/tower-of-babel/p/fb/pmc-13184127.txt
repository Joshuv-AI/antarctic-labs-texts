Rising Mortality from Sleep Apnea and Hypertension in U.S. Adults: A 23-Year Epidemiological Analysis And Impact of the COVID-19 Pandemic (2000–2023)
Authors: Mohid Zulfiqar, Muhammad Tahir, Hassan Abdul Aziz Dhedhi, Ammad Uddin, Muhammad Khalid Afridi, Hermann Yokolo, Saad Ahmed Waqas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) and hypertension (HTN) are prevalent, interrelated conditions associated with significant cardiovascular morbidity. Despite their rising burden, mortality trends for their co-occurrence remain underexplored.ObjectiveTo assess U.S. mortality trends related to OSA and HTN from 2000 to 2023, analyzing demographic, geographic, and temporal patterns.MethodsMortality data for adults aged ≥ 25 were extracted from the CDC WONDER database. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 and annual percent changes (APCs) were calculated using joinpoint regression, stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, age, urbanization, and Census regions.ResultsFrom 2000 to 2023, 127,668 deaths were attributed to OSA and HTN. AAMRs rose from 0.44 (95% CI: 0.41–0.47) in 2000 to 4.87 (95% CI: 4.79–4.96) in 2023, with an average annual percent change of + 11.55% (95% CI: 10.41–12.70). Men had higher mortality rates than women (2023: 7.05 vs. 3.05). Non-Hispanic Black adults exhibited the highest AAMRs, followed by Non-Hispanic Whites and Hispanics. Adults ≥ 65 years accounted for 62.4% of deaths. Rural areas had higher mortality than metropolitan areas (2.04 vs. 1.69). States like Oregon had rates nearly five times higher than New York. Mortality peaked in 2018–2021, aligning with the COVID-19 pandemic. Heart disease was the leading underlying cause.ConclusionsOSA and HTN-related mortality has surged over two decades, with pronounced disparities by sex, race, age, and region. Targeted prevention, screening, and intervention strategies are urgently needed, particularly for elderly, minority, and rural populations.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00552-9.

Introduction

In recent decades, Hypertension (HTN) has emerged as one of the most pressing global health challenges, representing a “silent killer” that affects an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years worldwide, with most (two-thirds) living in low- and middle-income countries [1]. This condition serves as the leading cause of cardiovascular disease and premature death globally, contributing significantly to the burden of stroke, heart attack, and heart failure [2]. Despite significant advances in treatment modalities, only 54% of adults with HTN are diagnosed, 42% receive treatment, and a mere 21% have their HTN controlled [3].

Concurrent with the rising prevalence of HTN, Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) has become increasingly recognized as a major contributing factor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. OSA affects a substantial portion of the population, with prevalence rates varying by demographic characteristics and diagnostic criteria used [4]. The relationship between OSA and HTN is particularly concerning, with studies demonstrating a strong bidirectional association between these conditions [5]. Furthermore, OSA significantly increases cardiovascular risk, with research showing elevated rates of mortality and cardiovascular events among affected individuals [6].

The pathophysiological mechanisms linking OSA and HTN are well-established and bidirectional. OSA is characterized by recurrent episodes of upper airway obstruction during sleep, leading to intermittent hypoxia, hypercapnia, and arousal from sleep, which trigger sympathetic nervous system activation and subsequent blood pressure elevation [7]. Prospective studies have demonstrated that sleep-disordered breathing is independently associated with the development of HTN, with a dose-response relationship between apnea severity and HTN risk [8]. Population-based research has shown that individuals with moderate to severe OSA have significantly increased odds of developing HTN compared to those without sleep-disordered breathing [9].

While the link between OSA and HTN is well established, limited data exist on the long-term trends, demographic patterns, and mortality burden of this comorbidity at the national level. This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of HTN-related mortality among individuals with OSA in the United States from 2000 to 2023 using CDC WONDER data. By examining temporal trends, regional variations, and disparities by race, sex, and geography, this analysis addresses a critical gap in the literature. Additionally, it evaluates the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on mortality trends in this high-risk population, offering timely insights for public health planning and clinical management.

Methods

Population and Study Design

The CDC Wonder (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research) database was employed to retrieve death certificates on OSA and HTN-related mortality from 2000 to 2023 [10]. Mortality data were obtained from the CDC WONDER Multiple Cause of Death Section. Deaths were included when both OSA (ICD-10 code G47.3) and HTN (ICD-10 codes I10–I15) were listed anywhere on the same death certificate within the multiple cause-of-death fields, including as either the underlying cause or contributing causes, among individuals aged 25 years and older. This approach was used because OSA is infrequently reported as the underlying cause of death, and the multiple cause-of-death framework captures all conditions documented on death certificates. Given that the data were extracted from a publicly accessible database, institutional review board approval was not required. This study was conducted in accordance with the reporting guidelines established by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) standards [11].

Data Abstraction

The data extracted for analysis specifically included variables such as gender, race, age groups, urbanization level, state, and census region. Gender categories included male and female. Racial groups were classified as NH White, NH Black or African American, and Hispanic or Latino. This classification follows the categories established by the CDC WONDER database and has been used in prior studies. The dataset incorporates cause-of-mortality information from all 50 states and the District of Columbia, primarily focusing on adults aged 25 years and older. Age groups were characterized as 25–44 years (younger adults), 45–64 years (middle-aged adults), and 65 + years (older adults). Based on the 2013 U.S. Census classification, the population was categorized by urbanization level into urban (large metropolitan, medium/small metropolitan) and rural (population < 50,000) counties [12]. Census regions were split into Northeast, Midwest, South, and West, under the regional definitions provided by the U.S. Census Bureau.

Statistical Analysis

To analyze national trajectories in HTN and HTN-associated mortality, we computed the crude and age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) per 100,000 population from 2000 to 2023, disaggregated by year, gender, race, age group, census region, and from 2000 to 2020 for urban-rural status, underlying cause of death, and states, employing a 95% confidence interval (CI). The U.S. population from the year 2000 was used as the baseline population for calculating age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) [13]. Furthermore, to assess changes in mortality related to OSA and HTN the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.3.0 National Cancer Institute) was employed throughout the study period [14]. Annual percent change (APC) and its corresponding 95% CI in the AAMRs were computed using this software. Substantial shifts in annual mortality trends were identified by fitting linear segments at points where evident changes over time were observed. The Monte Carlo permutation method was utilized to determine the annual percentage change (APC) in AAMRs, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To indicate the overall trend in mortality from 2000 to 2023, the average annual percentage change (AAPC) was calculated as a weighted mean of the APCs, with associated 95% CIs. A two-tailed t-test (test for parallelism) was performed to determine whether the APC and AAPC values indicated an increase or decrease in deaths during the study period. A p-value of < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of the primary findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using an alternative cause-of-death coding approach. In the primary analysis, both OSA (ICD-10 code G47.3) and HTN (ICD-10 codes I10–I15) were identified as multiple causes of death. In the sensitivity analysis, HTN was designated as the underlying cause of death, while OSA was included as a contributing (multiple) cause of death. Age-adjusted mortality rates and joinpoint regression analyses were performed using the same methodology as the primary analysis to assess whether the directional trends observed were consistent across different coding frameworks.

Results

A total of 127,668 OSA and HTN-Related deaths occurred among adults (aged ≥ 25 years) between 2000 and 2023. Information on the location of death was available for 127,485 deaths. Of these, 39.22% occurred in medical facilities, 10.81% occurred in nursing homes/long-term care facilities, 3.05% occurred in hospices, 42.63% at decedent’s home, and others (3.82%). Of the total deaths, 34.87% (44,413) were accounted for by Females while 65.13% (83,225) were accounted for by Males. By race/ethnicity, 99,015 deaths were among the White population, 19,422 were among the Black population, and 7,067 were among the Hispanics. Age-wise analysis showed 6,372 (4.99%) deaths in Young Adults [25–44], 41,591 (32.58%) in Middle Aged-Adults (45–64), and 79,705 (62.43%) deaths in Older Adults (65+) (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Annual Stratified

The AAMR for OSA and HTN-Related death in adults was 0.44 (95% CI: 0.41–0.47) in 2000 and 4.87 (95% CI: 4.79–4.96) in 2023, with an AAPC of + 11.55% (95% CI: 10.41 to 12.70; p < 0.001). The overall AAMR had a massive increase from 2000 to 2005 (APC: 19.94, 95% CI: 15.46 to 24.58; p < 0.001), followed by a significant rise from 2005 to 2018 (APC: 9.15, 95% CI: 8.70 to 9.60; p < 0.001). It then surged tremendously from 2018 to 2021 (APC: 20.20, 95% CI: 14.28 to 26.43; p < 0.001) before finally declining between 2021 and 2023 (APC: -4.18, 95% CI: -7.82 to -0.40; p = 0.033). In the sensitivity analysis, where HTN was designated as the underlying cause of death and OSA as a contributing cause of death, the AAMR increased from 0.1 per 100,000 in 2000 to 0.7 per 100,000 in 2023. Joinpoint regression identified one joinpoint at 2013 (95% CI: 2009–2020). The APC was 6.38 (95% CI: 2.04–10.91; p = 0.006) from 2000 to 2013, which then increased to 11.96 (95% CI: 9.82–14.14; p < 0.001) from 2013 to 2023. The AAPC over the full study period was 8.77 (95% CI: 6.26–11.34; p < 0.001).

Age Stratified

Elderly adults consistently showed higher AAMRs related to OSA and HTN compared to other age groups throughout the study period. In the young adults [25–44] age group, the AAMR increased from 0.11 (95% CI: 0.09–0.14) in 2000 to 0.58 (95% CI: 0.53–0.63) in 2023, with an average annual percent change (AAPC) of + 7.41% (95% CI: 6.44–8.40; p < 0.001). Similarly, in the middle-aged adults (45–64) age group, the AAMR rose from 0.56 (95% CI: 0.50–0.62) in 2000 to 3.66 (95% CI: 3.53–3.78) in 2023, with an AAPC of + 9.15% (95% CI: 7.84–10.47; p < 0.001). The elderly adults (65+) age group experienced the highest increase, with the AAMR rising from 1.0 (95% CI: 0.89–1.10) in 2000 to 17.13 (95% CI: 16.79–17.47) in 2023, with an AAPC of + 13.18% (95% CI: 12.06–14.32; p < 0.001).

AAMRs varied significantly across age groups. Among younger adults, the AAMR spiked from 2000 to 2023 (APC: 7.41; 95% CI: 6.44 to 8.40) (p value < 0.001). For the middle-aged adults, AAMR experienced a dramatic surge from 2000 to 2005 (APC: 18.51; 95% CI: 14.18 to 23.01) (p value < 0.001), followed by an increase from 2005 to 2018 (APC: 6.75; 95% CI: 6.19 to 7.31) (p value = 0.005), and a major rise from 2018 to 2021(APC: 18.35; 95% CI: 10.61 to 26.62) (p value < 0.001). AAMR declined from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -9.08; 95% CI: -14.20 to -3.66) (p value = 0.004). Among older adults, the AAMR showed a significant rise from 2000 to 2007 (APC: 18.43; 95% CI: 15.72 to 21.20) (p value < 0.001). The rate rose from 2007 to 2018 (APC: 10.64; 95% CI: 9.90 to 11.38) (p value < 0.001), followed by a tremendous hike from 2018 to 2021 (APC: 21.55; 95% CI: 15.37 to 28.06) (p value < 0.001). and then a minor decrease from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -1.66; 95% CI: -5.90 to 2.77) (p value = 0.427) (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 3).

Gender Stratified

Men consistently had higher AAMRs than women throughout the study period. In 2000, the AAMR for men was 0.6 (95% CI: 0.55–0.65), which increased to 7.05 (95% CI: 6.90 to 7.20) in 2023 (AAPC: +11.64%; 95% CI: 10.35 to 12.94; p < 0.001). Similarly, the AAMR for women increased from 0.28 (95% CI: 0.24 − 0.31) in 2000 to 3.05 (95% CI: 2.96 to 3.14) in 2023 (AAPC: +11.61%; 95% CI: 10.16 to 13.09; p < 0.001).

The AAMR for adult men had a tremendous hike from 2000 to 2005 (APC: 19.82; 95% CI: 15.01 to 24.83) (p value < 0.001), followed by a significant hike in AAMR trend from 2005 to 2018 (APC: 9.45; 95% CI: 8.87 to 10.02) (p value = 0.019), and from 2018 to 2021(APC: 19.93; 95% Cl: 13.20 to 27.07) (p value < 0.001). Lastly, it declined from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -4.44; 95% Cl: -9.05 to 0.41) (p value = 0.069). Congruently, the AAMR for adult women initially showed a sharp increase from 2000 to 2006 (APC: 19.86; 95% CI: 15.82 to 24.04) (p value < 0.001), followed by a large uptick between 2006 and 2018 (APC: 8.10; 95% CI: 7.27 to 8.93) (p value < 0.001), and a massive rise from 2018 to 2021 (APC: 20.93; 95% Cl: 12.77 to 29.69) (p value < 0.001). The AAMR then witnessed a decline from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -3.17; 95% Cl: -9.11 to 3.16) (p value = 0.292). (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 4).

Race Stratified

The AAMRs for OSA and HTN-related deaths were highest among NH Black or African American individuals, followed by NH whites, and then Hispanics. Specifically, the AAMR for NH Black or African American individuals was 3.07 (95% CI: 2.85 to 3.29), while NH White individuals had an AAMR of 2.25 (95% CI: 2.18 to 2.31), and Hispanics had the lowest AAMR of 1.19 (95% Cl: 1.05 to 1.34). The data indicates that NH Black or African American adults had the highest AAMR, with an increase observed from 2000 to 2023 with an AAPC of 8.25% (95% CI: 6.47 to 10.06; p < 0.001). Similarly, NH White adults experienced an increase in AAMR with an AAPC of 12.71% (95% Cl: 11.55 to 13.88, p < 0.001), followed by Hispanic or Latino adults with an AAPC of 8.52%; (95% CI: 6.23 to 10.86; p < 0.001).

The NH blacks experienced an increase in AAMR from 2000 to 2009 and from 2009 to 2018 with APCs of 11.33 and 5.19, respectively. After which the AAMR surged from 2018 to 2021 (APC: 21.77; 95% CI: 9.93 to 34.90) (p value < 0.001), before finally declining from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -8.99; 95% CI: -16.61 to -0.67) (p value = 0.037). In the NH Whites, the AAMR surged from 2000 to 2006 (APC: 20.94; 95% CI: 17.46 to 24.52) (p value < 0.001), followed by a large increase from 2006 to 2018 (APC: 9.47; 95% CI: 8.82 to 10.13) (p value < 0.001), and a major rise from 2018 to 2021 (APC: 20.56; 95% CI: 14.36 to 27.09) (p value < 0.001). The AAMR then declined from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -1.75; 95% CI: -5.99 to 2.69) (p value = 0.404). In Hispanics, the AAMR rose from 2000 to 2018 (APC: 9.21; 95% CI: 8.07 to 10.35) (p value < 0.001), followed by a massive uptick from 2018 to 2021 (APC: 19.97; 95% CI: 4.09 to 38.28) (p value = 0.015), and then a decline from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -11.81; 95% CI: -22.37 to 0.20) (p value = 0.053) (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 5).

Urbanization Stratified

Non-metropolitan areas exhibited higher OSA and HTN-related AAMRs than metropolitan areas from 2000 till 2020, with overall AAMRs of 2.04 (95% CI: 1.91–2.18) and 1.69 (95% CI: 1.63–1.74), respectively. The AAMRs in non-metropolitan areas demonstrated a more pronounced increase over the study period from 2000 to 2020, with an AAPC of + 14.07% (95% CI: 12.97 − 15.19; p < 0.001) for non-metropolitan and an AAPC of + 12.38% (95% CI: 11.14–13.64; p < 0.001) for metropolitan (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 6).

State Stratified

A significant difference in AAMRs for OSA and HTN-related conditions was observed across different states from 2000 till 2020, with the AAMRs ranging from 0.80 (95% CI: 0.76–0.83) in New York to 3.95 (95% CI: 3.79–4.11) in Oregon. States that fell into the top 90th percentile were Oregon, Wyoming, Minnesota, South Dakota, and Montana, which had significantly higher AAMRs compared to states in the lower 10th percentile, such as New York, Connecticut, Nevada, Georgia, and Massachusetts (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 7).

Census Region Stratified

Adults in different census regions consistently showed varying AAMRs related to OSA and HTN throughout the study period. In the Northeast, the AAMR increased from 0.32 (95% CI: 0.26–0.38) in 2000 to 3.30 (95% CI: 3.14–3.46) in 2023, with an AAPC of + 10.92% (95% CI: 9.07–12.80; p < 0.001). Similarly, in the Midwest, the AAMR rose from 0.47 (95% CI: 0.40–0.53) in 2000 to 5.84 (95% CI: 5.64–6.04) in 2023, with an AAPC of + 11.98% (95% CI: 10.97–13.00; p < 0.001). In the South, the AAMR increased from 0.44 (95% CI: 0.38–0.49) in 2000 to 4.68 (95% CI: 4.55–4.81) in 2023, with an AAPC of + 10.76% (95% CI: 9.31–12.23; p < 0.001). The West also saw a rise in AAMR from 0.44 (95% CI: 0.37–0.51) in 2000 to 5.59 (95% CI: 5.41–5.78) in 2023, with an AAPC of + 11.74% (95% CI: 9.82–13.70; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Tables 8 and 10).

Underlying Causes of Death

We analyzed the top 15 leading underlying causes of death among patients with OSA and HTN from 2000 till 2020. Our findings indicate that heart disease was the most prevalent cause, with an AAMR of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.67–0.68), followed by diabetes mellitus with an AAMR of 0.19 (95% CI: 0.18–0.19) and chronic lower respiratory diseases with an AAMR of 0.19 (95% CI: 0.18–0.19). Malignant neoplasms were the fourth leading cause with an AAMR of 0.13 (95% CI: 0.13–0.13) (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

Our study presents an in-depth analysis of OSA and HTN-Related mortality trends in U.S. adults from 2000 to 2023. Several significant points were uncovered. The Age-Adjusted Mortality Rate (AAMR) of OSA and HTN-Related deaths significantly increased during the two-decade period, rising sixfold, reaching its peak between 2018 and 2021, followed by a decline between 2021 and 2023. Mortality rates consistently had higher rates among men than among women, with men experiencing almost double the number of deaths. Significant racial disparities were observed as NH Black adults had the highest AAMR, followed by NH Whites and Hispanics. Older adults (65 years and above) had the highest burden of mortality, the Midwest and the Western regions had the highest increase compared to the Northeast and South. Rural (non-metropolitan) communities had disproportionately greater mortality rates than metropolitan communities. Finally, heart disease was identified as the most common underlying cause of death, pointing to the complex relationship between cardiovascular impairment and the comorbidity of HTN and OSA. The robustness of these findings was further supported by the sensitivity analysis. Although the absolute AAMRs were expectedly lower under this more restrictive coding framework, the directional trend remained consistent, with the AAMR rising more than seven-fold from 0.1 per 100,000 in 2000 to 0.7 per 100,000 in 2023 and an AAPC of 8.77% (95% CI: 6.26–11.34; p < 0.001). The attenuation in magnitude is anticipated, as restricting HTN to the underlying cause of death captures a narrower subset of OSA-HTN-related deaths compared to the multiple-cause-of-death approach employed in the primary analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4). Collectively, these findings confirm that the sustained upward mortality trend associated with the co-occurrence of OSA and HTN is not an artifact of cause-of-death coding methodology, but rather reflects a genuine and persistent increase in mortality burden over the study period (Table 1).

More recent research has also detected notable Sex disparities in OSA mortality and HTN. Men’s Age-Adjusted Mortality Rates (AAMRs) for OSA deaths are uniformly higher than those among women [15]. There may be several explanations for this disparity. Epidemiological research indicates men are twice as likely to acquire OSA through variations in fat distribution, airway structure, and hormonal regulation [16]. Testosterone affects upper airway collapsibility, with estrogen and progesterone having protective effects, accounting for reduced prevalence and severity among premenopausal women [17, 18]. Outside of biology, lifestyle and behavioral factors come into play. Men are more likely to be smokers, alcoholics, and have an unhealthy diet, all of which exacerbate HTN and reduce the quality of sleep [19]. Men also have poorer healthcare-seeking behavior than women, which can delay diagnosis and treatment of both conditions [20]. Psychological stressors and poor coping mechanisms further enhance cardiovascular risk [21]. These sex-specific risk factors follow the pattern of mortality and underscore the need for specially targeted awareness and screening programs among men, and especially those in middle age who are undiagnosed but are already accumulating subclinical cardiovascular damage [22].

There were dramatic racial disparities in our analysis. The highest AAMRs were in NH Black adults, followed by NH Whites and then Hispanics. There are several intersecting factors likely to be responsible for these results. NH Black adults have a greater burden of obesity and metabolic syndrome, both of which confer a large increase in risk for OSA and HTN [23]. Black adolescents are also disproportionately impacted by sleep-disordered breathing, laying the groundwork for earlier and more severe development of these conditions in adulthood [24]. Environmental and social determinants of health also further amplify these inequities. Black Americans are disproportionately seated in socioeconomically disadvantaged communities that are characterized by greater exposure to environmental pollutants, greater noise levels, and psychosocial stressors such as racial discrimination [25]. These stressors are the etiology of poorer quality of sleep and higher blood pressure variability [26]. Inadequate access to specialty medical care, underdiagnosis of OSA, and poor blood pressure contribute to these hazards [27].

Although Hispanics recorded the lowest AAMRs, they were not exempt from rising trends, especially 2018–2021, which recorded a steep increase followed by a decline. Differences in cultural health practices, occupational risks, and access to health care may partially explain the trend [28]. Together, these findings support addressing structural inequities and the necessity to develop interventions to racially marginalized populations.

Geographic variation was a unique characteristic of this research. The adults living in the Midwest and West had the highest AAMRs, while the Northeast had relatively low rates. Various contextual factors can be responsible for these regional differences. The most obese region in the United States is the Midwest, which raises the risk for both OSA and HTN [29]. In addition, states such as Oregon and Montana had AAMRs almost three times those of states such as New York and Massachusetts, indicative of extreme heterogeneity regarding healthcare delivery, knowledge, and risk factor prevalence [30].

Urban–rural differences also occurred. Non-metropolitan counties had persistently higher mortality rates compared with metropolitan peers. Obstacles to healthcare access such as fewer sleep specialists, longer distances to travel, and rural hospital closure can result in delay in diagnosis and restrict treatment compliance [31]. Rural populations also experience socioeconomic disadvantages, lower health literacy, and increased obesity and smoking prevalence [32, 33]. These forces synergize to intensify the burden of OSA and HTN death in rural America. Notably, the rural disadvantage increased over time, especially after 2005, and mirrors systemic deterioration of rural healthcare infrastructure [34].

Patients 65 years and older accounted for more than 60% of mortality and had age-adjusted mortality rates that were significantly higher than those of younger and middle-aged patients. This is consistent with the cumulative effects of long-term intermittent hypoxia, vascular remodeling, and chronic HTN, which collectively result in major cardiovascular effects later in life [35, 36]. Normal aging-related changes, such as decreased respiratory muscle tone, also add to the severity of OSA [37].

Middle-aged adults also exhibited alarming increasing trends, most notably between 2000 and 2005 and between 2018 and 2021, when AAMRs increased abruptly. This age group is of public health concern, since midlife OSA and HTN, if untreated, significantly elevate the risk of premature cardiovascular disease [38, 39]. While younger adults had the lowest burden of mortality, the consistent increase in AAMRs in this group is ominous, indicative of early disease onset and the necessity for active screening among them [40].

Heart disease was the top underlying cause of death in hypertensive patients with OSA, followed by malignancies and chronic respiratory disease [41, 42, 43]. The high prevalence of cardiovascular disease aligns with established pathophysiologic links between HTN, OSA, and conditions such as coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, and heart failure [44]. Notably, resistant HTN, common in overweight or obese patients with untreated OSA promotes cardiac remodeling and increases ischemic burden, accounting for the sudden increase in cardiovascular mortality [45].

The prevalence of malignant neoplasms as a frequent causative factor is also important. Recent evidence suggests that chronic intermittent hypoxia secondary to OSA can allow tumor growth and angiogenesis through mechanisms of oxidative stress and systemic inflammation; however, this association must be investigated [46]. Chronic lower respiratory diseases will most likely be indicative of common risk factors, such as smoking and obesity and reduced pulmonary function, which enhance the impact of both OSA and HTN [47].

The dramatic increase in mortality due to OSA and HTN has far-reaching implications both for policy and clinical care. The evidence provides support for routine screening for OSA in hypertensive patients, particularly those with resistant HTN [45]. Questionnaires such as the STOP-BANG and others offer inexpensive and simple means of early diagnosis [48], Increased compliance with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy is needed [49]. Though with proven efficacy, CPAP adherence is low, with non-adherence rates exceeding 30% [50]. Public education campaigns on the cardiovascular effects of untreated OSA may stimulate greater acceptance of treatment [51].

Sleep screening needs to be incorporated into standard HTN care by primary care physicians and cardiologists, establishing multidisciplinary care pathways [52]. Moreover, interventions need to be targeted at high-risk populations: Black populations, rural dwellers, and older adults need to be targeted for outreach, education, and subsidized care [53]. Lastly, policies to improve rural healthcare infrastructure, increase insurance coverage, and facilitate training of healthcare personnel could eradicate geographic disparities [54].

The COVID-19 pandemic has had an important effect on the rise in mortality linked to OSA and HTN between 2020 and 2022, as shown in Fig. 4. The age-adjusted mortality rate in this period was observed as being approximately three times higher than in the years 2000 to 2019. Patients with these comorbid conditions had greater susceptibility to severe COVID-19, due to impaired cardiopulmonary reserve, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [55]. At the same time, interruptions in access to care and lifestyle changes, such as weight gain and increased levels of stress, probably worsened health outcomes [56]. Moreover, COVID-19 infection may be associated with further exacerbated sleep-disordered breathing and chronic HTN [57]. Together, these factors provide a possible explanation for the rise in mortality associated with the early years of the pandemic.

This study takes full advantage of the strengths provided by a large, nationally representative data set covering more than two decades. The length of observation, coupled with stratification by race, sex, age, and geography, allowed close examination of mortality differences. There are, nonetheless, some limitations to remember. Reliability of CDC WONDER depends on accuracy of death certificate information, which might underestimate or misclassify OSA. Comorbidities, severity of OSA, treatment compliance, and socioeconomic status could not be ascertained. Cause of death is frequently multifactorial, and relative contribution of HTN and OSA cannot always be separated. Additionally, trends for States, Underlying cause of death, and urban-rural classification were restricted to the years 2000–2020, as analysis beyond these years was not feasible within the scope of the current study. Furthermore, while the 2020–2022 mortality peak temporally aligns with the COVID-19 pandemic, our analysis cannot establish direct causality, as the association remains ecological and observational. Unmeasured confounders, including pandemic-related healthcare disruptions and behavioral changes, may have independently contributed to the observed mortality surge. Also worthy of note is that the observed increase in AAMRs may not solely reflect a true rise in mortality burden. Improved clinical awareness of OSA, wider availability of sleep diagnostic testing, and evolving death certificate coding practices over the study period may have contributed to more frequent documentation of OSA on death certificates, potentially inflating the observed trends. As such, some portion of the apparent increase may represent enhanced recognition and reporting rather than an absolute increase in OSA and HTN-related mortality.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals a substantial and sustained increase in OSA– and HTN-related mortality in the U.S. from 2000 to 2023, disproportionately affecting men, Black adults, older individuals, and rural populations. Heart disease remains the most prevalent underlying cause of death, emphasizing the cardiovascular dangers of this comorbidity. Although a modest decline in mortality was observed after 2021, the overall burden remains alarmingly high. These findings underscore the urgency of improving awareness, enhancing screening and diagnosis, and implementing targeted interventions to reduce mortality in high-risk groups. Addressing the dual epidemic of OSA and HTN will require coordinated efforts across clinical practice, public health policy, and community engagement.
