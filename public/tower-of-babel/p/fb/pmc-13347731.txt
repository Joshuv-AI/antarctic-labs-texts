Serum Zonulin Levels in Patients with Heroin Addiction
Authors: Yusuf Cokunlu, Kamile Yucel, Ikbal Inanli, Sevdenur Kahraman, Aykut Senol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesOpioid use has been associated with alterations in gut–brain axis signaling and intestinal barrier function. Zonulin, a key regulator of intestinal permeability, has been implicated in various neuropsychiatric and inflammatory conditions; however, its role in heroin addiction and remission remains poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate serum zonulin levels in patients with heroin addiction (HA) during active heroin use (AHU) and remission (R) and to compare these levels with those of healthy controls (C).MethodsA total of 104 male participants were included: patients with AHU, patients in remission under buprenorphine/naloxone maintenance treatment, and C. Serum zonulin levels were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Group comparisons and correlation analyses were performed, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate the discriminatory value of zonulin.ResultsThe scores of addiction diagnostic criteria, the severity of craving, and the total API score were significantly higher in the AHU group than in the R group. The difference between the groups was significant when the R, AHU, and C groups were compared for zonulin levels (p=0.02). This difference was due to zonulin levels being higher in the R group than in the C group. When the HA (R+AHU) and C groups were compared for zonulin levels, zonulin levels were significantly higher in the HA group than in the C group (p=0.00). In the ROC analysis, the highest area under the curve (AUC) values were obtained in the R-C group comparison and the HA-C group comparison (AUC=0.683 and AUC=0.650, respectively).ConclusionThese findings suggest that increased intestinal permeability, as reflected by elevated serum zonulin levels, may persist during the remission phase of heroin addiction. However, given the cross-sectional design and the fact that remission was defined as a medication-stabilized state under buprenorphine treatment, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Longitudinal studies incorporating follow-up assessments and gut microbiota analyses are warranted to clarify the temporal dynamics and clinical significance of zonulin alterations in heroin addiction.

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is defined as the chronic use or abuse of prescribed or illicit opioids.[1-3] OUD, particularly heroin dependence, remains a major health and social issue worldwide.[4,5] To combat opioid dependence and achieve successful treatment outcomes, it is crucial to understand the mechanisms underlying the pathological changes resulting from opioid use.

The gut microbiome comprises the microorganisms living in the human gastrointestinal system, along with their genes and metabolites. Gut permeability and microbiome alterations have been associated with several neuropsychiatric disorders, including OUD.[6-10] Many gastrointestinal symptoms, including constipation, leaky gut, bloating, nausea, and vomiting, can occur with opioid use.[11] Clinical and preclinical studies have shown that opioid administration disrupts intestinal epithelial integrity and increases barrier permeability. Increased intestinal permeability triggers a systemic inflammatory response and neuroinflammation by facilitating the entry of bacteria and toxic metabolites into the systemic circulation. People with OUD have been shown to develop withdrawal symptoms and tolerance as a result of this type of neuroinflammation.[12-17]

The intestinal barrier is formed by tight junctions (TJs) between intestinal epithelial cells.[18,19] Zonulin is the precursor protein of haptoglobin-2 and regulates the function of TJs by binding to specific receptors on epithelial cells in the jejunum and distal ileum. By activating the epidermal growth factor receptor, it causes disintegration of TJs, transient loosening of intercellular junctions, and increased intestinal permeability. When zonulin levels increase, these junctions relax, and intestinal permeability increases. With this property, zonulin is accepted as a clinical indicator of intestinal permeability.[20,21] The relationship of zonulin with multiple sclerosis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, ankylosing spondylitis, celiac disease, and irritable bowel syndrome has been investigated, and it has been reported that increased intestinal permeability plays a role in the etiopathogenesis of these diseases.[22-25]

In recent years, there has been growing interest in the relationship between gut permeability and psychiatric disorders. Circulating zonulin levels have been investigated in relation to several psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and autism.[26–29] As opioid use has been shown to disrupt intestinal epithelial integrity and increase intestinal permeability, patients with heroin addiction (HA) may have elevated zonulin levels, which could be associated with clinical symptoms. Through the gut–brain axis, opioid use affects intestinal motility, microbiota composition, and mucosal inflammation, which can lead to impaired intestinal barrier function. As zonulin is a key regulator of tight junction permeability, it represents a biologically relevant marker for investigating alterations in intestinal permeability in opioid addiction. Although inflammatory and metabolic markers have been studied in addiction, intestinal permeability via zonulin has not yet been examined. Therefore, this study aimed to compare serum zonulin levels in patients with HA with those in age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HC) and to determine their relationship with clinical features indicating addiction severity.

Methods

Study design

We designed this study prospectively. Following ethics committee and institutional approval from the hospital, the study was initiated. It included 50 patients who applied to the Alcohol and Drug Research, Treatment, and Training Centre at the Beyhekim Psychiatric Department of Konya Health Sciences University Training and Research Hospital between December 1, 2023, and March 12, 2024. Heroin dependence was diagnosed using clinical interviews conducted by experienced psychiatrists according to the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5).[2] Patients and their relatives were interviewed to obtain detailed clinical and sociodemographic information. All patients underwent urine screening for various substances. Opioids, buprenorphine, benzodiazepines, cocaine, cannabinoids, synthetic cannabinoids, and amphetamines were investigated. Only male patients with positive urine results for opioids or buprenorphine were included in the study. Twenty-four patients were active heroin users (AHU), as identified by clinical interview and confirmed by urine opiate testing. Remission status was defined according to the early remission criteria of the DSM-5. Twenty-six patients who had not met any of the diagnostic criteria for opioid use disorder, except for excessive craving, for between 3 and 12 months were included in the remission group. All patients in remission were receiving buprenorphine/naloxone stabilization treatment. Fifty-four age-matched healthy male volunteers with no history of disease or drug use were included in the study as the control group.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: refusal to participate in the study; major psychiatric disorders, such as autism, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and intellectual disability; chronic diseases, such as metabolic diseases, autoimmune diseases, inflammatory and non-inflammatory gastrointestinal diseases, malignancy, and neurodegenerative diseases; obesity (BMI>30); active infection within the last month; and use of corticosteroids or drugs affecting the immune system within the last 6 months.

The Addiction Profile Index (API) scale was used to assess individuals with HA and determine the different dimensions and severity of addiction.[30] The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of all participants were recorded by clinicians using a data form prepared by the researchers. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after the nature and importance of the study had been explained to them.

The Addiction Profile Index (API)

The Addiction Profile Index is a self-administered questionnaire consisting of 37 questions divided into 5 subscales. These subscales measure the characteristics of substance use, diagnostic criteria for addiction, the impact of substance use on an individual’s life, the severity of cravings, and motivation to stop using substances. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.89 for the total API, ranging from 0.63 to 0.86 across the subscales. Item-total correlation coefficients ranged from 0.42 to 0.89. [30]

Biochemical analysis

Serum zonulin levels were measured using ELISA. No invasive procedures were performed during the study. Once the laboratory procedures had been completed for each group, the remaining serum samples were collected in Eppendorf tubes and stored at -80°C until the ELISA test was performed. For the zonulin assay, the sample was removed from the freezer and thawed at room temperature. Zonulin levels were measured in the samples in accordance with the instructions for the ELISA kit (Cat. No. E-EL-H5560, Elabscience, Wuhan, China). The zonulin study was performed at the KTO Karatay University Research Laboratory. The results were analyzed using a Multiskan Sky Thermo device (USA). A Combi Wash (Human) washing machine was used in the study. The assay sensitivity was 0.47 ng/mL, with a range of 0.78–50 ng/mL, and the r2 value of the calibration curve was 0.99.

Statistical analysis of data

The data were entered and analyzed using SPSS for Windows, version 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Visual methods, including histograms and probability plots, and analytical methods, including the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests, were used to analyze the conformity of the data to a normal distribution. The mean, standard deviation, and median (1st quartile–3rd quartile) were used to summarize numerical data, whereas frequency distributions and percentages were used for categorical data. An independent samples t-test was used to compare 2 independent groups of normally distributed numerical data, and one-way ANOVA was used to compare more than 2 groups. For groups with a significant difference according to one-way ANOVA, the post hoc Tamhane test was used to determine the responsible group. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare non-normally distributed numerical data between 2 groups. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess 3 or more non-normally distributed groups with numerical data. A post hoc Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction was used for pairwise comparisons between groups with significant Kruskal–Wallis test results. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to analyze correlations between numerical variables that were not normally distributed. Spearman correlation coefficients were interpreted as follows: no correlation below 0.19, weak correlation between 0.20 and 0.39, moderate correlation between 0.40 and 0.69, high correlation between 0.70 and 0.89, and very high correlation above 0.90. Categorical data were compared using the chi-squared test.

As there were significant differences in body mass index (BMI) between the groups, which showed a positive correlation with serum zonulin levels, additional analyses were conducted to determine whether group differences in zonulin levels were independent of BMI. As zonulin values were not normally distributed, Quade rank analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), the nonparametric equivalent of ANCOVA, was performed. Zonulin was used as the dependent variable, group was used as the fixed factor, and BMI was used as the covariate. For this analysis, zonulin values were first regressed against BMI, and unstandardized residuals were then obtained. These residuals were then ranked across the entire sample, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to the ranked residuals. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction when appropriate.

The diagnostic decision-making properties of zonulin levels in predicting disease were analyzed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Significant cutoff values were determined using the Youden index (max[sensitivity+specificity−1]). The cutoff values were analyzed in terms of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. In addition to p-values, effect size measures were reported for all analyses to quantify the magnitude of the differences. For normally distributed continuous variables, Cohen’s d was calculated for independent samples t-tests, and η 2 (eta-squared) values were reported for one-way ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis tests. For non-normally distributed pairwise group comparisons performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, effect sizes were expressed as r (r=Z/√n). For categorical variables analyzed using chi-square tests, effect sizes were reported using Phi (φ) or Cramer’s V coefficients. Effect sizes were interpreted according to conventional thresholds: small, medium, and large.

The minimum required sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7. For a t-test comparing 2 independent groups, assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d=0.50), two-tailed α=0.05, and power (1−β)=0.80, the required total sample size was n=102 (51 participants per group). The final sample size for this study (50 patients with heroin addiction and 54 healthy controls, total n=104) met and slightly exceeded this requirement. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Pharmaceutical and Non-Medical Device Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Karatay University, Konya, Türkiye (Ethics Committee No. 2023/002, Meeting No. 10, Date: October 27, 2023). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The study included a total of 104 male participants, including 50 patients diagnosed with HA (26 R and 24 AHU) and 54 C. The mean age of all participants was 32.56 years (range, 17.00–56.00). The mean age of the AHU group was lower than that of the C group (p<0.05), but there was no difference in age between the groups when patients diagnosed with HA (R+AHU) were compared with controls (p=0.053). The number of years smoked was significantly higher in both the AHU and R groups than in the C group. All patients in the R and AHU groups who used IV heroin also used heroin by inhalation. When the groups were analyzed in terms of BMI, a significant difference was observed, with higher BMI values in the C group than in the AHU group. Discussion

In this study, we investigated serum zonulin levels in patients with heroin addiction and compared them with those of a healthy control group. Our findings revealed that serum zonulin concentrations were notably higher in individuals with heroin addiction, especially those in remission, than in the control group. This suggests that intestinal barrier dysfunction may persist beyond the period of active heroin use, even during clinical remission and treatment. However, although patients in remission exhibited significantly higher zonulin levels than the control group, no significant difference was observed between active heroin users and the control group. These results reveal an unexpected pattern of intestinal permeability alterations across different stages of heroin use.

Although median zonulin levels were higher in active heroin users than in patients in remission, this difference was not statistically significant. This apparent discrepancy is likely due to the high variability observed within the active heroin user group, as reflected by the wide interquartile range. Therefore, the lack of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of lower zonulin levels in active users but rather as an indication of the varied intestinal permeability responses experienced during ongoing heroin use.

One of the most intriguing results of this study was the finding that serum zonulin levels were significantly higher in patients in remission than in healthy controls, but not in active heroin users. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Owing to the cross-sectional design of the study and the heterogeneity of remission duration (3 to 12 months), it is not possible to determine whether elevated zonulin levels reflect persistent impairment of the intestinal barrier or a transient state during recovery. Furthermore, it is important to note that all patients in the remission group were receiving buprenorphine/naloxone maintenance treatment. Therefore, the remission phase examined in this study represents a medication-stabilized state rather than full abstinence. Consequently, the observed zonulin elevation may reflect a combination of post-cessation physiological changes and ongoing pharmacological effects rather than being a harmful biological consequence of remission itself.

Notably, there was no significant difference in zonulin levels between active heroin users and the control group, despite elevated levels being found in patients in remission. One possible explanation for this finding is that acute or ongoing opioid exposure may transiently suppress immune activation and intestinal permeability through opioid receptor-mediated mechanisms. However, this remains a speculative interpretation, as the present study did not measure inflammatory cytokines or other immune markers. Furthermore, buprenorphine treatment during remission may independently influence gut motility, mucosal immunity, and barrier integrity. Therefore, any mechanistic interpretation regarding differential zonulin levels across disease stages should be approached cautiously.

Zonulin is a physiological modulator of intercellular tight junctions and is widely considered a biomarker of increased intestinal permeability, also known as “leaky gut.” Substance use has been shown to disrupt the integrity of the intestinal barrier through various mechanisms, such as altered gut motility, mucosal immune dysregulation, and changes in the composition of the gut microbiota.[31-33] Long-term opioid use has been associated with sphincter dysfunction, dysmotility, and reduced mucus secretion, all of which may compromise intestinal integrity. Previous studies have emphasized the important role of alterations in intestinal permeability and microbiota in the clinical manifestations of heroin addiction.[15] Preclinical studies have demonstrated that chronic heroin exposure leads to significant alterations in gut microbiota composition and tight junction protein expression. In animal models, long-term heroin use has been shown to disrupt the gut microbiota and reduce the expression of zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), a key tight junction protein, compared with healthy controls.[15,32,33] These findings support the idea that heroin use affects the molecular integrity of tight junctions, resulting in sustained intestinal barrier dysfunction.

In our study, group differences in serum zonulin levels were primarily observed in patients in remission. The persistence of increased zonulin levels during this period suggests that intestinal barrier dysfunction may not fully normalize after heroin use ceases. However, all patients in remission were also receiving buprenorphine/naloxone therapy. Buprenorphine is a partial agonist at the μ-opioid receptor and is an effective drug for the maintenance treatment of heroin dependence. Multiple studies have shown that opioid treatment also has a negative impact on intestinal barrier function. This prolonged “leaky gut” state could contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation and ongoing dysregulation of the gut–brain axis, which could influence long-term neuropsychiatric outcomes and vulnerability to relapse. Zonulin-mediated disruption of tight junctions is a key mechanism underlying increased intestinal permeability in chronic inflammatory and neuropsychiatric conditions. In the context of heroin addiction, opioid-induced alterations in gut motility, mucosal immunity, and microbiota composition may lead to sustained activation of the zonulin pathway. Therefore, zonulin may serve as a potential biomarker reflecting disrupted intestinal barrier function in patients with heroin addiction, with persistently elevated levels being observed even during periods of clinical remission.

In addition to these primary findings, a moderate positive correlation was observed between serum zonulin levels and BMI in the groups of people with heroin addiction and active heroin users. People with heroin addiction are known to be at increased risk of malnutrition and low BMI due to factors such as poor nutrition and social deterioration.[34,35] Previous studies have shown that people who inject drugs tend to have lower BMI values than the general population, a finding supported by our results, particularly in the active heroin user group. Although obese individuals were excluded from the study, previous literature has demonstrated that circulating inflammatory cytokines associated with increased BMI may contribute to intestinal barrier dysfunction by altering the structure and localization of tight junctions [36,37] and that serum zonulin levels increase with rising BMI. The BMI–zonulin correlation observed in our study is consistent with these findings and suggests that metabolic factors, alongside substance-related mechanisms, may contribute to alterations in intestinal permeability in heroin addiction.

Importantly, despite the positive correlation between BMI and zonulin, zonulin levels were significantly higher in both the remission and active heroin user groups than in controls, as revealed by BMI-adjusted analyses. This indicates that the observed differences between the groups cannot be attributed solely to BMI or BMI-related metabolic mechanisms. It also supports the notion that additional BMI-independent pathways contribute to intestinal barrier dysfunction in heroin addiction.

As expected, clinically significant differences were observed between active heroin users and patients in remission with regard to addiction severity, craving, and motivation. Active heroin users had significantly higher craving and addiction severity scores, whereas the remission group had higher motivation scores. These findings are consistent with previous evidence indicating reduced craving and increased motivation during remission.[38] Despite these clinical differences, serum zonulin levels were similar between active users and patients in remission. Although the correlation between zonulin levels and addiction severity scores did not reach statistical significance, the observed positive trend suggests a potential association that may become clearer in studies with larger sample sizes. Taken together, these findings imply that zonulin may reflect biological alterations associated with heroin addiction that are not directly proportional to clinical severity measures.

ROC curve analysis revealed that serum zonulin exhibited moderate diagnostic efficacy in distinguishing patients with heroin addiction, particularly those in remission, from healthy controls. Although the sensitivity values were relatively low, the specificity was notable, suggesting that zonulin may be more useful as a confirmatory biomarker than as a screening biomarker. Although zonulin alone is unlikely to serve as a standalone diagnostic marker, it could be beneficial as part of a multibiomarker panel reflecting intestinal permeability and gut–brain axis dysfunction in heroin addiction. To our knowledge, zonulin levels have not previously been evaluated in patients with heroin addiction, limiting the possibility of direct comparison with existing literature. Nevertheless, our findings add to the existing body of evidence regarding altered intestinal permeability in substance use disorders and shed new light on the persistence of gut barrier dysfunction during remission.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, it was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size and employed a cross-sectional design, which precludes causal or longitudinal interpretations. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of male participants, limiting the generalizability of the findings. In addition, although the groups were assessed for smoking and alcohol consumption, uncontrollable factors such as genetic characteristics, family history, lifestyle, dietary habits, microbiota variability, smoking intensity, and other psychosocial factors that could affect gut permeability could not be assessed. Although major clinical confounders were controlled for and comprehensive urine screening was performed at study entry, the potential influence of lifestyle-related factors, microbiota variability, and concurrent or intermittent use of other substances, such as benzodiazepines or cannabinoids, cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, the elevated zonulin levels observed in the remission group may reflect the combined effects of medication-assisted treatment and residual substance exposure rather than the biological state of complete abstinence. Despite these limitations, as the first study to examine serum zonulin levels in heroin addiction, our findings should be considered preliminary and warrant confirmation in larger, multicenter, prospective studies incorporating longitudinal follow-up and microbiota assessments.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to investigate the potential role of zonulin as a marker of intestinal permeability in patients with heroin addiction. Despite the limitations of our study, we found that serum zonulin levels were significantly higher in patients with heroin addiction than in healthy controls, with particularly elevated levels observed in patients in remission. These results imply that intestinal barrier dysfunction may persist beyond the period of active heroin use, even when the patient is clinically abstinent and undergoing treatment. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate serum zonulin levels in patients with heroin addiction, providing preliminary evidence of altered gut permeability in this population.

Overall, our results suggest that zonulin could be a useful biomarker for identifying disrupted intestinal barrier function in patients with heroin addiction, with persistently high levels even during remission. Notably, the observed elevation in serum zonulin levels remained significant after adjustment for BMI, suggesting that the link between heroin addiction and intestinal barrier dysfunction is not solely driven by BMI-related metabolic factors. However, zonulin alone is unlikely to be sufficient as a standalone marker, and its clinical utility may be greater when combined with other biomarkers related to gut permeability and inflammation. Further multicenter, prospective studies involving larger and more diverse populations are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between heroin use and intestinal barrier dysfunction and to establish whether elevated zonulin levels are a cause or consequence of chronic substance exposure.
