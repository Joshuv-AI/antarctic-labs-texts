Harnessing artificial intelligence for environmental sustainability via human capital and renewable energy
Authors: Zeeshan Khan, Numan Khan, Xufeng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the current era, the role of artificial intelligence is considered important in many aspects not only for environmental sustainability but also for sustainable development in general. Considering the importance of artificial intelligence, this study aims to investigate the impact of artificial intelligence on carbon emissions for a group of twenty-one panel countries by using data from 2010 to 2020. The study employed second-generation panel data econometric tests with nonparametric panel data tests for estimations. The results highlighted that the role of artificial intelligence is crucial for achieving a sustainable environment, as its coefficient is negative for all three models. Specifically, AI has a negative and significant influence on CO2 emissions in Model 1 but a nonsignificant influence in Model 2 across quantiles. A 1% change in the AIPTNTS will cause a decrease in CO2 emissions of -0.009 in Q25, -0.047% in the median quantile, -0.13% in Q75 and − 0.18% in Q90, with a high significance of 1% in Model. This finding suggests that AI largely reduces CO2 emissions in high-carbon-emitting economies. Moreover, income causes carbon emissions to increase; however, the nonlinear term of income curbs carbon emissions, confirming the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC) hypothesis. The effects of human capital, clean energy and renewable energy on carbon emissions are negative, i.e., they help to decrease carbon emissions. The interactive or joint effect of artificial intelligence and clean energy electricity output also helps to decrease carbon emissions in selected countries. In terms of policy recommendations, the study encourages investment in artificial intelligence, increasing the share of clean energy and renewable energy electricity in total energy shares and investment in human capital to achieve a sustainable environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20613-6.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Clean energy, Interactive effect, Human capital, Clean energy electricity Subject terms: Climate-change ecology, Environmental economics

Introduction

Climate change has become a significant concern for countries worldwide, especially those pursuing rapid economic growth and relying on fossil fuels. World leaders gathered in Dubai in 2023 at the Conference of Parties 28 to design policies for reducing fossil fuels and increasing renewable energy technologies. In addition, 2023 was considered the hottest year in the most recent decade, especially after, and most of its focus and attention are now focused on clean energy and the boosting of energy-efficient startups to reduce severe climate change1. Many nations are implementing measures to address this issue and its adverse consequences on the climate of planet earth.

Among these measures, some scholars and policymakers believe that artificial intelligence is one of the key factors that has received increasing attention from climate bodies and world political leaders because of its new robotic, machine learning and blockchain technologies2–7. They claim that AI patents have been adopted as one of the fastest among other technologies around the world. Figure 1 provides information on the countries applying for AI patents and investing more in new technologies, which can ultimately lead to a reduction in carbon emissions. Among these countries, China, Denmark, the United States, and Japan have remained at the top for the application and adoption of new AI technologies8. However, countries focusing on AI technologies were not part of the debate in early studies, and combating climate change through AI and its technologies has been largely ignored, particularly in the developing part of the world9–11. Earlier papers estimated that renewable and clean energy technologies can be used to combat climate change and improve environmental stability by reducing carbon emissions.

Previous studies have focused mostly on developed parts of the world, but the cross-sectional influences between developed and developing countries have remained unexplored in the current literature. To achieve a consensus on its policy recommendations and obtain rationales for targeting environmental degradation on the basis of the role of AI among these diverse sets of countries. For example, Fig. 2 provides the statistics of AI patents of diverse economies around the world in different industries and sectors that target sustainable development and the environment. However, the adverse impact of AI on climate has been recorded by a recent number of studies claiming that the introduction of modules and operation of AI results in greater emissions than production through fossil fuels and nonrenewable energy sources12. In addition to the controversial point of view, it is interesting to analyze the impact of AI in heterogeneous economies to support the idea of a sustainable environment. Moreover, renewable energy has remained the key determinant of carbon emissions worldwide13–15. Hence, it would be interesting to explore the indirect effect of artificial intelligence on CO2 emissions through renewable electricity production (RELTP)16,17. However, to date, the moderation effect of renewable electricity has not been analyzed as an energy variable with AI in previous studies. Moreover, the literature has ignored the cross-sectional dependency between economies and heterogeneity issues that may be presented within panel data.

The connections between AI, the environment and clean energy are subsequently proposed and versatile. In lieu of AI, advanced technologies, machine learning applications and their algorithms help in tracking and mapping forests, ensuring data replication for data-scarce regions, which in turn enables the efficacy of environmental sustainability and overall sustainable development initiatives (N. Khan et al., 2024). Similarly, advanced technologies such as AI reduce CO2 emissions and encourage ecological stability in developing and developed economies18,19. However, the linkages among these variables have not been explored in previous studies that specifically targeted determinants of CO2 emissions or environmental degradation9,11 and AI in terms of productivity and economic efficiency20,21 without emphasizing the crucial role of AI in the environment and the subsequent role of clean energy. Hence, there is limited evidence on the relationships between AI, clean energy, and the environment in developing and developed economies. Moreover, few studies have explored the direct and interactive effects of clean energy on CO2 emissions, hence addressing this gap in knowledge concerning the joint effects of AI and clean energy on CO2 emissions in a multi country (panel) context.

Concerning the gaps that exist in literature, our primary objective is to investigate the role of artificial intelligence and to understand whether AI contributes to environmental sustainability. We included a balanced panel dataset of twenty countries from 2010 to 2020. In addition, we check the role of renewable electricity production through the impact of AI on CO2 emissions. We found that AI has a favorable role in the reduction of CO2 emissions, and more investments and a focus on AI patents around the world should be adopted because of their overall and high magnitude in our diverse sample of economies. Similarly, we found that the interaction of AI with renewable electricity production is positive and adverse because of the lower percentage of renewable energy and high share of fossil fuels, which supports the adoption of AI and therefore increases CO2 emissions in these economies.

In this context, the motivation of this study subsequently arises from the persistent need for changes in AI, clean energy and the environment to reach sustainable development goals, particularly those related to technological advancements, environmental management and the energy transition. Moreover, despite existing policies and international agreements on environmental protection and sustainable development, many targets remain unmet, and progress toward carbon neutrality has been insufficient. Therefore, it is crucial to underscore the importance of AI and its leveraging technologies along with clean energy to accelerate the transition toward low-carbon economies. Furthermore, understanding the importance of AI, which can play a crucial role in improving the supply chain, productivity, efficiency and enhancement of climate monitoring, and quantifying evidence of AI related to environmental benefits and reducing CO2 emissions in the top AI-adopted economies is still rare. Henceforth, underscoring how AI and clean energy influence CO2 emissions is critical for formulating strategies regarding effective policies and outputs that can steer sustainable development and SDGs.

The diverse economies, which include OECD and non-OECD economies (major developing economies)1, are at the forefront of tackling climate change and environmental deterioration. These strategically balanced groups of economies are highly advanced in technology and are considered the global leaders in artificial intelligence, such as the US, Germany, Japan, China, and South Korea. Moreover, countries such as Denmark, Finland and the Netherlands are considered for their resilience against climate change and environmental deterioration. Finally, the inclusion of OECD economies and major developing economies such as China, Brazil, and India provides a strategically balanced study because several of these nations are the largest carbon emitters, making their inclusion vital and providing comparative insights related to AI, clean energy and CO2 emissions across the globe.

This study contributes to literature in several ways. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is one of the novel studies, which analyze the impact of AI on CO2 emissions for a group of developed and developing countries. Additionally, the study evaluated the interaction and joint effects of AI and renewable electricity (AIRELTP) on environmental sustainability. Second, most of the studies have ignored cross-sectional dependency and heterogeneity in their panels, which could lead to biased estimations, but addressing these issues in a diverse and large panel is important. Finally, the outcomes of this study may be useful for policy since this study categorically provides numerical measures of AI on CO2 emissions and divides them on the basis of different percentiles to obtain low- and high-impact outcomes.

Literature review and hypothesis development

Studies in the literature concerning the environment have been presented from technological and clean innovation perspectives. However, research on artificial intelligence and its role in sustainable development is a new dimension that needs to be explored. The mixed literature on AI and sustainable environments is specifically divided into three strands.

The first strand of previous work shows that the high influence of AI is better for sustainability and achieving a sustainable environment. For example22, concluded that AI and the use of industrial robots can offset emissions and further limit urban CO2 emissions. At the firm level23, modeled a baseline regression method and checked the role of AI in the corporate sustainability of Chinese firms. This study calculates the positive influence of AI on corporate sustainability. In addition, the study showed that AI supports environmental governance and social responsibility. AI generally supports sustainable development goals and reduces CO2 emissions. In a more in-depth study of industrial structure and energy intensity, 8 evaluated the role of AI as a transmission channel and reported that stimulating the use of AI in industrial operations can reduce energy intensity and increase energy efficiency. This, in turn, will lead to sustainable energy consumption and lower CO2 emissions from the industrial sector. More broadly, (Lucas, 2022) showed that AI can combat climate change and target fossil fuel reduction with alternative technologies that increase the sustainability of the environment while reducing CO2 emissions. In addition, 6 reported that, related to previous work on the effects of AI on climate change, the author concluded that AI has different mechanisms and heterogeneity in combating emissions.

Another strand of literature reveals both the adverse impact of AI on the environment by increasing CO2 emissions and the weak impact of AI on environmental quality. Moreover, AI reduces regional emissions and depends on energy consumption, which further suggests that AI is not recommended for sustainable environments and development (Kopka & Grashof, 2022). In addition, with respect to regional heterogeneity, there is inequality in the effects of AI on sustainability and its indicators. 3 show that AI has different and heterogeneous effects on sustainability indicators and provides diverse outcomes for sustainable practices.

Since the climate change dilemma exists in the literature and many technological aspects have been studied in relation to it, the impact of AI on CO2 emissions to check environmental sustainability should be considered to contribute more to the literature in this dimension. Moreover, owing to the heterogeneity of the outcomes in the previous outcomes, we employed diverse panel datasets to observe the overall impact of AI on CO2 emissions. Furthermore, we utilized second-generation panel techniques and considered cross-sectional dependency and heterogeneity in the data.

Factors affecting CO2 emissions

In addition to the primary influence of AI on CO2 emissions, we adopted controls that we checked could influence the environment. Renewable energy consumption, economic growth, renewable electricity, and human capital are determinants of the environment. Specifically24, renewable energy consumption is one of the significant determinants of CO2 emissions and highly decreases environmental degradation in developed economies. Similar outcomes and perspectives have been reported by25–27. According to28,29, renewable electricity has had dynamic effects on environment- and climate-related economic challenges. Moreover30–32, proposed that renewable electricity is one of the main contributors to the environment. In relation to the influence of development and progression, a growing number of studies in the literature have proposed that GDP can strongly direct changes in CO2 emissions (i.e., from an environmental perspective), for example33–37. In addition, 5reported that economic progression and technological innovation play significant roles in reducing CO2 emissions in most of the studies tested in the literature. Similar results have been reported in previous studies (i.e38–41.,).

Literature gap

This study differs from previous studies in many aspects. First, a growing body of literature is presented on technology, energy, growth, and resources, but new dimensions need to be explored to obtain a novel perspective for combating higher emissions and the challenges of climate change; for this purpose, we will use artificial intelligence and its role in combating higher emissions. Second, in addition to the novelty of artificial intelligence, we explored the channels and mechanisms through which AI could indirectly affect CO2 emissions. Third, we have included appropriate and applicable panel methods that investigate and address pertinent issues that could lead to biased and distorted outcomes (i.e., ignoring cross-sectional dependency and heterogeneity issues).

Unit root testing

Prior to explaining the unit root, we first check for cross-sectional dependency (CD) and slope homogeneity (SH) issues in the panel data. Notably, the identification of CD and SH issues is important for determining and employing correct unit root tests (URTs). These URTs include 1st−, 2nd− and 3rd -generation tests that target and resolve these issues50. To understand the cross-sectional dependency (CD) issue, countries are expected to share different traits, be somewhat dependent on each other and share similar qualities; hence, ignoring this issue and taking panel data cross-sectionally independently will create spurious regression and result in incorrect specifications of unit root tests and cointegration51,52. Hence, to properly resolve this issue, we employed the CD diagnostic test developed by53. Another issue that should be diagnosed before employing the unit root is the slope homogeneity issue in panel data. Countries with different characteristics can show homogeneity in the data, such as consistent effects of variables across countries, which poses a problem for panel data estimation. Cointegration testing

The integration of the variables allows us to determine the cointegration of these variables. The cointegration approach is necessary before we use our primary and main methods for the study. Second-generation panel unit root tests, such as the Kao (1999) cointegration test, address cross-sectional dependency and slope heterogeneity issues. Moreover, the Kao test ensures and addresses every point of the panel data variables and their long-term equilibrium. When no cointegration is found or exists in the variables, its null hypothesis remains stable, whereas the alternative hypothesis is for long-run cointegration among variables.

In addition, many previous studies claim that the inclusion of AI in the economy promotes a sustainable environment because it promotes the use of efficient technologies, advanced algorithms, and machine learning equations, which helps in tracking recycling projects and ensures that the mapping of deforestation to vanishing practices involves unsustainable practices.

Moreover, AI is a new solution in the current era because it targets sustainable development and sustainable practices ranging from industry to households, and it helps in providing technologies that are efficient and clean to reduce the use of traditional practices that cause emissions. In industry, AI has become a new tool that can avoid supply disruptions due to its powerful algorithms, ensure the tracking of goods and services while reducing the cost of transportation and pollution and, in turn, target highly sustainable environments. Similar work in this area of AI has been undertaken by the (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019; M. Lucas,2022; Wang et al., 2023). Our findings are consistent with the economic theory of technological innovation, which suggests that increasing technological expansion and installing advanced technologies promote energy efficiency, which in turn reduces the use of energy consumption and enhances carbon emissions. This theoretical explanation is especially true for the current situation of AI, as the world has increased its focus on artificial intelligence by replacing old and obsolete technologies with renewable energy robots or energy-efficient robots, machine learning and mapping techniques that target less pollution and waste of products and services and, in turn, promote a sustainable environment. In addition, in our sample of countries that are a mixture of developed and developing countries, it has been confirmed that promoting and adopting AI patents significantly improves environmental indicators and supports the idea that AI can combat climate change in different parts of the world. These findings are analogous to many of the findings in our literature review section.

Theoretically, from an energy point of view, renewable electricity is produced from the sun, wind, and turbines, which replenish itself from nature and emit zero to less emissions than fossil fuels do. Another argument is that an increase in the RELTP significantly reduces the cost of fossil fuels, which in turn will empower businesses and households to opt for energy options with low carbon emissions and greater benefits in terms of health and efficiency.

The outcomes suggest that rapid economic expansion cannot be achieved without the use of fossil fuels because these dirty resources enable most of the economy’s development and enable industrial operations along with energy generation for transport and households. Since fossil fuels are closely tied to greenhouse gas emissions and pollution, they increase carbon emissions. However, with increasing energy intensity and high economic development, more economic growth can be achieved with less use of energy, resulting in an inverted U shape in the economy. In this way, the peak in economic development will face an increase in carbon emissions and then a decline, which theoretically follows the environmental Kuznets curve. To represent this in our study, we took the GDP squared, which has a negative and significant effect on CO2 emissions across quantiles, particularly in Model 3 (Table 7). The outcomes of GDP and GDP squared are consistent with the conclusions of26,69–71.

Concerning human capital and its relationship with CO2 emissions, we find that the human capital index is highly significant and negative across quantiles in the 3 models reported in Tables 6, 7 and 8. Theoretically, according to the Solow growth model, strong economic development and advanced research and development (R&D) are driven by internal factors such as human capital. A strong and skilled workforce is likely to choose energy-efficient and cost-effective technologies to maximize output. Additionally, an increase in the number of skilled laborers enhances economic productivity via the use of clean and efficient technologies. Concurrently, R&D advancements have led to new methods and procedures that are both cost- and energy-efficient, ultimately reducing CO2 emissions. The outcomes of HCIs are consistent with the findings of42,70,72.

Conclusion and policy implications

Artificial intelligence has remained the key factor in technologies and machine learning; however, the role of AI has not been discussed mainly from an environmental sustainability perspective, particularly in mixed panels comprising developing and developed countries. We investigate the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on CO2 emissions in a diverse panel of economies from 2010 to 2020. Renewable energy consumption, renewable electricity production (RELTP), GDP and the human capital index are used as control variables along with the moderation of AI and RELTP (i.e., AIRELTP) in this study. First- and second-generation unit root and cointegration tests were employed to check for stationarity and long-term equilibrium between CO2 emissions and independent variables. We find that AI has been the key tool in achieving a sustainable environment. The significant increase in AI activity, especially in the upper quantiles, highlights substantial advancements in the field. This is further evidenced by the growing number of AI-related patents being filed in both developing and developed regions globally. However, the moderation of AI and RELTP remained positive and significant across quantiles despite their negative impacts on CO2 emissions. We also find that GDP has an adverse effect on CO2 emissions, whereas GDP squared has a negative influence across quantiles, indicating the existence of an inverted U-shaped curve in our sample economies. Estimations of renewable energy consumption and the human capital index have a negative influence on CO2 emissions across quantiles. The causal relationships among the variables show that GDP, RECNS, RELTP and HCI exhibit unidirectional causality with respect to the dependent variable of CO2 emissions, whereas AIPTNTS has no causal connection with this variable. However, no variables have bidirectional causality in this study.

The findings of this study could offer valuable policy implications for authorities and decision-makers regarding the use of AI in targeting CO2 emissions and achieving sustainable development goals. First, AI-related patents and funding should be allocated for the targeted reduction in carbon emissions in developed and developing economies. The idea is that enhancing and producing algorithms and models can enhance the monitoring and management of reductions in CO2 emissions. We find that AI could be a strong determinant of CO2 emissions, which provides an indication to climate change authorities to provide tax reductions/benefits or incentives to the private and public sectors, which could enhance AI-related technologies and further benefit the reduction of CO2 emissions. Second, we find that the interaction of AI with renewable electricity increases CO2 emissions, and that the role of AI is less important in the development of renewable energy products. Therefore, we recommend that tax subsidies and incentives be enhanced by policy makers to further increase energy efficiency and reduce CO2 emissions. For practitioners, including environmentalists and financial managers, research has shown that clean energy adoption should be expanded to amplify the effect of AI on CO2 emissions and enhance environmental sustainability while balancing economic expansion. The growing market of clean energy presents benefits that should be utilized to achieve overall sustainable development. For regulators, they should regulate more use of clean energy in different sectors of the economy, ensure the robust reporting of AI applications and its role in cleaner energy to increase environmental sustainability and reduce CO2 emissions. Third, the existence of the EKC curve suggests that increasing economic growth is not detrimental to environmental sustainability in the sample economies; however, traditional production methods should be replaced with efficient technology production methods to increase economic expansion and environmental sustainability. Finally, we found that human capital also plays a pivotal role in reducing CO2 emissions; hence, policy makers should invest in human capital development and training related to new technologies, most importantly AI, and in service sectors, which can further encourage more environmentally friendly opportunities and reduce CO2 emissions.

This study’s findings highlight the importance of how AI is one of the crucial enablers of the environment and highlights compliance with UN SDGs, particularly SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure), SDG 7 (Affordable energy) and SDG 13 (Climate action). Hence, these findings demonstrate the amplifying effect of AI and its role in optimizing clean energy systems, driving innovation and monitoring of environmental activities. Therefore, demonstrating the negative effect of AI on CO2 emissions will ensure overall sustainable development. Governments and policy makers should align these goals to carry out advanced innovation and technology use to amplify its effect on cleaner energy systems and environmental sustainability.

To better understand the role of AI and its combined effect with other variables on the environment, we recommend targeting a broader range of variables and sample countries. Although our selection of economies was based on the high number of AI patents, future research should focus more specifically on environmental factors. Additionally, researchers should consider including policy variables alongside AI in their analysis. This approach is particularly relevant in the contexts of China, the USA, and India and can be explored further via either time series or panel data.
