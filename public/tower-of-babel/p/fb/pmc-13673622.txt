Administration of human macrophage colony-stimulating factor-mFc fusion protein increases macrophage phagocytic capacity and stimulates liver regeneration in mice
Authors: Philip J Starkey Lewis, Jennifer A Cartwright, Lara Campana, Birgit U Baumgarten, Hilmar Ebersbach, Marie-Gabrielle Ludwig, Gregory Marszalek, Benjamin Stutchfield, Rhona Aird, Tak-Yung Man, Eoghan O’Duibhir, Klaus Seuwen, Stuart J Forbes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute and chronic liver diseases are rising contributors of the worldwide healthcare burden. Inflammation plays a key role in progression of both acute and chronic disease, and effective treatments are limited. Macrophages, a diverse population of professional phagocytes, are required for the timely resolution of tissue injury and are an emerging target to treat acute and chronic liver disease. Colony stimulating factor 1 (CSF1), promotes differentiation and survival of macrophages, which are essential in epithelial regeneration, but the CSF1 protein is rapidly eliminated in vivo. We therefore fused human CSF1 genetically to a modified mouse IgG2a Fc region and transiently expressed this in HEK293-6E cells. The protein was purified from culture supernatant to obtain > 97% monomeric purity, suitable for in vivo testing.ResultsThe nascent hCSF1-Fc fusion proteins were evaluated in vitro and in murine models of health and liver disease. hCSF1-mFc is pharmacologically active in healthy mice, resulting in increases in liver and spleen mass closely reflecting changes in tissue macrophage content. In an acetaminophen-induced acute liver injury model, hCSF1-mFc treatment increased hepatic macrophage accumulation at necrotic sites, reduced serum ALT activity, and serum IL-1β levels, and restored white blood cell counts within 72 h. Furthermore, hCSF1-mFc treatment also increased hepatic macrophage numbers in a carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis model corresponding with a striking 3.5-fold increased uptake of a soluble phagocytic label, indicating improved hepatic clearance capacity. hCSF1-mFc was also tested in a model of acute on chronic liver injury, which included a bolus delivery of proinflammatory E. coli-coated bioparticles to pre-existing fibrosis. Here, hCSF1-mFc treatment restored body weight, induced hepatomegaly, and reduced several plasma pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting reduction of systemic inflammation. Finally, immunofluorescence staining showed hCSF1-mFc treatment induced significant hepatocyte proliferation in healthy and all liver disease models.ConclusionThis report demonstrates that hCSF1-mFc treatment increases liver macrophage populations, improves phagocytic capacity, and stimulates hepatocyte proliferation in experimental liver disease models. hCSF1-based fusion protein therapy might represent a novel immune-based strategy to treat acute and chronic liver disease in the clinic.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12950-026-00507-8.Keywords: Macrophages, Phagocytosis, Colony-stimulating factor-1, Carbon tetrachloride, Interleukin-10

Background

Deaths attributed to liver disease continue to rise and represent an increasing significant worldwide healthcare burden [1]. Chronic liver diseases, including cirrhosis of the liver, account for the most cases of liver transplantations performed in the United States and United Kingdom, with an additional number arising to treat acute liver failure (ALF) [2, 3]. Whilst orthotopic liver transplantation is a life-saving treatment for end stage liver disease, it is constrained by a shortage of suitable donor organs, a risk of post-operative complications, and commits patients to lifelong immunosuppression [4]. Therefore, new therapeutic strategies are required to treat end-stage liver disease.

Tissue resident macrophages play an important role in tissue homeostasis where they serve as professional phagocytes to clear noxious agents and also possess trophic functionality to regulate and repair the local tissue microenvironment [5]. Liver resident macrophages (Kupffer cells, [KCs]) are highly phagocytic and provide a critical immunological barrier by efficiently clearing enteric bacteria and their components (e.g. lipopolysaccharides) from the portal blood supply. Recent evidence shows that KCs becomes compromised following acute toxic liver injury (ALI) resulting in a diminished hepatic barrier function [6–8]. Increased bacterial translocation resulting from impaired gut wall integrity predisposes liver disease patients to systemic infections, which are major determinants of mortality in both acute and chronic disease settings [9–13]. Since macrophages play distinct roles in the initiation and resolution of tissue injury and inflammation, and have the propensity to modulate the liver microenvironment, they have been described as potential therapeutic targets for liver disease [14]. Recently, experimental work in pre-clinical models supported by the development of GMP-compliant protocols to manufacture human macrophages have led to clinical trials using macrophages as a cell therapy for liver disease [15–19]. Macrophage colony-stimulating factor (CSF1) is a potent cytokine that controls tissue-resident macrophage populations by stimulating the macrophage CSF1 receptor (CSF1R), together with IL-34, to promote macrophage proliferation and survival [20, 21]. CSF1R-dependent macrophages have been shown to be essential in epithelial regeneration [22]. Previously, in liver disease models, evaluation of CSF1 was constrained by the rapid elimination of the protein in vivo [23]. However, fusion proteins that conjugated animal CSF1 proteins to the Fc region of immunoglobulins were recently described, which conferred a vastly improved pharmacokinetic profile in pre-clinical species [24, 25]. Porcine CSF1-Fc has been shown to induce hepatosplenic macrophage expansion and hepatocyte proliferation in healthy mice [24]. Reversible expansion of tissue macrophages has also been documented in other tissues in response to CSF1-Fc fusion protein with impacts on metabolism [26]. Previous work demonstrated that both porcine CSF1-Fc and human CSF1-Fc treatment increased tissue macrophage number in a mouse thioacetamide-induced liver fibrosis model and increased hepatic proliferation and liver regeneration following injury [27]. These studies demonstrate that CSF1-Fc fusion proteins have potent activity in chronic experimental disease models, but highlight a requirement to better understand the mechanistic action of human CSF1-Fc fusion protein treatment, particularly in acute liver conditions, to broaden its potential clinical utility.

Herein, novel CSF1-Fc fusion proteins were synthesised by conjugating human CSF1 (hCSF1; amino acids 36–190) to either Fc regions of immunoglobulins for either mouse (Fc2a) or human Fc (Fc1P). We tested these nascent fusion proteins have in healthy mice, and in murine models of acute and chronic liver injury to evaluate safety and efficacy. We report that hCSF1-mFc shows potent bioactivity in mice evidenced by a rapid and striking increase in hepatic macrophage numbers within 72 h and led to increased hepatic phagocytic capacity. Furthermore, hCSF1-mFc treatment reduced proinflammatory cytokine concentrations, reduced circulating transaminase levels, and stimulated hepatocyte proliferation in acute and chronic liver injury models. Therefore, we demonstrate that human CSF1-Fc treatment boosts hepatic macrophage populations to improve phagocytic clearance capacity and stimulate parenchymal regeneration. These findings provide evidence that hCSF1-based fusion proteins might have therapeutic value and clinical utility in the context of liver disease.

Materials and methods

hCSF1 fusion protein production

Human CSF-1 (uniprot P09603; aa36-190) was genetically fused to the Fc part of mouse IgG2a (mIgG2a-Fc) including the hinge region (Genebank CAA49868.1; aa215-447) via a linker. The linker consists of a PreScission protease cleavage site for potential removal of mIgG2a-Fc and short linkers upstream and downstream of the cleavage site (GSLEVLFQGPGSS). To reduce binding of the fusion construct to high and low affinity mouse Fc gamma receptors, two mutations were introduced in the CH2 domain of mIgG2a-Fc, at positions 234 and 235 (L234A/L235A) [28]. The complete construct (hCSF-1_mIgG2a-Fc) was cloned into a mammalian expression vector, including a signal peptide/leader of CD33. Large scale transient expression in HEK293-6E cells was initiated by polyethylenimine plasmid transfection and subsequent cultivation of transfected cells for approximately 5 days including feeding. Cell viability at 75% triggered harvest and filtration, resulting in a titre of approximately 60 mg/L culture volume. Supernatant was loaded overnight onto a 300 mL self-packed MabSelect Sure column. After a baseline wash, the protein was eluted with 50 mM citrate/140 mM NaCl (pH 2.7) and the eluate pH was immediately adjusted to 7.4 and subsequently sterilely filtered. Analytical Size-Exclusion-Chromatography (SEC) showed a monomeric fraction of 60%, which triggered a polishing step by preparative SEC. A third of the MabSelect Sure column eluate was concentrated on 30 kDa cut-off spin filters to 5 mL. Concentrate was applied to a 26/600 Superdex 200 column using PBS (pH 7.4) as running buffer, at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min, and 4 mL fractions were collected. Analytical SEC was performed and fractions with the highest amount of monomeric content were pooled, resulting in final protein purity of > 97%. Protein identity was confirmed by mass spectroscopy.

Mice

All mice (C57Bl/6J OlaHsd males, aged 6–10 weeks old) were sourced from Envigo (Indianapolis, IN) and group-housed in open top cages with free access to chow and water. Experimental mice were humanely euthanized, before complete exsanguination to collect trunk blood via cardiac puncture. All animal experiments were undertaken in accordance with criteria outlined in the procedure project license (70/7847 and P231C5F81) granted by the Home Office (United Kingdom) under the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 and approved by the University of Edinburgh Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body.

Experimental APAP-ALI

Mice were fasted for a minimum of 12 h before receiving APAP (350 mg/kg, i.p.; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) dissolved in warm saline (PanReac Applichem, Germany) to induce ALI. Chow was returned to cages 20 min post-APAP administration. APAP-ALI mice were randomized to receive hCSF1-mFc (1.0 mg/kg, s.c.) or vehicle alone (Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline [PBS]; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) at 12, 36, and 60 h post-APAP (n > 6 per treatment group). Mice received thermal support post-APAP by accommodating cages in a heated cabinet (28 °C) to limit hypothermia. All mice were labelled with BrdU (1 mg, i.p.) one hour before cull at 72 h post-APAP.

Carbon tetrachloride-induced experimental liver fibrosis

Liver fibrosis was induced in mice by iterative twice-weekly carbon tetrachloride (CCl4, 0.6 µL/g; Sigma-Aldrich; diluted 1:4 in sterile olive oil) administration for seven weeks. Fibrotic mice were randomized to receive 3 doses of hCSF1-mFc (0.1–1.0 mg/kg, s.c.), or vehicle alone, (PBS, n = 4–7 per treatment group) over 72 h. All mice were labelled with BrdU (1 mg, i.p.) one hour before cull at 72 h post-CCl4-induced fibrosis. In some experiments, mice received PKH26-PCL, (PKH26 Red Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit for Phagocytic Cell Labelling) (100 µL, 0.1 mM, i.v.; Sigma-Aldrich) 24 h prior to cull to label cells performing phagocytosis as described [29].

Liver fibrosis with acute insult

To recapitulate an acute on chronic liver insult, the the seven-week CCl4 fibrosis protocol was modified in some studies to include a single administration of E. coli-coated fluorescent pHrodo® bioparticles (100 µg, i.v.; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA), alongside the final CCl4 or control injection.

Serum and plasma biochemistry

Serum was collected by allowing whole blood to clot overnight (4 °C) before centrifugation (14,000 g, 5 min, 4 °C). Plasma was generated by collecting whole blood in EDTA blood tubes and collected after immediate centrifugation (6,000 g, 10 min, 4 °C). Serum and plasma were assayed for alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activities alongside albumin and total bilirubin determination using commercial kits (Alpha Laboratories Ltd, United Kingdom; Randox Laboratories, United Kingdm) adapted for use on a Cobas Fara centrifugal analyser (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) as previously described [30, 31].

Cytokine quantification

Cytokine panels were quantified in serum, plasma, and liver homogenates using commercial kits (Meso Scale Diagnostics, Rockville, MD) on a MESO Quickplex SQ 120 analyser (Meso Scale Diagnostics) following the manufacturers instructions, as described previously [17].

Immunodetection, imaging, and other assays

Please refer to the supporting materials for further materials and methods.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in Prism 8.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). In charts, scatter plots display each experimental unit, unless otherwise stated. Shapiro-Wilk test determined normality in datasets. When comparing two datasets, a Student’s t-test or a Mann-Whitney U test was performed on parametric or non-parametric datasets, respectively. When comparing three or more datasets, a one-way ANOVA was performed on parametric datasets with Dunnett’s post-hoc testing, otherwise a Kruskall-Wallis test was performed on non-parametric datasets. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

hCSF1-mFc administration induces macrophage accumulation in healthy mouse liver

Firstly, hCSF1 fusion proteins were synthesised by combining amino acids 36–190 of the human protein to either mouse (mFc2a) or human (hFc1P) Fc fragments. To confirm that the nascent fusion proteins were biologically active, hCSF1-mFc and hCSF1-hFc were tested in vitro alongside commercially sourced recombinant hCSF1 and vehicle alone (negative control) in cultured human monocytes freshly isolated from healthy volunteers. Both hCSF1 constructs increased resazurin turnover at lower concentrations than recombinant hCSF1 alone, demonstrated by EC50s (Fig. 1A). Reasurin floursence is reduced by metabolically active cells and signal intensity is proportional to viable cell number. Increased monocyte proliferation suggests potent bioactivity of hCSF1 constructs in vitro.

Furthermore, short-term hCSF1-mFc treatment in human monocytes induced ERK phosphorylation at similar concentrations, indicating CSF1 receptor (CSF1R) stimulation (Fig. 1B). Next, a range of single hCSF1-mFc doses were administered to healthy male mice (0–1.0 mg/kg, s.c.; study design: Supporting Fig. S1A). A rapid body weight increase was observed in mice after hCSF1-mFc treatment up to 72 h, compared to vehicle controls (Supporting Fig. S1B). Blood sampling confirmed that circulating hCSF1 levels were detected in mice at ng/mL concentrations 24 h after injection demonstrating that hCSF1-mFc was not rapidly eliminated in vivo (Supporting Fig. S1C). However, no reduction in blood platelet levels (an anticipated response attributed to CSF1-induced splenomegaly) was observed in mice after any hCSF-mFC dose. Therefore, a repeat-dose study was performed in mice receiving three consecutive daily hCSF1-mFC administrations over a dose range (study design: Fig. 1C). Similar to the single-dose response, all hCSF1-mFC treated mice rapidly gained weight on average compared to vehicle controls animals, which experienced an overall loss due to daily tail vein venepuncture [32]. (Fig. 1D). Haematology showed a dose- dependent drop in blood platelet levels at 72 h indicating bioactivity in vivo after repeat hCSF1-mFc treatment (Fig. 1E). Organ weights revealed a dose-dependent increase in the liver- and spleen-to-body weight ratios 72 h after hCSF1-mFc treatment, whilst kidney and brain weight ratios remained unchanged (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, liver and spleen weight ratios increased with hCSF1-mFc dose if kidneys or brain weights were used as normalizers instead (Supporting Fig. S2). To investigate hepatic macrophage dynamics following repeat hCSF1-mFc treatment, immunohistochemical (IHC) techniques were performed in harvested organs. A striking dose-dependent increase in F4/80 positive cells (i.e. a murine macrophage marker) was observed after hCSF1-mFc treatment in liver tissue, resulting in a 3.7-fold increase after three 1.0 mg/kg administrations (Fig. 2A-B; antibody details provided in Supporting Table S1). Similar F4/80 increases were observed in spleen, but only a modest increase was observed in kidney, which reflected the respective changes in organ weight (Fig. 2B).

A dual IHC approach demonstrated that a significant proportion of liver macrophages were BrdU-positive (i.e. in S-phase) after pulse-labelling, indicating macrophage proliferation in situ (Supporting Fig. S3A, B). Since hCSF1-mFC treatment induced a striking increase in liver macrophages, a panel of cytokines and biomarkers were characterised in plasma and liver tissue. A prominent dose-dependent increase in concentrations of plasma IL-10, an anti-inflammatory/T-cell regulatory cytokine, was observed following hCSF1-mFc treatment, alongside concomitant decreases in proinflammatory IL-1β and CXCL1 plasma concentrations (Fig. 2C). Plasma albumin and bilirubin levels, two clinically-used circulating liver function biomarkers, showed a dose-dependent reduction in healthy mice after hCSF1-mFc treatment (Fig. 2D), although the plasma transaminase activity was not different (Supporting Fig. S3C). Further cytokine changes were recorded in whole liver homogenates with dose-dependent reductions in IL-12p70, IL-5, and IL-6, and a dose-dependent increase in TNFα (proinflammatory cytokine), which likely reflect the compositional changes in the liver by macrophages (Fig. 2E). Finally, endogenous murine CSF1 was quantified in the sera of healthy mouse treated with and without hCSF1-mFc to check if ectopic CSF1 delivery influenced endogenous levels, but no differences were observed between treatment groups (Supporting Fig. S4). Mice tolerated hCSF1-mFC administration well and no adverse events were observed.

hCSF1-mFc treatment reduces serum proinflammatory cytokine levels following APAP-ALI

Since hCSF1-mFC treatment appeared safe and induced a systemic anti-inflammatory response in healthy mice, we hypothesised that hCSF1-mFc may have therapeutic value in an acute inflammatory model. A dose of 1 mg/kg was selected as this was well tolerated in healthy mice and showed biological activity with platelet reduction, indicating engagement of Fc‑dependent immune functions, alongside confirming increased macrophage numbers. The acetaminophen (APAP)-induced acute liver injury (APAP-ALI) model was selected due to its translational value. Therefore, hCSF1-mFc (or vehicle alone) was administered repeatedly to APAP-ALI mice (study design: Fig. 3A). Histological examination of liver tissue at 72 h showed evidence of centrilobular necrosis in APAP-ALI mice with a pronounced cellular infiltrate within necrotic areas in hCSF1-mFc treated mice (Fig. 3B). Importantly, hCSF1-mFC treated mice had lower serum ALT activity versus PBS-treated controls (Fig. 3C). Similar to effects in healthy animals, blood platelets were lower in hCSF1-mFc treated APAP-ALI mice indicating a conserved bioactivity in this model (Fig. 3C). In addition, APAP-ALI mice treated with vehicle alone displayed moderate leukopenia, however hCSF1-mFc treatment restored blood leukocyte counts to normal healthy levels (Fig. 3C). Dual immunofluorescent (IF) staining in liver tissue showed increased levels of cells positive for CSF1 receptor and F4/80 (i.e. macrophages), particularly at necrotic sites, which was approximately three-fold higher in hCSF1-mFC mice (Fig. 3D). Serum IL-10 was 8.5-fold higher in CSF1-mFC treated APAP-ALI mice compared to mice treated with vehicle alone. Conversely, serum IL-1β levels were 47% lower in hCSF1-mFc treated mice compared to vehicle controls. Finally, cytokine analysis in whole liver homogenate also showed a 49% increase in IL-10 levels following hCSF1-mFc treatment, whereas TNFα levels were 3.8-fold higher, similar to effects in healthy animals. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labelling (TUNEL) was performed in liver sections to identify necrotic/apoptotic cells, revealing a trend towards a reduced frequency in hCSF1-mFc treated mice (Supporting Fig. S5). Together, these results suggest hCSF1-mFc treatment induces a systemic anti-inflammatory response in experimental APAP-ALI.

hCSF1-mFc treatment improves hepatic phagocytic capacity during liver fibrosis

Since hCSF1-mFc treatment invoked a predominantly anti-inflammatory response in an acute injury model, and as porcine CSF-1-mFc improved thiocetamide (TAA) hepatic fibrosis [27] we tested whether hCSF1-mFc treatment could improve hepatic innate immune performance in fibrosis, where serious complications arise from hepatic immune deficiency [9, 11]. After seven weeks of CCl4 treatment (0.6 uL, twice weekly, i.p.), mice received iterative treatment with hCSF1-mFc (0.1–1.0 mg/kg, s.c.) or vehicle alone and were culled 24 h later. Alongside the final hCSF1-mFc (or vehicle) administration, all mice received a fluorescent soluble dye that is phagocytosed and thereby specifically labels phagocytic cells (PKH26PCL, i.v; study design: Fig. 4A). At cull, hCSF1-mFc bioactivity was evidenced in this model by reduced blood platelets and increased blood leukocytes in fibrotic mice receiving hCSF1-mFc (1.0 mg/kg), as previous (Fig. 4B). Similar to the acute model, plasma ALT was lower in fibrotic mice receiving hCSF1-mFC (1.0 mg/kg) compared to vehicle alone (Fig. 4C). In addition, we calculated the ratio between IL-10 (anti-inflammatory cytokine) and TNFα (proinflammatory cytokine) in plasma. hCSF1-mFc treated mice displayed a higher IL-10/TNFα ratio at 1.0 mg/kg indicating an anti-inflammatory response (Fig. 4C). Hepatic collagen content was equivalent between treatment groups as quantified by picrosirius red staining (Fig. 4D). Since extracellular matrix remodelling is not a rapid process, we designed a separate 12-week CCl4 fibrosis experiment to test if hCSF1-mFc dosed once-weekly over the final five weeks could elicit collagen regression (Fig. 5A). However, once-weekly hCSF1-mFc dosing was insufficient to alter hepatic macrophage levels or collagen content (Fig. 5D-G) demonstrating a requirement for at least twice weekly dosing to impact fibrosis [27]. In the seven-week fibrosis study, hepatic phagocytic capacity was quantified by PKH26PCL (herein referred to as PKH) epifluorescence in F4/80-stained IF sections. PKH26PCL was injected i.v. 24 h prior to cull, and particulates are only up taken by phagocytic cells. A 2.1-fold increase in the number of PKH-positive F4/80-positive cells, plus a 3.5-fold increase in total PKH signal intensity was observed after hCSF1-mFc treatment (1.0 mg/kg), compared to fibrotic mice receiving vehicle alone (Fig. 4E, F), showing increased phagocytosis in hepatic macrophages. In the same study, enzymatic digestion was performed on fibrotic liver tissue to retrieve the hepatic leukocyte population with and without hCSF1-mFC treatment. A flow cytometry gating strategy was used to identify resident (F4/80hi CD11bint) and infiltrating macrophage (F4/80int CD11bhi) populations (Supporting Fig. S6). hCSF1-mFC (1.0 mg/kg) treatment had the most striking effect on the infiltrating macrophage population showing an increased frequency of PKH-positive cells and mean fluorescent intensity (MFI), although the resident population also showed a higher MFI trend, but did not reach statistical significance (Supporting Fig. S7A), consistent with the findings of Keshvari et al., that both infiltrating and resident macrophages increase in number following CSF1-Fc. Finally, Ly6C, a macrophage phenotypic surface marker, was measured in hepatic macrophage populations. Ly6C is predominantly expressed on infiltrating macrophages (particularly proinflammatory populations), which was reflected by the MFIs in hepatic macrophage subsets (Supporting Fig. S7B). Importantly, hCSF1-mFc treatment did not change Ly6C status in infiltrating macrophages providing further evidence hepatic macrophages do not adopt a deleterious inflammatory phenotype post-treatment.

hCSF1-mFc treatment induces hepatocyte proliferation in healthy and liver disease models

To further profile the anti-inflammatory effects of hCSF1-mFc treatment, we modified the chronic CCl4 model by co-administering a bolus dose of a pro-inflammatory agent after fibrosis was established, to better model an acute-on-chronic injurious event (study design: Fig. 6A). Several commercially-available reagents are available, which mimic noxious infectious agents, including an array of pH-dependent fluorescently-labelled bioparticles (pHrodo®) coated with a selection of bacterial/fungal-derived substances. First, we tested if these coated bioparticles could provoke a proinflammatory response in murine bone-marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) in vitro. Gene expression profiling showed that only E. coli-coated bioparticles substantially increased Nos2 and Ccl2 expression in BMDMs, i.e. two genes associated with classical-activation and a proinflammatory response (Fig. 6B, primer details provided in Supporting Table S2). Next, E. coli-coated bioparticles were administered in vivo to test if they could elicit an inflammatory response in liver tissue after intravenous delivery (400 µg, i.v.) to healthy mice. Eight hours post-treatment, E. coli coated bioparticle-treated mice displayed weight loss, had reduced serum albumin, and showed a striking hepatic neutrophil infiltration compared to vehicle controls, consistent with a proinflammatory response (Supporting Fig. S8A-D). Importantly, E. coli bioparticles were colocalised with F4/80 cells in liver sections demonstrating that these bioparticles are engulfed by hepatic macrophages after i.v. administration (Supporting Fig. S8E). Next, hCSF1-mFc was tested using the repeat administration regimen as a treatment in the modified E. coli-pHrodo/CCl4 model. A lower trend for plasma ALT activity (p = 0.05) was observed after hCSF1-mFc treatment (Fig. 6C). More strikingly, hCSF1-mFc treatment led to reductions in a panel of plasma proinflammatory markers including IL-1β, IL-6, and CXCL1, and led to an increase in the IL-10/TNFα ratio indicating an anti-inflammatory response (Fig. 6C and Supporting Fig. S9). Gross observations showed that hCSF1-mFc treatment attenuated weight loss and led to an increase in the liver/kidneys weight ratio (Fig. 6D). Gene expression was higher for Mmp8 and Mmp9, which encode two collagenolytic proteases, was observed in whole liver tissue preparations of hCSF1-mFc treated mice, although Mmp13 expression was not different (Supporting Fig. S9). Acta2 and Mrc1 expression were higher in injured livers following hCSF1-mFc, indicating increased abundance of CD206 + macrophages linked to matrix remodelling [33]. Arg1 conversely was reduced, which may reflect changes in macrophage populations [34].

Since hCSF1-mFC treatment induced a robust proliferative response in hepatocytes, we postulated whether there was a reduction in cellular senescence occurred in these disease models. p21 is a p53‑induced CDK inhibitor upregulated in senescent cells, which is widely used as a senescence marker. Therefore, we measured nuclear p21 intensity in fibrotic mice with and without hCSF1-mFc treatment, but observed no difference between treatment groups (Supporting Fig. S10). Interestingly, a dose-dependent increase in nuclear p21 staining was observed following hCSF1-mFc treatment in both healthy and APAP-ALI mice (Supporting Fig. S11) – i.e. the same groups where a striking proliferative response was observed. It is possible that after hCSF1-mFc treatment, senescence and proliferation are occurring in tandem or that p21 may be acting as a assembly factor [35].

Discussion

Hepatic macrophage depletion occurs during acute and chronic liver disease leading to perturbations in barrier function and compromised clearance capacity [36]. Consequently, patients with acute and chronic liver disease are at risk of developing infections, typically E. coli or candidiasis, which are major determinants of death or delisting for transplant [37, 38]. Novel therapeutic strategies are required to potentially restore barrier function, improve hepatic innate immunity, and stimulate healthy parenchymal regeneration to improve clinical outcomes. Previous studies using animal-derived CSF1-Fc fusion proteins have increased our understanding of macrophage dynamics in experimental models of health and liver disease [24–27, 39]. Herein, we conjugated the bioactive amino acid sequence of hCSF1 to the Fc region of mouse or human immunoglobulin to yield fusion proteins suitable for in vivo testing in experimental models of liver disease.

hCSF1-mFc treatment induced a robust and reproducible increase in tissue macrophages, especially in mouse liver, which was conserved in all murine disease models. Human and mouse CSF1 proteins share high sequence homology, especially in the N-terminal domain, which confers CSF1R binding activity [40, 41]. As such, hCSF1 is active in the mouse [42]. There are several potential advantages of hCSF1-Fc protein therapy versus macrophage cell therapy for liver diseases and these include but are not limited to; the improved logistics of provision and lower cost, broad tuneable stimulation of endogenous macrophages and increased safety considerations, where adverse effects can be managed by dose interruption. Our fusion proteins contained the bioactive sequence only (amino acids 36–190) of hCSF1, which is 82% homologos to the corresponding mouse sequence. Native CSF1 is rapidly cleared from the circulation predominantly via receptor-mediated endocytosis by KCs leading to a half-life of only 1.6 h in mice [23]. Previously, Fc conjugation of animal CSF1 improved circulating half-life by increasing the molecular weight to prevent renal loss of residual circulating CSF1 [24]. Here, the same strategy was employed using hCSF1. We detected hCSF1 levels in mice for at least seven days after a single administration suggesting the fusion protein is not rapidly cleared in vivo. hCSF1-mFc administration was well-tolerated, reduced plasma CXCL1 and IL-1β levels, and led to a striking increase in plasma IL-10 concentrations. IL-10 is a regulatory T cell (Treg) cytokine and has intrinsic immunosuppressive activity via the inhibition of proinflammatory cytokine responses. Moreover, IL-10-producing macrophages have been found to be efficient at clearing apoptotic material [43]. Increasing systemic IL-10 levels after hCSF1-mFc indicates strongly activated regulatory/anti‑inflammatory pathways and may be secondary to macrophage production. This could represent a major therapeutic mechanism of hCSF-mediated therapy, especially in liver diseases where uncontrolled inflammation (e.g. Systemic inflammatory response syndrome [SIRS]) can be lethal. In the APAP-ALI model, hCSF1-mFc treatment attenuated systemic IL-1β concentrations, a key proinflammatory cytokine that potentiates the inflammatory response and injury during APAP-ALI [44]. In fact, systemic IL‑1β also decreased following hCSF1-mFc in co-stimulated fibrotic mice, which is likely secondary to expansion and reprogramming of hepatic macrophages to reparative phenotypes. This coincided with reduction of tissue injury, measured by serum ALT activity, a clinically-used ALI biomarker. Earlier assessment is likely to have identified reduction in necrosis with hCSF1-mFc, as this proceeds changes in ALT. hCSF1-mFc treatment also caused a dose-dependent reduction in plasma albumin in healthy mice. Macrophages scavenge a range of serum proteins [45], including albumin [46], which is used as a biomarker of hepatocyte function. Vigilance should be exercised when interpreting protein-based biomarkers in liver disease models when clearance mechanisms are targeted.

Importantly, hCSF1-mFc treatment increased PKH26PCL uptake (a soluble dye that labels cells performing phagocytosis) in the CCl4 fibrosis model. This finding demonstrates that hCSF1-mediated macrophage accumulation increases hepatic clearance capacity during liver fibrosis. Chronic liver disease patients are at risk of developing enteric infections associated with pathological bacterial translocation resulting from increased intestinal permeability and hepatic barrier failure. Consequently, infections are common in cirrhotic patients and confer a 3.75-fold increased risk of death [9, 36, 38]. Increasing numbers of resident and infiltrating macrophages to restore barrier function, via CSF1-Fc treatment, might be an attractive therapeutic strategy in chronic liver disease patients. However, further work is required in infection models to confirm if CSF-Fc therapy is safe and effective to prevent or control sepsis.

Fibrosis was not reduced by once weekly administration in this CCl4 fibrosis model indicating more frequent administration is required. Previously, increased hepatocyte proliferation was observed after porcine CSF1-Fc treatment in healthy mice [24] and in TAA fibrosis [27]. Here, we report CSF1-Fc treatment induced hepatocyte proliferation in fibrosis and in ALI models. The mechanism that underpins hCSF1-induced hepatocyte proliferation is not known, but trophic action by hepatic macrophages has been speculated, such as secreting growth factors and cytokines (e.g. HGF, IL‑6, TNF‑α, VEGF) and remodelling the microenvironment. We observed a dose-dependent increase in hepatic TNFα levels in liver homogenates from hCSF-mFc-treated mice. TNFα drives hepatocyte proliferation following partial hepatectomy [47]. However, other macrophage-derived molecules such as hepatocyte growth factor have also been suggested [39, 48] and found increased with increased with CSF1 [27]. Hepatocyte proliferation was also observed after alternatively-activated macrophage cell therapy in ALI models [17], which suggests that proliferative signals are macrophage-derived rather than directly attributed to CSF1R stimulation. Hepatocyte proliferation is fundamentally required for the restoration of healthy liver parenchyma during regeneration, and represents a key mechanism in hCSF-Fc therapy. Future studies may be indicated in chronic fibrosis models to certify a lack of impact on carcinoma progression, as CSF1 inhibitors have anti-tumour activity [49]. Consideration could be given to the use of combination therapies, such as hCSF1‑Fc paired with NK‑cell infusions to provide direct cytotoxicity against tumour cells alongside regeneration, building on emerging NK‑cell–based strategies in HCC [50]. Alternatively, with anti‑fibrotic agents such as Oltipraz or other TGF‑β/stellate‑cell–targeting drugs [51] so that pharmacologic suppression of new matrix deposition is coupled with an expanded pool of pro‑resolving macrophages to accelerate extracellular‑matrix degradation and liver repair. One limitation with hCSF1-mFc treatment was the presence of dose-dependent thrombocytopenia likely associated with increased splenic sequestration of platelets. Although reduced blood platelets caused no adverse events in mice, it might represent a hurdle for clinical translation. This could be addressed by prophylactic platelet monitoring with set interventions, such as treatment breaks or dose fractioning, at clinically relevant platelet reductions.

Conclusions

We report the production of a novel hCSF1 fusion protein, that robustly increases hepatic macrophage populations in models of acute and chronic liver disease. Consequently, hCSF1-mFc treatment improved hepatic clearance capacity during fibrosis, reduced circulating proinflammatory cytokines during fibrosis and ALI, and stimulated hepatocyte proliferation to promote liver regeneration. This report provides further evidence to facilitate the translation of CSF1-based fusion proteins towards clinical use.
