Brugia malayi miRNAs and potential targets within the feline host (Felis catus)
Authors: Erica Burkman, Lucienne Tritten, Christopher C. Evans, Guilherme G. Verocai, Caroline Sobotyk, Timothy G. Geary, Andrew R. Moorhead
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Host specificity is a critical feature of the survival and proliferation of parasites. In the context of interactions with the host, numerous mechanisms have been identified, particularly in parasitic helminths, that enable manipulation of the host immune system to enhance their own survival. The evolutionary history of these interactions often results in hosts becoming disease-tolerant or asymptomatic, even when burdened with a high number of worms, until a disruption in the host’s immune system can trigger a disease state. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying host specificity and the ways in which parasites alter the host’s immune system remain largely unexplored. Research conducted on parasite-derived microRNAs (miRNAs) suggests they may play a role in remodeling the host to improve parasite survival and growth, possibly through directed pathology. To further explore this host-parasite relationship, we analyzed plasma of four cats experimentally infected with the filarial nematode Brugia malayi, each with varying levels of microfilaremia, six months post-infection. Out of approximately 32 million sequencing reads, we detected 185 mature miRNA candidates potentially originating from B. malayi, with 26 miRNAs present in 10 or more copies. We also identified seven immunity-related host genes (Ptgs1, Irf4, Irf5, Numbl, Tnfsf15, Stat3, and Txlnb) that are predicted to be targets of parasite-derived miRNAs. Additional investigation is warranted to elucidate the role of these miRNAs in the host-parasite interaction. These data offer promising targets for further exploration, and potentially the discovery of novel therapeutics that disrupt parasite immune evasion and pathological alterations to the host.

Introduction

Parasites employ an array of mechanisms to facilitate their growth, reproduction, and transmission, which often exhibit high host specificity, enabling the parasites to survive only within a limited number of host species throughout their development. The parasitic nematodes Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi (Filarioidea: Onchocercidae), the primary causative agents of lymphatic filariasis (LF), predominately circulate within primate hosts (Hewitson et al., 2009; Girgis et al., 2013). Transmitted via mosquito bites, these parasites can reside within the human lymphatic system for years, leading to chronic and severe clinical manifestations. These morbidities are characterized by lymphedema, resulting in profound swelling in the limbs, or more severe elephantiasis and/or hydrocele due to impaired fluid drainage (Ottesen et al., 1997; Chandy et al., 2011). Adult nematodes residing in the lymphatic system produce larvae (microfilariae) that circulate in the bloodstream. Mosquitoes ingest microfilariae upon biting an infected individual and, after two molts, can transmit the infective larval stage to other humans.

Filarial parasites exhibit a high degree of host specificity and are mostly incapable of completing their life cycle in alternative hosts. This specificity is determined by an array of factors, including the parasite’s capacity to modulate the host immune system (Semnani et al., 2004; Hewitson et al., 2009; Mandanas, 2021), the compatibility of the host’s physiology for parasite development and reproduction (Spencer et al., 2003; Babu and Nutman, 2012; Desjardins et al., 2013), and receptors on host cells utilized for parasite establishment (Hussain et al., 1981; Hadadianpour et al., 2022). The host specificity of filarial parasites bears significant implications for the transmission and control of diseases instigated by filarial infections, such as LF and onchocerciasis (river blindness), caused by Onchocerca volvulus. Comprehending the mechanisms underpinning host specificity can offer valuable insights into the biology of these parasites and potentially aid in the development of novel strategies for disease control based on interference with the mechanisms deployed by parasites to create a permissive host.

Helminth parasites modulate the host immune system (Taylor et al., 2009; Buck et al., 2014; Loukas et al., 2016). The human immune response to filarioid nematodes such as W. bancrofti or B. malayi is complex and multifaceted. This immune regulation is typified by a CD4+ (Th2) response during the initial stages of infection (Spellberg and Edwards, 2001; Schroeder et al., 2012; Desjardins et al., 2013). Filarial parasites located in the lymphatics, lymph nodes, and circulation are vulnerable to attack by a wide range of immune effectors (Lawrence and Devaney, 2001; Allen and Maizels, 2011). In the context of filarial infections, the initial response involves effector cells such as neutrophils, eosinophils, macrophages, mononuclear phagocytes, natural killer cells, mast cells, and platelets. These cells secrete specific cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that not only enhance and recruit additional innate cells but also activate antigen-presenting cells to stimulate adaptive immunity. Additionally, some effector cells can secrete cytotoxic molecules, including reactive oxidative species and nitric oxide, which have been implicated in the clearance of B. malayi in both the multimammate mouse (Mastomys coucha and M. natalensis) and Mongolian jird (Meriones unguiculatus) models (Lytle et al., 2007; Friedman et al., 2009; Thomson et al., 2011). The humoral components of the immune response include complement, pentraxins, collectins, ficolins, and cytokines (Murphy et al., 2022). These cellular and humoral components cooperate to recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) on the parasite. Innate immune cells can detect PAMPs via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) located on their cell surface, within endosomal vesicles, or in the cytoplasm if the pathogen penetrates the host cell (Murphy et al., 2022). Once activated, these cells initiate a response by signaling to nearby phagocytic cells, such as through cytokine production, thereby triggering the activation of the adaptive immune system.

The ability of these cells to eliminate parasites often depends on one or more specific antibody isotypes (typically IgE or IgM) and complement (Chandrashekar et al., 1985; Spencer et al., 2003; Rajan et al., 2005). A consistent finding in filarial infections is elevated levels of IgE post-exposure (Hussain et al., 1981). In human subjects with a clinical history of filarial infection (LF, loiasis, or onchocerciasis), IgE monoclonal antibodies targeting filaria-specific excretory/secretory proteins were identified in serum (Hadadianpour et al., 2022). As the infection persists and becomes chronic, a general shift towards tolerance occurs, resulting in reduced T cell proliferation, and modification of regulatory B cells (Hussaarts et al., 2011), regulatory T cells (Taylor et al., 2012), and shifting towards an alternatively activated macrophage (M2) pathway (Gordon and Martinez, 2010). As the parasites age and die, polarization can shift toward pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 responses (Gazzinelli-Guimaraes and Nutman, 2018). Granulomas forming around the dead parasites may block lymphatics, while parasite-secreted metabolites, proteins, and even miRNAs can increase lymphatic permeability, all contributing to lymphedema, hydrocele, and elephantiasis (Cook and Zumla, 2009).

To survive in the harsh milieu of the host, these parasitic nematodes release a variety of excretory/secretory products downregulating local immune responses, including extracellular vesicles (EVs) (Johnstone et al., 1987). Extracellular vesicles, such as exosomes, have been identified as important mediators of cell-to-cell communication (van Niel et al., 2018). They are secreted throughout the parasite life cycle (Harischandra et al., 2018) and enter host cells by the endocytic pathway or by membrane fusion. Once EVs enter the cytoplasm, their cargo, including microRNAs (miRNAs) among other molecules, is released.

MicroRNAs are small, non-coding RNA molecules that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding to target messenger RNAs (mRNAs) in cells (Bartel, 2004). First discovered in the free-living nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, miRNAs are ubiquitous among eukaryotes (O’Brien et al., 2018). The binding of miRNAs results in inhibition of translation and transcript destabilization or both, and subsequent degradation (Lytle et al., 2007; Friedman et al., 2009). The 5′-end of mature miRNAs, a highly conserved 2- to 8-nucleotide seed region, binds with near-perfect complementarity to target mRNAs (Friedman et al., 2009).

Parasite-secreted miRNAs have been implicated in parasite establishment by down-regulation of host gene expression (Meningher et al., 2020; Soichot et al., 2022; Sais et al., 2024; Pérez et al., 2025). For example, the intestinal nematodes Heligmosomoides polygyrus and Nippostrongylus brasiliensis modulate the rodent host immune environment via miRNAs (Buck et al., 2014; Kozomara and Griffiths-Jones, 2014; Soichot et al., 2022). Research on filarial nematodes, such as Dirofilaria immitis, Loa loa, and Onchocerca spp., revealed that parasite miRNAs can be detected in host blood (Tritten et al., 2014b; Lagatie et al., 2017; Macfarlane et al., 2020). Recent studies have highlighted the role of Brugia-derived miRNAs in modulating both vertebrate and invertebrate hosts. Johnson et al. (2024) showed that B. malayi miRNAs disrupt lymphatic endothelial integrity, Poole et al. (2014) detailed miRNA diversity in B. malayi, and Loghry et al. (2023) demonstrated that B. malayi microfilarial EVs affect mosquito immune pathways. Similarly, Winter et al. (2012) reported novel miRNAs in Brugia pahangi and Haemonchus contortus, underscoring the expansive and largely unexplored repertoire of parasitic nematode miRNAs.

In this study, our aim was to identify parasite-derived miRNAs of the parasite B. malayi in feline plasma once patency is reached. Detected miRNA candidates were used in computational prediction analyses to identify cat genes that could be targeted for suppression by filarial miRNAs, and we compared these data to other datasets obtained from closely related filarial species or in biofluids from other hosts. As this feline experimental model is no longer available in the USA, this study represents a unique opportunity to evaluate this unique host-parasite interaction.

Materials and methods

Blood collection

Ten short-haired domestic cats (Felis catus) were maintained at the University of Georgia (Athens, GA, USA). Each cat was inoculated subcutaneously with 400 B. malayi third-stage larvae (L3) in the inguinal region of the hind limbs (200 per side). Between 10 and 20 ml of blood were collected from the internal jugular vein for all cats beginning at pre-infection and subsequently every 30 days for 180 days. Blood was collected in EDTA vacutainers (K2 EDTA, BD Vacutainer, Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and kept on ice or refrigerated (4 °C). After 30 min, blood tubes were centrifuged for 15 min at 1200× g. Plasma obtained from each sample was transferred to sterile DNase/RNase-free 2-ml centrifuge tubes (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA) and frozen at −20 °C for miRNA analysis.

Blood sample collection and slide preparation

Of 10 cats inoculated, 4 developed patent B. malayi infections and were used for miRNA assessment. At 6 months post-infection, the microfilarial (mf) counts for these four cats were: cat K: 3675 mf/ml; cat Q: 10,875 mf/ml; cat R1: 2475 mf/ml; and cat R2: 2125 mf/ml. Average microfilarial counts were determined for each animal by preparing thick blood smears in duplicate. Thick smears were prepared by gently inverting blood tubes to mix and aspirating 20 μl for dispensing onto a slide. An additional 20 μl deionized water was dispensed on top of the blood droplet and gently mixed to lyse red blood cells. The blood droplet was smeared uniformly in a 10 × 20-mm area and the slides were dried at room temperature for a minimum of 24 h prior to staining with 10% Giemsa solution (Acros Organics, Fair Lawn, NJ, USA) in TAE (2.0 M Tris-Acetate + 100 mM Na2 EDTA, National Diagnostics, Atlanta, GA, USA). Microfilariae were identified and quantified, with counts from both slides averaged for each cat.

Total RNA isolation

Plasma samples from the 4 cats with microfilariae at 6 months post-infection were analyzed. Total RNA, including small RNA, was isolated using a Norgen Biotek Plasma/Serum RNA Purification Midi Kit (catalogue number NC1557182, Norgen Biotek, Canada) with a few modifications to manufacturer’s instructions. Norgen MicroRNA (cel-miR-39) spike-in kit (catalogue number 59000, Norgen Biotek, Canada) was used as an internal reference control but removed from computational analysis. To isolate RNA, 4.5 ml lysis buffer and β-mercaptoethanol (molecular grade, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) were added to 250 μl plasma samples and cell lysates were homogenized using a syringe and 30-gauge needle. Modification to the Norgen Plasma/Serum kit involved initial sample processing of 500 μl rather than 1.5 ml, as cellular components in plasma obstruct isolation columns. Columns were washed and eluted an additional 1–2 times per manufacturer’s recommendation to reduce salt contamination and to increase RNA yields. Eluted total RNA was quantified by optical density (NanoDrop, 2000; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Santa Clara, CA, USA) and stored at −80 °C.

RNA samples were pooled for each cat individually for Illumina deep sequencing. A minimum of 100 ng RNA was submitted for RNAseq. Pooled samples were shipped to LC Sciences (Houston, TX, USA) on dry ice for cDNA library preparation, miRNA deep sequencing, and in-depth bioinformatics analysis. Remaining RNA was stored at −80 °C.

RNA-seq workflow and bioinformatics analysis

Small RNA libraries were prepared at LC Sciences from the pooled samples using an Illumina TruSeq Small RNA Preparation kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Clusters were generated with purified cDNA on Illumina’s Cluster Station and sequenced on Illumina GAIIx following the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time sequencing image analysis and base-calling were conducted using Real-Time Analysis version 1.8.70 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) and used for subsequent data analysis.

The LC Sciences proprietary pipeline script (ACGT101-miR v. 4.2) was utilized for data analysis. Raw data were organized into mappable reads and mapped to the domestic cat genome (F. catus, ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes) and mature and precursor miRNA sequences of F. catus and other selected mammalian species available from miRBase v. 21.0 (https://www.mirbase.org) (Griffiths-Jones, 2004; Griffiths-Jones et al., 2006, 2008; Kozomara and Griffiths-Jones, 2014; Kozomara et al., 2019). Remaining unmapped reads were filtered against mRNA from the Rfam database (https://rfam.org) (Gardner et al., 2011) and RepBase (https://www.girinst.org) (Jurka et al., 2005). Reads that did not map to the feline genome were aligned to the B. malayi genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/search/all/?term=brugia%20malayi) (Ghedin et al., 2007) and selected nematode miRNAs from Haemonchus contortus (Winter et al., 2012), Ascaris suum (Wang et al., 2011), Strongyloides ratti (Ahmed et al., 2013), Panagrellus redivivus (Srinivasan et al., 2013), C. elegans (Lall et al., 2006), Caenorhabditis brenneri (Shi et al., 2013), Caenorhabditis remanei, Caenorhabditis briggsae, and Pristionchus pacificus (de Wit et al., 2009). Reads were assigned to groups 1–4 based on mapping properties and secondary structure predictions as previously described, reflecting the confidence level of the candidate sequences (Tritten et al., 2014a) (Table 1). Read copy counts were normalized by dividing the counts by a library size parameter (Evans et al., 2018). Target analysis

The 26 highly expressed miRNAS and the 12 high-confidence mature miRNA sequences asssigned to groups 1b and 2a were submitted to an in silico target predition workflow. Putative biological targets of miRNAs were searched in the feline genome using TargetScan v. 7.2 implemented as a standalone workflow under iPortal (Kunszt et al., 2015) and OpenBIS (Bauch et al., 2011) using default parameters. All targeted cat genes with a weighted context++ score ≤ −1.0 were used in subsequent enrichment analyses (Benna et al., 2019; Duguet et al., 2020). No additional multi-site targeting threshold was applied to avoid excluding validated single-site targets. miRNA target predictions were performed in parallel using the miRanda v. 3.3a algorithm (Enright et al., 2003), applying standard alignment parameters (gap open penalty: −9.0; gap extension penalty: −4.0) and filtering hits by a minimum score of 145 and a maximum energy threshold of −20 kcal/mol (Guan et al., 2017). The analysis was run against the Felis catus 3′- and 5′-UTRomes, with post-processing to extract predicted target sites, scores, and energies. Target sequences were annotated using the Ensembl Felis catus gene build (release Fca126_mat1.0) via the biomaRt R package, linking Ensembl gene IDs to external gene symbols. Gene annotations from TargetScan were harmonized to match the Ensembl gene symbols used in the miRanda annotations. miRNA-gene pair-level overlap was assessed by matching miRNA-gene pairs between TargetScan and miRanda datasets, requiring exact concordance in gene ID and miRNA identifier. The final validated interaction list included all target sites predicted by TargetScan and independently confirmed by miRanda binding predictions. The total number of overlapping miRNA-targeted genes was recorded, counting the number of miRNA-gene pairs and the number of overlapping sites per pair, using R (v. 4.4.2), including bar plots to illustrate overlap patterns.

Enrichment analysis was carried out using targeted gene symbols, interrogating the “KEGG 2019_Mouse” database, available within the R version of the program Enrichr v. 2.1 (Kuleshov et al., 2016).

Results

MicroRNAs identified from B. malayi-infected cats

Total RNA isolated from the microfilaremic cats totaled 168.6 ng for cat K, 241.1 ng for cat Q, 163.7 ng for cat R1, and 129.5 ng for cat R2. From these samples, a total of 41,164,433 raw reads were compiled separately. The workflow involved removal of low-quality sequences (junk reads, 3ADT not found, < 15 bases, or > 32 bases after the 3ADT cut), then selection for sequence mapping to the feline or other mammalian reference genomes (Supplementary Table S1). The remaining 32,584,554 reads were mapped to the B. malayi genome or to the genome of selected nematodes. A total of 185 unique miRNA sequences of potential nematode origin were identified, with 43 known miRNAs and 142 predicted candidates. Of these 185 candidate miRNAs, 27 were present in 8 or more copies (Table 2). The workflow for the mapped reads was clustered into 8 groups. Of these mapped clusters, 5 were un-mapped miRNAs to selected nematode species while forming hairpins (group 2a) and only 1 miRNA without hairpin formation (group 2b). Filarial miRNA comparison in other hosts

To examine filarial miRNA conservation in samples from infected hosts, B. malayi miRNAs in cat plasma were compared to miRNAs attributed to other filariid species in other hosts, including dogs infected with D. immitis, baboons infected with L. loa, humans with O. volvulus, and cows infected with Onchocerca ochengi (Winter et al., 2012; Britton et al., 2014; Tritten et al., 2014b). Including B. malayi, 451 miRNA candidates were reported, with 323 unique miRNAs found in all 5 datasets. Only 47 of these miRNA families overlapped, with the largest number overlapping between B. malayi and D. immitis (data not shown).

Predicted targets in the feline genome

The 26 highly expressed B. malayi candidates and the subset of 12 high-confidence candidates were analyzed to identify potential biological targets in the feline genome with TargetScan and confirmed with miRanda software with functional enrichment analysis performed using Enrichr with the “KEGG 2019_Mouse” database. A total of 113,354 targets were predicted to bind the 27 most abundantly expressed B. malayi miRNAs by TargetScan and 6072 targets by the 12 high-confidence candidates, representing 4327 unique genes (Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, miRanda predicted the majority of interactions to involve bma-miR-34, bma-lin-4, and cbr-miR-40_2ss11TC21AT. Discussion

This study characterized parasite-derived miRNAs found in plasma of B. malayi-infected cats. MicroRNAs are highly stable in plasma and serum, making them ideal candidates for use as potential biomarkers and diagnostic targets (Lytle et al., 2007; Friedman et al., 2009; Hakimi and Cannella, 2011; Thomson et al., 2011; Winter et al., 2012; Tritten et al., 2014a, 2021; Lagatie et al., 2017; Macfarlane et al., 2020; Osei-Poku et al., 2025). However, it is important to note that circulating nucleic acids such as miRNAs and DNA have been shown to have limitations as biomarkers in filarial infections (Lagatie et al., 2017), and such computational predictions should be interpreted with care. MicroRNAs are involved in key regulatory processes in parasite development and establishment within a host (Zhu et al., 2016; Marks et al., 2019; Pérez et al., 2019). Identification of specific host genes targeted and regulated by parasite-derived miRNAs could suggest a molecular mode of action in parasite immunomodulation.

MicroRNA comparison in other hosts

Most mammalian miRNAs are highly conserved (Friedman et al., 2009), as are miRNAs across the phylum Nematoda (Tritten and Geary, 2016). When comparing miRNAs found in other closely related filarial parasites and their respective hosts (Fig. 1), few common miRNAs were noted. Notably, no miRNAs in these datasets were common among all 5 species. As few circulating miRNAs were initially documented for L. loa and Onchocerca spp., it is likely that additional sequences remain to be discovered. This is not unexpected, given that the total blood volume of larger hosts (e.g. baboons and cattle) can dilute the concentration of parasite-derived miRNAs more than in smaller hosts like cats, making these circulating miRNAs more difficult to detect. Sex- and stage-specific miRNA secretion has been reported in Brugia spp. and D. immitis, and we expect this phenomenon to occur in other filarial species (Winter et al., 2012; Tritten et al., 2016, 2021). The miRNA profile observed in cat blood likely reflects a mixture of parasite stages. The adults remain sequestered in lymphatics, whereas microfilariae circulate systemically. Comparing these miRNAs to orthologs and homologs in other parasites, rodent models, and in vitro studies not only helps predict their potential functions but also supports the validity of their identification in our dataset.

Immunomodulatory targets on the feline genome

For helminths to persist long-term within a mammalian host, they must deploy complex and highly adapted strategies to evade or modulate the host’s protective immune responses. Among immune genes identified as potential targets of highly expressed miRNAs found in B. malayi-infected cat blood were Ptgs1, Irf4, Irf5, Numbl, Tnfsf15, Stat3, and Txlnb. The Ptgs1 gene, also known as prostaglandin G/H synthase 1 (PGHS-1), encodes cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), responsible for catalyzing the conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandin H2, which is then metabolized into various biologically active molecules (Lytle et al., 2007; Thomson et al., 2011). The miRNA bma-let-7 is believed to target Ptgs1. Both cyclooxygenases and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) play critical roles in mediating host inflammatory responses (Kim and Lee, 2025). If these enzymes are dysregulated in the host when exposed to a pathogen, the resulting imbalance can weaken the inflammatory response. Prostaglandin G/H synthase isozymes are also pharmacologically significant, as they are the primary targets of aspirin and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (Smith et al., 1996). Although PGHS-1 is constitutively expressed in most tissues, it is not necessarily produced by every cell within a tissue. Because of its broad tissue distribution and the regulatory effect of prostaglandins in other parasitic nematode infections (Bohnacker et al., 2024), a parasite’s ability to manipulate this enzyme, such as through miRNA targeting, could improve its survival within the host.

Interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) are a family of transcription factors originally described for their role in the induction of type I interferons (IFNs) in mammals (Miyamoto et al., 1988). The genes Irf4 and Irf5 encode IRF-4 and IRF-5, respectively. IRF5 is one of 4 core molecules implicated in the induction of type I IFNs (Barnes et al., 2001). IFNs are cytokines that activate in response to Toll-like receptors (TLRs), PRRs, and PAMPs. TLRs are expressed by many immune cells, including dendritic cells, macrophages, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and endothelial cells. When TLRs are activated, a cascade of signaling events occurs, resulting in activation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), producing a pro-inflammatory response. If parasite miRNAs can suppress or reduce this pro-inflammatory signal cascade, this could explain at least in part what has been documented in other host-parasite interactions (Lall et al., 2006; de Wit et al., 2009; Shi et al., 2013; Srinivasan et al., 2013). It should be noted that TLRs are also strongly influenced by Wolbachia lipoproteins, which stimulate innate and adaptive immunity during filarial infections (Turner et al., 2009). Therefore, caution is needed in attributing these effects exclusively to parasite miRNAs without further evidence.

IRF4 is a transcription factor only expressed in lymphocytes, dendritic cells, and macrophages (Lewis et al., 2005; Huber and Lohoff, 2014; Agarwal et al., 2015). Only two miRNAs targeted this gene: bma-miR-228 and lin-4. Unlike IRF-5, which is induced by type I or type II IFNs, IRF-4 is induced upon activation of T and B cells, serving critical functions in regulation of IL-21 production by CD4+ T-helper cells, B cell class switching, and plasma cell differentiation (Lall et al., 2006; Srinivasan et al., 2013). IL-21 has potent regulatory effects on the immune system. In a study on allergic rhinitis, mice injected with IL-21 demonstrated a significant reduction in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by T cells and decreased IgE levels (Lin et al., 2015). Experiments with transgenic mice have shown that deletion of IRF-4 in B cells prevents their differentiation into plasma cells, resulting in inhibition of antibody production (Klein et al., 2006). Regulation of IRF-4 expression in T cells and B cells is similar, with either T cell mitogens or anti-CD3 antibodies inducing IRF-4 expression. In a set of in vitro experiments in which T cells with IRF-4 knockout (IRF4-/-) were incubated with lymph node cells with antibodies to CD3 or bacterial superantigen staphylococcal enterotoxin A (SEA), T cell proliferation was reduced (Mittrücker et al., 1997). This underlies the importance of this transcription factor in B and T cell proliferation. A recent study provides direct evidence that, by delivering synthetic helminth miRNAs into differentiating Th2 cells and macrophages in vitro, nematode-derived miRNAs can downregulate key transcription factors, including IRF-4 and mTOR, and subsequently alter cytokine production (Soichot et al., 2022). Inhibition of IRF-4, therefore, would likely impact the adaptive immune response within the infected host.

Both Numb/Numbl and Tumor Necrosis Factor Ligand Superfamily Member 15 proteins (TNFSF15) play roles in endothelial cell fate and T cell immune regulation. Only bma-miR-34 was predicted to target Numb/Numbl, while bma-miR-92 and lin-4 were predicted to target TNFSF15. Numb/Numbl genes encode proteins that have been linked to their ability to bind and antagonize Notch, a family of type-1 transmembrane proteins that specify cell fate (Nwaeburu et al., 2016; García-Heredia and Carnero, 2018). Numb/Numbl proteins regulate the function of T cells (Kueanjinda et al., 2015), and suppression of Numb/Numbl could therefore affect their function. TNFSF15, also known as VEGI (vascular endothelial growth inhibitor), is an endothelial cell cytokine that inhibits endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis (Lin et al., 2012; Tritten and Geary, 2016). It is also involved in the regulation of T cell-mediated immune responses, and suppression could result in excessive inflammation or autoimmunity.

The signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) gene is a member of the STAT protein family involved in transcription activation. The miRNA lin-4 targeted this gene twice in this study; STAT proteins are phosphorylated by cytoplasmic Janus kinases (Jak), part of the Jak-STAT pathway that plays a central role in both innate and adaptive immunity (Smith et al., 1996). These proteins mediate cytokine signals during Th1 and Th2 differentiation. The suppression of STAT3 leads to an increase in CD4+ T cells to induce transcription factor T-bet (Thomson et al., 2011). When naïve CD4+ T cells are STAT3-deficient, germinal center formation is impaired, and inversely, a Th1 cell phenotype has enhanced expression (Lytle et al., 2007). The STAT3 proteins are involved in many other functions. The fact that when STAT3 is downregulated, a pro-inflammatory state occurs, appears counter-intuitive for an invading parasite. Why this gene may be targeted by parasite miRNAs, if these computational predictions hold true, is a question to be further explored in filariasis infection models.

The gene encoding β-Taxilin (Txlnb) is expressed in skeletal muscle and cardiac muscle tissue (Sakane et al., 2016). The miRNAs bma-let-7 and cbn-mir-35f were predicted to target this gene. The potential functions of this gene and subsequent products are not well characterized. Taxilin family members interact with syntaxin family members, which are involved in intracellular vesicle trafficking. By influencing Txlnb expression, B. malayi could potentially disrupt normal vesicular transport pathways in host tissues, thereby impairing muscle integrity or dampening immune responses.

These genes span a wide range of immune and metabolic processes, suggesting that B. malayi-derived miRNAs could exert multi-faceted effects on the host. To further explore these functional implications, we performed an enrichment analysis using Enrichr, focusing on the KEGG 2019_Mouse database. AGE-RAGE signaling has been tied to chronic inflammatory and metabolic conditions, which could help explain the capacity of filarial parasites to establish long-lasting infections (Dong et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). RAGE is a pattern recognition receptor expressed on B cells, T cells, and dendritic cells, and its ligand-triggered activation has been observed in eosinophils (Curran and Bertics, 2011). When activated, RAGE drives immediate inflammatory responses and promotes a shift toward Th1 lymphocyte differentiation (Kierdorf and Fritz, 2013; Traoré et al., 2016). Multiple parasitic nematode species target AGE-RAGE signaling, reinforcing its potential significance (Duguet et al., 2020). mTOR signaling plays a central role in cellular growth, proliferation, and metabolic control, all of which are crucial for effective host responses to helminth infections. Brugia malayi has been shown to downregulate the mTOR pathway (Narasimhan et al., 2016; Ricciardi et al., 2021). Our findings strengthen the concept that parasite miRNAs may help to dampen or skew the host immune response, thereby favoring parasite establishment and survival. Future studies will be needed to confirm the direct involvement of these pathways in B. malayi infection.

Model limitations

Multiple techniques are available to detect miRNAs in blood, each offering distinct advantages. Despite its high cost and data-intensive nature, next-generation sequencing (NGS) remains the most powerful approach for the discovery of novel miRNAs. This study focuses on overall expression patterns rather than the precise quantification of individual miRNAs and emphasizes comparisons across closely related species and different host species. Consistent with established practice in global miRNA profiling studies, no qPCR validation was performed, as the focus was on overall expression patterns and cross-species comparisons (e.g. Tritten et al., 2014a; Ricafrente et al., 2022). Nonetheless, RT-qPCR validation may be valuable in cases where the expression patterns of specific miRNAs are of particular interest, such as in diagnostic applications.

In this study, we relied on some of the only available tools that enable analysis of a custom, non-model organism transcriptome. Among them, TargetScan is the only tool that is regularly curated, updated, and optimized for improved prediction accuracy and biological relevance. In contrast, miRanda has not been updated since 2010 and lacks ongoing maintenance and refinement, as is the case for many older but versatile tools. Consequently, TargetScan constitutes the most reliable and trusted source of miRNA target predictions.

In this study, apparently parasite-derived miRNAs were detected in B. malayi-infected cats, revealing possible immune-related gene targets based on computational predictions. Notably, the immune genes constituted just 1% of the predicted targets for the most highly expressed miRNAs, suggesting that additional pathways beyond host immunity may be affected. Future investigations that include infective L3 of this parasite could uncover stage-specific miRNA profiles, potentially revealing how B. malayi establishes and maintains infection across its life cycle. Further systematic analysis of these miRNA targets may clarify parasite survival strategies and identify novel opportunities for therapeutic intervention.

Conclusions

This study identified and characterized parasite-derived microRNAs (miRNAs) in the plasma of cats experimentally infected with Brugia malayi, revealing 185 unique miRNA candidates, including 26 highly expressed sequences. Computational analyses predicted that several of these miRNAs target feline immune-related genes, such as Ptgs1, Irf4, Irf5, Stat3, and Tnfsf15, suggesting a potential role in modulating host immune responses to facilitate parasite survival. Enrichment analyses highlighted key pathways, including mTOR and AGE-RAGE signaling, that may be influenced by these miRNAs. While immune-related genes represented only a small fraction of predicted targets, the findings suggest that B. malayi miRNAs may exert broad regulatory effects on host physiology. This work provides a foundation for future studies to validate these interactions and explore therapeutic strategies that disrupt parasite-mediated immune modulation and pathogenesis.
