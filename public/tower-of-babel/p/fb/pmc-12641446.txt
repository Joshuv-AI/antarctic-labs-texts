Tea Plant miR5368‐p5 Negatively Regulates Resistance Against Lasiodiplodia theobromae Through Targeting the CsWRKY57‐CsLRR‐RLK Module
Authors: Bin Wang, Ruixin Zhang, Dongqin Zhang, Jun Zhang, Jia Liu, Michael Wisniewski, Baoan Song, Zhuo Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Lasiodiplodia theobromae can cause severe diseases, including leaf spot, leaf necrosis and stem canker in tea plants, leading to substantial losses in both tea leaf production and quality. However, the mechanisms underlying host resistance remain poorly understood. In this study, we identified CsWRKY57 as a nucleus‐localised transcription factor whose expression dynamically responds to L. theobromae infection. Transient overexpression and antisense oligonucleotide (AsODN)‐mediated silencing in tea leaves, along with stable overexpression in transgenic Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, demonstrated that CsWRKY57 enhanced resistance to L. theobromae. Yeast two‐hybrid and bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays revealed that CsWRKY57 interacts with CsTIFY5A in the nucleus, further confirming that CsTIFY5A negatively regulates disease resistance through transient overexpression and AsODN‐mediated silencing in tea leaves. DNA affinity purification sequencing, electrophoretic mobility shift assay and dual‐luciferase assay indicated that CsWRKY57 binds to the AGTCAA motif in the CsLRR‐RLK promoter, thereby activating its expression. Transient overexpression and AsODN‐mediated silencing assays in tea leaves demonstrated that CsLRR‐RLK positively regulates resistance to L. theobromae. Additionally, degradome sequencing, β‐glucuronidase and dual‐luciferase assays revealed that miR5368‐p5 cleaves CsWRKY57 mRNA. Transient overexpression and AsODN assays in tea leaves, as well as stable overexpression of miR5368‐p5 in transgenic N. benthamiana, indicated that miR5368‐p5 negatively regulates resistance to L. theobromae. Our results suggest that the miR5368‐p5‐CsWRKY57‐CsLRR‐RLK module, which also includes CsTIFY5A interacting with CsWRKY57, plays a critical role in regulating the defence response of tea plants to L. theobromae. The results provide valuable insights into the mechanisms governing the response of tea plants to L. theobromae infection.

Introduction

Tea ( Camellia sinensis ), a key economic crop in the Theaceae family, is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions (Hao et al. 2018). However, fungal diseases can significantly impact the health of tea plants, adversely affecting both the yield and quality of tea leaves, both of which represent a substantial threat to tea cultivation (Guo et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2022; Pandey et al. 2021). The fungal pathogen Lasiodiplodia theobromae, a member of the Botryosphaeriaceae, infects more than 500 woody plant species worldwide (Burgess et al. 2006). To date, research on L. theobromae has been predominantly conducted on grapes ( Vitis vinifera ) and peaches ( Prunus persica ) (Reis et al. 2022; Rodríguez‐Gálvez et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2020; Zhang, Shen, et al. 2022). However, few studies have been conducted on the mechanisms responsible for the pathogenicity of L. theobromae and host plant defence response. Transcriptomic analysis of grapevine dieback revealed significant alterations in the fungal metabolism of phenolic compounds. The fungal L‐tyrosine catabolism pathway aids the fungus in evading the host's defence response and is likely associated with the early stages of hyphal colonisation in the intercellular environment (Paolinelli‐Alfonso et al. 2016). The virulence factor LtScp1 plays a pivotal role in protecting the fungus from degradation during infection in grapevines (Peng et al. 2022). As a virulence factor, LtCre1 acts as an effector, inhibiting Bax‐triggered cell death in Nicotiana benthamiana and modulating the sugar‐signalling pathway, which ultimately contributes to the development of disease symptoms in grapevines (Xing et al. 2023). Additionally, ethylene response factor 98 (ERF98) and ERF1 are recognised as negative regulators of the response of peach plants to L. theobromae infection (Zhang et al. 2023).

The ongoing rise in global temperatures enhances the ability of L. theobromae to cause a range of diseases in tea plants, including leaf spot, leaf necrosis and stem canker, in tea plantations across regions such as Guizhou and Fujian provinces in China, and in key production areas of India. These diseases lead to a decline in the health of tea plants, significantly reducing both the yield and quality of tea leaves (Ghosh et al. 2024; Li, Bao, et al. 2019; Li, Jayawardena, et al. 2019). However, research on L. theobromae‐induced diseases in tea plants remains limited. In our previous studies, we discovered that the LncRNA81246‐miR164d‐CsNAC1‐CsEXLB1 module plays a key role in defence against hyphal invasion in tea plants, thereby enhancing resistance to L. theobromae (Guo et al. 2025). Furthermore, CsMYB28 in tea plants, whose mRNA is targeted and cleaved by gma‐miR828a, binds to the promoter of the CsRPP13 gene, thereby activating its expression and helping to control tea plant disease caused by L. theobromae (Wen et al. 2025). However, the mechanisms regulating the pathogenicity of L. theobromae and the host response in tea plants are unclear due to the limited number of studies that have been conducted. Consequently, there is an essential need to investigate the defence mechanisms of tea plants against L. theobromae infection. Such studies could provide a foundation for the breeding of disease‐resistant tea cultivars.

WRKYs are widely recognised as critical regulators in plant defence responses, playing a major role in plant–pathogen interactions (Eulgem et al. 2000; Javed and Gao 2023). Studies have demonstrated that in the absence of pathogen infection, Arabidopsis thaliana WRKY70 directly inhibits systemic acquired resistance deficient 1, thereby suppressing salicylic acid production and the expression of defence genes (Zhou et al. 2017). PpWRKY46 in peach plants transcriptionally activates the energy metabolism‐related genes sorbitol dehydrogenase and cyclooxygenase 15, while PpWRKY53 represses the expression of these genes. PpWRKY46 interacts with PpWRKY53 to alleviate its transcriptional repression, thereby enhancing disease resistance in peach (Ji et al. 2022). JrWRKY21 in walnut ( Juglans regia ) activates the expression of the pathogenesis‐related protein (PR) gene pathogen‐triggered immunity 5‐Like by directly binding to W‐box elements in its promoter region. The transcriptional process induces the binding of a pathogen‐triggered immunity 5‐like protein to the GCCGAC motif in the promoter of the JrPR5L gene, thereby activating the expression of JrPR5L, which confers resistance to Colletotrichum gloeosporioides infection (Zhou et al. 2022). Additionally, WRKY65 in citrus ( Citrus sinensis ) directly activates the expression of respiratory burst oxidase homologue B, respiratory burst oxidase homologue D, calcium‐dependent protein kinases 33 and PR10 genes, enhancing disease resistance against Penicillium digitatum in citrus fruits (Wang, Li, Chen, et al. 2021). TaWRKY27 in wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) enhances susceptibility to Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici by promoting auxin accumulation mediated by aminocyclopropane carboxylate oxidase protein 3 and senescence‐related protein 1 (Yao et al. 2025). OsWRKY36 in rice ( Oryza sativa ) suppresses resistance to insect attack and pathogen infection by transcriptionally repressing the expression of genes encoding phenylalanine ammonia‐lyases (PALs) (Liu et al. 2025). These studies highlight the role of the regulatory network of WRKYs in plant disease resistance.

Accumulating evidence suggests that WRKYs in tea plants play a pivotal role in modulating developmental processes and orchestrating defence mechanisms against both biotic and abiotic stresses. For example, CsWRKY71 directly suppresses the biosynthesis of theanine by binding to W‐box cis‐elements in the promoter of theanine synthase I. Exogenous application of gibberellic acid significantly downregulates CsWRKY71 expression, thus relieving its transcriptional repression of theanine synthase I and rapidly promoting the accumulation of theanine (Xiang et al. 2024). CsWRKY13 directly binds to the promoters of CsPAL and cinnamate 4‐hydroxylase genes in the lignin biosynthesis pathway, thereby inhibiting lignin biosynthesis in C. sinensis (Li, Zhou, et al. 2024). The volatile organic compound (E)‐nerolidol, an elicitor of host defence in tea plants, upregulates the expression of CsWRKY3. This promotes the accumulation of antimicrobial and antifeedant compounds, enhancing resistance against both the tea green leafhopper and Colletotrichum fructicola (Chen et al. 2020).

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play essential roles in mediating the plant immune response to pathogen invasion (Li et al. 2010; Song et al. 2019; Zhang, Waseem, et al. 2022). Inhibiting the expression of miR168 using the target mimic (MIM168) increases rice yield, shortens flowering time and enhances resistance against rice blast (Wang, Li, Chern, et al. 2021). Additionally, miR172a in rice plants targets and cleaves transcription factor (TF) supernumerary bract (SNB) mRNA, while OsSNB inhibits the expression of MYB30 mRNA. Notably, the miR172a‐OsSNB‐OsMYB30 module promotes lignin accumulation in rice, which contributes to enhanced resistance against rice blast (Magnaporthe oryzae), sheath blight (Rhizoctonia solani) and bacterial leaf blight ( Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae) (Wang, Wang, et al. 2025). Teosinte branched 1/cycloidea/proliferating cell factor 10 in tea plants acts as a positive regulator of resistance against grey blight, while miR319a‐mediated suppression of its target gene expression diminishes resistance to Pestalotiopsis spp. (Liu et al. 2022). Transgenic A. thaliana lines overexpressing Csn‐miR477 exhibit reduced PAL expression, as well as elevated levels of H2O2, O2 −, and malondialdehyde. This is accompanied by decreased superoxide dismutase and peroxidase activities, which result in increased susceptibility to infection by Pseudopestalotiopsis spp. (Wang et al. 2020). However, studies on the role of miRNA‐WRKY regulatory interactions have not been conducted in relation to tea plant diseases.

The present study identified a differentially expressed gene, CsWRKY57, from the whole‐transcriptome data of tea leaves inoculated with hyphae of L. theobromae. This gene was selected as a candidate gene associated with disease resistance. The result of DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP‐seq) assay revealed that leucine‐rich repeat receptor‐like kinase (CsLRR‐RLK) in C. sinensis was a target gene of CsWRKY57, demonstrating its role in disease resistance. We also found that miR5368‐p5 bound and cleaved CsWRKY57 mRNA, while CsTIFY5A interacted with CsWRKY57. Both miR5368‐p5 and CsTIFY5A negatively regulated the resistance to L. theobromae infection in tea plants. Our findings provide the first evidence of a miR5368‐p5‐CsWRKY57‐CsLRR‐RLK regulatory module in tea plants, which involves an interaction between CsTIFY5A and CsWRKY57, playing a role in the resistance of tea plants to L. theobromae infection. These findings provide potential targets for breeding disease‐resistant tea cultivars.

Results

CsWRKY57 Functioned as a TF in Tea Plants

A pGBKT7‐CsWRKY57 vector was constructed and transformed into the yeast strain AH109 to determine the transcriptional activation potential of CsWRKY57. Results indicate that CsWRKY57 exhibited transcriptional activation activity in yeast cells (Figure 1a). A 35S::CsWRKY57‐enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) fusion vector was also generated and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana cells to determine the subcellular localisation of CsWRKY57. The results revealed that CsWRKY57 was primarily localised in the nucleus (Figure 1b). In contrast, the control displayed a diffuse localisation pattern in the nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane (Figure 1b). Collectively, these results provide evidence that CsWRKY57 functions as a TF in tea plants.

Spatiotemporal Expression of CsWRKY57 in Tea Leaves in Response to L. theobromae Infection

Leaves of different ages, from the first leaf to the third leaf, on shoots of tea plants were inoculated with L. theobromae hyphal plugs and used to determine the spatiotemporal expression of CsWRKY57 from 0 to 48 h post‐inoculation (hpi) (Figure 1c). Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) assay indicated that the trend of CsWRKY57 expression varied in leaves of different ages and at different times (hpi) (Figure 1d–f). CsWRKY57 levels were significantly lower at 24 hpi, relative to 0 h, in the youngest leaves (first leaf) (Figure 1d). The expression of CsWRKY57 in the next oldest leaves (second leaf) did not show any statistically significant changes from 0 to 48 hpi (Figure 1e). CsWRKY57 expression in the oldest leaf (third leaf) exhibited an initial upregulation at 12 hpi, relative to 0 h, followed by a downregulation at 24 hpi, a subsequent upregulation again at 36 hpi, and stabilisation of its expression level at 48 hpi (Figure 1f).

CsWRKY57 Positively Regulates Disease Resistance in Tea Plants

CsWRKY57 was transiently silenced in tea leaves using antisense oligonucleotides (AsODN) to investigate its role in disease resistance in tea plants. RT‐qPCR assays conducted at 24 h post‐treatment with AsODN revealed a 52.9% downregulation in CsWRKY57 expression compared to tea leaves treated with sense oligonucleotides (sODN) (Figure 2a). Notably, lesion areas at 24 hpi increased by 40.7% relative to control when silenced leaves were inoculated with L. theobromae hyphal plugs (Figure 2b,c). Subsequently, CsWRKY57 was transiently overexpressed in tea plants to further determine its functional role in disease resistance. RT‐qPCR assays conducted at 48 h post‐treatment showed a 10.7‐fold upregulation of CsWRKY57 expression compared to the pBI121 empty vector control (Figure 2d). At 24 hpi with L. theobromae, the lesion areas in leaves inoculated with L. theobromae were 35.8% smaller at 24 hpi in leaves in which CsWRKY57 was transiently expressed than in control leaves (Figure 2e,f). Stable CsWRKY57 overexpression lines were generated in N. benthamiana to further validate the role of CsWRKY57 in disease resistance. PCR assays confirmed the stable overexpression of CsWRKY57 in the transgenic N. benthamiana plants (Figure 2g). Transgenic and control leaves were then inoculated with Botrytis cinerea and lesion areas were monitored. Results indicated that transgenic lines exhibited a significant 41.4% reduction in lesion areas compared to wild‐type (WT) plants (Figure 2h,i). These collective results provide strong evidence that CsWRKY57 positively regulates the plant defence response against the pathogens.

CsWRKY57 Interacts With CsTIFY5A

We then identified potential interacting proteins of AtWRKY57, an A. thaliana homologue with high sequence similarity to CsWRKY57, to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying CsWRKY57‐mediated disease resistance. Bioinformatics analysis identified AtTIFY5A as a putative interaction partner of AtWRKY57. Subsequent sequence alignment revealed CsTIFY5A in Citrus sinensis as the closest orthologue of AtTIFY5A (Figure S1). Next, a yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay was used to investigate protein–protein interactions involving CsWRKY57. Preliminary experiments indicated that pGBKT7‐CsWRKY57 exhibited autonomous activation (Figure S2). Therefore, a truncated construct was constructed containing only the C‐terminal domain of CsWRKY57 (pGBKT7‐CsWRKY57 CT). Yeast cells possessing the various plasmid combinations all grew well on synthetic defined (SD)/−Leu/−Trp medium (Figure 3a). Yeast cells possessing the pGBKT7‐CsWRKY57 CT + pGADT7‐CsTIFY5A plasmid combination grew successfully on SD/−Leu/−Trp/−Ade/−His medium and developed blue colonies when cultured on medium supplemented with X‐α‐gal (Figure 3a). A bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay in N. benthamiana leaves was then conducted to further validate this interaction. Co‐expression of CsWRKY57‐YFP C and CsTIFY5A‐YFP N induced the presence of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) signals in the nucleus (Figure 3b). No YFP signals were detected in any of the negative control combinations (Figure 3b). These results provide evidence that CsWRKY57 interacts with CsTIFY5A and that both proteins are localised in the nucleus.

CsTIFY5A Negatively Regulates Disease Resistance in Tea Plants

CsTIFY5A was transiently silenced in tea leaves using AsODN to characterise its role in disease resistance. RT‐qPCR analysis of plants at 24 h post‐silencing by AsODN revealed a 31.4% reduction in CsTIFY5A expression compared to the sODN control (Figure 3c). Lesion areas in silenced leaves inoculated with L. theobromae exhibited a 27.9% reduction at 24 hpi, relative to the control at 24 hpi (Figure 3d,e). Transient overexpression of CsTIFY5A in tea plants was additionally performed to further corroborate its function. Results indicated that the CsTIFY5A expression in plants increased 74.1‐fold compared to control plants injected with the pBI121 empty vector (Figure 3f). Furthermore, lesion areas in transient overexpression tea leaves inoculated with L. theobromae increased by 73.1% at 48 h compared to control plants at 24 hpi (Figure 3g,h). Collectively, these results provide evidence that CsTIFY5A functions as a negative regulator of disease resistance in tea plants.

DNA Affinity Purification‐Seq Assay on CsWRKY57

A DNA affinity purification (DAP)‐seq assay was used to identify CsWRKY57‐regulated genes to provide a better understanding of the mechanism underlying CsWRKY57‐mediated defence against L. theobromae in tea plants. Results revealed that CsWRKY57 potentially bound to 12,325 putative genomic regions (Figure 4a). Genomic distribution analysis indicated that the binding peaks of CsWRKY57 protein were predominantly enriched in intergenic regions (91.65%), with less enrichment in promoter regions (2.78%), intronic regions (2.01%) and exonic regions (1.7%) (Figure 4b). Peak length analysis showed that all peaks were within 2700 bp, with the majority falling within the 200–300 bp range (Figure S3). Further analysis of the binding frequency within ±10 kb regions surrounding transcriptional start sites revealed that CsWRKY57 preferentially bound to DNA sequences proximal to the transcriptional start site (TSS) (Figure S4). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the potential target genes of CsWRKY57 highlighted the significant enrichment in several metabolic processes, including organic acid metabolic processes, oxoacid metabolic processes, carboxylic acid metabolic processes and response to organic substances (Figure 4c). Importantly, DAP‐seq data indicated that CsWRKY57 specifically bound to CsLRR‐RLK by targeting the AGTCAA motif (Figure 4d). Integrating evidence from previous studies suggests that the role of CsLRR‐RLK in plant resistance responses is consistent with the findings of the present study (Di et al. 2019; Wang, Gou, et al. 2025). We hypothesise that CsWRKY57 regulates plant defence responses through the modulation of CsLRR‐RLK expression.

CsWRKY57 Directly Regulates the Expression of CsLRR ‐ RLK

An electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was conducted to confirm the interaction between CsWRKY57 and CsLRR‐RLK. EMSA results reveal that incubating CsWRKY57 with a biotin‐labelled CsLRR‐RLK probe resulted in a shifted band, confirming their direct interaction. The addition of increasing amounts of cold probes gradually reduced the shifted bands, while the use of a mutant probe resulted in a complete loss of binding (Figure 4e). A dual‐luciferase (Dual‐LUC) assay was also conducted in N. benthamiana leaves to further confirm the CsWRKY57–CsLRR‐RLK interaction. Co‐expression of pGreenII 62‐SK‐CsWRKY57 with pGreenII 0800‐LUC‐pro‐CsLRR‐RLK significantly enhanced luminescence intensity and luciferase activity compared to the respective controls (Figure 4f,g). Our results demonstrate that CsWRKY57 directly binds to the CsLRR‐RLK promoter and activates its transcription. Lastly, an RT‐qPCR assay of tea leaves treated with AsODN‐CsWRKY57 and AsODN‐miR5368‐p5 was performed to elucidate the regulatory relationship between CsLRR‐RLK and CsWRKY57/miR5368‐p5. Results indicated that silencing CsWRKY57 led to a downregulation in CsLRR‐RLK expression (Figure 4h). In contrast, silencing miR5368‐p5 significantly upregulated CsLRR‐RLK expression (Figure 4i). These findings suggest that a dynamic balance exists between miR5368‐p5, CsWRKY57 and CsLRR‐RLK in tea plants.

CsLRR‐RLK Positively Regulates Disease Resistance in Tea Plants

An AsODN assay was employed to silence CsLRR‐RLK expression in tea leaves to investigate the potential role of CsLRR‐RLK in disease resistance. RT‐qPCR analysis conducted 24 h post‐silencing revealed a 50.8% reduction in CsLRR‐RLK expression compared to the sODN control (Figure 5a). When silenced leaves at this timepoint were inoculated with L. theobromae hyphal plugs, lesion areas were increased by 36.8% at 24 hpi relative to the sODN control (Figure 5b,c). A transient overexpression assay in tea plants was also conducted to further characterise the functional role of CsLRR‐RLK in disease resistance. RT‐qPCR assay of the samples taken at 48 h post‐overexpression exhibited a 9.1‐fold increase in CsLRR‐RLK expression compared to leaves treated with the pBI121 empty vector control (Figure 5d). Subsequent inoculation of overexpressing and control leaves with L. theobromae resulted in a 22.4% reduction in lesion areas at 24 hpi in CsLRR‐RLK overexpression plants compared to control plants (Figure 5e,f). These results indicate that CsLRR‐RLK functions as a positive regulator of disease resistance in tea plants.

miR5368‐p5 Can Cleave CsWRKY57

Degradome sequencing assay was conducted to confirm the cleavage of CsWRKY57 by miR5368‐p5. The analysis confirmed the interaction between miR5368‐p5 and CsWRKY57, with cleavage occurring at the 310‐nucleotide position (Figure 6a). Further validation was performed using a β‐glucuronidase (GUS) histochemical staining assay conducted on N. benthamiana leaves. Blue staining in the leaves indicated the presence of GUS activity. The co‐expression of 35S::GUS, 35S::CsWRKY57‐GUS and 35S::GUS with 35S::MIR5368‐p5 resulted in blue staining, suggesting that GUS activity was present. In contrast, the 35S::MIR5368‐p5 single construct exhibited no blue staining, indicating the absence of GUS activity (Figure 6b). Notably, co‐expression of 35S::MIR5368‐p5 with 35S::CsWRKY57‐GUS led to significantly reduced blue staining, indicating that GUS activity was substantially diminished or suppressed (Figure 6b). These findings demonstrate that miR5368‐p5 silences CsWRKY57 mRNA, thereby reducing GUS abundance and ultimately resulting in lower or absent GUS activity. The interaction was validated in a Dual‐LUC assay performed on N. benthamiana leaves. Co‐expression of pGreenII 62‐SK‐MIR5368‐p5 with pGreenII 0800‐LUC‐CsWRKY57 resulted in significantly lower luminescence intensity and luciferase activity compared to controls (Figure 6c,d), confirming that miR5368‐p5 targets cleavage of CsWRKY57 mRNA. An RT‐qPCR assay was performed on AsODN‐miR5368‐p5‐treated tea leaves to further elucidate the regulatory relationship between miR5368‐p5 and CsWRKY57. The result reveals a significant upregulation of CsWRKY57 transcript levels following miR5368‐p5 silencing (Figure 6e). Our findings indicate that miR5368‐p5 negatively regulates CsWRKY57 in tea leaves, and that a dynamic equilibrium exists between these elements.

miR5368‐p5 Negatively Regulates Disease Resistance in Tea Plants

Leaves of different ages, from the first to the third leaf on shoots of tea plants, were inoculated with L. theobromae hyphal plugs. The spatiotemporal expression of miR5368‐p5 was assessed using RT‐qPCR. The results indicated that in the first leaf, the relative expression levels of miR5368‐p5 gradually increased from 0 to 48 hpi (Figure 7a). In the second leaf, the relative expression levels of miR5368‐p5 initially increased, then decreased, and finally increased again from 0 to 48 hpi (Figure 7b). In the third leaf, the relative expression levels of miR5368‐p5 also showed a gradual increase from 0 to 48 hpi, although the increase was not significant between 0 and 36 hpi; however, a marked upregulation was observed at 48 hpi (Figure 7c). These results suggest that infection with L. theobromae hyphae affects the expression of miR5368‐p5 in tea leaves. Transient silencing of miR5368‐p5 was conducted in tea leaves using AsODN to investigate the potential role of miR5368‐p5 in disease resistance. RT‐qPCR analysis demonstrated that treatment with AsODN‐miR5368‐p5 for 24 h resulted in a 53.7% reduction in miR5368‐p5 expression compared to sODN‐miR5368‐p5 treatment (Figure 7d). Notably, inoculation with L. theobromae on miR5368‐p5‐silenced plants resulted in a 35.4% reduction in lesion areas at 24 hpi relative to control (Figure 7e,f). We also transiently overexpressed miR5368‐p5 in tea leaves to further determine the functional role of miR5368‐p5 in disease resistance. RT‐qPCR assay revealed that the relative expression level of miR5368‐p5 was 2.1‐fold higher compared to the pBI121 empty vector control (Figure 7g). Leaves at 48 h post‐overexpression were inoculated with L. theobromae. Notably, a 27.6% increase in lesion areas was observed at 24 hpi relative to control (Figure 7h,i). Lastly, we generated transgenic N. benthamiana lines stably expressing miR5368‐p5 to validate these findings in a heterologous system. PCR assays confirmed the successful integration of the miR5368‐p5 gene (Figure 7j). Transgenic lines inoculated with B. cinerea developed lesions that were 91.2% larger at 48 hpi compared to WT control at 48 hpi (Figure 7k,l). Collectively, the data obtained in both transient and stable expression systems demonstrate that miR5368‐p5 functions as a negative regulator of plant defence against pathogens.

Discussion

WRKYs play a functional role in regulating plant immunity and responses to various biotic and abiotic stress signals (Javed and Gao 2023; Wani et al. 2021). In particular, WRKY57 is a significant regulator that has been widely studied in several plant species. However, research on WRKY57 in tea plants has been primarily limited to its role in the methylation of EGCG (Luo et al. 2022). In the present study, the expression of CsWRKY57 mRNA was observed to exhibit a distinct spatiotemporal pattern in tea leaves of different ages and at various times after inoculation with L. theobromae hyphae (Figure 1d–f). These findings prompted us to further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying CsWRKY57‐mediated defence against L. theobromae infection in tea plants. Notably, to date, no studies have investigated the role of CsWRKY57 in tea plant disease resistance. Previous studies reported the involvement of WRKY57 in both abiotic and biotic stress responses in a variety of plant species (Bai et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2012; Jiang and Yu 2016; Ma et al. 2023). For example, SlWRKY57 in Solanum lycopersicum functions as a negative regulator of salt stress response by directly binding to the promoters of salt‐responsive genes, SlRD29B and SlDREB2, suppressing their expression. Furthermore, SlVQ16 and SlVQ21 competitively interact with SlWRKY57 and antagonistically modulate its transcriptional repression activity (Ma et al. 2023). AtWRKY57 in A. thaliana enhances drought tolerance by binding directly to W‐box cis‐elements in the promoters of stress‐responsive genes RD29A and NCED3, activating their expression and promoting abscisic acid biosynthesis (Jiang et al. 2012). Notably, AtWRKY57 also negatively regulates the jasmonic acid (JA) signalling pathway to mediate thplant defence response to B. cinerea infection (Jiang and Yu 2016). McWRKY57‐like in Mentha canadensis confers drought tolerance in transgenic A. thaliana by regulating plant growth parameters, osmotic balance, antioxidant enzyme activity and stress‐responsive gene expression (Bai et al. 2023). Our study provides the first evidence of the role of CsWRKY57 in the defence response of tea plants to the fungal pathogen L. theobromae (Figure 2a–i).

Previous studies have established that plant WRKYs interact with various proteins to accomplish critical biological functions. For instance, several studies highlighted the significant interactions between WRKYs and jasmonate ZIM‐domain proteins (JAZs) that play a significant role in the plant stress response. SCWRKY4 in sugarcane (Saccharum spp.) interacts with ScJAZ13 and suppresses its expression, thereby negatively regulating pathogen resistance (Wang et al. 2024). JA signalling repressors MdJAZ5 and MdJAZ10 in apple ( Malus domestica ) inhibit MdWRKY9 activity. In apples exposed to salt stress, JA signalling initiates the 26S proteasome‐mediated degradation of MdJAZ5/10, thereby releasing MdWRKY9. This release allows MdWRKY9 to activate the expression of the Na +/H + antiporter 1 gene and the salt response‐related gene salt overly sensitive 2, which enhance salt tolerance (Zhao et al. 2025). NCBI domain analysis of TIFY5A indicated the presence of domains similar to those of the JAZ subfamily (Figure S5). In this study, the investigation further revealed that CsWRKY57 physically interacts with CsTIFY5A (Figure 3a,b). Functional characterisation revealed that, in contrast to the disease resistance role of CsWRKY57, CsTIFY5A negatively regulates tea plant resistance against L. theobromae infection (Figure 3c–h). We determined that the interacting proteins CsWRKY57 (a positive regulator) and CsTIFY5A (a negative regulator) collaboratively regulate the defence response of tea plants to L. theobromae. We hypothesise that, under healthy conditions, CsWRKY57 and CsTIFY5A in tea leaves maintain a balanced state through their interaction. Upon L. theobromae infection, external stimuli trigger the dissociation of the CsWRKY57‐CsTIFY5A complex. In response to the pathogen's attack, the tea plant host activates CsWRKY57 to transmit disease resistance signals to downstream proteins, thereby facilitating disease resistance. Once the resistance signal has been transmitted, CsWRKY57 and CsTIFY5A rebind to restore their original complex. Our findings therefore provide a foundation for understanding the regulatory mechanisms carried out by the WRKY‐TIFY module in tea plants in response to L. theobromae.

We demonstrated that CsWRKY57 localise to the nucleus and possesses intrinsic transcriptional activation capacity (Figure 1a,b). The results of the DAP‐seq assay identified CsLRR‐RLK as a candidate target gene of CsWRKY57 (Figure 4d). EMSA and Dual‐LUC assays confirmed that CsWRKY57 directly binds to the promoter of CsLRR‐RLK and enhances its transcription (Figure 4e–g). Further experiments also validated the role of CsLRR‐RLK in disease resistance and revealed a dynamic equilibrium between the expression levels of CsWRKY57 and CsLRR‐RLK in tea leaves (Figures 4h and 5a–f). Thus, our study provides the first evidence that CsWRKY57 directly binds to and activates the promoter of CsLRR‐RLK and demonstrates the positive role played by CsLRR‐RLK in the defence response of tea plants to L. theobromae. Notably, only a few studies reported the involvement of LRR‐RLK family members in the disease response of tea plants. In this regard, combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses indicated that exogenous melatonin treatment upregulates key receptor kinase genes, including LRR‐RLKs, thereby activating a plant–pathogen interaction pathway (Di et al. 2019). However, LRR‐RLKs have been well characterised for their diverse functions in other plant species. For example, the maize ( Zea mays ) LRR receptor kinase ZmMIK2 is reported to interact with C2‐domain‐containing protein 1 (ZmC2DP1), thereby enhancing the phosphorylation and stability of ZmC2DP1. The ZmMIK2‐ZmC2DP1 module acts as a negative regulator in the response of maize to drought and salt stress (Yang et al. 2025). A. thaliana plants overexpressing the AhLRR‐RLK265 gene from peanut (Arachis hypogea) exhibit elevated superoxide dismutase, catalase and peroxidase activity, resulting in enhanced salt and drought resistance (Wang et al. 2023). Additionally, ScWRKY2 is reported to directly bind to the disease resistance gene ScLRR‐RLK and suppress its expression, thereby acting as a negative regulator of sugarcane resistance to smut disease (Wang, Gou, et al. 2025). Therefore, we believe that research on the disease resistance of CsLRR‐RLK in tea plants is highly worthy of attention in the future.

The functional studies of miR5368 in plant species have not been widely reported. An omics‐level study of miR5368 in Medicago truncatula indicated its responsiveness to drought stress (Li et al. 2017). Bioinformatics analysis suggested that Ipomoea batatas miR5368 may be involved in salt tolerance mechanisms through competing endogenous RNA networks (Jiang et al. 2025). In our study, degradome sequencing, GUS histochemical staining and Dual‐LUC assays indicated that miR5368‐p5 specifically targets and cleaves CsWRKY57 mRNA (Figure 6a–d). Further comparison of the dynamic expression of miR5368‐p5 and CsWRKY57 in leaves of different ages (from the first to the third) on shoots of tea plants inoculated with L. theobromae hyphae revealed a correlation in their expression patterns to some extent (Figures 1d–f and 7a–c). Therefore, we believe that the behaviour of miR5368‐p5 and CsWRKY57 in tea plant leaves following L. theobromae hyphal infection warrants significant attention. Building on previous results, RT‐qPCR analysis of tea leaves silenced with AsODN‐miR5368‐p5 further demonstrated a significant increase in CsWRKY57 expression and a marked decrease in CsLRR‐RLK expression (Figures 4i and 6e). This suggests a dynamic regulatory relationship between the expression of miR5368‐p5, CsWRKY57 and CsLRR‐RLK in tea leaves, indicating the presence of the miR5368‐p5‐CsWRKY57‐CsLRR‐RLK module in the tea plant. The presence of the module also raises the question: Is miR5368‐p5 associated with the resistance of tea plants to L. theobromae infection? The AsODN‐mediated silencing, transient overexpression in tea leaves, and stable genetic transformation in N. benthamiana conducted in our study indicated that miR5368‐p5 negatively regulates the disease resistance of tea plants to L. theobromae infection (Figure 7d–i). Therefore, we were able to document the presence of a miR5368‐p5‐CsWRKY57‐CsLRR‐RLK module and its involvement in the resistance of tea plants to L. theobromae.

In conclusion, our study provides the first evidence of a miR5368‐p5‐CsWRKY57‐CsLRR‐RLK module that mediates the defence response of tea plants to L. theobromae infection. Within this module, CsWRKY57 and CsLRR‐RLK positively regulates disease resistance in tea plants, while miR5368‐p5 negatively regulates disease resistance. Furthermore, CsTIFY5A, a transcription factor that interacts with CsWRKY57, negatively regulated disease resistance and participates in the regulation of the miR5368‐p5‐CsWRKY57‐CsLRR‐RLK module, thereby influencing disease resistance in tea plants. Thus, our study also provides potential targets for the molecular breeding of disease‐resistant tea cultivars.

However, the present study has several limitations. First, whether CsLRR‐RLK recognises pathogen‐associated molecular patterns needs to be determined. Previous studies demonstrated that leucine‐rich repeat receptor kinase flagellin‐sensitive 2 recognises bacterial flagellin in A. thaliana as a pathogen‐associated molecular pattern (PAMP) and mediates resistance against bacterial pathogens (Gómez‐Gómez and Boller 2000). Furthermore, Bri1‐associated kinase 1 has been shown to interact with the plant hormone receptor BRI1 to regulate developmental processes and with leucine‐rich repeat receptor kinase flagellin‐sensitive 2 to enhance innate immunity through the recognition of bacterial flagellin (Chinchilla et al. 2007). Second, the molecular mechanisms underlying CsTIFY5A‐mediated negative regulation of disease resistance remain unclear. JAZs are known to function as key repressors in the JA signalling pathway (Chini et al. 2007; Kazan and Manners 2012; Thines et al. 2007). JAZs play a critical role in plant defence against fungal pathogens infection and insect attack (Guo et al. 2018; He et al. 2018; Thatcher et al. 2016). Our future studies will be designed to further elucidate the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying this regulatory axis.

Experimental Procedures

Plant Materials and Pathogen Inoculation

Five‐year‐old tea plants ( C. sinensis ‘Fuding‐dabaicha’) grown under controlled greenhouse conditions were used for pathogen inoculation and subsequent gene expression analysis. N. benthamiana plants were cultivated in a growth chamber under controlled environmental conditions (25°C, 80% relative humidity, and a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod). L. theobromae CGMCC3.20151 and B. cinerea CGMCC3.20932 were cultured on potato dextrose agar and incubated at 25°C in darkness. Pathogen virulence was determined using hyphal plugs as described in our research group (Guo et al. 2025). L. theobromae was inoculated onto tea leaves for 24 h, while B. cinerea was inoculated onto N. benthamiana leaves for 48 h. Each experiment included at least three biological replicates per treatment.

Total RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the test samples using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TransGen). First‐strand cDNA synthesis for mRNA was performed using the Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen) in conjunction with oligo(dT)18 primers for reverse transcription. For miRNA cDNA synthesis, specific reverse transcription was carried out using the stem‐loop method with the Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen). RT‐qPCR was conducted using Hieff UNICON Advanced qPCR SYBR Master Mix (Yeasen). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). A list of all the primers used in this portion of the study is provided in Table S1.

Subcellular Localisation of CsWRKY57

The coding sequence (CDS) of CsWRKY57, omitting the termination codon, was fused to the pCAMBIA2300‐EGFP vector through homologous recombination, resulting in the 35S::CsWRKY57‐EGFP fusion vector. The fusion vector was subsequently introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup). Following this, epidermal tissues from N. benthamiana leaves were infiltrated with the transformed Agrobacterium and incubated for 48 h. GFP fluorescence signals were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss). pCAMBIA2300‐EGFP empty vector was used as a negative control. A list of the primers used in this portion of the study is provided in Table S1.

Transactivation Activity Assay

The CDS of CsWRKY57 was inserted into the pGBKT7 vector via homologous recombination to generate the pGBKT7‐CsWRKY57 fusion vector. pGBKT7 and pGBKT7‐p53 vectors were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. Plasmids were independently transformed into yeast strain AH109. The transformed yeast cells were cultured on SD media, including SD/−Trp, SD/−Trp−His, SD/−Trp−His+X‐α‐gal (20 mg/L), SD/−Trp−His−Ade and SD/−Trp−His−Ade+X‐α‐gal (20 mg/L), then incubated at 30°C for 3 days. Colony growth and X‐α‐gal staining reactions were observed. A list of the primers used in this portion of the study is provided in Table S1.

Spatiotemporal CsWRKY57 mRNA Expression

Mycelial plugs of L. theobromae were inoculated onto the first to third detached leaves of tea plants. Leaf samples were collected at 0, 12, 24, 36 and 48 hpi, and the mRNA abundance of CsWRKY57 was analysed using RT‐qPCR assay.

AsODN Silencing Assay in Tea Plants

AsODN probes were designed using the Soligo online tool (https://sfold.wadsworth.org/) (Ding and Lawrence 2003). AsODN was performed as previously described, with minor modifications (Jiao et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2021). sODN was used as a negative control. Tea shoots, with one apical bud and two leaves, were detached and then placed with 30‐μM oligonucleotide solutions via in vitro infiltration. Oligonucleotide sequences used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Stable Transformation of N. benthamiana

The CDS of CsWRKY57 and the precursor sequence of miR5368‐p5 were individually inserted into the pBI121 vector via homologous recombination. Transgenic N. benthamiana plants were generated according to an established protocol with minor modifications (Horsch et al. 1985). Briefly, explants were prepared from cotyledons excised from 2‐month‐old N. benthamiana seedlings using the leaf disk method. These explants were then inoculated by agroinfiltration with A. tumefaciens GV3101 harbouring either the pBI121‐CsWRKY57 or pBI121‐MIR5368‐p5. Putative transgenic plants were selected on Murashige and Skoog medium supplemented with 50 mg/L kanamycin. Genomic DNA was extracted from candidate plants and subjected to PCR analysis using gene‐specific primers to confirm the integration of the transgene. WT N. benthamiana plants were used as the control in subsequent virulence assays. Primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Prediction of Protein–Protein Interactions

Candidate interacting proteins were screened using the STRING database (https://string‐db.org) to elucidate the disease resistance mechanism mediated by CsWRKY57. A cross‐species approach based on A. thaliana orthologues was employed due to the lack of C. sinensis ‐specific interaction data. Sequence alignment revealed that CsWRKY57 shares the highest homology with AtWRKY57. As a result, a protein–protein interaction network was constructed for AtWRKY57 to identify potential interacting proteins for CsWRKY57.

Y2H Assay

CsWRKY57 CT was inserted into the pGBKT7 vector, and the CDS of CsTIFY5A was inserted into the pGADT7 vector. Control systems included pGBKT7‐p53 + pGADT7‐T (positive control) and pGBKT7‐Lam + pGADT7‐T (negative control). Plasmid combinations were co‐transformed into AH109 competent cells via polyethylene glycol (PEG)/lithium acetate‐mediated transformation. The transformed cells were cultured at 30°C for 3 days on SD/−Leu/−Trp, SD/−Leu/−Trp/−Ade/−His and SD/−Leu/−Trp/−Ade/−His + X‐α‐gal (20 mg/L) media to identify protein–protein interactions. Primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

BIFC Assay

The CDS of CsWRKY57 was inserted into the pSPYCE vector, while CsTIFY5A was inserted into the pSPYNE vector. Recombinant plasmids were separately transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup) and co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. YFP fluorescence signals were examined at 48 h post‐infiltration using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss). Primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Transient Overexpression in Tea Plants

The CDSs of CsWRKY57, CsTIFY5A and CsLRR‐RLK, along with the miR5368‐p5 precursor sequence, were independently inserted into the pBI121 vector via homologous recombination. Recombinant plasmids, along with the empty pBI121 control, were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101, and bacterial suspensions were uniformly infiltrated along both sides of the midvein of tea plant leaves. Samples were collected after 48 h post‐infiltration and subjected to RT‐qPCR and pathogen inoculation assays to determine levels of disease resistance. Primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

DAP‐Seq Assay

DAP‐seq assay was performed according to the previously described protocol (O'Malley et al. 2016). Genomic DNA was extracted from tea leaves and fragmented into approximately 200 bp fragments. Adenosine monophosphate was ligated to the end of the fragmented DNA to create an adaptor. The CDS of CsWRKY57 was inserted into the p30a‐HaloTag vector, and CsWRKY57 was synthesised using the TNT SP6 wheat germ cell‐free expression system (Promega). The synthesised protein was immobilised on Magne HaloTag magnetic beads. The CsWRKY57‐DNA binding reaction was carried out in binding buffer at 25°C for 1 h. Unbound DNA was removed by washing with the binding buffer. Subsequently, the bound DNA was eluted, and the library was amplified using Phusion High‐Fidelity DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher) to generate a sequencing‐ready library. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform. Raw sequencing reads were quality‐filtered using Trimmomatic (v. 0.36) and aligned to the tea plant genome database (TPIA; https://tpia.teaplants.cn) using Bowtie v. 2. Significant binding peaks were identified using MACS2 (v. 2.2.7.1). Binding motifs were predicted using MEME (v. 5.0.5), and genes associated with these peaks were subjected to functional enrichment analysis via Gene Ontology using Goatools (v. 0.9.9) (https://github.com/tanghaibao/GOatools). Primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

EMSA

EMSA was performed according to an adapted protocol (Zhang et al. 2017). The biotin‐labelled WT probe, corresponding cold probes and biotin‐labelled mutant probe were incubated with purified recombinant HaloTag‐CsWRKY57 protein to investigate the interaction using a Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Beyotime). The probes used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Dual‐LUC Assay

Dual‐LUC assay was conducted as previously described with minor modifications (Li, Ma, et al. 2024). The CDS of CsWRKY57 and the miR5368‐p5 precursor was inserted into the pGreenII 62‐SK vector, while the CsLRR‐RLK promoter and CsWRKY57 mRNA sequences were inserted into the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector. Recombinant plasmids were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 (pSou) and co‐infiltrated into distinct regions of N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h, 1 mM D‐luciferin potassium salt (Beyotime) was applied to the infiltration sites. Following a 5‐min incubation at 37°C, the leaves were immediately analysed using ChemiScope Capture image acquisition software and an imaging system. Firefly luciferase and renilla luciferase activity were measured using a Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Yeasen) on a Multi‐Function Measuring Instrument (Allsheng). Primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Degradome Sequencing Assay

Degradome sequencing assay was conducted using established protocols with minor modifications (Lin et al. 2016). Two independent degradome libraries were constructed: one from healthy tea leaves (control group) and another from L. theobromae‐infected leaves (treatment group). miRNA target prediction was performed in parallel using the ACGT101‐DEG analysis system (LC Sciences; http://www.lcsciences.com/) and CleaveLand v. 3.0 (https://sites.psu.edu/axtell/software/cleaveland3/).

Histochemical GUS Staining Assay

The CsWRKY57 mRNA sequence was inserted into the pBI121 vector upstream of the GUS coding region, while the miR5368‐p5 precursor sequence replaced the GUS gene within the pBI121 vector. This generated the fusion vectors 35S::CsWRKY57‐GUS and 35S::miR5368‐p5. The resulting fusion vectors were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 and co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves at a 1:1 ratio. GUS activity was visualised by histochemical staining following established protocols (Jefferson et al. 1987). Primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism v. 10.2. Student's t tests were used to assess significant statistical differences between the sample types. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (n ≥ 3).
