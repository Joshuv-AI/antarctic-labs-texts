Genetic Association of Lipids and Lipid‐Lowering Drug Target Genes With Atopic Dermatitis: A Drug Target Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Min Luo, Jinger Lin, Qianwei Zhuo, Lihang Lin, Yue Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSeveral observational studies have reported inconsistent associations between dyslipidaemia, stains use and atopic dermatitis (AD). Nevertheless, the available data on the effects of –C‐lowering as well as TG‐lowering drugs remain inconclusive and limited. The aim of this study was to evaluate the causal association of lipid traits and long‐term use of lipid‐lowering drugs on AD risk.MethodsDrug‐targeted Mendelian randomization analyses were performed in European ancestry. Pooled statistics on low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglyceride and AD were extracted from large genome‐wide association studies datasets. Instrumental variables located in and around lipid‐lowering target genes were used as proxies for therapeutic inhibition of these target genes. Inverse‐variance‐weighted approach was applied as the primary analysis.ResultsWe explored the role of seven lipid‐lowering target genes in AD, among which genetically proxied Niemann‐Pick C1–like 1 inhibition, a target of LDL‐C‐lowering drugs, was associated with an increased risk of AD (odds ratio, 3.03; 95% CI, 1.36–6.75; p = 0.007). This association was replicated in FinnGen cohort (odds ratio, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.09–2.72; p = 0.020). A series of sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the estimates.ConclusionWe found no genetic support for the repurposing of seven lipid‐lowering drug targets for the prevention of AD. However, pharmacovigilance of AD risk in Niemann‐Pick C1–like 1 inhibitor users may be warranted.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a recurrent and pruritic inflammatory skin disease characterized by skin barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation in genetically susceptible individuals [1]. Recent studies have suggested a potential link between AD and disordered lipid metabolism, which may contribute to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [2, 3, 4]. However, the existing evidence regarding these associations remains inconsistent [5, 6].

Statins are commonly used to lower low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) levels and are considered the standard treatment for hypercholesterolemia in clinical practice [7]. However, reports of certain dermatologic complications associated with statin use have raised concerns in the dermatological community [8, 9, 10]. However, Cheung et al. noted that their criteria included unspecified dermatitis, a potential confounder that may not accurately reflect eczema. On the other hand, a research has suggested that fluvastatin may alleviate skin inflammation in patients with AD [11]. Nevertheless, the available data on the effects of other classes of LDL‐C‐lowering and triglyceride (TG)‐lowering drugs, such as Ezetimibe, Mipomersen, and Fibrates, remain inconclusive and limited. Understanding the causal pathways related to lipid metabolism could enhance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving AD development and facilitate the development of more effective interventions for AD patients in high‐risk individuals with dyslipidemia.

Mendelian randomization (MR) takes advantage of the random inheritance of genotypes at conception, similar to the allocation in a randomized controlled trial (RCT), which reduces susceptibility to confounding and reverse causation biases [12]. MR is a highly sensitive method to assess whether genetically proxied drug targets are causal and is recommended as a preliminary step before conducting RCTs [13]. Moreover, since genetic variants remain fixed throughout a person's lifetime, MR provides valuable insights into the long‐term consequences of therapeutic exposure, aiding in drug target validation [13].

In this study, we utilized a drug‐target MR analysis to explore the association between genetically predicted lipid traits, including LDL‐C and TG, and seven lipid‐modifying targets. These targets comprised LDL‐C‐lowering target genes, namely LDL receptor (LDLR), HMG‐CoA reductase (HMGCR), Niemann‐Pick C1‐like protein 1 (NPC1L1), and apolipoprotein B‐100 (APOB), and TG‐lowering target genes, namely lipoprotein lipase (LPL), peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐a (PPARA), and apolipoprotein C‐III (APOC3), with the risk of AD.

Methods

Study Design

To investigate the relationship between genetically proxied inhibition of lipid‐lowering drug targets, as well as overall LDL‐C and triglyceride (TG) levels on the risk of AD, we conducted a two‐sample MR analysis by extracting summary statistics from large‐scale genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) with European ancestry. The overview of the study design is shown in Figure 1. The summary‐level GWAS data used in this study were publicly available and had obtained approvals from their respective Institutional Review Boards. Genetic Association for Atopic Dermatitis

In our primary analysis, the genetic instrumental variables (IVs) for AD were obtained from a GWAS meta‐analysis conducted by the Early Genetics and Lifecourse Epidemiology (EAGLE) eczema consortium. This GWAS meta‐analysis involved 20 population‐based cohorts comprising 10,788 cases and 30,047 controls, excluding the 23andMe study [14]. To replicate the analyses, we extracted AD summary‐level GWAS data involving 13,473 cases and 336,589 controls from the latest FinnGen study (release 9) [15].

Genetic Proxies for Lipid‐Lowering Drugs

We selected lipid‐lowering drugs based on recent guidelines for the management of dyslipidaemia [16, 17]. The pharmacological target genes for each class of lipid‐lowering drugs were identified using the DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com/) and references to previous reviews [18, 19].

These target genes were further classified into LDL‐C‐lowering target genes, namely LDLR, HMGCR, NPC1L1, and APOB, and TG‐lowering target genes, namely LPL, PPARA, and APOC3, based on their primary pharmacological effects. Genetic IVs for lipid‐lowering drug targets were obtained from the largest and most recent GWAS conducted by the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium (GLGC) on TG levels (n = 1,320,016) and LDL‐C (n = 1,320,016) [20]. To mitigate potential biases arising from sample overlap, we specifically extracted genetic associations for LDL‐C (n = 1,177,987) and TG (n = 1,177,987) from GLGC and excluded participants of Finnish descent, which were then used in our replication analysis.

The IVs for genetically proxied target genes were significantly associated with LDL‐C or TG at the genome‐wide significance level (p < 5 × 10−8) and showed minimal correlation (linkage disequilibrium r 2 < 0.1). We used PLINK and Phase 3 of the European 1000 Genomes Project as a reference panel to locate the single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within a 100 kb region surrounding the respective target genes [21]. Furthermore, we examined whether genetically predicted overall LDL‐C or TG levels were associated with the risk of AD. The IVs for overall LDL‐C or TG levels were identified at the genome‐wide significance level and showed pairwise correlations of r 2 < 0.001.

Positive Control Analyses

As a positive control, we included an analysis using coronary artery disease (CAD) as the outcome. The summary GWAS data for CAD were obtained from the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D consortium, which included 60,801 cases and 123,504 controls [22]. The inclusion of CAD in the analysis served to validate the instrument validity.

Mendelian Randomization Analysis

The F‐statistic was calculated to measure the strength of each IV, with values less than 10 indicating that the IV is weak, which may bias the results of the MR [23]. The F‐statistics was calculated by the following formula: F = (beta^2)/(SE^2) [24]. R 2 is the proportion of AD variability explained by each instrument, and R 2 was calculated using the 2 × beta^2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) [25]. To ensure consistency in effect estimates, we harmonized the exposure and outcome SNPs. We also excluded potentially ambiguous SNPs with inconsistent alleles and palindromic SNPs from the analysis.

The multiplicative random‐effect inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the main analysis method [26]. In addition, MR‐Egger and weighted median methods were applied as complementary MR methods [27]. The MR‐Egger method provides more robust estimates in the presence of pleiotropy, although with larger confidence intervals (CIs) [28, 29]. It is statistically less efficient, meaning that even if a strong relationship is observed using the IVW method, the estimates provided by the MR‐Egger method are likely to be near‐null.

Sensitivity Analyses

To verify the robustness of the results, we performed sensitivity analyses. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q statistics for the IVW method, with a p‐value < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity [26]. The presence of horizontal pleiotropy was assessed by examining the intercept term of the MR‐Egger regression, with deviation from zero indicating the presence of pleiotropy [28]. We also conducted a leave‐one‐out analysis to assess the influence of individual SNPs on the effect estimates.

Colocalization

For the NPC1L1 inhibitors, which showed a causal relationship with AD risk in the MR analysis, we conducted colocalization analyses using the coloc method. This method quantifies the probability of a shared genetic variant between gene expression and AD (H4) within a range of ±300 kb of the NPC1L1 gene region [30, 31].

Statistical Methods

All statistical analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR and coloc packages in R software (version 4.2.3). To account for multiple testing, the Bonferroni method was applied to adjust the nominal thresholds (p < 0.05). Specifically, a significance threshold of 0.05/7 = 0.007 was set for the seven drug targets. Relationships with p‐values < 0.05 but Bonferroni‐adjusted p‐values > 0.05 were considered suggestive.

Results

Genetic Instruments for Lipid‐Lowering Drugs

We utilized a total of 40 genetic variants as proxies for inhibiting LDLR, 21 for HMGCR, 11 for NPC1L1, 18 for APOB, 33 for LPL, 3 for PPARA, 24 for APOC3, 390 for total LDL‐C levels, and 454 for TG in the primary analysis.

These variants were identified through the latest GWAS conducted by the GLGC. More information on the specific SNPs used in this study could be referred to Tables S6 and S7. The F‐statistics for all the genetic IVs associated with the drug targets, as well as overall LDL‐C and TG were greater than 10. These statistics indicate that our results are unlikely to be biased due to weak IVs. Furthermore, genetically proxied inhibition of all drug targets showed an association with a reduced risk of coronary artery disease, which served as our positive control outcome (Tables S2 and S3).

Mendelian Randomization and Sensitivity Analysis

The genetically proxied inhibition of NPC1L1 was associated with an increased risk of developing AD (IVW MR odds ratio (OR) = 3.03, 95% confidence interval (95% CI) = 1.36 to 6.75, p = 0.007 per standard deviation reduction in LDL‐C) (Figure 2). This finding was further supported by the replication analysis using data from the FinnGen study (IVW MR odds ratio (OR) = 1.72; 95% CI, 1.09 to 2.72; p = 0.020) (Figure 3). However, we did not observe a significant association between the expression of inhibition of HMGCR, LDLR, APOB, LPL, PPARA, APOC3, and AD (Figures 2 and 3). To provide a comparative perspective, we also examined the impact of overall circulating LDL‐C and TG levels on AD risk. However, there was limited evidence suggesting a genetic effect of LDL‐C levels or TG on the risk of AD. This finding was consistent with the results of validation analysis using AD data from the FinnGen study.

The sensitivity analyses conducted in this study provided no statistical evidence indicating bias from horizontal pleiotropy (Tables S4 and S5). Additionally, the leave‐one‐out analyses suggest that the overall estimate was not influenced by a single variant of significant influence (Figures S1 and S2).

Colocalization Analysis

The probability of a causal variant associated with blood LDL‐C or AD risk within the NPC1L1 gene was found to be 90.8%, and 0.0%, respectively. The probability of distinct causal variants existing between these two traits was 7.6%, while the probability of a shared causal variant was 1.6% (Table S8 and Figure S3).

Discussion

In this MR study, inhibition of lipid‐lowering drug targets (HMGCR, LDLR, APOB, LPL, PPARA, and APOC3) was found not to be significantly associated with the risk of AD, possibly through a mechanism other than target effects on LDL‐C or TG, as no relationship was identified between overall LDL or TG levels and AD risk. Additionally, the study suggests a possible increased susceptibility to AD in individuals exposed to NPC1L1 inhibitors.

The available observational data have yielded inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between blood lipid traits and AD [3, 6, 32, 33, 34]. Adipose tissue secretes soluble adipokines, which can influence inflammatory and immune responses. These responses, in turn, stimulate the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, contributing to a chronic inflammatory state [35]. Dyslipidemia can shift the immune response from Th1 to a stronger Th2 response, creating an imbalance between anti‐ and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)‐10, IL‐17, and IL‐23, respectively, and this imbalance may exacerbate persistent inflammation seen in atopic conditions [36, 37]. These findings support the interpretation that dyslipidemia may contribute to chronic skin inflammation, such as AD. Nevertheless, it is important to recognize that observational epidemiology is susceptible to biases from confounding and reverse causation. Based on the available evidence, this study does not establish blood LDL‐C and TG levels as causal risk factors for AD, suggesting that primary dyslipidemia may not play a significant role in the development of AD.

Statins are widely used drugs that effectively lower cholesterol levels by inhibiting the enzyme HMG‐CoA reductase. Recent studies have highlighted their pleiotropic effects, including anti‐inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties [38]. One of the notable effects of statins is their ability to inhibit the differentiation of T cells into the pro‐inflammatory Th17 phenotype while promoting the formation of anti‐inflammatory T regulatory cells [39]. They also regulate Th2 responses mediated by dendritic cells and suppress the infiltration of Th2 cells into skin lesions, thereby reducing skin inflammation [11, 40]. These findings suggest that statins may have therapeutic potential in allergic diseases such as asthma and AD [41].

Glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and fatty acids are implicated in inflammation associated with AD. In AD patients, phosphatidylcholine is downregulated in both serum and lesional skin, but its levels increase following dupilumab treatment [42, 43, 44]. Linoleic acid is an omega‐6 PUFA, is found at elevated plasma concentrations in individuals with AD [45].

Considering the mechanism of action of statins, which involves reducing cholesterol, it is important to investigate the relationship between statins and epidermal lipids further. This MR study did not find an increased or decreased risk of AD with long‐term exposure to statins. Consequently, this discovery effectively alleviates previous concerns about the use of statins in AD patients. On the other hand, these MR results do not support the need for RCTs to explore the repurposing of HMGCR inhibitors for the treatment of AD.

Ezetimibe is a drug used to lower lipid levels by blocking the intestinal cholesterol transporter NPC1L1. Its mechanism of action reduces blood cholesterol levels [46]. Studies have shown that ezetimibe has anti‐inflammatory effects independent of its lipid‐lowering properties. It inhibits the differentiation of Th17 cells in a mouse model of ankylosing spondylitis [47]. However, the effect of NPC1L1 inhibitors on patients with AD has not been reported. In our study using MR analysis, we found a causal suggestive relationship between genetic variations in NPC1L1 and AD. Traditionally, AD has been considered a disorder that primarily affects early childhood, but recent studies have revealed that its prevalence in the adult population is considerable, suggesting that AD may be a chronic condition persisting throughout life [48]. Therefore, it is advisable to prioritise other lipid‐lowering therapies for patients susceptible to AD. Although the efficacy of NPC1L1 inhibitors as lipid‐lowering agents is well documented, these preliminary findings must be interpreted with caution until more extensive data are available.

Strengths and Limitations

This MR study has several strengths. The main advantages are the robustness of results to replication (using data from EAGLE eczema consortium and the FinnGen study) and the utilization of large‐scale GWAS datasets. MR study is relatively straightforward, cost‐effective, and time‐efficient compared to other study designs [49]. Conducting cohort studies to investigate the association between lipid‐lowering drugs and AD risk and prognosis would be challenging due to indication bias, reverse causation, and limitations in patient population size [50].

It is important to acknowledge some limitations when interpreting the results of this study. Firstly, the genetic variants used in this study reflect the long‐term consequences of lipid levels on AD risk, and their effect size may not directly reflect the short‐term impact of lipid‐lowering drugs [51]. Secondly, the original GWAS data were not stratified by specific AD subtypes or age groups, which limits our ability to perform stratified analyses. However, this remains an area of ongoing research interest, and future studies using specific datasets for these subgroups should consider stratified analysis. Lastly, since our study predominantly includes individuals of European ancestry, caution should be exercised when generalizing our findings to other ethnic groups.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our research indicates that levels of circulating LDL‐C and TG levels were not associated with AD risk. Meanwhile, this study did not find an increased or decreased risk of AD with long‐term exposure to six lipid‐lowering drugs, namely LDLR, HMGCR, NPC1L1, APOB, LPL, PPARA, and APOC3. Furthermore, our study identifies a potential link between inhibitors of NPC1L1 and an increased risk of AD, highlighting the importance of pharmacovigilance among users of NPC1L1 inhibitors. In patients with a predisposition to AD, it is recommended that other lipid‐lowering treatments be considered. To validate this finding, larger GWAS datasets and additional potentially associated genetic variants need to be investigated. Future research should prioritize elucidating the underlying mechanisms involved in this potential association.
