Enhancing Hepatitis B and C Prevention Among People Who Use Drugs: Evaluating an Educational Intervention at a Drop-In Center in Southwest Iran based on Protection Motivation Theory
Authors: Tayebeh Rakhshani, Siavash Chalesh, Ali Arman, Samira Taravatmanesh, Ali Khani Jeihooni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubstance abuse, particularly injection drug use, remains a significant public health challenge in Iran, where an estimated 2 million people use illicit drugs daily. Injection drug users are at high risk for bloodborne infections such as hepatitis B and C, which contribute substantially to morbidity and mortality in this population. Understanding individual factors that influence risky behaviors through frameworks like the Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) is essential for effective prevention. This study aimed to assess the impact of a PMT-based educational intervention on reducing hepatitis B and C risk behaviors among addicts attending Drop-In Centers (DICs) in Iran.MethodA quasi-experimental study was conducted on 101 injection drug users registered with the Drop-in Center (DIC) in southwest Iran. Participants were selected via census sampling and randomly assigned to intervention (n = 50) and control (n = 51) groups. Data were collected using a validated, researcher-designed questionnaire based on PMT, measuring constructs including knowledge, attitude, perceived vulnerability and severity, self-efficacy, maladaptive rewards, response costs, and protective behaviors. The intervention consisted of eight educational sessions over two months, designed to target these PMT constructs through lectures, discussions, role-playing, and problem-solving. Pre- and post-intervention questionnaires were administered to both groups, and data were analyzed using paired and independent t-tests, chi-square, and McNemar’s tests via SPSS version 27.ResultsAt baseline, there was no significant difference between the intervention and control groups in mean scores of knowledge, attitude, perceived vulnerability, perceived severity, perceived self-efficacy, perceived maladaptive rewards, perceived response costs, and behavior (p > 0.05). However, after the intervention, a significant difference was observed in the constructs of the model in the intervention group compared to the control group (p < 0.001), with large between-group effect sizes ranging from d = 1.45 to 6.36, indicating a substantial impact of the intervention on all PMT constructs.ConclusionThe PMT-based educational intervention significantly improved hepatitis B and C preventive behaviors among injecting drug users. These findings suggest that theory-driven, low-cost educational programs grounded in behavioral science can be effectively integrated into harm reduction services among high-risk, marginalized populations. Future studies should assess long-term sustainability, replicate results in diverse and larger settings, and validate outcomes using objective behavioral measures to support broader implementation and policy integration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24789-4.

Background

Substance abuse is characterized as the harmful use of a broad variety of psychoactive drugs, including cannabis, cough medicines, methamphetamine, heroin, sedatives, opioids, cocaine, and ecstasy, all of which have the potential to cause a wide range of physiological and psychological issues [1]. The global deaths due to drug use rose from 1.84 per 1,000,000 individuals in 1990 to 13.09 per 1,000,000 individuals in 2021 [2]. In Iran, drug use has increased in recent years, partly because it borders Afghanistan to the east, which is the leading global producer of opium. Iran also serves as a key transit route for drug shipments to Europe [3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), Iran has the highest rate of opium consumption globally, with usage rates approximately three times greater than the global average [4]. It is estimated that nearly 2 million individuals in Iran use illicit drugs daily, accounting for around 2.7% of the country’s population [5].

Among individuals who misuse substances, common methods of administration include ingestion, inhalation, smoking, and injection. Injection is one of the most hazardous methods of drug administration due to its rapid drug delivery and elevated health risks. Commonly injected substances include heroin, methamphetamine, cocaine, and prescription opioids [6]. In addition to the drugs’ direct effects, injection significantly increases the risk of bloodborne infections such as hepatitis B and C [7]. Hepatitis C, in particular, poses a major public health concern due to its high rate of chronic infection and potential to cause long-term liver damage. Thus, injection drug use represents both a behavioral and biomedical threat, underscoring the need for targeted prevention strategies [8, 9].

Chronic viral hepatitis can lead to severe conditions such as cirrhosis, liver cancer, and increased mortality. Fortunately, many of these outcomes are preventable through the reduction of high-risk behaviors, particularly unsafe injection practices [10]. To this end, educational interventions grounded in behavioral science have proven effective. For example, a study of 406 migrant workers in China, using the protection motivation theory (PMT), found that perceived severity, self-efficacy, and response costs significantly influenced HBV vaccination intentions [11, 12]. Another study conducted in the south of Iran, involved 300 adolescent boys and examined the impact of a Theory of Planned Behaviour-based intervention on preventing drug abuse, where the experimental group received five 60-minute training sessions. The findings demonstrated the effectiveness of the intervention in significantly enhancing behavior-related constructs among participants [13].

Educational models have shown strong potential in shaping health-related behaviors, particularly among high-risk groups such as injection drug users [14]. Among these, the PMT stands out as one of the most notable educational frameworks [15–17]. This theory, formulated by Rogers in 1975 based on the expectancy-value theory, describes the role of fear in health-related attitudes and behaviors. According to the theory, the intention to engage in suggested health behaviors is directly reliant on an individual’s drive to safeguard their health. Rogers states that fear can affect protection motivation via various constructs, including self-efficacy, response efficacy, response costs, perceived susceptibility, and perceived severity [18]. The PMT was selected over other models such as Health Belief Model, and Theory of Planned Behavior, because it uniquely combines threat appraisal, including perceived vulnerability, severity with coping mechanisms, offering a more comprehensive framework for influencing high-risk behaviors like injection drug use. While other theories emphasize beliefs or intentions, PMT directly addresses how fear and self-confidence shape protective health actions. Its constructs are especially relevant to hepatitis prevention, where recognizing risk and building confidence in behavior change are critical [19, 20]. Prior research supports PMT’s effectiveness in promoting risk-reduction behaviors among similar populations [12, 21].

Drop-In Centers (DICs) have a significant role in addressing the needs of individuals with substance use disorders in Iran. They work under the supervision of the Welfare Organization, Ministry of Health and Medical Education, and also other welfare organizations that provide services such as sterile injection equipment, condoms, hot meals, medical treatment, and counseling. The main role of these establishments is to promote low-risk behavior such as no needle sharing and safe sex, which in turn will stop the spread of infections like Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C and HIV. Also, the people in these communities also benefit from these services through educational projects and a connection with broader healthcare and support services [22, 23]. While DICs have expanded across urban and some semi-urban areas in Iran, their reach remains limited in rural and underserved regions. Accessibility may also be affected by stigma, lack of transportation, and limited awareness. Furthermore, many DICs face resource constraints, including shortages of trained staff and sustainable funding, which can impact the consistency and quality of services. These challenges are further exacerbated by economic sanctions and national budget limitations, which hinder the procurement of medical supplies and expansion of harm reduction programs [24, 25]. Despite these limitations, DICs serve as a practical and community-based setting for implementing and evaluating public health interventions.

Given the importance of DICs as a platform for health education and harm reduction, the current research was conducted to assess the impact of a PMT-based educational intervention on hepatitis B and C high-risk behavior reduction among substance use disorder patients attending DICs in a province in southwestern Iran during 2024–2025.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This quasi-experimental, pre-test/post-test study with a control group was conducted between 2024 and 2025 in Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad Province, located in the southwest of Iran. The target population consisted of all addicts registered with the DIC in the province during 2024. Of the 8,440 registered addicts in the province, 213 were covered by the DIC, including 116 individuals identified as injection drug users, who comprised the sampling frame for this study.

Additionally, addicts covered by welfare centers (Golestan Center: 95 individuals, Rahaee Center: 183 individuals) and four addiction treatment centers (Zendegi No: 71 individuals, Rah-e Omid: 88 individuals, Tavalod Dobareh: 125 individuals, and Etemad: 69 individuals) were included. - Knowledge: 10 yes/no questions (e.g., “There is an effective vaccine for all viral hepatitis types,” “Viral hepatitis can be cured with appropriate treatment”).

Each item had a yes/no response format, scored as “correct” = 1, “incorrect” = 0. Total possible score range: 0–10.

- Attitude: 10 questions using a 5-point Likert scale (e.g., “Individuals infected with hepatitis should be isolated from society,” “Only those at risk of hepatitis need to think about prevention,” “If I suspect I have been infected with viral hepatitis, I will seek counseling and testing”).

Scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Total possible score range: 10–50.

- Perceived Vulnerability: 4 Likert-scale questions (e.g., “I may be exposed to hepatitis B or C,” “People of all ages are at risk of hepatitis”).

Each item rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Score range: 4–20.

- Perceived Severity: 7 Likert-scale questions (e.g., “If I contract hepatitis B or C, I may die prematurely,” “If I contract hepatitis B or C, my liver may be affected”).

Rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Score range: 7–35.

- Perceived Self-Efficacy: 6 Likert-scale questions (e.g., “Avoiding handshakes with other addicts can protect me from hepatitis,” “Using disposable syringes can protect me from hepatitis”).

Rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Score range: 6–30.

- Perceived Maladaptive Rewards: 5 Likert-scale questions (e.g., “I do not use disposable syringes because injecting without them is easier,” “I do not wash my hands frequently because it is time-consuming”).

Rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Score range: 5–25.

- Perceived Response Costs: 6 Likert-scale questions (e.g., “Disinfecting contaminated areas is difficult and time-consuming,” “Maintaining social distancing is often not feasible at work”).

Rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Score range: 6–30.

- Behavior: 10 yes/no/no opinions questions (e.g., “Do you ask barbers to use disposable razors?” “Have you ever sought information about hepatitis?” “Have you completed the hepatitis B vaccination series?“).

Responses were yes/no/no opinion. “Yes” = 1 point, while “No” and “No opinion” = 0 points. Total possible behavior score: 0–10.

Higher scores in each domain reflected greater knowledge, stronger protective attitudes, greater perceived risk or severity, higher self-efficacy, or higher engagement in protective behaviors, depending on the construct.

Validity and reliability of the researcher-constructed questionnaire

The questionnaire was qualitatively and quantitatively validated. Qualitative validation was carried out by submitting the questionnaire to 12 experts in the field of health education, public health, medicine, and epidemiology for checking.

Quantitative validation was carried out by calculating the content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI). To evaluate the reliability of the instrument, the internal consistency method was used. The questionnaire was distributed among 30 individuals who met the inclusion criteria. After data analysis using SPSS version 27, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for each construct of the model as well as for the entire questionnaire. The Cronbach’s alpha values were: knowledge (0.76), attitude (0.77), perceived vulnerability (0.76), perceived severity (0.72), perceived self-efficacy (0.75), perceived maladaptive rewards (0.72), perceived response costs (0.75), behavior (0.76), and total questionnaire (0.78).

Educational intervention

After obtaining ethical approval (IR.SUMS.SCHEANUT.REC.1403.041) and the necessary permits from the university and health centers, the study was conducted in Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad Province. Participants were informed about the study’s confidentiality and purpose. Participation was entirely voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. No financial or material compensation was offered for participation. All harm reduction services routinely provided by DIC including access to free syringes and refreshments remained continuously available to all individuals, regardless of their involvement in the study. These services were not introduced or modified for the purposes of the intervention, ensuring no potential for coercion or undue influence.

The educational intervention, based on the PMT constructs, was delivered over two months, with four 60-minute sessions per month by the researcher and a DIC unit expert. Sessions 2 and 3, as well as Sessions 4 and 5, were each delivered as combined 60-minute sessions due to content overlap and logistical considerations. For attendance purposes, each pair was counted as one session. Therefore, a participant who missed each of these combined sessions and one additional session would still be considered to have missed two sessions total—not three—and would not be excluded under the study’s exclusion criteria. To maximize participation, free syringes and refreshments were provided during the sessions.

The intervention was structured to systematically address each PMT construct through tailored educational methods. Knowledge was targeted in Sessions 2 and 3 through lectures and pamphlets detailing hepatitis B and C transmission, complications, and prevention. Attitude was addressed using Q&A and group discussions to explore and reshape beliefs and stigma associated with the disease. To increase perceived vulnerability, case studies and peer testimonials were shared, making the risks more personally relevant to participants. Perceived severity was emphasized through visual aids and storytelling to illustrate the serious health outcomes of infection. Self-efficacy was strengthened through demonstrations and role-playing exercises in which participants practiced preventive behaviors such as proper syringe use. Perceived maladaptive rewards were addressed via group debates and cognitive reframing strategies, to help participants reconsider the short-term perceived benefits of risky behaviors. Perceived response costs—such as difficulty accessing clean needles—were addressed in problem-solving sessions where participants brainstormed practical solutions. Finally, to promote actual behavioral change, goal setting and follow-up reminders were incorporated, particularly in Session 8, which also included a summary and review of all prior material. To enhance retention and participation, the sessions were accompanied by the provision of free syringes and refreshments. This comprehensive, theory-driven approach was aimed at fostering sustained protective behaviors against hepatitis B and C among injection drug users. Data collection procedure

The researcher visited the addiction treatment centers to obtain informed consent and oversee the administration of the pre-test questionnaires. Pre-test questionnaires were self-administered in a private setting at the DIC. Standardized written and verbal instructions were provided to ensure consistency in completion. Although participants completed the questionnaires independently, the researcher was present to clarify any questions or misunderstandings, if needed. In cases where participants had lower literacy, questions were read aloud or explained in simple terms to ensure accurate understanding without influencing responses. To minimize response bias, participants were assured of anonymity, and no identifying information was collected. Additionally, the researcher responsible for questionnaire administration was not involved in delivering the educational intervention, reducing the risk of influence or bias. Post-test questionnaires were similarly administered to both the intervention and control groups two months after the intervention.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, and frequency) were used to summarize the data. Inferential analyses included chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact test, independent t-tests, and paired t-tests. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all tests.

Results

The demographics of the intervention and the control group in the study are shown in Table 3. Results showed that most participants were married with 26 (51%) in the control group and 25 (50%) in the intervention group. Of the control group, 27 (52.9%) had primary/middle school level education; while of the intervention group, 34 (68%) had primary/middle school level education. A history of injection drug use was uncommon, with 39 subjects (76.5%) reporting none in the control group and 38 (76%) in the intervention group. Regarding economic status, average economic status was observed in most individuals both in the control (n = 34 (66.7%)) and intervention (n = 28 (56%)) groups. The chi-square test also demonstrated that the two groups were homogeneous regarding marital status, education, smoking history, injection drug use history, and economic status (P > 0.05) The mean age in the control group was 39.64 ± 4.74 years, while it was 39.96 ± 4.64 years in the intervention group. The discrepancy in the mean age, as evaluated by the independent t-test, was non-significant between both groups (P = 0.924).

Table 4 presents the mean scores of knowledge, attitude and model constructs for the two groups before and after the intervention. The independent t-test results indicated that before the educational intervention, all variables of knowledge (P = 0.356), attitude (P = 0.230), perceived vulnerability (P = 0.074), perceived severity (P = 0.324), perceived self-efficacy (P = 0.243), perceived maladaptive rewards (P = 0.104), perceived response costs (P = 0.113), and behavior (P = 0.262) had no statistically significant relationship between the intervention and control groups. However, following the intervention, the intervention group demonstrated significant improvements across all PMT constructs compared to the control group. Knowledge scores increased from 12.27 to 18.82 (P = 0.001), with a large within-group effect size (d = 3.62), and the between-group effect size at post-test was also large (d = 2.90). Attitude scores rose from 16.43 to 31.52 (P = 0.001), with a within-group effect size of d = 5.32 and a between-group effect size of d = 6.36, indicating enhanced awareness and positive perceptions about hepatitis prevention. Perceived vulnerability increased significantly (from 6.64 to 12.66; P = 0.001) with a within-group effect size of d = 3.70 and between-group effect size of d = 3.40. Perceived severity improved from 12.29 to 22.21 (P = 0.001), with within-group d = 4.25 and between-group d = 4.43. Perceived self-efficacy increased from 9.81 to 17.80 (P = 0.001), with within-group d = 3.57 and between-group d = 3.50. Perceived maladaptive rewards showed a significant increase from 8.29 to 14.11 (P = 0.001), with within-group d = 2.52 and between-group d = 1.62. Perceived response costs also increased notably from 9.64 to 16.64 (P = 0.001), with within-group d = 2.26 and between-group d = 1.45. Lastly, actual protective behavior improved from 15.52 to 22.90 (P = 0.001), demonstrating a within-group effect size of d = 2.37 and a between-group effect size of d = 2.55, suggesting effective translation of knowledge into action. These changes were not observed in the control group, which showed minimal variation in scores pre- and post-intervention.

Discussion

The study aimed to evaluate a PMT-based educational intervention to prevent high-risk behaviors related to hepatitis B and C among addicts in southwest Iran (2024–2025). The results showed that the PMT-based educational intervention effectively enhanced preventive behaviors among injection drug users. This success is attributed to the intervention’s focus on key PMT constructs, which motivated protective behaviors.

Results revealed an educational intervention led to a significant difference in the mean knowledge score between the intervention group and control group. This is consistent with previous studies by Manley et al. [26], Elamouri et al. [27], and Makhoul et al. [28] which highlighted educational interventions led to increased knowledge about harms of injection drug use and viral hepatitis. In this study, the PMT-based intervention provided education regarding injection drug use, thus positively enhancing participants’ level of awareness with regard to the harmful effects of hepatitis. For example, Manley et al. [26] found that knowledge was a major barrier to hepatitis C treatment among injection drug users in Kenya and highlighted the need for educational interventions. Similarly, Elamouri et al. [27] found that educational intervention is effective in increasing awareness regarding the prevention of adverse effects associated with substance abuse among participants. The consistent findings highlight the need for PMT-based interventions for high-risk groups. However, knowledge scores improved less than other PMT constructs. There are a few factors that could explain this discrepancy. One possibility is that participants had a higher baseline level of knowledge about hepatitis than about other constructs, so there was less scope for improvement. Second, the intervention may have emphasized emotional and motivational factors (e.g., perceived severity and perceived self-efficacy) instead of detailed factual knowledge. Finally, intervention’s short time-frame likely inhibited participants from fully digesting hepatitis-related information such as differences between hepatitis B and C or mechanisms for transmission. Future interventions should include structured content, clear objectives, and reinforcement. Strategies such as interactive workshops, peer-led sessions, and role-playing can enhance engagement and behavior change, especially in high-risk groups. Visual aids like infographics and animations, alongside simple handouts and guided discussions, can improve understanding and recall. These methods are supported by our findings and prior research showing the effectiveness of participatory approaches in boosting self-efficacy and risk perception [29, 30].

Our findings revealed a significant improvement in attitude scores among participants in the intervention group compared to the control group. This aligns with previous studies using PMT-based interventions, although key differences in methodology, population, and outcomes must be acknowledged for a nuanced interpretation. For instance, Hosseini Zijoud et al. [31] focused on medical laboratory staff, employing a census method with six one-hour educational sessions. Their intervention notably improved constructs such as fear, self-efficacy, and protection motivation, all of which are known to influence attitude. However, attitude itself was not directly measured as a separate construct, limiting comparability. Furthermore, their longer intervention duration (six sessions) and occupation-specific focus likely impacted the magnitude and type of behavior change observed. In contrast, Japalaghi et al. [32] studied female university students, using a quasi-experimental design with only three one-hour sessions. They reported a significant increase in attitude-related constructs such as perceived susceptibility, self-efficacy, fear, and protection motivation, similar to our findings. Also, Rakhshani et al., working with hospital staff, adopted a randomized experimental design with five educational sessions over 2.5 months. However, similar to Hosseini Zijoud et al. [31] and Japalaghi et al. [32], attitude was not analyzed as a standalone outcome, making direct comparisons with our results more limited. In contrast, our study explicitly measured attitude and showed a significant PMT-related increase. The similarity across all studies in achieving behavior-related improvements supports the validity of PMT as a theoretical framework. However, our work uniquely contributes by isolating attitude as a measurable, modifiable construct, reinforcing its role as a mediating factor in behavior change.

Perceived vulnerability and severity scores significantly increased in the intervention group. Participants showed increased awareness about drug injection and the severity of its consequences, consistent with the findings of Roux et al. [33], Schwarz et al. [34], and Sharifi et al. [35]. Sharifi et al. [35] employed a pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design, similar to our study, using educational workshops to assess changes in health behaviors and awareness related to HIV and hepatitis. Both studies reported significant improvements in health-related outcomes following the intervention. However, Sharifi et al. [35] did not employ a theoretical model to inform their intervention, unlike our study, which was guided by the PMT to structure and deliver the educational content. Also, the key difference between our study and the one by Roux et al. [33] lies in the focus and delivery method of the intervention. While both studies used a pre-post design to evaluate an educational intervention among people who inject drugs, Roux et al. implemented an individualized, fieldworker-delivered harm reduction program emphasizing safer injection practices and reduction of physical harms like syringe sharing and abscesses. In contrast, our study used a theory-based group education intervention guided by the PMT to enhance perceived vulnerability and severity regarding blood-borne infections.

In our study, the intervention emphasized the high prevalence of hepatitis B and C among users of injection drugs and the possibility of irreversible health consequences such as liver failure, cancer. This was likely to increase participants’ vulnerability and to solidify how serious the threat really is. In a previous study by Bonar et al. [36] examined how perceived susceptibility and severity of health risks lead to safer behavior among injection drug users, specifically through the use of harm-reduction practices like test shots and skin cleaning. Their findings, like ours, emphasize the critical role of perceived vulnerability in motivating protective actions. However, they found that the influence of perceived risk varied depending on the injecting context—such as being in withdrawal or injecting alone—suggesting that interventions may be more effective when tailored to specific situations and conditions.

Although the intervention significantly improved the mean score of perceived vulnerability and severity, the study showed some participants still did not believe they were personally at risk (possibly due to optimism bias or not being directly exposed to severe cases of hepatitis). This finding indicates the potential usefulness of including real-life stories or testimonials from individuals affected by hepatitis in future interventions, which can provide a more immediate and relatable context for the hepatitis risk. Additional elements could be more active components of the intervention, including assess your personal risk tools or discussion of scenarios to assist participants in personalizing their susceptibility and likelihood. Further, PMT-based mechanisms may be influenced by these gaps and can be harnessed to improve the perception of risk and severity, which can then enhance motivation to perform prevention behavior.

Difference of mean self-efficacy score was highly significant in intervention compared to control. Our implementation of this approach agrees with Stevens et al. [37], Lopes et al. [38], Bailey et al. [39] and Künzler-Heule et al. [40], which similarly described improvements in self-efficacy and self-regulation in high-risk populations. Unlike Stevens et al. [37], who explored the acceptability of HCV testing through qualitative methods, our study employed a structured, theory-driven educational intervention to modify risk behaviors. While Lopes et al. [38] and Bailey et al. [39] focused on self-efficacy in relation to treatment adherence or injection safety, our intervention integrated self-efficacy as one of multiple PMT constructs to comprehensively address hepatitis B and C prevention.

The intervention in this study provided training on skills including using separate syringes that improved participants’ hepatitis prevention self-efficacy. This study’s intervention focused on practical skill training, like the use of separate syringes, disinfecting injection equipment and accessing harm reduction services, which directly helped participants feel competent to prevent hepatitis. For instance, participants learned how to dispose of used syringes properly and where to get sterile equipment so they could take charge of their health. But the study also found that some participants struggled to consistently use the skills they learned, especially in contexts where peer pressure or stigma relating to drug use was present. This indicates that though self-efficacy improved due to the intervention, structural factors could continue to be a deterrent in engagement and adherence suggesting that either peer mentorship programs or mobile harm reduction services may be necessary to attend to this higher-level barrier. Further studies need to examine whether positive changes in self-efficacy are sustainable, and how contextual factors, such as support networks or access to resources, affect whether participants can sustain these behaviors long-term.

In the intervention group; mean perceived rewards score also improved more significantly in the intervention group than in the control group. This aligns with studies by Cox et al. [41] and Amoako et al. [42], which emphasize the role of psychosocial factors and structural barriers in shaping hepatitis-related risk and care behaviors. However, unlike Cox et al. [41], who used a cross-sectional design to identify associations, our study applied a PMT-based intervention to actively change those psychosocial constructs and observed behavioral outcomes. Compared to Amoako et al. [42], which focused on treatment access barriers, our study targeted prevention through structured education before infection occurs.

To illustrate, in the PMT context, the reward was the absence of hepatitis, which was made salient by education about severe consequences of the disease, such as liver cirrhosis, cancer, and premature death. The intervention also emphasized the long-term rewards of adopting preventive behaviors, such as using sterile paraphernalia and refraining from sharing needles — rewards that the participants considered worthwhile compensation for the trivial effort involved in adopting the preventive behaviors. For example, they were shown that these behaviors could not only protect their health and improve their quality of life, but also reduce the financial costs of hepatitis treatment. However, the study also found that some participants found it difficult to motivate themselves to wait for these rewards rather than seeking the immediate gratification of drug use, especially in high-stress or high-risk environments. These findings indicate that future interventions may be enhanced by integrating techniques that help individuals negotiate the tension between instant gratification and delayed reward, perhaps through mindfulness training or motivational interviewing techniques. The intervention could also include additional personalized goal-setting activities for participants to help them identify and internalize the rewards in their life that are most meaningful to them. Further research should examine how perceived aids change over time and how contextual factors, such as access to social support or mental health services interact with participants’ ability to focus on longer term health gain.

Perceived response cost score increased significantly in the intervention group compared with the control group. This result is in line with studies by Belrhiti et al. [43], and Sherbuk et al. [44] which reported that perceived response costs—such as social stigma, emotional strain, and financial burdens—significantly hinder engagement in hepatitis C care among people who inject drugs. Like our study, both identified that individuals often weigh these costs heavily when deciding whether to adopt protective health behaviors or seek treatment. However, while Belrhiti et al. [43] and Sherbuk et al. [44] highlighted these barriers through qualitative insights post-diagnosis, our study uniquely demonstrated that a structured PMT-based intervention can quantitatively increase awareness of such costs before infection, motivating preventive behavior change. This underscores the value of proactive education in shifting perceptions of long-term consequences before individuals reach the treatment stage.

The intervention increased awareness of the financial, social, and emotional costs of risky behaviors. For instance, participants were informed about the substantial expenses associated with treating hepatitis complications by using antiviral medications for extended periods as well as the difficulties in obtaining potentially life-saving treatments like liver transplants that are both limited in availability and seldom prioritized for clients with a substance abuse history. This made them see the economic and life-threatening nature of their actions. The intervention also emphasized social and emotional consequences like a sense of isolation and a perceived strain on relationships, which reinforced the idea of the burdens of risky behaviors. Yet the study also found that some participants failed to appreciate the full range of these costs, especially long-term consequences or indirect effects, like lost productivity or diminished quality of life. So future interventions might be more effective if they are more descriptive and personalized such as presenting testimonials from individuals who faced having such consequences. In addition, the intervention may include interactive exercises like cost-benefit analysis to facilitate the internalization of tangible and intangible costs of risky behaviors. It should be followed up by research addressing in what way perceived response costs change over time and whether contextual factors such as accessibility to financial resources or social support networks of participants have an influence on how the costs are perceived. This gap can be bridged by conducting studies that utilize PMT in a comprehensive manner and use social cognition theories that further extend the findings obtained by PMT and it may strengthen participants’ understanding of the outcomes of quitting risky behaviors and possibly their motivation to lead preventive lifestyles.

Lastly the mean behavior scores significantly increased in the intervention group as compared with the control group. This aligns with findings from Khani Jeihooni et al. [45], where an educational intervention based on the Theory of Planned Behavior improved preventive behaviors among people who inject drugs by enhancing behavioral intentions, attitudes, and perceived control. However, unlike the Theory of Planned Behavior, our study used PMT, which may have influenced behavior more directly by emphasizing perceived severity, vulnerability, and response cost. While Akiyama’s study similarly reported improved knowledge and motivation among people who inject drugs in Kenya following integrated HCV education, it also highlighted persistent structural barriers and low perceived control that limited behavior change, factors our PMT-based intervention specifically addressed [46]. Malaguti’s findings further suggest that social identity within drug-use networks can maintain risky behaviors, indicating that individual-level motivation, as targeted in our PMT framework, may be more effective when paired with strategies to shift group norms [46].

In our study, the intervention led to improved preventive behaviors, including reduced needle sharing and increased use of harm reduction services. However, sustaining these changes was challenging in contexts with limited resources or high stigma. Supportive measures such as peer groups, mobile services, and practical skill-building could enhance long-term adherence. Future research should examine how access to healthcare and community support influences sustained behavior change. This finding differs from the study by Mukherjee et al. [47], where an HCV education intervention did not lead to significant improvements in behavior among clients of needle/syringe programs in Malaysia. The limited effect in that study may be related to ongoing structural challenges, such as stigma, lack of follow-up support, and low perceived accessibility of treatment. While both interventions aimed to raise awareness and promote safer practices, our study may have benefited from a more structured approach grounded in Protection Motivation Theory, along with context-sensitive support elements. These factors might have contributed to better engagement and uptake of preventive behaviors.

A limitation of this study is that it relied on self-reported data, which could lead to social desirability bias due to the sensitive nature of injection drug use and associated stigma. Future studies could incorporate objective measures such as biomarkers or observational assessments to validate self-reports. Although this study used a census approach, the small single-site sample limits generalizability due to regional socio-cultural, economic, and healthcare factors. Contextual factors such as stigma, religious beliefs, healthcare access, harm reduction infrastructure, and social support networks may further limit generalizability. To address this, future research should adopt multi-center designs across diverse settings to evaluate the consistency of outcomes and support the development of culturally adaptable prevention strategies. Moreover, the short follow-up period (two months) restricts the assessment of long-term effects; extended follow-ups of 6 to 12 months are recommended to determine the sustainability of behavior changes. Additionally, unmeasured factors such as mental health, health literacy, and drug use severity, may have influenced engagement and outcomes. Future studies should assess and control for these variables to better isolate the intervention’s true effects. Also, the newly developed PMT-based questionnaire, although underwent qualitative expert review and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, it has not yet been subjected to full-scale psychometric validation, such as construct validity analysis and test-retest reliability assessment. Future studies should complete these validation steps to strengthen the instrument’s robustness.

In this study we showed the PMT-based intervention is a low-cost, peer-led, scalable strategy for improving hepatitis prevention among injection drug users. Further multi-site, long-term studies are needed before scaling, with current findings serving as preliminary guidance for policymakers. Future programs should include interactive workshops and peer-led sessions that build skills, enhance risk perception, and strengthen self-efficacy through tools like personal risk assessments and personal stories, aligning with evidence that such strategies effectively engage participants and promote behavior change [48, 49]. Additionally, mobile outreach units and peer-led initiatives can help extend the reach of interventions and sustain behavior change in the face of stigma or limited resources. With adequate funding and policy support, these interventions can be scaled regionally or nationally to advance hepatitis prevention and strengthen harm reduction efforts.

Conclusion

The PMT-based educational intervention effectively enhanced preventive behaviors against hepatitis B and C among injection drug users by targeting key psychological constructs of PMT. It suggests that theory-driven, structured educational programs can serve as a valuable component in national hepatitis prevention strategies, particularly when integrated into harm reduction services at addiction treatment and community health centers. The success of this intervention highlights the potential of incorporating behavioral science into public health programming to improve outcomes among marginalized populations. Nonetheless, the study’s single-site setting design, reliance on self-reported data, and short-term follow-up limit generalizability. To support broader implementation, future research should utilize multi-site designs and assess long-term impacts while accounting for contextual variables such as healthcare access, stigma, and social support. These efforts will help refine and scale effective interventions for hepatitis prevention.
