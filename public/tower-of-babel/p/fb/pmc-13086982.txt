Healthcare professionals attitudes practices and challenges in managing posttraumatic stress disorder in Sudan during armed conflict

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a prevalent mental health condition in conflict zones. The ongoing conflict in Sudan has severely impacted the healthcare system, leaving healthcare professionals with significant challenges in managing PTSD. Despite the rising prevalence of PTSD, little is known about the attitudes and practices of Sudanese healthcare providers in addressing it.AimThis study aims to evaluate the attitudes, practices, and challenges faced by healthcare professionals in managing PTSD during the Sudan conflict, with a focus on identifying factors influencing their readiness to provide care.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 1,229 doctors in Sudan using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. The survey assessed socio-demographics, knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to PTSD. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26, with chi-square tests and logistic regression applied to identify significant associations.ResultsWhile 69.2% (n = 727) of participants demonstrated favorable attitudes toward PTSD, only 21.9% (n = 269) reported sufficient practices. Mental health training significantly increased the odds of sufficient practices (OR = 3.34, 95% CI: 2.47–4.52), as did prior displacement more than three times (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.11–3.36). However, only 9.3% (n = 114) used DSM-5 criteria for PTSD diagnosis, and 70.4% (n = 865) lacked formal psychiatry training. Barriers included resource limitations (64.3%, n = 790) and cultural challenges.ConclusionThis study highlights critical gaps in PTSD management among Sudanese healthcare professionals, emphasizing the need for targeted training, resource allocation, and culturally appropriate interventions to improve mental health during and after conflict.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-026-00414-7.

Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a psychiatric disorder that may occur in people who have experienced or witnessed a traumatic event, series of events or set of circumstances, such as during war, accidents, and natural disasters [1]. Characterized by symptoms such as intrusive memories, hyperarousal, and emotional numbing, PTSD affects millions globally, profoundly impacting quality of life, productivity, and overall health. It is estimated that the lifetime prevalence of PTSD in the world population is almost 4%, with the prevalence amongst trauma-exposed individuals being 5.6% [2].

Previously known as “shell shock” and “war neurosis”, PTSD is particularly prevalent in war-affected regions, where exposure to violence, displacement, and loss often reaches catastrophic levels [3]. Individuals living in conflict zones face a heightened risk of developing PTSD due to the intensity and recurrence of traumatic experiences [4]. Studies have shown that the prevalence of PTSD among war survivors can range from 15% to 37%, significantly higher than the global average [4, 5]. The long-term psychological impact of war extends beyond direct survivors, affecting entire communities through generational trauma, economic instability, and weakened support systems. In these settings, limited access to mental health services and the stigma surrounding psychological disorders further exacerbate the burden of PTSD [6]. As a result, addressing PTSD in war zones is critical to fostering societal recovery.

The conflict that erupted in Sudan on the 15th of April, 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) has without a doubt changed the landscape of medical services and needs in the country. Only a few months after the start of the conflict, 70% of hospitals in the country went out of service [7], and due to a substantial population displacement, there has been a severe shortage of essential medical resources and personnel [8]. In addition, multiple public health concerns including various outbreaks of diseases such as cholera and dengue fever have burdened an already debilitated healthcare system [9]. This leaves the healthcare workers in Sudan fighting to provide the most basic health necessities for a devastated population, while also facing new challenges that seem to be perpetual, as the war approaches its third year [10].

Amid this humanitarian crisis, the mental health toll on the Sudanese population is expected to be profound. Clinically significant PTSD symptoms were detected in more than 36% of participants in a recent study, amongst both displaced and non-displaced Sudanese individuals [11]. Healthcare professionals are uniquely positioned to address PTSD among patients; however, their ability to do so depends heavily on their knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding PTSD. This is particularly important in Sudan, where the ongoing conflict exacerbates mental health issues while simultaneously stretching the healthcare system to its limits [12].

This study aims to explore the practices and attitudes of Sudanese health professionals toward PTSD, an area that has been largely overlooked in the context of the country’s ongoing conflict. By exploring the knowledge, perceptions, and barriers health workers face in managing PTSD, this research seeks to provide valuable insights into the mental health challenges within Sudan’s healthcare system. Given the immense strain placed on healthcare providers and the general population by the ongoing war, understanding these attitudes is crucial for developing more effective, culturally appropriate interventions for PTSD not only during the conflict, but also after it resolves. In South Sudan, Sudan’s geographical and historical neighbour, more than 40% of the population in war-afflicted states met symptom criteria for probable PTSD, years after the conflict has relatively settled [13]. Due to the similarities in populations, similar figures can be expected for the Sudanese in the post-war future.

Methods

This study was conducted among doctors working in Sudan during August and September 2024 at all levels of care. Doctors included in the study were from all levels of practice (interns, general practitioners, residents, specialists and consultants). At least 385 participants were required to represent the study population.

Data collection tool

A self-administered, close-ended, structured questionnaire was used to record the participating doctors’ responses. The questionnaire is based on the questionnaire used by Kanwal et al. in a similar study based in Pakistan [14], with the addition of questions to explore the doctors’ displacement status during the war. For validity, the questionnaire underwent expert revision and was tailored accordingly. The questionnaire had a total of 29 items, divided amongst 3 sections. The first Sect. (17 items) contained questions on participants’ socio-demographic status (e.g., age, qualification level, residence, displacement status), as well as questions on general interest in mental health, number of trauma-exposed patients seen, and general knowledge regarding PTSD.

The second Sect. (5 items) focused on participants’ practices regarding PTSD screening, assessment for suicidality and psychiatric comorbidities, and referral practices. The final Sect. (7 items) dealt with participants’ attitudes regarding PTSD (e.g., whether or not PTSD is worthy of attention, and whether identifying patients with PTSD is challenging). At the end of the second and third sections, “Practice” and “Attitude” scores were respectively calculated, based on the participant’s answers. The scores were then classified as either “favorable” or “unfavorable” for the attitude, and “Sufficient” or “Insufficient” for the practice.

The questionnaire was entered onto the Chi Squares online platform, which facilitated the simultaneous upload of self-administered questionnaire responses while participants were completing the survey in different states.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. For descriptive analysis, mean and standard deviation were reported for participants’ age, while frequencies and percentages were reported for the remaining qualitative variables. Chi-square test was first conducted to examine associations between sociodemographic factors and attitudes and practices toward PTSD (supplementary Table 1). Following this, logistic regression analysis was performed to further explore the relationships between sociodemographic factors and PTSD attitudes and practices. To assess the fit of the regression model, we evaluated its statistical significance using the likelihood ratio test (χ² [2] = 23.56, p < 0.001). The Nagelkerke R² coefficient was calculated to assess the proportion of variation in PTSD attitudes and practices explained by the model, with a value of 0.51 indicating an acceptable level of explanatory power. Furthermore, the model’s predictive accuracy was evaluated by determining the percentage of cases correctly classified by the model, which was 83%. A p- values of 0.05 or less were considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic data

The study included a total of 1,229 participants, with a mean age of 28.76 ± 5.69 years (Table 1). The majority of participants were female (728, 59.2%), while 501 (40.8%) were male. The predominant qualification was general practitioner (549, 44.7%), followed by interns (384, 31.2%), residents (175, 14.2%), specialists (96, 7.8%), and consultants (25, 2.0%). Most participants worked in the public sector (1,003, 81.6%), and the majority had between less than one to five years of experience (1,070, 87%). Regarding monthly expenses, 705 participants (57.4%) reported spending more than 300,000 SDGs. Most participants resided in urban areas (905, 73.6%), and 927 (75.4%) were displaced, primarily from Khartoum State (757, 81.7%) (Table 1).

Interest in mental health and cultural familiarity

A significant proportion of participants (954, 77.6%) expressed interest in mental health, though the majority (865, 70.4%) had not undergone formal training in psychiatry (Table 1). On average, participants reported seeing a median of 3 (IQR: 2–10) post-trauma patients weekly (Table 1). Regarding familiarity with cultural differences related to post-traumatic stress, 559 participants (45.5%) reported being somewhat familiar, while 212 (17.2%) were very familiar (Table 2). Participants identified psychological factors (926, 75.3%), social factors (756, 61.5%), and biological factors (359, 29.2%) as contributors to post-traumatic stress. The most commonly recommended treatment methods were psychological interventions (1,029, 83.7%), lifestyle modifications (576, 46.9%), and medications (512, 41.7%) (Table 2).

Healthcare professionals’ attitudes and practices toward PTSD

A favorable attitude toward PTSD was observed in 727 participants (69.2%) (Table 3). The majority (940, 76.5%) believed patients should be evaluated following recent trauma exposure and considered PTSD a serious concern requiring attention. Most participants (831, 67.6%) agreed that general practitioners play a major role in the care of PTSD patients. Identifying patients with PTSD was perceived as challenging by 565 (46.0%), while 514 (41.8%) found it sometimes challenging. Similarly, managing PTSD patients was considered challenging by 652 (53.1%) of participants. Many (790, 64.3%) believed that limitations in resources and training hindered optimal care, and 932 (75.8%) expressed a need to improve their ability to evaluate and manage PTSD patients (Table 3).

Furthermore, the findings revealed that practices regarding PTSD were largely insufficient, with 960 participants (78.1%) reporting inadequate practices (Table 3). Only 142 (11.6%) screened patients with a known history of trauma, and 164 (13.3%) assessed suicidal tendencies. Immediate referral or intervention for patients with suicidal tendencies was provided by 335 (27.3%) participants. Additionally, 226 (18.4%) assessed for psychiatric comorbidities, and only 114 (9.3%) utilized DSM-5 criteria for PTSD assessment (Table 3).

Influential factors on healthcare professionals’ attitudes and practices toward PTSD

Key factors influencing attitudes and practices toward PTSD were identified (Table 4). Participants with monthly expenses exceeding 300,000 SDGs had 75% higher odds of having favorable attitudes compared to those with lower expenses (OR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.23–2.48). Undergoing psychiatry training was associated with a 234% increase in the odds of sufficient PTSD practices compared to those without training (OR = 3.34, 95% CI: 2.47–4.52). In addition, geographic location also played a significant role; with participants displaced more than three times had 94% higher odds of sufficient practices compared to those displaced once (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.11–3.36), and participants working in River Nile, White Nile, and Sennar states demonstrating significantly lower practice readiness, with 54%, 46%, and 94% reduced odds, respectively (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.29–0.72; OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.32–0.90; and OR = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.01–0.49). Moreover, participants with a declared interest in mental health had 52% higher odds of sufficient PTSD practices compared to those without such interest (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.03–2.23). Although general practitioners had 98% higher odds of sufficient practices compared to interns (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.35–2.87), their attitudes toward PTSD were not significantly different from other qualifications (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.66–1.19). Other factors, such as sex, years of experience, and urban versus rural residence, were not significantly associated with attitudes or practices. For example, male participants had similar odds of sufficient practices as females (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.80–1.46) (Table 4).

Discussion

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a prevalent condition during and after wars. The current Sudan war has significantly increased the prevalence of PTSD to 36.6%, a notable rise compared to pre-war levels [11]. Despite this high prevalence, the number of general psychiatrists in Sudan remains critically low at 0.08 per 100,000 [15], making it increasingly likely for physicians in non-psychiatric disciplines to encounter PTSD cases. This study aimed to explore healthcare professionals’ attitudes, practices, and challenges in managing post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) during the ongoing conflict in Sudan.

Over two-thirds of physicians demonstrated favorable attitudes toward PTSD, yet only about one-fifth showed sufficient practices. In contrast, a study conducted in Pakistan, a resource-limited country similar to Sudan, reported higher levels of both attitudes and practices, differing from our findings [14]. This gap may be attributed to insufficient mental health training, as nearly three-quarters of physicians in our study lacked formal training in this area. Additionally, the majority of participants acknowledged the need to improve their skills in evaluating and managing PTSD, emphasizing critical gaps in knowledge and practice. A notable contributing factor is the limited clinical experience among healthcare providers, as 87% had less than five years of practice. This lack of experience may hinder their familiarity with PTSD management and contribute to difficulties in accurately identifying and addressing trauma-related conditions.

A positive attitude among physicians may reflect an intrinsic understanding of PTSD’s impact or exposure to trauma cases, as participants reported seeing an average of three trauma patients weekly. However, nearly half of the physicians found PTSD identification and management to be challenging. This under-recognition is consistent with global trends, as the National Center for PTSD, USA, highlights similar challenges [16]. Barriers include patients’ reluctance to disclose traumatic experiences or seek help due to avoidance behaviors, as reported in a previous study [17].

A study from Saudi Arabia explored the awareness and practices of clinical social workers and psychologists in managing PTSD among military personnel [18]. While practitioners consistently inquired about trauma-linked symptoms—such as nightmares, aggression, and social withdrawal—they rarely used standardized diagnostic tools, with most reporting they “never” utilized PTSD screening scales. This is consistent with our findings in Sudan, where only 11.6% of physicians routinely screened patients with a known trauma history—a proportion comparable to findings from a previous study [19]. Additionally, only 9% of Sudanese physicians used DSM-IV or DSM-V criteria for PTSD diagnosis, which is slightly lower than figures reported in Pakistan [14]. These findings collectively highlight a significant gap in the application of standardized PTSD diagnostic methods across conflict-affected, resource-limited settings, contributing to potential underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis in clinical practice. However, it is important to acknowledge that the DSM-IV or DSM-V frameworks may have limitations in chronic conflict settings such as Sudan. Research involving displaced Iraqi and Syrian populations has shown that DSM-5 criteria may not adequately capture the psychological experiences of individuals exposed to ongoing violence [20]. In these contexts, trauma responses are often more complex, encompassing chronic stress, prolonged grief, and collective trauma. Alternative PTSD models, such as the Anhedonia and Affect model derived from this population, demonstrated a better fit and may offer a more accurate reflection of trauma symptoms in such settings.

Physicians’ engagement with mental health crises was similarly limited, as only 13% screened for suicidal tendencies, and less than one-fourth referred patients with such risks. This reflects a significant gap in addressing psychiatric emergencies. Evidence indicates that enhancing physicians’ ability to identify and manage psychiatric disorders, especially through better education, could improve suicide prevention. This is particularly important as nearly three-fourths of suicide victims reportedly had contact with a physician within the year prior to their death, and almost half within a month [21].

Regarding physicians’ knowledge of PTSD, our study found that 83% of respondents recognized the importance of psychological interventions, which aligns with recommendations for Bosnian refugees with PTSD [22]. Evidence suggests that psychological treatments for trauma survivors are the most effective for managing PTSD [17].

In this study, nearly half of the participants reported being somewhat familiar with cultural differences related to PTSD. Understanding these differences is crucial, as they influence the prevalence, impact, access to care, and treatment of PTSD [23].

Physicians’ attitudes varied significantly across states. For example, doctors in the White Nile State were half as likely to have positive attitudes compared to those in Khartoum, potentially due to Khartoum’s higher conflict exposure, fostering empathy toward PTSD patients. Socioeconomic factors also influenced attitudes, with physicians who had higher monthly expenses (over 300,000 SDGs) exhibiting more positive attitudes compared to those with lower expenses. In contrast, practice outcomes differed based on financial factors. Physicians with mid-range monthly expenses (200,000–300,000 SDGs) were less likely to demonstrate positive practices compared to those with expenses below 200,000 SDGs. However, in the Sudanese context, the salary differences across these groups are relatively small and may not reflect meaningful socioeconomic disparities, given the overall low income levels, high cost of living, and worsening economic instability due to the ongoing war [24].

General practitioners (GPs) demonstrated the highest levels of sufficient practice, with regression analysis showing that GPs were twice as likely to engage in positive practices compared to interns. However, this finding may be misleading and possibly due to sampling bias or a Type I error, as GPs constituted nearly half of the sample, while higher-qualified consultants represented only 2%. The overrepresentation of GPs may have inflated the association. Notably, a study from Pakistan reported that higher qualifications were linked to better practice [14], contrasting our findings. Future studies with a more balanced sample may provide a clearer understanding of the relationship between qualification level and practice.

Experience also played a role, as physicians with 1–5 years of practice were one and a half times more likely to demonstrate positive practices than those with less than a year of experience. This may be attributed to cumulative exposure to diverse cases, refining diagnostic and communication skills over time. Furthermore, a significant association was found between the number of trauma-exposed patients seen weekly and sufficient practice, consistent with evidence linking professional experience to higher-quality care [25].

Interest in mental health emerged as a strong predictor of better practice, with participants interested in psychiatry being 1.5 times more likely to demonstrate positive practices. Additionally, those with formal mental health training were three times as likely to engage in effective practices, aligning with a previous study [14]. Such training typically includes essential components like recognizing common psychiatric disorders, conducting PTSD screening, assessing suicide risk, and understanding referral pathways. This highlights the critical role of targeted mental health education in enhancing clinical competence and practice among physicians.

Urban physicians were less likely to exhibit positive practices compared to their rural counterparts, potentially due to higher workloads and stress levels in urban settings [26]. Geographic disparities were also noted, with physicians in the Northern, River Nile, and Sennar States being less likely to engage in positive practices compared to those in Khartoum. This underscores the need for targeted mental health training in these underserved regions.

Finally, displaced physicians who had experienced displacement more than three times were nearly twice as likely to demonstrate positive practices compared to those displaced only once. This may reflect increased empathy and resilience gained from repeated exposure to trauma, fostering a deeper commitment to addressing mental health challenges in others.

This study is the first to explore physicians’ attitudes and practices regarding PTSD during the ongoing Sudan war, offering valuable insights into this critical issue. However, several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships or observe changes over time. Reliance on self-reported data may have introduced bias in the assessment of attitudes and practices, a limitation that face-to-face interviews could potentially mitigate. The use of a non-probability sampling method limits the generalizability of the findings, though it was justifiable given the challenges of conducting research during the war. Additionally, the absence of objective measures for evaluating practices and reliance on self-reported PTSD case frequencies reduce the validity of the findings.

Conclusion

We found a high level of favorable attitudes among physicians toward PTSD but a low level of sufficient practice. Factors such as mental health training and clinical experience were associated with improved practices, while regular monthly expenses influenced both attitudes and practices. Initiatives should focus on enhancing physicians’ knowledge and skills in PTSD identification, diagnosis, and management through comprehensive training programs. Emphasis should also be placed on practical workshops and the use of culturally-sensitive tools, such as the anhedonia and affect model, to strengthen clinical practices. Additionally, training programs should incorporate early screening techniques for probable PTSD symptoms, which are easier to teach and could be effectively delegated to other healthcare providers or mental health professionals. This task-shifting approach may help alleviate the heavy burden currently placed on doctors and psychiatrists. Future efforts should also adopt a multidisciplinary approach by involving social workers and other mental health professionals in PTSD screening and care, recognizing their essential roles in trauma-informed response, particularly in conflict-affected and resource-limited contexts.

Future research should aim to build on these findings by employing longitudinal designs and probabilistic sampling methods to enhance reliability and generalizability. Incorporating objective measures, such as direct observation or patient feedback, will further improve the accuracy and validity of results, contributing to better PTSD management in conflict-affected settings.
