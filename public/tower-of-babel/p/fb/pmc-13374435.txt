Impact of vehicular data on trauma outcomes after motor vehicle crashes
Authors: Sarah D Diaz, Jamila K Picart, Shukri Dualeh, Staci T Aubry, Jonathan W Scott, Stewart C Wang, Mark R Hemmila, Raymond A Jean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionField triage guidelines exist to inform first responder personnel on criteria to expedite the transfer of motor vehicle collision (MVC) patients to trauma centers; however, these guidelines are minimally informed by the vehicle characteristics and collision characteristics. Given this, we studied associations of the vehicle characteristics and collision characteristics with patient injury and outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study using Michigan State Police crash reports linked with Michigan Trauma Quality Improvement Program collaborative quality initiative data. Univariable analysis was used to identify characteristics predictive of the primary outcome of severe injury, defined as an Injury Severity Score >15 (ISS>15), and multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed across primary and secondary outcomes of ISS>15, mortality, and the need for surgery, intensive care, or transfer to another hospital.ResultsThere were 13 154 linked cases between the two data sources. This contained 52.3% males with a mean age of 41.7 years (SD 20.7 years). In multivariable logistic regression, the collision characteristics associated with the highest increased odds of ISS>15 were single-vehicle crashes (OR 1.35, p<0.01) and head-on crashes (OR 1.54, p<0.01). Head-on crashes (OR 1.60, p<0.01) and single-vehicle crashes (OR 1.46, p=0.03) were associated with a significantly increased risk of mortality. Head-on crashes (OR 1.49, p<0.01) and single-vehicle crashes (OR 1.37, p<0.01) were associated with the highest odds of operative intervention or intensive care.ConclusionsSeveral vehicle and collision characteristics are associated with increased odds of severe traumatic injury and death after MVC. The addition of these characteristics to field triage assessments could improve triage decision-making to predict injury severity and the risk of mortality.Level of evidenceIII, prognostic, epidemiological.

Introduction

According to the National Center for Statistics and Analysis, there are an estimated 6 million motor vehicle collisions (MVCs) per year in the USA, resulting in over 40 000 fatalities. Furthermore, MVCs remain one of the most common indications for emergency medical services (EMS) field evaluation and transport.1 It is well established that many trauma-related deaths occur before reaching the hospital or within the initial hours post injury. Therefore, it is crucial for EMS to quickly evaluate injury severity and determine patients requiring transfer to high-level trauma centers.2 The National Guidelines for the Field Triage of Injured Patients, most recently updated in 2021, focus on four overarching categories when assessing a patient: vital signs and mental status, injury patterns, mechanism of injury, and subjective EMS provider judgment.3 Field evaluations based on these guidelines inform which individuals are expedited to designated trauma centers according to their assessed medical condition. The triage guidelines advocate for transferring patients with (1) anticipated high injury severity, (2) increased risk of mortality, or (3) the need for higher levels of care to a level I trauma center.4

Existing literature on pre-hospital evaluation after MVCs largely focuses on patient-related factors, such as vital signs and evident injuries. Although these parameters are undeniably crucial for predicting the severity of injuries and determining appropriate levels of care, a knowledge gap exists in incorporating vehicle and collision characteristics into the predictive framework. Although there are some vehicular-related variables included in the current guidelines, such as ejection or significant vehicle intrusion, other factors such as crash trajectory, airbag deployment, and vehicle age remain only qualitatively incorporated. Currently, there is a lack of quantitative data exploring the potential associations between standard vehicle characteristics—such as vehicle age, collision characteristics, crash type, airbag deployment, or restraint use—with patient injuries and outcomes after MVCs. Thus, the objective of this study is to address this knowledge gap by examining the associations of vehicle and collision characteristics to patient injury severity and outcomes. We hypothesize that crash-specific factors, such as crash angle and vehicle age, will be predictive of severe injury and mortality, thus providing potential justification for increased inclusion in field triage assessments.

Methods

Data source

The study dataset was derived through the linkage of crash reports (UD-10) from the state of Michigan, obtained through the Michigan Traffic Crash Reporting System (MTCRS), with clinical trauma registry data from the Michigan Trauma Quality Improvement Program (MTQIP). Crash data, sourced from statewide UD-10 reports collected by the Michigan State Police, adhere to legal requirements mandating completion by investigating officers and subsequent compilation by the state police for public dissemination. This compilation serves to furnish statistical information pertaining to the incidence and causes of crashes.5 The MTCRS encompasses all motor vehicle-related crashes on roadways resulting in injury, death, or property damage exceeding US$1000. MTQIP is a collaborative quality initiative involving 35 American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma-verified level I and level II trauma centers throughout Michigan. The MTQIP dataset comprises demographic and clinical data abstracted by trained trauma registrars in accordance with the National Trauma Data Standard.6

Dataset linkage

The two datasets were linked by employing a robust matching algorithm based on patient name, age, and crash date. UD-10 cases were linked to patients in the MTQIP registry who underwent inpatient hospitalizations for trauma. Subsequently, patient demographic and clinical data were combined with vehicular crash data across outcomes. The linkage was limited to the inclusion criteria of the respective datasets, as the MTCRS only captures crashes on public roads which generated a police report, and the MTQIP dataset captures patients submitted by participating hospitals. After dataset linkage, all patient identifiers were stripped from the analytic dataset.

Variables of interest

After linkage of the two datasets, variables pertaining to patient demographics, vehicle characteristics, and collision characteristics were analyzed. Patient-related variables encompassed sex (categorized as male or female), age in years, Hispanic or non-Hispanic ethnicity, race (white and non-white due to low frequencies of some racial demographics), and Injury Severity Score (ISS). We restricted the analysis to collisions involving passenger motor vehicles. Collision factors included collision direction, collision type, restraint use, vehicle year, and airbag deployment. The primary outcome of our study was the rate of severe injury, defined as ISS >15. Secondary outcomes included mortality and the need for complex care, defined as the admission to the intensive care unit, the need for operative interventions, and transfer to another facility. The ISS, introduced in 1974, provides a standard for level I trauma center certification of injuries and serves as a key descriptive measure in the trauma-related literature.7 8

Statistical analysis

First, descriptive statistics were summarized across the cohort by ISS >15, and any significant differences were described using χ2 test for categorical variables and t-test for continuous variables, respectively. Next, we performed a univariate analysis to investigate the individual relationships between vehicle and collision characteristics and the outcomes of interest. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to investigate the association between patient sex, age, race, vehicle year, airbag deployment, restraint use, and collision direction across primary and secondary outcomes. An alpha value of 0.05 was used to define statistical significance. All analyses were performed using Stata V.18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). UD-10 data were obtained and used through the International Center for Automotive Medicine (ICAM). ICAM has a memorandum of agreement with the State of Michigan—Office of Highway Safety to access Michigan State Police Report data and maintains institutional review board (IRB) approval from the State of Michigan Department of Health and Human Services. Both ICAM and MTQIP have approvals from the University of Michigan Medical School IRB, which includes a waiver of Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) authorization and a waiver of informed consent for secondary use of existing datasets (University of MIchiga (U-M) IRB HUM00041947 and HUM00043599).

Results

There were 13 154 cases during the study period between 2020 and 2022. This group had a mean age of 41.7 (SD 20.7 years) and contained 52.3% males. In multivariable analysis, the collision characteristics associated with increased odds of ISS >15 relative to rear-end collisions were head-on collisions (OR 1.54, p<0.01) and single-vehicle collisions (OR 1.35, p<0.01). Head-on collisions and single-vehicle collisions were also associated with increased needs for complex care (head-on OR 1.49; single-vehicle OR 1.37, both p<0.01) and risk of mortality (head-on OR 1.60, p=0.01; single-vehicle OR 1.46, p=0.03). Restraint use and unknown use of restraints were associated with decreased odds of ISS >15 (OR 0.45 and 0.73, respectively, both p<0.01), as well as increased mortality and need for complex care. Airbag deployment was associated with increased ISS >15 (OR 1.34, p<0.01), increased risk of mortality (OR 1.44, p=0.01), and the need for complex care (OR 1.18, p<0.01). Discussion

Current EMS field triage guidelines are designed to assess injured occupants after MVCs, and serve to predict potential severe injuries and care requirements. These guidelines prioritize the evaluation of patient factors to assess the necessity for advanced trauma center care. However, the existing predictive framework understates the consideration of some vehicle characteristics and related factors that could potentially be correlated with heightened severity of injury and increased care requirements.

This study analyzed more than 13 000 recent MVCs in the state of Michigan and identified several vehicle-specific parameters that were associated with severe injury, mortality, and the need for higher hospital resources. The results suggest vehicle characteristics, including vehicle age, and crash factors, including airbag deployment, restraint use, and collision type, could impart some prognostic data of occupants at risk of severe crash-related injury, elevated trauma care needs, and increased mortality. These data suggest that there may be further benefit to increased attention to these kinds of data for the development of an updated field triage algorithm, ultimately including direct vehicle telemetry. Furthermore, trauma registries should capture these data to aid in quality improvement.

Specific vehicle and collision characteristics, information gathered at the scene of the MVC, demonstrate significant associations with the severity of injury and mortality. Within known vehicle characteristics, increased vehicle age was shown in univariable analysis to be a significant predictor of increased risk of severe injury, specifically vehicles older than 1989. This is most likely related to improved vehicle safety features and technology in more modern vehicles. These associations, however, were not statistically significant in multivariable models, likely due to the more important contribution of other factors such as collision type, airbag deployment, and restraint use.

Airbags have been shown to increase the chance of survival after MVCs; however, they are also known to cause minor injury.9 10 Our regression model, consistent with prior work, demonstrates a significant association between airbag deployment and the severity of injury, increased level of hospital care needs, and mortality. We interpret this result in the context of the known collinearity between vehicle crash severity and airbag deployment.9 11 This is explained by the near-universal deployment of airbags for substantial vehicular crashes, and the subsequent higher impact energy sustained in collisions that leads to airbag deployment. However, airbag deployment is shown to have a net-protective effect in serious crashes, such as those with injured occupants having an increased ISS.12 And so, we conclude that airbag deployment could be considered an informative variable in EMS triage assessments, though, these results cannot specify causality.

Collision characteristics associated with heightened care needs and an elevated risk of severe injury included head-on collisions and single vehicle collisions.13 14 Rear-end collisions and same side-swiped collisions were not predictive of worsening outcomes. This underscores the untapped potential for an objective analysis of both vehicle and collision characteristics within field triage guidelines, facilitating earlier identification of patients predisposed to a high ISS, necessitating advanced treatment, or exhibiting a heightened risk of mortality.

Further adjustments to the EMS field triage guidelines to include vehicle and mechanism characteristics can provide predictive information about patient care needs after MVCs, leading to more informed triage decisions. Our study has several limitations inherent to using retrospective data from a single state. Primarily, the data are sourced from crash reports and clinical registries in Michigan, which may not accurately reflect clinical outcomes on a national scale. Similarly, given the de-identified nature of the dataset, we could not associate some factors with community factors which may explain some of our findings, such as associations between race and the primary outcomes. Future studies may be able to assess whether racial demographics in areas where crash severity is higher, like rural areas with higher speed limits, can explain these associations. Additionally, the data span from 2020 to 2022, a period potentially affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, which could have altered driving behaviors and MVC trends in the state. Finally, the analysis depends on the accuracy of the matching between datasets, which was performed by analysts and data scientists at ICAM. Internal checks by ICAM found the data to be highly accurate; however, given the large sample size, rare errors could have impacted the results.

Conclusion

Our data demonstrate that vehicle and collision characteristics, specifically vehicle age, airbag deployment, and restraint use, could improve field assessments of risk of severe injury, need for higher levels of care, and mortality. This underscores the need for further adjustments to triage assessments to include available vehicle and collision factors. Evaluations of vehicle and collision characteristics are necessary to accurately predict care needs and transfer injured motorists to appropriate trauma centers.
