Rowing to the moon
Authors: Stephen Workman
Published: 2025

Not always, but too often, my patient and I both watch my right hand as it shakes. Sometimes, to finish writing a prescription, I steady it with my left hand. Oops.

And I wonder: Should I address the elephant in the room? Now, as you can gather, there’s no elephant. Not even a chihuahua in a hand-knit sweater hiding from the Halifax cold. But there is my right arm. My good right arm. Having it flail around gets tiresome real fast. At dinner, you don’t want to sit to my right when I’m tucking into potatoes and gravy. My right hand is a trebuchet — I’ll bury you by the time dessert comes. Not my fault! It’s not really my right arm anymore. I have joint custody with Parkinson disease.

My other arm — the sinister one — overlooked for over half a century? Steady, reliable, my rock. Sure, it looks like a Cirque du Soleil rehearsal when I use it to eat, but it’s dependable. I’ve tried everything to get my right hand to behave, but it’s like herding cats. I’ve had to accept I’m no longer in control of it.

This whole sideshow started about 10 years ago, when my squash game fell off a cliff. Not that I was Federer — wrong sport — but at least I could cover the court and get to the ball. Then the ball seemed to take out a restraining order against me, and the squash-court floor felt like it had been coated with glue traps. My hard shots turned to fluffballs. Around the same time, my walks to work got weird. Nursery school kids holding that little rope would call, “Passing on your right!” I tried to match stride. Left foot, right foot — faster, faster. I felt like Marvin the Martian hurrying to blow up Earth, but my cadence was slow.

You may have heard of dopamine fasts — no screens, no alcohol, no thrills. I’m on one for life. I’m halfway through my 10-year levodopa honeymoon.

My dad (Poppa, as the grandkids called him) also had Parkinson’s, and he left me lots to aspire to. The man had guts and wit, right up to the end. The day before his medical-assistance-in-dying appointment, his best friend, Bob, took him for a final drive. In the car, Bob, a lifelong introvert, said, “Don, the way things are going … I think I’m going to have to give a speech at your funeral.” Without missing a beat, my dad grinned and shot back: “Well, I won’t!”

As for me? I’m trying to make the most of my healthspan — good advice for anyone who thinks they’re healthy, of course. And I’m conceited enough to try to change the slope of my decline — ideally to upward. Hope? Try to live without it. Like living without air.

So I take enough propranolol to give a woolly mammoth bradycardia before going onstage with my local theatre group. It turns out that having 300 people laugh at your jokes is good for dopamine and stage fright. The more the crowd laughs, the less I shake. And so far, I haven’t bombed.

Of course, I take levodopa to keep moving. And woo-woo treatments? Bring them on — if they’re safe and come with at least one PubMed citation. I’m currently giving krill oil a test. It works on round-worms1 — and has anyone ever seen a blue whale with Parkinson’s? Exactly! I also take vitamins from A to Z, and would probably take snake oil, too — if it were on sale. If I seem desperate, it’s because I am. I know for sure that business as usual was killing my dopaminergic neurons.

Unfortunately, proven disease-modifying therapies are rare — whole-body exercise being the one likely exception.2 That’s why I’m hoping to get to the moon on my rowing machine — 3400 km down, 347 000 to go. I’m taking sponsors for the rest of the trip. All proceeds to Parkinson Canada.

My goal? Not really the moon. Maybe just to be able to get down onto the grass in my backyard to play with my future grandkids — and back up again. Although my backyard does have a permanent population of European fire ants, so maybe just sitting with the kids on the deck will suffice.

I’m not too keen on the glass-half-full philosophy. I feel ridiculous and humiliated when I fall off my bicycle in the hospital parking lot or get invited to preboard after fumbling with my ID. I’ve been to support groups and I know I’m not the only one who feels burdened. One participant shared that he’d retired after 30 years only to be diagnosed 3 months later. After hearing this and other sad stories, I proposed buttons: Parkinson’s Sucks; Ask Me Why.

But pulmonary embolism, coronary disease, pancreatic cancer, endocarditis, car–bike collisions: These have abruptly ended the lives of friends, neighbours, classmates, and colleagues, so I’m not sure I can count myself unlucky. Obviously, I’d rather laugh than cry, which is something I suspect I learned from my father, who met his fate with courage and humour. I’ve come to believe — and there’s evidence to support it — that humour is one of the most powerful tools a parent can pass on.3 I hope I’ve given it to my children.

And there’s another gift I want to leave them — a gift that brings tears to my eyes just to contemplate, something I can’t joke about. I want them to inherit a world without Parkinson’s. And for that to happen, things need to change, because right now, business as usual isn’t nearly good enough.4 Not for them. Not for my family. Not for any of us.
