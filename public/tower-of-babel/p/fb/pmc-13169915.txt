Exercise-induced muscle exosomes: microRNA cargo as regulators of cardiovascular remodeling and disease progression
Authors: Peng Yu, FengYi Lv, WenTao Wang, Shunling Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVD) are a primary cause of global morbidity and mortality, with incomplete understanding of how lifestyle factors like exercise provide protection. Skeletal muscle functions as a secretory organ, releasing exosomes containing microRNAs (miRNAs) that facilitate intercellular communication and influence inflammation, angiogenesis, and cardiac remodeling.MethodsThis review synthesizes recent literature on exercise-induced, muscle-derived exosomal miRNAs in cardiovascular biology. It emphasizes key miRNAs (miR-1, miR-133a/b, miR-206, miR-486) modulated by physical activity, their roles in endothelial function, myocardial repair, and vascular adaptation, as well as underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms and biomarker potential. Insights are integrated from molecular biology, exercise physiology, and cardiovascular research.ResultsExercise dynamically alters exosomal miRNAs, with high-intensity interval training (HIIT) inducing a 2- to 3-fold increase in circulating exosomal miR-133a, which reduces cardiac fibrosis by targeting connective tissue growth factor (CTGF). These miRNAs mediate protective effects on endothelial function, myocardial repair, and vascular adaptation, positioning them as biomarkers of cardiovascular health.ConclusionsExosomal miRNAs serve as key mediators of exercise-induced systemic adaptations and hold promise as targets for precision diagnostics and therapeutic interventions in CVD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

As one of the foremost contributors to worldwide disease burden and mortality, cardiovascular disorders demand the refinement of diagnostic and prognostic strategies to improve patient outcomes and clinical decision-making [1] . In spite of evolving medical interventions, the prompt diagnosis and holistic monitoring of cardiovascular disease trajectories remain enduring difficulties within contemporary healthcare [2]. Currently utilized biomarkers—particularly cardiac troponins and BNP—function as fundamental diagnostic tools for identifying acute cardiac events, such as infarctions and episodes of heart failure, in clinical cardiology [3]. However, these markers often reflect significant cellular damage or physiological stress at later stages of the disease, potentially limiting their utility in tracking the subtle molecular changes indicative of long-term CVD development and progression [4–6]. This necessitates the exploration of novel biomarkers that can provide earlier and more nuanced insights into the pathobiology of CVDs.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), approximately 22 nucleotides in size, represent a group of endogenous, non-coding RNAs that orchestrate gene regulation through post-transcriptional mechanisms, influencing a wide array of cellular functions [7]. MicroRNAs regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding to the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of messenger RNAs (mRNAs), leading to translational repression or mRNA destabilization; through these mechanisms, they are integral to a wide spectrum of biological processes, including the initiation and advancement of cardiovascular disorders [7–9]. Aberrant miRNA expression patterns have been implicated in diverse cardiovascular ailments, pointing to their prospective application as key diagnostic and prognostic markers [10].

extracellular vesicles (EVs) called exosomes, characterized by their nanoscale size of approximately 30 to 150 nanometers, are secreted by nearly all cell types and circulate within various biological fluids, including blood plasma [11]. Serving as pivotal agents of cell-to-cell signaling, these vesicles encapsulate and deliver diverse biomolecular contents, including proteins, lipids, messenger RNA, microRNA, and DNA, thereby enabling communication between cells and tissues [12]. The protection afforded by the exosomal lipid bilayer to enclosed miRNAs against ribonucleases (RNases)-mediated degradation in circulation significantly increases their stability, rendering them valuable for biomarker discovery efforts [11, 13, 14]. The primary objective of this review is to systematically synthesize the current literature regarding the release of muscle-derived exosomal miRNAs during exercise and their subsequent impact on cardiovascular remodeling, disease progression, and their potential utility as diagnostic biomarkers.

Cellular mechanisms: skeletal muscle as an endocrine organ

Physical exercise is a well-established environmental factor that induces a multitude of systemic adaptations, promoting overall health and playing a significant role in the prevention of various lifestyle-related chronic diseases, including CVDs [15]. Through exercise-induced secretion of signaling molecules, skeletal muscle operates as an endocrine organ, influencing the function and homeostasis of distant tissues and organs, particularly the cardiovascular system [16]. Current evidence highlights that exercise-induced physical activity alters the secretion dynamics of exosomes and reshapes their miRNA content, predominantly from skeletal muscle sources [11]. Exercise-induced exosomes derived from muscle tissue, referred to as “exerkines,” carry characteristic miRNA cargoes that potentially mediate a range of favorable effects on overall systemic health. [16, 17]. Physical exercise is a potent physiological stimulus that induces profound cardiovascular adaptations, including enhanced angiogenesis, improved endothelial function, and pathological remodeling reversal. At the cellular level, skeletal muscle adapts to the mechanical and metabolic stress of exercise by acting as a highly active endocrine organ. It secretes a myriad of signaling molecules—termed exerkines—into the systemic circulation, facilitating cross-talk with distant tissues such as the heart and vasculature. To fully understand this systemic communication, it is first necessary to examine the molecular effectors of this process: microRNAs.

MicroRNA biogenesis

The endocrine function of exercising skeletal muscle is largely mediated by the synthesis and systemic release of non-coding RNAs. MicroRNA biogenesis involves a coordinated multistage pathway, encompassing transcription, RNA splicing and processing, cytoplasmic export, maturation, and precise target binding (Fig. 1). The initial process in the biogenesis of miRNAs is the nuclear synthesis of pri-miRNA—a complex transcript encompassing multiple miRNA sequences—facilitated by RNA polymerase II [18]enzyme, in conjunction with the DGCR8 cofactor, catalyzes the cleavage of pri-miRNA, forming a precursor miRNA molecule of roughly 70 nucleotides in length. The nuclear export of pre-miRNA into the cytoplasm is orchestrated by exportin-5 and RanGTP-binding proteins, which facilitate its passage through nuclear pore complexes [18]. Dicer enzymatically cleaves pre-miRNA in the cytoplasm to produce a double-stranded miRNA duplex roughly 22 nucleotides in length, which includes two potentially functional mature strands (miR-5p and miR-3p), though one arm is often preferentially selected while the other may degrade or also become active in certain contexts (e.g., both strands of miR-223 in cardiac tissue) [19]. Target binding induces unwinding of the miRNA duplex, enabling one or both mature strands to be assembled into the RNA-induced silencing complex.The RNA-induced silencing complex, composed of Argonaute proteins and diverse modulatory effectors, governs the suppression of target gene activity. The RNA-induced silencing complex directs the mature miRNA strand to engage its target mRNA via complementary base pairing at the seed sequence, completing the gene silencing process. In summary, miRNAs exert significant regulatory control over gene expression throughout the human genome [20, 21]. Extracellular miRNAs, following their synthesis and processing, are secreted into blood circulation encapsulated in various carriers, including exosomes, microparticles, lipid vesicles, or associated with argonaute-2, nucleophosmin-1 proteins, and high- or low-density lipoproteins, thereby safeguarding them against enzymatic digestion [22–24]. Thus, miRNAs preserve their stability in blood under a variety of harsh conditions such as elevated alkaline pH, high temperatures, multiple freeze-thaw cycles, prolonged storage, and degradation by ribonuclease enzymes. In the bloodstream, free synthetic miRNAs introduced from external sources are easily degraded by ribonuclease enzymes [22–24]. A majority of miRNAs are produced by blood cells and tissues such as the liver, heart, kidneys, and lungs, contributing to their systemic presence [25]. miRNAs are essential regulators in a variety of cellular processes, such as embryogenesis, proliferation, vasculogenesis, apoptosis, growth and differentiation, and the development of tumors. Exosome biogenesis

To survive the ribonuclease-rich environment of the bloodstream and successfully reach cardiovascular targets, these newly synthesized miRNAs require a robust biological delivery system. Through the inward folding of late endosomal membranes, intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) develop within multivesicular bodies (MVBs), a process that results in the formation of exosomes (Fig. 3). Exosomes, characterized by a diameter spanning approximately 30 to 160 nanometers, are produced through the exocytosis of MVBs, which release ILVs [27].

Exosomal packaging: a biological solution for miRNA stability and delivery

Exosomes are formed through a highly regulated endosomal pathway. The process begins with the inward invagination of late endosomal membranes to form intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) within multivesicular bodies (MVBs). Following cargo sorting—where specific proteins, lipids, and miRNAs are packaged into the ILVs—the MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane to release these vesicles into the extracellular space as mature exosomes [28, 29]. Rather than a passive disposal mechanism, exosomal biogenesis is a highly dynamic process responsive to physiological stress. In the context of cardiovascular biology and exercise, physical exertion and associated metabolic stressors robustly stimulate both the biogenesis of MVBs and the exocytosis of exosomes, simultaneously altering their specific miRNA cargo to facilitate systemic adaptive responses [30–33] (Fig. 3).

Review strategy and selection criteria

To ensure a comprehensive and rigorous narrative synthesis, a structured literature search was conducted. The search strategy encompassed three primary electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus, covering literature published from January 2010 through December 2025. The conceptual boundaries of the search were defined using the following Boolean keyword combinations: (“exercise” OR “physical activity” OR “high-intensity interval training”) AND (“exosomes” OR “extracellular vesicles”) AND (“microRNA” OR “miRNA” OR “myomiR”) AND (“cardiovascular disease” OR “cardiac remodeling” OR “skeletal muscle crosstalk”).

Inclusion criteria were restricted to peer-reviewed original research articles and comprehensive reviews published in English that directly investigated the dynamic changes of exosomal miRNAs following exercise interventions and their physiological or pathological effects on the cardiovascular system. Studies were excluded if they focused exclusively on non-exosomal miRNA transport, did not involve exercise/physical activity interventions, or lacked relevance to cardiovascular or skeletal muscle biology. By explicitly defining these parameters, this review aims to provide a reliable, structured, and reproducible overview of the current state of exerkine-mediated cardiovascular protection.

Exercise-dependent regulation of exosomal miRnas

To accurately interpret the systemic release of exomiRs, it is necessary to explicitly define the exercise modalities and conditions under study. ‘Acute exercise’ refers to a single bout of physical exertion, which typically elicits a transient spike in exosome release and rapid clearance. ‘Chronic exercise’ or ‘training’ denotes repeated bouts over weeks or months, leading to sustained physiological adaptations and altered baseline exomiR profiles. Furthermore, the stimulus varies significantly by modality: endurance training (prolonged aerobic activity), resistance training (anaerobic, load-bearing activity), and high-intensity interval training (HIIT; alternating short bursts of intense anaerobic exercise with recovery periods) each impose distinct mechanical and metabolic stressors on skeletal muscle, thereby generating unique exosomal miRNA signatures.

Significant alterations in miRNA expression within different tissues and bodily fluids can be elicited by physical activity, an influential environmental factor [34]. Numerous investigations have revealed that miRNAs play a vital role in mediating the physiological adaptations elicited by physical exercise [35]. Physical exertion has been shown to significantly modulate the expression of specific miRNAs, namely miR-1, miR-133, miR-206, miR-208, and miR-486, as demonstrated by multiple research investigations [11]. For instance, acute endurance exercise has been reported to induce a 1.5- to 4-fold upregulation of miR-133a in human plasma, correlating with improved endothelial function via targeting of RhoA and Cdc42 [36, 37]. The growth and upkeep of skeletal and heart muscle cells, mitochondrial generation, vascular system formation, and the sophisticated modulation of metabolic pathways are critically governed by these miRNAs [11, 38].

The detection of exercise-induced changes in specific miRNAs, combined with their correlation to various exercise regimens and intensities, presents promising prospects for their use as indicators of adaptive physical responses and as biomarkers reflecting the body’s reaction to physical activity in chronic disease scenarios [11, 39, 40]. Notably, the type of exercise performed, whether it be intensive resistance training or prolonged endurance exercise, appears to elicit distinct effects on hormone levels within tissues, which in turn plays a crucial role in the adaptive responses of muscle [11, 41]. This suggests that different exercise modalities may lead to unique alterations in miRNA profiles, potentially offering a more refined understanding of the physiological adaptations occurring. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that acute bouts of exercise can lead to the rapid upregulation and subsequent clearance of specific circulating miRNAs, indicating a dynamic and immediate molecular response to physical activity [42–44].

High-intensity interval training (HIIT), known for its popularity and efficacy, has been found to substantially reshape the miRNA landscape within circulating exosomes [45]. Evidence from animal studies indicates that HIIT protocols lead to significant rises in levels of miRNAs such as miR-133a and miR-133b within circulating exosomes [16]. pecifically, HIIT in mice resulted in a 2-fold increase in exosomal miR-133a, enhancing insulin sensitivity by downregulating hepatic FoxO1 (16). These findings highlight the potency of HIIT as a stimulus for the release of exosomes enriched with specific regulatory molecules. In contrast, research examining the effects of long-term regular exercise, spanning both shorter durations (around 0.5 years) and extended periods (25+ years), has revealed that the majority of the exosomal miRNA (exomiR) content released tends to overlap between these different durations of training [15]. This suggests that while the immediate responses to exercise might vary, sustained regular physical activity may establish a relatively consistent pattern of exosomal miRNA modulation, potentially contributing to the long-term health benefits associated with exercise. This consistency could be indicative of core molecular mechanisms through which chronic exercise exerts its protective effects (Fig. 2) [46, 47]. The stability intrinsic to exosomes allows them to impact numerous bodily processes through the transfer of molecular cargo to target cells, thereby affecting cellular behavior.

Exercise has been observed to provoke the discharge of exosomes from trained skeletal muscle. Exosomes generated through exercise exhibit specific miRNA compositions that facilitate gene expression modifications in tissues beyond muscle, for example, the liver [16, 49, 50]. This inter-organ communication mediated by exosomal miRNAs contributes to a range of metabolic benefits associated with exercise, including a notable improvement in insulin sensitivity [16, 51, 52]. Exercise-induced exosomes have been implicated as significant contributors to the propagation of beneficial impacts throughout the organism. These vesicles are implicated in regulating fundamental signaling pathways intimately linked to growth and development, specifically the Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT, and IGF1 signaling pathways [11, 53].

Current findings strongly imply that circulating exosomes are key players in orchestrating certain well-known health benefits resulting from exercise. This regulatory effect is carried out by microRNA transport occurring between multiple tissues in the body [15]. Skeletal muscle cells actively participate in the biosynthesis and extracellular release of exosomes. Skeletal muscle-derived exosomes (SKM-Exos) transport multiple myofactors in addition to other key biological regulators, including esRNA, cytokines, chemokines, and prostaglandins [54]. These factors within SKM-Exos are essential for their function in controlling metabolism, promoting cellular differentiation, and supporting tissue repair and regeneration [54]. In essence, exercise leads to a measurable increase in the number of exosomes circulating in the blood and simultaneously induces modifications in the profile of these circulating miRNAs [16]. For example, chronic exercise has been shown to alter 14 CVD-related miRNAs, including upregulation of miR-342-5p in exosomes, which targets apoptotic pathways like Casp3/Sp1 to confer cardioprotection [36, 46]. This coordinated response, involving both the quantity and the specific molecular content of exosomes, strongly suggests a targeted mechanism by which the body adapts to the physiological demands of physical activity, ultimately contributing to the wide-ranging health benefits associated with regular exercise.

A range of studies has demonstrated that certain miRNAs are preferentially enriched in skeletal muscle-derived exosomes upon stimulation by physical exercise. Notably, miR-133a and miR-133b have continually been recognized as principal agents within this subset. Evidence from animal studies highlights a pronounced increase in the presence of these miRNAs within circulating exosomes following HIIT in mice. These miRNAs are also integral components of SKM-Exos [54, 55]. Notably, research has indicated that the circulating levels of miR-133a also rise after prolonged endurance exercise, such as running a marathon [11]. Furthermore, both miR-133a and miR-133b have been shown to be upregulated in extracellular vesicles following acute exercise sessions [56], and their levels have been observed to increase in circulation within 1–2 hours and even up to 24 hours post-exercise [57].

Another prominent miRNA identified in this context is miR-206. Similar to miR-133a and b, miR-206 also shows an increase in circulating exosomes after HIIT in mice [16] and is found within SKM-Exos [54]. Interestingly, some studies have reported a downregulation of miR-206 in trained individuals immediately following an acute exercise bout [58], while others have observed its upregulation in extracellular vesicles after acute exercise [56] and an increase in circulation after intense exercise [42]. This suggests that the regulation of miR-206 by exercise may be influenced by factors such as training status and the specific characteristics of the exercise performed.

miR-486-5p presents another interesting case. Evidence demonstrates that physically active older men possess increased baseline expression of this miRNA in comparison to sedentary counterparts. However, its levels have been found to decrease in trained individuals immediately after an acute exercise session [58]. It has been reported that miR-486-5p undergoes changes in plasma and exosomal levels post high-intensity exercise and is upregulated within the myocardium following physical exertion [42, 59]. Additionally, exercise training-induced extracellular miR-136-3p has been found to modulate glucose uptake and myogenesis in human skeletal muscle by targeting NRDC, highlighting inter-organ crosstalk [60].

SKM-Exos have been demonstrated to harbor miR-1, a microRNA known for its muscle specificity [54]. Its levels have been shown to be altered in muscle, plasma, and exosomes following intense exercise [42], and it increases in circulation after prolonged exercise [57].

Physical activity has been found to affect the expression of additional exosomal miRNAs beyond those most commonly identified. Included in this collection are multiple miRNAs—such as let-7a-5p, let-7 g-5p, and miR-130a-3p—that show a significant decline in expression post-exercise [15, 61]. Additionally, miR-215-5p and miR-941 have shown increased baseline expression with regular exercise, while miR-151b exhibited decreased baseline expression [58]. The responsiveness of numerous exosomal miRNAs to exercise signifies the multifaceted molecular remodeling that occurs in the body as a consequence of engaging in physical activity. Contradictory findings and methodological confounders

While a consensus is emerging regarding the exercise-induced mobilization of specific exomiRs, the literature is not without contradictory and negative findings. This divergence is essential to acknowledge to avoid overinterpretation. For instance, while some studies report robust upregulation of miR-206 and miR-486-5p following acute exercise, others have documented significant downregulation or no change at all within trained individuals subjected to similar acute bouts [57, 58]. Similarly, discrepancies exist regarding the baseline resting levels of myomiRs (such as miR-1 and miR-133a) when comparing endurance athletes to sedentary controls, with some cohorts showing elevated baselines and others reporting no significant differences.

These inconsistencies are largely driven by methodological confounders. A primary limitation is the lack of standardization in exosome isolation techniques; methods such as ultracentrifugation, polymer-based precipitation, and size-exclusion chromatography yield vesicles of varying purity, often co-isolating free-floating ribonucleoprotein complexes that contaminate the miRNA pool. Furthermore, biological heterogeneity—including the participants’ baseline fitness, age, sex, and the specific timing of blood sampling post-exercise (e.g., 1 hour vs. 24 hours)—greatly influences exomiR kinetics. Consequently, drawing definitive physiological conclusions requires cautious interpretation of the specific experimental design and population studied.

Mechanisms by which muscle-derived exosomal miRnas influence cardiovascular disease progression

When evaluating the mechanisms by which muscle-derived exosomal miRNAs influence cardiovascular disease progression, it is critical to delineate the physiological context and target populations. The cardioprotective pathways discussed below—such as enhanced angiogenesis and the attenuation of fibrosis—are predominantly observed in models of pathological stress. Specifically, these exerkine-mediated benefits are most pronounced in sedentary subjects, individuals with metabolic diseases (e.g., obesity or diabetes), or preclinical models subjected to cardiovascular insults (e.g., myocardial infarction, ischemia-reperfusion, or pressure overload) who subsequently undergo exercise interventions. In these compromised populations, exercise-induced miRNAs act to reverse or mitigate pathological remodeling, functioning differently than they would in the baseline homeostatic maintenance of healthy, highly trained athletes.

Muscle-derived exosomal miRNAs generated during exercise play a regulatory role in cardiovascular disease progression by affecting various molecular signaling networks, chiefly through the modulation of gene expression within cardiac and vascular systems.

miR-1, a muscle-specific miRNA, exhibits aberrant expression patterns in various cardiac conditions. Attenuation of this element within heart tissue has been implicated in the pathogenesis of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), a condition characterized by myocardial dilation and compromised mechanical performance [66]. Upregulation of miR-1 is associated with the promotion of apoptosis in tumorigenic cells, pointing to its critical role in governing survival-related cellular pathways [67]. This miRNA is known to regulate several genes that are critical for both cardiac development and function [68]. Suppressed expression of miR-1 has been implicated in the pathophysiology of atrial fibrillation and acute myocardial infarction, both of which substantially impact cardiovascular health outcomes [69]. Experimental data from animal models indicate that the therapeutic reintroduction of miR-1 can reverse myocardial hypertrophy induced by hemodynamic pressure overload [70]. The early rise of circulating miR-1 following the initial manifestation of chest pain, preceding traditional indicators like troponin T, underscores its potential role as a prompt and sensitive marker for impending cardiac events [71, 72].

The suppression of miR-133a expression, a significant cardiac-enriched microRNA, is connected to the progression of heart failure and occurs concurrently with myocardial infarction (MI) and hypertrophic changes in cardiac tissue. Enhancement of miR-133a expression following myocardial infarction supports the survival and function of cardiac progenitor cells by limiting fibrosis and hypertrophy and stimulating angiogenic processes within the myocardium [37, 73]. This miRNA is also implicated in the broader process of cardiac remodeling [64]. In vitro studies have indicated that miR-133a functions as an inhibitor of cardiomyocyte proliferation [74]. The downregulation of miR-133a has been clinically documented in individuals with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions, including heart failure and stroke [75]. Experimental overexpression of miR-133a in models of diabetic cardiomyopathy effectively suppresses the disease’s emergence [76]. The application of miR-133a in therapeutic contexts has revealed protective properties that hinder heart failure progression [77]. Circulating miR-133a exhibits an early increase after chest pain onset, paralleling the elevation pattern observed with miR-1 [71].

The expression of miR-133b, closely related to miR-133a, is suppressed in coronary artery disease (CAD) cases and is functionally involved in the cardiac remodeling process [63, 64]. The suppression of cardiac fibrosis via direct targeting of connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) has been attributed to the overexpression of miR-133b [78]. Interestingly, miR-133b appears to be upregulated in the context of atherosclerosis (AS) [79] and may even aggravate this condition by activating the Notch signaling pathway [80, 81]. In cases of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), diminished miR-133b expression is commonly reported [63].

By modulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, miR-206 participates in cardiac remodeling and acts to hinder the progression of coronary artery disease, with VEGF serving as a principal factor in angiogenesis [64, 82]. This miRNA also exhibits protective effects against apoptosis in cardiomyocytes induced by acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [83]. miR-206 has been identified as a key mediator of cardiac hypertrophy and survival processes induced by Yes-associated protein (YAP) [84]. The association of circulating muscle-derived miR-206 with cardiac autonomic denervation highlights its potential significance in linking skeletal muscle pathology with cardiovascular health [85, 86].

Through targeting insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), the downregulation of miR-486-5p confers cardioprotective effects against hypoxia-induced injury, reflecting its nuanced role in cardiovascular health [87, 88]. By influencing Smad-dependent transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling, it confers defense against ischemia-reperfusion damage in renal tissue [89, 90]. The role of miR-486-5p includes the stimulation of angiogenesis subsequent to myocardial infarction [91]. In cases of atrial fibrillation with pronounced low-voltage cardiac areas, miR-486-5p plasma expression is significantly elevated [92]. miR-486-5p is instrumental in lessening cardiac ischemia/reperfusion injury and in conveying the protective effects of exercise on myocardial tissue [59, 93]. The presence of advanced atherosclerotic plaques is associated with an upregulation of this specific miRNA in affected patients [94]. An association exists between habitual exercise and the upregulation of baseline miR-486-5p levels [58]. The seemingly contradictory roles of miR-486-5p in different cardiovascular contexts underscore the intricate regulatory networks in which miRNAs participate. For instance, while its downregulation is protective under hypoxic conditions [90], its upregulation induced by exercise also confers cardioprotection against ischemia/reperfusion injury [59]. This suggests that the effects of miR-486-5p may be context-dependent, potentially reflecting different cellular signaling environments and target gene interactions. By regulating this miRNA, exercise could play a critical role in preparing the cardiovascular system to better resist subsequent ischemic episodes [90]{Matsuzaka, 2016 #1211}. Furthermore, exosomes and microRNAs serve as key mediators of exercise benefits in cardiovascular health, contributing to positive outcomes through circulating exosomal transfer [11].

Evidence from animal models and experimental manipulation

To move beyond descriptive correlations and establish causality, recent research has increasingly relied on animal models and targeted experimental manipulation of exosomal miRNAs. Studies employing direct in vivo transfer of exercise-induced exosomes have provided compelling mechanistic evidence. For example, Castaño et al. demonstrated that the systemic injection of serum exosomes isolated from HIIT-trained mice into sedentary, diet-induced obese mice effectively transferred the metabolic benefits of exercise, specifically improving insulin sensitivity via the exosomal delivery of miR-133a to the liver [16].

Furthermore, the use of synthetic miRNA mimics and antagomiRs (inhibitors) in murine models of cardiovascular disease has validated the functional targets of these exerkines. In models of pressure overload-induced heart failure, the administration of miR-133a mimics or nano-formulated miR-133a replacements successfully blunted pathological cardiac remodeling, fibrosis, and hypertrophy, mirroring the cardioprotective phenotype observed following chronic exercise [70, 77]. Conversely, the pharmacological inhibition of miR-486-5p in models of ischemia-reperfusion injury abolishes the protective, anti-apoptotic effects typically conferred by exercise preconditioning [59, 93]. These experimental manipulations confirm that muscle-derived exosomal miRNAs are not merely passive biomarkers, but active, necessary conduits for cardiovascular repair.

Challenges and future directions

Even with the encouraging prospects of exercise-induced myomiRs in exosomes as indicators of cardiovascular disease progression, significant hurdles and unexplored dimensions continue to necessitate comprehensive investigation. The inconsistency of methodological approaches for exosome isolation and miRNA quantification across studies constitutes a considerable barrier to progress [7, 95, 96]. Differences in methodological approaches hinder direct data comparison among laboratories and restrict the broader validation of discoveries in this domain. Ensuring the consistent and reproducible application of these biomarkers in clinical environments requires the development of standardized and robust protocols [97].

Another important area for future research involves a deeper understanding of tissue specificity. While studies have identified miRNAs enriched in exosomes released from exercising muscle, determining the precise cellular origin of these circulating exosomal miRNAs and their specific target tissues within the cardiovascular system in the context of exercise and CVD progression requires further investigation [15, 42]. Pinpointing the cells that release these exosomes and the specific cardiovascular cells they interact with will be essential for elucidating the precise mechanisms of action [98].

Furthermore, while emerging evidence suggests correlations between exercise, exosomal miRNAs, and CVD-related outcomes, more research is needed to functionally validate the causal roles of these miRNAs in the progression of cardiovascular diseases [15, 99]. Identifying the specific target genes of these exercise-modulated exosomal miRNAs within cardiovascular tissues and demonstrating their direct impact on disease-related pathways is critical for establishing their clinical relevance [100, 101].

To thoroughly comprehend how exosomal miRNAs influence the chronic progression of cardiovascular disease, it is essential to move beyond cross-sectional or acute exercise studies and implement longitudinal research designs [15, 102]. Tracking changes in exosomal miRNA profiles over extended periods in relation to individuals’ exercise habits and the development or progression of cardiovascular conditions will provide valuable insights into their potential as prognostic biomarkers [49].

Ultimately, translating these research findings into clinically useful biomarkers will require large-scale validation studies in diverse human populations [15, 58]. Future investigations should recruit participants across a spectrum of cardiovascular risk and exercise engagement to verify the effectiveness of specific exosomal miRNA profiles in forecasting and monitoring the course of cardiovascular disease.

In-depth understanding of the roles of exercise-induced exosomal miRNAs in cardiovascular pathology has the potential to drive the emergence of novel therapeutic strategies. This may involve engineered exosome-mediated transport of targeted beneficial miRNAs or the design of therapeutic approaches to adjust the endogenous release or uptake of these signaling factors to improve cardiovascular outcomes.

Conclusion

In closing, the analysis of exosomal miRNAs from exercising muscle provides a compelling and prospective direction for discovering novel indicators of cardiovascular disease progression. Exercise-induced regulation of specific miRNAs, including miR-1, miR-133a/b, miR-206, and miR-486-5p, is evident, with these molecules having established involvement in cardiovascular system physiology and pathology. The promising preliminary findings notwithstanding, further systematic research is imperative to address obstacles in standardizing methods, confirming tissue-specific functions, validating roles, and conducting longitudinal analyses. The ability to surmount these challenges is fundamental to unlocking the full diagnostic, prognostic, and monitoring capabilities of exosomal miRNAs in cardiovascular disease management. Comprehensive elucidation of the molecular mechanisms involved might contribute to the formulation of pioneering therapies that capitalize on the advantageous molecular effects of exercise to fight cardiovascular diseases on a global scale. Merging the fields of exercise physiology and molecular biology offers a compelling and innovative framework to advance research and therapeutic approaches for these widely occurring and serious health conditions.
