Spinal cord infarction is a rare cause of acute neurological deficits and may present as a stroke mimic.1–4 Cervical ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) has been reported to contribute to the acute onset or worsening of cervical myelopathy after minor trauma, occasionally resulting in severe neurological deficits.5,6 However, prospective studies have suggested that only a minority of patients without myelopathy at baseline subsequently develop myelopathy, and that the natural course of symptomatic OPLL is usually gradual.5,7 Thus, abrupt neurological presentation in the absence of trauma is uncommon in patients with OPLL. Chronic mechanical compression due to cervical OPLL may increase ischemic vulnerability at the compressed cord level, and spinal cord infarction occurring at the level of OPLL has previously been reported.8 The purpose of this illustrative case is to highlight that spinal cord infarction at the C3 level associated with cervical OPLL can mimic acute ischemic stroke, and to discuss the diagnostic and therapeutic implications of this presentation.

Illustrative Case

A 68-year-old man called emergency medical services after the sudden onset of left-sided weakness and a speech disturbance. He had no preceding trauma. On arrival, he was alert. Neurological examination revealed left hemiparesis (manual muscle testing grade 1/5 in the upper extremity and 2/5 in the lower extremity) and a speech disturbance characterized predominantly by hypophonia without clear dysarthria. Cranial nerve examination was otherwise unremarkable, with no aphasia, facial weakness, tongue deviation, or extraocular movement abnormalities. He did not report neck pain either at presentation or in the preoperative period. Deep tendon reflexes were not increased, and no pathological reflexes, including Hoffmann’s sign, were observed. The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score on arrival was 7.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the head and neck was performed to exclude intracranial hemorrhage and aortic dissection. The study showed no intracranial hemorrhage or large-vessel occlusion (Fig. 1A and B). Cervical CT images obtained simultaneously demonstrated mixed-type OPLL at C2–3 with severe spinal canal stenosis (Fig. 1C– E). No contraindications to intravenous thrombolysis were identified. Because acute ischemic stroke was strongly suspected and the patient was within the therapeutic window, intravenous alteplase was administered according to our institutional protocol. Shortly after alteplase administration, sensory disturbance developed in the right upper extremity and later extended to the left upper and lower extremities, with impaired fine motor function of the right hand. Pain sensation was impaired in both upper extremities and the left lower extremity, predominantly in the C5–6 dermatomes of the upper extremities, whereas proprioception and bladder and bowel function were preserved. Follow-up head CT obtained the next day showed no intracranial hemorrhage. Brain MRI, including diffusion-weighted imaging, obtained approximately 48 hours after symptom onset, showed no acute cerebral infarction (Fig. 2). Because persistent neurological deficits remained unexplained despite negative brain MRI findings, cervical MRI was performed with diffusion-weighted imaging and apparent diffusion coefficient sequences to evaluate for a spinal lesion. In contrast, cervical MRI demonstrated diffusion restriction at the C3 level, with hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted and T2-weighted imaging and corresponding reduction on the apparent diffusion coefficient map, consistent with spinal cord infarction (Fig. 3). The infarct was located adjacent to the segment of maximal cord compression caused by OPLL. The OPLL was K-line positive. Because severe OPLL-related canal stenosis persisted at the level of the infarct and the neurological deficits did not improve sufficiently with conservative management, posterior decompression was performed on hospital day 8. This consisted of C3–6 laminoplasty with an additional C2 dome laminectomy to decompress the cranial aspect of the C2–3 stenotic segment and a C7 dome laminectomy to extend decompression caudally. Postoperative CT showed no hemorrhagic complications, and cervical MRI obtained on postoperative day 7 confirmed adequate decompression, with posterior shift of the spinal cord (Fig. 4). At transfer, 1 month after surgery, left hemiparesis had improved to grade 3/5 in the upper extremity and 4/5 in the lower extremity, and both the speech disturbance and sensory deficits had partially improved. Discussion

Observations

This case yielded three principal observations. First, spinal cord infarction at the C3 level adjacent to severe cervical OPLL presented as a stroke mimic and was initially treated as an acute ischemic stroke. Second, the speech disturbance was characterized predominantly by hypophonia without aphasia, facial weakness, tongue deviation, or extraocular movement abnormalities, suggesting that the symptom was not typical of corticobulbar dysfunction. Third, substantial OPLL-related compression persisted at the level of the infarct despite conservative management, and posterior decompression with laminoplasty was subsequently performed.

These observations are in line with prior reports on spinal stroke mimics and cervical cord pathology. Spinal cord infarction is an uncommon but important stroke mimic.3,9 Acute hemiparesis, particularly when accompanied by speech disturbance, may closely resemble cerebral ischemia in the hyperacute phase. In such situations, early brain imaging may fail to reveal the responsible lesion when the pathology is located in the cervical spinal cord rather than the brain.10 Other spinal lesions, particularly spontaneous cervical spinal epidural hematoma, should also be considered because they are well-recognized stroke mimics and may deteriorate after inappropriate thrombolysis.11,12 Although neck pain and the absence of cranial nerve symptoms have been reported as clues to cervical spinal disorders presenting as stroke mimics,13 the absence of neck pain does not exclude cervical spinal cord infarction.11 Accordingly, cervical spine imaging should be considered when brain imaging findings are negative despite persistent stroke-mimicking neurological deficits. From a radiological perspective, diffusion-weighted imaging of the cervical spine was critical in the present case. Cervical diffusion-weighted imaging is not always routinely included in standard cervical MRI protocols, partly because of technical limitations such as motion and susceptibility artifacts. However, when persistent stroke-like deficits remain unexplained despite negative brain imaging, the addition of cervical diffusion-weighted and apparent diffusion coefficient sequences may be particularly valuable for identifying spinal cord infarction and distinguishing it from other spinal stroke mimics.

A further observation was the nature of the speech disturbance. The absence of aphasia, facial weakness, tongue deviation, and extraocular movement abnormalities, together with the lack of acute lesions on brain MRI, including the brainstem, made corticobulbar dysfunction unlikely. Instead, the speech disturbance was considered more consistent with impaired respiratory and phonatory support related to high cervical cord involvement. Previous studies of spinal cord injury have shown that dysarthria associated with cervical cord lesions primarily reflects respiratory dysfunction and its downstream effects on phonation, articulation, and prosody.14 Clinically, this may manifest as reduced or fading loudness, short breath groups, and impaired vocal support. Vocal dysfunction after cervical spinal cord injury has also been reported to include reduced vocal volume, difficulty sustaining longer utterances, and prolonged inspiratory time, further supporting the possibility that cervical cord dysfunction may produce dysarthria-like speech disturbance through impaired breath support.15 Speech disturbance is not entirely unprecedented in cervical spinal lesions. Hu et al. reported dysarthria in 6 of 51 patients with spontaneous cervical spinal epidural hematoma initially suspected of having ischemic stroke, suggesting that cervical spinal pathology may occasionally be accompanied by dysarthria-like symptoms, although the precise mechanism remains uncertain.11 Given that the infarct in our patient was located at the C3 level, near the phrenic motor segments, impaired breath support may plausibly have contributed to the observed hypophonia.

A final observation was the close anatomical relationship between the infarct and the OPLL segment. Chronic mechanical compression may have increased cervical cord vulnerability to ischemia. Although many patients with OPLL remain asymptomatic, chronic cord compression may eventually lead to myelopathy, particularly in those with severe canal compromise.6 Thus, acute neurological presentation in the absence of trauma is uncommon in patients with OPLL16,17 and may suggest an additional pathological process, such as ischemic injury.8 Yoshizawa suggested that chronic spinal cord compression may induce perilesional fibrotic changes,18 thereby compromising blood supply to the spinal cord and increasing vulnerability to ischemic injury. In line with this hypothesis, Novy et al. reported that spinal cord infarction may occur at the level of preexisting chronic spinal pathology, including compression fracture, spondylolisthesis, chronic arachnoiditis, and chronic cervical disc protrusion.19 In the present case, spinal cord infarction at the C3 level developed immediately adjacent to the segment of severe OPLL-related compression, supporting a possible association between chronic structural compression and spinal cord ischemia. This interpretation is also consistent with the report by Tanida et al., who described spinal cord infarction at the level of cervical OPLL and suggested that mechanical compression of the spinal arteries, together with preexisting vascular compromise, may contribute to its pathogenesis.8

Lessons

This illustrative case provides three main lessons. First, cervical spinal cord infarction should be considered in the differential diagnosis of stroke mimics, particularly when cortical or bulbar signs are absent and brain MRI findings are negative. Second, speech disturbance in this setting may reflect hypophonia due to impaired respiratory and phonatory support rather than true bulbar dysarthria. Third, in selected patients with persistent OPLL-related compression at the level of the infarct, posterior decompression may be reasonable after diagnostic confirmation and exclusion of hemorrhagic complications.

Intravenous thrombolysis in the present case should be interpreted in the context of real-world acute stroke management rather than as an established treatment for spinal cord infarction. Evidence regarding thrombolysis for spinal cord infarction remains limited, and no standardized guidelines for acute management currently exist.1,20 Nevertheless, several case reports and literature reviews have suggested that intravenous thrombolysis may be feasible and potentially beneficial in selected patients.20–23 In particular, Bishara and Bloch reviewed 19 reported cases of spinal cord infarction treated with intravenous thrombolysis and found favorable outcomes in most patients, without clinical worsening attributable to postthrombolysis hemorrhage, although they appropriately acknowledged the risk of publication bias.22 A recent nationwide analysis also suggested that thrombolysis was associated with lower 90-day readmission rates, although no significant differences were observed in in-hospital mortality, length of stay, or hospital costs.24 These findings should be interpreted with caution because the available evidence is highly susceptible to publication bias, and spontaneous early improvement may also occur in spinal cord infarction.22 At the same time, the consequences of inadvertent thrombolysis appear to vary according to the underlying spinal pathology. Tsou et al. reported that thrombolysis was not necessarily harmful in a patient with atlantoaxial dislocation mimicking acute ischemic stroke, whereas Hu et al. showed that symptom aggravation after administration of a recombinant tissue plasminogen activator was frequent in spontaneous cervical spinal epidural hematoma, underscoring the importance of rapidly distinguishing hemorrhagic spinal lesions from nonhemorrhagic stroke mimics.11,25 In the present case, alteplase was administered because the initial presentation strongly suggested acute ischemic stroke and no contraindications were identified on initial evaluation. Importantly, subsequent cervical imaging demonstrated no epidural hematoma or intramedullary hemorrhage, indicating that thrombolysis did not result in overt spinal hemorrhagic complications. The practical lesson is that cervical spinal pathology should be reconsidered early in patients presenting with stroke-mimicking symptoms, because thrombolysis may be reasonable in some nonhemorrhagic conditions yet harmful in hemorrhagic spinal lesions.

Although spinal cord infarction itself is not usually considered a primary indication for surgery, laminoplasty was considered reasonable in this case because significant mechanical compression persisted at the level of the infarct. Fusion was not selected because the primary surgical objective was decompression and posterior shift of the spinal cord at the level of the infarct, rather than stabilization for deformity or instability, and because the OPLL was K-line positive. In addition, we aimed to minimize surgical invasiveness in the subacute period after thrombolysis. Mixed-type OPLL at C2–3 caused severe canal stenosis, and substantial spinal cord compression remained adjacent to the infarct despite conservative management. This persistent structural compression was considered potentially detrimental to spinal cord perfusion and neurological recovery. Because the OPLL was K-line positive, posterior decompression was considered an appropriate strategy,26 with the expectation that laminoplasty would provide adequate posterior shift of the spinal cord. Surgery was performed on hospital day 8, after the diagnosis had been established and major hemorrhagic complications of thrombolysis had been excluded. This subacute timing allowed treatment of the persistent compressive pathology while avoiding intervention in the immediate hyperacute phase. Postoperative imaging confirmed adequate decompression, with posterior shift of the spinal cord and no hemorrhagic complications. Although Tanida et al. reported no neurological improvement after decompression in a patient with spinal cord infarction associated with OPLL, our patient showed partial postoperative recovery, suggesting that outcomes after decompression may vary among such cases.8

Several limitations should be acknowledged. A direct causal relationship between OPLL and spinal cord infarction cannot be definitively established from a single case. In addition, because brain MRI was not obtained before thrombolysis, hyperacute cerebral ischemia cannot be completely excluded. The mechanism underlying the speech disturbance remains inferential, because a direct causal link between focal C3 spinal cord infarction and hypophonia cannot be proven. Finally, the relative contributions of thrombolysis, decompression, and rehabilitation to neurological recovery cannot be determined. Despite these limitations, this case underscores that spinal cord infarction at the C3 level associated with cervical OPLL may present as a stroke mimic, even in the absence of preceding trauma. Early cervical spine imaging is crucial when acute hemiparesis is accompanied by speech disturbance but cortical or bulbar signs are absent and brain MRI findings are negative.
