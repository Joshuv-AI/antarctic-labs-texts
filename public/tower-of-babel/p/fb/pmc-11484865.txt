miR-326 overexpression inhibits colorectal cancer cell growth and proteasome activity by targeting PNO1: unveiling a novel therapeutic intervention strategy
Authors: Yi Fang, Yulun Wu, Xinran Zhang, Lihui Wei, Liya Liu, Youqin Chen, Daxin Chen, Nanhui Xu, Liujin Cao, Jie Zhu, Mian Chen, Ying Cheng, Thomas J Sferra, Mengying Yao, Aling Shen, Jun Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProteasome inhibition emerges as a promising strategy for cancer prevention. PNO1, pivotal for colorectal cancer (CRC) progression, is involved in proteasome assembly in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Hence, we aimed to explore the role of PNO1 in proteasome assembly and its up- and down-streams in CRC. Here, we demonstrated that PNO1 knockdown suppressed CRC cells growth, proteasome activities and assembly, as well as CDKN1B/p27Kip1 (p27) degradation. Moreover, p27 knockdown partially attenuated the inhibition of HCT116 cells growth by PNO1 knockdown. The up-stream studies of PNO1 identified miR-326 as a candidate miRNA directly targeting to CDS-region of PNO1 and its overexpression significantly down-regulated PNO1 protein expression, resulting in suppression of cell growth, decrease of proteasome activities and assembly, as well as increasing the stability of p27 in CRC cells. These findings indicated that miR-326 overexpression can suppress CRC cell growth, acting as an endogenous proteasome inhibitor by targeting PNO1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75746-x.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, PNO1, MiR-326, Proteasome assembly, P27KIP1Subject terms: Cancer, Genetics, Molecular biology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most diagnosed cancers in the world, which estimated 153,020 and 52,550 of new cases and deaths from CRC in the United States in 2023, respectively1. The pathogenesis of CRC is complex, including evasion of growth suppressors, bypass of cell cycle checkpoints, tolerance to DNA damage, and resistance to cell death, all of which limited the treatment efficacy for patients2. Therefore, further elucidating the pathogenesis and investigating potential molecular targets in CRC would provide a benefit to the treatment efficacy for patients and improvement of survival outcomes.

As a primary conserved protein degradation system in eukaryotes, the ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) accounted for approximately 80–90% of endogenous protein degradation and facilitates protein recycling, suggesting the essential role of UPS in the cellular process of protein quality control and homeostasis3. Therefore, disorders in proteasome assembly or activity involved in various kinds of diseases, including cancers4–9. Treatment of proteasome inhibitors suppressed the occurrence and development of tumors by inhibiting proteasome activity, and subsequently repressing the degradation of UPS downstream targets, such as cell cycle inhibitors or tumor suppressor proteins10–13. To date, these proteasome inhibitors had been successfully used in treating multiple myeloma and lymphadenoma14,15 and solid tumors, including lung cancer, epithelial ovarian cancer, and CRC16–19. However, the complex regulatory effects and mechanisms of proteasome and its role in CRC need to be further explored.

The partner of the NOB1 homolog (PNO1), also known as DIM2 and RRP20, was recognized as an RNA-binding protein involved in the cleavage of ribosomal 18 S rRNA in Saccharomyces cerevisiae20. Ongoing research had progressively clarified the function of PNO1 in ribosome biogenesis21–23 and assembly of the 26 S proteasome in Saccharomyces cerevisiae24. Additionally, our previous study found that PNO1 also partcipated in the cleavage of ribosomal 18 S rRNA and protein synthesis in CRC cells25. Subsequently, PNO1 had also been identifed to associated with progression of several cancers in various cancers, such as breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, glioma, esophageal cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, and bladder cancer26–31. However, the role of PNO1 in regulating 26 S proteasome assembly and its up- and down-stream need to be further addressed in CRC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Cell lines were purchased from Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Colorectal cancer HCT116 and SW480 cells, and Human embryonic kidney HEK-293T cell lines were maintained in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Keygen Biotech; Najing, Jiangsu, China), L-15 medium (Keygen Biotech), and DMEM high glucose medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA), respectively, all of which were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Hyclone; Logan, UT, USA).

Lentiviral transduction and miRNA/siRNA mimics transfection

To minimizes the off-target effect, three different shRNA sequences targeting PNO1 were designed and validated in our previous study. Since all three shRNA sequences targeting PNO1 demonstrated similar effects25, one of Lentivirus encoding shRNA against PNO1 named sh-PNO1 (sh-PNO1-1) was chosen for our study. The sh-PNO1 and the shRNAs-lentiviral vectors sh-Ctrl (GL427NC2) were purchased from OBiO Technology Corp., Ltd (Shanghai, China)25and used in our study following the instruction of manufacturer. After 12 h of infection, the culture medium was refreshed. After 72 h of continuous culture, cells were observed using a fluorescence microscope (DMI4000B, Leica; Wetzlar, Germany) and then harvested for cell function assays.

For miRNA mimics/miR-326 inhibitor transfection, 50 nM miRNA mimics/200 nM miR-326 inhibitor (anti-miR-326) or its negative control (NC)/ anti- negative control (anti-NC) (Guangzhou RiboBio Co., LTD; Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) were transfected into cells for 48 h using lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the provided instructions.

For co-transfection of siRNA targeting CDKN1B (p27) and sh-PNO1, cells were transfected with 50 nM ON-TARGET plus Human CDKN1B (1027) siRNA (si-p27) (Dharmacon; Lafayette, CO, USA) and its negative control (NC-siRNA) for 6 h, and then transducted with lentiviruses encoding sh-Ctrl and sh-PNO1 lentiviral vectors for additional 12 h. At the end of transfection and transduction, the cells were cultured with fresh medium for 72 h.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

RNA was extracted with TRIzol following the manufacturer’s instructions (Takara; Dalian, Liaoning, China). Reverse transcription PCR assay was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser or Mir-X™ miRNA First Strand Synthesis kit (Takara). The qPCR assay was performed using either SYBR™ Select Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara) following their instructions. Primers for qPCR were listed in Supplemental data Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology; Shanghai, China), which contained a protease inhibitors cocktail (MedChemExpress; South Brunswick, NJ, UK). 30–50 µg of protein extracts were separated on SDS-PAGE (Solarbio; Beijing, China) or native PAGE (Sangon; Shanghai, China), and then transferred to PVDF membranes (0.22 μm) (Millipore; Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked in TBS with 5% milk powder and probed with primary antibodies against PNO1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Santa Cruz, CA, USA), PMSA1 (Signalway Antibody; College Park, MD, USA), p27 (Proteintech; Wuhan, Hubei, China), ubiquitin and β-actin (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) or GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology; Danvers, MA, USA), and followed by second anti-mouse or anti-rabbit HRP-linked antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology). The bands were detected with either BeyoECL Plus (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) or Super ECL star-super sensitive chemiluminescence detection kit (US Everbright Inc.; Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). The intensities of the bands were analyzed using ImageJ.

CCK8 assays

Following of lentiviral transduction or co-transfection of si-p27 with sh-PNO1, the cells were collected and seeded into 96-well plates a density of 104 cells/well, and the cell viability was determined using CCK8 assays in 24 h later (Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd; Wuhan, Hubei, China). The cell viability of miRNA candidates was detected during the transfection of miRNA mimics in 3 days. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell viability was expressed as fold change of relative cell viability to 24 h in NC group.

Colony formation

Following of lentiviral transduction or siRNA/miRNA mimics transfection, the cells were collected and seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 500 cells/well. After 10–14 d of incubation, the colonies were fixed with 500 µl/well of 4% polyoxymethylene (Solarbio) and stained with 1% crystal violet staining solution (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology). Subsequently, the number of colony formation were counted and compared with the control groups for analysis.

Proteasome activity detection

The tranducted/transfected HCT116 cells were resuspended and reseeded into 96-well plates at a density of 0.5 × 104 cells/well. After 24 h of cultivation, the chymotrypsin-like activity, the caspase-like activity and the trypsin-like activity were measured respectively using the Proteasome-Glo™ 3-Substrate Cell-Based Assay System (Promega; Madison, WI, USA). A blank medium of equal volume was used for blank correction.

Luciferase assay

Sequences for vectors used in the luciferase assay were listed in Supplemental data Table S2. Sequences were constructed into the pMIR-Report Luciferase vectors with SpeI and Hind III by OBiO Technology Corp., Ltd. Total 50 nM miRNA mimics/negative control mimics (NC), 400 ng luciferase vectors of PNO1/control vector (C), and 40 ng Prl Sv40 Renilla Luciferase Reporter vector were co-transfected into HEK-293T cells using lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 48 h. Subsequently, the firefly and renilla luciferase activities were measured and calculated following the protocol of the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega).

miRNA prediction and clinical data analysis

The miRNA expression data in COAD were retrieved from the TCGA database using SangerBox (http://www.sangerbox.com/tool) on April 16, 2019. Differences with |log2fold change|≥1 and the P value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The miRNA candidates for PNO1 were predicted using the miRwalk database (mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de). Subsequently, the common miRNA candidates were identified using the Bioinformatics & Evolutionary Genomics tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). The expression levels of PNO1 and miRNAs in COAD tissue samples were obtained from the UALCAN database32. TCGA and UALCAN database which belong to public databases. The patients involved in the database have obtained ethical approval.

Statistics analysis.

Data was presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The statistical significance of differences was evaluated using SPSS 16.0 software (IBM Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Data with equal variance was analyzed using independent samples T-test or one-way ANOVA analysis, while data without equal variance was analyzed using Tamhane’s T2 or a nonparametric test. P < 0.05 was considered that the differences were statistically significant.

Results

PNO1 knockdown inhibited CRC cell growth invitro

Consistent with our previous study25, PNO1 knockdown in HCT116 and SW480 cells significantly reduced PNO1 expression on both protein (Fig. 1a) and mRNA (Fig. 1b) levels, as well as reduced the cell confluency, cell viability (Fig. 1c) and numbers of colony formation(Fig. 1d), indicating that PNO1 knockdown inhibited CRC cell growth in vitro.

Down-regulation of PNO1 suppressed CRC cell growth. HCT116 and SW480 cells were transduced with PNO1 lentivirus shRNA. (a) PNO1 protein level was detected by Western blot. (b) PNO1 mRNA level was quantitated by qPCR. (c) Cell imaging was taken using a fluorescence microscope, cell viability was measured by CCK8 assays and normalized with sh-Ctrl group. (d) Colony formation ability of cells was evaluated, quantitated and normalized with sh-Ctrl group. ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.05 vs.sh-Ctrl.

PNO1 knockdown inhibited proteasome maturation

Given that PNO1 is involved in the assembly of the 26 S proteasome in Saccharomyces cerevisiae24, its role in 26 S proteasome assembly in CRC cells was investigated. Proteasome activity analysis indicated that the chymotrypsin-like activity, the trypsin-like activity, and the caspase-like activity of proteasome were significantly reduced in HCT116 cells with PNO1 knockdown (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, PNO1 knockdown reduced 26 S and 20 S proteasome protein levels in HCT116 cells (Fig. 2b). These results suggested that PNO1 knockdown impaired 26 S and 20 S proteasome assembly and their activities in CRC cells.

(b) Western blot analysis of proteasome assembly was conducted by native PAGE and PMSA1 antibody; β-actin serves as a loading control. (c) Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate the expression of p27 in HCT-116 cells with PNO1 knockdown. (d) p27 mRNA level in HCT116 cells with PNO1 knockdown was quantitated by qPCR. Since PNO1 knockdown induced cell cycle arrest in our previous study25, which suggested that the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor CDKN1B/p27Kip1 (p27) might accumulated undergo for proteasome assembly inhibition33,34. Determination of p27 expression revealed that PNO1 knockdown significantly up-regulated the expression of p27 on protein level without accompanied by significant increase in its mRNA level (Fig. 2c and d). Instead, the decrease of p27 protein level in cycloheximide (CHX)-treated PNO1 knockdown cells was significantly less than that in cycloheximide (CHX)-treated sh-Ctrl group (Fig. 2e), which revealing that PNO1 knockdown further enhanced p27 stability. These results indicated that suppression of proteasome assembly and decrease p27 degradation was one of the underlying mechanisms of PNO1 knockdown on inhibition of cell growth and cell cycle arrest.

PNO1 knockdown inhibited cell growth partially dependent on p27

To further explore whether PNO1 knockdown suppressed cell growth dependent on p27, we observed cell morphology, cell viability and cell colony formation in HCT116 cells after knockdown of p27 or/and PNO1. The results indicated that the cell confluency, cell viability and the numbers of colony formation were increase after p27 knockdown, while they were decrease after PNO1 knockdown cells, compared with each control group (Fig. 3a-c). Interestingly, p27 knockdown attenuated the inhibitory effects of PNO1 knockdown on cell confluency and cell viability (Fig. 3b). However, the significant decrease in the numbers of colonies after PNO1 knockdown was not attenuated after p27 knockdown (Fig. 3c). The above studies suggested that the suppression of cell growth after PNO1 knockdown was partially attenuated by p27 inhibition.

miR-326 over-expression suppressed cell growth by directly targeting PNO1

To investigate the upstream molecular mechanism of PNO1, potential miRNAs targeting PNO1 were predicted. A total of 1,423 miRNA candidates were predicted to target PNO1 by miRwalk (Supplemental data Table S3), among of which 81 miRNAs expression were significantly down-regulated in CRC based on TCGA database (Supplemental data Table S4). Subsequently, 5 putative miRNA candidates targeting PNO1 (hsa-miR-326-5p, hsa-miR-375-5p, hsa-miR-3917-5p, hsa-miR-4420-5p, and hsa-miR-7706-5p) were identified as the common miRNA candidates in the above two analyses (Fig. 4a; Table 1). The levels of these five miRNA candidates and PNO1 in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) tissues and normal tissues were further analyzed using the UALCAN database32. The results showed that PNO1 was significantly up-regulated (Fig. 4b) in COAD tissue samples compared to adjacent non-cancerous colorectal tissues, while the expression levels of miR-326, miR-375, miR-3917 and miR-7706 were significantly down-regulated (Fig. 4c-g).

(h-l) Cell viability of miR-326, miR-375, miR-3917, miR-4420 and miR-7706 in HCT116 cells were detected by CCK8 assays, and all data were normalized to the cell viability of day 1 in NC group. ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.05 vs. NC.

To further validate the function of the five putative miRNA candidates, the cell viability of HCT116 cells after transfection of these miRNA mimics was assessed. The results indicated that overexpression of miR-326 and miR-7706 significantly reduced the cell viability of HCT116 cells, while other three miRNA mimics did not show the similar inhibition on cell viability (Fig. 4h-l, Table S5). Since miR-326 was supposed to be a tumor suppressor in various tumors35,36, it was selected for further investigation. Due to miR-326 was predicted to target PNO1 by binding coding sequence (CDS) region of PNO1, two putative sites with a higher minimum free energy (mfe) in CDS region were selected (Fig. 5a). Overexpression of miR-326 significantly reduced the luciferase activity in PNO1-WT and PNO1-MUT2 reporter but not that in PNO1-MUT1 reporter, which indicated that the mutation at the site 1 blocked the reduction of luciferase activity by miR-326 (Fig. 5b). The result revealed that site 1 (188–210 nt in the PNO1 CDS region) is the binding site for miR-326 in PNO1 CDS region. Furthermore, overexpression of miR-326 significantly decreased PNO1 protein expression without altering its mRNA expression in both HCT116 and SW480 cells (Fig. 5c-f), while its overexpression also obviously reduced the levels of cell confluency (Fig. 5g) and the numbers of colony formation (Fig. 5h). In contrast, downregulation of miR-326 inhibitor significantly increased PNO1 protein expression, while its mRNA level was also not altering in SW480 cells consistent with its expression in miR-326 overexpression (Fig. S1a-b). Meanwhile, downregulation of miR-326 also increased the cell viability of SW480 cells (Fig. S1c). These results suggested that miR-326 suppressed CRC cell growth and down-regulated PNO1 protein levels, and by directly targeting PNO1 on the CDS region without altering its mRNA level might be one of underlying mechanisms.

miR-326 inhibited the activity and assembly of the proteasome

Given the essential role of PNO1 on 26 S and 20 S proteasome assembly, whether miR-326 was involved in 26 S and 20 S proteasome assembly was further investigated. Overexpression of miR-326 in HCT116 cells significantly reduced both chymotrypsin-like and caspase-like activities of proteasome (Fig. 6a) and protein levels of both 26 S and 20 S proteasomes (Fig. 6b), indicating that miR-326 overexpression significantly impaired the proteasome activity and assembly. Since overexpression of miR-326 also could induce cell cycle arrest37, further determination of p27 expression revealed that miR-326 overexpression significantly up-regulated the protein expression of p27, but didn’t affect its mRNA levels (Fig. 6c and d). Additionally, the CHX assay revealed that the decrease of p27 protein by miR-326 overexpression with CHX treatment was significantly less than that in NC group treatment with CHX (Fig. 6e), which revealing that miR-326 overexpression further enhanced p27 stability. These results indicated that miR-326 suppressed cell growth might be resulted from its inhibition of proteasome activity and the increase of p27 protein stability.

Discussion

Currently, CRC treatment primarily included endoscopic and surgical local resection, reduced preoperative radio therapy, systemic treatment, local and metastatic surgery, local ablation of metastatic diseases, palliative chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Over past 20 years, dual chemotherapy based on fluoropyrimidine, had remained the principal approach for treating metastasis38. Although these new treatment regimens could double the overall survival rate of CRC, CRC was still one of the major causes of cancer-related incidence and mortality worldwide39. Personalized treatment methods tailored to the molecular and pathological characteristics of tumors had emerged in recent years40. Regulation of the molecules in UPS system to suppress the proteasome activity and the function of proteasomal degradation was one of the strategies to treat cancers41,42. As we known, proteasomal degradation includes not only the ubiquitin-dependent 26 S proteasome degradation pathway, but also the ubiquitin-independent 20 S proteasome degradation pathway43. Theoretically, inhibition of the ubiquitin-dependent 26 S proteasome assembly would induce the accumulation of poly-ubiquitinated proteins59,61. Our study demonstrated that PNO1 knockdown inhibited the 20 S and 26 S proteasome assembly and decrease p27 degradation. Interesting, PNO1 knockdown decreased the level of overall poly-ubiquitinated proteins (Figure S2), which was not consistent with the effect of ubiquitin-dependent 26 S proteasomal degradation59,61. Considering PNO1 knockdown also decreased global protein synthesis, as demonstrated in our previous study25, we supposed that PNO1 knockdown could also decrease the synthesis of ubiquitin protein which resulted in the decrease of overall poly-ubiquitinated proteins expression. Meanwhile, these evidences also suggested that the decrease of p27 degradation might be a result for the combined action of 20 S and 26 S proteasome assembly inhibition. However, its underly mechanism need to be elucidate by further research. Furthermore, our study uncovered the role of miR-326 as an endogenous proteasome inhibitor, which targeted PNO1 and suppressed the degradation of p27 protein by inhibiting proteasome assembly (Fig. 7). These results might provide more possibilities for their clinical application.

Schematic model of cancer cell proliferation inhibition through the miR-326-PNO1-proteasome assembly axis.

Our previous study first discovered that PNO1 was closely related to the cancer progression in CRC25 which provided a promising drug target for selective treatment44. In bladder cancer, PNO1 was involved in cell growth and apoptosis, and its high expression is significantly related to the poor prognosis of patients30. In the hepatocellular carcinoma cells, it regulated autophagy and apoptosis via MAPK pathway26,45, and regulated the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway to participate in cell migartion46. It promoted the development of lung adenocarcinoma through the Notch signaling pathway29,47. PNO1 was also involved in the progression of esophageal cancer and osteosarcoma cells, and its knockdown could inhibit cell growth, migration, and invasion, and induce apoptosis of cancer cells28,48. At the same time, upregulation of PNO1 could also activate THBS1/FAK/ Akt signaling and promote the glioma tumorigenesis27. PNO1 was identified to promote tumorigenesis in breast cancer regulated by E2F649. Our study provided additional evidence to that PNO1 is also involved in the regulation of proteasome assembly in CRC. Meanwhile, since our previous studies found that overexpression of PNO1 led to increased colorectal cancer cell growth and decreased the percentage of cells in G0/G1 phase25,44, we also examined the effect of PNO1 overexpression on proteasome assembly. The results showed no significant difference in 26 S and 20 S proteasome assembly between PNO1 overexpression and control group (data no shown), consistent with previous findings50. Based on these findings, we speculate that proteasome assembly may depend on PNO1 expression within a specific range. However, this mechanism requires further investigation in future study.

Current research indicated that inhibiting the activity or assembly level of proteasome would hinder tumor progression, primarily by activating the cell stress response, such as proteotoxic stress or endoplasmic reticulum stress, activating the inflammatory pathway, DNA repair system or apoptosis, and promoting the degradation of tumor suppressor genes, especially oncoproteins, and blocking the cycle regulation of cell replication11,13,51–58. Numerous targets including p53, p21 and p27 were studied in the stress responses for proteasome inhibition59–61. Among these targets, p27 (p27kip1) which was a cell cycle dependent protein kinase inhibitor (CKI), which regulated the cell cycle and promoted the cellular senescence60,62,63. A previous study had demonstrated that miR-101 could reduce the 26 S proteasome assembly level, diminish proteasome activity, and inhibit the p27 degradation by inhibiting the proteasome assembly factor POMP in CRC61. Additionally, p27 was found to be involved in the assembly process of proteasome RPT4 and RPT5 subunits64. Our previous study found that PNO1 knockdown upregulated the p21 and p53 expression25. Similarly, our results suggested that inhibition of the degradation of CKIs such as p27 by negatively regulating proteasome activity was a potential mechanism for PNO1 knockdown or miR-326 to prevent tumors. Meanwhile, we observed that p27 knockdown attenuated the inhibitory effects of PNO1 knockdown on cell confluency and viability. However, the significant reduction in colony numbers after PNO1 knockdown was not alleviated by p27 knockdown (Fig. 3b and c). We speculated that since the colony formation assay was performed over 10–14 days, and as is known, p27 is not the only downstream target of PNO1, several other proteins, such as p21, p53 and components of the FAK/Akt pathway, have been reported as downstream targets of PNO125,27, which might also play roles during this extended period. Based on these evidences, we could only conclude that PNO1 knockdown inhibit cell growth partially dependent on p27.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) constituted a class of conserved small noncoding RNAs, approximately 22 nucleotides in length. They typically existed their regulatory effects on targets by pairing with the 3’UTRs in the mRNAs65. Some studies had also shown that certain miRNAs could regulate targets by binding to the 5’UTR or the coding sequence of mRNAs66. Their different binding region might be associated with different regulating characteristics on their target mRNA destabilization/sequestration and/or translation repression67,68. For example, miR-10a could bind the 5’UTR of ribosomal protein mRNAs to enhance their translation69. miR-15a/-16 were reported to regulate the CDS region of Tie2, and significantly inhibited only the Tie2 protein level, without affecting its mRNA level70. Similarly, our results also showed that miR-326 binding to the CDS region of PNO1 with downregulating of the protein level of PNO1 without changing the levels of mRNA (Fig. 4c-e). These results suggested that down-regulation of PNO1 protein level might resulted from miR-326 regulating PNO1 translation repression.

In a variety of tumors, abnormal expression of miRNAs plays an important role in the occurrence and development of tumors68. Studies also have found that miRNA like miR-142-3p, miR-451a, miR-214, miR-148b, and miR-101 suppress tumor occurrence or development by inhibiting proteasome activity with regulating proteasome subunits or assembly factors61,71–74. To date, only miR-340-5p has been identified to inhibit the development of lung adenocarcinoma by regulating PNO1 29. However, no study has screened for the miRNAs targeting PNO1 in CRC. We believe that our work will enhance the the understanding of the molecular mechanism of miR-326 and PNO1 in CRC with the regulation of proteasome activity, providing a theoretical and experimental foundation for its clinical application.

MiR-326 is considered as a potential cancer biomarker, exhibiting low expression status in a variety of tumors, such as non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, cervical cancer, osteosarcoma, glioma, prostate cancer, esophageal cancer, colorectal cancer, and other cancers36. Many studies clarified that low expression of miR-326 is closely related to poor prognosis, tumor development, and metastasis36–78. Some studies also found that miR-326 could inhibit migration, invasion, cell proliferation, and apoptosis of tumor cells by regulating multiple signal pathways, including Hedgehog/Gli (Hh/Gli), PI3K/Akt, and RAS/ERK pathways36,77,79. It could reverse drug resistance by decreasing the expression levels of ABCC1 and Bcl-xL80. In addition, it also inhibited tumor development by participating in some circRNA and lncRNA pathways, such as the regulation of fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) by circ_0000518 or activation by lncRNA PCAT6 to regulate leucine repeat enriched eight family member e (LRRC8E)75,76. In CRC, miR-326 is suggested as a biomarker for CRC as it significantly correlated with tumor location, tumor stage, tumor progression, and patient survival36. MiR-326 could be regulated by HOTAIR and inhibited the development of CRC via FUT6-PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway77. It inhibited the CRC progression by suppressing the transcription factor E2F1, participating in the regulation of LASP1 oncogene by circRNA 000166 79,80. Furthermore, it also down-regulated the level of NOB1 in CRC, osteosarcoma, glioma, and gastric cancer cells37,81–83. Since NOB1 and its partner of PNO1 were involved in the assembly of the proteasome and the processing of ribosomal 18 S in Saccharomyces cerevisiae24. We also examined the NOB1 level after overexpression of miR-326 in HCT116 cells; the results showed that overexpression of miR-326 also decreased the expression level of NOB1 in HCT116 cells (data not shown), which was consistent with previous studies37. As our previous study demonstrated that downregulation of PNO1 would inhibit protein synthesis in CRC25, we speculated that overexpression of miR-326 affected the process of protein degradation and protein synthesis by simultaneously regulating NOB1 and PNO1 in CRC. We believed that the regulation of miR-326-NOB1/PNO1 pathway might emerge as an important pathway for antitumor research; of course, this hypothesis needs to be proved through future experiments.

Moreover, just as miR-326 could target multiple targets in its action of anti-cancers, it might also pair to PNO1 CDS region with multiple binding sites. Using restoring experiments with the mutated protein overexpression (not targeting by siRNA or miRNA) could not only minimize the off-target effect of small RNA, but also provide more information for the interaction between small RNA and its target protein84. Our finding revealed that 188–210 nt in the PNO1 CDS region is the binding site for miR-326 in PNO1 CDS region. However, whether other sites in the PNO1 CDS region could also be the binding sites for miR-326? Further exploration would provide a more comprehensive understand to the role of PNO1 and miR-326 in cancer cell growth, specifically using the overexpression of mutPNO1 (not targeting by sh-PNO1 or miR-326).

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that miR-326 as an endogenous proteasome inhibitor suppressed the CRC cell growth via targeting PNO1 and suppressing proteasome assembly, resulting from the inhibition of proteasome activity and increasing the stability of p27, and unveiled a novel therapeutic intervention strategy (Fig. 7).
