National trends in mortality attributable to arrhythmias among adults with obesity in the United States, 1999–2020
Authors: Ibrahim Nagmeldin Hassan, Siddig Yaqub, Muhsin Ibrahim, Nagmeldin Abuassa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity and arrhythmias are escalating public health concerns in the United States. While both have been independently associated with cardiovascular mortality, national trends in obesity-related arrhythmia deaths remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe conducted a population-based cross-sectional study using CDC WONDER mortality data from 1999 to 2020. Adults aged ≥25 years with arrhythmias listed as the underlying cause of death (ICD-10: I47–I49) and obesity as a contributing cause (E66) were included. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were calculated per 100,000 population. Joinpoint regression was used to assess trends and calculate annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC). Analyses were stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, census region, urbanization level, age group, and state.ResultsA total of 67,844 deaths were attributed to obesity-related arrhythmias. The overall AAMR was 1.35 (95 % CI: 1.11–1.60), with a significant AAPC of 6.19 % (95 % CI: 5.68–6.69; p < 0.0001). Males had higher AAMRs than females (1.57 vs. 1.15), with a steeper rise over time. American Indian/Alaska Native (1.61) and Black (1.55) populations experienced the highest mortality rates. Non-metro areas (1.82) and the Midwest (1.63) had the greatest regional burdens. Mortality increased steadily with age, peaking at 4.01 among those aged 75–84 years. State-level AAMRs ranged from 0.77 to 3.29. Most deaths occurred in hospitals or at home.ConclusionObesity-associated arrhythmia mortality has risen with marked heterogeneity; these findings underscore the need for prevention across the life-course and equitable access to rhythm evaluation, evidence-based cardiometabolic care, and targeted screening.Keywords: Arrhythmia, Obesity, Mortality trends, CDC WONDER, Joinpoint regression, Age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR)

Introduction

Arrhythmias represent a growing cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in the United States, with atrial fibrillation (AF) alone affecting over 6 million adults and accounting for a substantial share of stroke and heart failure burden [1]. Parallel to this rise, obesity has become a defining health crisis of the 21st century, now affecting more than four in ten U.S. adults [2]. The intersection of these two epidemics—arrhythmias and obesity—has created a complex and understudied domain of cardiovascular risk.

Obesity has been shown to significantly increase the risk of developing arrhythmias through diverse pathophysiological mechanisms, including atrial structural remodeling, epicardial fat infiltration, inflammation, and autonomic imbalance [3,4]. Additionally, obesity-associated conditions such as hypertension, insulin resistance, and sleep apnea act synergistically to promote arrhythmic susceptibility [5]. This interaction is particularly relevant in the context of atrial fibrillation, ventricular ectopy, and sudden cardiac death, all of which carry substantial mortality risk.

Despite increasing clinical awareness, few studies have quantified how obesity impacts national arrhythmia-related mortality patterns over time. While prior work has highlighted rising AF-related deaths among older adults and racial minorities [6], it remains unclear how obesity modifies this trend at the population level. Notably, the so-called “obesity paradox”—suggesting better survival among obese individuals with certain cardiovascular diseases—further complicates risk interpretation [7,8]. Reports of an “obesity paradox” predominantly arises from heart failure and coronary disease cohorts rather than arrhythmia-specific mortality and may reflect confounding or index-event bias; we therefore interpret BMI-related differences with caution in this context. Whether this paradox extends to arrhythmia-related mortality, and how it manifests across sex, race, region, and urbanization levels, remains largely unexamined.

A robust understanding of these trends is essential to informing future public health strategies. In a time when both arrhythmia incidence and obesity prevalence are increasing, clarifying their combined impact on mortality is not only timely but necessary.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of national mortality data using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) platform [9]. This publicly accessible resource compiles death certificate information for all U.S. residents. Our analysis covered the period from 1999 to 2020 and focused on deaths among adults aged 25 years and older in which arrhythmias were listed as the underlying cause and obesity as a contributing cause.

Deaths were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes [10]. Arrhythmia-related mortality was defined using underlying cause of death codes I47 (paroxysmal tachycardia), I48 (atrial fibrillation and flutter), and I49 (other cardiac arrhythmias). Obesity was defined using contributing cause code E66 (obesity). Only cases with an arrhythmia code as the underlying cause and an accompanying obesity code as a contributing cause were included (Supplementary Table S1).

Institutional Review Board approval was not required, as all data were de-identified and publicly available. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies [11] (Supplementary Table S2).

Data extraction

Mortality data were extracted for the overall U.S. adult population and stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, ten-year age groups (from 25–34 to ≥85 years), U.S. Census region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West), metropolitan status (metro vs. non-metro), state of residence, and place of death.

Race and ethnicity classifications were based on funeral director-reported data, typically provided by next of kin. Metropolitan and non-metropolitan designations followed the 2013 National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Urban–Rural Classification Scheme, with metro counties defined as having populations ≥50,000 and non-metro counties as <50,000 [12].

Statistical analysis

Annual death counts and population estimates were extracted directly from CDC WONDER. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were calculated using the direct method and standardized to the 2000 U.S. standard population. AAMRs were expressed per 100,000 population, with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) estimated under Poisson distribution assumptions.

Temporal trends in mortality were evaluated using Joinpoint regression analysis. Log-linear models were used to estimate the Annual Percentage Change (APC) for each trend segment and the Average Annual Percentage Change (AAPC) for the overall study period. We constrained models to a minimum segment length of ≥3 years and selected the number of joinpoints via permutation testing with Monte Carlo resampling (α = 0.05), prioritizing parsimony (0–2 joinpoints). Apparent changes <3 years were treated as unstable and not interpreted as distinct segments.

Trend analyses were conducted using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2) from the National Cancer Institute [13]. Models began with a single linear segment and added joinpoints where warranted by permutation testing [14]. Stratified models were constructed for subgroups defined by sex, race/ethnicity, age group, region, and urbanization level. APC and 95 % CIs were reported for each segment.

Comparative analyses were conducted to evaluate differences in AAMRs and trend trajectories across subgroups. Tests of parallelism assessed whether time trends differed significantly between demographic or geographic groups. All supplementary analyses and figure construction were conducted in Python (v3.10), using the statsmodels and seaborn libraries.

Results

Overall national trends

Between 1999 and 2020, there were 67,844 deaths attributed to obesity-related arrhythmias in the United States. The overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) was 1.35 (95 % CI: 1.11 to 1.60) deaths per 100,000 population (Table 1). During this period, mortality rates rose steadily and significantly, with both the annual percent change (APC) and the average annual percent change (AAPC) estimated at 6.19 % (95 % CI: 5.68 to 6.69; p < 0.0001), indicating a persistent nationwide increase (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Trends in obesity-related arrhythmia mortality stratified by sex and overall, United States, 1999–2020.

Trends by gender

Between 1999 and 2020, a total of 67,844 obesity-related arrhythmia deaths were reported in the United States, with males accounting for 54.5 % and females for 45.5 %. The average age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) was higher in males at 1.57 (95 % CI: 1.24 to 1.89) deaths per 100,000, compared to 1.15 (95 % CI: 0.95 to 1.34) in females (Table 1).

In females, mortality remained stable in 1999–2000, then increased consistently through 2020, with an average annual percent change (AAPC) of 5.52 % (95 % CI: 5.00 to 6.05; p < 0.0001). A brief decline was noted in males during 1999–2000, but this short segment was excluded. From 2001 to 2020, male mortality rose steadily, with an AAPC of 6.76 % (95 % CI: 6.23 to 7.30; p < 0.0001), indicating a sharper increase compared to females (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Trends by race/ethnicity

From 1999 to 2020, the highest average age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) for obesity-related arrhythmia deaths was observed among American Indian or Alaska Native individuals at 1.61 (95 % CI: 1.36 to 1.85) deaths per 100,000, followed by Black individuals (1.55, 95 % CI: 1.32 to 1.79), White individuals (1.38, 95 % CI: 1.12 to 1.64), Hispanic individuals (1.04, 95 % CI: 0.85 to 1.24), and Asian or Pacific Islander individuals (0.33, 95 % CI: 0.27 to 0.39) (Table 1).

Among White individuals, mortality rose steadily throughout the study period. From 1999 to 2016, the annual percent change (APC) was 6.02 % (95 % CI: 5.74 to 6.31; p < 0.0001), with a further increase from 2017 to 2020. These segments were combined for clarity, yielding an overall average annual percent change (AAPC) of 6.57 % (95 % CI: 6.13 to 7.01; p < 0.0001). In Black individuals, a sharp initial decline occurred from 1999 to 2000, but due to its brevity, it was excluded. From 2001 to 2020, mortality increased significantly, with an APC of 3.48 % (95 % CI: 2.81 to 4.16; p < 0.0001) and an AAPC of 4.37 % (95 % CI: 3.46 to 5.28; p < 0.0001). Among Asian or Pacific Islander individuals, mortality remained stable from 2007 to 2010 (APC: −4.29 %, 95 % CI: −20.71 to 15.54; p = 0.6930), followed by a nonsignificant rise from 2011 to 2020 (APC: 6.46 %, 95 % CI: 0.82 to 12.43; p = 0.0543). The AAPC for the full period was 6.46 % (95 % CI: 0.82 to 12.43). For American Indian or Alaska Native individuals, mortality increased consistently from 2006 to 2020, with an APC and AAPC of 3.80 % (95 % CI: 1.30 to 6.37; p = 0.0102). Hispanic individuals experienced a significant increase in mortality from 1999 to 2018 (APC: 5.92 %, 95 % CI: 3.60 to 8.29; p < 0.0001). Although there was a spike from 2019 to 2020 (APC: 72.77 %, 95 % CI: −38.81 to 387.80; p = 0.4104), it was statistically nonsignificant. The overall AAPC was 6.41 % (95 % CI: 4.35 to 8.50; p < 0.0001) (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Trends by urbanization status

From 1999 to 2020, the average age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) for obesity-related arrhythmia deaths was higher in non-metro areas—1.82 (95 % CI: 1.48 to 2.15) deaths per 100,000 population—compared to 1.26 (95 % CI: 1.03 to 1.49) in metro areas (Table 1).

In metro areas, mortality rose steadily over the study period. An initial APC of 5.49 % (95 % CI: 5.20 to 5.78; p < 0.0001) was observed, followed by an accelerated increase after 2018. These intervals were merged for clarity, yielding an overall average annual percent change (AAPC) of 6.12 % (95 % CI: 5.60 to 6.64; p < 0.0001). In non-metro areas, a modest early increase was followed by a consistent upward trend. From 2002 to 2020, the APC was 6.01 % (95 % CI: 5.63 to 6.40; p < 0.0001). A sharper rise in later years was integrated into the overall AAPC, estimated at 6.64 % (95 % CI: 6.18 to 7.10; p < 0.0001) (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Trends in obesity-related arrhythmia mortality stratified by urbanization level (metro vs non-metro), United States, 1999–2020.

Trends by census region

From 1999 to 2020, the Midwest had the highest average age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) for obesity-related arrhythmia deaths at 1.63 (95 % CI: 1.32 to 1.94) deaths per 100,000 population, followed by the West (1.48, 95 % CI: 1.23 to 1.73), South (1.20, 95 % CI: 0.98 to 1.42), and Northeast (1.17, 95 % CI: 0.96 to 1.39) (Table 1).

In the Northeast, mortality declined briefly between 1999 and 2000 but rose significantly from 2001 onward. The overall average annual percent change (AAPC) was 6.08 % (95 % CI: 5.44 to 6.71; p < 0.0001). The Midwest showed a similar pattern, with an initial dip followed by a sustained increase from 2001 to 2020. Its AAPC was the highest across all regions at 6.52 % (95 % CI: 5.97 to 7.08; p < 0.0001). In the South, a brief early decline was followed by a steady rise in mortality. From 2001 to 2020, the AAPC was 5.89 % (95 % CI: 5.18 to 6.61; p < 0.0001). The West experienced a consistent increase over the entire period. Although multiple segments were identified, they were merged due to overlap. The overall AAPC was 6.21 % (95 % CI: 5.79 to 6.63; p < 0.0001) (Table 2; Fig. 4).

Trends in obesity-related arrhythmia mortality stratified by U.S. Census region, United States, 1999–2020.

Trends by state

From 1999 to 2020, age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) for obesity-related arrhythmia deaths varied significantly across U.S. states. The highest AAMRs were recorded in Vermont (3.29, 95 % CI: 2.95–3.64), Iowa (2.54, 95 % CI: 2.40–2.69), and Wisconsin (2.49, 95 % CI: 2.39–2.60), highlighting a disproportionately high burden in parts of the Midwest and Northeast. In contrast, the lowest rates were observed in Connecticut (0.77, 95 % CI: 0.70–0.85), Alabama (0.80, 95 % CI: 0.73–0.86), and Virginia (0.84, 95 % CI: 0.79–0.89), reflecting notable geographic disparities (Fig. 5).

Geographic distribution of average obesity-related arrhythmia mortality rates by state, United States, 1999–2020.

Trends by ten-year age group

From 1999 to 2020, mortality from obesity-related arrhythmias increased consistently across all adult age groups in the United States, with a clear upward gradient by age. The average age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) rose from 0.18 (95 % CI: 0.17 to 0.20) deaths per 100,000 among those aged 25–34 years to 0.45 (95 % CI: 0.41 to 0.49) in the 35–44 group, and 1.05 (95 % CI: 0.93 to 1.18) in the 45–54 group. Rates further increased to 2.09 (95 % CI: 1.78 to 2.40) in those aged 55–64 years and 3.35 (95 % CI: 2.65 to 4.06) in the 65–74 group. The peak AAMR was seen in the 75–84 group at 4.01 (95 % CI: 2.96 to 5.07), while those aged 85 and older had a slightly lower rate of 3.19 (95 % CI: 2.33 to 4.06) (Table 1).

Mortality trends mirrored this age progression. The annual percent change (APC) increased from 2.24 % (95 % CI: 1.48 to 3.00; p < 0.0001) in the 25–34 group to 2.98 % (95 % CI: 2.41 to 3.56; p < 0.0001) in the 35–44 group. In older age groups, trends were steeper: APCs were 3.72 % (95 % CI: 3.08 to 4.37; p < 0.0001) in the 45–54 group, 5.05 % (95 % CI: 4.46 to 5.65; p < 0.0001) in the 55–64 group, and 7.30 % (95 % CI: 6.78 to 7.82; p < 0.0001) in the 65–74 group. The highest APC was in the 75–84 group at 9.58 % (95 % CI: 9.03 to 10.13; p < 0.0001), followed by 8.75 % (95 % CI: 7.62 to 9.90; p < 0.0001) in those aged 85 and older. These patterns highlight the growing burden of arrhythmia-related deaths with advancing age (Table 2; Fig. 6).

Crude mortality rates for obesity-related arrhythmia deaths stratified by ten-year age group and sex, United States, 1999–2020.

Trends by place of death

Among the 67,844 recorded deaths attributed to obesity-related arrhythmias from 1999 to 2020, the most common place of death was an inpatient medical facility, accounting for 23,095 deaths (34.04 %). A similar proportion died at home (22,412 deaths; 33.03 %), highlighting the substantial burden occurring outside healthcare institutions. Additionally, 11,103 deaths (16.37 %) occurred in outpatient or emergency department settings, and 975 (1.44 %) were classified as dead on arrival. A small number—29 deaths (0.04 %)—occurred in medical facilities with unknown status.

These patterns underscore the need for enhanced emergency response and preventive measures, particularly in non-hospital environments.

Discussion

Over the 21-year period from 1999 to 2020, our analysis identified 67,844 deaths attributed to obesity-related arrhythmias in the United States. The overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) was 1.35 per 100,000 population, with a sustained annual percent change (APC) of 6.19 % (95 % CI: 5.68 to 6.69; p < 0.0001), indicating a persistent upward trend in mortality. These findings align with broader national trends in arrhythmia-related mortality. A recent study showed that atrial fibrillation (AF)–related mortality increased from 1.9 in 1999 to 22.2 in 2020, with an average annual percent change (AAPC) of 11.4 % (95 % CI: 10.4 to 12.2) [15]. Similarly, the overall AAMR for arrhythmia-related deaths rose from 111.4 in 1999 to 137.3 in 2023 [16].

The increase in obesity-related arrhythmia deaths likely reflects the rising prevalence of obesity in the U.S., a known risk factor for atrial and ventricular arrhythmias as well as sudden cardiac death [17]. Obesity promotes structural and electrical remodeling of the heart, systemic inflammation, and autonomic dysfunction, all of which contribute to arrhythmogenesis [18]. Moreover, obesity-related heart disease mortality rose by 180 % from 1999 to 2020, disproportionately affecting middle-aged men, Black adults, and residents of Midwestern and non-metropolitan areas [19]. These disparities mirror those observed in our findings.

The rising burden of obesity-related arrhythmia mortality in the United States is driven by a multifaceted interplay of structural, electrical, inflammatory, and autonomic mechanisms. Obesity significantly alters cardiac structure and function, creating a proarrhythmic substrate that increases the risk of both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias. Cardiac remodeling is a central feature, characterized by left atrial enlargement, left ventricular hypertrophy, and myocardial fibrosis. These changes disrupt normal electrical conduction, particularly promoting atrial fibrillation (AF) [20]. Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), which expands in obesity, exerts paracrine and vasocrine effects on the myocardium, triggering inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis, thereby enhancing arrhythmogenic potential [21].

Inflammation plays a critical role, as adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). These mediators contribute to atrial remodeling and altered electrophysiology, correlating with larger atrial size and reduced conduction velocity [22,23]. Autonomic dysfunction further compounds risk. Obese individuals commonly exhibit heightened sympathetic tone and diminished parasympathetic activity, increasing heart rate variability and vulnerability to arrhythmias—particularly in the presence of structural heart disease [24]. Additionally, obesity is linked to metabolic disturbances like insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. These conditions lead to myocardial lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity, which impair ion channel function and intracellular calcium handling, further increasing arrhythmic susceptibility [25].

Sex-based differences in obesity-related arrhythmia mortality are multifactorial. Epidemiological data consistently show higher arrhythmia incidence and mortality in men, though this gap is narrowing as obesity becomes more prevalent in both sexes. Recent data indicate a rising arrhythmic burden in women. One study found that age-adjusted atrial fibrillation (AF) mortality attributable to obesity increased by 22 % in males (from 4.9 to 6.0 per 100,000) and by 26 % in females (from 3.5 to 4.4 per 100,000), highlighting a disproportionate rise among women [26]. Biological differences may partly explain these trends. Women typically have higher vagal tone and lower sympathetic activity, which may offer protection against arrhythmias. However, obesity attenuates this autonomic balance, increasing arrhythmia risk in females [27]. Hormonal factors, including the decline of estrogen during menopause, further heighten vulnerability to arrhythmias in older women due to estrogen's potential antiarrhythmic effects [28].

Sociocultural dynamics also influence disparities. Women often present with atypical symptoms, leading to underdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis of arrhythmias. They remain underrepresented in clinical trials, limiting sex-specific evidence to inform treatment [29]. Additionally, subclinical cardiac remodeling differs by sex. Obese women may experience more pronounced diastolic dysfunction, while men are more likely to develop left ventricular hypertrophy—variations that may influence arrhythmia risk and outcomes [30]. Although men currently exhibit higher obesity-related arrhythmia mortality, the rising obesity burden in women, combined with biological and systemic factors, may shift this pattern in the future.

Our analysis revealed pronounced racial and ethnic disparities in obesity-related arrhythmia mortality. From 1999 to 2020, American Indian or Alaska Native individuals had the highest average age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) at 1.61 per 100,000, followed by Black individuals at 1.55, and White individuals at 1.38. In contrast, Hispanic and Asian or Pacific Islander populations had lower AAMRs of 1.04 and 0.33, respectively.

These findings mirror national trends. One large study of 281,135 obesity-related cardiovascular deaths found the highest AAMR among Black adults (28.1 per 100,000) and the steepest increase among American Indian or Alaska Native individuals—a 415 % rise between 1999 and 2020 [31]. In comparison, the increase was 114 % for White adults and 74 % for Asian Americans [31]. Multiple factors may contribute to these disparities. Financial barriers to care are more common among Black and Hispanic adults—affecting 38 % and 33 %, respectively—compared to 18 % of White individuals [32]. These obstacles delay diagnosis and limit access to rhythm control strategies and advanced interventions like catheter ablation and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) [33]. Representation in clinical trials is also limited. Only 4–5 % of participants in major arrhythmia trials are Black, and less than 1 % are Native American, restricting the applicability of findings to these groups [34]. Higher obesity prevalence among racial minorities further compounds risk. Among U.S. adults aged ≥20 years, obesity rates are highest in Black women (57 %) and Hispanic men (45 %), compared to 41 % in White adults [35]. These upstream disparities likely contribute to downstream differences in arrhythmic mortality.

Geographic and urban–rural disparities significantly contribute to obesity-related arrhythmia mortality in the United States. Our analysis found that non-metropolitan areas had a higher average age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) of 1.82 per 100,000 compared to 1.26 in metropolitan areas—a sustained 44 % difference throughout the study period. This pattern reflects long-standing evidence of worse cardiovascular outcomes in rural regions. The Rural Health Information Hub reports elevated all-cause mortality, limited healthcare access, and higher rates of obesity and hypertension in rural communities compared to urban counterparts [36]. A persistent shortage of providers also exacerbates this gap; rural areas average 68 primary care physicians per 100,000 people versus 84 in urban areas [37].

Regional variation was also evident. The Midwest had the highest AAMR at 1.63 per 100,000, followed by the West (1.48), South (1.20), and Northeast (1.17). These findings are consistent with CDC data and past mortality mapping studies, which have long identified the Southern U.S.—the “Stroke Belt”—as a hotspot for cardiovascular mortality [38]. One comprehensive analysis of county-level deaths from 1980 to 2014 found persistently high mortality in parts of Mississippi, Alabama, and Louisiana [39]. Contributing factors include higher rates of smoking, physical inactivity, and poor diet in rural areas [40]. Structural barriers such as hospital closures and lower socioeconomic status further compound risk, creating an environment where arrhythmic mortality is more likely to thrive.

Age is a major determinant of obesity-related arrhythmia mortality. In our analysis, the highest age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were observed among adults aged 75–84 years (4.01 per 100,000), followed by those aged 65–74 (3.35). In contrast, younger adults aged 25–34 had an AAMR of only 0.18, with a gradual rise across age groups. This pattern reflects the known association between aging, structural heart disease, fibrosis, and electrical instability [41]. However, emerging evidence suggests that younger adults are experiencing a steeper relative increase in arrhythmia-related mortality. Early-life and intergenerational factors including prenatal and childhood obesity and exposure to obesogenic environments may shape cardiac structure and autonomic tone, potentially elevating arrhythmia susceptibility later in life. Life-course prevention may therefore be necessary to bend future mortality trends. A recent national study reported that while older adults still account for most deaths, the largest relative rise from 1999 to 2020 occurred among those aged 25–54 [42]. This may be due to earlier onset of obesity, diabetes, and sleep apnea in younger populations.

The “obesity paradox”—in which overweight or mildly obese patients with cardiovascular disease appear to have better outcomes—has been observed in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). Yet, a 2023 study showed that this effect was age-dependent: it was noted in individuals ≥65 years but not in younger patients, for whom obesity remained a mortality risk factor [43]. Timing of obesity onset also matters. A Swedish cohort of over 620,000 individuals found that becoming obese before age 30 was associated with a ≥75 % increased risk of early mortality, compared to those who became obese later or remained non-obese [44]. These findings underscore the long-term cardiovascular risks of early-life obesity exposure.

The growing burden of obesity-related arrhythmia mortality calls for coordinated public health and healthcare responses. Prevention should begin early, emphasizing physical activity, healthy eating, and weight control—especially in high-risk groups. School-based and community-level interventions have demonstrated success in shaping long-term behaviors and should be expanded nationally [45]. Equity-focused policies are essential to address the disproportionate impact on racial minorities and rural populations. These communities often face limited healthcare access, delayed diagnoses, and underuse of evidence-based treatments. Expanding insurance coverage, improving telemedicine infrastructure, and deploying mobile cardiovascular services may help close these gaps [46]. Within healthcare systems, multidisciplinary models integrating cardiologists, endocrinologists, primary care providers, and behavioral specialists are crucial. These teams support adherence, promote weight loss, and reduce arrhythmic burden. Incorporating arrhythmia risk screening into obesity management—particularly for individuals with AF, sleep apnea, or metabolic syndrome—should become standard practice [47]. Obesity frequently clusters with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, obstructive sleep apnea, and chronic kidney disease. These conditions independently elevate arrhythmia risk and mortality and may influence cause-of-death attribution. Integrated cardiometabolic management is therefore central to prevention and may partially account for subgroup differences in trends.

At the policy level, interventions that reduce obesity prevalence can indirectly mitigate arrhythmia risk. Fiscal tools like taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages, mandatory nutritional labeling, and zoning restrictions on fast food establishments have proven effective [48]. Urban planning that supports active living—such as walkable neighborhoods and safe recreational spaces—can further reinforce prevention. Obesity stigma remains a major barrier to care. It reduces patient motivation, increases healthcare avoidance, and negatively impacts mental health [49]. Public campaigns and provider training should aim to reduce bias and shift the focus toward health outcomes rather than appearance. Finally, ongoing investment in surveillance—such as improved ICD coding and longitudinal obesity tracking—is essential for monitoring trends and evaluating policy effectiveness. Consumer wearables can support earlier AF identification among high-risk adults with obesity. In a recent meta-analysis, PPG-based smartwatches achieved 97.4 % sensitivity and 96.6 % specificity for AF detection, outperforming ECG-based smartwatch detection in pooled comparisons [50]. A JACC Advances meta-analysis specific to Apple Watch ECG reported 94.8 % sensitivity and 95.0 % specificity (AUROC 0.96) [51]. Integration into cardiometabolic pathways should pair screen-positive results with confirmatory clinical ECG, streamlined referral, and evidence-based anticoagulation when indicated, while evaluating adherence, equity, and false-alert mitigation. These efforts can guide targeted interventions and support precision public health strategies.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents the first comprehensive national analysis of long-term trends in obesity-related arrhythmia mortality in the United States, spanning 21 years and including over 67,000 deaths. The use of the CDC WONDER database allowed for high-quality, population-based mortality data with standardized classification across time. We applied Joinpoint regression to identify inflection points and assess changes in mortality trends with statistical rigor. Furthermore, we performed detailed subgroup analyses by sex, race/ethnicity, region, urbanization, and age, providing a granular understanding of disparities and high-burden populations.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the analysis relied on death certificate data, which may be subject to misclassification or underreporting of obesity and arrhythmias as contributing causes. Obesity is under-recorded on U.S. death certificates—particularly in earlier years—and certifier practices may have changed over time. Moreover, obesity class (I–III) is unavailable in multiple-cause mortality files, precluding severity stratification. These factors likely bias absolute rates downward and may attenuate temporal differences; we therefore interpret magnitude and trends conservatively. The accuracy of ICD-10 coding depends on the certifying physician's judgment and may vary by region or institution. Second, our study is ecological and descriptive in nature, limiting causal inference. We could not adjust for individual-level risk factors, treatment data, or comorbidities due to the aggregated nature of the dataset. Third, obesity and arrhythmias may have been underdiagnosed or underreported in certain populations, potentially biasing estimates. The files do not include medication use, bariatric surgery, rhythm monitoring, or ablation/device implantation, precluding attribution of trends to changes in therapy or detection. Finally, while our trend analysis identified significant shifts in mortality patterns, it does not account for changes in diagnostic practices, healthcare access, or arrhythmia management over time.

Conclusion

Obesity-related arrhythmia mortality in the United States has increased substantially over the past two decades, with consistent upward trends across nearly all demographic groups. The burden is disproportionately higher among older adults, men, American Indian/Alaska Native and Black populations, and residents of rural and Midwestern regions. These findings underscore the intersection of metabolic risk, structural heart disease, and healthcare disparities in shaping arrhythmia outcomes. Urgent public health efforts are needed to address upstream drivers of obesity, enhance arrhythmia screening and management in high-risk populations, and reduce systemic barriers to care. Continued surveillance and targeted interventions will be critical to reversing these trends and improving cardiovascular equity.
