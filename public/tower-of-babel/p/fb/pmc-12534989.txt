Surgical lung volume reduction eligibility in an emphysema patient pool referred for endoscopic treatment
Authors: Christelle M Vandervelde, Jorine E Hartman, Karin Klooster, Wim Janssens, Stephanie Everaerts, Sofian Bouneb, Walter Weder, Dirk-Jan Slebos, Laurens J Ceulemans
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) and bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR) are effective treatments for certain patients with severe emphysema. However, treatment access varies across centres, as not all provide both treatment options. We aimed to assess the proportion of severe COPD patients referred for BLVR that could also be eligible for LVRS.MethodsA retrospective observational study was performed of the Groningen severe COPD cohort. Strict LVRS criteria included: age ≤ 75 years, BMI between 18 and 32 kg/m2, modified Medical Research Council scale ≥ 2, amount of acute exacerbations ≤ 2, forced expiratory volume in 1 s between 20% and 45% of predicted, residual volume ≥ 150% of predicted, total lung capacity ≥ 100% of predicted, PaCO2 ≤ 8 kPa, PaO2 ≥ 6 kPa, %voxel density score in any lobe ≥ 20% and a heterogeneity index of ≥ 10% (-950HU). Chest CT of the remaining population was visually assessed to determine target zones for surgery and any parenchymal changes contraindicating surgery.ResultsIn this cohort of 1030 pre-selected patients, 193 (19%) patients met the strict LVRS criteria. Visual chest CT assessment of those patients, excluded 73 patients, resulting in 120 (62%) patients eligible for LVRS. Main factors for exclusion were the absence of approachable surgical targets (18%) and presence of bronchiectasis (10%). Among these eligible patients, 55 (46%) were treated with BLVR.ConclusionOur analysis indicates that providing LVRS in addition to endoscopic treatment can double therapeutic options for severe emphysema patients, highlighting the importance of offering both treatments to benefit more patients.Trial registrationPatients who visited the UMCG between August 2014 and July 2019 have been included in the Groningen Severe COPD cohort (retrospectively registered at clinicaltrials.gov: NCT04023409 (15/07/2019), an observational cross-sectional cohort with the main aim to identify clinical phenotypes and endotypes in patients with severe COPD.Keywords: COPD population, Eligibility, Emphysema surgery reduction surgery

Take-home message

Incorporating LVRS alongside endoscopic treatment has the potential to double the arsenal of therapeutic options available for managing severe emphysema.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the third leading cause of death worldwide [1] and comes with a high burden on the patient’s quality of life (QoL) [2]. It comprises a spectrum of clinical phenotypes ranging from emphysema to small airway disease and bronchitis and could benefit next to the standard medical COPD treatment regimen, some advanced treatment options. Therefore, a specialised multidisciplinary approach is required to recognise treatable traits and personalise patient care [3–6].

Pulmonary emphysema is characterised by permanent enlargement of air spaces distal to the terminal bronchioles. Parenchyma destruction together with abnormal airway physiology, results in reduced gas exchange, extensive air trapping, and hyperinflation. In these patients, lung volume reduction (LVR) procedures have proven effective in improving dyspnoea, pulmonary function, quality of life (QoL) and exercise capacity [7, 8]. For many years, lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) was the primary treatment for patients with severe emphysema by reducing hyperinflation and improving elastic recoil.

However, broad implementation was mainly limited by the National Emphysema Treatment Trial (NETT), which showed inferior results in mostly open surgery. Nevertheless, in properly selected patients, high-volume centres have consistently demonstrated lasting improvements in lung function and QoL [9–11]. With the advent of bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR) since more than a decade [7], pulmonologists renewed their interest in patient referrals, creating a need for LVRS to adapt to the evolving clinical landscape.

Recent advancements in LVRS [12, 13] have refined patient selection criteria and reduced procedural risks, rendering LVRS a valuable option again. Unfortunately, only a small fraction of patients meets the criteria for these interventions [14–16]. Indicating the need for a centralised multidisciplinary lung failure and transplant team (LUFT) to evaluate all options like, BLVR, LVRS, and lung transplantation.

In this study, we aimed to assess the proportion of patients from a severe COPD cohort who were referred for BLVR that might be eligible for LVRS, to support institutions and clinicians to establish or expand their therapeutic options.

Methods

Study design and patient population

We performed a retrospective analysis of the Groningen severe COPD cohort (clinical trial: NCT04023409, 15/07/2019), involving 1030 patients who visited The University Medical Centre Groningen (UMCG), The Netherlands from 2014 to 2019 after initial pre-selection (based on referral information, excluding patients with significant comorbidities that rendered them ineligible for BLVR) at a multidisciplinary team meeting. All patients signed informed consent, and the study was approved by the Medical Ethical committee of the UMCG, The Netherlands (METC-number: 2014/102).

Measurements

Measures included demographics, high resolution computed tomography (HRCT), post-bronchodilator spirometry, body plethysmography, diffusing capacity and blood gas analysis. Pulmonary function tests were performed according to the European Respiratory Society (ERS)/American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines [17–20]. Baseline HRCT was quantitatively assessed (QCT) using LungQ software, Thirona, Nijmegen, The Netherlands. The QCT used variables were lobar volumes (at full inspiration), lobar emphysematous destruction (expressed as the percentage of voxel density, <−950HU) and the interlobar heterogeneity index was calculated as previously reported by the Groningen group [21].

Lung volume reduction

The proportion of patients treated with BLVR using endobronchial valves (EBV) and lung volume reduction coils was reported. At the time of the study, the UMCG did not offer traditional LVRS; only in exceptional cases an anatomical lobectomy was performed, while lung transplantation was available as the established surgical option.

Evaluation for eligibility

Initial screening for LVRS

We applied inclusion and exclusion criteria for LVRS according the ERS/ATS guidelines [22] to assess the proportion of patients eligible for thoracoscopic LVRS. Main inclusion criteria were age ≤ 75 years, body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 32 kg/m2, modified Medical Research Council scale (mMRC) ≥ 2, amount of acute COPD exacerbations in the past year ≤ 2, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) between 20% and 45% of predicted, residual volume (RV) ≥ 150% of predicted, total lung capacity (TLC) ≥ 100% of predicted, PaCO2 ≤ 8 kPa (60mmHg), PaO2 ≥ 6 kPa (45mmHg), voxel density score at less than − 950HU in any lobe ≥ 20% and a heterogeneity index of ≥ 10%. Patients with DLCO ≤ 20% of predicted were excluded.

Chest-CT morphology analysis

Chest-CT scans of the remaining potential candidates were evaluated, and potential treatment targets were identified by two surgeons with a high level of LVRS expertise (CMV, LJC). Emphysema morphology was classified following the Weder classification [23] (Fig. 1). We further considered patients ineligible when no surgical approachable targets could be identified (central centrilobular emphysema [24], peripheral intact parenchyma), when having severe destruction of the entire lung (vanishing lung), parenchymal abnormalities (nodules, fibrosis, postinfectious lesions) or bronchiectasis. In case of homogeneous morphology, we excluded patients with a lack of visual hyperinflation, as confirmed by plethysmography. In case of disagreement, consensus was reached.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range (IQR)). Categorical variables were reported as number (percentage). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh, version 29.0 (IBM, Armounk, New York, NY, USA) or GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA) with at significance level of 0.05.

Results

Evaluation for eligibility

Initial screening for LVRS

Of The 1030 patients who were invited for consultation throughout the study period, 193 were included for additional analysis (see patient flowchart in Fig. 2). Four hundred ninety-eight patients (48%) were excluded based on age (1% of total population), BMI (10%), mMRC (1%) and number of acute exacerbations (34%). Further exclusion based on spirometry (118 (22%)), plethysmography (16 (4%)), alveolar gas exchange (45 (11%)) resulted in 353 (34%) patients. Of them, additionally 27 (8%) and 133 (41%) were excluded due to insufficient lobar destruction < 20% and a heterogeneity index < 10%, respectively.

Chest-CT morphology analysis

Of the 193 patients included for chest-CT analysis, 73 (38%) were considered not suitable for LVRS after further visual assessment (Fig. 3). The majority of excluded patients in this group consisted of no approachable surgical targets (18%) and bronchiectasis (10%), resulting in 120 (12%) potential eligible LVRS patients. Out of these 120 patients considered eligible for LVRS, 93 (78%) demonstrated markedly heterogeneous morphology with affected upper lobes in 55 (46%), in 17 (14%) patients’ upper lobes with apical segment of lower lobes and in 21 (18%) patients’ lower lobes. Five (4%) patients presented with homogeneous and 22 (18%) patients with intermediate heterogeneous distributed emphysema.

Lung volume reduction

Out of 1030 patients, 220 (21%) underwent treatment with EBV and 46 (4%) with coils. If we would have applied stringent LVRS criteria to patients treated with EBV or coils, these numbers would decrease to 63 (EBV) and 16 (coils), indicating that 187 patients could potential receive LVR beyond the generally applied LVRS criteria. Among those eligible for LVRS, 55 (46%) received BLVR, with 47 (85%) treated via EBV and 8 (14%) with coils (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

Our findings showed that, when following these generally accepted strict criteria in a COPD cohort, approximately 1 out of 10 patients who were evaluated for BLVR (both in clinical trial and regular care) in this centre, could have been eligible for LVRS. Of them, only 46% received LVR treatment with EBV or coils, while an additional 54% could potentially benefit from LVRS if offered.

The selection criteria for LVRS were largely established following the NETT, a randomized controlled trial published in 2003, which compared maximal medical therapy with LVRS [25]. This trial demonstrated that LVRS can improve quality of life and functional capacity in properly selected patients. A 2001 analysis highlighted high postoperative mortality in patients with very low FEV1 (≤ 20%) and either homogeneous emphysema or DLCO ≤ 20% [26]. As a result, strict selection criteria were introduced, favouring patients with upper-lobe predominant heterogeneous emphysema and low baseline exercise capacity, while discouraging surgery in patients with homogeneous emphysema, severely impaired lung function, or low diffusion capacity. Although the final report demonstrated survival benefit in patients with predominantly upper lobe emphysema with low exercise capacity [25], the utilisation declined [27].

Together with the narrow eligibility criteria, the considerable interest in developing less invasive bronchoscopic methods (EBV, coils) for LVR arose. EBVs were demonstrated to achieve similar LVR effect without the invasiveness of surgery and the potential being reversible [28–30]. However, LVRS has evolved over the years and current modern surgical practices, such as better patient selection, minimal invasive procedures, improved anaesthesiologic management with pain control, the implementation of enhanced recovery protocols for LVRS [12] have contributed to lower morbidity and mortality rates.

An integrated, multidisciplinary approach is essential to address the diverse clinical manifestations of COPD and is key to the success of LVR interventions [3–6, 31]. Despite the proven effectiveness of both BLVR and LVRS, access to these treatments remains inconsistent across centres. Some facilities offering BLVR have not yet adopted LVRS, resulting in limited treatment options. To ensure access to the full spectrum of treatment options, and avoid competition between treatments, we believe that centralised Lung Failure and Transplantation (LUFT) teams should be established. Such a team should consist of COPD pulmonologists, transplant pulmonologists, LVRS and transplant surgeons, radiologist, anaesthesiologist, coordinators and allied health professionals including a psychologist and physiotherapist. The best treatment option for each patient should be considered in a monthly meeting discussing all newly referred COPD patients. No competition should occur between different treatment options. Consistent with this, the CELEB randomized controlled trial [32] reported no superiority of BLVR or LVRS in patients suitable for both, supporting that either treatment should be considered complementary and dependent on the general functionality of the patient, his/her comorbidities and location of target zones.

Data on suitable patients for BLVR or LVRS are limited, with approximately 19% being eligible for BLVR and 8–15% considered candidates for LVRS [14–16]. This study applied strict selection criteria, largely based on the NETT trial, followed by a stepwise elimination process using chest-CT morphology assessment. The initial elimination phase, the most frequent reason for ineligibility for LVRS were age, BMI and number of acute exacerbations. Patients who were ultimately eligible for LVRS demonstrated the classical COPD profile, with a mean BMI of 23.9 kg/m², minimal acute exacerbations, and lung function showing a FEV₁ close to 30%pred. In addition, they were more hyperinflated (RV of 217.9%pred), consistent with the expected physiological pattern of carefully selected LVRS candidates. Although these strict criteria do not reflect a typical real-world practice, and expert centres now often apply extended criteria [13, 33, 34], a more restrictive approach might be beneficial for centres new to adopting LVRS technique. However, experienced centres are likely to assess each patient with greater flexibility, as no criterion —whether demographic or spirometry—is absolute. In this cohort, 22% of the 837 patients excluded after the quantitative assessment were treated with BLVR. This suggests that the selection criteria may be overly restrictive and that broader treatment options could be considered, and most important: the development of expert recommendation criteria for LVRS similar to those established for BLVR should be prioritised. Analysis of this BLVR-only subgroup revealed that frequent exacerbations, FEV1 < 20%, and insufficient heterogeneity were the primary factors leading to LVRS ineligibility.

One of the keys to success in LVR is identification of the optimal volume reduction target area. Various methodologies are employed to identify target zones, utilizing computed tomography (CT), ventilation-perfusion scintigraphy, and Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT) [35]. Quantitative CT provides valuable insights for evaluating the extent of emphysema destruction, identifying potential target zones, and assessing fissure integrity. These methods are currently the standard of reference. A range of commercially available software tools can perform automated emphysema quantification (StratX™, Thirona LungQ, Pulmonx, USA; Coreline® AVIEW COPD, Coreline Soft, South Korea). However, the visual assessment by a radiologist remains essential to detect any unexpected findings or altered lung structure patterns of destruction [36]. Moreover, in BLVR or surgical lobectomy to achieve LVR, lobar emphysema quantification is of paramount. For peripheral shaving LVRS, the visual assessment to determine different target zones is important. Chest-CT analysis was performed in this study to identify potential treatment targets and abnormalities. We demonstrated that 18% (35/193) patients had no surgical approachable targets, current software lacks the opportunity to identify specific surgical approachable targets.

In a previous report on evaluating potential candidates for LVRS by Akuthota and colleagues, COPD GOLD stage III and IV patients with available chest CT were identified from a pulmonary function database. In 61 out of 413 patients, clinical inclusion criteria for LVRS were met with upper lobe predominance emphysema morphology [16]. In contrast to our study, this approach accounted for eligibility limited to upper lobe predominant disease. Of our 120 patients eligible, 72 (60%) presented with upper lobe predominant emphysema. Unlike previous studies, this centre had already conducted a primary selection of potential candidates for BLVR. This initial filtering, based on referrals and excluding those with significant comorbidities, allows for a more accurate estimation of potential LVRS candidates compared to previous approaches.

The following limitations should be noted. First, this study was conducted at a single centre (in preselected, referred group), and although our sample generally represents a large cohort of emphysema patients, the ability to generalise our findings to all emphysema patients is limited to the overall COPD population by preselection for BLVR. Second, the inherent retrospective nature of the study introduces potential confounding by variables not included in the database, such as ventilation/perfusion scintigraphy, more detailed quantification of comorbidities. Frailty, cardiovascular risk, psychosocial issues, and similar factors are often reasons to exclude patients from LVRS, even if all other criteria are met. Therefore, interpretation of results should consider potential patient attrition based on individual characteristics. Third, the potential bias of visual scoring interobserver variability. For this, we primary selected on heterogeneity index followed by visual scoring to avoid bias of this kind [36]. Despite these limitations, the classification used might be helpful for selecting candidates for LVRS.

In conclusion, this analysis, based on generally applied LVRS criteria, identifies a subgroup within a preselected BLVR cohort suitable for surgical treatment, as well as patients who may benefit from both surgical and bronchoscopic approaches. Despite being a preselected group, there could still be substantial benefit from applying LVRS. Incorporating LVRS alongside endoscopic treatments could potentially double the number of patients with access to effective therapy. This highlights the need for specialised multidisciplinary centres and initiatives should be undertaken to develop new expert guidelines.
