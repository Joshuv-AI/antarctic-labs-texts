Social and Environmental Correlates of Childhood Obesity Among Southern California Communities
Authors: Alexandra Descarpentrie, Joseph C. Cleveland, Vishal Midya, Juan Espinoza, Jonatan Ottino‐Gonzalez, Shana Adise, Tanya L. Alderete, Michael I. Goran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to identify key childhood obesity correlates in Southern California by analyzing individual components from four social determinants of health (SDoH) indices and explore their interactions.MethodsWe utilized publicly available data from 330 cities across 10 counties, incorporating childhood obesity rates from the 2019 California Department of Education Physical Fitness Test (684,419 children, 40% Latino). Fifty‐two individual SDoH were obtained from the Healthy Places Index, Social Vulnerability Index, CalEnviroScreen, and Child Opportunity Index (2015–2019). Weighted quantile sum regression and an interpretable machine‐learning tool were used to identify which individual SDoH were significantly associated with childhood obesity.ResultsWe identified a SDoH mixture associated with increased percentile of childhood obesity (β [95% CI]: 10.1 [8.1, 12.1]). Fourteen factors significantly contributed, with the top six being school poverty, minority status, asthma emergency room visits, public assistance rates, hazardous waste sites, and lead exposure from housing. We also found positive associations between Latino percentage and key correlates. Cities with high school poverty and low‐income housing burdens had higher obesity rates.ConclusionsThis analysis moved beyond composite indices to examine specific SDoH observed alongside childhood obesity in Southern California, drawing attention to dimensions related to school, healthcare, social services, and environmental exposures.

Introduction

High rates of childhood obesity remain concerning in the United States (US) due to the increased risks of current and future health issues like heart disease, diabetes, and cancer [1]. Tackling childhood obesity requires more than changing individual behaviors, it demands a deeper understanding of the broader social determinants of health (SDoH), which shape health outcomes across the life course [2]. These include income, education, food access, neighborhood safety, and healthcare availability, factors that collectively influence a child's ability to thrive. To track and address these upstream drivers, several publicly available composite indices have been developed to measure SDoH at the community level [3, 4]. Among the most widely used in the US are the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) and the Area Deprivation Index, which aggregate multiple socioeconomic and environmental indicators to identify communities at higher risk for poor health outcomes [5].

Additional indices (such as the Healthy Places Index (HPI) [6, 7] and CalEnviroScreen (CES) [8]) have been developed in California to assess community well‐being and environmental burdens. Focusing on California is particularly important due to its unique demographic profile as the most populous state in the US and home to the largest Latino population [9], which experiences a disproportionate burden of structural inequities and childhood obesity. Notably, in 2013, higher community‐level hardship, as measured by the Economic Hardship Index, was found to be associated with increased childhood obesity prevalence in Los Angeles County [10]. A more recent study in Southern California [5] found higher childhood obesity prevalence in communities with lower scores on the HPI, CES, and SVI [11], with Latino populations consistently scoring lower on these indices, highlighting their elevated vulnerability to adverse health outcomes.

Although composite indices are useful for identifying at‐risk communities, they lack specificity in identifying public health priorities. Most are built from dozens of variables, using weightings that may not reflect outcomes specific to childhood obesity. Additionally, few (with the exception of the Child Opportunity Index (COI) [12]) are designed with child overall health explicitly in mind. Furthermore, each index is shaped by the priorities of its creators, leading to inconsistent domains and limited cross‐comparability. As a result, critical drivers of specific child health outcomes may be underweighted or overlooked, and the interactions between individual SDoH factors (which may compound risk) are often missed, making the combined study of their individual SDoH components crucial for a comprehensive analysis [4].

In the current study, we thus aimed to unpack individual and interactive SDoH components from diverse sources, bypassing the limitations of broad, institutional composite indices, to inform the creation of more precise and tailored public health strategies for addressing children's health. More precisely, we sought to investigate which of the individual community‐level SDoH‐related factors that are used to calculate HPI, CES, SVI, and COI indices (90 factors overall) are most related to childhood obesity in Southern California. We also explored whether these key SDoH factors could contribute to disparities disproportionately affecting Latino communities, an especially salient focus given their substantial representation within California's population. Lastly, we sought to explore how individual SDoH interact to help identify subgroups of children who may be at increased risk of obesity based on specific contextual factors.

Methods

Population

This ecological study focused on analyzing data from 330 cities across 10 adjacent counties in Southern California (with a 40% Latino population) [13]. The study utilized publicly available data that had been deidentified. Of note, all data were collected pre‐COVID‐19, which may limit the current applicability of our findings given the significant social and health disruptions that followed.

Measures

Social Determinants of Health (SDoH)

We used the following versions of four different publicly available indices of SDoH: the HPI 2.0, the SVI 2018, the CES 4.0, and the COI 2.0. Unlike the SVI (using only data from the American Community Survey) the HPI, CES, and COI draw data from multiple sources. Among the 90 individual SDoH available from these four indices, certain indicators closely resemble each other due to the utilization of identical sources, although at different years. Briefly, the HPI 2.0 scores California census tracts on community conditions related to life expectancy at birth using 25 indicators from eight socioeconomic domains, based on data from 2010 to 2015 (Supporting Information 1) [6, 7]. The CES 4.0 assesses 21 environmental indicators in two domains: pollution burden and population characteristics, using data from 2009 to 2019 (Supporting Information 2) [8]. The SVI measures community resilience with 15 indicators across four domains, using data from 2014 to 2018 (Supporting Information 3) [11]. The COI 2.0 quantifies 29 indicators for children's development across education, health, and social/economic domains, based on data from 2013 to 2017 (Supporting Information 4) [12].

Childhood Obesity

This study examined school‐level body composition data from California's 2019 Physical Fitness Test, which assesses body mass index (BMI), skinfold measurements, and bioelectric impedance among 5th, 7th, and 9th graders [14]. Body composition results were grouped into categories including “needs improvement,” a health‐risk zone that effectively represents children with obesity, as its lower threshold corresponds approximately to BMI at the 95th percentile based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's age‐ and sex‐specific childhood obesity measures. More details are available elsewhere [13, 14].

Statistical Analysis

Data Processing

This study employed individual indicators from four indices (HPI, SVI, CES, COI) reported as percentiles at the census tract level: higher values reflected more or less favorable neighborhood opportunities relative to other census tracts across California. We averaged these percentiles across all census tracts within each city. Childhood obesity was assessed similarly by aggregating percentiles by city after mapping school locations to census tracts [13]. For our analysis, we selected 330 cities that had complete data for all variables considered, excluding any cities with missing data (covering 3829 schools with a total of 684,419 children). Due to high correlations among certain SDoH variables, we excluded those with correlations over |0.9|, reducing our SDoH count from 90 to 61. Some retained indicators still closely aligned, sharing data sources and overlapping time frames. Thus, since the weighted quantile sum (WQS) method employed in this study requires clear distinctions among indicators [15], we selected one variable from each group based on the strongest association with childhood obesity percentiles, prioritizing COI indicators when associations were equal. Supporting Information 5 presents the final selection of 52 variables across seven broader domains of SDoH: Education, Economy, Demography and Social Environment, Physical and Built Environment, Health, Housing, Employment and Working Conditions [16]. The correlation matrix of the selected exposures is provided in Supporting Information 6. To enhance the stability of the WQS model, all exposures were further converted into deciles. Finally, to ensure all indicators were consistently interpreted (the WQS approach requires an important assumption of uni‐direction [15]), SDoH negatively associated with childhood obesity percentiles were multiplied by −1, adjusting 16 out of the 52 indicators (see Supporting Information 7).

Descriptive Analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted to analyze the mean and confidence intervals (CI) for each individual SDoH factor. Subsequently, in accordance with previous literature [12, 17], the overall studied area was described as neighborhoods of: very low (0–20th percentile), low (20–40th percentile), moderate (40–60th percentile), high (60–80th percentile), or very high (80–100th percentile) opportunity or vulnerability relative to other neighborhoods across California, based on the specific variable under consideration.

Main Analysis

We utilized the WQS/rh‐SiRF algorithm, developed by Midya et al. [18], which combines WQS regression with repeated holdout signed‐iterative random forest (rh‐SiRF). The algorithm first constructs a composite index using WQS regression (to identify key childhood obesity drivers) and subsequently applies rh‐SiRF to identify potential interactions. Assuming the main mixture effect and interactions are additive, residuals from the WQS model are treated as the new outcome in rh‐SiRF. The final stage involved hypothesis testing (regression‐based inference) on the identified potential interactions and the WQS mixture. Supporting Information 8 summarizes the analysis procedure.

Weighted Quantile Sum (WQS) Regression

WQS regression was used to model the adverse mixture effect of SDoH and estimate indicator weights by regressing the 52 individual indicators against childhood obesity percentiles [15]. The weights sum to one, allowing each weight to represent the percentage of association between the corresponding indicator and childhood obesity. Stronger associations result in higher weights. A key correlate was defined as a variable whose contribution exceeded what would be expected by chance, with a weight greater than 1/52 (with 52 being the number of variables selected for the analysis). The use of weights addressed collinearity issues and enabled dimensionality reduction to create a new SDoH index. To ensure robustness, we used random subsets and repeated holdouts in the WQS analysis [19, 20]. We further examined the SDoH mixture's association with the percentage of Latino individuals through univariable linear regressions [6]. This was also done separately from the broader Minority variable considered in the mixture (i.e., all White non‐Latino groups) to specifically investigate how the results apply to the Latino population, a significant proportion of the population in California.

Residuals from the model, which considered childhood obesity as the outcome and the SDoH mixture as the exposure, were analyzed in the subsequent steps to identify interactions [18].

Repeated HoldOut Signed‐Iterative Random Forest (Rh‐SiRF)

Instead of exhaustively searching all possible combinations (interactions), rh‐SiRF identifies predetermined and potentially predictive combinations from the tree branches rather than exploring every conceivable combination [21, 22, 23]. More details about the rh‐SiRF are provided elsewhere [18, 22]. The model training involves using a subset of data, followed by bagging on the remaining held‐out testing data [18]. To enhance reliability, we adopted five different data partitions: 60%/40%, 65%/35%, 70%/30%, 75%/25%, and 80%/20% for training and testing. Only potential interactions ranking in the top 5 stability scores (i.e., how frequently an interaction is detected throughout the bootstrapped iterations) across all five data partitions were considered. Of note, the discovered interactions were based on thresholds. Since the algorithm does not directly estimate thresholds, we applied a quantile‐based threshold‐finding algorithm, ensuring a minimum prevalence of 10% for the combinations [24]. For each created interaction indicator, we estimated corresponding beta values while controlling for the SDoH mixture from the WQS stage (linear regression). Detailed tuning parameters and random seeds for the WQS/rh‐SiRF algorithm are available in Supporting Information 9.

Sensitivity Analyses

First, we used the Boruta algorithm to assess the robustness of the WQS approach in identifying contributors to childhood obesity [25]. This algorithm identified important variables using a random forest‐based feature selection method, comparing each variable's importance to randomized counterparts. Second, we increased the number of bootstraps in the rh‐SiRF bagging step from 250 to 500 to evaluate stability. Third, we repeated the regression analysis with thresholds adjusted up and down by 10 percentiles. Fourth, to account for community‐level variability, we reran all analyses at the census tract level (n = 2406) [13]. Finally, we used the MissForest procedure to impute missing values (Supporting Information 10) for individual SDoH variables and reran the main analyses (n = 344 cities) [26].

All data were analyzed in R version 4.1.2. The iRF package version 3.0 and gWQS package version 3.0.5 were used.

Main Analysis

In the WQS analysis, the SDoH mixture was positively associated with the percentile of childhood obesity (B [95% CI]: 10.1 [8.1, 12.1]). Fourteen individual indicators were identified as making a greater‐than‐chance contribution (weight > 1/52) to the overall mixture effect. Among these were: School Poverty (+, positive effect), Minority—all except White and non‐Latino (+), Asthma Emergency Room Visits (+), Public Assistance Rate (+), Voting (−), Hazardous Waste Dump Sites (+), Children's Lead Risk from Housing (+), Heart Attack Emergency Room Visits (+), Single Headed Household (+), Alcohol Outlets (+), Advanced Placement Course Enrollment (−), Limited Access to Green Space (+), People Aged 65+ (−) and Preschool Enrollment (−). Collectively, these factors accounted for 66% of the association between the SDoH mixture and childhood obesity (Figure 1). Figure 2 displays the relationship between school poverty, the most influential correlate within the SDoH mixture, and childhood obesity. We also found positive associations between the percentage of the population identifying as Latino and both the SDoH mixture and its key 14 contributors (Table 2). Figure 3 demonstrates that as the level of disadvantage increased, which was measured by deciles of the SDoH mixture and its main contributors, there was a corresponding rise in the percentage of Latinos.

Sensitivity Analyses

The Boruta analysis indicated that 10 of the 14 factors identified by the WQS algorithm were also key correlates to childhood obesity (Supporting Information 11). Increasing the number of bootstraps for the rh‐SiRF algorithm from 250 to 500 did not change the results, nor did altering the interaction thresholds (Supporting Information 12 and 13). The WQS results using census tracts and imputed SDoH data were consistent with the main analysis (Supporting Information 14 and 15). At the rh‐SiRF stage, for both analyses and each data partition, School Poverty significantly contributed to all top five stability scores across analyses and data partitions.

Discussion

Our study identified 14 community‐level SDoH factors that were related to childhood obesity in Southern California. School Poverty, defined by the percentage of students qualifying for free or reduced‐price lunches, was the most significant correlate and interacted with Low‐Income Housing Burden. Other potential contributors included demographic factors, health issues, and environmental exposures such as proximity to hazardous waste sites and lead risk from housing. Notably, Latino communities were characterized by less favorable SDoH for each of the 14 identified correlates.

Comparison with other studies

School poverty, as defined in the COI, may serve as a strong indicator of the quality of educational resources [27, 28], such as teacher quality, inadequate facilities, and health education. This, in turn, may lead to poor dietary choices due to a lack of nutrition education, whereas inadequate school facilities (such as limited access to healthy drinking water, safe play areas, or sports programs) further reduce opportunities for physical activity, both of which are key factors in preventing obesity [29]. Beyond individual school characteristics, school poverty may reflect wider poor neighborhood socioeconomic conditions that shape social environments, through role models, community norms, and shared values around health and education [27, 28]. In such settings, children may be less likely to adopt healthy behaviors, increasing their risk of obesity.

The interaction highlighted in this study between lower housing cost burdens and higher school poverty could thus be explained by the “Obesity Kuznets curve” [30], which indicates that as income increases, calorie consumption and obesity rates increase. In the context of this study, a lower housing cost burden may reflect higher disposable income, thus potentially contributing to higher obesity rates. However, the curve also suggests that beyond a certain income threshold, factors like education and health literacy (potentially reflected here by school poverty) become increasingly important. Communities with stronger educational resources are better equipped to make informed health choices, whereas higher school poverty may limit the protective effects of income [31]. Of note, using the same iRF algorithm, a prior study found that youth in neighborhoods with low education but high home values had higher waist‐to‐height ratios than those in areas with both low education and low home values [32]. Alternatively, this interaction may suggest that, despite nutrition standards in the School Breakfast and National School Lunch Programs, disparities in meal quality or implementation in high‐poverty schools could still affect children's dietary habits and health outcomes, regardless of household income, though this remains underexplored [33]. Our findings also highlighted the potential role of participation in Advanced Placement courses in high school, which are associated with academic achievement and future aspirations. These factors are linked to healthier lifestyle choices, which are often modeled by parents and transmitted to children, potentially correlating with a reduced risk of childhood obesity.

We found that the prevalence of childhood obesity was higher in Southern California cities with larger percentages of ethnic minorities, especially Latinos. This pattern aligns with previous research, which shows Latino communities are more likely to face prenatal, perinatal, and early childhood obesity risk factors, such as maternal smoking, limited breastfeeding, short sleep duration, and poor dietary patterns [34]. Cities with more single‐parent households, lower voter turnout, and fewer residents over 65 also showed elevated childhood obesity rates. Single‐parent families may face time and financial constraints that make it more difficult to provide regular, nutritious meals and opportunities for physical activity, increasing the risk of obesity among children [35]. Low voter engagement may signify broader challenges such as social isolation, diminished civic empowerment, and heightened psychological stress, all of which could suggest barriers to community participation and advocacy for resources. Such communities may be deprived of crucial investments in public infrastructure and health initiatives, that may be integral to shaping children's physical and social environments. Moreover, a dearth of older adults may indicate a lack of age‐friendly amenities [36], which may not only benefit seniors but also foster healthier lifestyles for children.

Although traditional views suggest that obesity typically precedes and predicts asthma and cardiovascular disease development [37, 38], our study reveals a more nuanced understanding, consistent with recent research. It has been suggested that people with asthma or cardiovascular problems may engage less in physical activity due to respiratory symptoms, and certain medications are known to cause weight gain [39]. Alternatively, asthma emergency room visits may be a proxy for healthcare utilization [40], with individuals who do not access healthcare more likely to lack preventive treatments or management options [41].

Our results also indicated that cities with a higher percentage of the population receiving public assistance, that is the percentage of households receiving cash public assistance or Food Stamps/Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) had higher childhood obesity rates [42]. SNAP provides financial aid for food, but studies on its impact have been mixed. In coherence with our results, Bitler found that SNAP participants often had poorer diets and higher obesity rates than eligible nonparticipants [43]. Hudak and Racine noted that SNAP's effects on weight vary by age, with less favorable outcomes for children Aged 6–11 [44]. These results may reflect ongoing barriers to accessing affordable, nutritious food and a lack of nutrition education, all of which can reduce the effectiveness of financial aid programs in promoting healthier outcomes. Further research is needed to clarify the connection between SNAP and child weight.

We observed an association between alcohol outlets and childhood obesity: bars and pubs are associated with higher consumption of calorie‐dense foods [45], potentially contributing to childhood obesity, especially in deprived communities where fast‐food and alcohol outlets cluster [46]. Areas with more impervious surfaces (such as roads, parking lots, and buildings) also showed higher obesity rates, probably related to limited access to green spaces where children and families can engage in physical activity, a key protective factor against obesity [47]. Our study also highlighted the potential influence of environmental factors like hazardous waste sites and lead exposure, which can contribute to obesity‐related diseases [48, 49] and disrupt biological mechanisms linked to weight gain [50].

Limitations and Strengths

Our study has several limitations. First, the ecological fallacy must be considered, as community‐level SDoH data may not accurately capture individual experiences. Furthermore, due to the study's nonrandomized design, causal relationships between SDoH and childhood obesity cannot be established. Second, individuals may live in areas with different SDoH compared to where they work or their children attend school [13], despite accounting for commuting variables here. Third, the data were collected before the COVID‐19 pandemic, limiting temporal relevance. The pandemic intensified many SDoH (such as food insecurity, disrupted schooling, and healthcare access) especially in vulnerable communities [51]. These shifts likely strengthened the associations between certain SDoH and childhood obesity. Future research should explore whether the patterns we observed pre‐COVID persist or have evolved in response to pandemic‐related changes and recovery efforts. Fourth, our specific percentile cutoffs for interaction indicators may not generalize beyond our 10 counties in Southern California. However, our primary goal was to provide focused conclusions relevant to this particular context. Incomplete data could also affect our analysis, though sensitivity tests on imputed SDoH data showed robust findings. Overfitting concerns arise from using the same exposures in both WQS and rh‐SiRF methods, but these were mitigated through random subsets, repeated holdout, and bootstrapping [18]. Finally, while we may highlight prevention priorities, we lack insight into underlying mechanisms, although exploring interactions has refined our understanding.

Despite these limitations, our study has notable strengths. We effectively untangled the contributions of individual SDoH across various environmental indices tailored for children's development (COI), social vulnerability (SVI) and two specifically designed for the California context (HPI, CES). We demonstrated that focusing solely on broad indices may overlook important contributions of individual factors to higher levels of childhood obesity [4]. Our unique integration of an exposure‐mixture method and machine learning tools allowed us to explore SDoH interactions with flexibility, enhancing our analysis [24].

Public Health and Research Implications

Our research highlights potential priority areas in Southern California for addressing childhood obesity, particularly in under‐resourced and Latino communities. Our findings suggest focusing on educational disadvantages in elementary schools and advocating for funding to enhance access to quality educational resources to support optimal lifestyle behaviors (access to health education and well‐maintained facilities). Additionally, strategies could foster environments that not only mitigate negative peer influences and promote aspirations for higher educational attainment but also support positive role models and cultivate community norms that encourage educational achievement. Promoting civic engagement and healthy aging policies may further improve community environments for children's development. Additionally, managing built environments(such as reducing alcohol outlet density and surroundings, improving access to green spaces, addressing risks from waste sites, and mitigating lead exposure from homes) could aid obesity prevention in this region. Public health policies may also prioritize the reduction of environmental risks, such as hazardous waste sites and lead exposure. Furthermore, our results seem to highlight the importance of integrating strategies that not only target environmental risk factors but also address the influence of asthma and its treatment on weight management. Finally, further research is needed to assess the impact of SNAP on children's nutritional health.

Notably, several key factors identified in this study have wide‐reaching implications beyond childhood obesity, affecting cognitive development, mental health, and overall well‐being [16]. By identifying actionable, context‐specific factors, our findings suggest opportunities for targeted interventions that may support broader health improvements. This study contributes to that effort through a refined approach that disaggregates individual and interacting SDoH components from diverse sources, moving beyond broad composite indices. It encourages a shift toward more precise, locally tailored strategies. Future research should build on this framework to evaluate place‐based solutions and guide equity‐driven health planning.

Conclusion

This analysis went beyond the use of composite indices to identify specific SDoH most strongly associated with childhood obesity in Southern California. This approach uncovered key correlates from diverse sources, including the school environment, healthcare access, social services, environmental exposures, and urban planning.
