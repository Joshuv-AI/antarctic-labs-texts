“We just don’t have time”: a qualitative study on health care providers’ experiences with smoking cessation counseling in Egyptian maternity hospitals
Authors: Ayat F Manzour, Waleed Salah Eldin, Isis M Mossad, Karim Labib, Omar El Shahawy, Wagida Anwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the established risks associated with tobacco exposure during pregnancy, the implementation of smoking cessation counseling by healthcare providers in Egypt is inconsistent. This study aimed to assess health care providers’ (HCPs) knowledge about the hazards of smoking exposure during pregnancy, investigate their attitudes and practices concerning smoking cessation, and determine the perceived barriers to addressing this issue during routine care.MethodsWe employed a qualitative approach to conduct semi-structured interviews with 22 health care professionals (15 obstetricians and 7 nurses) from four Cairo government hospitals. Purposive sampling was used to choose participants. Arabic-language interviews were recorded, verbatim transcribed, and subjected to thematic analysis using NVivo 12 Pro.ResultsSix major themes emerged: (1) differences in knowledge about smoking hazards; (2) differences in attitudes regarding counseling; (3) inconsistency regarding history taking and counseling practices; (4) obstacles to effective intervention; (5) recommendations for improvement; and (6) expressed desire for training. Although there was an overall sense of awareness of risk, counseling practices were found to be selective and faced barriers, including cultural sensitivity, time constraints, and role uncertainty.ConclusionTargeted training and structural support are necessary for HCPs to deliver consistent smoking cessation counseling. Systematic incorporation of cessation counselling into antenatal care is necessary to tackle the impact of maternal tobacco exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42506-026-00208-3.Keywords: Smoking cessation, Counseling, Healthcare providers, And pregnancy.

Introduction

Smoking during pregnancy is an established risk factor for a range of health problems for mothers and the baby [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) statistics, the prevalence of smoking is still a major public health concern among women [2].

Worldwide, 1.7% of expectant mothers smoke while pregnant [3]. Maternal smoking is an independent risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes, including placental abruption, placenta praevia, and preterm delivery. It also increases the risk of stillbirth, low birth weight, neonatal death, and sudden infant death syndrome [4]. Passive smoking also has the same side effects as active smoking [5] According to the Central Agency for Public Mobilization and Statistics (CAPMAS) data percentage of smokers among males is 35.6%, compared to 0.3% among females, but the percentage of households with at least one smoker at the level of Egypt is 41.3%, which means that there are about 24 million individuals who are not smokers but are exposed to second hand smoking due to the presence of a smoker in the family [6]. Men who smoke in the household are the most commonly reported barriers to preventing secondhand smoke exposure/adopting a smoke-free home or workplace [7]. Pregnancy provides an opportunity to educate partners or family members on the benefits of quitting for themselves, the woman, and the baby [1]while regular contact with clinicians provides ample opportunity to assess and reinforce advice or abstinence [4].

As such, expert consensus is that pregnant women who use tobacco should be identified early in pregnancy and given counseling on smoking cessation tailored to pregnancy [8–10]. Brief interventions include physicians offering information about smoking-related risks and frequent follow-up to assess progress, the use of pregnancy-specific or other self-help manuals, one or more sessions with a health educator, and videos about smoking risks and cessation. Despite evidence that brief interventions work to reduce maternal smoking, it is estimated that only 49% of obstetricians routinely advise women and provide follow-up for smoking cessation, with only 28% discussing strategies for cessation [11].

Although there have been calls for smoking cessation practices to be integrated into all maternity and antenatal care [12]. No established guidelines exist in Egyptian antenatal services that address this issue, and there is a lack of basic information on the risks of smoking during pregnancy. A systematic review of published qualitative research about smoking cessation care provided by HCPs found that there was a lack of key intervention functions and policies that could support health professionals to provide good smoking cessation care [13].

Despite growing recognition of the importance of smoking cessation during pregnancy, there remains a notable lack of qualitative research exploring how frontline healthcare providers perceive and deliver cessation support in Egypt and similar low-middle-income countries (LMIC) contexts. Most existing studies in Egypt focus on quantitative assessments of knowledge or prevalence, with limited insight into the socio-cultural, institutional, and personal factors shaping provider behavior [14, 15]. In this study, we aimed to qualitatively assess knowledge of the hazards of exposure to cigarette smoke during pregnancy, attitudes and practices toward smoking cessation, and perceived barriers to addressing cessation during routine care among doctors and nurses working in public antenatal services in Egypt.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative approach utilizing in-depth interviews was implemented to investigate the attitudes and practices of healthcare providers regarding smoking cessation counselling for pregnant smokers, former smokers, and individuals exposed to secondhand smoke. This methodology facilitated an in-depth examination of experiences and perceptions within the Egyptian healthcare system.

Study duration

The research took 11 months (September 2020-August 2021) and involved securing ethical approval from the Ain Shams University Ethical Committee, conducting a literature review, creating and reviewing the interview guide, training data collectors, and gathering, analyzing, and reporting data. The preparation phase took three months, data collection took three months, and data analysis and reporting took five months.

Setting and population

In Egypt, maternity care is mainly overseen by physicians, where obstetricians directly care for patients, and nurses assist in supportive roles on hospital teams. Midwives are generally not integrated into the formal hospital framework. By including both physicians and nurses, the study aimed to gain a comprehensive view of smoking cessation counseling across different professional roles. The research was conducted within the obstetrics and gynecology departments’ quiet clinic of one university hospital and four governmental hospitals located in Cairo. These facilities offer extensive maternity services and cater to a varied urban population.

Eligible participants included physicians or nurses actively employed in the obstetrics and gynecology departments of the chosen hospitals, with a minimum of one year’s experience in antenatal or maternity care, who were engaged in patient care. Individuals who were interns, students, administrative personnel, or on extended leave during the data collection period were excluded, as were those who chose not to participate or felt uncomfortable discussing smoking-related topics. Purposive sampling was employed to select participants based on their job roles, experience, and engagement in antenatal care and counseling. A total of 22 healthcare workers (15 obstetricians and 7 nurses) took part in the study, with the sample size being determined by data saturation, which occurred when no fresh themes were identified. Participants were contacted via department heads after the study information was shared. Before data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study tool

We constructed a semi-structured interview guide. The guide provided structure to ensure that each researcher collected information relevant to the study. It was developed through discussions among the research team, literature review, and multiple rounds of revision. Questions were included on HCPs’ practice regarding asking pregnant women about active/passive smoking, attitudes toward smoking during pregnancy, and barriers to counseling pregnant women. Finally, pilot interviews were done among five physicians, and modifications were made based on the outcomes (Appendix A).

The variables of interest in this study concerned the practice and attitude of HCPs towards smoking cessation during pregnancy. Knowledge about smoking included the dangers and impact of smoking on pregnancy and its outcomes. Exposure to SHS was also included. We asked about any intention to participate in training sessions.

Data collection and translation

Data was gathered through semi-structured, in-person interviews conducted by a total of six medical doctors trained in qualitative research who had no direct supervisory ties to the participants and were conducted in Arabic. Each interview was done by two of them, lasted around 25 to 35 min, and was recorded with the participants’ permission. The interview framework was created through a review of existing literature, discussions within the research team, and continuous revisions. One medical doctor was the main interviewer, directing the conversation according to a semi-structured guide to cover all essential topics. The second team member was a note-taker and observer. Both interviewers engaged in debriefing meetings following each interview to share their impressions. The head of the research team oversaw the whole process, examined transcripts for quality assurance, and was involved in coding and theme development to maintain analytic consistency throughout the dataset.

Interviews were transcribed word-for-word in Arabic and then translated into English before analysis. The translations were verified by bilingual team members and were back-translated to confirm accuracy.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed thematically following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [16]. First, small samples of text were read thoroughly to extract significant statements and phrases. Second, emergent themes or patterns were identified for each significant statement or phrase. Third, the themes were clustered into key concepts and categories. NVivo software, version 12 (QSR International Pty Ltd., Melbourne, Australia) [17] was used to manage and code the data. Two researchers independently coded part of the interview transcripts to develop an initial coding framework, which was refined through discussion and consensus. Additional data from the informants was gathered by reading the transcripts and incorporating each of the categories.

Results

We interviewed 22 healthcare providers in Cairo’s four maternity hospitals, including 15 obstetricians and 7 nurses. The sample size was eight men, ranging in age from 28 to 47 (Table 1). Six significant themes emerged from their testimonies, shedding light on the difficulty of managing tobacco use during pregnancy in a limited and culturally sensitive health system.

Knowledge of smoking hazards: widely recognized, but uneven

Active Smoking

Participants expressed a range of views regarding the effects of active smoking on pregnant women and pregnancy outcomes. Nearly all participants recognized that smoking is harmful to both the mother and the fetus. Most commonly, participants associate smoking with low birth weight and preterm labor. A smaller number mentioned more severe outcomes, such as impaired brain development, congenital malformations, or intrauterine fetal death. “Smoking reduces the baby’s weight and can cause early delivery; I’ve seen it many times” (38, Female, Obstetrician).

Regarding maternal health, participants linked smoking to various complications, including pneumonia, chronic cough, cardiovascular problems, bleeding during labor, and anesthetic complications. Some also noted shortness of breath, increased nausea in early pregnancy, and a higher long-term risk of lung cancer. Only one participant suggested that the impact of smoking might vary depending on the mother’s and fetus’s overall health, implying that it could be harmful or not based on individual conditions.

“It affects the mother’s lungs, heart, and even her tolerance for anesthesia during delivery” (40, Female, Nurse).

“Smoking can make pregnancy symptoms like nausea and breathlessness worse” (29, Female, Nurse).

Passive smoking

When asked about the harms of passive smoking, participants provided mixed responses. Some believed that passive smoking is equally harmful as active smoking, while one participant thought it was even more dangerous. Others were uncertain about its exact effects. Most participants, however, associated passive smoking with reduced newborn weight and respiratory difficulties in the infant.

“Passive smoking could be more harmful—she inhales more than she realizes,” (31, Female, Obstetrician). “It depends on how much smoke she’s exposed to,” (29, Female, Nurse).

Comparing active and passive exposure

The majority of healthcare providers perceived active smoking as more hazardous to pregnant women and their fetuses. However, one participant argued that passive smoking could be more dangerous because a non-smoking pregnant woman might inhale large amounts of smoke from nearby smokers. Another participant emphasized that the dose of exposure is more critical than the mode (active or passive). A few participants believed both exposures were equally harmful, while one admitted uncertainty.

Combined exposure

All participants agreed that combined exposure to both active and passive smoking has an additive harmful effect. One participant specifically stated, “This additive effect is evidence-based.” (42, Male, Obstetrician). Another physician explained, “This dual effect will lower the child’s IQ and hinder future academic success” (33, Male, Obstetrician).

Type of smoke considered most hazardous

When asked about the most harmful type of smoke, participants were divided. Approximately half believed cigarette smoke is more dangerous, while the other half identified waterpipe (shisha) smoke as more harmful, often citing the risk of infection associated with shared pipes. One participant emphasized that exposure dose, rather than the smoking method, determines the level of risk.

Attitudes towards counseling: a spectrum from hope to defeat

Opinions on whether smoking cessation counseling was worthwhile differed significantly. Some saw pregnancy as a perfect opportunity for behavioral change, as women are eager to safeguard their unborn child. Others thought that only individuals who explicitly requested assistance were likely to benefit.

“Some women will sacrifice their fetus just to keep smoking,” (38, Female, Obstetrician).

Despite their pessimism, most participants believed that pregnancy might be a tremendous motivator, with the correct assistance. “Pregnancy is an ideal moment to stop. Many women genuinely wish to provide the best for their children,” (31, Female, Obstetrician).

Providers were also asked about husbands’ smoking habits. Many people believed that pregnancy would drive men to quit or cut back on their smoking, while others believed that husbands should smoke away from their wives. “Some men try to reduce their smoking when their wife gets pregnant, while others simply smoke outdoors and believe that is enough,” (35, Male, Obstetrician).

“If a husband accompanies his wife during her visit, I advise him to ensure that smoke is kept away from his wife, but it is hard to monitor,” (42, Male, Obstetrician).

History-taking and counseling: hesitant, indirect, and inconsistent

A noteworthy finding was how rarely healthcare providers inquired directly about women’s smoking behaviors. Most people raised the issue based on symptoms such as cough, discolored teeth, or high hemoglobin levels. For some, cultural norms acted as a barrier to open communication.

“I am terrified to ask. She may think I’m insulting her,” (35, Male, Obstetrician).

When smoking was discovered, some doctors issued general warnings, while others conducted follow-ups during subsequent appointments. A few people employed storytelling—true-life cautionary tales—to emphasize concerns. Others used religious concepts to encourage people to quit. “I occasionally share a story about an infant influenced by smoking—it’s a gentle method to caution them,” (36, Male, Obstetrician).

While most participants recognized the harms of active smoking to fetal health, they less emphasized maternal risks, perhaps because women were previously aware. Some advocated instant abstinence, while others suggested gradual reduction, especially for heavy smokers. Surprisingly, several thought smoking cessation was outside their spectrum and should be deferred to specialists.

“I focus on the risks to the fetus over those to the mother, as most women are already aware of the potential dangers to themselves,” (47, Female, Nurse). “For individuals who smoke heavily, I recommend a gradual decrease instead of quitting suddenly,” 31, Male, Obstetrician).

Discussions about passive smoking were slightly more common, although brief. Most providers did not inquire about exposure in public areas or beyond the husband. Notably, husbands are not authorized to attend antenatal checkups; thus, guiding them directly was rarely an option. “We rarely ask about passive smoking beyond the husband,” (31, Female, Obstetrician).

Barriers to counseling: a pressured system

The primary barrier discovered was a lack of time. Overcrowded antenatal clinics made it difficult to conduct important conversations about smoking. “There’s barely enough time to check her blood pressure, let alone talk about smoking,” (47, Female, Nurse) said. “It’s always too noisy and packed. You are unable to speak with any of the women on a personal level,” (29, Female, Nurse).

“With every patient, we hardly have five minutes. Smoking is just not a priority when you’re rushing to cover the basics of antenatal care.” (36, Male, Obstetrician).

Common challenges to cessation counseling include negative attitudes, limited practitioner confidence, and a lack of clear protocols or resources. “To be honest, I’m not comfortable bringing it up. We never had any training on how to handle it, and I’m not sure what guidance I could offer other than to “just stop.” (32, Female, Obstetrician).

Improving smoking cessation counseling: from visual aids to group therapy

When asked how to improve therapy, participants made serious and reasonable suggestions. Many recommended health education materials—posters, pamphlets, and videos—were displayed in clinic waiting rooms. Others called for brief group therapy sessions or storytelling using patient testimonials.

“Let them witness actual situations where smoking affected babies. Perhaps a short film,” (32, Female, Obstetrician).

Several participants stressed the importance of reassuring women that admitting to smoking was not a sign of shame. A few people suggested including physical activity as a healthier alternative to smoking.

On a systemic level, many advocated for hospital-based smoking cessation clinics and more visible anti-smoking media campaigns. Others argued that smoking instruction should start much earlier, like premarital counseling or primary care visits. Legal steps to ensure smoke-free public locations were also proposed. “Public awareness campaigns and premarital counseling might assist in reducing smoking rates even before pregnancy,” (45, Male, Obstetrician).

Training needs: willing providers and missing tools

There was broad agreement on the importance of professional growth. Participants showed a significant need for structured training programs to improve their skills in smoking cessation counseling for pregnant individuals. Most expressed a keen interest in evidence-based programs that would enhance both their knowledge and practical counseling skills, ultimately helping mothers and fetuses from the dangers of tobacco exposure and adopting a smoke-free environment.

“We need materials and guidelines. Sometimes I don’t know what to say about smoking during pregnancy.” (29, Female, Nurse).

In addition to clinical knowledge, participants recognized the importance of training in effective communication skills to better engage with patients and their families. They expressed that the ability to communicate with empathy and persuasion is just as vital as having scientific knowledge.

“We need to learn how to talk to patients in a way that works—not just repeat the risks,” (38, Female, Obstetrician).

Nurses were broadly regarded as key partners in delivering cessation counseling, thanks to their prolonged interaction with patients during lengthy clinic visits. However, participants concurred that nurses would require a solid scientific foundation to fulfill this role successfully. As one participant remarked,

“We can help a lot because the patients talk to us with ease during the waiting time of their visit, but we need appropriate training and a scientific background.” (34, Female, Nurse).

Physicians also pointed out the potential involvement of junior physicians in patient counseling. They believed that house officers could play a valuable role in providing cessation support if such duties were integrated into their early training. One obstetrician emphasized,

“House officers should be more involved in providing counseling as a part of their training besides clinical skill training,” (38, Female, Obstetrician).

Overall, participants recognized that enhancing capacity through organized, evidence-based training addressing both scientific understanding and communication skills is crucial for empowering healthcare providers to assist pregnant women and their families in quitting smoking.

Discussion

Physicians in high-income countries are frequently at the center of providing smoking cessation counseling [18], while their colleagues in low- and middle-income nations (LMICs) typically have a less prominent role [19]. This discrepancy most likely results from a complex interaction of factors that make it difficult to routinely integrate counseling into antenatal care in LMIC settings. These factors include limited training, cultural sensitivities, high patient load, and the lack of institutional guidelines.

Nearly every participant had reasonable knowledge about the risks of smoking to the health of both the mother and the fetus, as reported by previous studies in Egypt [20]. HCPs agreed that compared to passive smoking, active smoking poses a greater risk of harm to a pregnant woman and her unborn child. While some participants stated that passive smoking was equally as harmful as active smoking, and a few stated that they could not decide. This result reveals a downplaying of the risk associated with tobacco smoking. It conflicted with that reported by Radwan et al., also in Egypt, who stated that most hospital employees knew that more exposure to second-hand smoke (SHS) was hazardous to health [15].The cause for the belief in our results may have arisen because some participants did not believe in the harm of tobacco smoking during pregnancy, with their opinion attributable to the fact that they may not have observed these complications in their practice, which is consistent with other studies in Egypt and neighboring countries, which have reported that HCPs have vague or inaccurate knowledge about the risk of SHS and poor counselling practice in relation to SHS exposure [21–23].

This research showed that participants’ understanding of the side effects of active smoking on maternal and fetal health was diverse but typically adequate. Common risks include low birth weight, early delivery, and respiratory problems, which were mentioned by many medical professionals. Eldein et al. [14] found that although family physicians in Egypt had positive attitudes toward smoking cessation counseling, their actual knowledge and practice levels were below ideal, with reports of just 44% and 45.3%, respectively. The participants’ clinical backgrounds could be a reasonable explanation for the increased knowledge levels seen in our study. They are more likely to encounter high-risk pregnancies and unfavorable outcomes since they are obstetricians and nurses employed at tertiary referral institutions, potentially increasing their awareness of tobacco-related complications compared to primary care physicians, which agrees with previous studies where better scores of knowledge were found among the older and more qualified physicians [14, 24].

Participants had varied opinions on the harms associated with cigarette smoking compared to water-pipe tobacco smoking. There is substantial evidence in the region regarding misconceptions about the safety of water-pipe use [25], often shaped by cultural acceptance and the incorrect belief that water filtration diminishes toxicity. This indicates that clinical education initiatives should specifically focus on water-pipe use, not only cigarettes, given its widespread prevalence and social acceptance in numerous Arab communities.

Participants’ attitude to the importance of smoking cessation counseling among pregnant women varied: some thought that pregnancy was generally the best time for that decision, which is supported by other studies [26–28]. Other participants had more pessimistic views, stating that pregnant women will never be able to stop smoking and that obstetric care is more important. This aligns with the findings of Mahoto et al. [29]which indicated that prioritizing the patients’ urgent healthcare needs was more important than providing smoking cessation counseling.

Participants’ opinions on the value of smoking cessation counseling for women ranged from the need to provide support to all pregnant smokers during prenatal care sessions to simply offering aid to those who request it. Due to the large volume of pregnant patients attending prenatal care clinics, health care professionals reported that time constraints were the main barrier to providing smoking cessation counseling. Findings from other studies indicate that time limitations have been identified as a factor causing healthcare professionals to omit smoking cessation counseling [26, 29]. This contrasts with a study where half of the HCPs disagree that time constraints are an issue for them [30].

It was interesting that most of our participants stated that they only asked about a woman’s smoking status if they noticed signs of smoking, with a few stating that they did not ask at all, and only a minority stated that they asked directly. This result may reflect cultural sensitivity, as was discussed with some participants. In Egypt, these difficulties are especially evident. Limited availability of formal training in tobacco-dependence treatment, cultural attitudes towards women smoking, and the heavy patient load in public antenatal clinics all lead to a decrease in counseling frequency. Social stigma has a unique impact in Egypt, where female smoking—particularly during pregnancy—is often viewed as disgraceful. This stigma can lead both women and healthcare providers to be reluctant to engage in open discussions about tobacco use. Moreover, systemic limitations such as restricted husband visits during antenatal appointments may diminish opportunities for couple-based counseling, even though paternal smoking is a significant source of household exposure. The absence of established guidelines and standardized screening processes further exacerbates these challenges, hindering providers’ ability to integrate smoking-cessation counseling into routine maternal care [21, 31, 32]. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends, whenever possible, that pregnant smokers be offered person-to-person psychosocial interventions that exceed basic advice to quit [5]. It is recommended that every patient who presents to a healthcare facility be asked if she/he uses tobacco and that all patients have their tobacco use documented regularly. Evidence has shown that clinic screening systems (e.g., expanding the vital signs to include tobacco status) or using other reminder systems (e.g., chart stickers or computer prompts) can significantly increase intervention rates by healthcare providers [33].

While most healthcare providers recommended complete quitting, some suggested merely reducing cigarette consumption. A small number of HCPs felt that cessation counseling should be the responsibility of chest specialists instead of obstetric healthcare providers. These findings point to a need for clearer roles and policies within institutions to ensure that smoking cessation is incorporated into routine prenatal care rather than perceived as an ancillary or specialized service. The realization that participants at one hospital were not aware of their own health system’s smoking cessation program further highlights communication issues. It emphasizes the necessity of establishing clear referral pathways. Previous research in Egypt has demonstrated that trained and nonsmoking physicians have shown better counseling performance [33], which underscores the importance of targeted professional development.

Collectively, these insights stress the need to enhance workforce capability, implement systematic screening and documentation practices, and align prenatal care protocols with global cessation recommendations. To improve smoking cessation support within Egyptian maternity services, a multifaceted approach is essential: enhancing provider training, developing consistent workflow systems, addressing cultural barriers to disclosure, and raising awareness of available cessation resources. By integrating these components into the existing maternal health framework, it may be possible to address current challenges and facilitate more consistent and effective counseling for expectant mothers.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be approached with caution. The sample was small and purposive, consisting of healthcare providers from only four hospitals in Cairo. This non-random sampling limits how well the results can be applied to other healthcare settings. Additionally, the study was conducted in a single urban area—Cairo—which may not fully represent the experiences, resources, or cultural differences found in rural or other urban regions of Egypt. The data were solely based on self-reported opinions from in-depth interviews, without observational data to verify the practices described by participants. This could lead to reporting or social desirability biases, especially given the sensitive topic. Finally, cultural norms and the hierarchical nature of healthcare in Egypt may have influenced participants’ willingness to discuss challenges or critique existing systems. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, few studies in Egypt have employed qualitative methods to explore HCPs’ beliefs, attitudes, and practices regarding smoking cessation and the barriers they encounter. Therefore, this research provides valuable initial insights into an underexplored area and suggests potential directions for future research.

Conclusion

The current situation for public health in Egypt presents a considerable challenge to implementing a smoking cessation intervention. However, this should not prevent doctors from adopting the recommendations that clinicians should offer simple strategies that take up only a few minutes of their time. Many evidence-based smoking cessation strategies are currently available that can be integrated into routine prenatal care for all pregnant women. To do so, doctors may need to be convinced that these interventions can be effective and that even small increases in smoking cessation rates have clinical significance.

Policy implications

To support smoking cessation efforts, Egypt’s public health policies should give priority to incorporating brief counseling into national maternal health recommendations during prenatal care. This is achieved by giving healthcare professionals specialized training, creating transparent protocols, and guaranteeing that they have access to resources for support and referrals. While public awareness campaigns can support clinical messages and encourage pregnant women to quit smoking, allocating resources and setting up monitoring mechanisms can also assist in maintaining these efforts.

Practice implications

This study identifies several doable actions that medical professionals can take to enhance smoking cessation assistance during pregnancy, even in LMICs with limited resources. Overcoming stigma and promoting disclosure can be achieved by normalizing regular conversations regarding active and passive smoking, provided in a nonjudgmental and culturally sensitive way. To increase effectiveness, brief counseling models like the “Ask, Advise, Assist” and “Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange” 3 A’s/5A’s should be incorporated into prenatal sessions. Junior physicians and nurses, who frequently spend more time with patients, can be extremely helpful in providing these therapies. In clinic waiting rooms, visual aids like posters and videos can spark discussions and reinforce ideas. Crucially, messages may be more effective if they are customized to appeal to local values, such as preserving religious beliefs or safeguarding the infant. If regularly used, these easy, inexpensive techniques can greatly improve the caliber and accessibility of smoking cessation counseling in standard maternal care.
