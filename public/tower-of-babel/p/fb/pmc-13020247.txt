Integrative transcriptomic profiling links telomere dysfunction to cGAS–STING activation in heart failure signatures in mice and humans
Authors: Moritz Brandt, Sana’a Khraisat, Qi Luo, Megan Mayerle, Uwe Raaz, Phil Tsao, Thomas Münzel, Philipp Lurz, Philip Wenzel, Helen M Blau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsCardiomyocyte telomere shortening is evident during heart failure pathogenesis. Conversely, mice with engineered telomerase deficiency develop myocardial dysfunction accompanied by p53 activation and mitochondrial repression. We aimed to address fundamental gaps in knowledge: whether cardiac dysfunction in mice lacking telomerase components arises from myocardial-intrinsic effects or systemic consequences of telomere shortening, which broader transcriptional programs follow, and what implications these have for clinical heart failure.Methods and resultsWe generated telomerase-deficient mice until generation 5 (mTRG5) and confirmed shortening telomeres along with increasing cardiac dysfunction by cardiovascular phenotyping and cardiomyocyte mitochondrial function assessment. Transcriptional and regulator analysis confirmed the telomere -p53 -mitochondria axis but extended beyond it, revealing additional involvement of neurohumoral activation, senescence, and inflammation, notably type I interferon signaling. To contextualize these findings, we compared this profile with hypertensive heart failure induced by neurohumoral dysregulation (angiotensin II infusion, nephrectomy, salt overload; ANS) and established a transcriptional hierarchy. In mTRG5 mice, regulators of telomere dysfunction and p53 activation ranked highest by significance and centrality, supporting telomere shortening as the primary upstream driver. Reciprocally, ANS mice showed higher-ranking neurohumoral regulators, indicating these govern secondary pathways. To pursue the strong type I interferon profile, we utilized myocardial profiles of mice lacking three-prime exonuclease 1, an established activator of the cGAS -STING pathway. Matching the profiles, we confirmed pronounced activation of cGAS -STING in mTRG5 mice –and slightly less in ANS mice –providing first evidence for cGAS -STING activation in telomere shortening and heart failure. Integration of the mTRG5 signature with curated transcriptomic datasets from murine and human dilated and ischemic cardiomyopathy revealed robust cross-species and cross-etiology overlap that increased with heart failure severity and decreased with functional recovery.ConclusionThese findings establish telomere dysfunction as a pathogenic driver that engages innate immune signaling via the cGAS -STING pathway, with direct relevance to human heart failure.Keywords: Telomere shortening, Heart failure, cGAS-STING pathway, Neurohumoral dysregulation

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome with diverse aetiologies, yet cardiomyocyte telomere shortening has been consistently observed across multiple forms of HF.1–3 Telomeres, the protective caps at chromosome ends, progressively shorten with cell division and are highly vulnerable to oxidative stress and DNA damage. In healthy ageing, cardiomyocyte telomere length remains stable1,4 whereas pathological remodelling markedly accelerates telomere attrition: failing human hearts exhibit significant cardiomyocyte telomere loss compared with age-matched controls.1,3,5,6 In adult cardiomyocytes—given their post-mitotic state, which precludes replicative senescence7—telomere shortening appears to reflect cumulative cellular stress rather than replicative turnover.

Genetically modified mouse models have provided evidence that telomere shortening may, in turn, contribute causally to myocardial dysfunction. Mice lacking key components of the telomerase complex, such as TERT or TERC (mTR), exhibit ‘engineered’ progressive telomere shortening over generations, together with cardiac dysfunction. These models recapitulate central features of HF, including reduced contractility, while activation of the p53 tumour suppressor pathway and repression of mitochondrial biogenesis suggest a link between telomere dysfunction and metabolic remodelling.8,9

Despite these advances, key questions remain unresolved. Although p53 activation and mitochondrial repression have been documented, the knockout of p53 only partially rescued the mitochondrial defects observed in TERT-deficient mice,8 such that additional pathways are also likely to be involved. Most importantly, a comprehensive landscape of transcriptional and signalling changes linked to cardiomyocyte telomere dysfunction has not been characterized, preventing the discovery of pathogenic mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. Furthermore, whether the cardiac dysfunction observed in telomerase-deficient mice is due to cardiomyocyte-intrinsic effects or results from systemic or extracardiac consequences of telomere dysfunction is a matter of debate. Finally, although mouse models implicate the telomere–p53–mitochondria axis in cardiac dysfunction, it remains uncertain whether these mechanisms translate to human HF. While several studies report shortened telomeres in patients with HF,3,10 it is unclear whether this is associated with similar transcriptional alterations or related molecular signatures observed in experimental models.

As a prerequisite to addressing the current gaps of knowledge and to define the cellular consequences of telomere shortening, we corroborated the cardiac phenotype down to the cellular level and performed cardiomyocyte transcriptional profiling. We used mTR mice rather than TERT-deficient models to avoid confounding effects of TERT’s non-canonical functions, such as mitochondrial translocation and antioxidant properties.11

We then compared transcriptional profiles with a model of hypertensive HF induced by aggravated neurohumoral activation to determine whether these effects are cardiomyocyte-intrinsic or systemic. To further characterize the transcriptional landscape of cardiomyocytes under telomere shortening and to validate identified regulatory pathways, we investigated whether telomere attrition activates innate immune signalling by analysing regulatory overlap with TREX1-deficient hearts. Finally, to evaluate translational relevance, we integrated our findings with curated transcriptomic datasets from murine and human HF. This design allowed us to clarify the mechanistic role of telomere dysfunction, establish regulatory hierarchies, and identify conserved pathways across species and disease aetiologies.

Methods

Animals

All animal studies complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, NIH guidelines, and were approved by the Stanford University Administrative Panel on Laboratory Animal Care or the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Mainz and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Landesuntersuchungsamt Rheinland-Pfalz, Koblenz, Germany; Tierversuchsantrag G 17-1-03).

mTR mice: Male Terc-deficient mice (mTR−/−) were generated from C57BL/6 mTR+/− mice as described.8,12 Progressive telomere shortening was achieved by intercrossing successive generations,13 yielding mTRG2 (modest shortening) and mTRG5 (advanced attrition) mice housed under SPF conditions on a 12-h light/dark cycle. For the experiments, male adult mice of 6–8 months were used to align our experiments with previous results.14 This age corresponds to early-to-mid adult human age and avoids late-life ageing effects that could obscure telomere-driven phenotypes.

ANS mice: Male C57BL/6 mice (10–12 weeks) were subjected to unilateral nephrectomy followed by implantation of an AngII-infusing osmotic mini-pump (1.2 mg/kg/day; ALZET) with 1% NaCl in drinking water ad libitum, for 6 weeks, as described.5 Control mice underwent sham surgery by exposing the left kidney without removal (‘Sham’). At the time point of organ collection, ANS mice or sham mice were 16–18 weeks (3–4 months) old.

Echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized with 1–2% isoflurane in 0.2 L/min 100% O₂, initially in a chamber and then via facemask. Ultrasound imaging was performed on a heated, rail-mounted table (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Bothell, WA, USA)) using a Vevo 2100 with a 400 MHz transducer. Heart rate and temperature (maintained at 37°C) were monitored. Left ventricular structure, fractional shortening, and strain were assessed.

Non-invasive blood pressure measurement

Blood pressure was measured in conscious, restrained mice using a tail-cuff plethysmography system (CODA, Kent Scientific, Torrington, CT, USA). Mice were acclimated to restraint and a pre-warmed platform (32–34°C) for 3 days prior to measurement, with chocolate cream applied at the front of the restrainer to reduce stress and improve compliance. On the measurement day, mice were placed on the heated platform, and systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressures were recorded using the manufacturer’s software. Each session included 10 acclimation and 15–20 measurement cycles; the average of ≥10 valid readings per mouse was used for analysis. The operator was blinded to the experimental groups.

Isolation, purification, and culture of adult murine cardiomyocytes

Mice received buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg, s.c.) 30 min before euthanasia. Deep anaesthesia was induced with isoflurane (3% in 2 L/min O₂, chamber) and maintained via facemask (2–3%), followed by cervical dislocation. Hearts were excised, and adult murine cardiomyocytes were isolated as described15 by collagenase type II coronary perfusion, gentle trituration, and size-based centrifugation. After gradual calcium reintroduction, cells were used for RNA isolation or plated on laminin-coated dishes in MEM-based plating medium, and later switched to serum-free culture medium.

Live-cell imaging of mitochondrial membrane potential and redox state using tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM+) and autofluorescence-based redox ratio

Cardiomyocytes were incubated with defined energy substrates—low (5 mM) or high (20 mM) glucose, or palmitoyl-carnitine (10 µM)—for 60 min before live-cell imaging. Mitochondria were labelled with tetramethylrhodamin-methylester (TMRM⁺) (10 nM, 30 min, 37°C, 2% CO₂, dark) to assess membrane potential. Imaging was performed in phenol red-free medium using an automated fluorescence imager (ImageXpress Micro, 4× objective, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) with laser autofocus and environmental control. TMRM⁺ fluorescence was acquired with 589/632 nm filters and a cooled charge-coupled device camera. Fluorescence intensity was quantified in FIJI/ImageJ after background subtraction; only particles >200 pixels were analysed. Images were pseudo-coloured for display. To assess redox state, autofluorescence imaging of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydride (NADH) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) was performed using the DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) excitation/emission filter sets respectively. The optical redox ratio [FAD/(NADH + FAD)] was calculated from processed images to quantify mitochondrial redox status. All imaging and analysis were standardized to ensure reproducibility.

Quantitative fluorescent in situ hybridization (QFISH) and high-throughput Q-FISH (HT-QFISH) in purified cardiomyocytes

Q-FISH was performed on purified cardiomyocytes plated on coverslips or 48-/96-well plates. After fixation in Carnoy’s fixative (methanol:acetic acid 3:1), cells were dehydrated in graded ethanol, air-dried, and hybridized with Cy3-labelled TelC-peptide nucleic acid (PNA) probe (10 nM; Bio-Synthesis, Lewisville, TX, USA) after denaturation (86°C, 6 min) for 2.5 h at room temperature in the dark. Slides were washed twice in PNA wash I (70% formamide, 20 mM Tris, pH 7.5) and three times in PNA wash II (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, 0.05% Tween-20), then mounted in DAPI medium (ProLong Diamond, Life Technologies, Eugene, OR, USA). Imaging was performed on an automated high-content microscope (ImageXpress, Molecular Devices), and telomere fluorescence was quantified using FIJI/ImageJ macros, normalized to centromere/DAPI signal.

Transcriptomic profiling and differential gene expression analysis

Total RNA was isolated from purified cardiomyocytes with shortened telomeres (mTRG2, mTRG5, C57BL/6J) using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) and analysed on the Affymetrix Mouse Gene ST 1.0 array at the Stanford Functional Genomics Facility. Differential expression was assessed using TAC 4.0.1, with significance defined as P < 0.05 and log₂ fold change >1 or <−1.

For RNA-seq of ANS vs. Sham-treated C57BL/6 mice, myocardial RNA was extracted using NucleoSpin RNA Plus I (Macherey-Nagel), verified for integrity (RIN >8.8), and processed via Novogene, GmbH, library was prepared with poly A enrichment of transcripts. Libraries were sequenced (150 bp paired-end) using NovaSeq X PlusSeries with a minimum of 9 Gb of data per sample, data processed using FastQC, aligned to GRCm39, and DESeq2 for differential expression.16

Expression data from TREX1⁻/⁻ and C57BL/6 mouse hearts were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (GSE59219), generated using the Illumina MouseWG-6 v2.0 platform (GPL6887) as described in the original study.17 Total RNA was isolated from heart tissue using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA quality was assessed with a Bioanalyzer RNA 6000 Nano Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Gene expression profiling was performed on Illumina Sentrix BeadChip arrays (MouseWG-6 v2.0) at the Oncogenomics Core Facility, University of Miami. Raw intensity values were processed in GeneSpring (Agilent) using quantile normalization and log2 transformation to the sample median. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by Student’s t-test with a significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05.

Upstream regulator analysis and network inference

Pathway and upstream regulator analyses were performed using QIAGEN Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA, QIAGEN Inc.) using default parameters unless otherwise stated. Analyses were conducted using a licensed version of IPA. For each transcriptional regulator, the overlap between its known targets and DEGs in the mTR-/- vs. B6 comparison was assessed using two metrics: the overlap P-value (Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.01) and the activation z-score, which infers regulator activity based on literature-supported directionality of gene expression changes.18

To evaluate similarity with failing myocardium signatures, IPA’s Analysis Match algorithm was applied. This compares analysis-specific signatures—sets of activated or inhibited entities (z ≥ ±2, P ≤ 0.05)—across four categories: Canonical Pathways, upstream regulators (URs), causal networks, and diseases and functions (up to 50 entities each).18 Match strength is quantified using a composite z-score: z = (N⁺ − N⁻)/√N, where N⁺ and N⁻ are the number of matching and anti-matching entities among N total overlaps. Additional metrics included the UR P-value (log₁₀-transformed) and an overall P-value score, normalized by comparing to a self-match benchmark. For the graphical summary (see Supplementary material online, Figure S2), all interaction edges were sourced from the QIAGEN Knowledge Base and supported by literature, canonical sources, or textbooks. Gene orthologs across species (mouse, rat, and human) were unified as single nodes. The network depicts all direct interactions of angiotensinogen (AGT) and interleukin (IL)-6, with indirect links shown as faded edges.

Analysis of regulatory influence in transcriptional regulatory networks

To assess the hierarchical position of transcriptional regulators, a regulatory influence score was calculated for each UR identified by IPA (QIAGEN), defined as: regulatory influence = out-degree—in-degree, where out-degree is the number of DEGs regulated by the UR, and in-degree is the number of regulators predicted to influence it, based on the QIAGEN Knowledge Base. High regulatory influence indicates a regulator likely acting near the top of the transcriptional hierarchy. The regulatory influence was computed for all URs with |z| ≥ 2 and P < 0.01 in both mTRG5 vs. control and ANS vs. control datasets and visualized alongside −log10(P-value) and z-scores to contextualize their regulatory influence.

Comparative transcriptomic overlap between telomere-shortened cardiomyocytes and HF datasets (dilated and ischaemic cardiomyopathy) across mouse and human models

To assess the clinical relevance of the mTR-/- vs. B6 transcriptional signature, comparisons were made to archived HF datasets from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). All datasets were ethically approved under the Declaration of Helsinki, with confirmation of informed consent and institutional review board oversight noted in the referenced publications (Table 1). Similarity between mTR-/- and HF profiles was evaluated using available IPA-derived metrics: UR z-score, UR P-value, overall z-score, and overall P-value. To focus on relevant cardiac phenotypes, ‘Analysis Match’ results were filtered using tissue-specific terms (‘heart, myocardium, cardio, ventric’) and disease-specific terms (‘dilated, DCM’ for dilated cardiomyopathy; ‘ischem, infarct’ for ischaemic cardiomyopathy [ICM]). Genotype, aetiology, and cardiac function data were retrieved from GEO and summarized per dataset, with GEO accession numbers (GSE####) provided for reference.

Results

The extent of cardiac dysfunction and mitochondrial impairment observed in mTR−/− mice increases with the extent of telomere shortening

In mice lacking functional telomerase, telomeres shorten by 4.8 ± 2.4 kilobases per generation, with telomere-related dysfunction emerging from the fourth generation onward.13 High-throughput quantitative fluorescence in situ hybridization (HT-qFISH) confirmed this trajectory in our model, mTR-/- mice bred on a C57BL/6 background (Figure 1A) and showed telomere attrition in cardiomyocytes across successive generations (Figure 1B). While second-generation mTR-/- mice (mTRG2) exhibited preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), fifth-generation mice (mTRG5) demonstrated a significant reduction of LVEF compared to C57BL/6 controls (Figure 1C), indicating the development of overt cardiac dysfunction. In contrast, left ventricular global longitudinal strain—a more sensitive measure of even subclinical myocardial impairment20—was significantly impaired already in earlier generations (mTRG2, Figure 1D).

The marked reduction in mitochondrial polarization and the presence of a largely stable oxidative redox ratio (ORR) imply that while mitochondrial redox pools remain relatively balanced, the ability to sustain proton motive force is impaired. This indicates a state of partial bioenergetic uncoupling rather than complete oxidative shutdown, likely reflecting structural or functional defects in the respiratory chain and inner mitochondrial membrane integrity. Based on these findings, we focused on mTRG5 as a model of advanced telomere dysfunction with established cardiac impairment. Beyond the HFrEF phenotype, the mTR model exhibits systemic, including cardiovascular, alterations. Blood pressure recordings showed an increase in mean arterial pressure to ∼125 mmHg in mTRG5 from baseline values of ∼100 mmHg in C57BL/6 animals (Figure 1E), consistent with previous findings that the mTR model is associated with increased endothelin signalling.21 This degree of hypertension alone is insufficient to induce an HFrEF phenotype, as demonstrated by several studies in which angiotensin II-induced hypertension or similar models did not progress to systolic failure without additional stressors.22–25

Cardiomyocyte telomere attrition alters the cardiac transcriptome

To investigate how telomere shortening impacts the cardiac transcriptome, we performed competitive microarray analysis using cDNA derived from total RNA isolated from cardiomyocytes of mTRG2 and mTRG5 mice compared to B6 controls (Figure 1A). Mapping the DEGs to their chromosomal loci revealed no specific accumulation, conveying an overall genome-wide transcriptional response (Figure 2B).

Among the most notable findings was the marked downregulation of myomiR family members—miR-1a-1/2 and miR-133a-1/2—both in early (data not shown) and advanced generations of mTR-deficient mice (Figure 2C). Previous functional studies have shown that suppression of miR-133 in vivo is sufficient to induce sustained hypertrophy and foetal gene reactivation, whereas its overexpression mitigates remodelling and fibrosis, confirming its causal role in structural adaptation26,27 with detrimental consequences following downregulation upon telomere attrition. These observations indicate that telomere erosion converges on well-characterized growth-control nodes.

The strongest upregulated genes included C3 and Cfh (complement factor H), both integral to the inflammatory complement cascade. We also observed upregulation of PAI-1 (Serpina3n), Lyz2 (a lysosomal phospholipase), and inflammation-associated genes such as COX-2 (Ptgs2), Cxcl13, and IL-6, alongside AGT (angiotensinogen), the sole precursor of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) (Figure 2C). With RAAS’s central role in cardiovascular and renal regulation, these findings suggest that cardiomyocyte telomere dysfunction activates a gene network linking inflammation, neurohumoral signalling, and cellular stress responses.

To better understand the transcriptional drivers of this response, we performed UR analysis based on the overlap between DEGs and curated regulator-target gene sets (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1). This revealed key URs shaping the mTRG5 transcriptional landscape (Figure 2D). TP53 and PGC-1α (PPARGC1A), core components of the telomere–p53–mitochondrial axis, ranked among the most prominent regulators, consistent with previous models.8 However, regulators with even greater significance and directional activity included pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF, TGFB1, IL1B, IL6) and neurohumoral mediators such as AGT. Together, these findings point to a regulatory network that integrates cellular ageing and senescence with neurohumoral activation and inflammation.

Strikingly, multiple regulators involved in nucleic acid sensing, DNA/RNA damage repair, and type I interferon (IFN) signalling were also perturbed. IRF2BP2 (a repressor of IFN-stimulated genes) and Irgm1 (a mitochondrial stress-responsive GTPase) were both downregulated, suggesting impaired repression of IFN signalling. In parallel, TREX1 and RNASEH2B, both crucial for clearing endogenous nucleic acids, were also suppressed. These changes likely contribute to the observed upregulation of IFN-α, consistent with activation of a type I IFN response. The combined pattern—loss of nucleic acid clearance, de-repression of IFN signalling, and elevation of IFN-α—resembles a type I interferonopathy-like state with potential for persistent inflammation and immune activation.28

This broadened understanding of the transcriptional consequences of cardiomyocyte telomere shortening reopened the question to what extent cardiac dysfunction would be driven by intrinsic telomere dysfunction vs. secondary, systemic signalling effects in our model.

We identified DEGs in ANS vs. B6 hearts using RNA-sequencing and performed regulatory analysis to identify upstream transcriptional drivers (Figure 3C). We focused on two key regulator groups: those related to telomere biology and TP53 signalling, and those associated with neurohumoral activation. For each regulator, we quantified statistical significance (Fisher’s P-value for target overlap), directionality (z-score), and network positioning by calculating regulator influence—defined as the difference between the number of regulators it targets (‘out-degree’) and the number of URs targeting it (‘in-degree’).

Regulators related to telomere function and TP53 signalling displayed greater statistical significance, higher directionality (mean z-scores) (Figure 3D middle), and greater regulator influence in the mTRG5 vs. B6 comparison than in the ANS vs. B6 dataset (Figure 3D, left and right). In the mTRG5 vs. B6 dataset, these regulators lacked upstream inputs, indicating a position high in the transcriptional hierarchy in telomere-dysfunctional hearts.

In contrast, neurohumoral activation-related regulators showed the opposite trend—greater significance, directionality, and regulatory influence in the ANS vs B6 dataset—placing them functionally upstream in the neurohumoral model, but further downstream in the telomere dysfunction context (Figure 3E).

These findings imply that the transcriptional architecture of mTRG5 vs. B6 is primarily shaped by intrinsic telomere dysfunction, with regulators of telomere maintenance and TP53 signalling occupying upstream positions in the regulatory network, functioning as primary drivers of the observed gene expression changes. In contrast, in the ANS model, driven by enforced neurohumoral stress, regulators of neurohumoral signalling occupy the upstream hierarchy, while telomere-associated regulators appear further downstream. This shift supports a model in which neurohumoral activation can secondarily contribute to telomere dysfunction, whereas in telomere-compromised hearts, neurohumoral activation may emerge as a downstream consequence of intrinsic genomic instability.

Comparison to the profile of three prime exonuclease 1-deficient mice reveals a pronounced cGAS–STING activation signature in telomere-shortened cardiomyocytes

Within the newly described transcriptional landscape of telomere-shortened cardiomyocytes, DNA/RNA repair (Figure 2D) and type I IFN signalling emerged as prominent features, processes centrally positioned at the interface between genome integrity and innate immune activation. Chronic inflammation is well-documented, but innate immune activation is usually attributed to cytokines, NF-κB, or TLR pathways, not type I IFN. However, in mouse models, TREX1 has proven essential for maintaining nucleic acid homeostasis through the degradation of cytosolic DNA, thereby preventing aberrant activation of innate immune pathways.29 In mice, TREX1 deficiency leads to spontaneous, severe inflammatory myocarditis with extensive lymphocytic infiltration, progressive DCM, circulatory failure, and premature death.30 These pathological features are dependent on self-DNA sensing via the cGAS–STING–type I IFN axis, as genetic ablation of cGAS or STING rescues the phenotype and prevents lethality.31 We analysed publicly available transcriptional profiles from TREX1⁻/⁻ vs. C57BL/6 (B6) mouse hearts (Figure 4B) and inferred upstream regulatory activity (Figure 4C). The analysis confirmed a pronounced activation of regulators associated with the cGAS–STING pathway and type I IFN signalling, as expected for TREX1 deficiency (Figure 4D, right). Remarkably, the mTRG5 vs. B6 comparison exhibited a similar regulatory activation pattern, mirroring the strong cGAS–STING signature observed in TREX1⁻/⁻ hearts (Figure 4D, left). Gene-level examination revealed overlap of the DEGs, indicating that the robust cGAS–STING activation in mTRG5 is mediated through a distinct, though functionally convergent, transcriptional profile (Figure 4E).

cGAS-STING in mice with aggravated neurohumoral activation

Returning to the transcriptional profile of mice with aggravated neurohumoral activation (ANS vs. sham), we confirmed significant activation of the cGAS–STING signalling pathway, which was further validated at the protein level (Figure 5). The observed lower statistical significance and reduced directionality in mTRG5 compared to TREX1⁻/⁻, and in ANS compared to both TREX1⁻/⁻ and mTRG5, suggest a hierarchical pattern of engagement. In TREX1 deficiency, activation is primary, driven by the engineered loss of TREX1 and direct cytosolic DNA accumulation. The progressively lower activation signals in mTRG5 and ANS imply that cGAS–STING activation occurs further downstream in the signalling cascade, likely reflecting secondary effects of telomere shortening in mTRG5 and tertiary activation in ANS, potentially linked to telomere shortening resulting from stress-induced DNA damage or impaired DNA repair mechanisms. The discovery of cGAS–STING pathway activation across the three models and the implicated clear hierarchy from primary activation in TREX1⁻/⁻ to more downstream activation in mTRG5 and in ANS underscores that telomere shortening and related stress do not solely perturb p53–PGC1α signalling but also engage innate immune pathways.

Translational relevance of telomere shortening: integration with human and murine HF profiles

To determine whether our findings in mTRG5 mice with advanced telomere shortening have a clinically meaningful correlate in human HF, we performed a bioinformatic integration of our transcriptomic dataset with publicly available profiles from mouse models of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), ischaemic cardiomyopathy (ICM), and from human DCM and ICM cohorts (Figure 6). From >108 000 datasets available in the IPA Winter 2022 release, we identified 41 DCM datasets (12 murine, 29 human) and 57 ICM datasets (16 murine, 41 human). Of these, 36 met predefined inclusion criteria (sufficient phenotypic annotation and statistical power) and were retained for analysis.

With one exception (GSE42955.1), the transcriptomic profile of mTRG5 vs. B6 exhibited significant overlap with failing myocardial tissue (HF vs. control) across diverse models and patient samples, as indicated by strong positive correlations (high UR z-scores and overall z-scores; Figure 6A, red circles) and high statistical significance of overlap (low UR P-values and overall P-scores). Importantly, in a subgroup of datasets representing aggravated HF, defined as clinical or experimental exacerbation of pre-existing HF, we observed an even stronger correlation with the mTRG5 profile (Figure 6A, orange circles). When these aggravated HF samples were compared relative to less severe HF samples, we found a positive correlation (Figure 6A, dark red circles), indicating that the degree of transcriptomic similarity to mTRG5 increases with HF severity. In contrast, mTRG5 showed a negative correlation with datasets from mice or patients exhibiting improved cardiac function after therapeutic or experimental interventions (Figure 6A and B, bright green circles). Similarly, comparison of attenuated HF states vs. control revealed an anti-correlation relative to baseline HF vs. control profiles (Figure 6A, dark green circles). These findings underscore that the mTRG5 signature aligns with progressive and aggravated HF states but diverges under conditions of recovery or functional improvement.

Among these regulators, TP53 activation and PGC1α repression emerged as highly consistent findings across all profiles, with significant upregulation of TP53 and downregulation of PGC1α in the telomere-shortened mTRG5 profile and in previously published murine and human DCM and ICM datasets (Figure 7B). In addition, regulators integrating neurohumoral activation (e.g. angiotensinogen), inflammation (e.g. nuclear factor ‘kappa-light-chain-enhancer’ of activated B-cells (NFκB), IFNα), and cellular senescence (e.g. IL-6) were consistently altered in the same direction across nearly all HF datasets. Confirming our identification and the broadened transcriptional landscape induced by cardiomyocyte telomere shortening, regulators associated with genome stability, DNA repair, and type I IFN signalling—including TREX1, RNASEH2B, and IFNα—also exhibited concordant dysregulation in these profiles (Figure 7A). These findings underscore that telomere shortening triggers a transcriptional programme that overlaps extensively with core regulatory networks driving human and murine HF, spanning mitochondrial dysfunction, neurohumoral stress, inflammation, and genomic instability.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive transcriptomic and regulatory network analysis of telomere-shortened cardiomyocytes, revealing that telomere dysfunction initiates a broad gene expression programme extending beyond the canonical p53–PGC-1α axis to include inflammation, neurohumoral activation, and, notably, innate immune signalling via the cGAS–STING pathway. By comparing telomerase-deficient (mTRG5) mice to a neurohumoral HF model (ANS), we establish that telomere-related regulators occupy upstream positions in the transcriptional hierarchy, supporting a cardiomyocyte-intrinsic mechanism of dysfunction. Importantly, integration with murine and human HF datasets demonstrates cross-species and cross-aetiology concordance, especially in advanced disease stages, affirming the translational relevance of telomere-driven molecular remodelling in HF pathogenesis. These findings fill critical gaps by defining the broader transcriptional consequences of telomere shortening, clarifying causality, and identifying immune pathways as potential therapeutic targets.

Telomere shortening and cardiomyocyte transcriptomic remodelling

Telomere attrition in cardiomyocytes provokes a broad transcriptional reprogramming that extends beyond the previously recognized p53–PGC-1α axis. In our telomerase-null mice with advanced telomere erosion, we observed downregulation of muscle-specific microRNAs (myomiRs miR-1 and miR-133) and upregulation of inflammatory and stress-response genes (e.g. complement factors C3 and Cfh, cyclooxygenase-2, IL-6, and angiotensinogen). This transcriptional landscape suggests that telomere dysfunction engages pathways of inflammation, innate immunity, and neurohumoral stress in addition to canonical DNA damage responses. The repression of myomiRs is notable, as these microRNAs regulate cardiomyocyte contractile protein expression; their downregulation may contribute to the contractile deficits seen in telomere-compromised hearts. Concurrently, the prominent induction of inflammatory genes and the RAAS component angiotensinogen indicates activation of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signalling. These findings put into context earlier observations that failing human hearts show telomere shortening with associated oxidative stress and DNA damage signals1 and align with and extend prior studies, which demonstrated that short telomeres activate p53, which directly downregulates PGC-1 coactivators and causes a cascade of mitochondrial compromise.8 Our findings confirm p53 activation and PGC-1α repression as central features of telomere-shortened cardiomyocytes, but importantly, further show that p53–PGC-1 signalling is only one part of a larger network. The incomplete rescue when p53/PGC-1 pathways are experimentally manipulated in telomerase-knockout mice8 suggested other pathways contribute to telomere-driven cardiomyocyte dysfunction. Our results expand on those observations, revealing that cardiomyocyte telomere attrition can trigger a state resembling ‘sterile inflammation’ within the myocardium, broadening our understanding of telomere shortening beyond a simple DNA damage checkpoint trigger. Instead, it highlights it as a multifaceted stressor that alters gene expression programmes linked to cardiomyocyte structure, metabolism, and immune signalling.

Intrinsic telomere dysfunction vs. extracardiac stress: hierarchy of drivers

Whether cardiac dysfunction in telomerase-deficient mice arises from cardiomyocyte-intrinsic telomere effects or secondary systemic consequences was a matter of debate. By comparing our telomere-dysfunctional model (mTRG5) to a neurohumoral stress-induced HF model, we found clear differences in the hierarchy of regulatory drivers. In telomere-shortened hearts, transcriptional regulators related to telomere integrity, DNA damage, and p53 signalling occupied the top of the network hierarchy—they were highly significant, strongly activated, and acted upstream of other changes. Classic telomere/p53-associated regulators (e.g. TP53, PPARGC1A for PGC-1α) showed the greatest regulatory influence in mTRG5 hearts, meaning they influenced many downstream targets and were not themselves driven by other factors. In contrast, in the neurohumoral activation model of HF (driven by chronic elevation of angiotensin II, salt overload, and chronic kidney disease), extrinsic stress regulators such as angiotensin signalling, NF-κB, and other cytokines were the top drivers, whereas telomere/p53-related signals appeared further downstream. This suggests that in primarily telomere-compromised hearts, the initial trigger is cell-autonomous genomic instability, which then secondarily activates inflammatory and neurohumoral pathways. Conversely, in hearts subjected to primary neurohumoral stress, telomere dysfunction may occur as a downstream consequence (e.g. due to oxidative DNA damage), consistent with evidence that mechanical or oxidative stress can shorten telomeres in post-mitotic cardiomyocytes.1

It is noteworthy that the telomere/p53 axis in mTRG5 hearts also drove secondary activation of well-known HF pathways, such as TNFα, TGF-β, IL-6, and angiotensin II signalling. These would typically be considered compensatory or systemic pathways in HF, but here they appear as downstream consequences of telomere dysfunction.

Telomere attrition as a pathogenic driver: links to cGAS–STING innate immunity

Our findings strongly support a causative, pathogenic role for telomere shortening, particularly through engagement of the cGAS–STING innate immune pathway. We discovered a striking upregulation of IFN-stimulated genes and nucleic acid-sensing enzymes in telomere-dysfunctional hearts, reminiscent of a chronic antiviral-like response. Notably, we found reduced expression of TREX1 and RNASEH2B—enzymes that normally degrade cytosolic DNA/RNA—alongside increased markers of IFN signalling (including Ifna and downstream IFN-stimulated genes). This combination mirrors a cGAS–STING activation signature. In fact, when we compared our mTRG5 transcriptome to that of TREX1−/− hearts (a model of primary cGAS–STING over-activation), the overlap in URs was remarkable: telomere-shortened hearts showed activation of the same IFN regulators and DNA-sensing pathway components that are robustly triggered in TREX1 deficiency. The major difference was one of degree: the cGAS–STING inflammatory response in mTRG5 hearts was strong but not as extreme as in TREX1−/− hearts, suggesting telomere dysfunction engages this pathway indirectly or sub-maximally.

This finding is significant because it connects telomere erosion to sterile inflammation and cardiac injury via innate immunity. The cGAS–STING pathway is a cytosolic DNA sensor mechanism usually defending against viruses, but when activated by nucleic acid species such as self-DNA or telomeric repeat-containing RNA,32 it can drive chronic inflammation and tissue damage. Hypothetically, critically short or damaged telomeres may generate DNA fragments, aberrant DNA structures that escape the nucleus (e.g. as micronuclei or telomeric DNA circles) and trigger cGAS–STING in cardiomyocytes. This concept has been demonstrated for chromatin fragments that pinch off from intact nuclei of fibroblasts during senescence, escape to the cytosol, and activate cGAS-STING, thereby reinforcing senescence and inflammation.33 In a recent organismal study, Şerifoğlu et al. showed that in telomerase-deficient zebrafish, the premature ageing phenotypes were driven by chronic cGAS–STING signalling, while genetic deletion of STING rescued the health span of telomerase-mutant fish despite persistently short telomeres.34 These reports, together with our data, support the notion that telomere attrition is not a passive indicator of cell age but an active instigator of damaging inflammation via innate immune pathways. In the heart, this means that telomere shortening could directly provoke myocardium-intrinsic inflammatory signalling (through cGAS–STING and downstream effectors like NF-κB and IFN regulatory factors), contributing to cardiomyocyte dysfunction, cell death, and fibrosis. Consistently, recent cardiovascular studies have begun to implicate cGAS–STING in HF pathogenesis: for example, genetic ablation of cGAS or STING alleviates cardiac hypertrophy and dysfunction in mouse models of pressure overload35 and diabetic cardiomyopathy.36

Telomere shortening in human HF: clinical relevance

The final question pertains to the clinical significance of myocardial telomere shortening in human HF. Our translational analysis suggests that telomere-associated transcriptional changes are highly relevant to human HF and correlate with disease severity. Using dozens of public datasets, we showed that the mTRG5 transcriptional signature positively correlates with the molecular signatures of advanced HF of failing human hearts from patients with dilated or ischaemic cardiomyopathy. Importantly, this correlation was strongest in the most severe cases—datasets from end-stage or exacerbated HF showed the highest overlap with our telomere-shortened profile, whereas samples from milder HF or recovered hearts (post-treatment) showed much less or even inverse correlation. In other words, the degree to which a heart sample’s transcriptome looks ‘telomere-dysfunctional’ tracks with how sick the heart is. This finding implies that telomere shortening is entwined with the progression to end-stage HF rather than being an incidental feature. It dovetails with clinical observations that patients with HF have significantly shorter cardiomyocyte telomeres compared to healthy individuals. Mourkioti, Sharifi-Sanjani et al. demonstrated that predominantly cardiomyocytes exhibit telomere shortening in failing human hearts,2,3 suggesting a cell-type specific ageing process within diseased myocardium. Our results extend this by showing that those same telomere-associated molecular changes (p53 activation, pro-inflammatory and RAAS signalling, IFN activation) are present across diverse aetiologies of human HF.

The overlap between telomere-dysfunctional and failing heart gene networks also provides evidence that telomere erosion is functionally implicated in the human disease process. Many of the key regulators we identified in telomere-shortened murine hearts (TP53, PGC-1α, NF-κB, IL-6, angiotensin/AGT, IFN-α) are well-known players in human HF pathophysiology. For example, excessive angiotensin II and inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 are hallmarks of maladaptive remodelling and predict worse outcomes in patients.37,38 The fact that these appear as top dysregulated factors in telomere-compromised hearts—and that human failing hearts share this pattern—suggests a common mechanism. Our integrative data now reinforce that telomere shortening is associated with the molecular signature of HF itself, not just with isolated cellular markers.

In summary, the convergence of evidence from our mouse models and human data argues that myocardial telomere shortening is not merely a secondary epiphenomenon but rather a contributing factor to HF pathogenesis. As hearts undergo repeated stress and injury, telomeres erode—even in predominantly non-replicating cells—leading to DNA damage responses, mitochondrial impairment, and, now, we appreciate, innate immune activation. These processes feed into the vicious cycle of myocardial remodelling and failure. Clinically, this raises the possibility that telomere length and integrity could serve as a biomarker for risk stratification in HF (as shorter leucocyte telomeres have been associated with worse HF outcomes in epidemiological studies39) and even as a therapeutic target. Interventions that preserve telomeres or blunt the harmful downstream consequences (e.g. STING inhibitors or anti-IFN therapies) might ameliorate the progression of HF driven by cardiomyocyte senescence. Definitive proof in humans remains challenging and would require mouse models of mono-generational, cardiomyocyte-specific telomere injury. Yet our findings provide a strong mechanistic rationale—telomere attrition is intertwined with the networks that drive HF, supporting a model in which telomere dysfunction actively contributes to cardiac decompensation rather than simply reflecting it.

Limitations

This study has limitations. It uses global telomerase-deficient mice, which differ from humans in telomere biology and may exhibit systemic effects; cardiomyocyte-specific models are needed for definitive proof. Multi-generational breeding to achieve telomere shortening does not mimic the single-lifespan telomere attrition seen in human HF. Transcriptomic analysis of bulk myocardial or enriched cardiomyocyte samples limits cell-type resolution. CGAS–STING activation was inferred from transcriptional signatures without biochemical confirmation (e.g. cGAMP, STING phosphorylation) or functional inhibition studies. Mitochondrial dysfunction was assessed via membrane potential and redox imaging without detailed metabolic assays (e.g. oxygen consumption, ATP synthesis). Translational analyses relied on transcriptomic similarity without protein-level validation in human myocardium or clinical correlation with telomere length. Obviously, at this stage, interventional studies in human tissue or clinical cohorts were not possible. While the transcriptional similarity between telomere-shortened cardiomyocytes and failing human hearts suggests a strong molecular convergence, this evidence remains correlative. Direct causality in humans cannot be inferred from these data, and future studies should include patient-derived cardiomyocytes or targeted rescue of telomere-driven pathways to establish causal relevance. Finally, IPA provides structured pathway insights but relies on a proprietary curated knowledge base, which may favour well-characterized genes and pathways. To address this, we interpreted results conservatively and validated key predictions, including following up on TREX1 and cGAS–STING activation in the core datasets.

Novelty

This study provides the first comprehensive characterization of the transcriptomic landscape in telomere-shortened cardiomyocytes, revealing that telomere dysfunction initiates a broad reprogramming that extends beyond the canonical p53–PGC-1α axis. We identify activation of inflammatory, neurohumoral, and innate immune pathways, including a robust cGAS–STING–type I interferon signature. Using transcriptional hierarchy and network centrality analyses, we demonstrate that telomere-associated regulators occupy upstream positions in mTRG5 hearts, confirming a cardiomyocyte-intrinsic aetiology of dysfunction. By comparing this profile to TREX1-deficient hearts and integrating murine and human HF datasets, we establish that telomere shortening converges with conserved molecular programmes in advanced HF, supporting its role as a pathogenic, translationally relevant driver rather than a passive bystander.

Further studies are needed to confirm the causal role of the telomere shortening–innate immunity axis and to investigate specific therapeutic approaches.

With the first comprehensive characterization of the transcriptomic landscape in telomere-shortened cardiomyocytes, integrated with evidence from our mouse models and human data, we propose that cardiomyocyte telomere attrition acts as an upstream, myocardium-intrinsic driver that organizes key pathogenic pathways in heart failure (HF), rather than a passive correlate of chronic disease. Our results show that telomere dysfunction in cardiomyocytes is associated with broad transcriptional reprogramming that extends beyond the classical p53–PGC-1α axis and includes mitochondrial impairment, neurohumoral activation, inflammatory signaling, and a pronounced cGAS–STING/type I interferon signature. Translationally, this telomere-dysfunctional program aligns closely with myocardial gene expression profiles from patients with dilated and ischemic cardiomyopathy, showing the strongest overlap in end-stage or exacerbated HF and weaker or inverse correlations in milder HF or recovered hearts. The extent to which a failing human ventricle appears “telomere-dysfunctional” at the transcriptomic level correlates with disease severity, indicating that telomere shortening is intertwined with progression to advanced HF. These insights, together with the convergence of telomere-linked regulators (TP53, PGC-1α, NF-κB, IL-6, angiotensinogen, cGAS–STING/type I interferon) and established HF driver networks, support a mechanistic role for telomere dysfunction in human disease and suggest that myocardial telomere length and integrity could refine HF risk stratification. Notably, specific therapeutic strategies targeting telomere maintenance or downstream DNA-damage and cGAS–STING/type I interferon signaling may complement standard neurohumoral blockade to modify HF progression.
