Development and validation of machine-learning models for monitoring individual behaviors in group-housed broiler chickens
Authors: Victoria R Merenda, Venkat UC Bodempudi, Monique D Pairis-Garcia, Guoming Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnimals’ individual behavior is commonly monitored by live or video observation by a person. This can be labor intensive and inconsistent. An alternative is the use of machine learning-based computer vision systems. The objectives of this study were to 1) develop and optimize machine learning model frameworks for detecting, tracking and classifying individual behaviors of group-housed broiler chickens in continuous video recordings; and 2) use an independent dataset to evaluate the performance of the developed machine leaning model framework for individual poultry behaviors differentiation. Forty-two video recordings from 4 different pens (total video duration = 1,620 min) were used to develop and train multiple models for detecting and tracking individual birds and classifying 4 behaviors: feeding, drinking, active, and inactive. The optimal model framework was used to continuously analyze an external set of 11 videos (duration = 326 min), and the second-by-second behavior of each individual broiler was extracted for the comparison of human observation. After comparison of model performance, the YOLOv5l, out of 5 detection models, was selected for detecting individual broilers in a pen; the osnet_x0_25_msmt17, out of 4 tracking algorithms, was selected to track each detected bird in continuous frames; and the Gradient Boosting Classifier, out of 12 machine learning classifiers, was selected to classify the 4 behaviors. Most of the models were able to keep previously assigned individual identifications of the chickens in limited amounts of time, but lost the identities throughout an examination period (≥4 min). The final framework was able to accurately predict feeding (accuracy = 0.895) and drinking time (accuracy = 0.9) but subpar for active (accuracy = 0.545) and inactive time (accuracy = 0.505). The algorithms employed by the machine learning models were able to accurately detect feeding and drinking behavior but still need to be improved for maintaining individual identities of the chickens and identifying active and inactive behaviors.Key words: poultry, machine-learning, behavior, welfare, computer vision

INTRODUCTION

Over the past two decades, poultry has become the most consumed livestock commodity in the world (USDA, 2022), because of the bird's rapid growth and improved performance associated with significant advances in nutrition, disease control, and management (Dawson et al., 2021). However, selection for higher production efficiency and performance can negatively impact bird welfare and behavior. Health challenges associated with fast growing birds includes increased incidences of skeletal disorders (Shim et al., 2012) and lameness (Knowles et al., 2008; Wilhelmsen et al., 2019). These conditions can directly impact both bird behavior and affective state, particularly in situations where pain develops as a byproduct of disease. Currently, lameness in broilers is evaluated primarily by caretaker observation of bird behavior and lameness assessment by severity (Kestin et al., 1992). Lame birds show decreased activity and perform sitting behavior more frequently (Dixon, 2020), while decreasing/increasing time at the feeder and drinker (Dixon, 2020). From a live observing standpoint, lame broilers are also reported to have more long-duration leg extensions, less preening, and less dustbathing activity than sound broilers (Weeks et al., 2000).

Lame broilers are a significant welfare issue and identifying these birds earlier on will not only improve bird welfare but will increase the likelihood of a successful intervention with treatment. In order to identify birds early on in the lameness process, bird behavior must be quantified efficiently, consistently and in real-time. However, visual observation is labor intensive, time consuming and subject to human error (Theurer et al., 2013) and many caretakers working directly with the birds do not have the training to identify behavior consistently.

Therefore, an alternative to mitigate these aforementioned challenges is to use precision poultry farming techniques. Precision poultry farming is the application of process engineering principles and techniques to automate data collection, monitoring, modeling, and management of poultry production (Wather et al., 2008). The sensors used in precision poultry farming can be divided into wearable and nonwearable devices. Wearable devices such as accelerometers, radio frequency identification systems, microphones and body temperature sensors can monitor individual animals precisely (Li et al., 2021a), however, equipping every broiler in a large poultry flock with surveillance equipment is impractical and cost prohibitive (Sassi et al., 2016). Nonwearable techniques, such as computer vision has emerged as a promising tool for real-time automation of poultry monitoring systems due to its noninvasive and nonintrusive nature, as well as its ability to provide a vast amount of information (Okinda et al., 2020). Currently, the most popular category of computer-vision based systems is based on machine learning.

In poultry farming, machine learning models (MLM) have been used to track feeding and drinking behaviors (Li et al., 2020), stretching (Li et al., 2021b) and walking around feeder (Li et al., 2021c) in groups of broilers. To the authors knowledge, no work to date has evaluated individual bird behavior in group settings or explored behavioral time budgets of activity in these birds. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to (1) develop and optimize MLM framework for detecting, tracking and classifying individual behaviors of group-housed broilers in continuous video recordings; and (2) use an independent data set to evaluate performance of the developed machine leaning models to differ individual poultry behaviors according to valid discriminative patterns. Broiler behaviors tested by the model include feeding, drinking, active and inactive.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of North Carolina State University (IACUC protocol #20–481-A). Animals were cared and handled in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching (FASS, 2010).

Study Animals, Housing

This study was conducted at the North Carolina State University, Raleigh, NC, in March 2021 and was part of a larger vaccine trial study (Gaghan et al., 2022). Briefly, Gaghan et al. (2022) weighed 360, 20-day-old broilers of mixed-sex Ross 708, and randomly allocated them to two treatment groups with 18 birds per pen and 10 pen replicates per treatment in an open floor barn.

For this study, 16 birds were used. Four pens were randomly selected, and within each pen, 4 birds had their back color identified with blue, green, pink or orange paint. Figure 1 depicts the setup of the 4 pens. Pens were bedded with black mulch (Rubberific Shredded, Lowe's, Mooresville, NC), and birds could see conspecifics in other pens via woven wire. Each pen was equipped with one feeder and PVC nipple drinkers (4 nipples/pen). Diet and water were provided to all pens on an ad libitum basis.

Figures showing the different pens setup for C1 (A), C41 (B), C43 (C), and C54 (D).

Data Collection

Videos were captured with high-resolution cameras (Sony HDR-CX405; New York, NY) that were installed centrally, over each pen, at a height (∼ 0.7 m) that enabled visualization of all broilers in the pen. One camera was used per pen. The videos were sampled at the rate of 20 frames per second and stored in .mp4 format on Google Drive. Pens were recorded continuously for various amounts of time. A total of 53 videos were recorded and total time of video recordings for pens C1, C41, C43, and C54 (Figure 1) were 178, 646, 805, and 317 min, respectively. Out of the 53 videos, 42 videos (total video duration = 1,620 min) were randomly selected based on the random numbers generated in Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA) and used to develop machine learning models, while another 11 independent videos (total video duration = 326 min) were used for continuous observation to validate the robustness and generalizability of the developed models.

Image Conversion

The 42 videos were converted into 5,203 image frames at the time interval of approximately 19 seconds, as previously described (Parico and Ahamed, 2021). The code used to extract the images from the videos, using the Python operation interface, is depicted in Supplementary Figure 1. The large time intervals could ensure sufficient variations for model development.

Data Splitting

A typical rule-of-thumb in data splitting for dataset sizes from 100 to 10,000 images dictates that images should be split in 70:30 or 80:20 for training and validation set (Li et al., 2021a; Parico and Ahamed, 2021). In this study we followed this general practice of splitting the dataset to ensure sufficient data for training while retaining enough for effective validation. This balanced approach, often seen in studies, helps in model optimization and prevents overfitting. The chosen dataset was split in 80:20. Therefore, out of the 5,203 generated images, 4,162 images were randomly selected to be used for model training and the remaining 1,041 images were used for model validation. The code used to randomly select 80% of the images for model training, using the Python operation interface, is depicted in Supplementary Figure 2.

Images Labelling and Bird Detection

Labels that are input into model training are the classes linked with images and influence what patterns the models learn from an image (Li et al., 2021a). In this study, bounding box labelling was performed on 5,203 images using visual observation and an open-source Python-based annotation tool (LabelImg and Broström, 2022). The focal birds marked with the 4 colors were labeled in each frame, and the assigned class was “chicken”. The export format of the labels were txt files that were stored for further use. Bounding box information was stored in the format of class, x, y, w, h, where x and y were the center of a bounding box and w, h were the width and height of a bounding box (Ju et al., 2020).

Object detection consists of detecting and locating individual objects of concern and enclosing them with bounding boxes (Li et al., 2021a). The images were input into the You Only Look Once version 5 (YOLOv5; Jocher et al., 2022) bird detector, which is one of the most popular object detectors. The YOLOv5 object detector simultaneously extracts many region proposals from an image (Ju et al., 2020). Features were then extracted from each region through a large convolutional neural network and used to assign a class label to each region.

Five YOLOv5 models (YOLOv5n [nano], YOLOv5s [small], YOLOv5m [medium], YOLOv5l [large], and YOLOv5x [extra-large]) were comparatively evaluated. We selected YOLOv5 because it is a widely used and reliable model for object detection, applied across various fields. YOLOv5 was not a bottleneck in the overall performance, as it was used solely for detection purposes, achieving detection accuracy over 0.99. While newer versions like YOLOv9 or YOLOv10 could have been used, YOLOv5 remains highly versatile and dependable. The available options within YOLOv5 are limited to 5 models, which is why only these 5 were chosen for this study. The number of parameters for YOLOv5n, YOLOv5s, YOLOv5m, YOLOv5l, and YOLOv5x were 1.9, 7.2, 21.2, 46.5, and 86.7 million, respectively (GitHub, 2022). The final performance of the bird detection was measured using recall, precision and mean average precision at the intersection over union threshold of 0.5 (MAP@0.5). The calculations of recall, precision and mean average precision are shown in equations (Eq) (1), (2), (3).

Recall measures the sensitivity of the detection tracker (Parico and Ahamed, 2021) whereas precision measures the positive predictive value of the detection tracker (Li et al., 2021a). The MAP@0.5 is the area under the precision-recall curve interpolated from points of recall and precision at the confidence threshold of 0.5 (Parico and Ahamed, 2021). Each of the 5 models were run with 40, 50, 60, 70 and 80 epochs, respectively. The term “epochs” is the total number of cycles through the entire set of training data when training a model. Each epoch involves the model making predictions, calculating errors, and adjusting its weights and biases to minimize those errors for the entire dataset. By training over multiple epochs with random data reshuffling, the model can iteratively improve its accuracy. We typically experiment with different epoch numbers to find an optimal balance between underfitting (too few epochs) and overfitting (too many epochs). The code used in Google Colab to run the different bird detectors, with different epochs is depicted in Supplementary Figure 3. After comparison, the optimal bird detection model was saved as .pt file and embedded into the MLM framework.

Multibird Tracking

The bird detection step was followed by the multibird tracking process. The tracking model was Strong Simple Online and Realtime Tracking (StrongSORT; Du et al., 2023) which combines motion and appearance information based on Omni-scale network (OSNet; Zhou et al., 2019) in order to track birds with occlusion and similarities. The algorithm made full use of the bird features across time, such as geometric features, temporal features for bird motion, and spatial features of bird appearance (Voulodimos et al., 2018). It is a step that involves assigning identities (ID) for broilers through continuous frames (Li et al., 2021a). In this study, the unique assigned IDs were chicken 1, chicken 2, chicken 3, and chicken 4 in each pen.

Optimization of the Multibird Tracking

The tracking models are typically trained in large datasets with millions of images, such as ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009; Russakovsky et al., 2015), and should be robust for assigning or re-assigning IDs for new objects (e.g., broilers in this case). After tracking individual birds, bird IDs and dimensions (x, y, w, h) of each bounding box were continuously extracted from an examined 4-min video recording. The pretrained tracking algorithms were comparatively evaluated to determine the optimal one. Using pretrained codes (Zhou, 2019), 5 different models (osnet_x0_25_msmt17, osnet_x0_25_market1501, osnet_x0_75_imagenet, osnet_ibn_x1_0_imagenet, osnet_ain_x1_0_imagenet) were randomly selected and tested with different confidence thresholds of 0.5, 0.6, 0.75, 0.8, 0.9, and 0.95.

The calculation for the confidence threshold, for the processing speed, in seconds per frame, and for the time for first ID switch, in seconds, are depicted in Eq 4–6.

The codes osnet_x0_25 and osnet_x0_75 were in the group of same-domain ReIDs, whereas osnet_ibn_x1_0 and osnet_ain_x1_0 were in the group of cross-domain ReIDs (Zhou, 2019). we evaluated 5 different pretrained tracking algorithms: osnet_x0_25_msmt17, osnet_x0_25_market1501, osnet_x0_75_imagenet, osnet_ibn_x1_0_imagenet, and osnet_ain_x1_0_imagenet, at various confidence thresholds (0.5, 0.6, 0.75, 0.8, 0.9, and 0.95) to determine the most optimal one. These models were selected based on their performance in re-identification tasks, and we tested their performance by calculating the confidence threshold, processing speed (in seconds per frame), and the time it took for the first ID switch (i.e., when the model mistakenly reassigned the ID of the same bird). Briefly, Osnet consists of a deep ReID CNN designed for omni-scale feature learning (Zhou et al., 2019), msmt17 is a multiscene multitime person re-identification dataset (Wei et al., 2018), Market-1501 is a large-scale public benchmark dataset for person re-identification (Masson et al., 2021), and ImageNet is a large-scale collection of images constructed upon the backbone of the WordNet structure (Deng et al., 2009). The models osnet_x0_25 and osnet_x0_75 were classified as same-domain re-identification models, whereas osnet_ibn_x1_0 and osnet_ain_x1_0 belonged to the cross-domain category. The tracking algorithms were chosen from person re-identification datasets like msmt17 (a multiscene person re-identification dataset) and Market-1501 (a public benchmark dataset for person re-identification). The number in each model name (0, 25, 75) reflects the model's network configuration. The Omni-scale Network (OSNet) was designed to capture features at multiple scales, improving its ability to track objects in diverse scenarios. This approach ensured that we selected the most efficient tracking model for our experiment, balancing detection accuracy and computational speed. The number 0, 25, or 75 in the pretrained model names are the number of layers of network (Li et al., 2021a).

Multibird Tracking Comparison

Upon selection of the best multibird tracking model, videos from the 4 pens (i.e., C1, C43, C54, C41) with different video durations (4, 7, 17, 33, 53, 68, and 86 min) were tested. Processing speed is the estimation of how much time the model takes to process 1 frame. Therefore, the optimal model for tracking birds had a lower processing time per frame. Identity (ID) switch was the number of the first frame in which one of the birds’ identities was different from the original identities 1, 2, 3, or 4. The optimal model for tracking birds was expected to keep the original identities for a longer time (or more frames). The tracking model was then saved as a .pt file for deployment.

Building Multibehavior Classification Model

Machine learning classifiers use input training data for predicting the predetermined behavior categories. Therefore, for each pen, video segments containing feeding, drinking, active and inactive behaviors were used as input training data. Video segments were trimmed using a video editing software (CapCut, Beijing, China). Given that labels may be influenced by judgment bias from different individuals (Tuyttens et al., 2014), a sole well-trained labeler was responsible for completion of all the behavior identification and trimming.

Video clips were obtained at the mark where a chicken was first seen performing one of the 4 behaviors and clipped immediately at the end of the behavior. In order to reach 30 min per behavior and per pen, 550 videos were trimmed (42 = feeding, 147 = drinking, 319 = active, and 42 = inactive). The frame number, bird ID number, and the 4 coordinates of the bounding box were extracted from the developed bird tracker, for each bird, within each trimmed video. Fifteen features including spatial and temporal characteristics of each track bird were calculated from the bounding box coordinates using the code depicted in Supplementary Figure 4. Spatial features included dimensions (x, y, w, h), top left coordinates (xmin, ymin), and area of each bounding bound, which enclosed a detected and tracked bird. Temporal features included Euclidean distance of centroid coordinates and changes of areas, dimensions, and top left coordinates for each detected and tracked bounding box between 2 adjacent frames.

All the video segments were then merged into 1 csv file and offered to the model as input data using the code depicted in supplemental figure 5. After processing the csv file, the final result was a combined txt file. The whole dataset was split into 80% for training and 20% for validation. A total of 12 machine learning classifiers were selected from a Python-based library, Scikit-learn, and comparatively evaluated. The classifiers included Multilayer Perceptron (MLPClassifier), K Nearest Neighbors (KNeighborsClassifier), Gradient Boosting Classifier, Decision Tree Classifier, Extra Trees Classifier, Random Forest Classifier (RandomForest Classifier), Adaptive Bosting Classifier (AdaBoostClassifier), Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GaussianNB), Quadratic Analysis, Stochastic Gradient Descent Classifier (SGDClassifier). The final performance of recall, precision, accuracy, and F1-score were reported to determine the optimal classifier for behavior classification, and calculating procedures of the 4 metrics are presented in Eqs. 1,2, 7, and 8, respectively.

The optimal classifier was saved as sav (binary) file and embedded into the MLM framework for individual broiler behavior classification.

Model Inference

The final step was inference using the developed MLM framework. The 11 videos initially selected for continuous behavioral watching included recordings from pen C43 (total video duration = 129 min) and C41 (total video duration = 197 min). These 11 videos were fed to the MLM framework. Individual broilers were detected and tracked from each video frame via the developed bird detector and multibird tracker, and the bird ID and bounding box dimensions were continuously output and organized into csv files. The results were sorted with bird ID in Excel to obtain individual results, which was then fed to developed machine learning behavior classifier for classifying the 4 behaviors of individual broilers. The time intervals for the behavior classification were 1 second namely 20 frames, and the second-by-second behavior predicted by the machine was recorded. The code used to generate the predicted behavior by the machine learning model is depicted in supplemental figure 6.

Behavioral Watching for Independent Dataset

The 11 videos selected for continuous observation were processed with the developed multibird tracker in order to have bounding boxes around the birds. The observer then watched the 11 processed videos, 4 times, to record second-by-second the behavior performed by each of the 4 individual birds. The behaviors watched by the observers were recorded in a new spreadsheet to avoid any bias of having the predicted class by the model on the same spreadsheet. These manual observation results served as gold standards for evaluating the individual behavior classification performance via the developed MLM framework.

Statistical Analysis

For each behavior, to evaluate the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and negative and positive predicted values, the investigators compared the observers’ recorded behavior (gold standard) each second with the MLM predicted behavior at the same second. The equations for calculating the metrics are presented in Eqs. 1, 7, and (9), (10), (11).

To determine the coefficient of determination and the concordance correlation coefficient between the observer and the MLM, the summation of total seconds of behaviors predicted by the MLM and the summation obtained by continuous video watching were used.

The coefficient of determination and concordance correlation coefficient analyses were conducted using MedCalc (MedCalc software, Mariakerke, Belgium). The results obtained with test characteristics, coefficient of determination, and concordance correlation coefficient were defined as follows (adapted from Hinkle, 1988): 0.00 to 0.30 = negligible; 0.31 to 0.50 = low; 0.51 to 0.70 = moderate; 0.71 to 0.90 = high and 0.91 to 1.00 = very high.

RESULTS

Table 2 depicts the final performance of the bird detector. The overall bird detection performance was fairly good with over 0.99 precision, recall, and MAP@0.5. After reaching to specific performance, increasing the epoch number could not improve model detection performance any more. For example, the baseline epoch number was 40 in this case, and the bird detection performance could not increase 0.001 when the epoch number increased from 40 to 80. Some heavier models (i.e., YOLOv5x) did not significantly outperform lighter model (i.e., YOLOv5s). In contrast, the YOLOv5x had the smallest recall (0.940) with 60 epochs. In sum, the model YOLOv5l with 70 epochs had the slightly higher precision (0.998), recall (0.996), and MAP@0.5 (0.995), thus being selected as the bird detector in the MLM framework.

Table 5 depicts the individual behavior classification performance with 12 machine learning classifiers. Overall, these classifiers achieved 0.31 to 0.84 accuracy, 0.23 to 0.90 precision, 0.02 to 0.92 recall, and 0.04 to 0.91 F1 score. Precision values were higher than recall values (0.647 ± 0.181 vs. 0.626 ± 0.261) on average, indicating that these classifiers were less likely to classify target behaviors as others. Most of the classifiers classified feeding and drinking behaviors more accurately than active and inactive behaviors. Some classifiers presented extreme performance for various behaviors. For instance, the SGDClassifier had 0.02 recall for active behaviors while 0.90 recall for drinking behavior, indicating extreme preferences or biases for specific features of behaviors and poor generalizability and robustness. Based on these metrics, Gradient Boosting Classifier had higher accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score and more uniform values of the performance for classifying the 4 behaviors, thus being selected as the individual behavior classifier for the MLM framework.

DISCUSSION

This study developed, optimized, and deployed a MLM framework for detecting and tracking broilers and classifying individual behaviors using continuous video recording. The framework predicted individual broiler behaviors (i.e., feeding, drinking, active, inactive) with F-1 scores greater than 0.77. The Gradient Boosting Classifier offered the best performance of 0.84 weighted average F1 score and between 0.79 and 0.89 precision across all 4 behaviors. The framework performance demonstrated great potential to use machine learning to perform continuous behavioral monitoring, which is traditionally performed through direct observation and manual coding of behaviors (Zamansky et al., 2021). Thus, this offers an opportunity for a more efficient behavioral monitoring system at least for laboratory poultry behavior studies.

Even though the developed framework had high accuracy of 0.84, this performance was still subpar compared to previous models tracking feeding and drinking behaviors (Li et al., 2020), stretching (Li et al., 2021b) and walking around feeder (Li et al., 2021c). However, these previous studies were not developed for identification of individual behaviors in group-housed broilers. The diverse data (i.e., different pens setup, different birds, different time frames) used to train the models might have provided more realistic results. For instance, 4 pens with different setup were used, contributing to external validity of the models. Furthermore, the birds used in this study were from different sizes and with different number of birds within pen which created variations for model development. Different locations within the same farm can capture various backgrounds contributing to data diversity (Chen et al., 2020) and, in this study, sufficient variations in image contents fed into Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based architectures might have increased the robustness of the models in unseen situations. Therefore, it is safe to assume that the video frames used in this study were truly independent observations that did not cause overfitting of the training data or over-reporting of the MLM framework performance.

This study is the first to our knowledge to test the models with a new set of continuous observation data that was unseen during model development. From the 81,257 seconds recorded by continuous behavior watching, 794 seconds were lost because the framework did not recognize the object (i.e., bird) within the frames. This failure mainly happened under bird occlusion, when a random bird would cover the bird under observation. Even though data loss was <1%, it does bring to attention that the developed framework may be hard to implement in barns housing birds at higher stocking densities. In higher stocking density scenarios, a human observer may be able to assume motion consistency across consecutive frames compared to the MLM, which did not have the same ability.

A systematic review on automated detection revealed that cattle and pig were the species with the highest implementation of this technology (Li et al., 2021a) despite poultry being one of the major sources of meat protein (USDA, 2022). There are some explanations for the challenging implementation of automated detection in poultry production. Poultry are generally raised in higher densities than the cattle and swine (Fu et al., 2016; Krawczel and Lee, 2019; Li et al., 2021a). The occlusion and overlapping among birds caused by high stocking density in poultry farms can impact the accuracy of the models (Okinda et al., 2020). In addition to that, the low light intensity present in poultry production generates low-resolution images (Okinda et al., 2020; Parico and Ahamed, 2021) which is another challenge for machine learning modeling. Thus, whenever building a machine learning model, researchers should be mindful of the number of video recordings since higher data losses are anticipated in typical commercial production systems for broilers.

Although machine learning models are prone to data loss, the speed of the MLM to predict second-by-second behaviors, for individual chickens, is remarkable. Using the inference from the MLM, all the birds in a 50-min video had their behaviors predicted, individually, in 3 min. In order to get the same measurements, for the 4 birds, using continuous video observation, 3h and 20 min were invested in continuous behavioral watching. Another advantage of using MLM is the fact that birds don't need any specific characteristics to be identified (e.g., dyes, collars, tags, marks). In order for a human to continuously observe the chickens and differentiate them, the birds require some sort of identification whereas the MLM relies on the detected spatial or temporal feature patterns. While tagging is an effective method to identify individuals, it requires animals to be caught and manipulated, which may be difficult (Mersch et al., 2013) and impact the animal's behavior [by the presence of the tag or during its application (Pankiw and Page, 2003; Dennis et al., 2008)]. Therefore, by using MLM, all birds can be identified without administering an identification marker, resulting is good behavioral prediction at least in laboratory behavior studies.

Nonetheless, machine learning is not a silver bullet (Valletta et al., 2017). Given machine learning algorithms look for patterns in the data that are the best predictors for an outcome, there may be a conflict between the way machines and humans distinguish these patterns (Valletta et al., 2017). Specific to bird activity, test characteristics for active time were high or moderate but with a negligible positive predictive value when comparing MLM to visual observation. In fact, the MLM framework overestimated active time. One of the explanations for the low positive predictive value is the diversity and variation of activity that broilers can perform. Unlike feeding and drinking, activity includes a variety of behaviors with significant behavioral variation and patterns collected under one defined behavior. Future work should assess more subtle active behaviors and consider refining behavioral definitions to specific behavioral patterns of activity.

When selecting individually expressed chicken behaviors to build the classification model, it is necessary to investigate significant and differentiable shape parameters related to each behavior, which usually present repeatable and similar shapes (Pereira et al., 2013). In this study, because any behavior that was not defined as inactive, feeding, or drinking was considered active, it is possible that the MLM could not find a pattern for “active”, and therefore, anything that was slightly different from the typical inactive/ feeding/drinking was then considered active, causing the overestimation of active behaviors. The main issue was the model predicting that the bird was active when in fact it was inactive. In this study, standing and resting were considered “resting”. Therefore, it could be that when birds were standing, any minimal movement caused the machine to assume that the bird was active. Another explanation is the previously mentioned occlusion of the bird of interest by other birds due to overcrowding. This hypothesis is corroborated by the fact that the MLM underestimated inactive time and only had a negligible sensitivity and low negative predicted value.

The classification performance of the MLM developed in this study for feeding is consistent with the findings of previous studies. For example, Li et al. (2020) used a region-based convolutional neural network object detector, a bird tracker, and a support vector machine behavior classifier to classify restricted feeding behavior, achieving over 93% precision for the object detector. Li et al. (2020) attempted to develop and validate image processing algorithms for automatic monitoring of feeding and drinking behaviors of group-reared broilers. They achieved high sensitivity when classifying both feeding (89–93%) and drinking (87–90%) behaviors. Similarly, Pu et al. (2018) achieved an accuracy of 99.17% an automatic CNN-based method to recognize the chicken behavior within a poultry farm and a Kinetic sensor. However, the models in these aforementioned studies were developed targeting group of broilers, and not individual birds. Furthermore, their models were not tested against a dataset that was not part of the training model. In this study, the fact that the model for feeding was very accurate shows the appropriateness of machine learning models when attempting to classify feeding behavior in individual chickens, in different farm setups.

For drinking behavior, even though the overall test characteristics were high (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy), very high (negative predictive value) and low (positive predictive value), the coefficient of determination and concordance correlation coefficient for total behavioral time were still subpar. Yang et al. (2021) evaluated bird triaxial accelerations and analyzed its acceleration data to classify drinking behaviors through machine learning and found an accuracy of 83%. Similar to our findings, the models previously developed (Yang et al., 2021) were confused about differentiating resting, feeding and drinking behaviors. In addition, Yang et al. (2021) also considered standing as resting behavior. In their study, accelerometers were used to classify broiler behaviors, primarily detecting animal motions for behavior classification. Standing, being relatively stationary, was often misclassified as resting. While defining standing as a distinct behavior might be beneficial, it could easily be confused with walking or other slow movements in specific frames. Furthermore, separating behaviors in finer detail could increase predictive variation and demand more model complexity. These remain open questions for future research. Therefore, one of the possible explanations for the subpar performance of the MLM when identifying drinking behavior in this study is the fact that standing was defined as resting which may have similar pattern as drinking. The challenge in differentiating drinking behavior from resting lie in the fact that both behaviors exhibited similar motions (i.e., standing still and pecking nipple drinker), producing similar numerical patterns that may confuse the models.

This study highlighted the importance of testing the machine learning models with an independent dataset. Particularly because even after reaching an 0.84 accuracy with the Gradient Boosting Classifier model, the overall test characteristics for active, inactive and drinking were low. Reproducibility of a predictive model over multiple independent datasets over time is the most rigorous form of validation (Valletta et al., 2017). Therefore, future research should focus on validating their MLM with extensive datasets instead of solely relying on the model score.

The main limitation of using MLM was the fact that all the behaviors were subject to error with respect to maintaining individual broilers identities. Even though the model was excellent for predicting feeding and accurate to predict drinking, the spreadsheet had to be manually sorted in order to extract the behavior for each individual bird. To address this issue, color markings were used to help track certain birds more consistently. By focusing on the video segments where the marked birds' identities remained stable, we were able to validate the machine learning classifiers more effectively. Keeping identities is essential for the investigation of individual-level effects (Walter, 2022). Even with a perfect tracking algorithm, when multiple animals emerge in a scenario, and with no other source of identity information, the algorithm cannot differentiate the individuals, and mistakes can quickly accumulate (Walter, 2022).

FUTURE WORK

Future work should explore the actual time savings and effects on accuracy of machine learning models with more specific behavioral definitions. The actual gains seen in practice need to be quantified. Additionally, the effect of such a MLM on human accuracy needs to be explored. Accuracy could be impacted if humans are more likely to accept incorrect suggestions from MLMs, but could also be improved if the model suggests information that humans may have mislabeled by trivial reasons (e.g., human fatigue). Future work should also train the MLM with completely different setups. While our current model was trained with 4 different configurations, expanding this to various environments, such as different pen sizes, bird numbers, or bedding materials, could improve generalizability. For example, the use of black mulch in this study may have enhanced detection due to the contrast with the birds, and future research should investigate how other materials, such as wood shavings, may affect prediction accuracy. Finally, future work should focus on developing models that are able to keep the individual identities of the chickens thoroughly. In this study, none of the models were capable of maintaining the 4 original chickens’ identifications throughout the examination period.

CONCLUSION

This research developed and evaluated the accuracy of a machine learning model framework for monitoring individual behaviors in chicken (feeding, drinking, active, inactive) and determined if the total time predicted by the MLM corresponded to the total time per behavior recorded by visual observations. The codes used in this study have been made open source in order to overcome the current restrictions on applying MLM to animal research and to enable their use in future research. In this work, the MLM was able to accurately detect feeding and drinking behavior but was unable to accurately detect activity and inactivity times. None of the models were able to keep the previously assigned individual identifications of the chickens thoroughly. The algorithms employed by the MLM used still need to be improved for maintaining individual identities of the chickens.
