The LIBRA index of modifiable risk factors for mild cognitive impairment and dementia in a late-life population-based cohort study
Authors: Yueting Wang, Erin Jacobsen, Tiffany F. Hughes, Beth E. Snitz, Chung-Chou H. Chang, Mary Ganguli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Lifestyle for Brain Health (LIBRA) index, a weighted measure of modifiable risk factors for dementia, has been widely applied in European cohorts but less explored in US populations. We applied it to a cohort aged 65+ years, sampled from a relatively low-socioeconomic US region, and compared its predictive validity for incident mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and incident dementia risk. We used both reference factor weights derived from published meta-analyses and cluster-specific factor weights based on estimation results using our own cohort data. We also compared the original LIBRA index with an updated version (LIBRA2), which added three new risk factors including hearing impairment, sleep disturbance, and low social contact. We used joint modeling to account for informative attrition over time. Higher baseline LIBRA indexes were consistently associated with increased dementia risk across factor weight estimation methods. LIBRA2 demonstrated better predictive validity for incident dementia compared to MCI, further improving its performance. Cohort-specific factor weights strengthened associations with cognitive impairment risk but may limit comparability across studies. These findings contribute to valid use of LIBRA for dementia prediction in a late-life US cohort and support the incorporation of additional factors and cohort-specific adjustments to enhance its utility in diverse populations.

Introduction

Dementia, a major public health concern worldwide, is inherently difficult to treat due to the complexity of the underlying disease processes. Therefore, the primary prevention of dementia and mild cognitive impairment (MCI)—a state intermediate between normal cognition for age and dementia—hinges on identifying modifiable lifestyle-related risk and protective factors, particularly those actionable by individuals and their health care providers.1–3

Several cumulative risk scores have been reviewed and compared.4 We selected the Lifestyle for BRAin health (LIBRA) index because it was developed through a systematic literature review and Delphi expert study instead of a single-cohort study.5–8 It is a weighted sum score of 12 modifiable health and lifestyle factors, including: unhealthy lifestyle behaviors (eg, elevated alcohol consumption, smoking, physical inactivity, low engagement in cognitively stimulating activities, unhealthy diet), poor cardiovascular health (eg, history of heart disease, diabetes, high cholesterol, obesity, hypertension, renal dysfunction), and depression.5,7,9 The LIBRA index evaluates the additive effect of all included factors; higher scores indicate higher risk for dementia. Designed to identify individuals at elevated risk of dementia and facilitate prevention in real-world practice, LIBRA has demonstrated predictive validity for incident dementia and cognitive impairment across several population-based cohort studies,10–22 and has even been utilized as a surrogate measure of dementia risk in some intervention trials.23–25

Challenges to the predictive validity of LIBRA include issues of both external and internal validity. Most previous studies were conducted in European cohorts, limiting generalizability to non-European populations.10–21 Additionally, these cohorts often represent urban, economically advantaged populations, with limited validation in low-income and rural settings.19–21 As LIBRA includes some risk factors, such as obesity and hypertension, that peak in midlife rather than late life,26,27 its predictive performance may vary across the adult lifespan, as previously shown.10,11,13 Internal validity concerns arise from reliance on reference factor weights derived from published meta-analyses6,7 that may not be universally applicable to all cohorts. Moreover, the LIBRA index was recently updated to LIBRA2, incorporating three new risk factors, hearing impairment, sleep disturbance, and low social contact, along with revised weights, warranting further validation.28,29

The over-arching aims of this study were as follows: (a) to evaluate the predictive validity of the LIBRA and LIBRA2 indexes for both MCI and dementia risks in a US population-based cohort, the Monongahela-Youghiogheny Healthy Aging Team (MYHAT) study, a late-life US population30; and (b) to compare these results with findings from prior cohort studies. Specifically, we assessed predictive validity by examining (1) its performance in predicting incident MCI and incident dementia, (2) the impact of using different factor weight estimation methods, including those derived from the MYHAT cohort, and (3) the additive effects of three new LIBRA2 risk factors (hearing impairment, sleep disturbance, and low social contact).

Methods

Study population and sample selection

We used data from MYHAT,30 a prospective late-life American population-based cohort study investigating the epidemiology of MCI in a group of “Rust Belt” Pennsylvania communities of relatively low socioeconomic status. The cohort was selected by age-stratified random sampling from the publicly available voter registration lists for the targeted small towns between 2006 and 2008. Inclusion criteria at study entry (initial baseline, Wave 1) were age 65 years or older, living in the selected towns, not already in long-term care, with decisional capacity to provide informed consent, and hearing and vision sufficient to undergo neuropsychological testing. Those who already had moderate to severe cognitive impairment were screened out by age-education-corrected Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores <21.31–33 Twelve annual follow-up assessments were conducted from Wave 2 through Wave 13, collecting sociodemographic data, neuropsychological assessment, self-report medical history, lifestyle, and other health parameters.

Of the initial 1982 participants retained after screening, we excluded 154 individuals with only baseline visits, and 23 who already had dementia (Clinical Dementia Rating ≥ 1, described later) at Wave 1, resulting in 1805 dementia-free participants with follow-up data.

To compute the LIBRA Index, MYHAT does not have diet data, and our measure of cognitive activity, the Florida Cognitive Activities Scale (FCAS),34 was only introduced at Wave 5. We therefore applied the method of last observation carried forward (LOCF) to impute missing FCAS data, and set Wave 5 as our analysis baseline, using it as the reference point for all LIBRA factor measures.

We then excluded participants without complete records on the 11 remaining original LIBRA factors. Based on CDR records up to Wave 5, we had 712 cognitively normal (MCI-free) participants up with follow-up through Wave 13 as the study sample for MCI risk. Similarly, we had 1118 participants dementia-free till Wave 5 with follow-up through Wave 13 as the study sample for dementia risk (Figure 1).

Incident MCI and incident dementia

We used the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) Staging Instrument to measure functional independence in everyday cognitively driven activities with ratings of CDR: 0 (no dementia), 0.5 (mild cognitive impairment, very mild dementia), and 1.0 through 3.0 (mild, moderate, and severe dementia).35

We defined incident MCI as the first occurrence of a CDR rating of 0.5 in an individual whose previous year’s rating was CDR = 0. Incident dementia was defined as the first occurrence of a CDR rating of 1 in an individual whose previous year’s rating was CDR < 1. The onset date of both conditions was defined as the midpoint between the two consecutive ratings.

LIBRA factors

Excluding diet, we used MYHAT data on 11 out of the 12 modifiable risk and protective factors in the original LIBRA index (Table S1).6,7 All factors were dichotomized based on cut-offs used in previous studies,6,13,36,37 with definitions as follow.

Based on self-report

Low to moderate alcohol intake was defined as drinking ≤ weekly or consuming ≤2–3 alcoholic drinks on each occasion, while high alcohol intake was defined as greater frequency or quantity of alcohol consumption. We do not have data on drinks per day as required for the CDC definition.38 Smoking status was categorized as ever smokers (lifetime including current smoking) versus never smokers. Physical inactivity was assessed based on engagement in exercise and incidental physical activity over the past year.

Coronary heart disease, renal dysfunction, diabetes, and high cholesterol were identified based on participants’ self-reports of having been diagnosed by healthcare professionals.

Based on examination

Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 or a waist-hip ratio (WHR) > 0.85 for women and > 0.9 for men.39 Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mm Hg, or the use of anti-hypertensive medication.

Based on standard questionnaires

Depression symptoms were measured on the modified Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression scale (mCES-D) score ≥ 2,30 representing the cohort’s 90th percentile cut-off value. The mCES-D includes all 20 original CES-D items scored as 0/1 reflecting absence or presence of the symptom over the preceding week.40,41

High cognitive activity was defined as FCAS scores above the sample-based median value (ie, ≥45 for dementia study sample, ≥43 for MCI study sample).37

The updated LIBRA2 index included three additional factors, all available in MYHAT.28,29 Hearing impairment was defined as hearing loss in at least one ear, based on the finger-rubbing test. Sleep disturbance was determined by a 4-item composite of any persistent self-reported sleep complaints, including (1) excessive daytime sleepiness, (2) difficulty falling asleep, (3) trouble falling back asleep after waking during the night, and (4) early morning awakening. Low social contact was measured using composite scores for social isolation and loneliness, calculated from items indicative of social engagement.38

Assessment of LIBRA

We adopted three different methods to estimate factor weights for the LIBRA(2) index.

Method 1

Factor weights were assigned proportionally to the natural logarithm (LN) of that factor’s relative risk (RR) estimate for dementia incidence, as derived from published LIBRA meta-analyses results.6,7 To standardize the weights, proportional scalers were applied such that the lowest absolute value of the assigned weights equaled 1 point. The scalers were approximately 10/3 for LIBRA and 6 for LIBRA2. Most previous studies have directly used these established factor weights.

Methods 2 and 3

Unlike Method 1, these methods were based on Cox regression results using MYHAT data. Factor weights were proportional to the natural logarithms of the hazard ratio (HR) estimates for incident MCI risk (Method 2) and 1 to the natural logarithms of the HR estimates for incident dementia risk (Method 3). To ensure comparability across Methods 1–3 when measuring associations with incident MCI or incident dementia risk. Methods 2 and 3 adopted the same proportional scalers as Method 1, resulting in the following formulas: For LIBRA:Weight=(10/3)×ln(HR), For LIBRA2:Weight=6×ln(HR).

Using these methods, six different LIBRA indexes were calculated, including three versions of 11-factor LIBRA index: (1) LIBRA[Ref] derived by Method 1, (2) LIBRA[MCI] by Method 2, and (3) LIBRA[Dementia] by Method 3: and three versions of the 14-factor LIBRA2 index: (4) LIBRA2[Ref] by Method 1, (5) LIBRA2[MCI] by Method 2, and (6) LIBRA2[Dementia] by Method 3.

Statistical analyses

Baseline differences in the prevalence of each LIBRA factor were analyzed using χ2 tests, comparing individuals with and without incident MCI (n = 712) and those with and without incident dementia in the follow-up (n = 1118).

To obtain factor weights for LIBRA(2)[MCI] using estimation Method 2, two Cox models were fitted to predict incident MCI risk among the study sample (n = 712). The first model included the 11 original LIBRA factors, while the second included the 14 LIBRA2 factors. Similarly, to estimate weights for LIBRA(2)[Dementia] using Method 3, two Cox models were fitted to predict incident dementia risk among the sample (n = 1118): one included the 11 original LIBRA factors, and the other included 14 LIBRA2 factors. Each Cox model was adjusted for age, sex, and education, and accounted for informative attrition due to death or illness using a joint modeling approach.42–46 The proportional hazards assumption was checked using Schoenfeld residuals. Baseline differences in the mean LIBRA indexes between diagnostic groups (incident MCI or dementia vs. no incident diagnosis) were tested using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Cox regression models were fitted to evaluate the predictive validity of the calculated LIBRA(2) indexes for incident MCI or dementia risk over 8 years of follow-up (from the reset baseline Wave 5 to Wave 13), using HR and Harrell’s C-index. Models were adjusted for age, sex, and education, with or without joint modeling to account for attrition. Each index was tested both as continuous on its original scale or as categorical based on quartiles to improve robustness and reduce the bias from extreme values caused by discrepancies in the index range. This quartile-based approach also allowed an assessment of the spread of the index. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2) and SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC), with significance set at P < 0.05 based on two-sided tests. We used SAS procedures PROC PHREG to fit the Cox models and PROC NLMIXED to apply joint modeling.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics, including the prevalence of the LIBRA and LIBRA2 factors, were compared for incident MCI risk (Table 1), and for incident dementia risk (Table 2, with further details in Tables S2 and S3).

For incident MCI risk, the study sample included 712 MCI-free individuals with a mean age of 78.99 years (SD = 6.80) at baseline, of whom 136 (19.1%) developed incident MCI over 8 years. As expected, individuals who developed incident MCI were older, less educated, more depressed, and more likely to have hearing loss and sleep disturbances compared to those who remained MCI-free (all P < 0.05, Table 1).

For incident dementia risk, the study sample included 1118 dementia-free individuals with a mean age of 80.33 years (SD = 7.06) at baseline, of whom 79 (7.1%) developed incident dementia over 8 years. Individuals who developed incident dementia were older, less educated, had lower BMI, lower cognitive activity, and higher social isolation, less likely to be lifetime smokers, and more likely to have hearing loss than dementia-free individuals (all P < 0.05, Table 2).

Baseline LIBRA by different weight estimation methods

We generated three sets of factor weights each to calculate the different 11-factor LIBRA indexes (Table 3) and for the three different 14-factor LIBRA2 indexes (Table 4). We compared mean baseline LIBRA(2) indexes between individuals who developed incident dementia and those who did not, and similarly for incident MCI (Table 5). Among individuals who subsequently developed incident dementia, five calculated LIBRA(2) indexes were significantly higher at baseline, whereas only three indexes were significantly higher among those who developed incident MCI (P < 0.05, Table 5).

The predictive performance for incident MCI and dementia was assessed with baseline LIBRA as both a continuous variable (Table 6) and a categorical variable divided into quartiles (Tables 7 and 8, Figure 2), reporting the corresponding HR estimates for incident MCI and dementia.

Baseline LIBRA and incident MCI

After adjusting for age, sex, and education, using joint modeling to address informative attrition, among the three 11-factor LIBRA indexes (ie, LIBRA[Ref], LIBRA[MCI], and LIBRA[Dementia]), only higher baseline LIBRA[MCI] was significantly associated with an increased risk of incident MCI risk (P < 0.05, Table 6). When the three new factors were added, both the 14-factor LIBRA2[MCI] and LIBRA2 [Dementia], but not LIBRA(2)[Ref], were associated with increased risk of incident MCI (see Table 6).

When each calculated LIBRA index was categorized into quartiles at baseline, only the highest quartile (Q4) of baseline LIBRA2[MCI] significantly predicted a higher risk of incident MCI compared to the lowest quartile (Q1). Consistent results for incident MCI were obtained without joint modeling, aligning with those using joint modeling (Table 7).

Baseline LIBRA and incident dementia

Adjusting for the demographics and applying joint modeling to account for informative dropout, higher baseline values of each calculated 11-factor LIBRA index were associated with an increased risk of incident dementia: LIBRA[Ref], LIBRA[MCI], and LIBRA[Dementia]. Similar associations were consistently detected for each calculated 14-factor LIBRA2 (all P < 0.05, Table 6). Without joint modeling, LIBRA(2)[Ref] was no longer associated with incident dementia risk.

When categorized by baseline quartiles, both the third and fourth quartiles (Q3 and Q4) of the 11-factor LIBRA[Dementia] significantly predicted higher incident dementia risk compared to Q1, with HR estimates increasing sequentially. This pattern was also observed for the 14-factor LIBRA2[Dementia] (Table 8). In contrast, only the Q4 of LIBRA(2)[MCI] and LIBRA(2)[Ref] predicted higher incident dementia risk. Without joint modeling, none of the quartiles of LIBRA(2)[Ref] were associated with incident dementia risk. Overall, baseline LIBRA(2)[Dementia] demonstrated the strongest associations with incident dementia.

Discussion

The LIBRA index for dementia risk can help to identify at-risk populations for research and also help high-risk patients mitigate their individual risk factors. Using data from a population-based cohort of older Americans of relatively low socioeconomic status, we observed consistent associations between a higher baseline LIBRA(2) index and increased incident dementia risk over 8 years, across different factor weight estimation methods, with estimated HRs ranging from 1.04 to 1.40. Given current interest in the earliest possible detection of diseases underlying dementia, to facilitate early intervention, we also examined the LIBRA and LIBRA2 indexes as predictors of MCI which often precedes dementia. However, in the sub-cohort of 712 individuals who were cognitively normal at baseline and followed for the same period, baseline LIBRA(2) was not consistently associated with incident MCI risk. This contrast likely reflects the fact that, at the population level, most individuals with MCI do not progress to dementia, possibly because more conditions cause mild impairment than cause severe impairment.47

Comparisons with previous LIBRA studies

Direct comparisons of effect sizes across studies are challenging due to differences in LIBRA factor data availability and adjusted covariates. However, our results align with prior evidence linking higher LIBRA to increased dementia risk or cognitive decline in US population-based observational studies. For example, one study reported a strong longitudinal association between higher LIBRA and overall cognitive decline.48 Another study combining data from three distinct US cohort studies similarly found that higher LIBRA were associated with increased dementia risk.49 Additionally, in a study of former National Football League players aged 55 years and older, a cross-sectional association was detected between higher LIBRA and increased MCI risk.50

Our study also corroborates the predictive validity of LIBRA for dementia and MCI risk detected in international observational studies. A Dutch study found that higher LIBRA were associated with increased dementia risk over 16 years and of cognitive impairment risk over 12 years.6 In a multicenter study involving 8 harmonized European cohorts, LIBRA demonstrated good predictive validity for incident dementia among participants aged 55–79 years.10 More recently, a Finnish study showed that LIBRA effectively predicted the incidence of MCI and dementia up to 30 years later.13 Similarly, in a French cohort aged 74+ years, LIBRA predicted dementia incidence over a 17-year period.51

We compared the HR estimates for incident dementia obtained using LIBRA[Ref] calculated with the established reference weights, to those reported in previous studies that used the same weights, from two perspectives: (1) European vs. US population-base cohort studies, and (2) midlife or late-life (<80 years) vs. the oldest-old (80+ years) (Table S4).

European vs. US population-based cohort studies

A recent meta-analysis compared LIBRA’s performance across cohorts aged <80 years from different continental regions.21 When LIBRA was rescaled to its full range as all 12 factors available, HR estimates for dementia ranged from 0.98 to 1.10 in European cohorts and from 1.00 to 1.09 in US cohorts, adjusting for age, sex, and education. These findings suggest similar dementia predictive validity for LIBRA between European and US cohorts, regardless of whether the target population is in midlife or late-life.

Midlife or late-life (<80) vs. the oldest-old (80+)

Existing studies indicate that LIBRA performs less well in individuals aged 80+ compared to those under 80, consistent with its initial design to capture modifiable dementia risk in individuals aged 40–75 years. Among European cohort studies of midlife or late-life populations, HR and odds ratio (OR) estimates for dementia range between 1.01 and 1.27 per 1-point increase in LIBRA, adjusting to age, sex, and education. Studies focusing on the oldest-old are more limited. For example, an English study of participants aged 80+ reported an adjusted OR of 0.93 for dementia.11

In MYHAT, adjusting to age, sex, and education, the estimated HR for incident dementia by 11-factor LIBRA[Ref] using reference weights was 1.08 without joint modeling, and 1.06 with joint modeling. These results are consistent with the meta-analysis findings, which showed similar predictive validity for LIBRA between European and US cohort. These estimates also fall within the range observed in prior European cohort studies focusing on midlife or late-life populations; this alignment is expected, as approximately 70% of our participants analyzed for incident dementia were under 80 years old at baseline.

Despite these similarities, variations in factors such as ethnicity and healthcare systems highlight the need for more data from low- to middle-income countries and diverse ethnoracial groups. Expanding research in these areas could further refine the broad applicability of LIBRA.

LIBRA factor weight estimation

Most studies used reference factor weights derived from dementia risk estimates in published meta-analyses to calculate LIBRA.6,7 We also calculated LIBRA using factor weights based on MYHAT-specific data to compare the HRs derived from the two approaches.

When combining the results from both continuous and categorical baseline LIBRA(2), and addressing attrition bias through joint modeling, notable patterns emerged. Only the 14-factor LIBRA2[MCI] using MYHAT-specific HR estimates for MCI risk consistently demonstrated a strong association with incident MCI risk. Similarly, both the 11-factor LIBRA[Dementia] and the 14-factor LIBRA2[Dementia] using MYHAT-specific HR estimates for dementia risk consistently showed strong association with incident dementia risk. In contrast, LIBRA(2)[Ref] using the reference weights, was not associated with incident MCI. A possible explanation is that the reference weights were derived from HR estimates for dementia risk, not for MCI risk.

Furthermore, LIBRA(2)[Ref] was only associated with incident dementia when joint modeling was applied to address attrition bias. Because attrition (especially from mortality) shares risk factors with MCI and dementia, it is important to address attrition bias in longitudinal data analyses.42–46

Our findings suggest that following the LIBRA algorithm,6 the association between the LIBRA index and incident MCI or incident dementia risk can be strengthened by using cohort-specific factor weights based on the same risk outcome. This approach improves the predictive validity of LIBRA within the targeted population. However, the use of cohort-specific factor weights limits the comparability of the calculated LIBRA indexes across different cohort studies. For studies restricted to single cohorts, both cohort-specific factor weights as well as the common-used LIBRA factor weights established by existing literature and meta-analyses, deserve exploration when calculating LIBRA indexes.

LIBRA factor extension

The LIBRA index has been updated to LIBRA2 with three new modifiable factors: hearing impairment, sleep disturbance, and low social contact.28,29 While LIBRA2 has been less widely validated than the original LIBRA, a recent comparison of the dementia-predicting performance of the original LIBRA and LIBRA2 29 showed similar performance in an English cohort, while LIBRA2 outperformed LIBRA in a Dutch cohort.

In the MYHAT study, the calculated 14-factor LIBRA2 indexes generally performed as well as the 11-factor LIBRA for predicting incident dementia but performed better than LIBRA for predicting incident MCI. Specifically, when categorized by baseline quartiles, a significant association was observed only between LIBRA2[MCI] and incident MCI risk (Q4 vs. Q1). Based on MYHAT-specific HR estimates, only two of the new factors added in LIBRA2, hearing impairment and sleep disturbance, were associated with MCI incidence. Incorporating these new factors into LIBRA2[MCI] may strengthen its association with MCI incidence, corroborating findings from the umbrella review and confirming the importance of adding newly identified factors to enhance LIBRA’s performance.

Some previous studies have also showed that including non-modifiable demographic and other characteristics can improve LIBRA’s dementia predictive performance.6,10 For example, a study of former football players achieved better model fitting by extending LIBRA with a cohort-specific factor of concussion history for MCI diagnosis.29 Adding flexibility in the selection of factors for LIBRA, particularly the non-modifiable demographic or cohort-specific factors, could improve its adaptability and predictive performance for diverse targeted population.

Strengths and limitations

The major strengths of the present study include its population-based design, the relatively long follow-up period starting in late-life, the use of reliable measures validated in previous analyses of the MYHAT study, the inclusion as outcomes of both incident MCI and incident dementia, the use of joint modeling, and the innovative comparison of different estimation methods for LIBRA factor weights. Additionally, the cohort was drawn from a stable population in a low-socioeconomic Rust Belt area of the United States, a type of community that is not well-represented in prior research.

Regarding study limitations, we could only evaluate 11 of the 12 original LIBRA factors as we did not collect dietary data. Further we did not collect data on cognitive activities at study entry but only starting with the fifth annual assessment. Our response codes for alcohol use do not capture the CDC definition of moderate drinking.38 The study was limited to later life, and only around 5% of the cohort identified as non-White, restricting generalizability to midlife populations and ethnoracial minority groups in the United States. Additionally, reliance on self-reported health conditions and lifestyle factors limits comparisons with other studies that utilize medical record data. Moreover, the analyses results do not entirely account for the potential reverse causation in the associations between the LIBRA index and dementia.

Conclusion

In this late-life cohort from a relatively low-income area of the United States, our results demonstrated a stronger association between higher baseline LIBRA indexes with increased incident dementia risk compared to incident MCI risk over 8 years. These findings confirm the dementia predictive validity of the LIBRA index and highlight its potential utility in dementia prevention strategies.

Incorporating three new modifiable factors improved the overall dementia predictive performance of the updated LIBRA2 index. Additionally, our results suggest that using cohort-specific factor weights improves the predictive validity of LIBRA for dementia risk. However, established reference weights facilitate comparisons across cohorts. Future studies should evaluate the utility of LIBRA with a broader range of modifiable factors and explore alternative weight estimation methods to further improve its adaptability for diverse populations.
