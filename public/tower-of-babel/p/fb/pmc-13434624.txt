Multimodal deep learning model for AI-based functional prognostic risk stratification in patients undergoing radical nephrectomy
Authors: Yunhan Luo, Yatian Wang, Xiangpeng Zou, Shiying Tang, Xin Luo, Zhaohui Zhou, Longbin Xiong, Yulu Peng, Chunsen Yang, Ning Wang, Haitian Song, Gaoyu Zou, Jinhao Shi, Xiangyu Zi, Ming Gao, Nan Jia, Ping Yang, Fengfeng Yang, Zaosong Zheng, Peng Wu, Wen Dong, Pei Dong, Shengjie Guo, Hui Han, Shimiao Zhu, Jinchao Chen, Junhang Luo, Wei Zhai, Yawen Xu, Jianhui Chen, Yu Fan, Le Qu, Xiaonan Chen, Jiaxin Zhuang, Hao Chen, Chunping Yu, Xuepei Zhang, Qifeng Liu, Fangjian Zhou, Shudong Zhang

ABSTRACT
Making the decision between technically challenging partial nephrectomy (PN) and radical nephrectomy (RN) in patients with complex renal cell carcinoma (RCC) remains a significant challenge for urologists. Rapid glomerular filtration rate (GFR) decline (annual decline >3 mL/min/1.73 m²) after RN is considered an abnormal renal function state, and if this risk can be predicted preoperatively, PN may be pursued even when technically demanding. We retrospectively analyze contrast-enhanced computed tomography images and clinical data from 1621 patients across multiple centers. A multimodal deep learning model is developed to predict rapid GFR decline after RN. The model achieves an area under the curve of 0.788–0.873 in external test sets. It stratifies patients into high- and low-risk groups with significantly different risks of chronic kidney disease progression. Here we show that the model demonstrates potential for assisting treatment decisions in patients with complex RCC for whom PN is challenging but feasible.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for approximately 2–3% of all malignancies. In 2022, the global burden of RCC was reflected by an estimated 434,840 new diagnoses and 155,953 associated deaths1. For nonmetastatic RCC, surgery remains the primary curative treatment. When technically feasible, partial nephrectomy (PN) is the reference standard for treating localized renal masses as it provides better preservation of renal function. However, for tumors with high complexity, radical nephrectomy (RN) is more commonly performed, as PN may be challenging or technically unfeasible.

According to data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program from 2018 to 2022, the median tumor size among patients undergoing RN was 5.5 cm (IQR: 4–7 cm), with 23.7% classified as T1a and 27.6% as T1b. These data suggest that a nonnegligible proportion of RN patients might have been candidates for kidney preservation. According to the NCCN guidelines2, PN is generally the preferred approach for T1a tumors, and PN is also the first therapeutic recommendation for T1b-T2 tumors.

Two major considerations may influence surgeons’ decision-making when managing a complex renal mass: can PN be performed safely? Is renal function preservation imperative for the patient? The first question largely depends on tumor complexity; however, for moderately complex renal masses (R.E.N.A.L score 7–9), surgeons’ experience also plays a crucial role. Several studies have reported the feasibility of PN for moderately to highly complex renal masses when performed by experienced surgeons3–5. Evidently, the answers to the first question vary among surgeons with different levels of PN expertise, particularly when facing technically challenging cases. Regarding the second question, long-term renal function following nephrectomy remains the key consideration. Approximately 80% of patients experience a decline in kidney function within the first year after surgery, after which renal function generally stabilizes over time6. However, a certain proportion of patients go on to develop progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD) once a new baseline renal function has been established. Rapid GFR (glomerular filtration rate) decline is an abnormal renal function state defined as an annual GFR decrease of >3 mL/min/1.73 m², after reaching their new baseline glomerular filtration rate (NBGFR). Rapid GFR decline is strongly associated with an increased risk of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) and cardiovascular events, ultimately contributing to higher all-cause mortality rates among patients7,8. Given these risks, it is critical to identify patients at higher risk for rapid GFR decline after RN. For such patients with complex RCC, pre-surgical neoadjuvant therapy should be considered to shrink the tumor and facilitate the application of PN9,10, and referral to more experienced surgeons should also be considered. Alternatively, if patients with a low risk of rapid postoperative GFR decline can be accurately identified before surgery, this information could support surgeons in selecting RN as an appropriate treatment option for complex RCC patients. In clinical practice, however, rapid GFR decline remains difficult to predict using traditional methods.

Currently, most related studies have focused on predicting relatively short-term changes in renal function (typically around 12 months) following RN, often relying on simple regression analysis11,12. However, prediction models capable of assessing long-term postoperative renal function changes extending beyond two years remain scarce. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a powerful tool in the diagnosis and management of tumors13. In the field of renal cancer, applications of radiomics and deep learning (DL) based on contrast-enhanced CT imaging are well established, particularly for diagnostic and prognostic purposes14–16. Despite these advances, there is a notable lack of research leveraging these advanced AI technologies to predict long-term postoperative renal function in patients with RCC.

In this study, we initially developed a multivariate logistic regression model using preoperative clinical information to predict rapid GFR decline after RN. However, the model’s accuracy was insufficient for reliable clinical use. Given that contrast-enhanced CT is routinely performed as a pre-surgical assessment for renal masses17, and that CT-derived parameters (such as renal cortical attenuation and volume) are closely correlated with renal function18,19, we sought to enhance predictive performance by integrating both contrast-enhanced CT imaging features and clinical data. To achieve this, we conducted a retrospective, multicenter analysis incorporating data from 15 centers. With the comprehensive 3D CT imaging data and corresponding clinical data, we constructed a multimodal prediction model for postoperative rapid GFR decline after RN, termed the rapid GFR decline prediction model (RDPM), which is intended to assist surgical decision-making for patients with complex RCC. We compared the RDPM with comparative models, evaluating its ability to stratify patients by risk of rapid GFR decline following RN. Additionally, we validated the RDPM by correlating its predicted outcomes with both the incidence of CKD and the histologic CKD score. The model demonstrates potential for assisting treatment decisions in patients with complex RCC for whom PN is challenging but feasible.

Results

Basic information of the cohorts

Between September 10, 2007, and September 12, 2023, a total of 2365 potentially eligible patients from 15 hospitals underwent RN, were pathologically diagnosed with RCC without evidence of distant metastasis on either imaging or pathological examination, and had available serum creatinine measurements. No missing data were observed for these patients. The flowchart of patient selection is detailed in Fig. 1. Following the application of exclusion criteria and subsequent randomization, 1039 patients were assigned to the training set, 260 patients to the internal test set, and 322 patients (126 from hospital 13, 85 from hospital 14, and 111 from hospital 15) to the external test sets. During a median follow-up period of 3.50 years (IQR, 2.59–4.93), 306 (18.9%) patients experienced rapid GFR decline. R.E.N.A.L nephrometry score and the feasibility of PN

Representative CT images illustrating the PN feasibility assessment are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The median R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry score for all patients was 9 (IQR: 8–10). A summary of the feasibility of PN is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Overall, 51% of patients were considered suitable for PN by experienced nephron-sparing surgeons (4% feasible and easy, 47% feasible but challenging), with a median R.E.N.A.L. score of 8 (IQR: 7–9), indicating moderate complexity. 33% of patients were judged to be completely unrealistic for PN, with a median R.E.N.A.L. score of 10 (IQR: 9–11); among them, 3% had renal vein or inferior vena cava tumor thrombus, 22% had tumors ≥7 cm involving the renal hilum, and 7% had indistinct tumor margins. An additional 16% of patients were deemed potentially feasible after 20% tumor shrinkage following neoadjuvant therapy20,21, with a median R.E.N.A.L. score of 9 (IQR: 8.75–10). Within this group, 5% of tumors were closely abutting the renal hilum and 10% extended into the renal sinus. It was also observed that there was substantial inter-center variability in surgeons’ subjective indications for RN (Supplementary Table 2). The proportion of RN cases with low R.E.N.A.L. scores varied markedly across centers, ranging from 3% to 21%.

RDPM construction and performance evaluation

In the logistic regression analyses, age, preoperative estimated GFR (eGFR), diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HTN), and tumor diameter—which showed statistically significant p-values (p < 0.05) in the univariate analyses and were also considered clinically relevant—were included in the multivariate analysis to construct the clinical model (Supplementary Table 3). These clinical predictors were also utilized to construct the RDPM using a multi-head attention mechanism. The workflow of the kidney segmentation and processing is shown in Fig. 2A. The architecture of the RDPM is presented in Fig. 2B. Figure 3A shows the Representative examples of CT images. As shown in Fig. 3B–E and Supplementary Fig. 2, the RDPM achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.864 (95% CI: 0.809–0.919) in the internal test set, and AUCs of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.804–0.941), 0.814 (95% CI: 0.722–0.906), and 0.788 (95% CI: 0.641–0.934) across different external test sets, consistently outperforming all other models and confirming its robust generalizability. RDPM demonstrated a superior AUC compared to the CT with Attn DL model, the CT-only DL model, and the clinical model (all p < 0.05), as assessed using the DeLong test. Additionally, RDPM exhibited higher sensitivity than each of the other models (binomial test, all p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4). Comparative tables of the RDPM with state-of-the-art methods, along with additional ablation analyses, are shown in Supplementary Tables 5–7. AUC values of the RDPM across various subgroups are summarized in Supplementary Table 8. The model was also well-calibrated, showing strong agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event frequencies (Fig. 3F). The Brier scores for the different models were 0.120 (RDPM), 0.133 (CT with Attn DL model), 0.162 (CT-only DL model), and 0.242 (clinical model). Notably, the decision curve analysis (DCA) plot confirmed its clinical applicability, revealing that the RDPM provided the highest net benefit over the widest range of clinically relevant risk thresholds (Fig. 3G), establishing it as a more reliable and useful tool for guiding clinical decisions. The performance metrics—including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, and F1-score—of all models in all test sets (Fig. 3H), the internal test set (Fig. 3I), and the external test sets (Fig. 3J) are visualized using radar plots, which demonstrate that RDPM exhibits a more balanced overall performance. Additionally, more detailed information on the performance of all models is provided in Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4, where radar plots and heatmaps further illustrate their results. Associations between RDPM and incidence of stage 3b CKD or stage 4 CKD

The prognostic utility of the RDPM was further evaluated. Based on the binary predictions generated by the RDPM, patients were classified into “high-risk” and “low-risk” groups (training set: 234 vs. 805; internal test set: 47 vs. 213; external test set: 91 vs. 231, respectively). In Kaplan–Meier analyses across different cohorts, the hazard ratios for developing CKD stage 3b or higher were all significant between the high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 4A–C and Supplementary Fig. 6A; log-rank test, all p < 0.05). Similarly, the hazard ratios for progression to CKD stage 4 or higher were also significant in all cohorts (Fig. 4D–F and Supplementary Fig. 6B; log-rank test, all p < 0.05). Discussion

The probability of CKD progression following kidney surgery remains a major concern for both urologists and patients with RCC. In recent years, the GFR slope has emerged as a key indicator, demonstrating a strong association with subsequent kidney failure across diverse patient subgroups22. A large retrospective study focusing on postoperative renal function after nephrectomy demonstrated that patients experienced a mean decline in GFR of 0.66 mL/min/1.73 m² per year following RN, with a proportion of patients developing advanced CKD beyond the first year after surgery6. In our study, we found that 306 patients (18.9%) experienced rapid GFR decline after reaching their NBGFR, which is considered an abnormal state of renal function, during a median follow-up of 3.50 years. Substantial evidence indicates that rapid GFR decline (annual decline >3 mL/min/1.73 m²) is significantly associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD)7,8. Therefore, accurately predicting post-RN rapid GFR decline before surgery is crucial for informing optimal surgical decision-making in patients with complex RCC, in which PN is challenging but remains feasible.

The feasibility of PN largely depends on tumor characteristics, including size, location, and degree of endophytic growth. The R.E.N.A.L. score is currently one of the most widely used objective indices for assessing PN complexity. In our cohort, the median R.E.N.A.L. score was 9 (IQR 8–10), representing intermediate difficulty for PN. For such intermediate cases, successful PN is not universally unachievable, as several studies3,4 have reported that PN can be successfully performed in cases of moderate complexity, particularly considering that there are 22% of the patients in the current study had a R.E.N.A.L. score ≤7. Assessments by two experienced surgeons in our group also supported this view, estimating that 51% of our patients were potential PN candidates, despite the considerable surgical challenge (median R.E.N.A.L. score 8, IQR 7–9). More importantly, the baseline tumor characteristics, including tumor stage and size of our RN cohort, were comparable to those reported in large international series and in the SEER database, suggesting that undergoing RN for technically feasible PN cases was not unique to our cohort but rather reflects broader clinical practice patterns23. The main reason for this phenomenon may be attributed to heterogeneity in surgeons’ PN experience. Under these circumstances, accurately identifying which patients undergoing RN are at risk for postoperative renal functional decline is crucial, supporting its potential for assisting treatment decisions in patients with complex RCC for whom PN is challenging but remains feasible.

With the rapid advancement of deep learning technology, its role in medical imaging has become increasingly significant, particularly in prognostic analysis, where it provides powerful exploratory tools. While certain CT imaging features may reflect aspects of renal function24, relying solely on visual inspection to evaluate long-term CKD progression remains challenging and of limited utility in clinical practice. In this study, with CT images and clinical data collected from 15 centers, we developed a multimodal deep learning framework that synergistically integrates imaging and clinical data, leveraging a triple-input 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture enhanced with multi-head attention mechanisms to enable feasible and accurate prediction of long-term renal outcomes. The fusion of multimodal data is critical, as it allows for a more comprehensive and robust risk assessment than models that rely on a single data source alone. Previous studies have largely neglected the potential of fully utilizing 3D CT images, often focusing only on individual slices, which results in the loss of valuable volumetric and structural information. Our use of a 3D CNN backbone is particularly effective for analyzing volumetric CT data, as it captures complex spatial information and avoids the information loss inherent in 2D slice-based methods. Furthermore, the innovative triple-input design and the Visual Attention Module provide a more holistic and effective representation by processing the raw image, segmented parenchyma, and surrounding context in parallel, allowing the model to learn the most representative semantic information from both intra-renal features and their interaction with adjacent structures. This design enables the model to capture features from adjacent structures, such as perirenal fat, which has been shown to be significantly associated with CKD25,26. Another key advancement of our framework is the use of a cross-attention mechanism to fuse the imaging and clinical data streams. This approach moves beyond simple concatenation, empowering the model to dynamically weigh each clinical risk factor in the context of the patient’s specific anatomy. As a sophisticated tool for multimodal analysis, our attention-based fusion model enhances predictive performance by capturing the intricate interplay between imaging biomarkers and clinical variables, offering a promising pathway toward more accurate and personalized risk stratification. Based on a holistic assessment of performance, RDPM demonstrates superior and well-balanced predictive performance compared to other models.

In both the training and test sets, patients were significantly stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups according to RDPM predictions. Kaplan–Meier analysis for incidence of CKD further demonstrated the prognostic value of the RDPM, showing that incidence of CKD events occurring after reaching NBGFR were significantly associated with the risk categories predicted by the RDPM. Notably, stage 3b CKD or higher is widely regarded as severe renal insufficiency, both in the general population and in patients following PN or RN, and has been closely correlated with increased mortality and further functional decline6,27. By comparing the risks of stage 3b and stage 4 CKD, the model’s predictions demonstrated a strong association with long-term postoperative CKD progression, further highlighting the clinical utility of the RDPM. Numerous studies have demonstrated associations between quantitative features of NNRP and renal function decline after RN28,29. In previous assessments using validated histologic CKD scores30, the high-risk group identified by the RDPM also exhibited more pronounced deterioration in histologic status compared to the low-risk group. These findings suggest that RDPM predictions are closely linked to histologic changes in the NNRP and contribute to the interpretability of the model. To further enhance model accuracy by incorporating additional modalities, a DL model based on NNRP pathology slices is currently under investigation at our center.

Various models have been developed to predict postoperative renal function following surgery for nonmetastatic renal tumors31. However, most studies have primarily focused on assessing renal function at a single, specific postoperative time point within 12 months, while few investigations have explored renal function decline over longer periods (beyond 24 months)31. Bhindi et al. developed a multivariable model to predict GFR value at any time point beyond 30 days following RN or PN, incorporating age, GFR, diabetes, proteinuria, and tumor size; however, the predictive capability was limited, with a marginal R² of 0.41 for RN32. Our study introduces rapid GFR decline as a metric for postoperative renal function evaluation in RN patients. Furthermore, previous predictions of postoperative renal function after RN based on contrast-enhanced CT images have been limited, with outcomes typically restricted to GFR values or binary classification based on reaching specific CKD stages, such as CKD stage 3b or 4, within 2 years after surgery12,33. In contrast, our model integrates CT imaging and deep learning approaches to predict the postoperative GFR slope, offering a more nuanced and dynamic assessment of renal function following RN.

Our study demonstrates substantial potential for assisting clinical decision-making in patients with complex renal tumors for whom PN is challenging but remains feasible, especially given that higher intraoperative and postoperative complication rates have been reported for PN compared to RN in cases of larger tumors (≥7 cm)34. For tumors characterized by high surgical complexity, recent studies have evaluated the efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy for tumor downsizing and have shown that such therapy may enable safe and feasible PN with preservation of renal function in these patients9,10. In our study, for patients scheduled to undergo RN and identified as high-risk for rapid GFR decline by the RDPM, we recommend the administration of neoadjuvant therapy, followed by PN when feasible. For high-risk patients who ultimately proceed to RN, more frequent monitoring of renal function and timely referral to nephrology are advised35. Conversely, in patients scheduled for challenging PN who are predicted to be at low risk for rapid GFR decline by the RDPM, opting for RN instead of PN may help reduce the risk of intraoperative and postoperative complications while maintaining comparable renal functional outcomes (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8).

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective analysis with a relatively limited follow-up period (median follow-up of 3.50 years), which may limit the assessment of long-term renal outcomes. Future studies with prolonged follow-up are necessary to more fully capture and evaluate long-term renal outcomes after RN. Second, the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria applied in our study may introduce selection bias and thus limit the generalizability of our model. With advances in oncology, targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors are increasingly used in specific subtypes and may affect serum creatinine levels36. The predictive performance of our model in this patient subgroup warrants further validation in future research. Third, GFR in our study was estimated using serum creatinine without adjustment to standardized values, which may compromise the accuracy and consistency of the measurements. Studies utilizing cystatin C alone or in combination with creatinine, with appropriate corrections applied, are needed to improve the accuracy and reliability of renal function assessment. Moreover, urine protein levels were not included in our analysis. Future multicenter studies that incorporate markers, such as proteinuria or serum albumin, may help enhance the predictive accuracy of the model. Finally, our analysis included only Asian patients. Further validation of our model in racially and ethnically diverse cohorts is necessary. In addition, the reliance on contrast-enhanced CT and advanced computational resources presents practical barriers to implementation in many clinical settings, although our center is currently developing related platforms and software to improve accessibility.

In summary, we developed a deep learning-based prediction model, RDPM, on preoperative CT and clinical information for the rapid GFR decline of RCC patients after RN. RDPM demonstrated promised prediction performance and good interpretability, which could be used to assist the treatment choice of complex RCC patients, for whom PN is challenging but remains feasible.

Methods

Patient population

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and complies with all relevant ethical regulations. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, Peking University First Hospital, Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital, Peking University Third Hospital, Eastern Theater Command General Hospital, Renji Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Fujian Medical University Union Hospital, The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, The Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University, Zhujiang Hospital, Zhejiang Cancer Hospital, Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Nanfang Hospital, Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University, and The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Sex and/or gender of participants was determined based on self-report. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex were performed to evaluate model performance (AUC) across male and female participants in different test cohorts. Informed consent for patients was waived, as the study involved only the analysis of pre-existing data, posed no risk to patients, and involved no intervention. Nonmetastatic RCC patients who underwent RN at 15 hospitals between September 10, 2007 and September 12, 2023, were included in this study. Ethical standards were strictly followed, and patient confidentiality as well as data integrity were rigorously safeguarded. eGFR was calculated using the creatinine-based CKD-EPI 2021 equation37. Rapid GFR decline was defined by eGFR decline >3 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year. Serum creatinine levels were retrospectively collected for each patient. eGFR would be calculated as 0 mL/min/1.73 m2 after receiving dialysis or undergoing kidney transplantation. NBGFR was defined as the highest eGFR within 1–12 months post-RN38, and the eGFR decline rate was calculated between NBGFR and the latest eGFR. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) receiving unilateral RN and pathologically diagnosed as RCC without evidence of distant metastasis on either imaging or pathological examination. (2) Having complete clinicopathological data. (3) With available serum creatinine at pre-RN (within 1 month before RN), new baseline (1–12 months post-RN), and latest follow-up (>24 months post-RN). If the patient received systemic therapy due to tumor progression more than 2 years after surgery, the last serum creatinine measurement prior to the initiation of treatment was considered as latest follow-up. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) without available contrast-enhanced CT performed within one month before RN. (2) Poor-quality CT imaging data or CT scan did not provide complete coverage of the contralateral kidney. (3) Patients with RCC who received any systematic therapy within 3 months prior to RN or within 2 years after RN. (4) Patients who routinely received dialysis or had a history of kidney transplantation before surgery, as well as those with stage 5 CKD. (5) Patients with a history of kidney surgery either before or after RN; (6) patients with bilateral kidney tumors. (7) Patients with congenital kidney abnormalities (such as polycystic kidney, horseshoe kidney, etc.). (8) Patients with familial hereditary kidney cancer (e.g., VHL syndrome, HPRC). Demographic, clinicopathologic, and perioperative parameters were collected through a retrospective review. CT images were collected in DICOM format. The cohort combined of 12 hospitals’ datasets was randomly divided into two subsets: the internal training set (n = 1039) and the internal test set (n = 260) in a 4:1 ratio. The hospital 13 set (n = 126), hospital 14 set (n = 85) and hospital 15 (n = 111) set served as external test sets.

Evaluation of the R.E.N.A.L nephrometry score and the feasibility of PN by two experienced surgeons

The R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry score was evaluated according to the system proposed by Kutikov et al.39, with scores of 4–6, 7–9, and 10–12 representing low-, moderate-, and high-complexity PN cases, respectively. Evaluation of the R.E.N.A.L. score and the feasibility of PN was jointly performed in consensus by two experienced urologic oncologists (Z.L.Z., P.D.; each with more than 10–15 years of experience in nephron-sparing surgery and having performed over 1000 PNs, including more than 200 complex cases), and a third senior expert (F.J.Z.; >20 years of experience in nephron-sparing surgery and having performed over 1500 PNs, including more than 300 complex cases) was consulted in cases of disagreement. The feasibility of PN was categorized as feasible (easy), feasible (challenging), potentially feasible after 20% tumor shrinkage following neoadjuvant therapy40–42, or completely unrealistic. In the potentially feasible group, the tumors, despite exceeding 4 cm in diameter and being closely abutting the renal hilum or extending into the renal sinus, demonstrated a well-defined pseudocapsule. Cases considered completely unrealistic for PN included those with renal vein or inferior vena cava tumor thrombus, tumors ≥7 cm involving the renal hilum, or lesions with indistinct margins.

CT image segmentation

The information about the CT dataset collected from fifteen centers are detailed in Supplementary Table 10. The workflow illustrating kidney segmentation and processing is shown in Fig. 2A. To start, all patient data were rigorously de-identified to ensure privacy. Then, we segmented CT images to obtain the cortex and medulla of the contralateral kidney using a pre-trained UNesT model, which consists of a transformer-based architecture43. To maintain anatomical accuracy and guarantee segmentation accuracy, a rule-based post-processing was applied to eliminate segmentation artifacts, keeping only the largest spatially contiguous region corresponding to the kidney. In addition, the automatically segmented kidney parenchyma was manually reviewed and adjusted as needed in a small number of cases. Modifications to the contralateral kidney were performed jointly by two experienced urologic oncologists (Z.L.Z., P.D.; 10–15 years’ experience) in consensus, with a third expert (F.J.Z.; >20 years’ experience) consulted in case of disagreement. For each patient, CT scan images were evaluated, and regions of interest (ROIs) were adjusted utilizing the 3D Slicer software (version 5.8.1; https://www.slicer.org/). Segmentation accuracy evaluation and representative examples are presented in Supplementary Table 11 and Supplementary Fig. 9, respectively.

The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule. To enhance robustness and prevent overfitting, we employed several strategies: data augmentation (rotations and flips), 5-fold cross-validation, a weighted loss function to handle class imbalance, and dropout regularization. Training was accelerated using mixed precision on NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

The model’s performance was meticulously evaluated using several metrics. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for the RDPM in the internal test set, and the hospital 13, hospital 14, and hospital 15 cohorts, and the corresponding AUC was calculated. Comparisons between AUCs were performed with the DeLong test. Comparisons between accuracy and sensitivity were performed with the binomial test. The agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes was evaluated using calibration curves and Brier scores. DCA plot was conducted to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the RDPM across different threshold probabilities. Radar plots were generated to compare the performance of different models in predicting rapid GFR decline in all test sets, the internal test set, and external test sets. Based on RDPM predictions, patients in both the training and test sets were classified as high-risk if they were predicted to experience rapid GFR decline, and as low-risk if they were predicted not to experience rapid GFR decline, for further analysis.

Our proposed RDPM integrates features from preoperative CT images with structured clinical data to predict the risk of rapid renal function decline following RN. Comparative analysis of incidence of stage 3b CKD and stage 4 CKD

The outcomes were defined as the time from surgery to the first observed eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 (CKD 3b) and the first observed eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 (CKD 4)6, considering NBGFR and all subsequent eGFR measurements during follow-up as eligible for event determination. For these outcomes, patient follow-up was censored at the time of the last available serum creatinine measurement. The incidence of stage 3b and stage 4 CKD was subsequently compared between high-risk patients and low-risk patients defined by RDPM.

Assessment of parenchymal histologic CKD score

To enhance the interpretability of the RDPM, 2-μm paraffin-embedded sections were obtained from eligible patients at hospital 5, hospital 1, hospital 4, and hospital 15 for H&E staining and subsequent evaluation of CKD score. For each patient, NNRP paraffin sections containing at least 30 glomeruli, sampled from areas distant from the tumor, were selected for analysis48. Cases lacking adequate NNRP in remote regions or exhibiting suboptimal staining quality were excluded. The CKD score was assessed following the Mayo Clinic protocol with minor modifications30. The pathological parameters evaluated included global and segmental GS, TA, IF, and AS. Each parameter was semi-quantitatively scored on a 0–3 scale, corresponding to involvement of <10%, 10–25%, 25–50%, and >50% for GS, TA, and IF, respectively; AS was graded analogously based on the degree of luminal occlusion49. To enhance the consistency and reproducibility of the scoring results, TA and IF were scored using identical scales, with concordance between the two required as part of the scoring criteria. All CKD scores were independently evaluated by two experienced nephropathologists (N.J. and P.Y.), who were blinded to clinical outcomes and RDPM predictions, demonstrating high inter-observer agreement. The CKD scores were then compared between the high-risk patients and the low-risk patients defined by RDPM. All histopathological assessments were performed using QuPath (version 0.5.1).

Statistics and reproducibility

Continuous variables are reported as median [IQR], and categorical variables as number (percentage). Group comparisons for CKD scores used the Mann–Whitney U test. The cumulative incidence of CKD was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank test. For multiple groups, continuous variables were analyzed by the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables by chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate logistic regression were entered into multivariate analysis. All p values are two-sided; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in R 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism 10.1.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA); the deep learning model was built using Python version 3.11.11. Sample size determination was performed using PASS version 15.0.5. Information on data exclusion (including reasons for exclusion) is provided in Fig. 1. The dataset was randomly split into a training set and an internal test set, with the training set further divided into a training subset and a validation subset using Python’s random module. CKD scores were evaluated by two experienced nephropathologists who were blinded to clinical outcomes and RDPM predictions.
