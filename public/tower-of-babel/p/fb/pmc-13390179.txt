The predictive value of the TAPSE/PASP ratio for major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with chronic heart failure: evidence from a prolonged follow-up cohort
Authors: Xiaohan Wang, Jiatong Li, Ping He, Tianyue Li, Weimeng Cheng, Zhonghai Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to pulmonary artery systolic pressure (TAPSE/PASP) ratio is a noninvasive surrogate of right ventricular–pulmonary arterial coupling. Previous evidence focused mainly on in-hospital, 90-day, or 1-year outcomes; long-term prognostic value across heart failure phenotypes remains unclear.MethodsThis cohort included 440 chronic heart failure patients admitted between 2019 and 2021. Median age was 67 years; 31.1% were female; median follow-up was 41 months. Restricted cubic spline analysis assessed association between TAPSE/PASP and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), defined as all-cause mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or heart failure rehospitalization. Kaplan–Meier and multivariable Cox regression analyses evaluated associations. Incremental value was assessed using C-statistics, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsDuring follow-up, 193 patients experienced MACEs. TAPSE/PASP showed an approximately linear inverse association with MACE risk, with an exploratory cut-off of 0.036. After adjustment including natriuretic peptides, each 0.01-unit increase in TAPSE/PASP was associated with lower MACE risk (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.66–0.87; p = 6.02 × 10−5), whereas TAPSE/PASP ≤0.036 was associated with higher risk (HR 1.98, 95% CI 1.37–2.85; p = 2.57 × 10−4). Adding TAPSE/PASP modestly improved discrimination (C-statistic 0.80 vs. 0.77), reclassification (NRI 0.31), and IDI (0.04) (all p < 0.05). Associations were consistent across prespecified subgroups.ConclusionsIn chronic heart failure patients, lower TAPSE/PASP was independently associated with long-term MACE risk across LVEF-defined phenotypes. TAPSE/PASP may provide complementary prognostic information, but its clinical utility requires prospective external validation.

Introduction

Chronic heart failure, representing the common end-stage outcome of diverse cardiovascular diseases, remains a significant clinical and public health challenge [1,2]. Studies indicate that approximately 64.6 million individuals live with heart failure globally, with an all-cause mortality rate of about 20%-30% within one year of diagnosis and a staggering 45%-60% within five years [3]. The progressive decline in cardiac function not only severely impairs patients’ quality of life but also imposes a substantial economic burden on families [4,5]. Despite notable advances in therapeutic strategies in recent years, the prognosis for many patients with chronic heart failure remains suboptimal, as evidenced by persistently high rates of rehospitalization and mortality. This underscores the ongoing need for refined risk-stratification strategies [6,7].

Cardiac performance in chronic heart failure depends on complex interactions among the cardiac chambers. Although most studies focused on left ventricular function, right ventricular (RV) dysfunction and deranged pulmonary haemodynamics have prognostic relevance across heart failure phenotypes with different left ventricular ejection fractions [8–10]. It is hypothesized that elevated left-sided pressures induce structural and functional alterations in the pulmonary vasculature, leading to increased capillary pressures and, ultimately, impairment of RV function [11]. The RV is particularly sensitive to changes in afterload, and RV–pulmonary arterial (RV–PA) coupling reflects the ability of the RV to adapt to pulmonary vascular load and to maintain adequate RV output [12]. Importantly, pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) alone may be misleading in advanced RV failure because severely reduced RV contractility can limit pressure generation, rendering PASP normal or even low despite a high haemodynamic burden [13]. For this reason, PASP should be interpreted together with measures of RV function for accurate haemodynamic assessment. Right heart catheterization (RHC) remains the invasive gold standard for assessment of RV–PA coupling, but it is costly and not readily applicable to outpatient or serial assessments. Transthoracic echocardiography, by contrast, can evaluate RV function and pulmonary pressure noninvasively—for example, by measuring tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and estimating PASP. The TAPSE/PASP ratio integrates an index of RV systolic function and an estimate of RV afterload, and is therefore proposed as a practical noninvasive surrogate of RV–PA coupling [14,15]. Nevertheless, the relationship between TAPSE/PASP and clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure remains under active investigation.

Owing to its simplicity and reproducibility, the TAPSE/PASP ratio has attracted increasing attention as a potential prognostic marker in heart failure. For example, among elderly patients with acute decompensated HFpEF, reduced TAPSE/PASP was associated with higher in-hospital mortality and increased 90-day readmission risk, independent of age and comorbidities [16]. Similar prognostic associations were also observed in patients with HFrEF, in whom lower TAPSE/PASP predicted 1-year major adverse cardiovascular events [17]. Moreover, studies focusing on HFpEF populations across different clinical settings have suggested that a reduced TAPSE/PASP ratio is associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and heart failure–related rehospitalization [18,19]. Despite these findings, most available evidence is derived from studies of acute heart failure or from cohorts restricted to specific heart failure phenotypes, with outcomes mainly assessed during hospitalization, within 90 days, or at approximately 1 year of follow-up. Therefore, the relationship between TAPSE/PASP and long-term prognosis across the full spectrum of left ventricular ejection fraction in chronic heart failure remains incompletely characterized.

To address this gap, we performed a retrospective cohort study with a prolonged follow-up period to investigate the prognostic value of the TAPSE/PASP ratio for long-term major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), defined as a composite of all-cause mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and heart failure rehospitalization, in patients with chronic heart failure across LVEF-defined phenotypes.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study consecutively screened all hospitalized patients diagnosed with chronic heart failure at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, the Affiliated Hospital of the Medical School of Nanjing University, between January 2019 and December 2021. The diagnosis of CHF was established in accordance with the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure, as referenced in the 2023 ESC focused update [20]. Briefly, CHF was diagnosed based on typical symptoms and/or signs of heart failure, such as dyspnea, fatigue, peripheral edema, pulmonary congestion, or elevated jugular venous pressure, together with objective evidence of structural or functional cardiac abnormality on echocardiography and/or elevated natriuretic peptide levels.

Because the present study was designed to evaluate long-term post-discharge outcomes, the index date for follow-up was defined as the date of hospital discharge. Therefore, patients who died during the index hospitalization were not included in the post-discharge follow-up cohort. A total of 813 patients were initially screened. Of these, 373 were excluded for the following reasons: in-hospital death; loss to follow-up, defined as the absence of at least one post-discharge follow-up assessment; presence of an active malignancy; age ≤18 years; a history of or current diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); incomplete echocardiographic data precluding measurement of both tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP); or missing essential baseline clinical information at admission. After application of these exclusion criteria, 440 patients remained and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Data collection

Data were extracted from the electronic medical record system by two trained cardiologists. Collected information encompassed demographic characteristics, medical history, medication use, laboratory results, and echocardiographic parameters. Vital signs at admission, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure and heart rate, were recorded.

Demographic variables included age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and alcohol use. Alcohol use was defined as current alcohol consumption documented at admission or self-reported regular alcohol intake before hospitalization. Clinical history comprised pulmonary infection, pacemaker implantation, coronary artery disease, history of percutaneous coronary intervention, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, valvular heart disease, atrial fibrillation, prior myocardial infarction, and prior stroke. Medication data recorded at baseline included beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, diuretics, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), digitalis, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, statins, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), and soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) stimulators.

Laboratory measurements were obtained from the first available fasting morning venous blood samples during the index hospitalization. Samples were processed and analyzed the same day in the hospital’s centralized laboratory. The following laboratory measurements were collected: white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, hemoglobin, platelet count, hematocrit, total bilirubin, fasting plasma glucose, serum creatinine, uric acid, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, apolipoprotein A1, apolipoprotein B, serum potassium, serum sodium, C-reactive protein, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), NT-proBNP.

Echocardiographic images were acquired with Philips IE33. The parameters included left atrial diameter, pulmonary artery diameter, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), aortic root diameter, interventricular septal thickness at end-diastole, left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end-diastole, left ventricular end-diastolic dimension, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), and pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP).

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed during the index hospitalization by experienced echocardiographers using the same Philips IE33 ultrasound system and following standardized clinical protocols throughout the examination process. TAPSE was measured using M-mode echocardiography in the standard apical four-chamber view, with the sampling cursor positioned at the lateral tricuspid annulus. The maximal longitudinal displacement from end-diastole to end-systole was measured, and the average value from three consecutive cardiac cycles was used for analysis. PASP was estimated from the peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity combined with estimated right atrial pressure using the modified Bernoulli equation. To ensure data quality, echocardiographic records were reviewed for completeness and interpretability. Patients with poor image quality or incomplete echocardiographic data precluding reliable measurement of either TAPSE or PASP were excluded from the analysis. Data extraction was performed using the original echocardiographic reports and electronic medical records.

Follow-up and endpoint events

Follow-up commenced at the time of hospital discharge and continued until 5 years after discharge or the end of October 2025. Trained study personnel performed follow-up using structured telephone interviews, scheduled outpatient visits, and systematic review of the electronic medical record. Reported endpoint events were verified whenever possible using source medical documents, including discharge diagnoses, laboratory results, imaging reports, procedure records, and death-related records.

The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), defined as a composite of all-cause mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and rehospitalization for worsening heart failure. All-cause mortality was confirmed through medical records, death records, or information provided by family members during follow-up. Nonfatal myocardial infarction was adjudicated based on clinical diagnosis supported by cardiac biomarker elevation, electrocardiographic changes, coronary imaging, or relevant hospitalization records. Nonfatal stroke was confirmed according to clinical diagnosis supported by neurological assessment and cranial CT or MRI findings. Rehospitalization for worsening heart failure was defined as an unplanned hospital admission due to worsening symptoms or signs of heart failure requiring intensification of heart failure therapy.

Endpoint events were adjudicated according to pre-specified criteria by trained investigators who were blinded to baseline TAPSE/PASP status. When event classification was uncertain, discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

The optimal cut-off for the TAPSE/PASP ratio was derived using a two-step approach that combined restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with a maximization-of-survival-difference method. RCS was first used to characterize the functional form of the association between TAPSE/PASP and risk of major adverse cardiovascular events. If the RCS indicated a nonlinear relationship, the cut-point suggested by the spline model was selected; if the relationship was approximately linear, an exploratory cut-off was derived by maximizing survival separation between groups using the log-rank statistic. This internally derived threshold was used for descriptive stratification and Kaplan–Meier visualization. The primary Cox regression analyses treated TAPSE/PASP as a continuous variable, scaled per 0.01-unit increment, to preserve prognostic information and avoid overreliance on a data-driven dichotomous threshold.

Patients with missing essential baseline clinical information or incomplete echocardiographic data precluding calculation of TAPSE/PASP were excluded from the final analysis. For the variables included in the final analytic cohort, complete-case analysis was performed. Continuous variables were entered into the models in their original units unless otherwise specified.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables or the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

The association between TAPSE/PASP category and event-free survival was evaluated with Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate the independent association of TAPSE/PASP with MACE risk; results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Prior to model fitting, multicollinearity among candidate covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Variables with VIF > 5 were removed iteratively (in descending order of VIF) until all retained covariates met the prespecified VIF threshold.

Three sequential adjustment models were specified: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 included variables in Model 1 plus body mass index, coronary artery disease, history of percutaneous coronary intervention, diabetes mellitus, prior myocardial infarction, prior stroke, and baseline use of beta-blockers, angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), SGLT2 inhibitors, and soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) stimulators; Model 3 additionally adjusted for laboratory covariates (total bilirubin, serum creatinine, uric acid, triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, apolipoprotein A1, apolipoprotein B, serum sodium, C-reactive protein, eGFR, neutrophil percentage, hemoglobin, hematocrit, NT-proBNP and platelet count). For ease of clinical interpretation, TAPSE/PASP was scaled per 0.01-unit increment in the Cox models.

A base predictive model was derived from a full model containing all candidate predictors except TAPSE/PASP by backward stepwise selection. TAPSE/PASP was then entered into this base model as a continuous variable. Time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated and compared for the base model and the TAPSE/PASP-enhanced model at prespecified time points (12, 24, 36, 48, 60 months) to assess discriminatory performance over time. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to compare the predictive performance of the base model and models additionally incorporating TAPSE, PASP, or TAPSE/PASP. AUCs and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the DeLong method.

Internal validation of the prediction models was performed using bootstrap cross-validation with 1000 resamples. Time-dependent AUCs and Brier scores were calculated at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months to assess model discrimination and prediction error, respectively.

Incremental prognostic value of TAPSE/PASP was evaluated by comparing the base and enhanced models using the C-statistic, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and the net reclassification improvement (NRI).

As an exploratory analysis, Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the relationships between TAPSE/PASP and selected clinical and laboratory variables, providing additional clinical context and helping evaluate potential collinearity among variables included in the prediction model. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were also calculated to assess multicollinearity in the final model. Predefined subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of the TAPSE/PASP effect across strata defined by age (<60 vs ≥60 years), sex (male vs female), history of stroke (no vs yes), history of myocardial infarction (no vs yes), diabetes mellitus (no vs yes), coronary artery disease (no vs yes), smoking (no vs yes), eGFR(≤60 ml/min vs >60 ml/min), and left ventricular ejection fraction (HFrEF, HFmrEF vs HFpEF). Interaction effects were tested using likelihood ratio tests and results were displayed in a forest plot.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 440 patients with COPD-free chronic heart failure diagnosed at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the cohort was 64.1 ± 15.9 years, and 137 patients (31.1%) were female. During follow-up, 193 patients experienced MACEs.

RCS analysis demonstrated an approximately linear inverse association between TAPSE/PASP and MACE risk, with no evidence of significant nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity = 0.6129; Figure 2). Because no clear nonlinear threshold was identified, an exploratory cut-off value of 0.036 was derived using the maximization-of-survival-difference method. This internally derived threshold was used to categorize patients into low and high TAPSE/PASP groups for descriptive comparisons and Kaplan–Meier visualization.

Compared with the high TAPSE/PASP group, patients with a low TAPSE/PASP ratio exhibited significantly higher heart rates and NT-proBNP levels, lower body weight and body mass index, larger left atrial diameter, lower left ventricular ejection fraction, and—by definition—lower TAPSE and higher PASP. They were also more likely to have advanced symptoms, as reflected by a higher proportion of New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III–IV.

Regarding laboratory parameters, the low TAPSE/PASP group had significantly higher levels of total bilirubin, uric acid, and C-reactive protein, accompanied by lower triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, apolipoprotein A1, apolipoprotein B, serum sodium, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. Hematocrit levels were also significantly lower in this group. In terms of comorbidities and medical history, atrial fibrillation was more prevalent among patients with low TAPSE/PASP, whereas pacemaker implantation was less common. Significant differences were also observed in medication use: angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, and soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators were more frequently prescribed in the high TAPSE/PASP group, while angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers were more commonly used in the low TAPSE/PASP group. No significant differences were observed between groups with respect to age, sex, blood pressure, coronary artery disease, diabetes, hypertension, prior myocardial infarction, or several other clinical variables.

Associations between TAPSE/PASP and MACEs

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significant difference in event-free survival between the low and high TAPSE/PASP groups (Figure 3). The survival curves separated early and remained divergent during follow-up (log-rank p < 0.0001), with patients in the low TAPSE/PASP group showing a lower probability of MACE-free survival.

Median follow-up duration was 37.6 months in the low TAPSE/PASP group and 43.8 months in the high TAPSE/PASP group. Before multivariable model construction, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were assessed for all candidate covariates. Variables exhibiting multicollinearity were excluded, resulting in final models in which all retained covariates had VIFs below 5.

In multivariable Cox regression analyses, TAPSE/PASP was standardized per 0.01-unit increment to facilitate clinical interpretation of hazard ratios. To avoid overreliance on the exploratory dichotomous threshold and to preserve prognostic information, TAPSE/PASP was analyzed as a continuous variable scaled per 0.01-unit increment in multivariable Cox regression models. When analyzed as a continuous variable, each 0.01 increase in TAPSE/PASP was associated with a significantly lower risk of MACEs, with hazard ratios of 0.65 (95% CI, 0.58–0.74; p = 9.93 × 10–12) in Model 1, 0.67 (95% CI, 0.59–0.76; p = 1.02 × 10−9) in Model 2, and 0.75 (95% CI, 0.66–0.87; p = 6.02 × 10−5) in Model 3. When treated as a categorical variable, patients in the low TAPSE/PASP group had a significantly higher risk of MACEs than those in the high ratio group, with hazard ratios of 2.81 (95% CI, 2.02–3.91; p = 7.90 × 10−10), 2.47 (95% CI, 1.75–3.50; p = 2.15 × 10−7), and 1.98 (95% CI, 1.37–2.85; p = 2.57 × 10−4) in Models 1–3, respectively (Table 2). In this cohort, a 0.01-unit increment in TAPSE/PASP approximately corresponded to one standard deviation of the ratio, indicating that this scaling represented a clinically appreciable change rather than a trivial numerical difference. Because TAPSE/PASP integrates two routinely assessed echocardiographic parameters, a 0.01-unit difference would correspond, for example, to an approximately 0.4-cm difference in TAPSE at a PASP of 40 mmHg. Therefore, clinicians should interpret a lower TAPSE/PASP ratio as a marker of impaired RV–PA coupling rather than as an isolated numerical change.

Predictive values of TAPSE/PASP in CHF

The base model was derived by backward stepwise selection from a full model that included all candidate predictors except TAPSE/PASP. The resulting base model comprised stroke, CAD, Scr, SGLT2i, UA, Tbil, ApoA1, age, Hb, LDL-C, and TG.

The final base model included 11 predictors, and the TAPSE/PASP-enhanced model included 12 predictors. Given 193 observed MACEs, the corresponding events-per-variable ratios were 17.5 and 16.1, respectively. To assess model stability, bootstrap cross-validation with 1000 resamples was performed. In the internally validated analysis, the TAPSE/PASP-enhanced model showed numerically higher time-dependent AUCs than the base model at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months: 0.764 vs. 0.741, 0.757 vs. 0.728, 0.700 vs. 0.672, 0.710 vs. 0.655, and 0.732 vs. 0.649, respectively. The enhanced model also showed consistently lower Brier scores across these time points, suggesting a trend toward reduced prediction error (Supplementary Table 2).

Receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed to compare the predictive performance of the base model and models additionally incorporating TAPSE, PASP, or TAPSE/PASP for long-term clinical outcomes in patients with chronic heart failure. The base model yielded an AUC of 0.766 (95% CI, 0.722–0.811). After adding TAPSE, PASP, or TAPSE/PASP, the AUCs were 0.782 (95% CI, 0.739–0.826), 0.775 (95% CI, 0.731–0.819), and 0.800 (95% CI, 0.747–0.832), respectively. Among these models, the TAPSE/PASP-enhanced model showed the highest AUC and a modest improvement in discrimination compared with the base model (p = 0.03) (Figure 4A).

Time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) analyses demonstrated that the model incorporating TAPSE/PASP consistently outperformed the base model throughout follow-up (0–80 months) (Figure 4B). Improvements in AUC were statistically significant at 24 months (p = 0.030), 48 months (p = 0.010), 60 months (p = 0.007), whereas differences at 12 months and 36 months did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.070 and 0.09, respectively).

Consistently, the addition of TAPSE/PASP significantly improved MACE prediction (Table 3). Model discrimination increased, with the C-statistic rising from 0.766 to 0.800 (p = 0.03). Both reclassification and discrimination metrics improved substantially, with a net reclassification improvement of 0.31 (95% CI, 0.13–0.50; p = 9.30 × 10−4) and an integrated discrimination improvement of 0.04 (95% CI, 0.02–0.06; p = 1.0 × 10−5).

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the association between TAPSE/PASP and MACE risk across clinically relevant patient characteristics (Figure 6). Specifically, the adverse prognostic association was observed across strata defined by age, sex, history of myocardial infarction, history of stroke, diabetes status, coronary artery disease, smoking, and eGFR. When stratified by left ventricular ejection fraction phenotype, the TAPSE/PASP ratio demonstrated significant prognostic value across patients with HFpEF, HFmrEF, and HFrEF. Although effect sizes differed numerically among LVEF categories, a lower TAPSE/PASP ratio was consistently associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes in all strata, with no evidence of heterogeneity (P for interaction = 0.954).

No significant interaction was detected between TAPSE/PASP and any subgroup variable (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the prognostic value of TAPSE/PASP was homogeneous across these clinical subgroups.

Discussion

In the current study, a lower TAPSE/PASP ratio was independently associated with a higher risk of long-term MACEs in hospitalized patients with chronic heart failure. This association remained evident after adjustment for clinical, laboratory, echocardiographic variables, and natriuretic peptides, and was generally consistent across LVEF-defined HF phenotypes. Specifically, each 0.01-unit increase in TAPSE/PASP was associated with an approximately 25% lower risk of MACEs, while patients with TAPSE/PASP ≤0.036 had nearly a two-fold higher risk than those with TAPSE/PASP >0.036 after full adjustment. These effect sizes suggest that TAPSE/PASP captures clinically relevant information on the balance between RV systolic function and pulmonary arterial load.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that, irrespective of left ventricular ejection fraction, the presence of right ventricular dysfunction (RVD) is associated with a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality, heart failure rehospitalization, and sudden cardiac death [21,22]. Pulmonary vascular hemodynamic impairment represents a critical pathophysiological link between left-sided and right-sided heart failure, with the progressive increase in pulmonary vascular resistance serving as a key driver of clinical deterioration and the development of RVD [23,24]. Assessment of right ventricular systolic function alone may be insufficient to fully capture the adaptive capacity of the right ventricle under conditions of increased afterload. In contrast, evaluating the relationship between right ventricular contractile performance and pulmonary arterial load provides a more comprehensive reflection of right ventricular compensation and prognostic vulnerability [25,26].

Growing evidence has supported the prognostic value of echocardiographic surrogates of RV–PA coupling, particularly TAPSE/PASP, in patients with heart failure [27,28]. However, prior evidence has often focused on acute heart failure, selected HF phenotypes, or relatively short-term outcomes [29–31]. Against this background, the present study should be interpreted primarily as an extension and confirmation of previous evidence rather than as the identification of a fundamentally novel prognostic marker. Its incremental contribution lies in demonstrating that the association between TAPSE/PASP and adverse outcomes persists over long-term follow-up in a hospitalized chronic heart failure cohort, remains robust when TAPSE/PASP is analyzed as a continuous variable, and is generally consistent across LVEF-defined HF phenotypes, thereby extending observations from previous chronic heart failure cohorts [32,33]. This distinction is important because it frames TAPSE/PASP as a complementary echocardiographic risk marker requiring further validation, rather than as a stand-alone tool ready for clinical implementation. Moreover, the consistent inverse association observed in both continuous and dichotomous analyses support the concept that RV–PA uncoupling represents a progressive pathophysiological continuum rather than a binary condition defined by an arbitrary threshold.

Importantly, when TAPSE/PASP was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a dichotomous variable, a consistent inverse association with MACE risk was observed. This supports the concept that RV–PA uncoupling represents a progressive pathophysiological continuum rather than a binary condition defined by an arbitrary threshold. Therefore, the cut-off value of 0.036 should be interpreted as an exploratory, internally derived threshold for descriptive stratification rather than a standardized clinical decision point. Given the absence of external validation, greater emphasis should be placed on the continuous-variable analyses, and future prospective studies are needed before any specific TAPSE/PASP threshold can be recommended for clinical risk stratification.

Beyond confirming an independent association with outcomes, prior studies conducted in acute heart failure settings have suggested that incorporation of TAPSE/PASP into established clinical risk models provides incremental prognostic information, including improvements in discrimination and risk reclassification [34,35]. In line with these observations, the addition of TAPSE/PASP to our base model resulted in statistically significant, albeit modest, improvements in model discrimination and reclassification performance. Although the improvement in C-statistic was modest, decision curve analysis suggested a higher net benefit of the TAPSE/PASP-enhanced model across part of the clinically relevant threshold probability range. These findings support TAPSE/PASP as a complementary marker for risk stratification, although its clinical utility requires further external validation. Notably, subgroup analyses revealed that reduced TAPSE/PASP was consistently associated with adverse outcomes among patients with LVEF in different scopes, with no significant interaction between TAPSE/PASP and ejection fraction category. This observation suggests that the prognostic impact of impaired RV–PA coupling is largely independent of left ventricular systolic function. While previous studies have often evaluated TAPSE/PASP within selected heart failure phenotypes—frequently focusing on acute presentations or a single ejection fraction category—our findings extend prior evidence by demonstrating a stable and consistent long-term prognostic association across ejection fraction–defined phenotypes in chronic heart failure. This observation is consistent with prior meta-analytic evidence suggesting that right heart–related parameters convey prognostic information across the heart failure spectrum [36,37]. Taken together, these findings suggest that TAPSE/PASP may provide complementary prognostic information across LVEF-defined HF phenotypes. However, its clinical implications should be interpreted cautiously. Given the modest improvement in model performance, TAPSE/PASP may serve as an adjunctive marker for risk assessment rather than a replacement for established clinical evaluation, pending further prospective validation.

Noninvasive echocardiographic estimation of PASP has inherent limitations, as its accuracy may be influenced by tricuspid regurgitation signal quality, right atrial pressure estimation, loading conditions, and impaired pressure-generating capacity in severe right ventricular dysfunction. Nevertheless, the tricuspid regurgitation pressure-gradient method remains the most practical and widely used noninvasive approach for routine PASP assessment in clinical practice.

This study has limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design precludes causal inference and may limit external validity. Echocardiograms were not obtained serially for all patients, so we could not evaluate how temporal changes in TAPSE/PASP relate to outcomes. Patients were excluded because of in-hospital death, loss to follow-up, COPD, incomplete echocardiographic data, or missing baseline information. Since follow-up began at discharge, patients who died during hospitalization were not included, which may have introduced survivorship bias and underrepresented the most severely ill patients. Although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, residual confounding remains possible and the sample size restricts precision for some subgroup estimates. Finally, the cohort reflects a Chinese population, which may limit geographic generalizability. Prospective multicenter studies with external validation are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

In this prolonged follow-up cohort of patients with chronic heart failure, a reduced TAPSE/PASP ratio was associated with more advanced clinical features and independently predicted major adverse cardiovascular events after comprehensive adjustment. TAPSE/PASP provided modest incremental prognostic information and showed generally consistent associations across LVEF-defined HF phenotypes. These findings support the potential value of TAPSE/PASP as a complementary echocardiographic risk marker, but prospective multicenter studies with external validation are needed before its role in clinical decision-making can be established.
