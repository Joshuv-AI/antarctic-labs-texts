Berberine alleviates soybean meal-induced enteritis in hybrid yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco ♀ × Pelteobagrus vachelli ♂) by regulating intestinal microbiota and tryptophan metabolism
Authors: Zihao Zhang, Jiale Hong, Meina Zhang, Yuqing Liu, Chengjia Wu, Xiaocheng Huang, Pengsheng Dong, Zhenjiang Yang, Guojun Guo, Shouqi Xie, Guozhao Wu, Guoxi Li, Ming Li, Guangqing Yu, Bianzhi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The effects of berberine (BBR) on intestinal health suffering from soybean meal (SBM)-induced enteritis (SBMIE) in hybrid yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco ♀ × Pelteobagrus vachelli ♂) were investigated in this study, focusing specifically on oxidant-antioxidant capacity, intestinal morphology, microbiota homeostasis and tryptophan catabolites. A total of 270 individuals with the initial body weight (IBW) of 23.27 ± 0.60 g were randomly divided into 9 tanks at the density of 30 fish per tank. Three diets, the SBM diet (75% of fish meal substituted by SBM, SBM), BBR1 (75 mg/kg BBR was added to the SBM diet, BBR1) and BBR2 diet (150 mg/kg BBR was added to the SBM diet, BBR2), were used to feed fish for 42 d. The results indicated that the final body weight (FBW), weight gain rate (WGR) and specific growth rate (SGR) were all significantly increased in BBR2 group compared with SBM group (P < 0.05). Intestinal barrier proteins and genes (ZO-1 and occludin) expression (P < 0.05) and intestinal interleukin-10 (IL-10) content (P = 0.001) were all greatly improved with BBR administration, especially in the BBR2 group. The apoptosis related genes (caspase 3 and caspase 9) (P < 0.05) of intestinal epithelium cells were also significantly reduced by dietary BBR compared with SBM group. Comparative analysis of intestinal microbial composition and tryptophan catabolites between the SBM group and BBR2 group revealed that BBR significantly ameliorated the imbalance of intestinal microbiota and improved the intestinal tryptophan catabolites, especially reflected in the relative abundance of Romboutsia and the levels of L-5-hydroxytryptophan (L-5-HTP), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and kynurenine (KYN) in intestinal contents (P < 0.05). As a result, the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling was then activated. Correlation analyses showed that the relative abundance of Romboutsia was positively correlated with IAA and KYN levels in the intestinal contents (P < 0.05), and negatively correlated with interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels in hindgut (P < 0.01). The levels of L-5-HTP, IAA, and KYN in intestinal contents were positively correlated with key factors of hindgut AhR signaling pathway, tight junctions (TJs) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (P < 0.05). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional prediction results showed that the intestinal microbial functions were mainly enriched in metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins. In conclusion, BBR ameliorated SBMIE in hybrid yellow catfish by reducing oxidative stress, enhancing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capacity, alleviating intestinal pathological damage and microbial dysbiosis. The microbiota-tryptophan catabolite-AhR axis may play an essential role in this protective process.

Introduction

Soybean meal (SBM) is extensively utilized as the preferred substitute for fish meal in aquatic diet because of its inexpensive and convenient traits. However, a high inclusion level of dietary SBM often causes an enteritis known as SBM-induced enteritis (SBMIE) (Torrecillas et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017), which seriously impairs fish growth performance, antioxidative capacity and immune system, regardless of whether they are herbivorous, omnivorous, or carnivorous (Fischer et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2018). Seeking solutions to alleviate or eliminate SBMIE is increasing the focus of researchers with the development of aquaculture and the limited supply of fish meal (Liu et al., 2024). Berberine (BBR), an isoquinoline alkaloid obtained from Berberis, is often used to treat intestinal diseases in mammals (Li et al., 2020b). In fish farming, BBR is listed in the Chinese Fishery Pharmacopoeia as a prescription drug and exerts multiple beneficial effects, including against bacterial diseases (Xia et al., 2022), the amelioration of hepatic lipid metabolism (Wang et al., 2020), and the mitigation of environmental and nutritional stresses (Wang et al., 2023a, 2020). Detailed investigations proved that BBR could inhibit intestinal inflammation and oxidative stress, improve antioxidative status, repair intestinal mucosal barrier damage and intestinal microbiota disturbance (Gong et al., 2017; Hou et al., 2019; Pan et al., 2019; Xia et al., 2023b; Zhou et al., 2019). However, there are few studies on how BBR alleviates SBMIE induced fish intestinal damages.

Intestinal tract is an important barrier for the organism's contact with the external environment, and is the most vital organ for nutrients digestion, nutrient absorption, and immunity of organisms (Xia et al., 2023a). Intestinal microbiota promotes the structure and function of the intestinal microecosystem, mainly in nutrient digestion and absorption, and the body's immune defense (Ma et al., 2022). Fish suffered SBMIE often have disbalance of intestinal microbiota simultaneously (Wang et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2024). On the other hand, pharmacological studies demonstrated that BBR has a low oral bioavailability, and most of it (> 90%) remains in intestinal contents (Gu et al., 2014; Xia et al., 2023b). The inevitable fact is that the intestinal physiology and microbiota are affected by the accumulated BBR. This interaction between BBR and gut microbiota promotes intestinal health by modulating the microbiota and metabolic transformation (Dehau et al., 2023). But the specific microbial species involved in this kind of therapeutic effects need more research to be identified. Therefore, the first step for digging therapeutic measures to improve fish SBMIE is to evaluate the effects of BBR on SBMIE and to elucidate its impact on intestinal microbiota and intestinal metabolomics.

Hybrid yellow catfish is a widely farmed economic freshwater fish in China, well known for its delicious taste, no intermuscular spines and rich nutrition. Data from previous research showed that hybrid yellow catfish is particularly prone to suffer SBMIE when fed a SBM-based diet (Yi et al., 2022). Hence, this study not only provides a systematic evaluation of the effects of BBR on intestinal health, intestinal metabolomics and microbiota of hybrid yellow catfish suffering from SBMIE, but also develops a theoretical basis for further research into small-molecule metabolites as potential therapeutic agents for improving the SBMIE of hybrid yellow catfish, based on the interplay between BBR and the microbiota.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The feeding management and sample collection of hybrid yellow catfish followed the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, authorized by Henan Agricultural University the Ethics Committee for Laboratory Animals (SKLAB-B-2010-003-01).

Experimental diet and fish management

Three isonitrogenous and isolipidic experimental diets (43% crude protein and 8% crude lipid), the SBM diet (75% of fish meal substituted by SBM, SBM), BBR1 diet (75 mg/kg BBR was added to the SBM diet) and BBR2 diet (150 mg/kg BBR was added to the SBM diet), were prepared. The raw feed materials were pulverized, filtered through a sieve (screen aperture: 21 μm) and then homogenously blended. The mixture was granulated into sinking dietary particles by a LX-feed extruder (LX-70, Senzhixin Machinery Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Xingtai, Hebei, China). The particles were then desiccated to about 12% moisture in a shaded, ventilated and dry area. Dietary nutrient compositions were determined according to the standard methods of AOAC (2005). Moisture content was determined by drying the diets to a constant weight in an oven at 105 °C (method 2001.12). Crude protein, crude lipid, ash, and crude fiber contents in the diets were determined via the Kjeldahl method (method 2001.11), Soxhlet extraction (method 920.39), muffle furnace incineration (method 942.05), and acid-base digestion (method 962.09), respectively. The gross energy of the diets was analyzed using an oxygen bomb calorimeter (C2000, IKA Works GmbH & Co. KG, Staufen, Germany). The experimental fish were obtained from Henan Province yellow catfish commercial aquaculture center (Zhengzhou, Henan, China). Fishes are temporarily kept in a tank (3 m × 3 m × 1.3 m, volume: 9 × 103 L) located in ponds (45 m × 90 m × 2 m, volume: 6 × 106 L). After 2 weeks’ adaptation, 270 individuals with the initial body weight (IBW) of 23.27 ± 0.60 g were randomly divided into 9 tanks (1 m × 1 m × 1.3 m, volume: 900 L) at the density of 30 fish per tank. During the experiment, the hybrid yellow catfish were fed twice daily to achieve apparent satiety (06: 30 and 18:30). Water quality parameters were maintained within an appropriate range (pH: 7.79 ± 0.1, dissolved oxygen > 5.5 mg/L, temperature: 27 ± 2 °C, total ammonia-N < 0.1 mg/L). A natural photoperiod was employed throughout the experiment.

Sample collection

After a 6-week feeding, all the yellow catfish were starved for 24 h before sample collection, and the fish of each tank were counted and weighted. Then, thirteen individuals were randomly chosen from each tank and anesthetized with MS-222 (100 mg/L) for blood samples and hindgut tissues. The remaining fish continued to be fed for another seven days, and four fish in each cage were randomly selected to collect intestinal contents. The samples of intestinal contents were stored in sterile cryopreservation tube. One half of them was used to detect tryptophan catabolites, and the other half was used for intestinal microbiota sequencing. According to the ethical standards for animal experimentation, the remaining fish in each cage, which were not included in the planned analysis, were euthanized using MS-222.

Blood samples were gathered from the caudal vein of yellow catfish using a 1-mL sterile syringe. The collected blood was immediately transferred into 1.5-mL heparinized centrifuge tubes and then centrifuged for 15 min (1400 × g, 4 °C) to extract plasma. After blood collection, the abdominal cavity of fish was carefully opened with sterile scissors and forceps, and the hindgut segment was gently separated from the mesentery and adjacent tissues. Then, two individuals were used for histopathology, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical analysis. Two individuals were used for transmission electron microscope (TEM; HT7700, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) ultrastructure analysis. Three individuals were used for oxidation and antioxidant capacity analysis. Three individuals were used for enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis. Three individuals were used for analysis of mRNA expression.

After sampling, all the samples were quickly frozen using liquid nitrogen and then preserved at −80 °C until analysis. However, the tissues for histopathology and TEM analysis need be stored at 4 °C.

Growth performance analysis

The growth data was calculated by the following formulates:Survivalrate(SR,%)=(Harvestednumber/Placednumber)×100;Initialbodyweight(IBW,g/fish)=Initialweight/Initialnumber;FinalbodyweightFBW,g/fish=Finalweight/Finalnumber;Weightgainrate(WGR,%)=(FBW-IBW)/IBW×100;Specificgrowthrate(SGR,%/d)=(ln[FBW]-ln[IBW])/Days×100;FeedintakeFI,g/fish =Dietdryweight/Fishnumber;FeedingrateFR,%/d=Dietdryweight/FBW+IBW/2×Experimentaldays×100;Feedconversionrate(FCR)=Dietdryweight/(FBW-IBW).

Biochemical indicators

The values of total-antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), total-superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were determined by reagents according to the manufacturer's instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China; product codes: A015-2-1, A001-1-2, A007-1-1, A005-1-2, and A003-1-2, respectively).

Diamine oxidase (DAO), D-lactate (D-LA), reactive oxygen species (ROS), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-10 (IL-10) were determined by ELISA kits (Keqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China; product codes: KQ45614, KQ37799, KQ37137, KQ51159, KQ22595, and KQ17643, respectively).

Histopathological and TEM observation

After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde buffer for 48 h, the hindgut tissues were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin wax. The paraffin blocks were then sectioned into 5 μm slices, cleared in xylene, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The images were gathered using a microscope (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). The length of intestinal mucosal fold (LMF), the width of intestinal mucosal fold (WMF), the width of the submucosa (WSM), the width of the lamina propria (WLP), and the thickness of circular muscle (TCM) were measured following the method of Liu et al. (2022b).

Tissues for TEM analysis were first fixed for 2 h using 2.5% buffered glutaraldehyde before dehydration, then embedded in resin, sliced into 60 nm sections. Subsequently, sections were placed on copper mesh, stained with uranyl acetate (2%) and lead citrate (2.6%) for observation. The photographs were taken using the TEM (HT7700, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

For immunofluorescence analysis, the 5 μm paraffin slices were incubated overnight with zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) (#21773-1-AP, 1:200, Proteintech Group, Inc., Wuhan, Hubei, China), occludin (#27260-1-AP, 1:200, Proteintech Group, Inc., Wuhan, Hubei, China), and claudin-1 (#BY0106, 1:100, Shanghai Ebiwei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 4 °C, respectively. Then, goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin g (IgG) antibody (#PN0051, 1:200, Pinuofei Biological Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China) was used to conjugate with specific antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. 4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining buffer was used for nuclei staining for 5 min. Images were captured by digital slide scanners (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) and observed by digital microscope (SlideViewer, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary).

For immunohistochemistry analysis, the 5 μm paraffin slices were first incubated at room temperature in a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 25 min in a dark background, and then incubated overnight with 1:200 diluted antibodies of AhR (#D261443, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), CYP1A1 (#D120518, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and IL-22 (GB11259-100, Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China) at 4 °C, respectively. Subsequently, these sections were conjugated with goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (#GB21303, 1:300, Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were stained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining for 5 min and hematoxylin staining for 3 min. Images were captured by digital slide scanners (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) and observed by digital microscope software (SlideViewer, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). The full names of the abbreviations of all proteins can be found in Table S1.

The positive staining areas of each slice was captured and analyzed by ImageJ (version 1.54p, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP-biotin nick end labeling (TUNEL)

The apoptosis staining of the hindgut paraffin slices was performed following the instructions of the manufacturer for the TUNEL Apoptosis Kit (P0017-100T, Pinuofei Biological Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China). These sliced images were gathered using a digital camera mounted on a microscope (Microscope Imaging System, DS-U3, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Detection of tryptophan catabolites in intestinal contents

The intestinal contents (50 mg) were extracted using 500 μL buffer of formic acid (0.1%) containing isotopically-labelled internal standard (H2O: methanol: acetonitrile = 1: 2: 2, precooled at −40 °C). The content solution was ground (35 Hz) for 4 min and then ultrasonically treated for 5 min in ice-water bath, and this procedure was repeated twice. The solution was centrifuged (14,000 × g, 4 °C) for 15 min, and then 320 μL of the supernatant was taken and dried in a stream of nitrogen, and then redissolved again with 80 μL of extract buffer, centrifuged (14,000 × g, 4 °C) for 15 min to get the supernatant for ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-MS/MS (UHPLC-MS/MS) to detect tryptophan catabolites. The UHPLC conditions were as follows: analytes in the sample solution were separated using a binary elution system, consisting of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the flow rate was set at 5 μL/min. The separated compounds were detected using a mass spectrometer (Triple Quad 6500+, AB Sciex LLC, Framingham, MA, USA). Electrospray ionization was implemented for MS data acquisition. The instrument was set to ensure the accuracy was within 80% and 120%.

Intestinal microbiota analysis

The total microbiota DNA of contents was isolated with the HiPure Stool DNA Kit (D314103, Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). The DNA integrity and quantity were evaluated using 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometer (NC2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), respectively. Qualified samples were amplified using bacterial primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) to amplify the V3 to V4 region and then sequenced on Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. The raw data were decoded with DEMUX before being filtered and denoised using DADA2 to form valid sequences. Finally, all sequences were grouped together using QIIME 2 (with 99% sequence similarity) to generate characteristic sequence variants (ASVs). Alpha and beta diversity indices were computed using QIIME and the vegan package. Analyses of the composition and relative abundance of the bacterial community were conducted at both the phylum and genus levels. Additionally, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed, and Spearman correlation heatmaps were generated to visualize relationships between antioxidant indices, inflammatory factors, tight junctions, tryptophan catabolites and microorganisms at genus level. The intestinal microbial functions were mainly enriched using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the experiment were expressed as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM) and statistical analyzed with SPSS26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests were applied to evaluate homogeneity and normal distribution of the data. All experimental data conformed to a normal distribution except tryptophan catabolites. Specifically, data of tryptophan catabolites were analyzed by the rank sum test. The remaining data were analyzed using ANOVA and Tukey's comparison for significant differences between groups. Graphics were constructed using GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with highly significant differences at P < 0.01. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman analysis, and the correlation coefficient was denoted by R, where |R| > 0.7 indicates a strong correlation. The model for ANOVA is expressed as:Y=β0+β1X+ε,where Y, β0, β1, X and ε are dependent variable, constant/intercept, egression coefficient, independent variable and random error, respectively.

Discussion

The FBW, WGR, SGR, FR, and FCR in BBR2 group were significantly improved in hybrid yellow catfish suffering SBMIE. Similar results were obtained in largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) (Chen et al., 2022), blunt snout bream (Megalobrama amblycephala) (Zhou et al., 2019), and Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) (Doan et al., 2020) fed with high-fat or high-carbohydrates diets, indicating that appropriate dietary BBR improves fish growth rate and feed utilization efficiency. In freshwater grouper (Acrossocheilus fasciatus) suffered Cu2+ stress, and black sea bream (Acanthopagrus schlegelii) suffered high starch diet and ammonia stress, dietary BBR also exhibited significant protective effects against Cu2+-induced toxicity and ammonia stress, including mitigating intestinal oxidative stress, improving liver antioxidant status and reducing inflammation response (Wang et al., 2023a, 2020). In this study, dietary BBR has been proved to reduce oxidative stress levels of hybrid yellow catfish caused by SBMIE, and enhance the fish's antioxidant capacity. This is reflected by the reduced oxidative stress and improved antioxidant capacity observed in hybrid yellow catfish fed BBR diets, as shown by the decreased levels of ROS and MDA, as well as the increased activities of GSH-Px, CAT, T-SOD, and T-AOC in plasma and hindgut.

Intestine is a crucial organ for fish digestion and absorption, serving as a key barrier between fish body and the external environment. Foodborne enteritis represented by SBMIE is manifested as damaged intestinal structure, which evidenced by the inflammatory cell infiltration, shortened intestinal mucosal folds, swelled intestinal mucosal folds, as well as changes in the lamina propria and subepithelial mucosa (Liu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021). In this research, fish from SBM group exhibit typical symptoms of SBMIE. Berberine significantly protects the intestine injury from SBMIE, improves intestinal histopathology and histopathological inflammation. The value of LMF was significantly raised, while the values of WMF, WLP, and WSM were significantly decreased by dietary BBR. Moreover, the inflammatory cell infiltration was significantly reduced, and the intestinal morphology was more integrated in the BBR groups compared with SBM group. Plasma DAO and D-LA, the two key parameters to reflect intestinal permeability and intestinal mucosal barrier function (Yang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2021), were also significantly decreased in BBR groups. Intestinal TJs has an essential role in maintaining the integrity of intestinal physical barrier, SBM has been reported to impair the intestinal TJs and consequently to enhance intestinal permeability and increase the sensitivity to pathogenic infection (Ulluwishewa et al., 2011). While, BBR has been proved to increase the expression of TJs (protein and transcription levels of ZO-1 and occludin) both in mammals (Jing et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2022) and fish species (Ma et al., 2023; Gong et al., 2022), and its repaired effects on the intestinal integrity and intestinal mucosa function were also reported in fish suffered high-carbohydrate (Gong et al., 2022) or high-fat diets (Yu et al., 2020). In this research, the TJs fractures between intestinal epithelial cells reflected by ultrastructure of hindgut intestinal epithelial cell, combined with the loose, disordered and shortened intestinal villi caused by a SBM diet were significantly repaired by BBR diet. The expressions of ZO-1 and occludin in both protein and mRNA levels were also significantly improved in BBR2 group.

The inflammatory process triggered by SBM diet has been proved that it is an T cell-dependent manner both in herbivorous, omnivorous, and carnivorous fish (Coronado et al., 2019; De Santis et al., 2015; Peng et al., 2020). The equilibrium between the proinflammatory cytokines and the anti-inflammatory regulates the intestinal immune homeostasis, and the release of these inflammatory cytokines is closely correlated with enhanced intestinal permeability and immune function (Wang et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2021). In the present study, hybrid yellow catfish fed the SBM diet exhibited significantly higher levels of IL-1β and IL-6, and lower levels of IL-10 in both plasma and hindgut, which is consistent with many previous studies (Tan et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025). Meanwhile, the relative mRNA expression level of nf-κb was also significantly higher in SBM group. This enhancement of immune homeostasis by BBR were also observed in the research of blunt snout bream (Yu et al., 2020) and freshwater grouper (Wang et al., 2023b).

Excessive intestinal apoptosis induced by SBM diet has been confirmed in diverse fish species (Yi et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). Anti-nutritional factors including the two main soybean antigen proteins of glycinin and β-conglycinin, were partially regarded as the factors inducing detrimental intestinal apoptosis and then worsening the barrier damage (Liu et al., 2023; Yi et al., 2022). Once the apoptosis is activated, caspase 3 and caspase 9, the downstream effector caspases of the apoptosis pathway, will be activated to induce cell death (Kist and Vucic, 2021). Results from apoptosis-related genes in this study demonstrated that dietary BBR significantly decreased apoptosis in intestine of yellow catfish suffering from SBMIE. The gene expression of caspase3 and caspase9, which was upregulated in the SBM group, was significantly reduced by the dietary BBR, consistent with the fluorescence intensity of TUNEL staining. The rise of the relative mRNA expression levels of bcl-2 and reduction that of bax also indicated that dietary BBR suppressed intestinal apoptosis (Edlich, 2018). Similar results were reported in largemouth bass, where dietary BBR suppressed the intestinal apoptosis induced by high carbohydrate diet, thus alleviating the intestinal inflammation (Gong et al., 2022). Studies in blunt snout bream (Lu et al., 2017) and mouse (Li et al., 2020a) also have shown that BBR can alleviate liver apoptosis and podocyte apoptosis induced by high-fat diets. Therefore, it is speculated that the anti-apoptosis effect of BBR persists in the intestine of yellow catfish suffering from SBMIE.

Recent years, increasing literatures has been reported that intestinal microbiota and their metabolites participate in regulating various aspects of host physiology (Sonnenburg and Bäckhed, 2016; Wu et al., 2021). The microbial small-molecule metabolites might have an essential role in the interactions between the intestinal microbiota and the host, and finally modulating the host health and disease (Bishai and Palm, 2021). Previous researches have demonstrated that BBR firstly restores microbial imbalance, then enhances specific microbial tryptophan metabolism and the production of their derivatives, eventually improving the intestinal barrier through triggering the AhR signaling pathway to regulate the TJ proteins (Chen et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2022; Jing et al., 2021). In fish, the relation between nutrients, intestinal microbiota, and host health, and how this relation affects the fish's health, has also received increasing attention (Luan et al., 2023). In the current research, to further explore the potential mechanism by which dietary BBR acts on the intestinal barrier, this study systematically analyzed the intestinal microbiota and metabolites in hybrid yellow catfish from the SBM and BBR2 groups, mainly focusing on the tryptophan catabolites. The microbiota results showed that Proteobacteria and Firmicutes were the main two dominant phylum in the intestine, which was corresponding with the other research of yellow catfish (Li et al., 2024). This study found that the alpha diversity and the top 10 phyla were not affected by dietary BBR. At the genus level, SBM diet reduced the relative abundance of Romboutsia in the hindgut of hybrid yellow catfish, whereas BBR supplementation could effectively restore this decrease. Changed microbiota lead to the changes in gut microbial metabolites, thus affecting the host's immune response (Gao et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023). According to previous researches on mammals, the relative abundance of Romboutsia is often significantly decreased when the intestinal diseases or metabolic disorders occurred, but it was restored when the adverse atmosphere was reversed (Chen et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024). The increased abundance of Romboutsia may represent the potential mechanism underlying the improved intestinal health (Liu et al., 2022a). In the research of Wu et al. (2023), the clusting analysis of Romboutsia and fecal metabolites, combined with the correlation heatmap of gut microbiota and fecal metabolism, provide that altered Romboutsia contribute to the metabolites of tryptophan and then affect the host's inflammatory response. In fish, the decreased abundance of Romboutsia was also observed when fish suffered adverse environment, accompanied with intestinal epithelium edema, degeneration and inflammatory cell infiltration (Yang et al., 2022). A high-carbohydrate diet with 0.15% sodium acetate has been proved to improve the carbohydrate utilization of largemouth bass by increasing the intestinal Romboutsia (Xun et al., 2024). Fan et al. (2024) reported a strain of genus Romboutsia obtained from tilapia intestine and proved that fermented feed with this bacterial strain can regulate fish glycol metabolism by producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are functional substances enhance fish immune responses and intestinal health (Li et al., 2020c; Taherpour et al., 2023). These reports highlight the significant contribution of Romboutsia in maintaining intestinal health. In this experiment, the relative abundance of Romboutsia was significantly raised by dietary BBR in the intestinal of hybrid yellow catfish, indicating that Romboutsia could play a significant positive function in SBMIE.

On the other hand, the KEGG functional prediction results in the present study revealed that the functions of intestinal microbiota were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, which are fundamental to nutrient utilization, energy supply, and biosynthesis of essential biomolecules, thereby highlighting the critical role of intestinal microbiota in mediating host microbial metabolism (Fan and Pedersen, 2021). Meanwhile, the relative abundance of Romboutsia and the levels of intestinal tryptophan derivatives, particularly 5-HTP, IAA, and KYN, were all significantly enhanced in BBR2 group. Furthermore, the correlation results indicated that the levels of tryptophan derivatives (especially IAA and KYN) were positively correlated with the relative abundances of Romboutsia, Paraclostridium, and Terrisporobacter, whereas the levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in hindgut were negatively correlated with these abundances. The findings described above were consistent with the current research which demonstrated that tryptophan is primarily metabolized into various bioactive metabolites by gut microbiota (Hua et al., 2025), and indicated that BBR regulates intestinal inflammation by modulating both the gut microbiota (specifically increasing Romboutsia abundance) and tryptophan catabolites (Wu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2022), a conclusion consistent with evidences from mammalian studies (Chen et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2022; Jing et al., 2021).

In mammals, there is a definite association among intestinal microbiota, tryptophan catabolites and the AhR signal pathway (Agus et al., 2018). Jing et al. (2021) confirmed that BBR ameliorates colitis in rats through triggering AhR activation via tryptophan catabolites of intestinal microbiota. Microbial tryptophan catabolites, such as 5-HTP, IAA, KYN, and indole-3-aldehyde have been recognized to act as ligands of AhR to ameliorate intestinal immune responses and the intestinal barrier function (Tan et al., 2022). But in fish, little is known. To conduct a preliminary investigation on whether the association between intestinal microbiota, tryptophan catabolites and the AhR signaling pathway also exists in fish, the expressions of key factors in the AhR signaling pathway was examined, and the correlation between tryptophan catabolites and the main factors of AhR signaling pathway was analyzed. The results showed that tryptophan catabolites including L-5-HTP, IAA, and KYN, were positively correlated with AhR signaling pathway, TJs, and anti-inflammatory cytokines, while negatively correlated with levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in hindgut. Combined with the results on intestinal health, microbial functions and the abundance of Romboutsia, it was speculated that BBR markedly improves the dysregulated gut microbiota, particularly the abundance of Romboutsia. Consequently, the gut microbiota-associated tryptophan catabolites (L-5-HTP, IAA, and KYN) are increased. These tryptophan catabolites then activated the AhR pathway, and the intestinal health has been improved. But whether the mechanism of BBR improve the intestinal health as speculate, further experimental evidence is needed.

Conclusions

Dietary BBR improved intestinal health in hybrid yellow catfish suffering from SBMIE, including improving growth performance, intestinal antioxidant capacity, morphological structure, intestinal permeability, reducing the levels of inflammation, and apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells. Berberine treatment also improved the relative abundance of genus Romboutsia and the levels of intestinal microbiota-associated tryptophan catabolites, activated the AhR pathway. Berberine may improve SBMIE by triggering AhR pathway via the modulation of intestinal microbiota and the correlated tryptophan catabolites. In conclusion, the findings elucidate the mechanism by which BBR alleviates SBMIE and provide a theoretical basis for addressing intestinal health issues associated with SBMIE in fish.
