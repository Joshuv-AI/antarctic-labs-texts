Medication non-adherence among heart failure patients: a multi-hospital, multi-dimensional analytical study in Punjab, Pakistan
Authors: Muhammad Arshed, Hafsa Arshad, Mehwish Kiran, Hina lateef, Syed Mehmood ul hassan, Syed Muhammad Fauzan Ali, Muhammad Farooq Umer, Javeria Saleem, Ali Hassan Gillani, Ali Jawa, Yu Fang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) has been a significant health problem among the population, with a significant impact on morbidity, mortality, and health care expenditure across the world. In Pakistan, HF-specific non-adherence data are limited, which restricts clinicians to come up with interventions that can be used to address the at-risk population. Accordingly, the newly-conducted study intended to determine the incidence of medication non-adherence and predictors at various levels (sociodemographic, clinical, treatment-related, and behavioral) among heart failure patients.MethodsThis survey was a cross-sectional questionnaire survey among 9,377 eligible patients between January 2024 and April 2025 in 20 public and private healthcare facilities across Punjab, Pakistan. The primary outcome was non-adherence to medications assessed through the Self-Efficacy for Appropriate Medication Scale (SEAMS) and self-reported number of pills. Chi-square/Fisher’s exact test, independent t-test/ANOVA, and a generalized linear model (GLM) with a logit link function (multivariable logistic regression) were employed to assess associations with demographic characteristics. Background

Heart failure (HF) has remained a major issue in the health of the population, causing a considerable burden on morbidity, mortality, and health care spending around the globe [1]. Although there are developments in pharmacotherapy, poor medication adherence has been noted as a significant barrier to therapeutic success [2]. Poor adherence is always shown in literature to be related to repeat hospitalizations, deteriorated symptom control, and higher mortality, and thus its urgency in both high-income and low- and middle-income nations (LMICs) [3, 4].

Most of the literature on HF non-adherence cites multifactorial determinants [5]. The factors that affect non-adherence are patient-level variables such as knowledge deficits, distorted perceptions of illness and episodic memory loss, and regimen complexity, which enhances these problems [6]. The socio- behavioral factors that have been identified as very influential in Latin America include low health literacy and lack of family support [7]. Provider views also emphasize the role of quality of patient-clinician communication, follow-up arrangements, and offering of medication counselling on adherence behavior [8]. Another decisive aspect is economic constraints [9]. Cost-related non-adherence (CRN) is prevalent in cardiovascular patients in the United States, and patients will frequently skip or ration medications because of economic constraints [10]. The same trends have been reported in HF-specific populations, with out-of-pocket expenses being the key factor predicting the discontinuation [11]. Such results are relevant to LMICs, where health financing gaps are high [12].

Pakistan’s healthcare system is fragmented, with limited resources and uneven access. There are only 1.1 physicians per 1,000 people, and health insurance covers just 3% of the population [13, 14]. The experience of Pakistan is another depiction of these challenges and according to a nationwide survey, there was a high rate of non-adherence to lipid-lowering treatments and diabetic medication, mainly affected by socioeconomic disparities and behavioral issues [15, 16]. Research also shows disparities in the level of adherence between the public and private healthcare institutions, whereby the financial and accessibility issues tend to have an uneven impact on patients seeking medical services in the public hospitals [17]. Acute HF cases reported in local hospitals are often reported to be related to medication withdrawal or non-adherence, highlighting downstream consequences of non-adherence [6].

Non-adherence to HF treatment is a significant problem of public health in Asia and systematic reviews have revealed high non-adherence rates across the region [18]. Cultural norms and beliefs regarding health and inefficiencies within the system determine patient behavior in that situation and are also observed in Pakistan, where ethnicity, gender, and socioeconomic status also contribute to the result of adherence [19]. These observations highlight the need to come up with culturally sensitive interventions that are aligned with local realities [20, 21]. There are various transition points at which compliance will collapse in a clinical setting. As reported in emergency departments, a history of non-adherence to medications is the typical feature of acutely decompensated HF patients [21]. Also, compliance rates dwindle to significantly lower levels when people are no longer in the hospital [22].

However, the research is not conducted in Pakistan concerning HF to a significant extent, no matter what is considered to be global [5]. Current national studies mainly assess the compliance with antihyperlipidemic or antihypertensive treatment, and little focus is on HF patients as a specific group [15, 21]. Clinicians thus lack the data in context that would enable them to formulate interventions that would benefit this at-risk group [4]. These are the gaps that should be filled in as non-adherence to medication is a direct violation of the benefits of evidence-based HF treatments [1, 8]. These findings may be used to inform specific adherence interventions, improve the interaction between patients and clinicians, and promote cost-sensitive approaches to improve the long-term outcomes [7, 22]. We therefore planned a study to understand the rate of non-adherence in heart failure patients in Pakistan and to identify sociodemographic, clinical and behavioral variables that may be related to non-adherence.

Methodology

Design and setting of the studies

The goals of this cross-sectional questionnaire survey were to find out the prevalence to non-adherence of medications and the factors linked with non-adherence among patients with heart failure in Punjab, Pakistan. We used multi-stage sampling in choosing heart failure participants. To begin with, this cross-sectional questionnaire survey was implemented to identify the level of prevalence of medication non-adherence and other related factors among patients with HF in Punjab, Pakistan.To this end, we first shortlisted 30 eligible hospitals by purposive sampling (i.e., both private and public with indoor and outdoor capacity to treat HF patients). Among 30 of these 14 were private and 6 were public hospitals that gave consent and were included. Second, a consecutive sampling method was used to select HF participants in the 20 hospitals in the medical and cardiological outpatient departments. The research was done following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supplementary File 1).

The sampling of participants and recruitment

The eligibility criteria were adults aged 21 to 75 years visiting the outpatient departments of our 20 chosen hospitals in Punjab during the period of January 2024 to April 2025. The participants who were eligible were adults between the ages of 21 and 75 years. The patients younger than 21 years were excluded because various etiologies of heart failure in younger groups and older patients older than 75 years were excluded to limit the influence of cognitive disabilities, and multimorbidity on adherence evaluation. Patients who had at least the last three months, were prescribed an HF treatment medication one, or more, had family/caretakers who would consent to participate, were able to give a verbal response in English, Urdu, or Punjabi, and could narrate their medication adherence behaviour. Patients who failed to satisfy these criteria or were severely disabled, patients who reported a previously diagnosed psychiatric or cognitive disorder that might have debilitated them to give reliable answers, pregnant patients, and those with a history of cancer were excluded. Potential participants were informed about the study through a study information sheet, questions were answered, and informed consent was written and sent back (Fig. 1).

Outcome and exposure variables

The primary outcome of the study was medication non-adherence, defined using self-reported pill counts and categorized as adherent or non-adherent based on established thresholds. The Self-Efficacy for Appropriate Medication Scale (SEAMS) was used as an independent variable to assess patients’ confidence in medication-taking behavior [23]. Medication non-adherence was assessed using two complementary measures: self-reported pill counts and the Self-Efficacy for Appropriate Medication Scale (SEAMS) [5, 24, 25]. These measures were used to capture both behavioral and self-efficacy dimensions of adherence. Exposure variables were grouped into four domains: sociodemographic, clinical, treatment-related, and behavioral factors, based on theoretical relevance and prior literature [23, 26].

Comorbid conditions included diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and obesity, either individually or in combination. Participants were further categorized based on the number of comorbid conditions into those with one and those with more than one comorbidity.

Clinical factors considered include smoking status, health insurance coverage, the presence of comorbidities (including their number and specific types), family history of HF and ischemic heart disease, memory loss, and fear of dependence on treatment. Treatment-related factors include ongoing physiotherapy, blood pressure control, disease duration, daily medication frequency, and the number of medications prescribed. Healthcare access and utilization are assessed by the distance to healthcare facilities, while patient beliefs encompass the effectiveness of medications for HF prevention and perceptions of medication side effects. Finally, satisfaction with the treating doctor was assessed using a single self-reported question (‘Are you satisfied with your doctor?’).

Tool development and data collection

We developed a structured questionnaire in Urdu and English that included four sections: (i) socio-demographic data; (ii) SEAMS, translated from English to Urdu ; (iii) pill count data; and (iv) potential risks for non-adherence (e.g., adverse effects, forgetfulness, financial concerns). SEAMS is an established instrument for measuring medication adherence [5, 6, , 25, 27–31]. In Pakistan, the SEAMS tool was translated using the standard “forward-backward” method from English to Urdu (SEAMS-U). The translated SEAMS-U exhibited good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.897) [32]. The test-retest reliability was assessed (Spearman’s P = 0.686, P < 0.001) with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) score of 0.814. The Urdu version of SEAMS is currently being published. Data were collected through interviewer-administered questionnaires in English, Urdu or Punjabi, based on participant preference. Although the instruments, including SEAMS, were originally in English, items were administered in the participant’s preferred language.We thus relied primarily on standard self-reported pill counting, with SEAMS data used for triangulation. Count data included the quantity of prescribed pills, the number of pills used, and the number not used within a specified timeframe [29, 33]. We piloted the data collection tool on 1,100 HF patients (equivalent to 10% of the sample) at a non-participating hospital. The Self-Efficacy for Appropriate Medication Scale (SEAMS) consists of 13 items, each rated on a 3-point Likert scale (1 = not confident, 2 = somewhat confident, 3 = very confident), yielding a total score ranging from 13 to 39. Higher scores indicate greater self-efficacy in medication-taking behavior, which is associated with better adherence. For analytical purposes, SEAMS scores were treated as a continuous variable, and where applicable, categorized based on established or study-specific thresholds.

Training and calibration/quality assurance of enumerators

Data collection was done by a group of trained research assistants (RAs) in all the participating centers. RAs were subjected to standardized training before the study to allow uniformity in data collection processes, such as interviewing patients and administering questionnaires. Data was collected by the same trained RAs who collected the data of the study participants, whereas the senior members of the research team controlled the data collection process, periodically checked the quality of data, and ensured that the study protocol was adhered to. These are as follows;

Ten medical students (fourth-year MBBS) were recruited as research assistants (RAs) to participate in this research. They contacted the eligible HF patients identified by clinicians in outpatient departments across the 20 facilities, and informed consent was obtained. Anonymity was guaranteed to all participants, and they were made aware of their right to pull out of the study at any time.

Health settings were the location of Master trainers in data collection, and these were the Medical Officers (MOs). These MOs were trained through web-based training via Zoom sessions to train other future MOs who have graduated from medical colleges. The RAs recruited by the principal investigator to collect the data were then guided by master trainers on how to proceed with the data collection. The members of the research team (a medical specialist and an epidemiologist) jointly trained RAs to achieve clinical accuracy and methodological consistency. After the training, a refresher session was also arranged for all RAs and MOs through Zoom. This training covered all the areas of medication adherence, adherence questions, non-adherence, counseling, and assisting the participants to fill in questionnaires. These were trained RAs who, four days after being on-site trained, went to the eligible HF survivors to volunteer to join the study.

The patients with MOs and medical specialists who identified HF patients in the outpatient departments were referred to the research assistants in the same rooms, informed consent was taken, and data were gathered.

Data analysis and management

Internal cross-verification of data was also done, team meetings were regularly conducted, and spot checks by field supervisors (MOs) were done to maintain quality, and it was analyzed using IBM SPSS version 26 and Python v3.10.12. The independent variables were divided into four domains (e.g., sociodemographic factors: age, sex, income, education), clinical factors (e.g., comorbidities, disease duration, New York Heart Association (NYHA), treatment-related factors (e.g., number of daily medications, regimen complexity, side effects), and behavioral factors (e.g., self-efficacy, patient satisfaction, health beliefs). This categorization was used throughout all the analyses. Study variables were summarized in descriptive statistics: continuous variables were represented in means and standard deviation, and counts (percentages) in the categorical variables. Non-adherence was determined by dividing the number of pills taken by the number of pills prescribed within 15 days, with a cut of less than 80% considered non-adherent, which is in line with commonly used adherence cut-offs [5, 24, 25]. The first analysis was done using Chi-square/Fishers exact test and incorporated into a generalized linear model (GLM) to be further analyzed using multivariate logistic regression on socio-demographic (e.g. age, gender, education, income) and clinical factors (e.g. comorbidities, disease duration, number/frequency of medication/day) and their association with the outcome on medication non-adherence (using the number of pills taken) was taken as the dependent variable, and the multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted. SEAMS scores were added as an independent variable to test the relationship between self-efficacy and adherence behavior. Matplotlib and Seaborn (Python-based data visualization libraries) were used to create the heatmap and odds ratio, respectively. As a measure of the extent of the relationship between clinical and sociodemographic variables and non-adherence to HF medication, Cramer’s V was calculated. Cramér’s V = , 0 ≤ V ≤ 1, where n is the size of the sample, r and c are the row and column numbers, respectively, and 2 = Chi-Square. To illustrate the correlation matrix obtained from Cramér’s V values, a heatmap was made. Variable associations were classified as weak (< 0.2), moderate (0.2–<0.4), strong (0.4–<0.6), and very strong (≥ 0.6).

In order to overcome the possible problem of multiple testing, the variables included in the multivariate model were not chosen only on the basis of statistical significance of the results of bivariate analyses but on the basis of their theoretical relevance and previous literature. The focus was laid on adjusted effect estimates and confidence intervals to provide interpretation.

De-identification of all data collected through unique codes was done and all the data were kept in safe places (locked cabinets and passwords) and were only accessed by the principle investigator and the legitimate members of the research team. The data will be stored safely in a period of 5 years according to the institutional ethical requirements.

Ethical approval, confidentiality, and consent

Study approval was provided by the Institutional Ethical Review Board of the University of Lahore (REC-UOL-/124/23), the Rehmatul-Lil-Alameen Institute of Cardiology’s (RAIC PESSI/Estt/2022/94), the Employers’ Social Security Tertiary Hospital Lahore (PESSI-1026), and Integrated Health Solutions international. All the participants volunteered to take part and provided informed consent before the study. Maximum confidentiality was provided.

Results

Sociodemographic, health-related, and clinical characteristics of participants

A total of 9,377 heart failure patients were included in the study. The study population was predominantly male 7080 (75.5%), with the majority aged 50–75 years 6798 (72.5%). Most participants were married 7761 (82.8%) and had attained graduate-level education 3966 (42.3%), with Punjabi ethnicity being the most represented group 4396 (46.9%). A substantial proportion of participants were smokers, 6086 (64.9%), and nearly half reported a monthly income between PKR 51,000–100,000, 4414 (47.1%). The majority of patients did not have health insurance 6969 (74.3%) and had been living with heart failure for more than five years 6989 (74.5%). Clinically, all participants had at least one comorbid condition, with the majority reporting multiple comorbidities 8701 (92.8%), most commonly diabetes 4618 (49.2%). Over half had no family history of stroke or cardiovascular diseases 5197 (55.4%). In terms of disease severity, New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class III was the most frequent category, 4374 (46.6%), and slightly more participants had an ejection fraction ≥ 40%, 4892 (52.2%). Uncontrolled blood pressure was prevalent, 5145 (54.9%), and a considerable proportion of patients were prescribed multiple medications daily, with most taking 5–9 medications, 4241 (45.2%). While the majority perceived their treatment as effective 6982 (74.5%), more than half reported experiencing medication side effects 4979 (53.1%). Behaviorally, a large proportion of participants were physically inactive (< 30 min/day; 407343.4%), and most resided within 30 min of a healthcare facility 5367 (57.2%). Additionally, the majority did not express fear of dependence on treatment 6911 (73.7%). Detailed characteristics are presented in Table 1.

The SEAMS responses to adherence to heart failure medication

The results of a 13-item questionnaire survey assessing adherence to HF medication are summarized using the SEAMS score. As presented in Supplementary Table S1, the mean SEAMS score for medication non-adherence was 31.65 with a standard deviation of ± 7.69.

Factors affecting non-adherence to heart failure medication

The multiple logistic regression analysis revealed several factors positively associated with non-adherence to HF medication. Socio-demographic predictors, including female gender, were more likely to be non-adherent compared to males, adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 1.415, 95% CI: 1.039 − 1.926, p = 0.027. Similarly, individuals with primary and secondary education (AOR = 1.285, 95% CI: 1.074–1.481, p = 0.005) and graduate education (AOR = 1.261, 95% CI: 1.089–1.515, p = 0.003) and those with monthly income 10,000-25000 PKR ((AOR = 1.292, 95% CI: 1.061–1.573, p = 0.044)) had increased odds of non-adherence. Likewise, individuals with no health insurance (AOR = 1.276, 95% CI: 1.138–1.430, p < 0.001) revealed higher non-adherence.

Clinical and health-related predictors, including the presence of comorbidities particularly diabetes (AOR = 1.164, 95% CI: 1.051–1.228, p = 0.010), were also linked to higher non-adherence. Additionally, patients who were not hospitalized (AOR = 1.236, 95% CI: 1.122–1.362, p < 0.001) were more likely to be non-adherent. Similarly, patients with minimally active lifestyles (30–60 min/day) (AOR = 1.333, 95% CI: 1.160–1.531, p < 0.001) and inactive (< 30 min/day) (AOR = 1.158, 95% CI: 1.011–1.326, p = 0.034) were more likely to be non-adherent.

Contrariwise, patients without memory issues were significantly less likely to non-adhere to medication (AOR = 0.273, 95% CI: 0.138–0.540, p < 0.001), suggesting a protective effect against non-adherence. Similarly, controlled blood pressure (AOR = 0.716, 95% CI: 0.648–0.792, p < 0.001) and taking less than five medications daily (AOR = 0.692, 95% CI: 0.601–0.797, p < 0.001) were also associated with lower odds of non-adherence. Taking medication once daily (AOR = 0.292, 95% CI: 0.246–0.346, p < 0.001) and twice-daily dosing (AOR = 0.830, 95% CI: 0.727–0.948, p = 0.006) potentially reduced the odds of non-adherence compared to taking it thrice daily. These findings highlight the complex interplay of sociodemographic and clinical factors influencing medication-taking behavior in HF patients (Table 2).

Overall, correlations between variables were generally weak (r < 0.20). Discussion

Medication non-adherence, assessed using pill count, was observed in 50.1% of participants. The mean SEAMS score was 31.65 ± 7.69, indicating moderate to high self-efficacy. The coexistence of relatively high self-efficacy with substantial non-adherence highlights the multifactorial nature of medication-taking behavior in heart failure patients.

Socio-demographic factors

The research results of this study help to shed light on a number of sociodemographic aspects that are greatly linked to medication non-adherence among Pakistani HF patients, which are consistent with and add to the global discussion. The higher risk of non-adherence in the female patients is echoed in the literature on the same situation, including the report by Saeed et al. in Peshawar that found gender disparities in adherence behaviours, possibly due to gender differences in access to care, health literacy, or other household priorities [19]. Moreover, the close positive correlation between increased educational levels (graduate and primary/secondary levels) and non-adherence is a somewhat counterintuitive result. This can be attributed to increased intentional and at times harmful self-management of medication due to increased health literacy, or to the fact that educated people are in more demanding jobs and this interferes with their medication routines, which has been less extensively studied in the adherence literature [5, 8]. The substantial relationship between a higher monthly income (51,000 to 100,000 PKR) and low non-adherence (AOR = 0.782) directly responds to the relationship between economic factors and the simplistic belief that more wealth always leads to more adherence. This is in line with the current studies of cost-related non-adherence (CRN), including that by Gandrakota et al., who indicate that even more affluent people are vulnerable to financial toxicity or focus on other needs, which is especially important in an LMIC like Pakistan [4, 10]. These sociodemographic correlations directly respond to the second research objective and show that poverty and lack of education do not determine the profile of a non-adherent patient in this population, but are a complex environment, interconnected with gender, socioeconomic status, and education, which requires multifaceted, specific interventions.

Clinical/Medication related factors

The safeguarding influence of a less complex medication schedule, as shown by the drastically lower likelihood of non-conformity with a daily dose versus a tri-dose, is in agreement with worldwide agreement that regimen complexity is a key obstacle, a conclusion that has been invariably demonstrated in reviews on enhancing adherence in cardiovascular disease [34]. This highlights an evident and practical goal for clinicians: to make prescriptions as simple as possible. Moreover, a correlation between side effects in patients and their higher non-adherence levels (AOR = 1.152 when patients felt that drugs had side effects) is a well-reported phenomenon, indicating patient biases that can make long-term therapy ineffective unless addressed and reported on [28, 35]. In the adjusted analysis, there was a significant correlation between memory-related variables and medication non-adherence. This is in line with certain studies, but this suggests the importance of a support system [36]. It is particularly important that the controlled blood pressure was found to be associated with reduced non-adherence (AOR = 0.716) since it shows that there is a practical clinical implication of adherence, that medication-taking behaviour directly correlates with a central treatment objective, and justifies the significance of adherence in the achievement of positive health outcomes [31]. All these clinical variables directly respond to the second research objective by going beyond the sociodemographics to highlight the variables of the treatment plan and patient experience that can be altered and that have a great impact on adherence behaviour.

Bottlenecks to the health care system and the patient-provider alliance

The barriers to adherence in the healthcare system and the dynamics between providers and patients reported in the study show important structural and relational obstacles to adherence. Distance to healthcare facility in the adjusted model has a statistically significant or consistent relationship with medication non-adherence. This implies that physical proximity in itself is an inadequate facilitator of adherence and can imply that patients living closely get a false sense of security or become complacent, knowing that help is easily available should complications occur, which is a less prevalent behavioural complexity that has been less well reflected in adherence literature [33, 37]. Despite the study of patient satisfaction, the adjusted analysis did not find any statistically significant relationship between patient satisfaction and medication non-adherence. This is contrary to existing research that points out that the quality of patient-clinician communication and trust is a foundation of long-term adherence [29, 35].

Mechanisms of behaviour and psychology

We also discovered that some of the most essential behavioural and psychological processes that inform non-adherence include self-efficacy, which is a very complicated phenomenon. Self-efficacy in medication-taking behavior was found to predict better adherence, with higher SEAMS scores, which equate to higher self-efficacy, being related to better medication adherence. Although the SEAMS tool is meant to assess confidence in medication management, this result indicates that in this group, high self-reported confidence does not necessarily translate into actual adherence behaviour. This might reflect an important discrepancy between perceived and actual capacity, or that self-assured persons will be more deliberate, but ultimately harmful, in self-management of their regimen, including omitting doses that they think they do not need [30, 37]. This difference has pointed out the limits of using only self-reported confidence measures and has indicated the necessity of more objective measures of adherence. The psychological factors directly deal with the aim of the research by shifting outside determinants and showing that inside mental and emotional processes are core to comprehending and eventually enhancing medication-taking behaviour in heart failure patients [29, 35].

Practical and comparative implications

The correlates of non-adherence identified in this Pakistani cohort show significant similarities with study findings in nearby South Asian countries, and this implies that the issues are not unique to this region, but are rather a result of a common local environment of socio-economic disadvantage, strong cultural and religious effects, and systemic constraints that permeate healthcare systems in the region [28, 33]. This similarity indicates that interventions that have proven successful in similar environments can be modified. As an example, community-led initiatives that focus on family-centred support systems and systematic peer counselling have proved to be effective in enhancing medication adherence [29, 38].

Increasing and maintaining medication adherence should be a national health issue. Multi-faceted approaches are needed to overcome barriers at the patient, provider, and health system levels to achieve success. This is a highly pressing practice given the high burden of cardiovascular diseases in the globe [39, 40]. This desirable objective can be helped by policymakers through increasing health insurance cover, developing medication subsidy initiatives, and ensuring that monitoring the adherence process becomes a routine aspect of clinical care cycles. Such systemic alterations are of particular significance in treating complex chronic diseases like congestive cardiac failure [41]. The establishment of national compliance guidelines and enhancement of the community-based support initiatives may significantly enhance the outcomes, especially in the areas where the cardiovascular disease burden is high [42, 43].

Reminder systems based on Electronic Health Records (EHRs), pharmacist-led counseling, patient-centered education materials, and simplified medication regimen should be among effective adherence strategies. Such practical applications not only enhance the adherence rates but also result in better clinical outcomes [44, 45]. Community health workers have been able to enhance medication adherence as well as health outcomes in many LMICs.

System and policy level adherence promotion recommendations.

Encouraging and maintaining medication adherence must be a health priority in the country, and it should be addressed by the use of multi-facet approaches that focus on the patient, provider, and system levels. This is more so important based on the fact that there is a high burden of cardiovascular diseases, which is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in the world [39, 40]. Health policy makers need to facilitate this initiative by providing coverage to more people under the health insurance, providing subsidies on medications, and incorporating the ability to monitor adherence as a regular part of the clinical care cycle, which is crucial in the management of chronic diseases such as congestive cardiac failure [41]. The establishment and enforcement of national compliance strategies and community-based intervention programs would greatly enhance this strategy, especially in areas with high CVD rates [42, 43].

Effective adherence-enhancing tools include Electronic Health Record (EHR)-based reminders, pharmacist-led counseling, patient-centered education, and simplified medication regimens. Additionally, an alternative viable and scalable solution can be provided by mobile health technologies and the integration of artificial intelligence [46–48]. These not only enhance compliance but also result in a clinical outcome that is better, which is of central concern in enhancing the quality of life in CVD patients [44] and can be guided by predictive models of patient survival [49]. Such strategies are particularly relevant in rural and underserved areas, where the involvement of community health workers (CHWs) can play a transformative role. This is justified by the epidemiological evidence that demonstrates the different incidence and risk factors of CVD in different regions, such as rural environments [45]. CHWs have been shown to be successful in enhancing adherence and health outcomes in a number of LMICs.

Clinical implications

The results of this paper have significant clinical implications for the treatment of HF in low- and middle-income countries such as Pakistan. The prevalence of non-adherence to medication is so high that the need to consistently evaluate medication adherence as an element of routine clinical care has become an urgent topic. Patient-specific interventions and care plans can be achieved by early detecting patients at risk using sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral predictors.

Patients need to be educated by clinicians on the importance of long-term medication use, whether they are asymptomatic and may feel they do not need adherence. Adherence can be improved by incorporating culturally sensitive counseling and simplifying medication regimens, as well as by addressing financial or access-related barriers. It is also important to enhance communication between healthcare providers and patients to gain trust and achieve better treatment results.

Future recommendations

Research on the exact forms of non-adherence (intentional and unintentional) is needed to implement the interventions targeted at the particular cases. A qualitative study would offer additional information on patient perceptions, obstacles, and facilitators of adherence. Future research can include post-hoc or pairwise analyses to investigate the differences between certain subgroups further. Also, interventions are to be created and tested to enhance medication adherence, such as health education interventions, mobile health (mHealth) applications, and enhanced provider-patient communication interventions. Adherence support should also be considered by policymakers as a part of routine care, especially in resource-limited countries such as Pakistan.

Limitations

There are some key limitations of this study. First, self-reported data were used to measure medication adherence, which could have caused the social desirability and recall bias, thus overestimating the actual rates of non-adherence. Even though self-reported pill counts were used to measure medication adherence, other objective measures (electronic monitoring devices, pharmacy refill records) were not accessible, and this can also reduce the accuracy of adherence measurement. Second, the cross-sectional study limits us to establishing causality among the identified factors and non-adherence. Third, the data did not contain the information about the exact forms of non-adherence (e.g., intentional vs. unintentional), which would have given a more in-depth view of the behaviors of patients. Finally, despite the numerous identified predictors of non-adherence, the impact of unmeasured confounding variables (including health literacy, cultural beliefs, or provider-patient communication) could not be controlled, which should be followed up with more detailed, longitudinal studies. With this many variables being looked at, it may result in a Type I error as a result of multiple testing. Even though no formal correction was made (e.g., Bonferroni adjustment) since the study was exploratory in nature, the results were viewed with a grain of salt, with less focus on effect sizes and consistency with existing literature.

Conclusion

This research underscores the paradoxical issue of drug non-adherence by Heart failure patients in Pakistan. A significant share of people do not adhere to the treatment plans they are prescribed, which indicates a significant gap regarding the effective management of heart failure. Analyzing a set of multidimensional predictors such as sociodemographic, clinical, health-related, and behavioral variables, the research offers an in-depth insight into the determinants of non-adherence. Findings indicate that adherence behaviors are shaped by both modifiable influences, such as treatment complexity and blood pressure control, and non-modifiable characteristics, including age and gender, with some unanticipated patterns linked to socioeconomic status. In the context of scarce observational data on heart failure in Pakistan, this study offers timely evidence to inform policy and programmatic action, underscoring the importance of integrated approaches that address the economic, social, and behavioral dimensions of medication-taking behavior.
