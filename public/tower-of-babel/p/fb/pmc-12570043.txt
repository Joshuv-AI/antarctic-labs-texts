Preliminary Validation of the FCD‐Q8 Tool in Functional Cognitive Disorder and Early Alzheimer's Disease: A Biomarker‐Verified Case–Control Study
Authors: Ernesto García‐Roldán, Ángela Almodóvar‐Sierra, Andrea Luque‐Tirado, Alba Marta Marín Cabañas, María Bernal Sánchez‐Arjona, Emilio Franco‐Macías
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDifferentiating functional cognitive disorder (FCD) from early Alzheimer's disease (eAD) through clinical interview alone is challenging and may lead to unnecessary testing. We aimed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the Functional Cognitive Disorder Questionnaire‐8 (FCD‐Q8) and examine its psychometric properties.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional, phase 1 case‐control study at a tertiary memory clinic in Seville, Spain, 78 adults with memory complaints were recruited: 44 with biomarker‐verified eAD (positive CSF or amyloid PET; GDS 3–4) and 34 with FCD (negative biomarkers and clinical internal inconsistency). A blinded neuropsychologist administered the FCD‐Q8. The primary outcome was diagnostic accuracy (area under the ROC curve [AUC], sensitivity, and specificity). Secondary outcomes included internal consistency (Cronbach's α with 95% CI) and item characteristics using two‐parameter logistic Item Response Theory (2PL IRT).ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 67.5 years (SD ± 7.1); 59% were female. The FCD‐Q8 demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy with an AUC of 0.87 (95% CI 0.78–0.95). At a cut‐off ≥ 5, sensitivity was 76.5% and specificity 88.6%. Internal consistency was moderate (α = 0.61; 95% CI 0.47–0.70). 2PL IRT analysis showed that items related to checking behaviours were the most discriminative (difficulty 3.70), while questions on compound statements and premorbid self‐appraisal were the least discriminative. Attending the clinical interview alone was the most difficult item (1.47).ConclusionThe FCD‐Q8 demonstrated good diagnostic utility to distinguish FCD from eAD in biomarker‐verified populations. These findings support progression to a subsequent prospective validation phase, which could further establish its clinical and research applicability.Keywords: biomarker‐verified diagnosis, cognitive screening, diagnostic accuracy, early Alzheimer's disease, functional cognitive disorder, internal consistency, item response theory, psychometric assessment

Introduction

Functional Cognitive Disorder (FCD) is an increasingly recognized cause of subjective memory complaints and perceived cognitive decline, accounting for up to 24% of patients seen in specialized memory clinics [1, 2]. Diagnosis rests on identifying internal inconsistency between the patients' self‐reported deficits and objective performance [2, 3, 4]. Observational studies in memory clinic settings reveal that patients with FCD frequently attend appointments unaccompanied, provide detailed accounts of their symptoms (preserving language, episodic, and working memory), exhibit impaired metacognitive monitoring with catastrophic appraisals of minor lapses, engage in repetitive checking (e.g., excessive notetaking that slows task completion), and avoid challenging cognitive activities [4, 5, 6, 7]. Besides this, diagnostic criteria for the cognitive subtype of FCD have been proposed, requiring: (1) one or more cognitive complaints; (2) clinical evidence of internal inconsistency; (3) exclusion of other medical or psychiatric causes; and (4) significant distress or functional impairment necessitating medical evaluation [8]. Furthermore, a recent systematic review and diagnostic meta‐analysis synthesized the clinical signs of FCD, identifying signs such as frequent checking, elaborated examples of memory failures, longer duration of spoken answer, and unaccompanied clinic attendance as the most distinguishable features of FCD [9]. These findings reinforce the need for structured tools to assess such symptoms systematically.

Improved understanding of the clinical features of FCD and its prompt recognition is essential to mitigate patient anxiety, avoid unnecessary diagnostic workups, and ensure appropriate treatment [4]. Although experienced clinicians may recognize interactional differences between patients with FCD and those with early Alzheimer disease (eAD), distinguishing the two can be challenging, especially when patients with eAD are younger and retain partial insight into their deficits. Clinicians detect internal inconsistency when reported cognitive difficulties conflict with conversational fluency, task performance, or collateral history; however, this sign remains largely subjective and requires formal quantification. Accordingly, brief, consultation‐friendly tools are needed to capture the characteristic symptoms and positive signs of FCD. Existing questionnaires [10, 11] have not been validated against biologically defined cohorts and do not fully address the multidimensional nature of FCD described in previous reports [4, 6, 7].

The FCD‐Q8 (Functional Cognitive Disorder Questionnaire‐Eight) is a structured clinician‐rated questionnaire developed to capture the most specific and characteristic signs of FCD. It is completed by a trained clinician based on patients and their companion interaction (Items 1–3) and direct questions to the patient (Items 4–8) during the standard memory clinic interview. Items are based on features described in prior qualitative and observational studies of this syndrome [2, 7, 8, 9]. The final questionnaire was created by specialists in the Memory Unit of Hospital Universitario Virgen del Rocío (Sevilla, Spain) and underwent preliminary pilot testing in routine clinical practice to assess feasibility and clarity. Finally, in this paper, which represents a phase 1, preliminary validation of a diagnostic tool, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of this novel instrument in patients with cognitive complaints, using Alzheimer disease (AD) biomarker status for group classification.

Method

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a phase I diagnostic validation study with a cross‐sectional, case‐control design using a convenience sample. Recruitment and all assessments took place in the Memory Unit of the Neurology Service at Hospital Universitario Virgen del Rocío (HUVR) (Seville, Spain).

Participants

Consecutive patients meeting the following criteria were assigned to one of two groups:

Group A: Early Alzheimer's Disease (eAD).

Age 50–85 years.

Referral for memory complaints.

Comprehensive evaluation in the Memory Unit, including neuropsychological testing, neuroimaging (Brain CT or MRI), and AD biomarker study (CSF or amyloid PET).

Positive AD biomarker.

Global Deterioration Scale (GDS) stage 3 or 4.

Group B: Functional Cognitive Disorder (FCD).

Age 50–85 years.

Referral for memory complaints.

Same evaluation protocol as Group A.

Negative AD biomarker.

Evidence of internal inconsistency meeting the international diagnostic criteria for FCD [8].

Exclusion Criteria

Diagnosis of any neurodegenerative disease other than AD.

Negative Alzheimer's disease biomarkers without evidence of internal inconsistency.

Severe psychiatric illness (active major depression, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder).

Visual or hearing impairments precluding neuropsychological assessment.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was approved by the HUVR Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion.

Comprehensive Evaluation in the Memory Unit, in the Diagnostic Phase, Preceding Validation

All participants underwent a standardized evaluation, including:

Sociodemographic and cognitive reserve: Age, sex, education level (less than primary, primary, more than primary education) and Total score on the Cognitive Reserve Questionnaire [12].

Global cognition: Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) [13].

Memory domain: Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test + Immediate Recall (FCSRT+IR) [14], and Seine‐Saint‐Denis associative memory test (TMA‐93) [15].

Executive domain: Stroop Test (words, colours, interference, effect) [16], Phonemic fluency [17], and Digit Span (forward and backward) [18].

Language domain: Abbreviated Boston Naming Test [19], and Semantic verbal fluency [20].

Visuospatial domain: Copy of the RBANS figure [21], VOSP Number Location subtest [22], and Barcelona Overlapping Figures subtest [23].

Behavioural domain: Goldberg Anxiety and Depression Scale [24] (self‐report), and Mild Behavioural Impairment Checklist (MBI‐C) [25] total and domain scores (informant report).

Functional status: ad8 Dementia Screening Interview [26], Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ) [27], and Functional Assessment Staging Tool (FAST/GDS) [28].

Neuroimaging and biomarkers: Brain CT or MRI to exclude non‐AD structural pathology, and one of the following biomarker procedures: Amyloid PET (Florbetaben) read visually by a nuclear medicine specialist (positive/negative), or CSF assays (Lumipulse or Euroimmun): Aβ₄₂ (pg/mL), Aβ₄₂/₄₀ ratio, total Tau (pg/mL), phosphorylated Tau (pTau, pg/mL).

Validation of the FCD‐Q8

At the return visit when biomarker results were disclosed, a neuropsychologist—blinded to each patient's biomarker status—administered and scored the FCD‐Q8 in a structured interview with both patient and informant.

The questionnaire presents eight items that evaluate the four domains of the FCD:

Internal inconsistency (Items 1, 3, 4).

Awareness of illness (Items 1, 2).

Failure of metacognition (Items 5, 6).

Obsessive and avoidance behaviours (Items 7, 8).

Each item is answered “No” (0) or “Yes” (1). Total score ranges from 0 to 8, with higher scores indicating greater likelihood of FCD (Table 1).

Statistical Analysis

We compared sociodemographic and neuropsychological variables (including FCD‐Q8 total score) between eAD (Group A) and FCD (Group B) patients. Quantitative data were assessed for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test). Normally distributed variables were compared with Student's t‐test; non‐normal variables with the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Categorical data were compared using Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. For each FCD‐Q8 item, we constructed 2 × 2 contingency tables and used chi‐square tests to compare response frequencies across groups. We then calculated odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals to quantify the likelihood of a positive response in FCD versus eAD. To evaluate the overall diagnostic utility of the FCD‐Q8, we generated a receiver‐operating characteristic (ROC) curve and computed the area under the curve (AUC). We interpreted AUC values as follows: Optimal: ≥ 0.90, Good: 0.80–0.89, Reasonable: 0.70–0.79, Low: < 0.70 [29, 30]. Sensitivity and specificity at the optimal cut‐off were determined by maximizing Youden's index. We assessed internal consistency of the FCD‐Q8 with Cronbach's alpha (α) and examined each item's impact via “alpha if item deleted.” Finally, we fitted a two‐parameter logistic Item Response Theory (2PL IRT) [31] to estimate item difficulty and discrimination. In this context, “difficulty” reflects the level of the latent trait (here, FCD‐related features) required for a respondent to have a 50% probability of endorsing the item, while “discrimination” indicates how well the item distinguishes between individuals with slightly different levels of that trait. All analyses and figures were produced in RStudio 2024.04.2 + 764. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Conversely, FCD patients scored higher on forward Digit Span (7 [6–8] vs. 6 [5–8]; p < 0.01), with no group difference on backward span. Global cognition, language, and visuospatial measures did not differ between groups (Table 3). Item‐level analysis of the FCD‐Q8 (Table 4) revealed that several items significantly differentiated FCD from eAD. Items 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8 showed significantly higher endorsement rates in the FCD group. The strongest discriminative power is seen in Item 1 (“Alone in the interview”), present in 32.4% of FCD patients versus only 2.3% of eAD (OR = 20.56, p < 0.001). In addition to this, Items 3 and 8, related to over‐elaboration and activity avoidance, also showed large effect sizes (presence of 91.2% in FCD vs. 56.8% eAD patients with ORs = 7.85, p < 0.01, and 58.8% vs. 18.2%, with ORs = 6.48, p = 0.01, respectively). Among all the items, only Item 5 did not significantly differentiate groups (p = 0.58).

Overall diagnostic utility of the FCD‐Q8 was good: AUC: 0.87 (95% CI: 0.78–0.95). The optimal cutoff was ≥ 5, yielding sensitivity 76.5% and specificity 88.6% (Figure 1A). Internal consistency was moderate (Cronbach's α = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.47–0.70). “Alpha if item deleted” and the 2‐parameter IRT model (2PL) revealed that Item 4 was the only item whose removal slightly increased α (to 0.63). In the 2PL IRT analysis, Items 4 and 5 were the easiest (difficulty −2.61 and −2.36, respectively) and least discriminative (discrimination 0.25 and 0.41) (Table 5). Omitting these items did not change the AUC but increased sensitivity at the expense of specificity (Figure 1B). By contrast, Item 1 was the most difficult (1.47) with good discrimination (1.66), and Item 7 was the most discriminative (3.70) and relatively easy (0.25) (Table 5).

Discussion

The FCD‐Q8 demonstrated robust performance for discriminating FCD from eAD in a memory clinic population enriched by biomarker verification. Our principal finding—AUC of 0.87 (95% CI 0.78–0.95)—places the FCD‐Q8 squarely in the “good” range for diagnostic accuracy, and close to the “optimal” (≥ 0.90) threshold. At the empirically derived cut‐off ≥ 5, the instrument achieved a sensitivity of 76.5% and specificity of 88.6%. The demonstrated usefulness of the tool invites progression to advanced validation phases, in memory specialized settings, where FCD prevalence is high and increasing [1, 2].

Concerning sociodemographic data, FCD patients were younger than those with eAD, in line with other studies [2, 32]. However, in this work, the age of FCD patients was not low, placing them closer to the age range of early‐onset AD, which could make the results even more relevant in clinical practice. Females were predominant in the FCD group, as described in other studies, but the distribution was similar to the eAD group. No differences arose in educational attainment or cognitive reserve, aligning with evidence that FCD prevalence is independent of these factors [33].

Regarding neuropsychological profiles, FCD patients scored low on memory tests (FCSRT + IR) but higher than eAD patients. In executive tasks, FCD patients showed lower forward Digit Span and Stroop interference scores; however, the Stroop effect was more pronounced in the eAD group—suggesting that while dysexecutive features appear in FCD, they are more severe in eAD. Behaviourally, informant ratings (MBI‐C) indicated motivational and affective impairments non‐distinctive between groups. Conversely, self‐reports tests (such as Goldberg Anxiety and Depression Scale) revealed markedly higher anxiety and depression in FCD than in AD, likely reflecting anosognosia for behavioral symptoms in eAD versus “hyperinsight” in FCD.

Item‐level analysis of the FCD‐Q8 demonstrated heterogeneity across the internal inconsistency domain (Items 1, 3, 4). Item 1 was moderately discriminative—rare in eAD yet common in FCD—yielding moderate difficulty. Item 3, though highly prevalent in FCD, also appeared in over half of eAD cases, reducing its discrimination relative to Item 1. Item 4 exhibited low difficulty and discrimination despite significant group differences, perhaps because working memory remains intact in eAD; its removal increased Cronbach's alpha but did not alter overall diagnostic accuracy (Figure 1B). We hypothesize that Item 4 may gain discriminative power in later AD stages, when working memory deficits emerge. Items 1 and 2 (awareness of illness) performed well as markers of FCD's “hyperinsight.” Item 2 showed moderate difficulty and discrimination, driven by 80% negative responses in eAD consistent with anosognosia; the 44% negative rate in FCD suggests some informant‐patient symbiosis, wherein patients perceive more memory problems while informants focus on functional repercussions.

A novel contribution of the FCD‐Q8 is its metacognition failure items (5–6). Item 5 did not differ significantly between groups—76.5% of FCD and 68.2% of eAD affirmed it—suggesting good insight into prior memory in both groups. Although nondiscriminative, Item 5 supports FCD diagnosis since patients with FCD tend to overestimate their past memory in contrast to the very negative value they assign to their current memory; in addition, its removal did not improve AUC, sensitivity, or specificity (Figure 1B). Item 6 was more discriminatory, reflecting FCD's metacognitive distortion versus AD anosognosia [6, 34]. The inclusion of obsessive‐compulsive dimension items (7–8) also represents an advance. McWhirter and colleagues identified repetitive checking and avoidance as FCD hallmarks, yet these behaviors had not been quantified before. In this validation, both items showed high positive rates and strong discrimination for FCD.

Our study has several key strengths. First, it was conducted under real‐world conditions in a tertiary memory clinic, enhancing its applicability to routine practice. Second, we enriched diagnostic groups with biomarker results (CSF or amyloid PET)—negative for FCD and positive for eAD—a novel approach that adds certainty to group classification. These aspects differ from a recent pilot study [11] which has developed both 11 items and 7 items checklist to distinguish FCD from other neurocognitive disorders. Their checklists achieved excellent AUCs of 0.97 and 0.96, respectively, and demonstrated good internal consistency (α > 0.80). However, from our point of view, this work differs from ours based on their methodology. Their creative process relied on international consensus using a three‐round Delphi process and did not include biomarker‐verified validation. Another strength of our study is that we have not excluded patients for having abnormal cognitive examination. In fact, the FCD group showed scores in global cognition and memory and executive domains [5, 6, 10, 35, 36], in the range of cognitive impairment, in relation to what is observed in clinical practice, making discrimination more difficult and giving more validity to the result obtained by the FCD‐Q8.

Limitations include the preliminary, case‐control design and biomarker‐enriched sampling, potentially inflating accuracy compared to unselected referrals. Prospective validation in broader clinical populations is required. The moderate Cronbach's α (0.61) reflects both the short scale length and its multidimensional structure; further psychometric work may refine item composition. Finally, although bootstrap methods estimated α's confidence interval, 2PL IRT parameter confidence intervals were unstable—future studies might employ alternative modeling approaches.

Conclusions and Future Directions

The FCD‐Q8 is a concise, multidimensional tool that reliably differentiates FCD from eAD in a biomarker‐verified memory‐clinic cohort. Future work should include prospective cohort studies, cross‐validation in primary care, integration with plasma biomarkers, longitudinal follow‐up, and cultural adaptation. By facilitating early and accurate identification of FCD, the FCD‐Q8 may reduce unnecessary investigations and guide appropriate management strategies in cognitive disorders.
