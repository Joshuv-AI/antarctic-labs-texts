Pre-treatment with creatinine alters the perceived bitterness–sweetness balance of L-histidine in young Japanese female participants
Authors: Takashi Kondoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaste modulation induced by interactions among specific compounds remains poorly understood. L-histidine and creatinine are both bitter compounds abundant in dried bonito dashi. This study investigated whether 10 bitter compounds could shift the taste quality of L-histidine from bitterness toward sweetness, as assessed by sensory evaluation using a fixed-sum method. When presented alone, 4% L-histidine elicited bitterness accompanied by weak sweetness, whereas iso-bitter creatinine evoked predominantly bitterness. Remarkably, pre-stimulation with creatinine shifted the taste quality of L-histidine toward sweetness. Similar effects were observed after pre-stimulation with isoleucine, tryptophan, phenylalanine, and creatine, but not with histidine, valine, leucine, caffeine, or carnosine. This effect was transient, as increasing the interval after creatinine pre-stimulation restored the bitterness of L-histidine. In addition, 0.1% NaCl altered the bitterness-to-sweetness balance of L-histidine toward sweetness after pre-stimulation with creatinine. The taste quality of histidine alone was highly sensitive to pH: bitterness peaked at pH 7.0, whereas sweetness increased under alkaline conditions. In contrast, varying the pH of the pre-stimulation solution between 5.0 and 8.0 did not affect the modulation of L-histidine taste. These findings demonstrate a previously unrecognized histidine-specific form of taste modulation, suggesting interactions among naturally coexisting bitter compounds may dynamically alter taste qualities in traditional broths and other complex foods.

Introduction

Taste modulation refers to the phenomenon in which the perceived quality or intensity of a taste stimulus changes depending on the stimulus context, including prior adaptation and sequential presentation of different tastants. Textbooks in food and nutrition science describe, for example, that water can taste sweet after exposure to sour, bitter, or very salty solutions (Esumi 2007; Mizushina et al. 2021). Such phenomena suggest that taste perception is dynamically shaped by preceding gustatory stimulation. This context dependency has long been studied in relation to adaptation and cross adaptation. Lawless (1982) showed that adaptation to taste mixtures can produce paradoxical changes in subsequent taste perception, indicating that perceived taste quality cannot always be predicted from the adapted stimulus in a simple additive manner. In addition, Lawless and Stevens (1983) reported cross adaptation between sucrose and intensive sweeteners, demonstrating that prior exposure to one sweet stimulus can influence the perceived intensity of another. Together, these early studies established that gustatory responses are strongly affected by recent stimulus history and provided a foundation for interpreting sequential taste interactions.

In other studies, unusual taste sensations evoked after adaptation, including altered taste quality of water and changes in the balance among taste components following exposure to specific tastants have been characterized. Bartoshuk et al. (1964) reported that water presented after adaptation to NaCl is not tasteless but elicits a distinct taste sensation, suggesting that the taste of water may emerge through release from adaptation. Furthermore, McBurney and Bartoshuk (1973) showed that interactions among different taste qualities can alter the perceived quality of a subsequent stimulus. Together with the cross-adaptation studies of Lawless and colleagues, these reports positioned taste perception not as a fixed property of a stimulus itself, but as a context-dependent percept shaped by adaptation, contrast, and interactions among different taste qualities.

Taste alteration can also be induced by taste-modifying proteins (Mizushina et al. 2021; Izumi and Kumazawa 2022; Watanabe et al. 2026). The sweet-inducing proteins miraculin, curculin, and neoculin, elicit a sweet sensation under acidic conditions by activating sweet taste receptors (Kurihara and Beidler 1968, 1969; Yamashita et al. 1990; Nakajima et al. 2008; Koizumi et al. 2011; Misaka 2013). In another case, a sweet “water taste” has been observed after rinsing off lactisole, an inhibitor of the sweet taste receptor (Galindo-Cuspinera et al. 2006; Alvarado et al. 2017); this effect has been explained by transient activation of sweet taste receptors when lactisole dissociates during rinsing (Alvarado et al. 2017). A similar sweet “water taste” has been reported after exposure to high concentrations of artificial sweeteners such as sodium saccharin and acesulfame potassium (Galindo-Cuspinera et al. 2006). More recent examples are found in complex foods. Kondoh et al. (2025) reported that when rice cake (boiled or grilled) is consumed together with acidic beverages (juices), a bitter sensation is perceived, even though both the rice cake and the beverage alone are sweet. Occurrence of bitterness with the juice was also observed in cooked foods made from glutinous (sticky or waxy) rice, wheat, and millet flours, but not in those made from non-glutinous food materials or from purified amylopectin (Kondoh and Tanaka 2026). At the same time, examples in which a single compound reproducibly shifts from its original taste quality to a different basic taste under specific conditions remain limited.

Dried bonito (katsuobushi) dashi contains a variety of bitter components (Fuke et al. 1989), among which 2 major compounds L-histidine and creatinine are bitter ones (Kondoh 2014, 2017). In the course of preliminary sensory experiments conducted as part of a taste reconstitution study of dried bonito dashi (Kondoh 2025), I unexpectedly observed that the perceived taste quality of L-histidine changed from bitterness toward sweetness when it was evaluated immediately after creatinine exposure. Although this observation was made in the context of dried bonito dashi, the phenomenon itself may not be limited to dashi, and to my knowledge, it has not been documented previously. Its characteristics and underlying mechanism therefore remain unclear.

The present study examined this taste modulation phenomenon in detail using sensory evaluation, focusing on the shift in the perceived taste quality of L-histidine from bitterness toward sweetness under specific conditions. To assess the specificity of this effect, 10 bitter compounds were tested, including 9 compounds found in dried bonito dashi and another typical bitter compound caffeine as a representative bitter compound.

Materials and methods

Participants

Twelve healthy female students (21 to 22 years old) enrolled in the Faculty of Agriculture, Kindai University participated in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Review Board of the Faculty of Agriculture, Kindai University (approval Nos. 2021-13A and 2023-21A) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects. All participants received a written explanation of the aims and procedures of the study and provided written informed consent before participation.

All participants had prior experience with sensory evaluation of taste using solutions representing the 5 basic tastes (NaCl, sucrose, monosodium L-glutamate, citric acid, and caffeine), as well as other tastants such as glucose, fructose, sucralose, lactic acid, tartaric acid, and KCl. They had also received training in standard sensory evaluation methods, including paired-comparison tests, triangle tests, ranking tests, staircase procedures for detection thresholds, and evaluations using the general Labeled Magnitude Scale (gLMS) and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Because the purpose of this study was to compare subtle changes in the balance of taste qualities across multiple bitter compounds and conditions, a trained panel was used to improve response consistency and sensitivity to small sensory differences. Through these prior assessments, I confirmed that none of the participants showed evidence of ageusia or partial taste dysfunction. Individuals who smoked were excluded.

Test solutions

Ten bitter compounds were used: L-histidine, L-valine, L-leucine, L(+)-isoleucine, L-tryptophan, L(−)-phenylalanine, creatine monohydrate, creatinine, caffeine, and L-carnosine (all from Fujifilm Wako, Tokyo, Japan; Table 1). L-carnosine (β-alanyl-L-histidine) is a histidine-containing dipeptide composed of β-alanine and L-histidine. With the exception of caffeine, these compounds are components of dried bonito dashi (Fuke et al. 1989). Among the amino acids, valine, leucine, and isoleucine were selected as representative branched-chain amino acids, whereas phenylalanine and tryptophan were selected as representative aromatic amino acids. Thus, the compounds were selected not only because they occur in dried bonito dashi, but also because they represent structurally distinct hydrophobic amino acids. Tyrosine, another aromatic amino acid, was not included because of its very low solubility in water. The concentration of L-histidine was set at 4% because lower concentrations (e.g. 1%) were nearly tasteless, whereas 4% L-histidine produced clearly perceptible bitterness. The concentrations of the other bitter compounds were determined in preliminary sensory tests so that their bitterness was approximately matched to that of 4% L-histidine. In the present study, creatinine was used at a lower concentration than creatine (1% [88 mM] vs. 2% [134 mM], respectively). This was consistent with the report by Lawless (1979b) that creatinine elicits a stronger bitter taste than creatine. In addition, 0.1% NaCl, 4% sucrose, 0.2% NaH2PO4, and 0.2% Na2HPO4 (all from Fujifilm Wako, Tokyo, Japan) were used.

All chemicals were dissolved in distilled water (Aquarich Water; Kyoei Pharmaceuticals, Chiba, Japan). L-histidine was dissolved by heating to 50 °C and then left at room temperature for at least 1 h before use. Phosphate buffer solutions at pH 5.0 to 8.0 were prepared by mixing appropriate volumes of 0.2% NaH2PO4 and 0.2% Na2HPO4. For non-phosphate solutions, pH was adjusted with small amounts of HCl or NaOH. The pH was measured using a pH meter (D-52; Horiba, Kyoto, Japan).

Sensory evaluation

Participants were instructed to refrain from eating or drinking anything except for water for at least 90 min before the start of the experiments. The experiments were conducted in the afternoon, typically beginning at 15:00 and ending approximately 60 min later. Before each experimental session, they rinsed their mouths with distilled water and confirmed that the water was tasteless. Ten-milliliter aliquot of the test solutions were presented at room temperature (∼23 °C) in 60-ml polyethylene terephthalate cups (Sunnap, Tokyo, Japan).

Participants were instructed to hold each solution in the mouth for 5 s and then swallow it. Although the sip-and-spit method is commonly used in taste evaluation, the sip-and-swallow method was adopted in the present study to better simulate actual broth consumption and to allow stimulation of the entire oral cavity and the pharyngeal region. This choice was also supported by Running and Hayes (2017), who showed that sip-and-spit versus sip-and-swallow procedures can differentially alter taste intensity estimates across stimuli. In experiments involving pre-stimulation with bitter compounds, the pre-stimulus solution was held in the mouth for 5 s and swallowed, and the test solution (L-histidine or L-valine) was then tasted for 5 s immediately afterward. In experiments examining the effect of the interval between the pre-stimulus and test stimulus, interstimulus intervals of 0 s (immediately), 30 s, 180 s, or 300 s were used without mouth rinsing during the interval.

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the relative balance among taste qualities rather than the absolute intensity of individual taste qualities. Therefore, instead of using high-resolution intensity scales such as the gLMS or VAS, I used a fixed-total point-allocation method. This procedure was conceptually based on that of Narukawa et al. (2008), in which the balance of taste qualities was assessed by distributing a fixed total number of points across taste categories. In the present study, participants allocated a total of 10 points among 3 categories—bitterness, sweetness, and other taste qualities—according to their perceived relative contribution to the overall taste of each sample. For example, a sample perceived as 80% bitter and 20% sweet was scored as 8 points for bitterness, 2 points for sweetness, and 0 points for other taste qualities. In experiments examining pH effects, sourness was added as a fourth category. This simplified procedure was adopted to capture relative taste balance with minimizing participant burden. After each sample, participants rinsed their mouths at least twice with distilled water and waited at least 2 min before tasting the next sample. After each session, they rested for at least 5 min. If any aftertaste remained, additional rinsing was performed until the sensation disappeared. The number of trials per day, defined as the number of solution intakes, was limited to a maximum of 20 samples.

Data analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± SD. Main effects and interactions of factors such as compound, taste quality, interval time, and pH were analyzed using 2-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Sphericity was assessed by Mauchly's test, and when the assumption of sphericity was violated (P < 0.05), the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni or Dunnett method, as appropriate.

Under the fixed-total point-allocation method, a stimulus perceived exclusively as bitter would, in principle, receive a bitterness score of 10 and a sweetness score of 0. Accordingly, 1-sample t-tests against these reference values were used to assess whether the observed ratings deviated from the theoretical profile of a purely bitter stimulus. For example, if a nominally bitter compound also evoked sweetness, its bitterness score would be expected to fall below 10 and its sweetness score to exceed 0. Paired t-tests were used for comparisons between 2 related conditions. Effect sizes for paired comparisons were expressed as Cohen's d, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively (Cohen 1988). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Taste qualities of individual compounds

L-histidine is known to elicit bitterness at concentrations below 100 mM (Ninomiya 1968; Kawai et al. 2012), whereas at higher concentrations it evokes slight sweetness in addition to bitterness (Ninomiya 1968). In the present study, I evaluated the taste qualities (bitterness, sweetness, and other taste qualities) of 10 bitter compounds including L-histidine and creatinine.

A 4% (258 mM) L-histidine solution was perceived predominantly as bitter, with a weaker sweet sensation (bitterness score: 7.08 ± 1.62, d = 1.799; sweetness score: 2.83 ± 1.47, d = 1.796; Fig. 1). L-valine showed a similar profile (bitterness: 7.75 ± 0.97, d = 2.331; sweetness: 2.25 ± 0.97, d = 2.331). For both histidine and valine, bitterness scores were significantly lower than the maximum score of 10 (P < 0.001, 1-sample t-test; Table 2), whereas sweetness scores were significantly greater than 0 (P < 0.001).

Modulation of histidine taste quality by prior exposure to different bitter compounds

I next examined the effect of prior exposure to various bitter solutions on the taste quality of L-histidine. Participants held each bitter solution in the mouth for 5 s, swallowed it, and then immediately tasted L-histidine for 5 s. The taste qualities of L-histidine were then evaluated.

When histidine itself was used as the prior stimulus, no significant change was observed in the taste quality of the subsequently presented histidine solution (bitterness: first 7.08 ± 1.62 vs. second 7.83 ± 1.27; sweetness: first 2.83 ± 1.47 vs. second 2.17 ± 1.27; both P > 0.05; Figs. 1 and 2). Similarly, prior exposure to valine, leucine, caffeine, or carnosine did not significantly alter either the bitterness or sweetness scores of histidine (Fig. 2). Under these 5 prior-stimulation conditions, the bitterness score of histidine was significantly greater than its sweetness score (P < 0.001, paired t-test), and no other taste qualities were observed.

Effect of interval between prior creatinine exposure and histidine presentation

Because the largest modulation of histidine taste quality was observed following prior exposure to creatinine, I next varied the interval between creatinine exposure and histidine presentation, without mouth rinsing during the interval, to examine the time course of this effect.

When histidine was tasted immediately (0 s) after swallowing creatinine, sweetness was rated significantly higher than bitterness (bitterness: 2.08 ± 1.31; sweetness: 7.42 ± 1.38) (Fig. 3). As the interval increased, the taste profile changed gradually: at 180 s, bitterness and sweetness were approximately equal (bitterness: 4.17 ± 1.27; sweetness: 5.08 ± 1.44), whereas at 300 s bitterness exceeded sweetness (bitterness: 5.67 ± 1.67; sweetness: 3.58 ± 1.93). Scores for other taste qualities did not change over time. Mauchly's test indicated that sphericity assumption was violated for the interaction (χ2 = 12.777, P < 0.05). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of taste quality (F(1,11) = 14.616, P < 0.01) and a significant interaction (F(1.671, 18.380) = 25.144, P < 0.001). Post hoc Bonferroni comparisons revealed significant differences between sweetness and bitterness scores at 0, 30, and 300 s (P < 0.001 or P < 0.05). One-way ANOVA for each taste quality showed that bitterness scores increased over time (F(1.840, 20.245) = 22.682, P < 0.01), whereas sweetness scores decreased over time (F(1.614, 17.756) = 23.863, P < 0.001); scores for other taste qualities did not change significantly.

Effect of NaCl addition

In humans, NaCl has been reported to suppress bitterness (Breslin and Beauchamp 1995, 1997; Keast et al. 2001, 2004; Kondoh et al. 2026) and enhance sweetness (Hamajima 1969; Breslin and Beauchamp 1997; Uchida et al. 2013). I therefore examined how the addition of NaCl to histidine affects its taste quality, both with and without prior exposure to creatinine. The NaCl concentration was set at 0.1% (17 mM), which is below the recognition threshold for saltiness (Ishida and Kikuchi 1991; Webb et al. 2015).

Without prior creatinine exposure (Fig. 4a), the bitterness score of histidine was significantly higher than the sweetness score (bitterness: 6.58 ± 1.44 vs. sweetness: 3.33 ± 1.30; P < 0.001, paired t-test). Addition of 0.1% NaCl significantly decreased bitterness score (3.92 ± 1.51; P < 0.001) and significantly increased sweetness score (4.25 ± 1.82; P < 0.05), such that the difference between bitterness and sweetness was no longer significant. In addition, 0.1% NaCl significantly increased score for other taste qualities (without NaCl: 0.08 ± 0.29 vs. with NaCl: 1.83 ± 1.85; P < 0.01).

Effect of pH on taste modulation

For histidine alone, bitterness scores were highest at pH 7.0 (7.08 ± 2.11) and decreased at both lower and higher pH values (Fig. 5a). Sweetness scores increased at pH ≥ 6.5 and reached the highest value within the tested range at pH 8.0 (6.67 ± 2.31). Mauchly's test indicated violations of sphericity for the main effects of taste quality and pH. Two-way ANOVA showed significant main effects of taste quality (F(1.571, 17.280) = 50.374, P < 0.001) and pH (F(2.559, 3.401) = 34.154, P < 0.001), as well as a significant interaction (F(18,198) = 44.863, P < 0.001). Discussion

L-histidine is generally classified as a bitter amino acid (Kawai et al. 2012), but at high concentrations it exhibits slight sweetness in addition to bitterness (Ninomiya 1968). At the concentration used in the present study (4%, 258 mM), the taste quality of histidine showed a bitterness-to-sweetness ratio of approximately 7:3. In contrast, when histidine was evaluated after a preceding exposure to the bitter compound creatinine, this ratio was reversed to approximately 3:7, and histidine was perceived as predominantly “sweet.” In other words, I observed a pronounced shift in the relative balance of taste qualities in histidine following preceding stimulation with creatinine. Furthermore, preceding stimulation with other bitter amino acids and related compounds—namely isoleucine, tryptophan, phenylalanine, and creatine—also changed the taste-quality balance of histidine, shifting the bitterness-to-sweetness ratio to approximately 5:5. However, not all bitter compounds exhibited such effects: no significant change in the taste-quality balance of histidine was observed when histidine itself, valine, leucine, caffeine, or carnosine was used as the preceding stimulus. Thus, it is likely that the mere quality of “tasting bitter” does not determine the presence or absence of taste modulation; rather, differences in receptor-recognition profiles and mechanisms of action at the taste-cell level for each compound are likely to be of greater importance.

Lawless (1979b) reported that creatinine exhibits a distinct bitter taste, whereas creatine is less bitter. Thus, the use of creatinine as a bitter pre-exposure stimulus in the present study is consistent with previous sensory findings. However, my results extend those observations by suggesting that prior exposure to creatinine may alter the subsequent taste balance of histidine, rather than simply reflecting the intrinsic bitterness of creatinine itself. Taste modulation of histidine by creatinine was characterized by 3 features: (i) the effect gradually weakened as the interval between creatinine and histidine stimulation increased; (ii) the effect was enhanced by adding NaCl to the histidine solution; and (iii) it was not influenced by the pH of the preceding solution. In contrast, the taste quality of histidine alone was highly pH dependent: under acidic or basic conditions its bitterness-to-sweetness balance changed, and under basic conditions the relative contribution of sweetness increased, indicating high sensitivity to protonation state. By comparison, the taste qualities of sucrose, leucine, isoleucine, tryptophan, and creatinine were scarcely affected by pH changes, suggesting that pH-dependence of histidine taste quality is most likely related to the protonation state of histidine rather than to that of sweet or bitter taste receptors. Notably, valine, a neutral amino acid, showed a trend similar to histidine in that preceding stimulation with creatinine shifted its taste-quality balance. Thus, the phenomenon whereby a subset of amino acids with dual taste qualities (bitterness and sweetness) undergoes a perceptual shift in taste-quality balance when preceded by specific bitter compounds indicates a novel type of taste modulation that, to my knowledge, has not been reported previously.

Taste qualities of individual bitter compounds

The bitter compounds used in this study fell into 2 groups: those that elicited almost exclusively bitterness and those that elicited both bitterness and sweetness. Branched-chain and aromatic amino acids such as leucine, isoleucine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine have been reported to be bitter amino acids (Ninomiya 1968; Kawai et al. 2012), and in the present study they were also perceived exclusively as bitter. In contrast, high concentrations of histidine and valine were perceived as both bitter and sweet, consistent with previous reports (Ninomiya 1968; Kawai et al. 2012), confirming that they possess dual taste qualities at high concentrations.

Carnosine is a dipeptide composed of β-alanine and L-histidine and is present in dried bonito flake and their dashi (Fuke et al. 1989), and histidine-containing peptides often exhibit bitterness (Kato et al. 1989). In the present study, 4% (177 mM) carnosine exhibited both bitterness and sweetness, resembling histidine, but with a higher relative contribution of sweetness, yielding a bitterness-to-sweetness ratio of approximately 5:5. This suggests that, while carnosine shares some taste properties with histidine, the presence of the β-alanine moiety may alter the balance between bitterness and sweetness relative to histidine alone.

Interestingly, none of the bitter compounds tested evoked taste qualities other than sweetness in addition to bitterness; that is, sourness, saltiness, and umami taste were not reported. Bitterness is mediated by bitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs) expressed in bitter taste cells, whereas sweetness is mediated by sweet taste receptor T1R2/T1R3 expressed in sweet taste cells. Although these receptor mechanisms are distinct at the cellular and molecular levels, previous studies have shown that bitterness and sweetness can be related in the perceptual profiles of certain compounds. For example, sucrose is a prototypical sweet substance, whereas sucrose octaacetate, in which 8 acetate groups are introduced, is strongly bitter (Bachmanov et al. 2001). In addition, many sweeteners other than sucrose, fructose, and glucose are known to elicit bitterness in addition to sweetness (Horne et al. 2002; Riera et al. 2007; Winnig et al. 2008). Some artificial sweeteners also activate bitter receptors, thereby producing unexpected bitter sensations (Meyerhof et al. 2010; Hellfritsch et al. 2012). For example, saccharin and acesulfame K activate TAS2R31 (formerly T2R44) and TAS2R43 (Kuhn et al. 2004; Rodgers et al. 2006; Behrens et al. 2017), whereas cyclamate activates TAS2R1 and TAS2R38 (Meyerhof et al. 2010). Amino acids such as valine, leucine, isoleucine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine taste bitter in their L-forms but taste sweet in their D-forms (Kawai et al. 2012). In addition, L-tryptophan activates TAS2R4, and L-phenylalanine activates TAS2R1 (Kohl et al. 2013). Collectively, these observations suggest that bitterness and sweetness can be linked in the taste profiles of some compounds.

The phenomenon of “bitterness elicited by sweet substances” described above and the “shift in the bitterness–sweetness balance after preceding stimulation with bitter compounds” observed in the present study both suggest a possible relationship between bitterness and sweetness in taste perception. However, the mechanism underlying the shift observed here remains unclear. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, and further studies are needed to determine how prior bitter stimulation influences the qualitative balance between bitterness and sweetness. Because the present study was not designed to identify the underlying mechanism, any interpretation regarding receptor-level or central perceptual processes remains tentative.

Changes in histidine taste quality induced by pre-application of certain bitter compounds

The major finding of this study is that preceding stimulation with certain bitter compounds systematically alters the taste-quality balance elicited by subsequent histidine stimulation. Notably, after exposure to creatinine, the bitterness-to-sweetness ratio of histidine shifted dramatically from approximately 7:3 to 3:7, resulting in a perceptual transformation from a “bitter amino acid” to a “sweet amino acid.” This shift is highly distinctive because it reflects a change in the dominant taste category within the evaluated balance of taste qualities.

Preceding stimulation with certain other bitter amino acids (isoleucine, tryptophan, phenylalanine) and with creatine also shifted the bitterness-to-sweetness ratio of histidine, but to a lesser extent than creatinine; in these cases, the ratio reached approximately 5:5. This suggests that different bitter compounds differ in their potency to alter the taste-quality balance of histidine, possibly reflecting differences in the activation patterns of bitter receptors and the response profiles of bitter taste cells. In contrast, other bitter substances such as histidine, valine, leucine, caffeine, and carnosine did not affect the taste-quality balance of histidine, demonstrating that not all bitter-tasting compounds trigger taste modulation.

The taste-modifying action of creatinine was characterized from 3 aspects: stimulus interval, coexistence of NaCl, and pH dependence. First, when the interval between creatinine exposure and histidine presentation was increased, the shift in taste-quality balance gradually diminished and almost disappeared beyond a certain time point (∼300 s). This suggests that creatinine induces a transiently altered state in taste processing, with limited duration. Second, addition of NaCl to the histidine solution enhanced creatinine-induced taste modulation. Indeed, NaCl has been reported to suppress bitterness (Breslin and Beauchamp 1995, 1997; Keast et al. 2001, 2004; Kondoh et al. 2026) and enhance sweetness (Hamajima 1969; Breslin and Beauchamp 1997; Uchida et al. 2013) in humans. A plausible interpretation is that NaCl alters the responsiveness of sweet/bitter taste receptors or taste cells, or their downstream signaling pathways, thereby amplifying the shift in taste-quality balance. Third, changing the pH of the preceding solution (valine, creatinine, or phosphate) did not affect the taste modulation of histidine. Thus, taste modulation of histidine cannot be attributed solely to simple pH changes in the oral cavity caused by the preceding solution; rather, more specific mechanisms are likely involved, such as receptor activation and altered intracellular signaling in taste cells.

This issue should be distinguished from the classic psychophysical finding that, in simultaneous mixtures of bitter and sweet compounds, the perceived intensities of bitterness and sweetness can suppress one another (Lawless 1979a). Importantly, Lawless (1979a) examined mixture interactions during concurrent tasting and showed perceptual mutual suppression in bitter-sweet mixtures, but did not test sequential effects of prior stimulation or provide direct physiological evidence for underlying neural mechanisms. The phenomenon observed in the present study is therefore not equivalent to the mutual suppression reported for binary bitter-sweet mixtures. Here, preceding exposure to a certain bitter compound (creatinine) was followed by a change in the taste-quality profile of histidine itself, specifically a decrease in bitterness scores and an increase in sweetness scores. Thus, rather than reflecting reciprocal suppression between bitter and sweet components presented at the same time, my results suggest that prior stimulation with certain bitter compounds altered the subsequent perceptual balance in the taste-quality profile of histidine itself.

Bitter taste receptors TAS2Rs comprise many subtypes and are known to respond to multiple structurally diverse bitter compounds (Behrens and Meyerhof 2013). A single bitter compound can activate multiple TAS2R subtypes, and conversely, some TAS2Rs (e.g. TAS2R10, TAS2R14, TAS2R46) are broadly tuned and respond to multiple bitter chemicals (Behrens et al. 2004; Meyerhof et al. 2010; Nowak et al. 2018; Behrens and Schaefer 2025). Therefore, it is plausible that creatinine, other bitter amino acids, and caffeine each activate different combinations of TAS2R subtypes with different efficacies. Although information on which TAS2R subtypes are activated by histidine and creatinine is currently lacking, my finding that only creatinine and a subset of bitter amino acids modified the taste quality of histidine suggests that activation or suppression of specific TAS2R subtypes may induce a shift in the relative contributions of bitterness and sweetness in responses to subsequent stimuli.

Other studies on sweetness also support the idea that interactions at the level of bitter receptors can lead to changes in perceived taste. Choi et al. (2024) reported that suppression of bitter receptor activity was observed only for monosaccharides and not for sucrose, erythritol, or aspartame. This suggests that monosaccharides may directly interact with bitter receptors and act as antagonists or negative allosteric modulators. In addition, when cyclamate and saccharin are mixed, cyclamate works as an antagonist at bitter receptors, thereby reducing the bitterness of saccharin and consequently enhancing sweetness (Behrens et al. 2017). The combination of acesulfame K and fructose exhibits mutual enhancement at both the receptor and perceptual levels (Choi et al. 2024). Furthermore, in binary mixtures of acesulfame K or saccharin with carbohydrate sweeteners, the sweetness intensity increases nonlinearly with the ratio of the carbohydrate sweetener, reaching a maximum at ≥50%, while bitterness is markedly suppressed (Choi et al. 2024). Based on these results, Behrens et al. (2017) concluded that the synergistic effect observed with saccharin and cyclamate is more likely due to inverse modulation at bitter receptors than to allosteric modulation at sweet receptors.

These previous findings share conceptual similarities with the phenomenon observed in the present study, namely a shift in perceived taste-quality balance following preceding bitter stimulation. In both cases, changes in the state of bitter receptors (activation followed by inactivation, adaptation, or antagonism) may dynamically shift the bitterness–sweetness balance. A distinctive feature of this study is that it used temporally separated stimuli rather than mixtures. It is therefore plausible that time-dependent changes in receptor occupancy and taste cell responsiveness underlie the altered taste quality of subsequent stimuli such as histidine and valine.

Limitations and future research

In this study, I demonstrated by sensory evaluation that the taste quality of L-histidine can shift from bitterness toward sweetness after preceding stimulation with certain bitter compounds. However, several limitations must be acknowledged.

First, my findings are based on subjective reports from sensory evaluation in healthy young female university students and do not fully control for individual differences in taste sensitivity, genetic background, hormonal status, and dietary experience. In particular, because only female participants were included in this study, the generalizability of our findings to males or mixed-sex populations remains unclear. The sample size was also limited, constraining statistical power and broader applicability of the results. Therefore, it remains to be determined whether the taste changes observed here are reproducible in larger and more diverse populations, including both sexes, different age groups, and cultural backgrounds.

Second, this study focused on characterizing taste modulation at the level of human perception and did not directly address mechanisms at the molecular and cellular levels. I did not identify which specific subtypes of bitter taste receptors are involved, nor did I determine whether preceding stimulation alters receptor sensitivity or signal transduction cascades. Moreover, the contribution of central processes, including gustatory centers in the medulla, thalamus, and cortex, remains unexplored.

Third, this study primarily evaluated the taste of single compounds in aqueous solutions; however, real food matrices contain proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and various other compounds, and factors such as viscosity, temperature, and odor also influence taste. Careful evaluation is therefore needed to determine to what extent the taste changes in histidine observed here occur and are perceptible in real foods such as broths and processed products. Additionally, my assessment of temporal aspects was limited to varying the interval between stimuli; I did not perform detailed analyses of time–intensity curves or aftertaste.

Fourth, the range of bitter compounds and concentrations tested was limited. Although I examined 10 bitter substances, bitter compounds exhibit large diversity in chemical structure and receptor profiles, and many classes such as peptide-based, alkaloid, and polyphenol remain unexamined. Furthermore, each compound was tested only at a single concentration; I did not systematically assess concentration–response relationships for taste modulation, which will be crucial for a more precise understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

Fifth, I did not measure absolute taste intensities but instead assessed the relative contributions of taste qualities using a fixed-total point-allocation method. Consequently, when the bitterness-to-sweetness ratio changed, I could not determine whether the absolute intensity of bitterness decreased, the absolute intensity of sweetness increased, or both occurred simultaneously. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting changes in the relative balance of perceived taste qualities rather than isolated changes in absolute intensity. Although future studies using highly resolved scales such as the gLMS or VAS would help dissociate these possibilities, the current study was designed specifically to evaluate relative taste-quality balance.

Lastly, some amino acids such as cysteine, methionine, lysine, and arginine have been reported to exhibit both bitter and sweet taste qualities. However, because the number of standard amino acids is large, I was unable to examine the effects of all of them in the present study. Therefore, based on the current data, I cannot conclude that these amino acids also induce a histidine-like taste-modifying effect.

Despite these limitations, the present findings provide evidence that the perceived quality balance of histidine can shift depending on the immediately preceding taste stimulus. Future studies integrating broader sensory evaluation with mechanistic experiments will be important for determining the generality and underlying basis of this effect.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a novel form of taste modulation, in which prior exposure to specific bitter compounds transiently alters the perceived taste-quality balance of L-histidine from predominantly bitter to predominantly sweet. Sensory evaluation revealed that L-histidine alone elicited bitterness with weak sweetness, whereas creatinine elicited predominantly bitterness. However, when creatinine was presented as a preceding stimulus, the subsequent taste-quality balance of L-histidine shifted markedly toward sweetness, representing a qualitative change in the dominant taste category within the relative balance of taste qualities evaluated in this study. This effect was also observed after stimulation with several other bitter substances, including isoleucine, tryptophan, phenylalanine, and creatine, but not with histidine itself, valine, leucine, caffeine, or carnosine, indicating that the modulation depends on the specific bitter compound rather than general bitterness per se. The shift in taste quality was reversible and diminished over time, showing that the effect is transient. Furthermore, addition of 0.1% NaCl further shifted the taste-quality balance of histidine after pre-stimulation with creatinine. The taste quality of histidine alone was highly sensitive to pH: bitterness-to-sweetness balance varied with pH, and the relative contribution of sweetness increased under alkaline conditions. However, altering the pH of the pre-stimulation solution between 5.0 and 8.0 did not affect the taste modulation of L-histidine. These findings identify a unique and novel taste modulation phenomenon and suggest complex, compound-specific interactions at the receptor and/or neural level, which warrant future mechanistic investigation.
