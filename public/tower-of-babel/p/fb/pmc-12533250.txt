Analysis of Expansin genes in Cucurbitaceae and identification of candidate genes for stress resistance
Authors: Saike Qi, Shan Liu, Yingying Wen, Xiaodi Zhang, Hongmei Mu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExpansin (EXP) genes are critical for plant growth and abiotic stress adaptation, yet their evolutionary and functional dynamics in Cucurbitaceae remain poorly characterized. Here, we systematically identified 136 EXP genes across four Cucurbitaceae species (Cucurbita argyrosperma, Cucurbita pepo, Citrullus lanatus, Lagenaria siceraria) and the transcriptome of ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria). Phylogenetic analysis classified these genes into four subfamilies (EXPA, EXPB, EXLA, EXLB), with EXPA predominating and EXPB notably absent in ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria). Physicochemical profiling revealed EXP proteins as hydrophilic (84.66%) with isoelectric points (pI 8–10), indicative of basic properties. Cis-regulatory element analysis identified ABRE and TCA-elements as highly enriched, linking EXP genes to hormone signaling, cell wall integrity, and stress responses. Protein interaction networks highlighted functional associations with cell wall-modifying enzymes (e.g., XTHs, GH9) and developmental regulators. Tissue-specific expression profiling in ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) showed predominant EXP expression in leaves, with minimal activity in flowers. Under cold, drought, and salt stresses, four genes (LSEXPA2, LSEXPA3, LSEXPA7, LSEXPA13) exhibited marked upregulation, suggesting roles in stress tolerance. This study elucidates the evolutionary diversification of EXP genes in Cucurbitaceae and identifies candidate targets for enhancing stress resilience in L. siceraria breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18901-2.Keywords: Expansin genes, Cucurbitaceae, Abiotic stress, PhylogenySubject terms: Plant stress responses, Gene regulatory networks

Introduction

The Cucurbitaceae family, ranked as second most significant economic crop after the Solanaceae in China, with an annual production of 70 million tons as reported by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) in 2022 1,2, encompassed around 115 genera and over 1,000 species, including prominent economic crops such as Citrullus lanatus, Cucurbita pepo, Cucurbita argyrosperma, and Cucumis sativus3,4. Fruits of Cucurbitaceae plants serve multiple purposes beyond being food sources, as they are rich in essential nutrients, including vitamins, minerals, and dietary fiber5. Additionally, the mature fruits of Lagenaria siceraria were crafted into household utensils, musical instruments, and decorative items6. The cultivation of Lagenaria siceraria served as a vital economic pillar industry in the Liaocheng region of Shandong, China, with annual marketing turnover reaching 1.9 billion RMB yuan. During the Gourd Festival held in 2024 in Liaocheng Shandong, sales of Carving gourd (L. siceraria) over four days amounted to 290 million RMB yuan. However, Cucurbitaceae plants, including L. siceraria, were highly susceptible to abiotic stresses such as extreme temperatures, drought, and soil salinization during their growth. Research indicates that Expansin (EXP) genes played a critical role in maintaining plant morphology under stress conditions by regulating cell wall structure, thereby contributing significantly to plant growth, development, and stress tolerance7–9. These results suggest that EXP gene may play a certain role in the growth and development of cucurbitaceae plants and coping with adverse stress. However, there are still gaps in the research of EXP gene in cucurbitaceae plants, and its function needs further study.

EXP which was a non-enzymatic protein first identified by Cosgrove in studies of cucumber cell wall extension10functions by disrupting the hydrogen bonds between cellulose and hemicellulose11which regulates the degree of relaxation among cell wall components, thereby enhancing cell wall flexibility12. The mechanism of cell wall relaxation induced by EXP proteins facilitates turgor-driven cell expansion, promoting plant growth and development13including plant height14root elongation15and fruit color change and ripening16,17while also having an indispensable role in plant stress responses under adverse conditions18,19. The EXP gene family, which was a conserved multi-gene family, had been identified and characterized in various plant species, with the advancement of whole-genome and transcriptome sequencing20. Comprehensive identification and functional analysis of the EXP gene family had been conducted in diverse plants, including ginkgo9pomegranate21soybean22tobacco23and melon24.

In the study, genome-wide data from four Cucurbitaceae species, which are Cucurbita. argyrosperma, Cucurbita pepo, Citrullus lanatus, Lagenaria Siceraria, along with transcriptome data from the primary landraces ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria), were utilized to identify and analyze the EXP gene family, and its evolutionary relationships and functional roles were analyzed. Our study established a foundation for future research on the molecular regulatory mechanisms of the EXP gene. Additionally, candidate EXP genes associated with enhancing stress resistance in Liaocheng ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) were identified.

Results

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of the EXP gene family

Identification and physicochemical properties of EXP genes

Based on BLAST and Hidden Markov Models (HMM) screening, this study identified 50 EXP genes in A. thaliana, 39 EXP genes in C. argyrosperma, 22 EXP genes in C. pepo, 33 EXP genes in C. lanatus, 26 EXP genes in L. siceraria, and 16 EXP genes in the ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria). The analysis result revealed that the lengths of the 186 identified EXP proteins were predominantly concentrated between 200 and 300 aa (Fig. 1). The molecular weight of EXP proteins from ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) ranged from 20 to 30 kDa, while those from other species were primarily within the range of 25 to 35 kDa. The majority of the EXP proteins exhibited isoelectric points (pI) concentrated between 8 and 10, categorizing them as basic proteins. Notably, 84.66% of the EXP proteins demonstrated hydrophilic properties.

Basic physicochemical properties of EXP proteins. The different letters indicate significant differences at the p < 0.05.

Gene structure and phylogenetic analysis of EXP gene family

Phylogenetic analysis indicated that the 136 EXP genes could be classified into four subfamilies: α-expansin (EXPA), β-expansin (EXPB), expansin-like A (EXLA), and expansin-like B (EXLB) (Fig. 2a). The EXPA subfamily represented the largest proportion across all species (Fig. 2b). Notably, there were none EXPB subfamily identified in ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria). Within the EXLA subfamily, C. lanatus exhibited the highest representation at 27.27%, whereas the EXLB subfamily comprised only 9.09% in C. pepo.

Phylogenetic tree of the EXP gene family.

Utilizing MEME for the identification of conserved motifs across species, while parameter was set to 10, it was revealed that common conserved motifs exist among the EXP genes (Supplementary Fig. 1), such as motifs 1, 2, 4, and 6 in A. thaliana corresponding to motifs 2, 4, 5, and 7 in C. argyrosperma, motifs 1, 2, 4, 8, and 10 in C. pepo, motifs 1, 2, 5, 10, and 9 in C. lanatus, motifs 5, 3, 4, and 8 in L. siceraria, and motifs 4, 1, 7, 9, and 5 in ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria).

According to our analysis, the arrangement of conserved motifs within the same subfamily was consistent, while differences were observed between different subfamilies. All species contained the conserved domains Expansin-A (PLN00193, PLN00050) and Expansin-like B1 (PLN03023). Addition to these, other species except ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) also harbored conserved domains such as DPBB-EXPB-N and Expansin-C. These findings indicate a degree of structural conservation within the EXP genes.

Evolution of the EXP gene family

Chromosomal localization and gene duplication of EXP gene family

Chromosomal localization analysis demonstrated that EXP genes in A. thaliana were distributed across five chromosomes, exhibiting clustered arrangements (Fig. 3a). In contrast, the distribution of EXP genes among Cucurbitaceae species was uneven, with a predominant localization at the ends of chromosomes (Fig. 3b-f).

Intraspecific collinearity analysis of EXP gene family. (a) The localization of the EXP gene in the chromosome of A. thaliana, (b) localization of the EXP gene in the chromosome of C. argyrosperma, (c) the localization of the EXP gene in the chromosome of C. pepo, (d) the localization of the EXP gene in the chromosome of C. lanatus, (e) is the localization of the EXP gene in the chromosome of L. siceraria, and (f) is the localization of the EXP gene in the chromosome of ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria).

Analysis of gene duplication types revealed that whole-genome duplication (WGD) predominated in six species, accounting for 34.00% of EXP genes in A. thaliana, 89.74% in C. argyrosperma, 68.18% in C. pepo, 48.49% in C. lanatus, 38.46% in L. siceraria, and 68.75% in ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria). Proximal duplications (PD), dispersed segmental duplications (DSD), and tandem duplications (TD) exhibited significant variation among different species. For example, in C. argyrosperma, DSD and PD types accounted for only 10.26%. In C. pepo, DSD (18.18%) and PD duplications (13.64%) were relatively less frequent. In C. lanatus, DSD and TD were more balanced, occurring at rates of 27.27% and 24.24%, respectively. Notably, only one PD event was observed in L. siceraria. These findings indicate that WGD has been the primary force shaping the evolution of EXP genes.

Collinearity analysis of the cucurbitaceae EXP gene family

Collinearity analysis revealed significant genomic conservation and variation patterns (Supplementary Fig. 2). Pairwise comparisons of collinearity relationships among different species identified 326 gene pairs that exhibited high levels of collinearity. Between L. siceraria and ‘Yayao’ L. siceraria, and between C. lanatus and L. siceraria, there were 52 and 46 EXP gene pairs that retained similar genomic positions, showing strong collinearity. Despite the overall high conservation between C. pepo, C. argyrosperma, and C. lanatus, specific EXP gene regions showed considerable structural variations, reflecting distinct genomic rearrangement events these species may have undergone during evolution (Supplementary Table 1). The collinearity between C. lanatus and L. siceraria was well-maintained, suggesting that these two species have experienced fewer genomic rearrangements in their recent evolutionary history. In order to better understand the evolutionary relationship of EXP genes in Cucurbitaceae, the Ka/Ks ratio of co-linear genes was analyzed (Supplementary Table 2). It was found that 95.3% of the gene pairs had Ka/Ks < 0.5, indicating that EXP gene family was subjected to strong functional constraints in evolution and was conserved in core functions.

Functional prediction of the EXP gene family

Analysis of cis-acting elements in EXP gene promoters

Cis-acting element prediction of the 2000 bp upstream promoter sequences identified 69 distinct cis-acting elements (Supplementary Table 3), which can be categorized into five classes: Transcriptional Elements (10), Hormone-Responsive Elements (12), Developmental and Tissue-Specific Elements (10), Stress and Defense-Related Elements (6), and Light-Responsive Elements (31).

Analysis revealed that among the Hormone-Responsive Elements, ABRE and TCA-element were particularly prevalent (Fig. 4). This suggested that EXP genes could respond to variations in abscisic acid (ABA) and salicylic acid (SA), indicating their potential importance in stress responses. Within the Stress and Defense-Related Elements, ARE, MBS, LTR, and TC-rich repeats were notably abundant across all six species. Functional annotations indicated that MBS responded to drought stress, while LTR responded to low-temperature conditions. These findings reflected the adaptive mechanisms of EXP genes under abiotic stresses, particularly drought and cold. Light-Responsive Elements, including G-box, Box-4, I-box, and AE-box, were significantly present in all six species, suggesting that EXP genes were highly responsive to light regulation. This conservation was consistent with the core functions of EXP genes in cell expansion and growth regulation. By responding to light and photoperiod-related environmental factors, EXP genes helped maintain fundamental growth mechanisms.

Cis-acting element analysis of promoter region of EXP gene family.

GO enrichment analysis of the EXP gene family

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that the functional roles of EXP genes were primarily concentrated in Biological Processes (BP) and Cellular Components (CC) (Fig. 5). The BP enrichment analysis revealed that EXP genes were predominantly involved in anatomical structure development (100), anatomical structure formation involved in morphogenesis (80), anatomical structure morphogenesis (100), and developmental processes (100). These results underscored the critical roles of EXP genes in plant growth and development. In terms of CC, EXP genes were enriched in cell periphery (162), cell wall (162), external encapsulating structure (162), and membrane (143). The enrichment of these terms suggested that EXP genes played vital roles in the structural and functional maintenance of the cell wall and extracellular membrane. The enriched GO terms reflected the core roles of EXP genes in plant growth and development, particularly in cell expansion and tissue formation.

Prediction of protein interaction networks

The protein-protein interaction network analysis unveiled that a total of 110 EXP proteins were engaged in a functional interaction network (Fig. 6). Interacting partners within this network could primarily be classified into two major categories: one comprising proteins associated with cell wall architecture, such as xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolases (XTHs), cellulases (CELs), and glycoside hydrolase 9 (GH9); the other encompassing proteins involved in plant growth and development. The prominence of XTHs, CELs, and GH9 in the interaction network underscored their probable pivotal roles in modulating EXP protein function, particularly in terms of regulating cell wall structure and mechanical attributes. This modulation likely occurred via influencing cell wall plasticity through pathways pertinent to lignin biosynthesis and remodeling. Additionally, the presence of multiple transcription factors within the second category of interacting proteins highlighted their role in orchestrating plant growth, development, and responses to environmental stresses. Collectively, these interactions suggested that EXP proteins played a crucial role in mediating cell expansion and division, thereby impacting the establishment of plant morphology and functionality.

Transcriptional co-expression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3) further validated these interactions, revealing significant functional coordination between EXP proteins and key cell wall modifiers. Specifically, 38.2% of EXP-XTHs and GH9 pairs showed strong co-expression (p < 0.05; |r|>0.7 in 42.1% cases), with XTHs and GH9 members exhibiting predominant positive correlations.

Expression patterns of LSEXP genes in different tissues

The results from Fig. 7 indicate that LSEXPA11 was found to be the most highly expressed In the roots, and LSEXPA5 and LSEXPA13 also showing relatively high expression levels, while LSEXPA3 and LSEXPA4 were expressed at lower levels, indicating their relative suppression in root tissue. In the stems, the expression pattern shifted, with LSEXPA3 reaching the highest expression, followed by relatively high levels of LSEXPA4 and LSEXPA7, whereas LSEXPA1 and LSEXPA2 exhibited comparatively lower expression levels. The expression levels of LSEXPA2 and LSEXPA3 in the leaves were found to be extremely high, while LSEXPA12 and LSEXPA11 also exhibited relatively high expression levels, which may be associated with the mechanisms of leaf expansion and growth, highlighting the distinct physiological characteristics of the leaves compared to the roots and stems. In contrast, the expression levels of EXP genes in the flowers were lower than in other tissues, suggesting that the role of EXP genes in flowers differs significantly from their functions in other tissues.

Expression patterns of LSEXP genes under abiotic stress

As shown in Fig. 8 results, the expression of several EXP genes was significantly upregulated under cold stress. The expression levels of LSEXPA3 and LSEXPA7 reached their peak following exposure to both 10 °C and 4 °C, while LSEXPA2, LSEXPA4, LSEXPA8, and LSEXPA13 also exhibited considerable upregulation. In contrast, the expression of LSEXPA1, LSEXPA10, LSEXPA12, and LSEXLB1 progressively declined under low-temperature conditions. Under drought stress, LSEXPA2, LSEXPA3, and LSEXPA7 maintained elevated expression levels, particularly during the early stages of treatment, whereas LSEXPA13 and LSEXLA1 also showed notable upregulation. Conversely, LSEXPA10 and LSEXPA12 exhibited relatively low expression following drought treatment. Under salt stress, LSEXPA2, LSEXPA3, and LSEXPA7 again demonstrated high and stable expression throughout the treatment period. Furthermore, LSEXPA4, LSEXPA8, LSEXPA13, and LSEXLB2 were expressed at relatively higher levels under 25 mmol/L and 50 mmol/L salt treatments. In contrast, the expression of LSEXPA1, LSEXPA10, and LSEXLB1 was markedly downregulated.

Discussion

EXP were regulated in their function through auxin activity without the requirement for chemical energy13,25which was a class of cell wall-expanding proteins that was recognized to act irreversibly and be influenced by the growth cycle. ,To further explore the role that EXP exerted in the growth and development of Cucurbitaceae plants, we analyzed using bioinformatics tools, which led to the identification and characterization of the EXP genes family in Arabidopsis thaliana and various Cucurbitaceae species. As a result of this comprehensive analysis, 136 members of the EXP genes family were identified. The number of EXP genes identified in Cucurbitaceae species was comparable to that found in dicotyledonous plants such as Cucumis melo (33)3 and Corchorus olitorius (26)26. EXP genes in Cucurbitaceae clustered with Arabidopsis EXP genes across different subfamilies, indicating a degree of conservatism in their evolution. The proportional relationships of subfamilies were consistent with those observed in other dicotyledonous plant families, such as Arabidopsis15 and strawberry27.

Systematic evolution analysis of cucurbit EXP gene family

To clarify the evolutionary relationships of EXP genes, collinearity analysis and gene duplication type analysis were conducted. The proportional relationships of EXP subfamilies showed similarities to the evolutionary relationships among Cucurbitaceae species. According to Chen’s study on the evolution of the Benincasa hispida within the Cucurbitaceae family, a common ancestor was found between C. argyrosperma and C. pepo, both belonging to the genus Cucurbita1. However, their divergence occurred later than that of the common ancestor of C. lanatus and L. siceraria1. Notably, C. pepo and C. argyrosperma exhibited significant genomic structural variations, potentially linked to their specific evolutionary paths28. Chen’s analysis of the phenylalanine ammonia-lyase family indicated substantial genomic loss during the evolution of Cucurbitaceae1. This is consistent with our results, which showed the absence of the EXPB family in ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) during the phylogenetic analysis. The transcriptome data of PRJNA530553 is derived from the whole flower samples of ‘yayao’ (L. siceraria). During the identification of the LSEXP gene family, it was found that the EXPB family was not included, which differs from the identification results of genome white-flowered L. siceraria. L. siceraria has a long history of cultivation, and its gene sources are complex29. Years of interspecific and intraspecific hybridization have led to differences in its genes29. Due to the differences in germplasm resources between white (L. siceraria) and ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria), the EXP gene probably also different. However, this difference does not exclude the possibility of tissue-specific expression of specific genes. During transcriptome sequencing of whole flower samples from ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria), the required sample size was little, meaning pollen or pollen tube tissues constituted a relatively low proportion. According to research by Liu30. the EXPB subfamily is highly and specifically expressed in plant pollen. Consequently, genes from the EXPB subfamily were not identified in the PRJNA530553 dataset. Meanwhile, such differences also maybe attributable to technical limitations encountered during the filtering and assembly processes of the transcriptome data. To investigate further, we analyzed leaf transcriptome data from ‘Yayao’31. Notably, we identified three EXPB subfamily gene sequences in the leaf data, but the expression levels of the three genes were extremely low.

Victoria H’s research on the evolution of EXP genes in A. trichopoda highlighted the significant role of WGD in the growth and diversification of gene families32. Chen also noted that EXP genes in wheat experienced chromosomal rearrangements during evolution, facilitating the expansion of the EXP gene family33. In this study, gene duplication type analysis revealed that WGD was the primary mode of gene duplication in C. argyrosperma and C. pepo, significantly influencing collinearity patterns. In these two species, WGD events led to the amplification and rearrangement of multiple EXP genes, thereby affecting gene collinearity33. This study further confirms that in C. argyrosperma and C. pepo, WGD events not only led to the amplification of multiple EXP genes but also triggered significant genome rearrangements, including chromosomal position shifts and asymmetric retention of homologous gene pairs34. The local inversions and translocations following fragment duplications altered the co-linearity patterns of genes, potentially driving the functional differentiation of EXP genes. This WGD-driven rearrangement pattern provides a structural foundation for the adaptive evolution of EXP gene families in Cucurbitaceae crops. However, based on the Ka/Ks values of syntenic gene pairs, it was found that the EXP genes in cucurbits have undergone strong purifying selection during evolution. This helps maintain their function and indicates they have not undergone significant divergence over the course of evolution35.

EXP gene tissue-specific expression

The expression patterns of EXP genes differed significantly between developmental stages and among tissues. The expression patterns of EXP genes varied significantly among different tissues. According to Li’s research, AcEXPA1 can respond to salt stress and promote root growth in Axonopus compressus36. In this study, LSEXPA11 and LSEXPA13 were found to be highly expressed in the roots, indicating that expansin genes play a role in promoting root growth. The significant expression of LSEXPA3, LSEXPA4, and LSEXPA13 in the stems highlights the supportive and structural stabilizing functions of EXP genes during plant growth. According to Liu’s research, EXP genes played a crucial role in reproductive development30. Liu’s research also revealed that AtEXPB5 and AtEXPA4 in Arabidopsis were strongly expressed in mature pollen grains and pollen tubes, while their expression levels were extremely low in tissues like leaves and stems37. In Brassica, EXPB genes were highly expressed during the inflorescence and three-nucleate pollen stages, but their expression was very low in nutritional tissues such as roots, stems, and leaves30. This suggests that EXP genes might play a unique role in reproductive processes, particularly in the development of pollen tubes and pollen grains30. The above research indicates reinforce the regulatory role of EXP in plant growth and development19,38emphasizing their significance in plant morphological formation and functional regulation14.

EXP gene function analysis

Functional prediction analyses indicated that EXP genes play a crucial role in cell wall extension and structural remodeling. Research has shown that plants are often regulated by hormones during growth and development, as well as when facing adverse stresses. The cis-acting elements located in the promoter are key factors in gene regulation39. Zhang, et al. identified various cis-acting elements related to hormones and stress responses40such as ABRE, ARE, TGA, P-box, and TATC-box, in the promoter of the EXP gene in sweet potato, which is similar to the findings of this study. Elements like ABRE and TCA can respond to signals such as ABA and SA, thereby regulating genes under adverse conditions. The study on the EXP gene in sweet potato also confirmed that the expression level of the IbEXP gene was induced by ABA, SA, and jasmonic acid (JA)40. Additionally, we identified binding sites for transcription factors such as MYB and AP2 in the EXP gene promoter. These cis-acting elements play a crucial role in abiotic stress responses41–43indicating that EXP genes can respond to related stress regulatory networks. Li’s analysis of cis-acting elements in alfalfa EXP genes revealed that key cis-acting elements in the MsEXPs gene family are primarily associated with light and abiotic stress responses44. This aligns with the findings of the current study, revealing the diversity and conservatism of cis-acting elements in EXP genes, which provides important clues for understanding the growth regulatory mechanisms of Cucurbitaceae under varying environmental conditions. Lior Artzi’s analysis of EXP genes family identified CclEXL1 as having a degrading effect on cellulose45. Additionally, our protein interaction network results indicate that EXP proteins closely interact with proteins such as XTH, suggesting that EXP may play a central role in regulating cell wall structure8. This synergy corroborates their joint role in cell wall remodeling pathways (p < 0.05), providing orthogonal evidence that EXP proteins regulate cell wall plasticity through collaborative control of XTHs and GH9, CELs.

Stress resistance function of EXP gene

Prince Marowa’s research demonstrated that expansin genes can alleviate abiotic stress by regulating the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the cell wall46–48. Specifically, EXP genes have been shown to mitigate the effects of drought47,48cold18and salt stress19. This conclusion was further supported by studies conducted by Synan Abuqamar, Zörb, and Bauerfeind49–51. In this study, an abiotic stress experiment was performed on ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria), and the results from qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression of certain genes, such as LSEXPA2, LSEXPA3, LSEXPA7, and LSEXPA13, was influenced by stress and showed high responsiveness, indicating their potential functions for stress resistance. Based on the results of co-expression analysis (Supplementary figure.3), the LSEXP3 gene shows a positive correlation with stress-related genes such as PSMD2 and HSP90, which can help coordinate protein homeostasis under stress52. LSEXPA2 is significantly co-expressed with cell wall modification factors XTH1 and GH9, suggesting its role in cell wall remodeling during stress adaptation53. The EXP protein may relax the cell wall by disrupting the hydrogen bonds between cellulose and hemicellulose, working in tandem with XTH and GH9 to enhance cell wall extensibility54. This allows for osmotic regulation or adjustment of growth patterns under stress to adapt to environmental pressures55. Maintaining cell wall plasticity helps reduce cell wall rigidity, thereby decreasing the growth of ROS induced by mechanical stress56. Additionally, the strong positive correlation between LSEXPA3 and HSP90 may also play a significant role. As an important molecular chaperone, HSP90 can stabilize various stress-responsive proteins, including key antioxidant enzymes and their regulatory factors57. Therefore, the upregulation of genes such as LSEXPA3 may indirectly enhance the cell’s antioxidant defense system by stabilizing HSP90 and its protein network, promoting ROS clearance, and thus reducing oxidative damage. However, Kwon’s research found that overexpression of some EXP genes could also increased the plant’s sensitivity to stress58.

Transcriptome analysis of leaves under drought stress revealed that the expression levels of LSEXPA2, LSEXPA3, LSEXPA7, and LSEXPA13 were significantly upregulated. In contrast, the plant height, leaf dry and fresh weight, and photosynthetic rate of ‘Yayao’ L. siceraria decreased compared to the control group, while antioxidant enzymes and osmotic regulation substances increased. This suggests that the EXP gene family may enhance the cell’s antioxidant defense system, thereby reducing the damage caused by stress29. Based on the expression patterns of the EXP genes, Candidate genes were selected based on a fold change > 3 under all three stress treatments with statistically significant differential expression (p < 0.05)40. LSEXPA2, LSEXPA3, LSEXPA7, and LSEXPA13 were proposed as key genes for the future improvement of ‘Yayao’ L. siceraria’s stress resistance. These candidate genes not only had the potential to enhance the stress tolerance of L. siceraria but could also played a significant role in future genetic improvement programs.

Conclusion

A total of 136 EXP genes were identified, with C. argyrosperma, C. pepo, C. lanatus, L. siceraria, and ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) contributing 39, 22, 33, 26, and 16 genes, respectively. The physicochemical properties of the identified EXP proteins were consistent, characterized as basic hydrophilic proteins with lengths of 200–300 amino acids and molecular weights ranging from 25 to 35 kDa. Phylogenetic analysis revealed four subfamilies, with EXPA being the most predominant. Collinearity analysis indicated that WGD is the primary driving force behind the evolution of EXP genes and a significant contributor to interspecies gene variation. Predictions of cis-acting elements suggested that EXP genes possessed functions responsive to abiotic stress. Functional enrichment and protein interaction predictions indicated that EXP proteins interact with pathways involved in the synthesis of lignin, cellulose, and β-glucanase, collaboratively regulating the plasticity of cell wall structure. Additionally, interactions between EXP proteins and transcription factors modulate mechanisms of plant growth, development, and stress responses. An abiotic stress experiment was conducted on the locally important landraces ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) in Liaocheng. By analyzing the expression patterns of EXP genes, LSEXPA2, LSEXPA3, LSEXPA7, and LSEXPA13 were selected as Candidate Genes for subsequent improvement of ‘Yayao’ L. siceraria’s stress resistance.

Materials and methods

Screening and identification of EXP genes in cucurbitaceae

Sequences from A. thaliana and Cucurbitaceae species, including C. argyrosperma, C. pepo, C. lanatus, L. siceraria, and ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria), were obtained from public databases and the transcriptome data of ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) (PRJNA530553) (Supplementary Table 4). ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) (PRJNA530553) was sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq500 platform, yielding a total of 35,906,462 raw reads from the sample. After removing adapter contamination and quality filtering, 34,133,058 clean reads were obtained, with the GC content of both transcripts and Unigenes being approximately 44%. The base percentage of Q20 (base quality value) is no less than 91.62%, indicating high sequencing quality that meets the analysis requirements. (Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Table 6). A preliminary screening of EXP gene family in Cucurbitaceae was conducted using BLAST against functionally consistent EXP genes from A. thaliana. Secondary screening was performed based on conserved domains I (PF01357) and II (PF03330). The final collection of EXP gene family members for each species was determined based on the results of both screenings, with non-compliant and duplicate sequences being excluded.

Bioinformatics analysis of EXP genes family

The physicochemical properties of EXP proteins were analyzed using ExPASy (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/) and visualized with Origin2024. Conserved domains and motifs of the EXP gene family were predicted using NCBI Batch CD-search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) and MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/). MEGA11 was employed for multiple sequence alignment of EXP proteins, utilizing the Neighbor Joining (NJ) method. Phylogenetic trees were constructed based on gene conservation and multiple sequence alignment results, leading to subfamily classification. TBtools was utilized for chromosomal localization and collinearity analysis of EXP genes, alongside statistical analysis of gene duplication types. At the same time, the non-synonymous substitution rate (Ka) and synonymous substitution rate (Ks) of each gene pair were analyzed according to the Ka/Ks Calculator plug-in. PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was employed to predict cis-acting elements in the promoters of EXP genes. Annotation of EXP genes and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis were conducted using eggnog-mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/). The STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was utilized to predict the protein interaction network of EXP proteins, with visual analysis performed using Cytoscape software. Gene co-expression analysis was performed through WeShengXin59 (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) for data analysis and origin 2024 for data visualization.

Expression profile of EXP genes

Roots, stems, leaves, and flowers of ‘Yayao’ (L. siceraria) cultivated in the experimental field of the College of Agriculture and Biology, Liaocheng University, were harvested and immediately stored at -80 °C to prevent degradation. The seeds were then germinated under controlled conditions in a climate chamber. When the seedlings developed three true leaves, they were subjected to abiotic stress treatments. To ensure uniformity, plants with similar growth vigor were divided into three distinct treatment groups: a low-temperature stress group (10 °C, and 4 °C), a drought stress group (induced with PEG-6000 at concentrations of 10%, and 20%), and a salt stress group (induced with NaCl at concentrations of 25 mmol/L, and 50 mmol/L). The untreated leaves were used as the control (0 h). Leaf samples from the same position were collected 24 h after treatment for subsequent analysis. qRT-PCR was conducted using the Taq SYBR® Green qPCR Premix (Universal) kit to analyze the dynamic expression patterns of LSEXP genes in different organs and under abiotic stress conditions. LSH3 60,61 was selected as the internal control gene to analyze the expression patterns of EXP genes (Supplementary Table 7). The relative expression levels of genes were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. The data was statistically analyzed using Excel and SPSS 27.0 software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, and each treatment was repeated three times, followed by Tukey-Kramer’s multiple test (P < 0.05). Graphs were generated using Origin 2022 software.
