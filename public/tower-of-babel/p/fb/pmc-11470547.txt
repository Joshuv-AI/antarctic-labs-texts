Prevalence, pattern and predictors of clinically important parasites contaminating raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zelalem Animaw, Addisu Melese, Dereje Bedane, Bedemariam Tadesse, Dawit Degarege, Finot Admasu, Mihretu Jegnie, Bottieau Emmanuel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a growing concern regarding food safety, with the potential presence of parasitic pathogens in raw produce globally. Contaminated fruits and vegetables can be sources of various parasitic infections imposing significant health consequences for general population, particularly, vulnerable populations such as pregnant women, young children, and immunocompromised individuals. Therefore, the aim of this meta-analysis is to systematically assess and synthesize the available evidence on the magnitude of parasitic contamination in raw fruits and vegetables obtained from local markets in Ethiopia.MethodsThoroughly search of published and unpublished studies was conducted in the databases of MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, Google Scholar, CINAHL, Cochrane library and Science Direct. PRISMA checklist was strictly followed to select potential studies. Microsoft Excel 2016 sheet template was employed to extract the data. Quality of included studies was assessed by using the Newcastle – Ottawa Scale. STATA software version 14.0 was used to compute the estimated pooled prevalence. Heterogeneity among included studies was assessed using percentage of variance (I2) and P-value.ResultsThirteen studies with a total sample size of 3769 fruits and vegetables were included. The pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination in raw fruits and vegetables with at least one parasite in Ethiopia is 43.38% (95% CI: [36.64, 50.12]). The highest pooled prevalence of contamination with at least one parasite is reported from Oromia region, 51.95% (95% CI: [40.53, 63.37]). The most prevalent parasite is strongyloides stercoralis, followed by Toxocara spp., G. lamblia and A. lumbricoides. The most contaminated vegetables with at least one parasites is spinach followed by cabbage and lettuce. Banana has emerged to be the most frequently contaminated fruit with a contamination prevalence of 38.34% (95% CI: [1.84, 56.83]). Being unwashed fruits and vegetables, placement of the products and literacy status of the sellers are significantly associated with the occurrence of parasitic contamination.ConclusionsThe result of this study showed that contamination of raw fruits and vegetables with clinical important parasites is high. Therefore, adequate public awareness on hygienic harvest, storage, placement and consumption shall be created in order to mitigate such a significant public health concern.Keywords: Raw vegetables, Raw fruits, Parasite, Contamination, Meta-analysis

Background

Consumption of raw fruits and vegetables has various health benefits, as they are rich in nutritionally essential biomolecules such as vitamins, minerals, fiber and antioxidants [1, 2]. Due to this favorable health impact, the demand of raw fruits and vegetables showed significant increment since the last century that entailed enhanced production globally [3]. However, unhygienic production, marketing, storage, and consumption of these raw products imposed significant public health problem because they are prone to microbial contamination [4].

Microbial contamination of raw fruits and vegetables can occur for a number of reasons, including conditions related to harvesting, storage, transportation and marketing; cleanliness of irrigation water; utilization of manure-based fertilizer made from animal fecal matter; exposure to rodents and insects; and the overall agricultural environment hygiene [4, 5]. Furthermore, Parasite transmission in raw vegetables and fruits is influenced by a variety of interconnected factors, including packaging, washing techniques, and the involvement of food handlers. While packaging is necessary for safeguarding and storing food goods, it can also act as a source of contamination. Washing is an important technique for reducing the danger of parasite transmission from raw vegetables. Furthermore, food handlers play an important role in the food safety chain, with a significant impact on parasite dissemination [6, 7]. In view this, protozoa and helminths are the common types of parasites that are related to clinically relevant contamination due to ingestion of raw fruits and vegetables. The problem has been shown up to light as having major global implications for clinical and public health, particularly in case of low-income countries [8, 9].

According to previous global report, the Western Pacific WHO region had the highest incidence rate of parasitic infection (54%), with the most common parasitic agents being A. lumbricoides eggs and Strongyloides stercoralis larvae in vegetables, S. stercoralis larvae and Toxocara spp. eggs in cucurbits, and Trichuris trichiura eggs in fruits [10]. Similar articles further revealed that the highest incidence rates were linked to lower-middle income nations and areas with a tropical rainforest climate. Similarly, a pooled prevalence from 19 research across Nigeria revealed that one-third of samples fruits and vegetables were infected by parasites [11]. Another report from Ghana depicted that 57.5% of studied vegetable samples were infected at least with one parasite [12]. These findings can be used as evidence because they show how severe the issue is in less developed countries, especially in sub-Saharan areas, even though the situation is also concerning in northern Africa [13].

Although the issue of parasitic contamination in raw fruits and vegetables is a crucial public health concern in developing nations, Ethiopia lacks summarized data regarding the problem. However, there are individual studies depicting the magnitude, pattern and factors related to parasitic contamination in raw fruits and vegetables across different part of the country in a fragmented manner.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to depict a comprehensive data regarding the magnitude, pattern and factors related to parasitic contamination in raw fruits and vegetables in Ethiopia context.

Methods

Thoroughly two authors (ZA and AM) in the databases of MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, Google Scholar, CINAHL, Cochrane library and ScienceDirect, conducted search of published and unpublished studies pertaining to parasitic contamination among raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia. Additionally, gray literatures were searched manually from other online sources. Menedley referencing was utilized to list eligible studies. All articles published and/or reported up to 30th July, 2023 were included. PRISMA checklist was utilized to select potential studies (Fig. 1). MEDLINE/PubMed database were searched by generating a search engine using Medical Science Heading (MeSH) terms: (((((“parasite”) AND ((“contamination”) OR “parasitic diseases“[MeSH Terms])) AND ((“vegetables”) OR “vegetables“[MeSH Terms])) AND ((“fruits”) OR “fruit“[MeSH Terms])) AND ((“market”) OR “marketing“[MeSH Terms])) AND ((“ethiopia”) OR “ethiopia“[MeSH Terms]).

PRISMA chart flow showing article selection process

Inclusion criteria

Studies should be published in English before July 30th, 2023. Only cross-sectional studies were analyzed. The key outcome of interest was parasite infection of fresh vegetables and fruits. The study population was confined to fresh vegetables and fruits, and the research had to be conducted in Ethiopia. The analysis covered both published and unpublished articles.

Exclusion criteria

Articles that failed to indicate the number of study participants or cases were discarded. Furthermore, articles with full text that were unavailable due to non-responsiveness from the respective authors, despite repeated email inquiries by two authors (ZA and AM), were eliminated. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were not eligible for inclusion.

Data extraction

Microsoft Excel 2019 sheet template was employed to extract data regarding authors name with publication year, study design, study area/region, diagnostic modalities used, sample size, numbers of raw vegetables and fruits with parasitic contamination, and prevalence of parasitic contamination among raw vegetables and fruits.

Quality assessment

Three writers (ZA, AM, and DB) evaluated the quality of included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, which allows for the assessment of each article’s methodological worth, comparability caliber, and result excellence. Arguments between the authors were addressed by discussion and articles were included after the authors reached an agreement.

Statistical analysis

STATA software version 14.0 was used to compute the estimated pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination among raw vegetables and fruits. Heterogeneity among included studies was assessed using percentage of variance (I2) and P-value.

Results

General characteristics of included studies

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 13 studies with a total sample size of 3769 fruits and vegetables. The sample size ranges from 123 to 384. All of them were conducted via cross-sectional study design. The studies were published between the year 2012 and 2023. The researches came from five different regions: Amhara, Dire Dawa city administration, Oromia, Southern Nations Nationalities and Peoples (SNNP), and Tigray. Amhara region has the highest and lowest percentages of contamination, 63.33% and 21.1%, respectively (Table: 1).

Pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination among raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia

As depicted in the forest plot, Fig. 2, the pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination in raw fruits and vegetables with at least one parasite in Ethiopia is 43.38% (95% CI: [36.64, 50.12]. The pooled prevalence was estimated using random effect model with a statistically significant heterogeneity, I2 = 94.7%, p ≤ 0.001, among the studies.

Forest plot showing meta-analysis of parasitic contamination among raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia

Subgroup analysis by study regions

The Oromia region had the highest pooled prevalence of parasite contamination in raw fruits and vegetables (51.95% (95% CI: [40.53, 63.37], I2 = 90.1%, p = 0.001), followed by Dire Dawa and Tigray regions (47.34% (95% CI: [42.29, 52.39] and 44.53% (95% CI: [19.34, 69.73], I2 = 97.3%, p ≤ 0.001), respectively). SNNPR has the lowest pooled prevalence at 39.57% (95% CI [26.55, 52.59], I2 = 95.9%, p < 0.001). However, the heterogeneity between locations is not statistically significant (p = 0.634) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot showing subgroup analysis by region of parasitic contamination among raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia

Distribution of parasites identified in the included studies

The most common parasites found in raw fruits and vegetables were S. stercoralis, Toxocara spp., G. lamblia, and A. lumbricoides, with a pooled prevalence of 12.87% (95% CI: [8.34, 17.41], I2 = 94.7%, p ≤ 0.001), 11.45% (95% CI: [5.61, 17.28], I2 = 94%, p ≤ 0.001), 10.96% (95% CI: [7.69, 14.25], I2 = 92.6), and 10.18% (95% CI: [6.83, 13.53], I2 = 94.4, respectively. On the contrary, Taenia spp, Cyclospora sp and I. belli were found to be the least prevalent parasites identified accounting 5.66% (95% CI: [4.31, 7.01], I2 = 98.6%, p ≤ 0.001), 5.21% (95% CI: [2.47, 7.95], I2 = 84%, p ≤ 0.001) and 4.45% (95% CI: [2.12, 6.78], I2 = 71.2%, p = 0.031), respectively (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis by diagnostic techniques

Another subgroup analysis conducted based on the type of wet mount utilized during investigating parasites from the raw fruits and vegetables showed that those studies utilized iodine wet mount technique has reported higher proportion of parasitic contamination 50.71% (95% CI: [52.34, 62.24], I2 = 95.9%, P ≤ 0.001). The pooled prevalence of contamination from studies that used saline wet mount technique is 47.51% (95% CI: [41.39, 53.64], I2 = 87.5%, P ≤ 0.001). However, studies conducted using both iodine and saline wet mount depicted a lower pooled prevalence. The heterogeneity of the pooled prevalence between the type of wet mount utilized in this sub group analysis is statistically significant, p = 0.017 (Fig. 4).

Forest plot showing subgroup analysis by diagnostic techniques of parasitic contamination among raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia

Another subgroup analysis was based on the utilization of modified Ziehl Neelson technique. In this regard, the pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination in raw fruits and vegetables from studies used modified Ziehl Neelson technique is 46.19% (95% CI: [38.18, 54.21], I2 = 95%, p ≤ 0.001) while those studies that didn’t use modified Ziehl Neelson technique reported a pooled prevalence of 38.73% (95% CI: [26.19, 51.27], I2 = 94.1%, p ≤ 0.001). However, the heterogeneity between the two groups is not statistically significant, p = 0.326 (Fig. 5).

As illustrated in Fig. 6, our meta-analysis indicated that both vegetable and fruit samples are proportionally contaminated with at least one parasite with a contamination prevalence of 45.9% (95% CI: [38.16, 53.65], I2 = 95%, P ≤ 0.001) and 41.75% (95% CI: [33.85, 49.65], I2 = 82%, P ≤ 0.001) respectively.

Types of contaminated raw vegetables

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined the levels of parasite contamination in six vegetables: lettuce, cabbage, spinach, green pepper, tomato, and carrot. Spinach was the most contaminated vegetable with at least one clinically important parasite, with 63.95% (95% CI: [52.27, 75.63], I2 = 72.3%, p = 0.006) contamination from the total spinach samples examined, followed by cabbage and lettuce with 54.05% (95% CI: [42.19, 65.91], I2 = 90.4%, p ≤ 0.001) and 52.08% (95% CI: [43.3, 60.85], I2 = 81.5%, p ≤ 0.001), respectively (Table 3).

Types of contaminated raw fruit

According to the results from the pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination among the types of fruits examined, banana has emerged to be the most frequently contaminated fruit with a contamination prevalence of 38.34% (95% CI: [1.84, 56.83], I2 = 81.3, p ≤ 0.001). Mango and avocado are the rest of fruit types with a contamination rate of 34.77% (95% CI: [20.23, 49.32], I2 = 80%, p = 0.002) and 33.96% (95% CI: [21.64, 46.29], I2 = 70.6%, p = 0.017), respectively (Table 3).

Number of parasite

Among the total samples of vegetables and fruits, only one parasite is found in 32.7% (95% CI: [22.64, 42.76], I2 = 97.1%, p ≤ 0.001) of the samples. On the other hand, dual parasitic contamination was found in 9.2% of the samples with 95% CI: [5.76, 12.64], I2 = 91.3%, p ≤ 0.001 while triple and quadruple contamination was detected in 3.3% and 2.3% of the examined sample raw vegetables and fruits respectively (Fig. 7).

Predictors of parasitic contamination

The current meta-analysis identified three predictors that are significantly associated with the occurrence of parasitic contamination in raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia. Unwashed fruits and vegetables have 1.6 times higher probability of being contaminated than those washed vegetables and fruits with p ≤ 0.001. Vegetables and fruits collected from venders who had no formal education had nearly ten times higher odds of being contaminated by parasites than who had formal education. Vegetables and fruits displayed for sale on the floor had nearly six times higher odds of being contaminated by parasites than those displayed on a shelf (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Contamination of edible fruits and vegetables with clinically important parasites that have the potential to cause health problems is becoming a major global health concern [4]. The problem has been highlighted as having enormous clinical and public health ramifications around the world, with significant repercussions for underdeveloped countries [27, 28]. In view of this, this systematic review and meta-analysis is determined to assess the pooled prevalence, pattern and predictors of clinically important parasites contaminating raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia.

According to the current systematic review and meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination in raw fruits and vegetables containing at least one parasite in Ethiopia is 43.38%. Moreover, the current meta-analysis indicated that both vegetable and fruit samples are proportionally contaminated with at least one parasite with a contamination prevalence of 45.9% and 41.75%, respectively. Nearly similar prevalence were reported from elsewhere [29, 30]. A systematic review and meta-analysis from Nigeria reported a lower pooled prevalence (32.4%) of parasitic contamination than the current study [11]. The pooled global prevalence of helminthic contamination in vegetables was 31% (26–37%) and 20% (8–37%) in fruits [10]. The pooled global prevalence of intestinal protozoan contamination was 13% (7–21%) for fruits and 20% (16–24%) for vegetables [31]. This shows that the parasitic contamination of raw vegetables and fruits is much greater in Ethiopia compared to the global meta-analysis result. Likewise, individual studies from different regions of Africa and Asia reported lower prevalence [28].

These discrepancies might be attributed to the difference in the nature of study population of the included articles where the current review pooled the prevalence of parasitic contamination from studies done in low socio-economic population, which is a key factor for highly prevalent parasitic infection [32, 33]. Moreover, the disparities in the prevalence of parasitic contamination could be attributed to differences in geographical location, climatic conditions, and extent of water contamination, study sample size, and parasitic detection mechanisms used in the studies. Due to variations in livestock management techniques, Ethiopia may have a higher incidence of animal feces and livestock contamination. This could lead to increased rates of parasite infection in fruits and vegetables. Higher rates of contamination may also result from disparities in Ethiopian farmers’ and food handlers’ awareness and practices regarding food safety as compared to other nations.

The current study’s subgroup analysis found that Oromia region (51.95%) had the greatest pooled prevalence of contamination, followed by Dire Dawa (47.34%) and Tigray regions (44.53%). One possible justification for the higher prevalence of parasitic contamination in fruits and vegetables in the Oromia, Dire Dawa, and Tigray regions compared to the Amhara and SNNP regions of Ethiopia could be differences in agricultural practices and environmental factors. A number of factors, including weather, sociocultural status, sample collecting season, fruits and vegetables studied, and other factors, influence the rate of contamination and type of parasites across the included studies. Furthermore, farmers in the Ethiopia frequently employ human and animal excrement as a natural fertilizer, and irrigation water is generally contaminated [16, 22]. The use of contaminated irrigation water or soil may be more common in certain regions, or there may be differences in the prevalence of animal feces contamination due to differences in livestock management practices. Additionally, differences in food safety knowledge and practices among farmers and vendors in different regions may also contribute to differences in contamination rates. The other important reason for the regional disparity in the prevalence of parasitic contamination of vegetables and fruits might be the variation in the regional human intestinal parasitic infection burden [34, 35]. Further research is needed to better understand the factors contributing to regional differences in parasitic contamination of fruits and vegetables in Ethiopia.

Studies that used iodine and saline mount microscopic examination indicated similar prevalence of parasitic contamination, 57.7% and 47.51% respectively, according to sub-group analysis based on the diagnostic processes to detect parasitic contamination. Nevertheless, compared to the pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination identified using either of the two techniques, the pooled prevalence of parasitic contamination from those studies that combined iodine and saline mount microscopic inspection is lower. This is a paradoxical finding because the diagnosis can be made more precisely when the two diagnostic modalities are combined. This could be because research using both approaches came from areas where parasite contamination in fruits and vegetables is low.

Spinach, cabbage, and lettuce were the most contaminated vegetables in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The prevalence of parasitic contamination in spinach was higher than green pepper and tomato. In Nigeria, the highest level of parasitic contamination was identified in Pineapple and lettuce [11]. This is because the uneven surfaces of spinach, lettuce, and cabbage allow parasite stages to cling to them more readily while tomato and green pepper have smooth surfaces that may slow the process of parasite attachment and hence lower contamination rates [36]. In Ethiopia, where the culture of eating raw spinach, tomato, and lettuce is higher, there is a greater chance of acquiring intestinal parasitic infections, especially when ingested without thorough washing.

In the current systematic review and meta-analysis, banana, mango, and avocado were the most predominantly contaminated fruit by parasites (38.34%, 34.77%, and 33.96%, respectively). This is in agreement with the study in Cameron that reported avocado pear, mango, and banana as having the highest contamination [37]. The fact that certain fruits, as certain vectors, easily contaminate mangoes and bananas, such flies, may help to explain the variance in contamination levels among the goods. Customers regularly contact the other highly contaminated fruits with their bare hands during purchases, which has the potential to contaminate them.

In this meta-analysis, S. stercoralis was the most predominantly prevalent parasite detected in the raw fruits and vegetables. This is in agreement with systematic review and meta-analysis results of Nigeria [11] and Iran [29]. S. stercoralis was also the predominant parasite identified in Ghana [12]. However, in Thailand, the most frequently identified parasite was Hookworm [38]. The frequent detection of S. stercoralis could be because, in addition to its parasitic lifestyle, the parasite also possesses a free living state and does not need a host to multiply [39]. It has been observed that S. stercoralis is extensively found in unsanitary and unhygienic environments. Animals (dogs, cats, etc.) and human waste may have contaminated the soil and water, resulting in infection [12, 40].

The ova of Toxocara spp was the second most common contaminant. The enormous fecundity of adult Toxocara females up to ten thousand eggs per day and the eggs’ resilience which allows them to withstand harsh environmental conditions for up to ten years may be the reasons for their high prevalence [41]. The other possible reason for the common occurrence of Toxocara spp in vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia could be due to the exposure of farm lands to dogs and cats. The zoonotic roundworm parasites Toxocara canis and Toxocara cati live in the digestive systems of infected foxes, dogs, and cats. Animals with the infection may excrete large quantities of Toxocara spp. eggs in their feces, which then pollute the environment and spread disease [42]. This was evident in a study done in four commercial vegetable farmlands in United Kingdom. The number of Toxocara spp positive samples were statistically greater in the farmland with no policy restricting pet dogs than the farmlands that strictly control the entrance of dogs [43].

G. lamblia was the third commonest parasite identified in this systematic review and meta-analysis. G. lamblia is the most predominant protozoa detected contaminating fruits and vegetables followed by Entamoeba histolytica. This finding is in harmony with the high predominance of E. histolytica (14.09%) and G. lamblia (10.03%) reported in a meta-analysis of human intestinal protozoan parasitic infections in Ethiopia [34]. It is also similar to the 8.8% and 6.8% prevalence of G. lamblia and E. histolytica in vegetables, respectively, reported in Egypt [44]. However, this finding is not in conformity with the systematic review and meta-analysis result of parasitic contamination of vegetables and fruits in Nigeria that reported Cryptosporidium oocysts as the dominant protozoa identified [11]. The most common protozoan parasite found in vegetables a global meta-analysis was Cryptosporidium spp [31] which is not in line with the current systematic review and meta-analysis. Furthermore, in the global meta-analysis, the most frequent agent discovered in fruits was E. histolytica [31]. The use of contaminated and untreated water sources for irrigation, animal manure as fertilizer, and poor hygiene practices during handling could contribute for the increased occurrence of G. lamblia and E. histolytica in Ethiopia.

A. lumbricoides was the fourth commonest parasite found in vegetables and fruits in this systematic review and meta-analysis. A. lumbricoides was the most abundantly found helimenth in vegetables in the global systematic review and meta-analysis [10]. Its abundance might be explained by the fact that A. lumbricoides is a geo-helimenth that basically spreads through the use of soil. This is evident also in the recent systematic review and meta-analysis of human helminthic infection prevalence, which reported A. lumbricoides as the most prevalent intestinal helminth in Ethiopia (10.84%) [35].

The other parasite reported in the majority of the included studies (7 out of the 13 studies) was Cryptosporidium Spp. The primary medium for the spread of Cryptosporidium spp sporulated oocysts is contaminated water [45]. Therefore, it’s probable that vegetables were irrigated and that contaminated water was used to wash both fruits and vegetables, contributing to the presence of Cryptosporidium oocysts. The pooled global prevalence of Cryptosporidium in soil was estimated to be 8.13%. The continent, air pressure, temperature, and technique of detection all had a significant impact on the frequency of Cryptosporidium in soil.

The current meta-analysis identified three factors that are significantly associated with the occurrence of parasitic contamination in raw vegetables and fruits in Ethiopia. These include washing produce before display, educational status of vendors, and product placement. Unwashed fruits and vegetables have 1.6 times higher probability of being contaminated than those washed vegetables and fruits. The finding emphasizes how crucial it is to wash produce before consuming it. Produce can have its surface dirt, debris, and possible pathogens removed by washing, which lowers the chance of contracting a foodborne illness.

The other factor that is significantly associated with the parasitic contamination of vegetables and fruits in the current systematic review and meta-analysis is educational status of vendors. Vegetables and fruits collected from venders who had no formal education had nearly ten times higher odds of being contaminated by parasites than who had formal education. The finding suggests that vendors who have no formal education may have less knowledge and awareness about food safety practices, including proper handling and storage of produce. This lack of knowledge could contribute to higher rates of contamination. The finding may indicate a need for targeted training and support for vendors who have no formal education. In addition, policy makers may need to consider measures to promote education and training among vendors.

Vegetables and fruits displayed for sale on the floor had nearly six times higher odds of being contaminated by parasites than those displayed on shelf. The finding highlights the importance of proper display of fruits and vegetables in minimizing the risk of contamination by parasites. Displaying produce on the floor may increase the likelihood of contact with contaminants, such as dirt and dust, which can harbor parasites. Policymakers may need to consider regulations or guidelines for proper display of produce in markets and other retail settings.

Conclusion

This comprehensive review and meta-analysis demonstrate the high frequency of parasite contamination in raw fruits and vegetables in Ethiopia. The study found differences in contamination rates between areas. The parasites with the highest prevalence was S. stercoralis. Factors such as washing status, vendor educational background, and product display strategies were discovered to be associated with higher contamination rates. These findings highlight the necessity of implementing appropriate food safety measures, such as adequate product washing and vendor education, to lower the risk of parasite diseases caused by contaminated fruits and vegetables.

Limitation of the study

This systematic review and meta-analysis included only studies conducted with a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between factors, as could be done with case-control study designs. Additionally, this meta-analysis was based on studies from only five regions, which may not accurately represent the true extent of parasitic contamination.
