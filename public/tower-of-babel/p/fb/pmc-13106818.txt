Integrating ANI and phylogenies for re-evaluation of Fusobacterium taxonomy and disease associations
Authors: Dexi Bi, Yuli Wu, Guo Ji, Xingchen Zhu, Hao Li, Ruting Xie, Yaohui Gao, Zixin Deng, Dongyan Han, Huanlong Qin, Qing Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genus Fusobacterium encompasses significant pathogens implicated in diseases spanning from infections to cancer. However, taxonomic ambiguities persist within the genus, particularly concerning Fusobacterium nucleatum (sensu lato). Through genus-wide average nucleotide identity (ANI) and phylogenetic analyses of 540 Fusobacterium genomes, we identify an ANI gap (93.38%−93.89%) for species delineation, leading to comprehensive taxonomic revisions that resolve these ambiguities. We further establish gyrB and rpoB as high-resolution taxonomic markers with phylogenies consistently supporting the revised taxonomy. Leveraging these markers, we develop B&B, a general strategy for precise species identification without whole-genome sequencing, and validate its accuracy in clinically relevant strains. Integrating the revised taxonomy with genomic/metagenomic toolkits demonstrate broad utilities, reinterpreting key colorectal cancer-associated species. This work establishes a unified taxonomic framework and enables standardised species classification for Fusobacterium isolates and microbiomes, highlighting the genetic divergence among Fusobacterium species and providing the taxonomic precision essential for advancing Fusobacterium-related research.Subject terms: Bacterial genomics, Taxonomy, Bacterial genetics, Pathogens

Introduction

The genus Fusobacterium has garnered broad attention due to its emerging roles in human diseases beyond infection, functioning as key components of the dysregulated microbiota1,2. In the recent decade F. nucleatum (sensu lato) has been extensively studied as an oncobacterium in various malignancies, such as colorectal cancer and breast cancer, acting through multiple mechanisms3–9. Its presence has been implicated as potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets10,11.

However the taxonomy within this genus has long been challenging and complex, particularly regarding F. nucleatum (sensu lato)12,13. Historically F. nucleatum (sensu lato) has been classified into four subspecies, animalis, polymorphum, vincentii and nucleatum, with documented genotypic and phenotypic heterogeneity12,14–20. However genomic evidence, including average nucleotide identity (ANI) and genome-to-genome distance analyses has suggested that these subspecies are sufficiently divergent to be species; and accordingly, they were reclassified as F. animalis, F. polymorphum, F. vincentii and F. nucleatum (sensu stricto) by Kook et al.19. This revision has been further supported by studies with genome/marker-based approaches and/or biological species concept (BSC) strategies21–24. Recently, Zepeda-Rivera et al. proposed that subsp. animalis comprises two clades, Fna C1 and Fna C225. Subsequent works noted that Fna C1 represents a lineage distinct from F. animalis20,24. Most lately Sivertsen et al.26, through ANI and BSC analyses, proposed that Fna C1 corresponds to F. watanabei, a species previously described by Tomida et al.27, while Fna C2 corresponds to F. animalis. Notably the annotation of the Fna C1 lineage has evolved rapidly and fluctuated across studies. Most strains defined as Fna C1 were initially identified as F. nucleatum (sensu lato) subsp. animalis based on 16S rRNA gene profiling25. Zepeda-Rivera et al. designated them as Fna C1 after distinguishing them from the F. nucleatum (sensu lato) subsp. animalis 7_1 lineage based on pangenomic, ANI and phylogenetic analyses of F. nucleatum (sensu lato) genomes25. Meanwhile, Ma et al. assigned a strain (FNU) of this lineage as a novel subspecies (subsp. unique) based on pangenome‑based phylogeny28. A recent metagenomic study by Piccinno et al. regrouped Fna C1 with F. vincentii based on phylogenetic analysis of the species-level genome bins (SGBs) within F. nucleatum (sensu lato)29. Almost in parallel with the proposal reclassifying Fna C1 as F. watanabei26, Zepeda-Rivera et al. also observed, based on the genome taxonomy database (GTDB) analysis, that F. watanabei, Fna C1, subsp. unique (FNU) and the F. nucleatum_J group defined by GTDB may represent the same lineage13,30. These dynamic shifts in classification likely stem from differences in the datasets and methodologies employed across studies, highlighting the need to integrate genomic, phylogenetic and marker-based approaches within a coherent framework. Recent studies have further highlighted their distinctive roles in disease pathogenesis12. For instance these taxa are differentially distributed in CRC18,22,31,32 with Fna C2 (F. animalis) reported to dominate the CRC niche25. Similarly it has been shown that subsp. animalis (F. animalis), rather than the well-characterised subsp. nucleatum (F. nucleatum sensu stricto), is clinically relevant to inflammation in oral diseases23. Such studies have either supported the notion of reclassifying F. nucleatum (sensu lato) subspecies/clades as species or provided genomic evidence such as ANI values supporting that reclassification23,25. However mixed use of nomenclature in literature, coupled with uncertain annotations risk raising ambiguity and have fuelled ongoing debate12,13,26,33,34. More critically this inconsistency often fails to adequately account for the underlying genetic and pathogenic diversity among these taxa, potentially obscuring precise correlations between species and their specific biological or clinical relevance. While experts in microbiological taxonomy and/or clinical microbiology may readily adopt properly updated classifications, a significant practical challenge remains in achieving consensus on consistent implementation across the broader research and clinical community (especially among those without the specialised expertise) now actively engaged in Fusobacterium studies. This challenge is particularly pressing given the expanding relevance of Fusobacterium in diverse diseases. Furthermore the high level of similarity in 16S rRNA genes often limits their utility for species-level resolution within Fusobacterium22,25,35, particularly when partial regions such as V3–V4 are used, and may also lead to misclassification of non-nucleatum (sensu lato) taxa. Therefore a timely clarification of Fusobacterium taxonomy, along with development of standardised, practical solutions to ensure consistent, accurate and reproducible species-level identification, both for current applications and future research, is urgently needed.

Genome-based ANI has been widely recognised as a quantitative standard for defining bacterial species boundaries36,37. Phylogenomic approaches have also been adopted to clarify taxonomic ambiguities in other genera38–41. Although several studies have included ANI and/or phylogenetic analyses of Fusobacterium genomes13,22–26, genus-wide assessments remain scarce and a robust species boundary for the entire genus remains undefined. In addition alternative genetic markers, including the RNA polymerase beta subunit gene rpoB and zinc protease gene znpA, have been proposed for species discrimination23–25,35. Dedicated profiling methods targeting these markers including FrpoB-seq for rpoB V1 (variable region 1)22 and znpA amplicon sequencing23, have been developed for community analysis within the human microbiota, to overcome the low-resolution limitations of 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Similarly primers targeting the DNA gyrase subunit B gene gyrB have been designed to differentiate the subspecies of F. nucleatum (sensu lato) as well as F. necrophorum42. However the utility of gyrB has not been evaluated in a genus-wide context. Furthermore the recent expansion of available Fusobacterium genome necessitates reassessment of the resolution of these markers and the establishment of high-resolution phylogenetic markers.

In this work to address these gaps, we conduct a genus-wide study integrating large-scale whole genome ANI and phylogenetic analyses. This study has systematically established a unified taxonomic framework for Fusobacterium, resolved the taxonomic ambiguities, defined high-resolution genetic markers, and developed standardised species identification approaches applicable to both individual strains and complex microbiota.

Results

Genus-wide analysis revealed an ANI gap delineating species

To identify a unified species boundary within the Fusobacterium genus we performed ANI and phylogenetic analysis on 540 genomes of Fusobacterium strains/isolates (Supplementary Data 1) collected from the NCBI RefSeq, a high-quality genome database. The frequency distribution of pairwise ANI values revealed a natural gap spanning 93.39–93.94% (centred at 93.67%) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Data 2), with complete discontinuity. To further characterise this discontinuity we sorted all pairwise ANI values in descending order (Supplementary Fig. 1a) and calculated the first derivative (Δ) of the distribution to measure the rate of change in ANI, using an approach similar to that described by Passarelli‑Araujo et al.43. The maximum Δ among ANI values > 90%, defined as the ANI discontinuity (δ), precisely corresponded to this gap (Supplementary Fig. 1b), supporting the presence of a significant genetic break. Given that the bacterial species boundary is typically around 95% ANI36,37 we investigated whether this observed ANI gap could serve as a threshold for Fusobacterium species delineation. To anchor taxonomic references we used the reference genomes (n = 20) defined by the RefSeq, each representing a well-established species, to standardise and stabilise species definitions throughout this study. In the dendrogram derived from the ANI matrix strains with pairwise ANI values above this the gap consistently formed discrete clades, each corresponding well to a specific species (Fig. 1b). The taxonomic anchors were uniquely distributed in individual clades. We then constructed a whole-genome phylogenetic tree using kSNP444 to evaluate species-level relationships and validate the ANI-based groupings (Fig. 1c). The whole-genome tree and the ANI-based dendrogram showed a normalised quartet score (NQS) of 0.904 (Fig. 2) indicating very strong concordance. Critically the ANI-based grouping was corroborated by the whole genome-based phylogenetic tree, where strains within each ANI-defined group consistently clustered together within the same major clades parallel to each other (Figs. 1c and 2), confirming that the ANI gap represents a standardised boundary for defining Fusobacterium species. Considering that recombination might influence the topological accuracy of the whole genome-based phylogenetic tree we additionally constructed trees based on single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci occurring in at least 25, 50 and 75% of genomes (Supplementary Fig. 2). All these trees showed 0.96–0.99 NQS to the primary tree suggesting that the core phylogenetic relationships were robust against potential noise introduced by recombination. Finally species names within each ANI-defined group were assigned based on the taxonomic anchor where available. Clades lacking a reference anchor either corresponded to unspecified species (previously recognised) or were designated as novel species. Thus the 540 genomes were finally classified into 34 species (Table 1), six of which were newly proposed. The intra- and interspecies ANI distributions showed no overlap around this boundary (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Impact of genome number and completeness on ANI boundary definition

To assess the sufficiency of the genome sample size for capturing this boundary we performed a rarefaction curve-like analysis. We randomly selected increased amounts of genomes with 100 replicates and plotted the changes in the upper and lower limits of the ANI gap (Fig. 3a). Both limits reached plateau with over 350 genomes. Furthermore considering that over half of the genomes analysed were draft assemblies, we evaluated the impact of genome completeness on the ANI gap range. Draft genomes exerted a shrinking effect on the gap width as the gap range calculated using the draft genomes was narrower than that obtained from the complete genomes (Fig. 3b). Nevertheless the above-identified ANI gap based on both complete and draft genomes remains a distinct boundary as it still spans an interval of over 0.5% ANI.

Taxonomic revisions resolved species designation ambiguities

The genome-based phylogeny and the standardised ANI species boundary enabled a comprehensive review of the Fusobacterium taxonomy. Strikingly we found that the species designations of 100 (18.5%) strains in RefSeq required correction, update, or redefinition (Supplementary Data 2 and Table 2). Corrections for 59 genomes were consistent with prior reports, validating our ANI-based species boundary. Crucially our results confirmed that F. nucleatum (sensu lato) comprises multiple distinct species; specifically the subspecies vincentii, polymorphum, nucleatum (sensu stricto) and the animalis clade 1 (Fna C1) and 2 (Fna C2) are separate species, consistent with previous observations19,22,23. In our analysis Fna C2 was represented by the F. animalis anchor strain (KCOM 1325), whereas Fna C1 was clustered with the F. watanabei anchor (CCUG74246T). Our data also supported that the strain F. nucleatum (sensu lato) W1481 lacking subspecies designation belongs to F. simiae26 and validated the proposed reclassification of F. equinum into F. gonidiaformans45. Among the revisions proposed here a group of species-unspecified isolates from bovine digital dermatitis form a new species, proposed here as Fusobacterium sp. bovis. Four strains misclassified as F. ulcerans or F. varium constitute another new species proposed as F. heteroulcerans sp. nov. Notably F. heteroulcerans was previously designated Fusobacterium sp. 81A6 to describe the F. ulcerans ATCC 49185 isolate 81A622. Our results further clarified that 81A6 is distinct from the original F. ulcerans ATCC 49185 strain. We also identified that five strains misclassed as F. varium, F. mortiferum or F. perfoetens actually represent four additional novel species. Meanwhile six strains were corrected from F. periodonticum to F. pseudoperiodonticum, and 14 unspecified species were assigned into established species, some of which were also in agreement with earlier reports20,24,26. Note that some of the RefSeq reference genomes used as taxonomic anchors might not be derived from type strains as we employed a standardised, database-driven approach to maintain methodological consistency. For instance while KCOM 1325 was used as the F. animalis anchor, ATCC 51191 (type strain) and 7_1 are commonly used for this species25. Our analysis confirmed their classification within the same species, indicating that the type strains or commonly used ones, though they might not be used here as anchors, remain important and reliable representative strains. In addition given that several Fusobacterium species, such as F. perfoetens, exhibited distinct phylogenetic relationships to well-established species (Fig. 1c) and were observed to relate with non-Fusobacterium taxa in previous studies46,47, we performed an extended whole-genome phylogenetic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4). This analysis included representative genomes (Supplementary Data 3) from other genera within the Fusobacteriaceae family, as well as F. naviforme, which has been proposed for reclassification as Zandiella naviformis45. Interestingly our result indicated that although F. perfoetens, Fusobacterium sp. An877, sp. FSA-380-WT-3AF, sp. SB021 (F. sphaericum)48, sp. IOR10, sp. MFO224 and sp. An888 remained phylogenetically distinct from other genera, their degree of divergence from other Fusobacterium species appeared comparable to that observed in Cetobacterium. Meanwhile Z. naviformis (F. naviforme) maintained a relatively close phylogenetic association with F. gonidiaformans. This evidence collectively suggested that certain highly divergent lineages may further warrant a taxonomic re-evaluation at the genus level.

Evaluation of gyrB and rpoB as high-resolution taxonomic markers

We then evaluated Fusobacterium taxonomic markers (Supplementary Data 4) using these species-curated genomes. Phylogenetic analysis of the 16S rRNA gene (including all available copies within a genome) revealed inadequate species discriminatory power particularly for distinguishing F. animalis, F. polymorphum, Fusobacterium sp. SB042 and F. hwasookii (Fig. 4a). The tree also did not efficiently cluster many species into distinct clades. The 16S rRNA gene tree showed a NQS of 0.829 to the whole-genome phylogeny indicating moderately strong agreement. Accounting for its multi-copy nature we calculated the maximum, minimum and average 16S rRNA gene identities between each strain pair (Supplementary Data 5). The resulting interspecies and interspecies pairwise identity distributions showed extensive overlap precluding reliable species boundary delineation at any threshold (Fig. 4b). In contrast we demonstrated that rpoB, gyrB and the znpA genes are effective molecular markers for resolving phylogenetic relationships within Fusobacterium (Fig. 5a). We confirmed that rpoB provided high species-level resolution (NQS = 0.864) consistent with previous reports22,35. The znpA phylogeny also showed high discrimination power as previously documented23,35 but still exhibited moderately strong agreement with whole-genome phylogeny (NQS = 0.827), likely because it did not fully cluster F. watanabei and F. polymorphum into distinct clades. Additionally znpA was found to be absent in F. necrophorum and F. gonidiaformans. Notably we identified the housekeeping gene gyrB exhibiting strong discriminatory power for Fusobacterium species, with an NQS of 0.905 to whole-genome phylogeny. The concatenated rpoB + gyrB phylogeny also resolved species well (NQS = 0.894; Supplementary Fig. 5). A PHI test detected no significant recombination signals in all analysed genes except for rpoB (Supplementary Table 1). Nevertheless the major phylogenetic structure of the rpoB tree remained highly informative for species-level clustering. To statistically compare marker performance we calculated the nucleotide diversity (π) of all analysed genes and performed a bootstrap-based difference test. We also compared the distributions of NQS values obtained from bootstrap-resampled gene trees relative to whole-genome phylogeny. Given the draft nature of many genomes and the natural absence of znpA in certain species, not every RefSeq genome contained complete marker sequences; therefore all comparisons and corresponding NQS calculations were performed only on the subset of genomes containing complete sequences for the markers being analysed. Within the analysed subsets rpoB, gyrB and znpA all exhibited higher nucleotide diversity than the 16S rRNA gene (Supplementary Fig. 6). The gyrB trees consistently yielded the highest NQS values followed by rpoB and znpA, all of which were statistically higher than that of the 16S rRNA gene tree (Supplementary Fig. 7). However the concatenated rpoB + gyrB phylogeny did not improve the NQS compared to gyrB alone (Supplementary Fig. 7). Notably phylogenies of all three markers consistently supported the taxonomic revisions of Fusobacterium. However for each of the three genes, defining a universal pairwise identity threshold to delineate species remain unsuccessful by persistent, albeit slight, overlap between intra- and interspecies identity distributions (Supplementary Data 5 and Fig. 5b). In addition our previous study had identified 45 undescribed Fusobacterium species in the gut microbiota of CRC patients via FrpoB-seq22. Phylogenetic analysis of rpoB V1 incorporating the FrpoB-seq-derived sequences revealed that the recently or newly defined F. watanabei, Fusobacterium sp. SB042, sp. SB021 (F. sphaericum), sp. An877, sp. An888 and sp. An425 matched the previously identified species22 Fusobacterium sp. B1_6, sp. B1_45, sp. B3_19, sp. B2_19, sp. B3_87 and sp. B3_12, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8), supporting the notion that there are more Fusobacterium species to be explored. This phylogenetic analysis also assigned all species except Fusobacterium sp. PH-29 into previously established Fusobacterium lineages (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Table 1).

A general method for Fusobacterium species identification

We sought to develop a practical and accurate approach to overcome the limitations of the 16S rRNA gene-based method for Fusobacterium species identification. Given the absence of established identity thresholds for individual genetic markers we investigated the feasibility of using a marker combination and considering only the best hit. We selected gyrB and rpoB, which are ubiquitous in Fusobacterium. An ~1.5 kb gyrB variable region (n.t. 240–1737; designated the gyrB V1 region) and the rpoB V1 region (n.t. 81–399)22 compatible for PCR with degenerate primers were assessed (Fig. 6a, b). These regions exhibited nucleotide diversity comparable to their full-length genes (Supplementary Fig. 6) and no significant recombination signal was detected in rpoB V1 (Supplementary Table 1). Pan-Fusobacterium primers gyrB-219F and gyrB-1759R were designed for gyrB V1 whereas those for the latter were rpoB-55F and rpoB-422R, modified from fuso-rpoB-F2/R222. Analysis considering only the intra- and interspecies best hits of these regions from one genome against the counterparts in others revealed that identity thresholds of 97% for gyrB V1 and 99% for rpoB V1 could accurately define species for nearly all genomes (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Data 6). Only four genomes exhibited intraspecies gyrB V1 identities below 97% (96–97%) and much lower interspecies identities (Fig. 6c). While the 97% gyrB V1 threshold failed to define their species, interestingly, three of them could be subsequently defined using the rpoB V1 region at the 99% threshold (Fig. 6c). The only exception was F. necrophorum KG35 (Fig. 6c). Therefore we propose a B&B (gyrB and rpoB) strategy for Fusobacterium species identification (Fig. 6d). First one can compare an amplified gyrB sequence (primer regions excluded) against its counterparts (Supplementary Data 4) from species-curated genomes, using a ≥97% best-hit identity threshold to assign species. If this proves inconclusive on some occasions, the rpoB V1 can then be analysed with a ≥99% best-hit identity threshold. If both markers fail probably in rare instances, genomic ANI and/or phylogenetic analysis is recommended. The gyrB V1 is prioritised as the primary marker because rpoB V1 exhibited limited discriminatory power between F. perfoetens and Fusobacterium sp. FSA-380-WT-3A. Moreover if species assignment is successful using gyrB alone, rpoB analysis is unnecessary. We emphasise that this workflow is specific to the B&B method for practical purposes and does not imply that gyrB is superior to rpoB for all taxonomic applications. To validate the performance of the B&B method we applied it to nine Fusobacterium strains isolated from CRC tissues (sequenced in this study) and 36 public genomes from the NCBI GenBank database (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Data 7). These public genomes were excluded from the RefSeq database exclusively because of incorrect taxonomic assignments, not quality issues. They mostly represented medically relevant isolates including 14 from the human gut (11 CRC-related, two IBD-related and one unspecified), 14 from the human oral cavity (5 CRC-related, one periodontal disease-related, and eight unspecified), two from the human trans-tracheal site, one from the human cervico-facial site, four from human unspecified sites, and one from the dog mouth. Strikingly the B&B species classifications for all 45 strains perfectly matched the reference assignments based on ANI values (Supplementary Data 7), whole-genome phylogeny (Supplementary Fig. 4) and previous curation20,24,26, confirming the classification accuracy of the method. One F. heteroulcerans strain (THCT13E1) was thus designated as the type strain of this newly proposed species. We further applied B&B to the genome of the recently established species F. paranimalis (type strain NCTC15132; Supplementary Data 7)26. The method correctly identified it as a novel species consistent with whole-genome phylogeny (Supplementary Fig. 4). Interestingly a few of its pairwise ANI values with RefSeq genomes (71.30–93.51%) fell within the species gap described above. As its maximum ANI (93.51%) to any RefSeq genome remained below the midpoint (93.67%) and upper limit (93.94%) of the gap, this supported its novel species status and suggested that inclusion of F. paranimalis would refine the gap boundary to 93.52–93.94%. Note that universal tails were incorporated into the primers to facilitate sequencing in this study and in silico B&B analysis was applied to the public genomes. The gyrB and rpoB sequences from the species-curated RefSeq genomes were used as a reference dataset (Supplementary Data 4).

Integration of the revised taxonomy with bioinformatics toolkits

To demonstrate the broader applicability of the revised Fusobacterium taxonomy, we integrated it with two representative bioinformatics toolkits, the genome taxonomy database (GTDB)49 and MetaPhlAn50. GTDB is a widely utilised database for microbial taxonomy and genomic analyses based on genome phylogeny. We herein mapped the revised Fusobacterium taxonomy to GTDB assignments (Table 1) and particularly reassigned ambiguous GTDB taxonomic entities, such as F. nucleatum_J, F. nucleatum_D, F. priodonticum_D, F. periodonticum_B, Fusobacterium_A ulcerans_A and Fusobacterium_A varium_A, to defined species. Furthermore given the importance of Fusobacterium in the human microbiota, which is commonly profiled via metagenomic approaches, we mapped the revised taxonomy to the SGBs used by MetaPhlAn (Fig. 7a, Table 1 and Supplementary Data 8), a state-of-the-art tool for species-level microbial profiling from metagenomic data. This mapping identified SGBs specifically matching 29 Fusobacterium species including those reclassified from F. nucleatum (sensu lato). We found that all genomes of a given species exhibited > 92% ANIs to their species-specific SGB and lower ANIs to other SGBs, with very few exceptions observed only in F. canifelinum and F. nucleatum (sensu stricto) (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Data 8). Critically this revised SGB annotation refined the interpretation of key CRC-associated species identified in previous large-scale metagenomic studies25,29, demonstrating that certain CRC-associated SGBs previously assigned to F. nucleatum (sensu lato) actually correspond to distinct species (Fig. 7c).

Discussion

This study established a unified taxonomic framework for the genus Fusobacterium by defining a general ANI boundary for species delineation and identifying high-resolution taxonomic markers. We further developed a method and tuned bioinformatic toolkits to facilitate accurate species classification of isolates and improve community profiling within complex microbiota, respectively.

Taxonomic classifications continuously evolve with advances in molecular techniques. Our large-scale genus-wide analyses integrating whole-genome ANI and phylogenetic approaches revealed a natural ANI gap that serves as a boundary for Fusobacterium species demarcation, applicable to both complete and draft genomes. These analyses systematically clarified longstanding taxonomic ambiguities within the genus. Critically our evidence supports recognition of the historical F. nucleatum (sensu lato) subspecies/clades as distinct species, as these taxa exhibit genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity consistent with species-level distinctions, particularly in disease contexts. Moreover the former designations could not adequately capture the extent of diversity now observed. Given the broad research interest in F. nucleatum (sensu lato) and increasing recognition of its heterogeneity in disease associations, we advocate for universal adoption of the revised nomenclature13. While consistent use of the updated taxonomy and nomenclature require consensus within the community, this work provides a foundation to support such efforts.

Moreover our genomic analyses underscore the substantial genetic divergence among Fusobacterium species. The observed ANI threshold is notably lower than the conventional 95% cutoff for bacterial species36,37 reflecting greater genomic diversity within this genus. It is therefore crucial to incorporate these species-level genetic differences in both research and clinical diagnostics. Such attention to the genetic variations will improve the accuracy of clinical associations, refine pathogen identification, and aid in discovering virulence factors and pathogenic mechanisms, which will ultimately support progress in precision medicine. Our data provide a foundation for reliable genomic/metagenomic analyses appropriate strain selection in experimental studies, and precision in clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Our expanded genomic analyses which included non-Fusobacterium genera, also provide a broader phylogenetic context for evaluating the taxonomic placements of certain species that exhibit distinct evolutionary relationships to well-established Fusobacterium species. Previous studies based on core-gene or 16S rRNA gene analyses have linked F. perfoetens and F. naviforme with other genera and the latter has already been proposed for reclassification as Z. naviformis45–47. Our genome-scale evidence indicated that while these species remain phylogenetically distinct from other genera a re-evaluation of their taxonomic positions at the genus level is necessary. However the currently limited number of available genomes for these taxa and other members of the Fusobacteriaceae family still precludes the establishment of a robust, genome-based boundary for genus-level classification.

In our analysis a rarefaction curve-like analysis was employed to assess the stability of the observed ANI gap range. Nonetheless its dynamic nature must be emphasised. The gap range should be iteratively updated as more genomic data become available as illustrated by the inclusion of the F. paranimalis genome. While the current range may not capture all biological variation, it is expected to gradually converge toward the true genetic discontinuity between species as technology advances and more genomes are added. The present relatively broad range also offers a practical buffer that accommodates future convergence and supports the handling of borderline cases in real-world applications.

Although the 16S rRNA gene is the mostly used molecular marker for bacterial species classification, our results confirm its inadequacy for Fusobacterium due to limited species-level resolution. This limitation likely arises from the high sequence conservation across Fusobacterium species and the variations among copies within individual strains. Thus the use of the 16S rRNA gene for Fusobacterium species identification risks misclassification, potentially resulting in accumulated errors that compound historical classification ambiguities. Alternative markers rpoB, znpA have been employed for species or certain subspecies discrimination based on their phylogenies22–24,35,51. Our phylogenetic analyses reiterate rpoB as a genus-wide taxonomic marker and further establish gyrB as possessing comparable discriminatory power. The gyrB gene has shown greater species discriminatory power than the 16S rRNA gene across diverse bacterial taxa52. Collectively our results demonstrate that gyrB and rpoB are high-resolution taxonomic markers for Fusobacterium. It should be noted however, that recombination signals were detected in rpoB, consistent with a prior report24, and thus its tree topology should be interpreted with caution in evolutionary inference.

These findings enable precise species-level identification of Fusobacterium isolates and enhance community profiling in the human microbiota. The genomes with curated species designations constitute a reference dataset for ANI and phylogenetic analyses of sequenced genomes. Furthermore leveraging gyrB and rpoB, we developed B&B, a practical method for rapid Fusobacterium species identification without requiring whole-genome sequencing. This method demonstrated complete concordance with ANI-based classifications across diverse species. We further demonstrated integration of the revised taxonomy with GTDB49 and MetaPhlAn50, providing curated annotations that improved taxonomic precision in genomic analysis and metagenomic profiling. Most of the species had a uniquely matched GTDB taxonomic entity and a SGB (both derived from genome-based approaches), corroborating in turn the reliability of the revised species classification. These integrations exemplified the flexible utility of the revised taxonomy with existing toolkits. Importantly the resulting taxonomic clarifications of isolates and Fusobacterium communities within microbiomes redefined disease-associated species and could thereby guide mechanistic studies of pathogenicity. Moreover as the 16S rRNA gene sequencing normally applied in tumour tissues (where metagenomics is challenging) is unable to differentiate between the Fusobacterium species, the high-resolution markers could also facilitate the development of targeted sequencing approaches to elucidate Fusobacterium composition in tumour microbiomes.

This study has limitations. The kSNP4-based whole-genome phylogenetic analysis did not account for recombination due to current technical barriers particularly given the large dataset comprising over 50% draft genomes. Although we have demonstrated the robustness of the whole-genome tree in species clustering using varying fractions of SNPs, the precise evolutionary relationships among strains warrant further investigation. The numbers of available genomes across species are inevitably uneven. Thus additional strains are still needed to validate this research and iteratively refine the ANI gap range. For certain species a comprehensive taxonomic re-evaluation at the genus level will also be necessary as more Fusobacteriaceae genomes become available. The proposed taxonomic markers, newly developed classification method B&B and extended applications require prospective evaluation. As this is a genome-based analysis future work should also integrate phenotypic and pathogenic characterisation to fully understand the biological and clinical implications of individual species.

In conclusion, this study proposes a unified taxonomic framework for Fusobacterium, which resolves species classification ambiguities and delivers standardised methodological solutions for accurate species identification applicable to both individual strains and complex microbiota. Our work highlights the genetic divergence within Fusobacterium and provides the taxonomic precision essential for advancing mechanistic and clinical research on Fusobacterium pathogenesis. While consistent adoption of the revised Fusobacterium taxonomy and nomenclature within the expansive cross-disciplinary research community remains a practical challenge, our work lays the groundwork for such convergence.

Methods

Public bacterial genomic data

A total of 542 publicly available Fusobacterium genomes were retrieved from the NCBI RefSeq database as of 20 June 2025 (Supplementary Data 1) for genome-based taxonomic analysis. While the RefSeq has filtered out low-quality genomes with strict criteria (see https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/docs/v2/policies-annotation/genome-processing/genome_notes/), we further assessed genome quality using CheckM2 (version 1.1.0)53. Based on this assessment, 540 genomes with >95% completeness and <5% contamination were retained for analysis. The reference genomes designated by the RefSeq (see https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/docs/v2/policies-annotation/genome-processing/reference-selection/) were selected as taxonomic anchors (Supplementary Data 1). Typically one reference genome was available for each well-defined species. A set of representative genomes from other genera within the Fusobacteriaceae family were retrieved from RefSeq or GenBank database for extended genome-based taxonomic analysis (Supplementary Data 3). In addition 36 genomes excluded from the RefSeq solely due to ‘unverified source organism’ status (indicating incorrect taxonomic assignment rather than quality concerns) were obtained from the NCBI GenBank database (Supplementary Data 7) to validate the newly developed Fusobacterium species identification method. The genome of NCTC15132 (Vestland19T), the type strain of the newly defined species F. paranimalis26, was also included from GenBank for this validation (Supplementary Data 7). All genomes acquired for validation and the non-Fusobacterium genomes were also subjected to quality assessment with CheckM2. All included genomes were derived from cultured strains/isolates and not assembled from metagenomic data.

Bacterial strains

F. pseudoperiodonticum THCT18E1, F. varium strains THCT4E2, THCT4E4, THCT23B1, THCT23E1 and THCT23E3, as well as F. heteroulcerans strains THCT1E1, THCT1E2 and THCT13E1, were previously isolated from CRC tissues22. They were cultivated anaerobically on Columbia agar supplemented with 5% defibrinated sheep blood (Comagal Microbial) at 37 °C22. Notably THCT18E1 had been identified as F. periodonticum, whereas THCT1E1, THCT1E2 and THCT13E1 as F. varium based on16S rRNA genes22. They were reassigned in this study. The strain THCT13E1 was submitted to the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) and China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC) with the deposition numbers CGMCC 46909 and CCTCC M 20253012, respectively.

Whole-genome sequencing of bacterial strains

Genome sequencing and assembly of bacterial strains were conducted at the Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI), Shenzhen. THCT18E1 was sequenced with the PacBio RSII platform (2639× coverage) combined with the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (469× coverage). PBdagcon (version 0.1; https://github.com/PacificBiosciences) was used for subread correction. The genome was assembled with Celera Assembler v8.3 (http://wgs-assembler.sourceforge.net). GATK v1.6 (https://github.com/broadinstitute/gatk) and the SOAP2 package54 were used for single-base correction. THCT1E1, THCT4E2, THCT4E4, THCT13E1 and THCT23E1 were sequenced with the PacBio Sequel II platform (1000× – 3000× coverage) combined with the BGISEQ-500 platform (330× – 450× coverage). Canu v1.5 was used for subread correction and genome assembly55. GATK v3.4 (https://www.broadinstitute.org/gatk/) was used to perform single-base corrections. THCT1E2, THCT23B1 and THCT23E3 were sequenced only with the BGISEQ-500 platform (310× – 375× coverage), and the sequenced reads were assembled with SOAPdenovo v2.0456.

Whole-genome ANI analysis

ANI analysis of the genomes was performed using pyani (version 0.3.0-alpha) with the BLASTN method57. Pairwise ANI values using one as the query and the other as the subject and vice versa were visualised as a heatmap with the gplots package (version 3.3.0; https://github.com/talgalili/gplots) in R (version 4.4.2). A dendrogram was drawn with default parameters.

Whole-genome phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic trees inferred from genome sequences were constructed based on the parsimony method using the kSNP4 (version 4.1)44 with a kmer size of 23 as determined by its integrated tool Kchooser4. All other parameters were set to default. To assess the potential impact of recombination on phylogenetic inference additional trees were built based on SNP loci occurring in at least 25, 50 and 75% of genomes using the ‘-min_frac’ option.

Nucleotide sequence comparison and phylogenetic analyses of makers

Nucleotide sequence comparison was conducted with BLASTN (BLAST+ version 2.9.0). Multiple sequence alignments were performed with MUSCLE v3.8.3158 based on which phylogenetic trees were constructed with the maximum-likelihood method and 1000 bootstrap replicates using IQ-TREE (version 3.0.1)59. The ModelFinder Plus (MFP) option60 was used to automatically select the best-fit nucleotide substitution model by minimising the Bayesian information criterion and the ultrafast bootstrap mode61 was applied. The resulting consensus trees were visualised in MEGA 5.262. The PHI test was performed to examine signals of recombination with PhiPack (version 1.1)63. Nucleotide diversity (π) was estimated with the pegas package (version 1.4)64 in R (version 4.4.2). Pan-Fusobacterium primers gyrB-219F and gyrB-1759R for gyrB were designed within the conserved regions identified through multiple sequence alignment. The gyrB-219F refers to a set of forward primers gyrB-219Fa (5′-WGATAATGGAAGAGGRATACC-3′), gyrB-219Fb (5′-NGATAAYGGDAGAGGDATTCC-3′), gyrB-219Fc (5′-RGACAATGGWAGAGGAATTCC-3′) and gyrB-219Fd (5′-WGATAAYGGAAGGGGWATTCC-3′) while the gyrB-1759R contains reverse primers gyrB-1759Ra (5′-CAAATAATTGGTCAGGATTCAT-3′), gyrB-1759Rb (5′-CCCANARYTGTTCNGGATTCAT-3′) and gyrB-1759Rc (5′-CCCAWARTTGTTCHGGGTTCAT-3′). Similarly pan-Fusobacterium primers rpoB-55F and rpoB-422R were modified from fuso-rpoB-F2/R222. The rpoB-55F contains rpoB-55Fa (5′-GCCACATTTTCTTGAATTTCAATT-3′), rpoB-55Fb (5′-GCCTCATTTTYTDGARTTYCAATT-3′, same to the fuso-rpoB-F2) and rpoB-55Fc (5′-GCCTCAYTTCCTTGAATTYCAATT-3′), while the rpoB-422R contains rpoB-422Ra (5′- ACTACTCTTTCTGCTCCATTTAC-3′), rpoB-422Rb (5′-ACDACTCTTTCHGCHCCATTDAT-3′) and rpoB-422Rc (5′- ACTACTCTTTCWGCTCCGTTTAT-3′). The universal M13F (5′-CGCCAGGGTTTTCCCAGTCACGAC-3′) was added to the 5′ of each forward primer while M13R (5′-AGCGGATAACAATTTCACACAGGA-3′) to the 5′ of each reverse primers, serving as sequencing primers of the amplicons. The sequences of 16S rRNA genes rpoB, gyrB and znpA are provided in Supplementary Data 4. Note that due to the draft nature of certain genomes not all marker gene sequences were available for every RefSeq genome. Specifically while gyrB sequences were available for all 540 genomes, rpoB sequences were available for 539 genomes, znpA for 436 genomes (also due to its natural absence in certain species), and at least one 16S rRNA gene copy for 414 genomes. Whole-genome phylogenetic trees were also constructed based on these genome subsets as described above.

Bacterial genomic DNA preparation

Genomic DNA was prepared from the bacterial strains with the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (TIANGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

PCR was performed with Premix Taq™ (Ex Taq™ Version 2.0 plus dye; Takara) on a Mastercycler nexus gradient thermal cycler (Eppendorf) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In each reaction 10 ng of bacterial genomic DNA was used, and 30 cycles of 30 s at 98 °C, 30 s at 50 °C and 90 s at 72 °C were applied. PCR products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and then sequenced by the Sanger method at Saiheng Biotech.

Integration of the revised taxonomy with GTDB classifications

GTDB taxonomic assignments and representative strain information for the genomes were retrieved via direct accession number mapping to the GTDB database49, as of 23 June 2025. These assignments were then mapped to the revised Fusobacterium species taxonomy and summarised at the species level.

Integration of the revised taxonomy with MetaPhlAn SGBs

Genomes were aligned against the MetaPhlAn SGB database (vJan25_202503)50 with BLAST+ version 2.9.0. The best-matched SGB (highest sequence identity and longest alignment coverage) of each genome if available, was selected. The sequences of the selected SGBs were then extracted and subjected to ANI analysis against the genomes with pyani (version 0.3.0-alpha) as described above57. The mean ANI value between each SGB and all genomes assigned to a Fusobacterium species was calculated as a species-level ANI value. The ANI values were visualised as heatmaps.

Statistics

Frequency distributions of ANI values (bin width = 0.01%) and nucleotide identity (bin width = 0.05%) were analysed with GraphPad Prism (version 7). The ANI discontinuity was estimated following a method similar to Passarelli‑Araujo et al.43. Briefly all pairwise ANI values were sorted in descending order. The first derivative (Δ) of ANI with respect to the rank position (j) was then calculated as the change in ANI between two consecutive ranks j and j + 1, divided by the rank difference: Δ = (ANIj–ANIj + 1)/(j + 1 − j) = ANIj–ANIj + 1. The ANI discontinuity (δ) was defined as the maximum Δ among ANI values > 90% (δ = max{Δ|ANI ∈ [0.90, 1.00]}), representing a break or sharp change. A rarefaction curve-like analysis was performed to evaluate whether the current genome number was sufficient for ANI gap detection. Genomes were randomly subsampled at sizes of 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, 400, 450, 500 and 540 using the ‘sample’ function in R (version 4.4.2), with 100 replicates per size. The upper and lower limits of the ANI gap for each replicate were determined through frequency distribution analysis. Normalised quartet scores (NGS), which quantified the proportion of shared evolutionary relationships (quartets) between two trees, were calculated with ASTRAL v5.7.865 to assess overall topological concordance between the kSNP-derived tree and ANI-derived dendrogram or phylogenetic trees of individual markers. For each marker, we also computed the NQS for all trees generated during bootstrap resampling. The distributions of these bootstrap NQS values were then compared across markers using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test, as applicable. To compare nucleotide diversity (π) across markers, we employed a bootstrap-based difference test. For each marker, bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was performed. Crucially whereas the NQS comparison utilised bootstrapping to approximate the sampling distribution of the statistic itself, the π comparison employed bootstrapping to evaluate the uncertainty of a parameter estimate. Therefore for each marker pair, we calculated the distribution of differences by subtracting the bootstrap π values of one marker from those of the other. Statistical significance was determined by examining whether the 95% confidence interval of this difference distribution contained zero. The corresponding two-sided p- value was calculated as twice the proportion of bootstrap differences on the smaller side of zero, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. All NQS and π comparisons were performed only on the subset of genomes that contained complete sequences for the markers being analysed. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.
