Risk assessment of heavy metals in north of Iran (Sari) rice and implications for human health
Authors: Ali Zafarzadeh, Ali Shahryari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice, as a global staple food, requires safety monitoring to protect public health. This study evaluated heavy metal contamination in rice crops in Sari, Iran using hazard quotients (HQ) and cancer risk assessment (ILCR). In this study, 144 rice samples were collected—comprising 48 samples from each of the three dominant local brands (H, Sh, and B)—across 16 rice mills situated in the four cardinal directions (north, south, east, west) of Sari (Iran) between June and September 2022. The concentration of Lead (Pb), Cadmium (Cd), Copper (Cu) and Zinc (Zn) was performed using a Metrohm 797 VA Computrace polarographic trace analyzer (Metrohm, Germany). Results showed mean concentrations of 2.77 ± 2.14 (Pb), < 0.0001 (Cd), 3.95 ± 2.11 (Cu), and 11.73 ± 5.62 mg/kg (Zn), with Pb exceeding safety limits (0.2 mg/kg) in all samples. Risk assessment indicated significant non-carcinogenic risk from Pb (HQ > 1), while other metals posed acceptable risks. Spatial analysis revealed Pb hotspots near the Caspian Sea (9.88 mg/kg) versus southern regions (0.38 mg/kg), reflecting anthropogenic pressures from tourism and wastewater irrigation in coastal areas. Contamination patterns followed land use: northern highways (Pb), eastern farms (Zn/Cu from fertilizers), and urban centers (multiple metals). These findings mirror global coastal pollution dynamics, showing cleaner rural versus polluted urban-adjacent fields. We recommend immediate agricultural interventions including source identification and remediation strategies, particularly for Pb mitigation. Comprehensive coordination between environmental management and agricultural practices represents the optimal approach for simultaneously protecting human health and ecosystem stability in Mazandaran vital rice production areas.Keywords: Food safety, Rice, Lead, Cadmium, Copper, Zinc, IranSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Risk factors

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a vital food crop for nearly half of the global population, with the highest levels of consumption occurring in Asia and Europe1. In Iran, rice is estimated to be the second-largest source of calories in the human diet, after wheat-based products such as bread2. It is also a significant source of energy, vitamins, and essential elements; moderate consumption may improve digestion and promote heart health. However, excessive intake of refined rice can increase the risk of obesity and diabetes due to its high glycemic index3. Rice’s role in achieving the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)—particularly in eradicating hunger and poverty, alongside other critical targets—is undeniable4. Consequently, the systematic evaluation of rice hygiene quality and strict compliance with health benchmarks are crucial not only for safeguarding public health but also for ensuring the long-term ecological balance and resilience of agricultural ecosystems within a sustainable development framework5.

The accumulation and biomagnification of toxic heavy metals in rice ecosystems pose a dual threat to both human health and agricultural sustainability. These persistent contaminants disrupt physiological systems in humans and impair the ecological balance of soil6. In this context, soils are an important indicator of environmental quality as they act as sinks and sources for metals and other pollutants. Soils polluted with metals can threaten human health, harm soil microbial communities, contaminate water resources (surface and ground), compromise food quality, and damage flora and fauna7. Under EPA guidelines, lead (Pb) is categorized as a potent neurotoxicant with no established safe exposure threshold, as even trace levels can induce childhood cognitive deficits and adult hypertension8. Cadmium (Cd), recognized as a Group 1 human carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), is of particular concern due to its extraordinary biological half-life of 10–30 years and its preferential accumulation in renal and osseous tissues9. Although copper (Cu) and zinc (Zn) are essential micronutrients, supra-nutritional exposure can trigger hepatotoxicity, gastrointestinal dysfunction, and immunological disruption, with significant implications for agricultural sustainability due to their transfer through the food chain10,11.

Several studies have highlighted that heavy metal contamination in rice is a major global concern, establishing this staple food as a significant route for human exposure to hazardous elements, which poses serious risks to public health and agricultural sustainability12. Lin et al. reported substantial heavy metal contamination in rice, underscoring potential health hazards for consumers in their respective study areas13. The findings of Mohammadi et al. (2024) indicated that the heavy metal contamination in southwestern Iran’s rice crops likely originates from multiple pollution sources, including industrial discharge, agrochemical inputs (e.g., pesticides and fertilizers), and transport-related emissions14. Additionally, a study by Ramezani et al. on heavy metal concentrations in Iranian and imported rice consumed in Neyshabur, Iran, revealed that among the tested metals (Cu, Pb, Ni, Cr, Zn, Mn, Cd, Co, and As), Zn had the highest mean concentration (14.21 mg/kg), while Cd showed the lowest (0.02 mg/kg). A total hazard quotient (HQ) > 1 for all metals indicated a potential non-carcinogenic risk. Furthermore, As, Ni, and Cr posed unsafe carcinogenic risks (up to 7.00 × 10−3), whereas the risks from Pb and Cd were negligible (up to 1.49 × 10−5)15. Similarly, Cui et al. (2022) quantified heavy metals in rice grains across Hunan Province, China, reporting mean concentrations (mg/kg) of 0.48 (As), 1.28 (Cr), 0.03 (Co), 0.84 (Ni), 2.39 (Cu), 15.73 (Zn), 0.28 (Cd), 0.66 (Pb), and 0.0043 (Sb). Their Source Fingerprint and Pollution Index (SFPI) analysis identified As and Pb as universally present (100% detection frequency), followed by Cr (64.70%), Ni (47.05%), and Cd (44.12%). The assessment of carcinogenic risk prioritized the metals as Cd > Ni > As > Cr > Pb, with the risk levels for all exceeding the 1.0 × 10−4 safety limit16.

Given the widespread global consumption of rice and its high potential for heavy metal accumulation, monitoring these contaminants is not only a preventive measure but also a public health imperative for reducing the burden of chronic diseases associated with environmental pollution. In particular, nowadays, fertilizers and herbicides are used intensively in agriculture to increase rice yields. However, these agrochemicals often contain significant amounts of heavy metals, and their application contributes directly to the accumulation of these metals in rice crops17. In Iran, Mazandaran Province, located in the north of the country, is a primary hub for rice production. However, the intensive focus on cultivating high-yielding rice varieties in Mazandaran, frequently without adequate environmental safeguards, has led to undesirable ecological consequences18. Despite numerous studies on heavy metal contamination in rice globally, comprehensive data is critically lacking for high-yielding varieties specifically cultivated in the Sari region of Mazandaran. While previous research in Iran (e.g., Mohammadi et al.14 and Ramezani et al.15) has provided valuable insights, most have neither evaluated the health risks for both children and adults using US EPA methods, nor linked the contamination levels directly to the intensive agricultural practices (fertilizers, herbicides) prevalent in this high-production hub. Furthermore, the status of Sari rice as a key national commodity necessitates a dedicated assessment to protect both consumer health and its economic reputation. Therefore, this study addresses this critical gap through a focused investigation of the most consumed high-yielding varieties in Sari, providing the first comprehensive health risk assessment for the local population and offering source-specific insights crucial for developing targeted remediation strategies in this agriculturally significant zone.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Sari, the capital and most populous city of Mazandaran Province in northern Iran, situated at geographic coordinates 36°33′38″N, 53°03′30″E. The region is characterized by a hot-summer Mediterranean climate, with average annual temperatures ranging from 7 (minimum) to 27 °C (maximum). The area receives an annual precipitation of approximately 864 mm, distributed over 109 rainy days. Encompassing an area of roughly 60 km2, the city has a population of approximately 330,000 inhabitants19. The spatial distribution of the sampling stations is presented in Fig. 1.

Sample collection and laboratory analysis

In this study, a total of 144 rice samples were collected from 16 rice mills situated in the four cardinal directions (north, south, east, and west) of Sari between June and September 2022. The samples comprised 48 samples from each of the three dominant local cultivars (H, Sh, and B). From each brand, approximately 100 g of sample was placed in pre-cleaned glass bottles. Prior to use, the bottles were washed with 1% nitric acid and subsequently rinsed three times with deionized distilled water to prevent potential contamination. After collection, samples were immediately stored in dark, temperature-controlled boxes (~ 4 °C) and transported within two hours to the Environmental Chemistry Laboratory in ice-packed coolers to preserve sample integrity.

The concentrations of Cd, Pb, Cu, and Zn in the rice samples were measured using the following procedure: First, grains were thoroughly cleaned with deionized water to remove any adhered particulate matter and then oven-dried at 80 °C for 1 h until a constant weight was achieved. Samples were digested using a modified acid-assisted protocol. Briefly, 1–2 g of the dried sample was accurately weighed into a 100 mL round-bottom flask, mixed with 10 mL of concentrated HNO3 (69%, analytical grade), and heated to 120 °C. During heating, 1 mL of H2O2 (30%, analytical grade) was added incrementally until digestion was complete, as evidenced by the formation of a clear, colorless solution. The resulting digestate was then quantitatively transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask and diluted to the mark with distilled water. Heavy metal analysis was performed using a Metrohm 797 VA Computrace polarographic trace analyzer (Metrohm, Germany)20.

All chemical reagents used were of high-purity analytical grade, purchased from Merck, Germany. For quality assurance and control (QA/QC), 10% of the samples were analyzed in duplicate to assess methodological precision. The accuracy of the method was verified through standard spike recovery tests, which yielded satisfactory recoveries exceeding 92% for all target metals.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) with a two-tailed significance threshold of α = 0.05. Concentrations below the method detection limit (DL) were substituted with a value of DL/√2 to facilitate statistical computation. This approach minimizes bias in parameter estimation for left-censored data and is recommended by environmental statistics guidelines [X, Y]. Following verification of data normality (Shapiro–Wilk test, p > 0.05) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test), we performed comprehensive analyses including: (1) Descriptive statistics (reported as mean ± standard deviation with 95% confidence intervals and ranges); (2) One-way ANOVA tests to identify significant differences among rice brands; (3) Independent samples t-tests to compare measured values against FAO/WHO safety standards.

Cd concentrations were below the instrument’s detection limit (DL = 0.01 mg/kg wet weight) in all samples. In contrast, the average concentrations of Zn (p = 0.99) and Cu (p = 0.07) were not significantly different among the three brands (Table 1).

Our USEPA-compliant risk assessment28 identified rice consumption as a critical exposure pathway for heavy metals, with particularly alarming Pb risks for children. The analysis revealed a THQ of up to 3.399—significantly exceeding the safety threshold of 1—and a CR of 3.43 × 10−3, which is three orders of magnitude above the 10−6 negligible risk benchmark. The ADI for Pb was calculated at 0.0122 mg/kg/day, a value 3.3-fold higher than the RfD of 0.0037 mg/kg/day. The analysis revealed a THQ of up to 3.399 -significantly exceeding the > 1 safety threshold), an ADI of 0.0122 mg/kg/day (3.3-fold above the 0.0037 mg/kg/day RfD25, and a CR of 3.43 × 10−3—three orders of magnitude above the 10−6 negligible risk benchmark. In contrast, Cu (ADI: 0.0175 mg/kg/day) and Zn (ADI: 0.0518 mg/kg/day) concentrations remained below WHO/FAO safety standards (0.04 and 0.3 mg/kg/day, respectively)29, while Cd was undetectable (< 0.01 mg/kg). The persistent and significant Pb contamination necessitates immediate intervention through soil remediation in agricultural fields, enhanced regulatory monitoring of rice products, and targeted nutritional education for at-risk child populations30. This widespread soil contamination likely originates from multiple sources. As soils are a well-established indicator of environmental quality7, the possible sources of Pb contamination in the Sari region include atmospheric deposition from vehicle emissions and industrial activities, as well as the historical application of certain fertilizers and agrochemicals17, which can introduce heavy metals into agricultural systems. Therefore, mitigating this threat requires not only managing direct exposure but also addressing the primary source of contamination: the soil itself. Discussion

The presence of heavy metals in rice constitutes a critical global food safety challenge, necessitating rigorous monitoring and management to mitigate consumer health risks. This concern is amplified by the crop’s well-documented propensity for heavy metal bioaccumulation in contaminated agroecosystems31. Our findings align with this global issue, revealing alarmingly high Pb concentrations in rice from Mazandaran, with 100% of tested samples exceeding the FAO/WHO permissible limit (0.2 mg/kg). This widespread Pb contamination underscores a significant public health threat, particularly for populations with high rice consumption. Our results are consistent with previous national studies reporting elevated Pb levels in rice from Mazandaran Province2,17. The elevated heavy metal levels detected likely stem from anthropogenic activities, primarily the excessive application of agricultural chemicals and the discharge of untreated urban and industrial wastewater into irrigation systems32,33. This pattern is not unique to Iran; similarly elevated Pb concentrations have been documented in rice grains from other major producing regions, including Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, and China34.

Our findings on Pb contamination are consistent with global studies that reveal complex contamination pathways. For instance, Liu et al.35 demonstrated that atmospheric deposition, geological sources, fertilizers, and irrigation contribute variably to soil Pb accumulation. A striking finding from their work indicates that 76–98% of Pb in rice grains originated from atmospheric deposition—primarily from mining and smelting emissions—while soil contributed only 0–26%. This underscores that atmospheric pollutants can pose a substantially greater risk to grain contamination than soil-mediated transfer. Given Pb’s non-biodegradability, extreme toxicity, and environmental persistence, chronic low-level exposure promotes its bioaccumulation in vital organs. This can lead to irreversible neurological deficits, renal failure, and carcinogenesis8,29,36. Therefore, our findings underscore the imperative for precise source apportionment in Mazandaran—particularly discriminating between atmospheric and soil-derived Pb—to develop targeted mitigation strategies. Furthermore, local geogenic factors, such as soil type, also play a crucial role in metal bioavailability, as demonstrated by Mirzae et al. in Iranian topsoils37. This further complicates the contamination picture. Implementing evidence-based interventions tailored to the specific sources and local conditions is, therefore, critical to disrupt these contamination pathways and safeguard public health.

Our results demonstrated Cd concentrations below the detection limit (0.01 mg/kg) in all rice samples from Sari. This finding is consistent with research from nearby regions. Zazouli et al.38 reported similarly negligible Cd levels (< 0.01 mg/kg) in rice from Babol County in Mazandaran Province (located less than 30 km away), while Mohammadi et al.14 documented low concentrations (0.0165–0.0295 mg/kg) in southwestern Iran. Although Sharafati Chaleshtori et al.17 observed a wider range of Cd variations (0.008–0.3802 mg/kg) across different Iranian farms, the consistently low to non-detectable levels in our study area suggest minimal local contamination. Crucially, Yan et al.39 identified industrial dust as the dominant source of Cd in rice (87%), with substantially smaller contributions from fertilizers (9%) and automobile exhaust (4%). This source apportionment strongly supports our conclusion that any potential Cd contamination in Sari’s rice fields is more likely to originate from urban and industrial atmospheric deposition (e.g., via wastewater or dust) rather than from agricultural inputs. This highlights the critical need for targeted industrial emission controls and air quality management in the region to safeguard agricultural productivity.

Cu concentrations exhibited remarkable consistency across all tested brands (mean ≈ 3.95 mg/kg), remaining well below the WHO/FAO safety threshold of 10 mg/kg40. Zn levels showed greater variability, with Brand Sh demonstrating the highest concentration (13.52 ± 5.69 mg/kg); however, all measured values remained significantly lower than the stringent EU regulatory limit of 50 mg/kg. These results align with international benchmarks, which report typical concentrations of approximately 2.31 mg/kg for Cu and 15.43 mg/kg for Zn in commercial rice16,41. This confirms that the rice from the study area possesses both nutritional value and a high margin of safety. The observed patterns primarily reflect regional pedogeochemical baselines, which govern natural metal availability, coupled with standardized agricultural practices—such as fertilizer application and water management—across the cultivation areas16,41. The stability of these essential micronutrients across different brands suggests homogeneous growing conditions. Furthermore, their consistent presence at concentrations that are below regulatory limits yet within nutritionally relevant ranges indicates balanced and effective agroecosystem management in the study region.

Spatial analysis of Pb contamination (Fig. 2) revealed significant geographical heterogeneity. The highest concentrations were identified in northern downstream areas near the Caspian Sea (up to 9.88 mg/kg), contrasting sharply with lower levels in southern upstream regions (as low as 0.38 mg/kg). This north–south gradient reflects intensified anthropogenic pressures in the coastal zone, likely driven by tourism-related vehicle emissions and the use of wastewater for irrigation. In contrast, the cleaner southern upstream areas benefit from greater distance from pollution sources and potentially favorable wind patterns that disperse contaminants. Spatial hotspots aligned strongly with specific land-use types: northern sectors exhibited elevated Pb along major highways (potentially from historic leaded fuel residues), eastern farmlands showed Zn/Cu accumulation (likely from phosphate fertilizer runoff), and central urban areas concentrated multiple metals due to high road network density and traffic emissions42.This distribution pattern mirrors observations in global coastal cities, where industrial activity, traffic density, and hydrology are primary drivers of metal deposition42. Although brand-specific variations were observed, a consistent trend emerged: upstream fields demonstrated systematically lower contamination. This finding supports Yang et al.’s43 model of cleaner agricultural peripheries versus polluted urban-industrial cores. The non-uniform contamination profile underscores how Sari’s historical land use, current agricultural practices, and urban infrastructure collectively shape heavy metal distribution. The northern zone emerges as a Pb hotspot, the eastern zone shows enrichment of Zn/Cu, and the central urban area acts as a multi-metal sink, highlighting the need for targeted, location-specific remediation strategies.

Our health risk assessment, conducted using U.S. EPA methodologies, revealed significant public health concerns from heavy metal exposure via rice consumption in Sari, Iran, with Pb posing the most severe risk28,44. Both non-carcinogenic (Hazard Index, HI) and carcinogenic risk (CR) values exceeded safety thresholds. Children exhibited particularly alarming vulnerability (THQ = 3.399; CR* = 3.43E−03) compared to adults (THQ = 1.209; CR* = 1.22E−03), a pattern consistent with findings from industrial zones such as Huludao, China45, and Catalonia, Spain44. Children’s heightened susceptibility is attributable to key physiological factors, including lower body weight and higher calorie and food intake per kilogram46. Although food preparation methods (e.g., washing, which may reduce Pb content by ~ 35%) and human digestive processes can partially mitigate bioavailability and subsequent internal exposure47–49, the cumulative long-term risks remain a serious concern50. These findings align with global studies that establish rice as a major dietary pathway for heavy metal exposure1,24,28. This underscores the urgent need for targeted public health interventions, including stringent source control, dietary diversification, and dedicated monitoring programs, particularly for vulnerable pediatric populations.

Study limitations

This research had several important limitations that should be acknowledged: (1) the assessment focused primarily on the analysis of heavy metals in rice grains. Key contamination sources—such as irrigation water, soil composition, or chemical fertilizers—were not evaluated, which is crucial for a complete understanding of the exposure pathway and for developing effective mitigation strategies. (2) The study relied on measured total metal concentrations and did not assess heavy metal bioavailability, which is highly dependent on local soil chemistry (e.g., pH, organic matter content) and specific agricultural practices. (3) Potential variations in metal absorption during human digestion were not verified experimentally; instead, the risk assessment relied on standardized exposure factors from international guidelines, which may not fully reflect the local population’s physiology and dietary habits. (4) While Cd concentrations were confirmed to be below the detection limit, this finding is specific to the sampled grains and time period. The potential for low-level or episodic Cd contamination related to future changes in agricultural or industrial practices cannot be ruled out. (5) The analysis measured metal concentrations in raw rice and did not comprehensively evaluate the reduction or transformation of metals across different traditional cooking methods, which could significantly alter actual dietary exposure. These limitations highlight the need for future research to incorporate agricultural source analysis, multi-pathway exposure assessment, region-specific bioavailability studies, and long-term environmental monitoring to establish more accurate and dynamic risk profiles.

Conclusion

This investigation identifies significant public health hazards associated with Pb contamination in rice cultivated in Mazandaran Province, Iran, with pediatric populations demonstrating particularly elevated vulnerability—showing approximately 2.8 times greater exposure risk compared to adult consumers. Quantitative risk evaluation yielded alarming results, as both THQ values (reaching 3.40 for children and 1.21 for adults) and carcinogenic risk estimates (3.43 × 10−3) substantially surpassed established safety limits. To address these critical concerns, we urgently recommend implementing comprehensive Pb monitoring systems across agricultural supply chains, introducing soil remediation techniques and regulated fertilizer use in rice-growing areas to reduce metal uptake, and fostering interdisciplinary collaborations among researchers, policymakers, and farmers to translate scientific findings into actionable solutions. Ultimately, coordinated integration of environmental management and agricultural practices represents the most effective strategy for simultaneously safeguarding human health and ensuring ecosystem stability in Mazandaran’s vital rice production regions.
