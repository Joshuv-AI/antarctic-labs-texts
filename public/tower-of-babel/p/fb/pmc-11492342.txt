Application of Second‐Generation Sequencing Technology in Lower Respiratory Tract Infection
Authors: Chong Wang, Shuo Yang, Qi Liu, Hongchao Liu, Shangjia Jin, Jiajia Zheng, Xiumei Xiao, Xin Hou, Jing Li, Sisi Ma, Liyan Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) has long been an important threat to people's life and health, so the rapid diagnosis of LRTI is of great significance in clinical treatment. In recent years, the development of the sequencing technology provides a new direction for the rapid diagnosis of LRTI. In this review, the advantages and disadvantages of second‐generation sequencing techniques represented by metagenomics next‐generation sequencing (mNGS) and droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) in LRTI were reviewed. Furthermore, it offers insights into the future trajectory of this technology, highlighting its potential to revolutionise the field of respiratory infection diagnostics.ObjectiveThis review summarises developments in mechanistic research of second‐generation sequencing technology their relationship with clinical practice, providing insights for future research.MethodsAuthors conducted a search on PubMed and Web of Science using the professional terms ‘Lower respiratory tract infection’ and ‘droplet digital polymerase chain reaction’ and ‘metagenomics next generation sequencing’. The obtained literature was then roughly categorised based on their research content. Similar studies were grouped into the same sections, and further searches were conducted based on the keywords of each section.ResultsDifferent studies discussed the application of second‐generation sequencing technology in LRTI from different angles, including the detection of pathogens of LRTI by mNGS and ddPCR, the prediction ability of drug‐resistant bacteria, and comparison with traditional methods. We try to analyse the advantages and disadvantages of the second‐generation sequencing technology by combing the research results of mNGS and ddPCR. In addition, the development direction of the second‐generation sequencing technology is prospected.Keywords: droplet digital polymerase chain reaction, lower respiratory tract infection, metagenomics next‐generation sequencing, pathogen analysis, prediction of drug resistance

Introduction

Respiratory infections refer to those caused by pathogenic microorganisms invading and multiplying within the respiratory tract. According to its location, it can be divided into upper respiratory tract infection and lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI). The former includes rhinitis, pharyngitis and laryngitis. The latter include bronchitis, bronchitis and pneumonia. LRTI refers to an infection that occurs in the trachea, main bronchi and all levels of bronchi in the lung, including community‐acquired pneumonia, hospital‐acquired pneumonia, bronchitis, bronchiolitis and tracheitis. Recently, the morbidity and mortality rates of LRTIs have been gradually increasing. Statistics from 2015 indicate that 2.74 million individuals worldwide died from LRTI, making it the fifth leading cause of death globally [1]. Notably, the COVID‐19 pandemic has resulted in a significant surge in LRTI cases worldwide, emphasising the need for heightened research attention in this area.

As we all know, the cause of LRTI may be a variety of bacteria, fungi, viruses may be the cause of LRTI. Studies have shown that severe pulmonary infections are often accompanied by multiple pathogenic bacteria and drug‐resistant pathogens. Conventional detection methods (such as Microbial culture and drug sensitivity identification) can only diagnose common pathogens, and gradually cannot meet the clinical needs of rapid and accurate diagnosis of LRTI. According to a study by Zhu et al. [2], nearly half of the patients with community‐acquired pneumonia in China have unknown causes during clinical treatment, which also indicates that accurate and rapid diagnosis of the lower respiratory tract by conventional detection methods is difficult to achieve in clinical treatment. With the use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics and the gradual increase of pathogen resistance, the empirical and prophylactic use of antibiotics has become the clinical norm [3, 4], but it is not guaranteed that the empirical treatment of the past can have a reliable effect in the clinical treatment of the future.

The second‐generation sequencing technology represented by mNGS and droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) has a broad prospect in the diagnosis and drug resistance of LRTI pathogens. It is expected to rapidly detect the distribution of LRTI pathogens, identify possible resistance genes, and reduce unnecessary broad‐spectrum antimicrobial therapy. At present, mNGS and ddPCR have been widely used in clinical diagnosis and treatment. This paper reviews the research progress of traditional detection techniques, mNGS and ddPCR in detection of LRTI pathogens and drug resistance genes. Firstly, the application status and shortcomings of several traditional LRTI detection methods are summarised. Secondly, we discussed the advantages and disadvantages of LRTI new generation sequencing technology represented by mNGS and ddPCR. Finally, the development prospect of second‐generation sequencing technology is summarised.

Application and Current Situation of Routine Detection Methods in LRTI

The traditional detection methods of Lower respiratory tract infection include qPCR, microbial culture, nucleic acid hybridisation and serological antibody detection, pathological detection and so on. As one of the conventional detection methods, qPCR has a long turnaround time, so it needs to design specific probes and primers for specific gene fragments of pathogenic microorganisms, and its detection ability for unknown pathogenic microorganisms is limited [5]. Microbial culture is a widely used detection method, but the detection results may be different because of the differences in laboratory operation and interpretation. Limited by the availability of the detection targets, it is difficult to meet the needs of clinical diagnosis when combined with multiple bacterial infections. Serological antibody detection requires the clinician to complete blood collection before antibiotic treatment, although the specificity is high, the sensitivity is poor (20%), and limited by the existence of the window period, the detection ability of pathogen infection in the window period is poor [6]. As the gold standard for the detection of fungal infection, pathological detection cannot meet the needs of rapid clinical diagnosis because of its time‐consuming, invasive and lack of pathogen specificity. For atypical pathogens (such as chlamydia, mycoplasma, Legionella), conventional detection methods are difficult to detect quickly. In addition, the standardised retention of sputum samples is more difficult for most patients, which leads to doubts about the authenticity of the test results. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid seems to be a good sample type, but the collection of BALF is often invasive and expensive, which may lead to resistance in patients.

Lower respiratory tract infection account for one‐fifth of the mortality rate among children aged under 5 years globally [7]. Among the elderly and people with low immunity, LRTI also has a very high morbidity and mortality [8]. Due to a long testing time, lack of diagnostic ability for multiple pathogens and poor detection for rare pathogens, traditional detection methods are gradually unable to meet the needs of clinical diagnosis. At present, clinicians' use of drugs and antibiotics in patients with LRTI are mostly empirical, which may increase the risk of infection and lead to the emergence of multiple drug‐resistant pathogens [9]. Due to the above reasons, the application of new detection technology in the detection of LRTI is particularly necessary. Pathogen Identification of LRTI

Application of mNGS in Identifying Pathogens of LRTI

The earliest clinical application of mNGS can be traced back to 2013. Wilson et al. [18] diagnosed a 14‐year‐old critical child with neuroleptospirosis by using mNGS, which proved the feasibility of mNGS in clinical diagnosis for the first time. In recent years, with the continuous progress of sequencing technology, mNGS has been widely used in the detection of pathogens and drug resistance genes in the fields of nervous system infection, hematological infection, LRTI and so on, and has shown great potential (Table 2).

The pathogens of LRTI are various, bacteria, viruses, fungi and so on are the possible causes of LRTI. Therefore, the ability of mNGS to detect pathogens should be comprehensively evaluated in different dimensions. A 2021 study by Zheng et al. showed that mNGS has a high diagnostic value in LRTI. The sensitivity of mNGS and its diagnostic ability for unknown pathogens and mixed infections are superior to traditional detection methods [23].

We found that mNGS seems to show stronger detection ability than conventional methods in the detection of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and atypical pathogens. In the 2020 study by Fang et al. mNGS also performed much better than traditional detection techniques: of the 72 patients with pulmonary infection, 30 were negative for traditional aetiology and positive for mNGS. For the virus infection which cannot be detected in time by traditional etiological detection, mNGS often has a better performance [19]. In a prospective study of 100 cases of LRTIs in 2020, the sensitivity of mNGS was much higher than that of traditional methods (95% vs. 54%, p < 0.001), and an additional 14 viruses that could not be detected by traditional methods were detected [10]. A prospective study by Zhang et al. in 2022 showed that mNGS is often superior to traditional methods in detecting patients infected with multiple pathogens. 84 kinds of bacteria, eight kinds of fungi, 16 kinds of viruses and two kinds of atypical pathogens were detected in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of 47 patients with LRTI. In contrast, only 12 species of bacteria and four species of fungi were identified by traditional culture methods [11].

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid is considered to be an excellent sample type in the diagnosis of LRTI, and its detection accuracy is higher than that of other sample types (mainly sputum). In the study of BALF samples from 112 children with pneumonia by Yang et al. [24], we found that the detection ability of mNGS in BALF samples also had obvious advantages over conventional methods. The positive rate of mNGS diagnosis (91.07%, 95% CI 83.80% vs. 95.40%) and diagnosis coincidence rate (72.32%, 95% CI 62.93% vs. 80.15%) were significantly higher than those of conventional detection methods (70.54%, 95% CI 61.06% vs. 78.58% and 56.25%, 95% CI 46.57% vs. 65.50%) (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05). After adjusting antibiotic therapy based on both mNGS and routine methods, 109 cases (97.32%) exhibited improvement. This shows that mNGS of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) has a great advantage in the etiological diagnosis of pneumonia in children, which is helpful for clinicians to adjust antibiotic treatment in time, improve the prognosis of patients and reduce mortality.

Due to the destruction of the immune system, there is a significantly elevated risk of lower respiratory tract opportunistic infections and multiple infections among patients infected with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Studies by Liu et al. have shown that mNGS also has significant advantages in detecting LRTIs in HIV patients. In this study, more bacteria (25 vs. 2), fungi (5 vs. 3) and viruses (9 vs. 2) were identified by mNGS, and the positive coincidence rate with clinical diagnosis was significantly higher than that of conventional microbiological tests (75.6% vs. 43.0%) [25].

The biggest advantage of mNGS is in the detection of rare or potential pathogens for LRTI. Langelier et al. tested mNGS in 92 patients with acute respiratory failure. Among the patients with positive pathogenic microorganisms, the results of mNGS detection were in good agreement with the results of routine detection, which indicated that the results of mNGS had good reliability. Of the patients with negative pathogen test results, 62% had potential pathogenic microorganisms that were not detected by routine tests. Of those with unknown causes, 42% were found to have suspected disease‐causing microbes. This study demonstrates the accuracy of mNGS in screening respiratory pathogens and evaluating potential infection factors. However, it is unfortunate that there exists a discrepancy in the interpretation of mNGS detection results [12].

Fungal infection of the lower respiratory tract is a serious respiratory disease. Timely and accurate diagnosis and treatment have a positive effect on improving the quality of life and prognosis of patients. Conventional detection methods (mainly microbial culture) have limited ability to detect fungi. In the study of Fang et al., the sensitivity of mNGS to fungal detection was 71.9% (95% CI 61.5%–82.3%), and the specificity was 77.5% (95% CI 67.9%–87.1%). Among the routine culture negative samples, 9 out of 40 bronchoalveolar lavage fluid samples were positive by mNGS analysis. This study showed that the application of mNGS to detect fungi was better than the traditional culture method [19].

Although the application of mNGS in LRTI is relatively extensive, we found that compared with conventional detection methods, the application of mNGS is still relatively limited in the world, which is caused by its expensive price and relatively high‐end technical requirements. So far, the largest research on mNGS is Miao et al., this study shows that mNGS is not superior to conventional detection methods in the detection of common bacteria, but better than conventional detection methods in the diagnosis of infectious diseases. Its sensitivity and specificity are 50.7% and 85.7%, respectively, which is superior to the culture method, especially in Mycobacterium tuberculosis and virus (mNGS only). Excellent in anaerobes (p < 0.01), anaerobes (p < 0.01) and fungi (p < 0.01) [26].

Although mNGS has a wide application prospect in LRTI, mNGS itself has the problem of high false positive rate. In the prospective study of Huang et al., the positive rate of mNGS was 88.30%, while the positive rate of traditional detection methods was only 25.73%. Although mNGS could effectively and quickly identify pathogens, the specificity was slightly lower than that of traditional detection methods (81.6% vs. mNGS 88.41%) [13]. At present, the biggest difficulty of mNGS is to identify the high false positive rate of pathogens. The larger the genome tested, the higher the false positive rate calculated. How to explain the test results of mNGS and how to treat the differences between mNGS and conventional detection methods for patients are the problems that mNGS needs to face and solve.

The research of Liu et al. has put forward a solution to the problem of high false positive rate of mNGS. The identification of pathogens by mNGS is mainly based on three indicators: sequencing readings, gene coverage and relative abundance of organisms. Liu et al. [20] described these three indicators systematically. In this study, Liu et al. used ROC curve to detect three indicators of true positive pathogens: sequencing readings (in order to eliminate the effects of sequencing depth and gene length on different pathogens, the sequencing readings were replaced with logarithms per thousand bases, and the concept of logarithm per thousand bases [lg (RPKM)] was introduced), genome coverage and relative abundance of organisms. The areas under the ROC curve of the three groups were 0.99, 0.98, 0.83, respectively. Therefore, we can find that Ig (RPKM) and genome coverage are effective in distinguishing true positive pathogens from false positive pathogens. Because lg (RPKM) has excellent recognition performance, this study uses lg (RPKM) threshold to determine false positive viruses. Of the 61 pathogens identified in this study, 35 were true positive according to the lg (RPKM) threshold. However, due to the influence of the total number of genomes in the sample, the relative abundance has poor ability to distinguish true positive pathogens from false positive pathogens. Although Liu et al. provided ideas for solving the problem of high false positive rate of mNGS, the judgment of true and false positive bacteria of mNGS is still one of the difficult problems that need to be overcome. Exploring better detection indicators and reducing the false positive rate of mNGS is the main development direction of mNGS in the future.

Another technical difficulty of mNGS is the decrease in diagnostic sensitivity in high‐throughput background, which is usually due to a small sample size of pathogens and an increase in the misdetection of possible pollutants in high‐throughput background [14]. Application of ddPCR in Identifying Pathogens of LRTI

Since the inception of ddPCR, the scientific community has been diligently investigating its potential clinical applications [28, 29]. Compared with the previous sequencing technology, ddPCR has a significant increase in sensitivity and specificity, especially in the detection of low‐abundance nucleic acid with limited detection ability of qPCR, ddPCR can often achieve more ideal results [15]. Rigorous research has demonstrated that ddPCR offers enhanced speed and precision in diagnostic outcomes among individuals infected with M. tuberculosis. Consequently, this technology significantly diminishes the duration required for tuberculosis detection, thereby facilitating informed clinical decision‐making and medication administration [30]. For critical patients who need mechanical ventilation, patients with immune deficiency, the application of ddPCR to quickly and accurately detect the types of pathogens will be of great help to clinical medication and treatment.

Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction is an improved technique based on traditional PCR. Compared with traditional PCR, ddPCR achieves absolute quantitative detection of nucleic acid molecules. It has the advantages of high sensitivity, specificity, repeatability and good tolerance to PCR inhibitors [16, 17]. It is expected to be widely used in low‐abundance nucleic acid detection [15] and many pathogens detection [31]. DdPCR is anticipated to play a crucial role in the detection of pathogens and drug resistance genes associated with LRTI.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis can invade the susceptible body through respiratory tract, digestive tract or skin injury, causing tuberculosis in a variety of tissues and organs. Conventional detection methods can effectively identify infections; however, their capability in detecting M. tuberculosis is hindered when the bacterial concentration is low, especially during the initial stages of infection. DdPCR is capable of accurately detecting M. tuberculosis at low concentrations during the early stages of infection, enabling prompt diagnosis and treatment of tuberculosis. Compared with the traditional quantitative polymerase chain reaction (sensitivity 65%, specificity 100%, detection rate 65%), acid‐fast staining and sputum culture (sensitivity 23% and 58%, respectively), the specificity, sensitivity and detection rate of ddPCR are all 100% [32]. The research of Dickson et al. evaluated the application of ddPCR in ICU. The pulmonary microflora of critically ill patients often changes greatly with the change of disease, and ddPCR can be used to quantitatively detect the changes of pulmonary microflora. It has the capability to anticipate the clinical course of severely ill patients, as well as the prognosis of infants afflicted with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchiectasis, and respiratory tract infection [33]. Wang et al. selected sediment, alpine steppe, typical steppe and forest samples in the natural ecosystem, and established a simulated community. The results show that the ddPCR has good precision, repeatability, sensitivity and stability, and is superior to the qPCR in the quantification of bacteria and fungi and the evaluation of the ratio of bacteria to bacteria in natural samples. Currently, the detection of fungal infections in the lower respiratory tract using ddPCR remains an underexplored area despite its potential as a clinical application in the future.

Hijano et al. employed ddPCR to assess the viral burden of various viruses, including respiratory syncytial virus, in LRTI. The findings revealed that ddPCR exhibited a specificity of 100% for influenza A, with a positive predictive value of 79.3% and a negative predictive value of 0.818. For influenza B, the analytical specificity was maintained at 100%, with a positive predictive value of 1 and a negative predictive value of 68.2% [21]. For patients with LRTI, timely evaluation and quantitative determination of viral load can evaluate the effect of treatment and determine whether the treatment plan needs to be changed, so as to improve the accuracy of clinical decision‐making of LRTI. Another study by Dickson et al. evaluated the concentration of 16SrDNA in microbronchoalveolar lavage and used ddPCR to quantify the bacterial DNA load in the lungs when ICU was admitted to hospital. The research demonstrated that individuals exhibiting the highest bacterial DNA concentration in their lungs exhibited a reduced likelihood of being successfully extubated and surviving on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 compared to those with lower bacterial DNA loads [33]. Due to the long culture period and difficult detection of M. tuberculosis, traditional detection methods are difficult to give timely and accurate diagnosis to patients in the early stage of tuberculosis infection [17]. DdPCR can effectively detect low‐abundance M. tuberculosis in the early stage of infection, thus achieving the purpose of early diagnosis and treatment of tuberculosis. Compared with traditional detection methods such as qPCR (sensitivity 65%, specificity 100% and detection rate 65%), sputum acid‐fast staining and sputum culture (sensitivity 23% and 58%, respectively), the specificity, sensitivity and detection rate of ddPCR are 100% [32]. The study of Dickson et al. evaluated the application of ddPCR in ICU. The microflora in the lungs of critically ill patients often varies greatly with the change of the patient's condition, while the ddPCR technique can be used to quantitatively detect the changes of microflora in the lungs of patients. It can predict the clinical progression of critically ill patients and the disease outcome of infants with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchiectasis and respiratory tract infection [33]. Wang et al. showed that sediment, alpine grassland, typical grassland and forest samples were selected and simulated communities were constructed in natural ecosystem. The results showed that ddPCR had better precision, repeatability, sensitivity and stability, and was better than qPCR in the quantification of bacteria and fungi and the evaluation of fungus: bacteria ratio in natural samples. Unfortunately, there are relatively few studies on the detection of fungal infection in lower respiratory tract infection by ddPCR, which is one of the clinical applications that ddPCR can be used to explore in the future [34].

Since the epidemic of coronavirus caused by COVID‐19 in 2019, people have begun to explore the application of ddPCR in the detection of COVID‐19. Yu et al. explored the application of ddPCR in viral load and quantitative detection of pneumonia caused by novel coronavirus. 323 samples from 76 patients were detected by RT‐PCR and ddPCR, respectively. According to the results of RT‐PCR, 95 samples were positive and 161 samples were negative. The ddPCR conducted on 95 positive samples confirmed their positivity. Among the 161 samples identified as negative by RT‐PCR, the ddPCR test further validated the negativity of 157 samples (97.5%), while revealing four samples to be positive. The results showed that RT‐PCR and ddPCR were accurate and reliable in both high viral load samples and negative samples, but ddPCR was more effective in detecting low viral load samples [35]. The research of Alteri et al. has proved that ddPCR has more advantages than the current RT‐PCR method when the currently used RT‐PCR monitoring results were persistent and repeatedly negative. Of the 19 RT‐PCR‐negative patients, they were positive according to ddPCR tests, and these patients were characterised by generally low viral loads (never more than 2000 copies/mL). DdPCR can supplement RT‐PCR‐based detection and diagnosis to improve the rapid identification of COVID‐19 infection as much as possible, and can also be used for treatment monitoring of suspected COVID‐19 patients [36]. Identification of Drug Resistance Genes of LRTI

Application of mNGS in Drug Resistance Genes of Pathogenic Bacteria LRTI

While using mNGS to detect pathogenic bacteria, the detection of drug resistance genes of pathogenic bacteria can predict the possible drug‐resistant bacteria, which plays a positive role in guiding clinical drug use.

Liu's research has demonstrated the precision of mNGS in the detection of drug resistance genes [20]. Specifically, among the five strains of Acinetobacter baumannii analysed, four strains were found to harbour drug resistance genes including blaOXA‐23, blaOXA‐51, and blaTEM, while one strain carried blaOXA‐23 and blaOXA‐51. Additionally, among the six strains of enterobacter examined, four strains possessed β‐lactamase resistance genes. These findings were corroborated by antibiotic sensitivity testing, confirming the high concordance between mNGS results and those obtained through conventional detection methods.

In the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), patients are at a higher risk of acquiring infections caused by drug‐resistant bacteria, thereby highlighting the significant utility of mNGS in this setting. Yin, Liu, and Fan evaluated the application of mNGS in ICU. The results showed that Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Streptococcus haemolyticus were all resistant to penicillin, but the resistance rate to erythromycin and tetracycline antibiotics was low, and the resistance rate to linezolid, vancomycin and other antibiotics was 0 [37]. Through the mNGS study of drug resistance genes of ICU pathogens, to avoid the use of macrolide antibiotics to treat Gram‐positive bacterial infection, in order to improve the clinical treatment effect [38].

Serpa et al. [39] systematically evaluated the ability of mNGS to predict drug resistance of microorganisms in LRTIs. The study pointed out that because the abundance of drug resistance genes of pathogens in respiratory tract and other clinical body fluids was relatively low, the ability of traditional methods to detect drug resistance genes was limited. FLASH enrichment (hybridisation to find low‐abundance sequences) can be used to effectively detect drug resistance genes of pathogens. For gram‐positive pathogens, the sensitivity, specificity and accuracy of simultaneous detection of DNA sequence and RNA sequence for drug resistance gene detection were 70% (CI 47%–87%), 95% (CI 85%–99%) and 87% (CI 78%–94%), respectively. For gram‐negative pathogens, the combination of DNA sequence and RNA sequence had 100% sensitivity (CI 87%–100%), 64% specificity (CI 48%–78%) and 78% accuracy (CI 67%–87%). The study fully demonstrated the potential of mNGS in the detection of drug resistance genes.

Although metagenomics next generation sequencing can overcome the shortcomings of time‐consuming, labour‐intensive and possible pollution in the detection of drug resistance genes, and is more suitable for the detection of slow‐growing or difficult‐to‐culture pathogens. However, it cannot be ignored that there is also a problem of high false positive rate in drug resistance gene detection by mNGS. Unlike bloodstream infections and infections in many sterile sites, there are a large number of microbial colonisation in the lower respiratory tract, which makes the respiratory tract samples detected by mNGS can be divided into the following three categories: suspected pathogenic microorganisms, colonised microorganisms and contaminated microorganisms [12]. Identification and determination of pathogenic microorganisms and colonised microorganisms, and accurate identification of drug resistance genes are the core challenges of mNGS in the detection of drug resistance genes in lower respiratory tract infections. In a recent study conducted by Charalampous et al., researchers identified a total of 183 drug resistance genes in 41 samples of respiratory tract infection, but only 24 drug resistance genes (13.11%) were consistent with the results of drug sensitivity test. 16 drug resistance genes did not match the phenotype of cultured strains, and 56 genes may come from normal or colonised respiratory tract bacteria. Some drug resistance genes may even come from strains other than laboratory culture [38]. Starting with sequencing readings and genome coverage, establishing an effective threshold for accurate identification of pathogens of LRTI will be one of the directions of mNGS in the future development.

With the progress of related clinical work, more and more studies have shown that mNGS has great advantages in pathogen detection, especially in the detection of pathogens that is difficult to culture or have a long culture cycle. It is expected to play an important role in guiding clinical drug use and preventing clinical antibiotic abuse. However, we cannot ignore the high false positive rate of mNGS and the relatively limited ability of macrogenomic detection in some low‐abundance pathogens such as M. tuberculosis [39]. In the realm of detecting drug resistance genes and anticipating drug resistance among pathogenic bacteria, the sensitivity and specificity exhibited by mNGS are indeed commendable. However, there remains a discernible disparity between mNGS and the ddPCR within the same second‐generation sequencing technology framework. It is imperative that mNGS continues to strive towards bridging this gap in the forthcoming era [40].

Application of ddPCR in the Detection of Drug Resistance Genes

Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction is expected to occupy a significant position in the process of discovering drug resistance genes among pathogenic microorganisms. In a study from Emory University School of Medicine in Atlanta [41], it successfully proved the application prospect of ddPCR in discovering examples of defective interference of influenza viruses. Currently, there is a limited amount of research examining the direct application of ddPCR in detecting pathogenic microorganisms and drug resistance genes in lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI). Nonetheless, Hu et al. [40] successfully identified pathogens and AMR genes in samples from a group of patients suspected of having bloodstream infection (BSI) septicaemia using ddPCR, mNGS, and blood culture methods. We can foresee the research potential of ddPCR in AMR detection. Compared with the traditional blood culture methods, among the 60 patients, 50 cases were detected by ddPCR, while only 10 cases were detected by routine blood culture, and only 41 cases were positive by mNGS. In the identification of drug resistance genes, a total of 17 AMR genes were detected by ddPCR (AMR gene was not detected by mNGS in this study), and the turnover speed and sensitivity were much stronger than traditional methods. It is expected to play an important role in clinical use of antibiotics. Shallom and Zelazny successfully detected A2058C and A2059G clarithromycin resistance mutations in rrl gene by ddPCR, and provided absolute quantification of target DNA molecule and 23SrRNA gene, and the detection time was much shorter than that of conventional PCR detection. This indicates that ddPCR can provide a better basis for clinical research in identifying drug resistance mutations and rapid drug resistance gene diagnosis which are difficult to be detected by conventional detection methods [42].

It must be emphasised that, despite the remarkable specificity and sensitivity exhibited by ddPCR within its recognition range, its turnaround time is considerably longer, and its cost is significantly higher compared to traditional qPCR. Furthermore, the need to predesign detection targets and specific primers limits its capability in identifying unknown pathogens. When compared to mNGS, there is a relative paucity of research exploring the utilisation of ddPCR in the detection of pathogens and drug resistance genes in LRTI.

Conclusion

As the next‐generation sequencing technology, we found that mNGS and ddPCR are expected to play an important role in the detection of LRTI pathogens and drug resistance genes in the future. Metagenomics next‐generation sequencing (mNGS) has good performance in the detection of pathogens (mainly bacteria, fungi, viruses and atypical pathogens) in LRTI. Compared with the traditional methods, it has its unique value and advantages. Microbial culture requires patients to take sputum samples, but most patients cannot achieve standardised retention of samples, which may lead to oral colonisation flora contamination, making the reliability of the test results in doubt. Despite their apparent suitability, the collection of BALF samples involves invasive procedures for patients, potentially resulting in discomfort and resistance. Compared with traditional methods, mNGS does not need invasive operation. Sputum, BALF, blood and cerebrospinal fluid can be used as samples for mNGS detection. Although qPCR is relatively fast and simple, but the ability to find unknown pathogens is weak, cannot find the potential pathogens that may exist in patients; mNGS can detect the distribution of microflora in patients macroscopically, and find the possible pathogens in time, which has obvious advantages. In contrast to blood culture, mNGS effectively evades the post‐infection window period, enabling prompt identification of the patient's pathogen. Additionally, mNGS exhibits notable superiority in the detection of drug resistance genes and multiple pathogen infections.

However, it must be acknowledged that mNGS is not flawless. Firstly, it must be acknowledged that the cost of mNGS is considerable. In numerous remote regions, the existing medical infrastructure is inadequate to facilitate mNGS testing for patients. Consequently, traditional testing methods remain the predominant approach for the detection of LRTI in the majority of locations. Nest mNGS has a high false positive rate. The difficulties encountered when applying mNGS in LRTI primarily revolve around explaining the outcomes of the testing and addressing the significance of discrepancies between mNGS and traditional detection methods. To ensure accurate interpretation of mNGS test outcomes, additional clinical data and the backing of clinical trials are imperative. However, it is concerning to note that the current implementation of mNGS in LRTI often involves a limited sample size, (seldom exceeding 50 cases), which makes it impossible for us to form an objective evaluation of mNGS results. We hope that mNGS studies with a larger sample size can appear to fill this gap. Lg (RPKM) proposed by Liu et al. seems to be a good idea to solve the problem of false positive rate, but we need to admit that the present approach to addressing the issue of high false positive rates in mNGS is inadequate. Whether to establish a more reliable research index based on other indicators of mNGS for pathogen detection is the development direction of mNGS in the future.

As a molecular absolute quantitative study, ddPCR can accurately analyse pathogen types and drug resistance genes. Its ability to achieve early detection and diagnosis of pathogens, particularly those with long culture cycles such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, along with the accurate quantification of pathogen load in patients, represents a significant advantage of ddPCR. But unfortunately, ddPCR seems to be rarely used in the detection of LRTI. Additional research is necessary to establish the advantages and disadvantages of ddPCR, as well as its distinction from conventional detection methods.

Compared with other infections such as bloodstream infection, there are more kinds of colonised bacteria in LRTI. It is a technical difficulty to correctly distinguish between true and false positive pathogens in the application of second‐generation sequencing technology. At this stage, due to the lack of relevant intuitive comparative research, it is difficult to systematically evaluate the application advantages of these two technologies in LRTI. We hope that the subsequent experimental design will accurately represent the strengths and weaknesses of both methods in terms of sensitivity, specificity, outcome, drug sensitivity, and other pertinent factors. Furthermore, we envision the second‐generation sequencing technology being increasingly utilized in clinical medicine.
