Enhancing postharvest storage quality and cuticular wax composition of summer black grapes through preharvest calcium application
Authors: Yaoying Zeng, Hui Su, Le Xie, Yubo Xiong, Zhuojun Yu, Jiehai She, Kai Zhang, Hongyan Huo, Wei He, Wenhua Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of preharvest calcium treatments at different developmental stages on the cuticular wax composition of Summer Black grapes and their correlation with fruit storage quality (4 ± 0.5 °C) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism. Calcium chloride (5 g/L) was applied at the flowering, fruit-setting, and veraison stages. Changes in cuticular wax content and composition were analyzed, along with associated indicators of storage quality and ROS metabolism. Calcium treatment increased wax content and preserved wax structure integrity during storage. Fatty acids dominated wax composition (41.38–95.64% across groups). After 60 d of storage, calcium-treated grapes showed lower weight loss and berry detachment rates and higher fruit firmness, reducing sugars, titratable acids, and protopectin content than those of the control group, with the flowering stage treatment yielding the best results. Additionally, grapes treated at the flowering stage exhibited lower levels of superoxide anion (O2·-), malondialdehyde (MDA), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), and lipoxygenase (LOX) activity and higher scavenging rates of DPPH, hydroxyl, and ABTS radicals. They also showed higher activities of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and catalase (CAT), and increased levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants such as vitamin C compared to the control group, indicating the calcium treatment resulted in the highest antioxidant capacity. This study elucidates how calcium treatment maintains fruit storage quality and regulates ROS metabolism through modulation of cuticular wax composition, thereby providing a theoretical basis for improving fruit preservation.

Introduction

The Summer Black grape is an early-maturing, seedless grape variety (Wang et al., 2024). Its growth and development progress through distinct stages, including budbreak, shoot elongation, flowering, fruit set, berry enlargement, veraison, and maturation. At full maturity, the fruit exhibits a dark purple-black color and is coated with a relatively thick layer of cuticular wax, which plays a critical protective role during fruit development and postharvest storage (Shi et al., 2022). The cuticular wax of the Summer Black grape primarily consists of long-chain aliphatic compounds (C20-C34), such as fatty acids, alkanes, aldehydes, terpenes, primary alcohols, and ketones, as well as cyclic compounds such as flavonoids, triterpenes, and phenylpropanoids (Li et al., 2020; Wang, Su, Chen, Mao, & Jiang, 2020).

Postharvest storage is a period during which the nutritional content of fruit gradually declines, and overall quality deteriorates, leading to issues such as water loss, berry detachment, browning, and mold growth (Zhang et al., 2024). Therefore, reducing the rate of nutrient consumption within the fruit and inhibiting its energy metabolism and physiological activities are crucial for preserving fruit quality during storage. Cuticular wax, a natural protective barrier on the fruit surface, fulfils multiple biological functions, including slowing down water loss (Hongbin et al., 2022), inhibiting microbial and pest invasion (Bo et al., 2022), delaying the onset of fruit softening (Zhang et al., 2024), and supporting the regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism (Zhang et al., 2022). Preharvest calcium application has been shown to promote growth, development, and quality improvement in fruit (Lu, Meng, Jin, & Gao, 2018; Wang, Zhang, Du, & Li, 2024; Chaudhary, Azam, Khan, & Abbas, 2025; Abdelaziz, Shaarawi, & Ibrahim, 2022). As the first line of defense against environmental stress, the cuticular wax is also affected by calcium treatment. Calcium can indirectly influence the synthesis and metabolism of cuticular wax by regulating various physiological and metabolic processes within the fruit. Studies have shown that calcium influences the regulation of enzymes involved in cuticular wax biosynthesis, thereby modifying its composition and overall content (Jiang, 2022). Martins, Szakiel, Pączkowski, Teixeira, and Gerós (2021) reported that preharvest calcium treatment reduced the oleanolic acid content in grape cuticular wax, while the content of sterols and neutral triterpenes varied depending on the grape variety.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the regulatory effects of preharvest calcium treatment on the composition of cuticular wax. Specifically, it explored how calcium application at different developmental stages influences the cuticular wax characteristics of Summer Black grapes and evaluated its correlation with fruit quality from both physiological and microscopic perspectives. Additionally, the study examined the relationship between cuticular wax and ROS metabolism in Summer Black grapes by assessing antioxidant capacity and the activities of related enzymes. The findings provide a novel theoretical basis and practical insights for enhancing fruit preservation strategies.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

Five-year-old Summer Black grapes, cultivated under rain-shelter conditions in Zhanggongmiao, Li County, Changde City, Hunan Province (111°40′E, 29°37′N), were used as the experimental material. Calcium chloride (CaCl2) was applied as a foliar spray at a concentration of 5.0 g/L during three key developmental stages: flowering (April 28–30, 2024; Group F), fruit-setting (May 5–7, 2024; Group FS), and veraison (June 17–19, 2024; Group V), following the method described by Shi et al. (2022). Each treatment group consisted of six grapevines and Spray 200 mL on each grapevine. The control group received no calcium treatment. Upon reaching full maturity (July 31, 2024), grape berries were randomly harvested from each treatment group. For each group, 20 kg of grapes exhibiting intact cuticular wax, free from mechanical damage, and uniform in size and maturity were selected. The harvested grapes were kept in a cool area for 24 h to dissipate field heat. Subsequently, the grape bunches were packaged individually in perforated, transparent, trapezoidal Oriented Polypropylene (OPP) bags and stored at 4 ± 0.5 °C. Cuticular wax content, structure, and composition, along with fruit storage quality, were measured at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 d of storage. For each group, six grape bunches were selected as samples at each sampling time point (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 days). Each bunch was considered as one replicate, and all measurements were conducted independently on each replicate.

Determination of wax

Determination of wax.

Determination of fruit quality

2.2.1 Extraction of cuticular wax and calculation of its content

Cuticular wax extraction was performed following the method described by Su et al. (2025). Intact grape berries were carefully removed from the cluster, retaining approximately 2 mm of the pedicel to prevent leakage of lipophilic substances from the pulp. A total of 150 g of berries were washed, air-dried, and weighed, with the weight recorded as M. The berries were then immersed in an extraction solvent containing dichloromethane and n-hexane in a 3:1 ratio, using a liquid-to-material ratio of 2:1 mL g−1. The immersion lasted for 7.5 min with gentle stirring using a glass rod to facilitate wax dissolution. The weight of a clean, dry petri dish was recorded (m₁). After extraction, the solvent was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C. When the volume of the concentrate approximately matched that of the petri dish, the rotary evaporation was stopped, and the concentrate was transferred to the dish to allow the solvent to evaporate naturally, leaving a white residue, identified as cuticular wax. The weight of the petri dish containing the wax was recorded (m₂). The wax content was expressed in mg kg−1 and calculated using Eq. (1):(1)ExtractedWaxContentmgkg−1=m2−m1M×100

Microscopic observation of the cuticular wax structure on grape surfaces

Microscopic observation of the cuticular wax structure was performed following the method described by Su et al. (2025) with slight modifications. A skin section measuring 6 mm × 6 mm was excised from the equatorial region of each grape using a scalpel. The samples were frozen at −25 °C for 24 h and then freeze-dried. After drying, the samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer and examined using Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at a magnification of 5000× to observe the surface morphology of the cuticular wax.

Determination of waxy components by GC–MS

The wax components and their relative contents (%) were determined following the method described by Su et al. (2025), with slight modifications. A 50 mg portion of wax powder was accurately weighed and transferred into a flask. Subsequently, 5 mL of 14% boron trifluoride-methanol solution was added, along with 100 μL of a 0.2 mg/mL methyl isobutyl ketone solution, which served as an internal standard to correct for injection volume and instrument response variability. The mixture was refluxed in a water bath at 70 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 5 mL of n-heptane was added through the top of the condenser, and the mixture was refluxed for an additional 2 min. Once the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, 5 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution was added to promote phase separation. The upper organic layer was collected and passed through 0.5 g of anhydrous sodium sulfate to remove residual moisture. The solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane filter for subsequent analysis.

The chromatographic conditions were as follows: an Agilent 19091S-433 HP-5MSI capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) was used. Helium served as the carrier gas and nitrogen as the collision gas. The flow rate was maintained at 1 mL/min. The injection port temperature was set to 260 °C, and the auxiliary heater was maintained at 280 °C. The temperature program was as follows: the initial temperature was held at 50 °C for 1 min, then increased to 290 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min and held for 22 min. The ion source was set at 280 °C, and the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) scanning range was 50–600.

Wax components were identified by comparing with spectra from the NIST 20.L spectral library. The relative content of the components was calculated using the internal standard and normalization methods. Specifically, the ratio of the peak area of each target compound to that of the internal standard (methyl isobutyl ketone) was used to express the percentage content of each component relative to the total wax composition.

Determination of weight loss percent and threshing rate

For each group, six grape clusters were selected as samples. The weight loss percent and threshing rate were measured using the weighing method (Zhang, Shi, et al., 2024). Determination of firmness, reducing sugars, titratable acids, Souble pectin and pectin content

For each treatment group, 10 grape berries were randomly selected. A 6 mm × 6 mm piece of skin was removed from the equatorial region of each berry. Fruit firmness was measured by vertically inserting a firmness meter (Beijing Jinke Lida Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China, equipped with a Φ3.5 mm cylindrical prob) into the berry and recording the reading, with results expressed in N. Reducing sugar content was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) following the method described by Zhang, Shi, et al. (2024). One gram of sample was ground and brought to 25 mL with distilled water. The suspension was incubated at 80 °C for 30 min, cooled to room temperature, and filtered. The residue was washed with 20 mL of distilled water and filtered again; both filtrates were combined and made up to 100 mL in a volumetric flask to obtain the reducing-sugar extract. For colorimetric assay, 2.0 mL of the extract and 1.5 mL of 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid reagent were transferred to a 25 mL graduated tube, mixed, and heated in a boiling water bath for 5 min. After rapid cooling to room temperature, the volume was adjusted to 25 mL with distilled water, mixed thoroughly, and the absorbance was read at 540 nm. Titratable acidity was determined by titration with sodium hydroxide, with the results expressed as a percentage (%). Ten grams of sample were ground and brought to 100 mL with distilled water. After filtration, a 20 mL aliquot of the filtrate was titrated with standardized NaOH using 1% (w/v) phenolphthalein as indicator. The endpoint was the first persistent pink color that remained stable for 0.5 min.

Referring to a previously published method (Zeng et al., 2025) with slight modification, 1.0 g of grape tissue samples were taken, mixed with 25 mL of 95% ethanol homogenate, heated in a boiling water bath for 30 min, cooled down, and then centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 15 min; this was repeated three times to remove sugar and other substances. Afterward, 20 mL of distilled water was added to the precipitate, which was kept at 50 °C before cooling to room temperature; this was centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 15 min, and the supernatant was taken as souble pectin. Next, 25 mL of 0.5 mol/L sulfuric acid solution was added to the precipitate, the original pectin was hydrolyzed in a boiling water bath, and samples were centrifuged to obtain the original pectin determination solution. A volume of 1.0 mL of the extract was taken separately, and the absorbance value at 530 nm was determined. The results were expressed as g kg−1.

Determination of ROS metabolism

Determination of DPPH, hydroxyl radical, and ABTS scavenging activities

The DPPH radical scavenging rates was measured using commercial assay kits (A153–1-1, Nanjin Jiancheng Institute Bioengineering, Nanjing, China). The hydroxyl radical scavenging rates was measured using commercial assay kits (A018–1-1, Nanjin Jiancheng Institute Bioengineering, Nanjing, China). The ABTS radical scavenging activity was also measured using a commercial kit (BC4770, Solarbio, Beijing, China). All antioxidant capacities were expressed as scavenging rates in percentages (%). DPPH radical carries an unpaired electron and exhibits a strong absorption band at 517 nm, giving its alcoholic solution a characteristic purple hue. In the presence of a free-radical scavenger, the single electron becomes paired, causing this absorption to fade. The paler the color, the lower the absorbance. This decrease in absorbance is exploited to quantify the DPPH-scavenging capacity of the test sample. The amount of H2O2 is directly proportional to the yield of OH• generated in the Fenton reaction. After an electron acceptor is supplied, the mixture is developed with Griess reagent to form a red chromophore whose absorbance at 550 nm is linearly related to the concentration of OH• produced. Upon oxidation, ABTS is converted into the stable bluish-green cation radical ABTS·+, which displays a maximum absorption peak at 405 nm. When a test substance is introduced into the ABTS·+ solution, its antioxidative constituents reduce the radical, bleaching the reaction mixture and decreasing the absorbance at 405 nm. Within a defined range, the magnitude of this decrease is directly proportional to the extent of radical scavenging. In the present kit, the ability of a sample to eliminate ABTS·+ is quantified by measuring the resulting decline in absorbance.

Determination of vitamin C, MDA, and O2·- content

Vitamin C content was determined using a commercial kit (A009–1-1, Nanjin Jiancheng Institute Bioengineering, Nanjing, China), with results expressed in mg kg−1. The contents of O2·- (BC1290) and MDA (BC0025) were determined using commercial assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China), with results expressed as μmol min−1 kg−1 and μmol kg−1, respectively. Fe3+ is rapidly reduced to Fe2+ by ascorbic acid. The Fe2+ formed is then chelated by ferrozine to yield a colored complex whose absorbance at 536 nm is directly proportional to the reduced ascorbate (vitamin C) content. Under acidic and high-temperature conditions, MDA condenses with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) to form a trimethine adduct that exhibits an intense pink chromophore. The MDA concentration is quantified by measuring the absorbance of this trimethine pigment at 532 nm. Superoxide anion reacts with hydroxylammonium chloride to yield NO₂−. In the presence of sulfanilamide and N-(1-naphthyl) ethylenediamine dihydrochloride, the NO₂− formed is converted into a magenta azo dye whose absorbance is measured at 530 nm, providing a direct index of superoxide generation.

Assays of enzyme activities: SOD, POD, CAT, PPO, PAL, APX, and LOX

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD, BC0170), peroxidase (POD, BC0090), polyphenol oxidase (PPO, BC0195), ascorbate peroxidase (APX, BC0220), lipoxygenase (LOX, BC0320), catalase (CAT, G0105W), and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL, G0114F) were determined using commercial assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Enzyme activities were expressed as units per gram (×103 U kg−1). The xanthine/xanthine oxidase system generates superoxide anion radicals (O₂·−) that reduce nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) to a blue formazan product with maximal absorbance at 560 nm. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) scavenges O₂·−, thereby suppressing formazan formation. Consequently, the deeper the blue color of the reaction mixture, the lower the SOD activity. POD catalyzes the oxidation of specific substrates by H2O2, the enzymatic activity of POD is determined spectrophotometrically by measuring the characteristic absorbance of specific substrates at 410 nm. Hydrogen peroxide exhibits a characteristic absorption peak at 240 nm. CAT decomposes H₂O₂, causing a time-dependent decrease in absorbance at 240 nm. CAT activity is calculated from the rate of this absorbance decline. PPO catalyzes the aerobic oxidation of catechol to ortho-benzoquinone. The enzymatic activity of PPO is determined spectrophotometrically by measuring the characteristic absorbance of ortho-benzoquinone at 410 nm. PAL catalyzes the non-oxidative deamination of L-phenylalanine to yield trans-cinnamic acid and ammonia. The enzymatic activity of PAL is quantified by monitoring the characteristic absorbance of trans-cinnamic acid at 290 nm. APX catalyzes the oxidation of ascorbate (AsA) by H2O2. APX activity is determined by measuring the rate of AsA oxidation. LOX catalyzes the oxidation of linoleic acid to generate oxidation products, and the enzymatic activity of LOX is determined by the characteristic absorbance of these products at 234 nm.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test was used to assess differences among groups. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically. Charts were generated using Origin 2021. Volatile compounds were identified and characterized using the NIST spectral library. The peak areas of the compounds were normalized using the analytical software, and the relative content of each component was expressed as a percentage of the total peak area. Unless otherwise stated, all biochemical parameters were expressed on a fresh weight (FW) basis.

Changes in cuticular wax composition

A total of 131 cuticular wax components were identified using GC–MS analysis. As shown in Table S1 and Fig. 1b, the cuticular wax of Summer Black grapes primarily consisted of fatty acids, primary alcohols, esters, aldehydes, alkanes, and terpenoids. Among these, fatty acids exhibited the highest relative abundance and remained relatively stable during storage. Octacosanoic acid and cerotic acid were the most abundant fatty acids, forming the fundamental structural framework of the wax layer. During storage, preharvest calcium treatment decreased the relative content of fatty acids in the cuticular wax. The relative fatty acid content in Groups F, FS, V, and C ranged from 41.38% to 85.67%, 49.23% to 89.87%, 70.86% to 93.56%, and 70.42% to 95.64%, respectively, exhibiting fluctuating trends and reaching their maximum values on 60 d, 30 d, 50 d, and 50 d, respectively.

Primary alcohols accounted for a relatively small proportion of the cuticular wax components, with relative contents ranging from 0 to 2.88%. Hexadecanol and octadecanol were the predominant primary alcohols. The relative content of primary alcohols gradually decreased during storage in the cuticular wax of Groups F, FS, and V, whereas in The control group, they were detectable only on 10 d and 40 d. The relative content of esters varied among the four groups, ranging from 0% to 21.98%, with the control group exhibiting higher levels than the calcium-treated groups. No differences were observed in the relative content of aldehydes among the groups. Alkanes, primarily 2,3,6-trimethylheptane, 2,3,4-trimethylheptane, and 2,4-dimethylhexane, showed differences in relative content among the groups. Group F showed an initial increase followed by a sharp decrease, reaching a maximum on 50 d. Group FS exhibited a fluctuating trend, with a maximum on 40 d. Group V increased and then decreased, reaching a maximum on 40 d. The control group showed a sharp decrease, with a maximum on 0 d. Terpenoids were detected only in Group F, with contents ranging from 0% to 1.29%. Lupeol was the primary terpenoid detected.

The results indicate that preharvest calcium treatments applied at different growth stages of Summer Black grapes can effectively optimize the composition and relative content of the cuticular wax. Specifically, these treatments decreased the relative content of fatty acids and esters while increasing the relative content of alkanes, primary alcohols, and terpenoids, thereby contributing to improved fruit storage quality.

Effects of Preharvest calcium treatment on the cuticular wax and quality of summer black grapes

Changes in the appearance of summer black grapes

As shown in Fig. 3, on 0 d of storage, the cuticular wax layer appeared more pronounced in the preharvest calcium-treated groups compared to the control group. During storage, berries in the control group began to show water loss and wilting by 10 d, accompanied by greening and browning of the pedicels. By 20 d, the bunch tips in the control group exhibited severe water loss and shriveling, with the berries lacking plumpness. In contrast, the bunches in Groups F and FS remained plump and intact during the storage. However, after 30 d, Group FS began to show loosening of the bunch structure, and Group V experienced berry detachment on 50 d. These results indicate that preharvest calcium treatment, particularly during the flowering stage, was most effective in preserving the appearance of Summer Black grapes during storage.Fig. 3Appearance changes of Summer Black grapes during storage Flowering (Group F), Fruit-Setting (Group FS), and Veraison (Group V).Fig. 3

Changes in weight loss rate and threshing rate

As shown in Fig. 1c, the weight loss rate of Summer Black grapes gradually increased during the storage period. Group F exhibited a lower weight loss rate compared to the other treatment groups, while the control group showed the highest rate of weight loss. As shown in Fig. 1d, the berry threshing rate of Summer Black grapes exhibited a similar trend. The threshing rate of the control group was higher than that of the other groups during storage (except at 30 d), reaching a maximum of 13.49% on 60 d. In contrast, Group F only began to exhibit berry threshing on day 20 and maintained the lowest rate, reaching just 6.62% by the end of storage. These findings indicate that preharvest calcium application, particularly during the flowering stage, effectively decreases weight loss and berry threshing rate during postharvest storage.

Changes in reducing sugar content and titratable acidity

As shown in Fig. 1e, there were no differences in reducing sugar content among the grapes from the four treatment groups during the first 50 d of storage. However, Group F had higher reducing sugar content than the other groups throughout the storage period, decreasing from 21.38% to 12.17%, while the control group decreased from 21.36% to 10.62%, representing reductions of 9.21% and 10.74%, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1f, starting from 10 d of storage, Group F also exhibited higher titratable acidity than the other treatment groups. Compared to 0 d, the titratable acidity content of Groups F, FS, V, and C decreased by 0.25%, 0.28%, 0.26%, and 0.32%, respectively, by the end of storage. These results indicate that while preharvest calcium treatment does not affect the initial sugar and acid content of grapes, calcium treatment during the flowering stage is especially effective in reducing the degradation of sugars and acids throughout postharvest storage.

Changes in firmness

As shown in Fig. 1g, the firmness of the fruit gradually decreased during the storage period. On 0 d, Groups F and FS exhibited higher firmness than Groups V and C. By the end of the storage period, the firmness of Group F decreased to 1.62 kg/cm2, representing a 40% reduction, while the firmness of the control group decreased to 0.68 kg/cm2, representing a 59% reduction. The firmness of Groups FS and V showed a decreasing trend, which was between Groups F and C. These results indicate that preharvest calcium treatment enhances fruit firmness, with application during the flowering stage showing the highest efficacy.

Changes in soluble pectin and pectin content

As shown in Fig. 1h, on 0 d of storage, the soluble pectin content in Groups F, FS, V, and C was 0.20 g kg−1, 0.22 g kg−1, 0.23 g kg−1, and 0.26 g kg−1, respectively. By 60 d, these values had increased by 60%, 59%, 65%, and 77%, respectively. Meanwhile, the pectin content in Groups F, FS, V, and C was initially 1.55 g kg−1, 1.41 g kg−1, 1.36 g kg−1, and 1.25 g kg−1, respectively, and decreased by 49.68%, 48.23%, 50.74%, and 60.32%, respectively, by the end of the storage period (Fig. 1i). Throughout the storage period, Group F consistently exhibited lower soluble pectin content and higher pectin content than the other treatment groups (except at 30 d and 40 d). These results aligned with the trends in firmness observed in Fig. 1g. These findings indicate that preharvest calcium treatment can increase the internal pectin content of Summer Black grapes and inhibit the formation of soluble pectin, thereby enhancing fruit firmness, with calcium application during the flowering stage being the most effective.

ROS metabolism

Changes in vitamin C and MDA content

As shown in Fig. 1j, on 0 d of storage, the vitamin C content of the control group was 324.9 mg kg−1, while the contents in Groups F, FS, and V were 1.32, 1.19, and 1.02 times higher, respectively. By the end of storage, the vitamin C contents in Groups F, FS, V, and C were 214.3 mg kg−1, 157.7 mg kg−1, 124.4 mg kg−1, and 92.3 mg kg−1, respectively. At this point, the vitamin C content in Groups F, FS, and V was 2.32, 1.71, and 1.35 times higher than that of the control group, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 1k, during storage, the groups that received preharvest calcium treatment exhibited lower MDA content than the untreated group. Throughout the 60 d storage period, the control group had higher MDA content than the other treatment groups (except on 0 d and 40 d). After 60 d of storage, the MDA contents in Groups F, FS, V, and C were 19.61 μmol kg−1, 20.22 μmol kg−1, 26.00 μmol kg−1, and 29.20 μmol kg−1, respectively.

Changes in O2·- content, and DPPH, hydroxyl radical, and ABTS scavenging rates

As shown in Fig. 1l, the control group had a lower DPPH radical scavenging rate than the other groups throughout the storage period (except at 40 d). After 60 d of storage, the DPPH scavenging rates for Groups F, FS, V, and C decreased to 62.08%, 56.13%, 54.21%, and 49.40%, respectively, representing reductions of 13%, 19%, 19%, and 18%. As shown in Fig. 1m, Group F had the highest hydroxyl radical scavenging rate throughout the storage period, higher than the other three groups, while the control group had the lowest rate. After 60 d of storage, the hydroxyl radical scavenging rates in Groups F, FS, V, and C decreased to 52.45%, 49.54%, 44.37%, and 33.08%, respectively, representing reductions of 0.39-fold, 0.37-fold, 0.47-fold, and 0.82-fold, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1n, Group F also showed a higher ABTS scavenging rate than the other three groups throughout the storage period, while the control group had the lowest ABTS scavenging rate. After 60 d of storage, the ABTS scavenging rate of Groups F, FS, V, and C decreased to 43.70%, 36.99%, 32.99%, and 30.08%, respectively, representing reductions of 25%, 31%, 37%, and 37%, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1o, throughout the storage period, the O2·- content was lower in the preharvest calcium-treated groups than in the control group.

In contrast, POD activity increased during storage. After 60 d of storage, the POD activity in Groups F, FS, V, and C reached 19.85 × 103 U Kg−1, 17.98 × 103 U Kg−1, 15.34 × 103 U Kg−1, and 12.18 × 103 U Kg−1, respectively.

On 0 d, CAT activity in Groups F, FS, and V was 225.63 × 103 U Kg−1, 212.08 × 103 U Kg−1, and 206.99 × 103 U Kg−1, respectively, higher than that in the control group (159.22 × 103 U Kg−1). Although CAT activity decreased as storage time progressed, the decrease was less pronounced in calcium-treated fruit, suggesting that preharvest calcium application enhanced the initial CAT activity and helped maintain it during storage. As shown in Fig. 4d, PPO activity was lower in the calcium-treated group than in the control group throughout the storage period. The results indicate that preharvest calcium treatment regulated PPO activity and slowed down oxidative browning in the fruit, contributing to improved fruit appearance and extended storage life. As shown in Fig. 4e, PAL activity in all groups increased and then decreased. The control group reached a maximum PAL activity of 46.90 × 103 U Kg−1 on 30 d, while the calcium-treated groups reached maximum values of 65.68 × 103 U Kg−1, 61.38 × 103 U Kg−1, and 63.14 × 103 U Kg−1 for Groups F, FS, and V, respectively, after 40 d of storage. On 60 d of storage, APX activity in Groups F, FS, and V was 5.43 × 103 U Kg−1, 5.17 × 103 U Kg−1, and 4.77 × 103 U Kg−1, respectively, higher than that of the control group (4.25 × 103 U Kg−1).

In contrast, Group F showed the lowest LOX activity throughout the storage period. These results indicate that preharvest calcium treatment effectively increased APX activity while reducing LOX activity, thereby enhancing the antioxidant capacity of the fruit and delaying postharvest deterioration.

Discussion

Effects of Preharvest calcium treatment on Cuticular wax and quality of summer black grapes

Cuticular wax plays a crucial role in maintaining fruit quality during storage. In fruit of the same variety, higher cuticular wax content is generally associated with enhanced storability than those with lower wax content (Bo et al., 2022; Ren, Dong, Zhang, Li, & Li, 2019). Calcium, a key secondary messenger in plant physiological processes, can regulate various cellular activities in grape berries. According to Martins, Szakiel, et al. (2021), preharvest calcium treatment can affect both the synthesis and deposition of wax, thereby reducing surface water loss, enhancing the water retention capacity of the fruit, delaying the rate of weight loss, and enhancing the water retention capacity of the fruit.

The structure of cuticular wax plays a critical role in determining the postharvest storability of fruit (Li, Min, Song, Shao, & Zhang, 2017). In apples, for example, the wax crystal structure is dense and highly ordered, with small pores and no depressions, effectively preventing water transpiration. Moreover, during storage, apples with thicker wax layers show lower respiration rates and reduced ethylene production. Similarly, in kumquat mutants (‘Huapi’ kumquat), the cuticular wax crystals are larger than those of the wild-type control, and during cold storage, both the weight loss rate and decay rate are lower than those of the control fruit (Yang et al., 2023). In the present study, Summer Black grapes were used to investigate the effects of preharvest calcium treatments on cuticular wax characteristics and their relationship with storage quality, revealing the intrinsic link between cuticular wax and storage quality. The results demonstrated that preharvest calcium treatment at different growth stages increased the cuticular wax content of the grapes. Additionally, calcium treatment reduced the number of pores within the wax layer and delayed the collapse of the wax structure, thereby preserving the integrity of the wax morphology. This effect may be due to the role of calcium in enhancing cell wall rigidity, which limits cell wall relaxation, thereby affecting wax deposition and spatial distribution. These findings are consistent with the observation reported by Liu et al. (2023).

The primary components of grape cuticular wax include fatty acids, alkanes, alcohols, aldehydes, and esters. These constituents play essential roles in maintaining fruit quality, preserving nutritional value, and providing resistance against pathogens and environmental stresses during fruit growth and storage. Changes in wax composition impact fruit preservation. Fatty acids are the primary component of grape cuticular wax (Su et al., 2025), and the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids is closely associated with fruit quality deterioration during storage (Wang et al., 2014). In the present study, preharvest calcium treatment reduced the peak concentration of fatty acids while increasing the abundance of alkanes. This result was consistent with the findings of Yang et al. (2022), who reported that increased alkane levels in the cuticular wax of citrus fruit reduce water loss from the fruit. This effect is likely attributable to alkanes, which, as highly hydrophobic hydrocarbons, form a protective barrier that enhances the water retention capacity of the fruit (Rahman et al., 2025). Furthermore, calcium treatment applied during the flowering stage enhanced the biosynthesis of terpenoids. A comparable effect was observed in blueberries treated with salicylic acid (Bo, Ruiling, Xiangjun, Chuan, et al., 2022). Terpenoid compounds within the cuticular wax contribute to the defense of the fruit against pathogens and environmental stressors (Flora et al., 2014). Preharvest calcium treatment optimized the cuticular wax composition of ‘Summer Black’ grapes by reducing the proportion of fatty acids and increasing the relative contents of alkanes, primary alcohols, and terpenoids. This compositional shift is likely attributed to calcium's regulatory role in lipid metabolism and wax biosynthesis pathways. Calcium is known to influence the activity of transcription factors such as SHINE/WIN1 and MYB96, which directly activate genes involved in wax biosynthesis, including those encoding fatty acid elongases and wax transporters(Yeats & Rose, 2013). Additionally, calcium can modulate the expression of genes involved in the conversion of fatty acids to alkanes and primary alcohols, thereby altering wax composition(Tiika et al., 2025). The increase in terpenoids, particularly under flowering-stage treatment, may enhance fruit antioxidant capacity and stress resistance, as terpenoids are known to possess ROS-scavenging properties(Wu et al., 2023). Therefore, calcium-induced wax compositional changes not only improve water retention but also contribute to postharvest stress tolerance and quality maintenance. The findings of this study indicate that calcium application during fruit growth and developmental stages can lead to the deposition and stratification of cuticular wax, reduce surface porosity, decrease the relative content of fatty acids, and increase the proportion of primary alcohols. In particular, calcium treatment during the flowering stage increases the proportion of alkanes and the content of terpenoids in the cuticular wax. This reduction in epidermal water loss and enhanced resistance to external environmental stresses can help maintain the storage quality of the fruit. Therefore, preharvest calcium treatment can optimize the function of cuticular wax in Summer Black grapes by regulating the composition and content of the wax.

Cuticular wax is essential for maintaining fruit integrity. It not only helps maintain fruit firmness and delays the softening process, but also reduces the activity of cell wall-related degradative enzymes, thereby delaying the degradation of protopectin and cellulose (Liu et al., 2023). Additionally, calcium regulates the expression of cell wall-related genes, influencing wax deposition. Martins, Szakiel, et al. (2021) proposed that calcium modulates the methylesterification activity of pectin in cell wall components by regulating the expression of pectin methylesterase (PME), thereby controlling both the structure of the cell wall and the localization of wax deposition. Moreover, calcium can regulate the expression of key genes involved in the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds, such as PAL1 and STS, which are crucial wax constituents, thereby regulating wax synthesis (Gao et al., 2024). Our study shows that preharvest calcium treatment increases protopectin content while inhibiting the formation of soluble pectin. The content of protopectin is closely related to fruit firmness, and calcium-treated Summer Black grapes maintain a higher protopectin content (Mirdehghan et al., 2006). This result is consistent with the observed trend in fruit firmness, indicating that preharvest calcium treatment regulates pectin metabolism by affecting cuticular wax synthesis, thereby maintaining fruit storage quality. These findings are consistent with studies on apricot (Liu et al., 2021), blueberry (Lobos, Retamales, Escobar, & Hanson, 2020), and mango (Muengkaew, Whangchai, & Chaiprasart, 2018). In addition to these effects, preharvest calcium treatment also influences the content of reducing sugars and titratable acids in stored fruit. Our study found that the reducing sugar content in the calcium-treated group was higher than in the control group. This effect may be because calcium treatment regulates the composition of cuticular wax and reducing the consumption of sugar substances, thus maintaining most of the nutritional quality of the fruit (Martins, Unlubayir, Teixeira, Lanoue, & Gerós, 2021).

Effects of preharvest calcium treatment on cuticular wax and ROS metabolism in summer black grapes

Cuticular wax plays a crucial role in protecting fruit against biotic and abiotic stresses, and it may affect the accumulation of ROS by maintaining cell membrane integrity (Vishalakshi, Vivek, & Jitendra, 2021). In this study, we found that fatty acid content in the cuticular wax of the preharvest calcium-treated grapes was lower than that of the control, while the content of primary alcohols was higher, especially in the group treated at the flowering stage. Su et al. (2025) demonstrated that fatty acids in cuticular wax can promote oxidative reactions in fruit, while primary alcohols may support antioxidant defense. These findings suggest that preharvest calcium treatment can regulate the composition of cuticular wax by modulating wax biosynthetic pathways, thereby enhancing the antioxidant capacity of Summer Black grapes.

ROS are involved in the growth and development of fruit and are produced through aerobic metabolism in organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts (Zeng et al., 2025). Studies have shown that ROS play a crucial role in fruit maturation and the postharvest process (Wei et al., 2025). Therefore, regulating ROS metabolism during postharvest storage helps maintain fruit quality. In the present study, the DPPH, hydroxyl radical, and ABTS scavenging rates of the calcium-treated groups were consistently higher than those of the control group, with the highest rates observed in the flowering stage calcium-treated group. Additionally, the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD, APX, and CAT, as well as the contents of non-enzymatic antioxidants such as vitamin C, were higher in the calcium-treated groups than in the control group. This phenomenon slowed down the generation of ROS metabolic products and the rate of lipid peroxidation in the fruit, thereby delaying the rate of oxidative stress during storage and improving fruit quality. These findings are consistent with the results of Li et al. (2009). This observation indicates that preharvest calcium treatment can maintain the homeostasis of ROS metabolism in fruit, thereby enhancing the antioxidant capacity of the fruit. Moreover, supplementing calcium during fruit growth and development is an effective strategy to extend the postharvest storage period, with the best application timing being the preharvest stage rather than postharvest treatment (Lydakis & Aked, 2003). Similarly, Shi et al. (2022) reported that Preharvest calcium treatment was more effective in enhancing cuticular wax barrier function, maintaining fruit firmness, reducing weight loss, and improving ROS scavenging capacity when applied at flowering.

There is a key correlation between cuticular wax and ROS metabolism. Zhang, Wang, et al. (2022) demonstrated that cuticular wax removal from melons disrupted ROS metabolism, preventing the fruit from clearing the continuously accumulating ROS products. Similar findings have been reported in blueberries (Chu, Gao, Chen, Fang, & Zheng, 2018), apples (Chen et al., 2020), and citrus (Ning et al., 2014), where cuticular wax was shown to enhance ROS scavenging capacity of the fruit, maintain cellular homeostasis, delay lipid peroxidation of fruit cell membranes, and improve overall fruit quality. In the present study, calcium treatment during the flowering stage reduced the fatty acid content while increasing the content of primary alcohols and terpenoids in the cuticular wax. Terpenoids, known for their antioxidant properties (Wu et al., 2023), can directly scavenge excess ROS, thereby enhancing the antioxidant capacity of the fruit. The observed increase in terpenoid content after calcium treatment during the flowering stage indicates that calcium indirectly affects ROS metabolism by modulating the wax synthesis pathways. This finding is consistent with the study of Martins, Szakiel, et al. (2021).

The strongest effects were observed when calcium was applied at the flowering stage

Among the three preharvest application timings, calcium treatment at the flowering stage consistently produced the most pronounced improvements in cuticular wax structure, storage quality, and ROS scavenging capacity. This stage-specific effectiveness can be attributed to several interconnected physiological and molecular mechanisms. The flowering stage represents a critical window for epidermal cell division and early fruit morphogenesis, during which the foundation of the cuticle is established(Pan, Huani, Jing, Shiyou, & Huayan, 2021). Calcium, as a secondary messenger, plays a pivotal role in regulating cell wall integrity and cuticle biosynthesis. By stabilizing pectin cross-linking in the cell wall, calcium enhances epidermal cell rigidity and creates a more favorable scaffold for wax deposition. This early structural reinforcement likely leads to a denser, more uniform wax layer that persists throughout fruit development and storage. Calcium application during flowering may temporally coincide with the peak expression of genes involved in very-long-chain fatty acid (VLCFA) and alkane biosynthesis, such as CER1, CER3, and WAX2(Batsale et al., 2021; Sakuradani, Zhao, Haslam, & Kunst, 2013). The upregulation of these genes at flowering could explain the higher alkane and primary alcohol content observed in Group F, which are key components for reducing water loss and enhancing barrier function. Calcium is integral to ROS homeostasis by modulating both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems (Martins, Soares, Spormann, Fidalgo, & Gerós, 2021). Early calcium application may prime the fruit's antioxidant defense network during a period of high metabolic activity, leading to sustained higher activities of SOD, CAT, APX, and elevated vitamin C levels throughout storage(Martins, Soares, et al., 2021; Zhang, Liu, Du, & Wang, 2022). This priming effect likely reduces oxidative damage and delays senescence more effectively than later applications. Calcium treatment at flowering may influence hormonal balances, particularly by reducing ethylene biosynthesis and delaying ripening. Since ethylene is a key regulator of fruit softening and ROS accumulation(Tipu & Sherif, 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2026), its suppression could contribute to the observed maintenance of firmness and lower MDA content in Group F.

In summary, the flowering stage represents a developmental checkpoint where calcium can exert maximal influence on cuticle formation, antioxidant system establishment, and hormonal regulation. This temporal specificity underscores the importance of precise timing in preharvest calcium interventions for optimizing postharvest performance in grapes.

Conclusion

Pre-harvest CaCl₂ sprays, especially at flowering, reshaped ‘Summer Black’ grape cuticular wax by increasing alkanes and terpenoids while reducing fatty acids, which translated into firmer fruit, less weight loss, and higher antioxidant capacity during 60 d storage. Calcium thus offers a simple, sustainable tool to extend grape shelf-life. Mechanistic details of wax-ROS crosstalk warrant future omics dissection.
