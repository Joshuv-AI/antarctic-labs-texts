High-energy impact and vapor recondensation history of the angrite parent body revealed by nickel isotopes
Authors: Ke Zhu, Akira Yamaguchi, Paolo A. Sossi, Audrey Bouvier, Lu Chen, Peng Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAngrites are the most volatile-depleted planetary materials known, yet the processes driving such extreme depletion remain uncertain. Using high-precision nickel isotope measurements, this study reveals that the angrite parent body (APB) experienced a complex, multistage volatile evolution. While plutonic angrites preserve chondritic Ni isotopic compositions indicative of an undisturbed core, mantle-derived olivine-rich samples display heavy Ni isotopic signatures, pointing to evaporation from high-energy impacts after core formation. In contrast, isotopically light Ni in volcanic crustal materials suggests partial recondensation of Ni vapor. Together, these data provide direct isotopic evidence for impact-induced vapor loss and atmospheric recondensation on a differentiated asteroid, offering a framework to understand volatile depletion and redistribution on early planetary bodies.

Differentiated planetary bodies, including Earth, are characterized by significant depletion of volatile elements compared to chondritic meteorites (1). The acquisition and loss of volatiles (including water) play essential roles in planetary habitability and also provide records of planetary formation and differentiation. However, the mechanisms responsible for planetary volatile depletion remain a topic of debate (1–3). Among the differentiated bodies (e.g., Earth, Moon, Mars, Vesta), the angrite parent body (APB) is the most volatile-depleted, with K/U and Rb/Sr ratios approximately 100 and 1,000 times lower than those of Ivuna-type (CI) chondrites, respectively (1, 2, 4, 5). Understanding the processes driving extreme volatile depletion in the APB can shed light on the mechanisms that contributed to volatile depletion across the solar system.

Angrites are basaltic meteorites composed primarily of Ca-Al-Ti-rich pyroxene, Ca-rich olivine, and anorthitic plagioclase (6). The APB underwent core formation ~2 My after Solar System formation (7), followed by mantle differentiation at 4,563.3 ± 0.4 Ma, i.e., ~4 to 5 My after Solar System formation (8), with magmatism continuing until ~4,556 Ma (7, 9–11). Angrites are typically classified into plutonic and volcanic types based on their origin. Plutonic angrites are characterized by coarse grains, near-equilibrium textures, and slower cooling rates compared to volcanic angrites (6). More recently, other petrological groups of angrites have been identified, including intermediate (e.g., Northwest Africa [NWA] 10463) and dunitic (e.g., NWA 8535) types (6, 12). All angrites exhibit the highest Fe/Mn ratios among achondrites and homogeneous mass-independent isotopic signatures in O, Cr, and Ti, consistent with a magma ocean phase on their parent body (8, 13, 14).

Stable isotope fractionation is a powerful tracer of planetary evaporation processes (15), and isotopic studies of both refractory and volatile elements have been applied to angrites, including Fe (16), Mg (17), Si (18), K (19), and Rb (20). Fe, Mg, and Si are major elements that, together with O, comprise much of the mass of rocky planetary bodies. Although Fe, Mg, and Si are typically to be nonvolatile during planetary formation (21) due to their high nebular half condensation temperatures (Tc50%) of 1,338 K, 1,343 K, and 1,314 K, respectively, they may exhibit volatile behavior under extreme conditions. Angrites have heavier Fe (22), Mg (17), and Si (18, 23) isotope compositions relative to chondrites (Fig. 1). However, the causes of these heavy isotope signatures for angrites are still debated. For example, multiple hypotheses have been proposed to explain the heavy Fe isotopic signatures of angrites. Sossi et al. (16) attributed this to Fe evaporation but were agnostic as to whether this occurred in the solar nebula or during accretion of the APB, while Wang et al. (22) and Dauphas et al. (24) proposed that magmatic and nebular processes, respectively, could also contribute to this heavy Fe signature. High-precision Mg isotope data revealed a 25Mg/24Mg difference of ~0.06‰ between one angrite and chondrites, interpreted as Mg evaporation during planetesimal magma ocean events (17). However, the mega-cryst olivines (up to a centimeter in size) and matrix in volcanic angrites (e.g., D’Orbigny) do not appear chemically equilibrated with each other (25), and the potential sample heterogeneity may complicate interpretation of the bulk composition of individual samples. Additionally, high-precision Mg isotope data are limited to only one angrite, D’Orbigny, which may not be sufficient for estimating the bulk Mg isotopic composition of the APB. There is also debate regarding the heavy Si isotopic composition of angrites, which may be due either to Si evaporation during planetesimal impacts (18) or to equilibrium Si isotope fractionation between gaseous SiO and solid forsterite during condensation from nebular gas (23).

A recent study employing K isotopes (Tc50% = 993 K) to trace volatile depletion in angrites reported a large range of 41K/39K fractionation of ~1.5‰, with especially strong enrichment in the light isotope relative to chondrites. This was interpreted as partial K condensation following K evaporation on the APB (19). However, K is fluid-mobile and a major component in continental crust (~18,100 ppm), making it susceptible to terrestrial contamination through fluid–rock interactions in desert environments (27). Previous studies of desert eucrites (from asteroid 4 Vesta) with [K] < 700 ppm have shown systematically light K isotope enrichment, likely due to terrestrial contamination of the budget of K, estimated to range from 2 to 51% by mass (28). All angrite samples studied for K isotopes by ref. 19 were desert meteorites, and angrites generally have even lower K contents (16 to 110 ppm) than eucrites, making them more prone to terrestrial contamination. The correlation between [K] and K isotopes in angrites may therefore result from a mixture of primitive K in angrites and terrestrial contamination with light δ41K values, as low as –1.23‰ (28). Rubidium isotope measurements (Tc = 752 K), using the same sample aliquots from ref. 19, have also been applied to angrites (20). Similar concerns about weathering effects may apply to Rb isotopes (29). Nevertheless, both Hu et al. (19) and Wang et al. (20) argued that the samples experienced limited terrestrial weathering based on rare earth elements (REEs). However, REEs do not necessarily behave like alkali elements such as K and Rb, which are more soluble in water and susceptible to redistribution during fluid–rock interactions.

Owing to its high cosmochemical abundance (>1 wt% in chondrites), nickel (Ni) is another element whose isotopic composition may provide insights into planetary differentiation processes in the early Solar System. Nickel is a siderophile element, but core formation does not appear to strongly fractionate Ni isotopes, as evidenced from comparative planetology, experimental, and first-principles studies (30–34). Consequently, Ni can serve as a useful proxy for tracing extreme volatile depletion on the APB. For example, recent studies have identified Ni isotope fractionation resulting from impact processes and surface evaporation on Vesta (35). Nickel, with Tc50% = 1,363 K (21), is more volatile than Mg, Fe, and Si during evaporation of silicate melts relevant to planetary differentiation stages (36, 37), suggesting that Ni isotopes may help elucidate the heavy isotope signatures of Fe, Mg, and Si in angrites.

Discussion

Chondritic δ60/58Ni of the APB Core: Core Formation.

The APB is thought to have been an asteroid, possibly comparable in size to Vesta (~260 km in diameter; refs. 6 and 8), although some estimates extend to nearly the size of the Moon (42). Based on the partition coefficient of Ni between metal and silicate, Ni should predominantly partition into the core rather than remain in the silicate portions of the APB under low-pressure (than Earth) conditions at the core–mantle boundary (43). The siderophile behavior of Ni during planetesimal core formation is consistent with the very low Ni contents (<20 ppm) observed in unbrecciated eucrites, which are thought to have originated from the silicate portion of Vesta (44, 45). However, plutonic angrites—except for AdoR—exhibit exceptionally high Ni concentrations, ranging from 2,000 to 6,000 ppm (Table 1), significantly higher than those of terrestrial basalts, which typically contain <400 ppm Ni (46). The elevated Ni concentrations of plutonic angrites can be attributed to the occurrence of metal (4 to 10 vol.%), with kamacite grains containing approximately 6.5 wt% Ni (47).

The presence of such high-Ni kamacite grains contributes significantly to the high Ni concentration in plutonic angrites, causing them to be significantly Ni-enriched relative to volcanic angrites, eucrites, and terrestrial basalts. In addition, the inverse correlation of [Ni] with Mg# (Fig. 2B) also supports mixing between silicate and Ni-bearing kamacite in plutonic angrites, producing a trend opposite to that predicted for fractional crystallization of mafic minerals. For example, olivine crystallization or accumulation is expected to simultaneously decrease or increase Ni concentration and Mg# of the melt, due to the higher compatibility of Ni and Mg in olivine than Fe.

The origin of metal in plutonic angrites remains debated, with possibilities ranging from an exotic source, such as chondrites (48) or iron meteorites (47), or a domestic source from the APB core. Chondrites, which are rich in Ni (~10,000 ppm) and highly siderophile elements (HSE) (8, 49), could theoretically contribute to the Ni and HSE enrichment observed in some plutonic angrites. However, as noted by Baghdadi et al. (47), to match the metal and sulfide content observed in certain plutonic angrites, at least 20% chondrite addition would be required. If ordinary chondrites (noncarbonaceous; chosen for their isotope similarity) were added, this would have likely shifted the mass-independent O isotope composition (Δ17O) by 0.2‰ (47), which is inconsistent with the highly homogeneous Δ17O compositions (within 0.007‰) observed between bulk volcanic and plutonic angrites (13). Baghdadi et al. (47) proposed that iron meteorites, which lack O, could be the source of the metal, as this would explain the absence of an O isotope shift. However, the metal phases in some plutonic angrites are very small (10 to 100 μm) relative to normal iron meteorites (47, 50). Introducing an iron meteorite would require mechanisms to fragment it into such small particles, which is challenging to account for. Furthermore, only plutonic angrites display Ni and HSE enrichment, while volcanic angrites, which are poorer in Ni, contain far less metal (51). Based on their petrology and geochronology, volcanic angrites likely crystallized at or near the APB’s surface, undergoing rapid cooling, whereas plutonic angrites formed at greater depths (6, 8). Thus, it is unlikely that the addition of iron meteorite material occurred only in deep plutonic melts.

Alternatively, it is also possible that the metal phases (e.g., kamacite) originated from the APB’s core. This phenomenon, where core material is present in silicate portions of the parent body, is observed in other differentiated meteorites, such as aubrites (52). Two primary explanations for the presence of core material in silicate melts include incomplete core segregation (52) and core–mantle interaction (53). Either scenario aligns with the crystallization or migration of core material into deep melts, subsequently forming plutonic angrites. The well-defined internal Hf–W isochron of NWA 2999/4931, including its metal phases, yields an absolute model age of ~4,560 Ma, consistent with the internal U–Pb and Mn–Cr ages of NWA 2999/4931. This concordance further supports an endogenous origin for the metals and their derivation from the APB core (7, 8, 11, 54).

Generally, angrite mineralogy and petrology show limited evidence of brecciation or shock effects, except for the two metal-rich plutonic angrites such as Rafsa 005 and NWA 2999 and paired stones (7, 55) and the volcanic angrite NWA 7203 (56), which would suggest that most of the angrites are free from chondritic contamination after recent melting and crystallization (6). Nevertheless, additional angrites such as the intermediate angrite, NWA 10463, with [Ni] of 129 ppm, have a near-chondritic δ60/58Ni value 0.203 ± 0.012‰, which might reflect a chondritic contamination. The average δ60/58Ni value of Ni-rich plutonic angrites (0.202 ± 0.028‰; 2SE, N = 10) overlaps with the chondritic δ60/58Ni average of approximately 0.24‰ (31, 32, 40, 41). This similarity supports the notion that core–mantle differentiation does not effectively fractionate Ni isotopes (30–34). Consequently, for Ni isotopes, Ni-rich plutonic angrites likely represent the core composition of the APB.

Suprachondritic δ60/58Ni in the Mantle of APB: A High-Energy Impact.

Olivine is a dominant mineral in the mantles of many terrestrial asteroids, including those of Vesta (57), parent bodies of ureilites (58), and pallasites (59). Given Ni’s high compatibility in olivine (60), most Ni (>99%) in the asteroidal mantle is expected to be hosted by olivine. Although mantle rocks from the APB have not been directly observed, angrite dunites [such as NWA 8535 (12)] and mega-olivine crystals in volcanic angrites (50) offer clues as to the APB mantle composition. Chemical and O isotope analyses of the mega-olivine in D’Orbigny volcanic angrites suggest that these olivines are not exotic, implying they represent the APB mantle (25, 50). Our elemental mapping of mega-olivine from two volcanic angrites, A 881371 and A 12209, reveals no chemical zoning (e.g., Mg, Fe, and Ni) in the cores of these olivines (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2). In contrast, the olivine in NWA 8535 is highly zoned with a Mg-rich core and an Fe-rich rim (12), indicating that diffusion processes (61, 62) have possibly fractionated Ni isotopes within and among olivine grains in NWA 8535. Such diffusive re-equilibrium and associated large kinetic Ni isotope fractionation has been previously reported for iron meteorites and pallasites (63–65). We note that this does not affect the bulk composition of the whole rock (with mass > 1 g), provided a representative sample is taken.

In order to avoid the potentially large kinetic fractionation effects caused by zonation of olivine grains in NWA 8535, a large chip weighing over 1 g was powdered for Ni isotope analysis in this study. Based on the modal abundance of olivine and their average grain size, this powder averages ~1,000 individual olivine grains from NWA 8535, which should be sufficient to overcome its internal heterogeneity. Based on the above evaluation of volcanic angrites, we interpret the equilibrated mega-olivines in A 881371 and A 12209, and the bulk composition of NWA 8535 to best represent the APB’s mantle δ60/58Ni composition.

Ni concentrations of the two internally equilibrated mega-olivine cores (~85 ppm) from A 881371 and A 12209 are lower than that of the olivine in NWA 8535 (~266 ppm) and the bulk NWA 8535 (~220 ppm). Primitive angrite melts are expected to become progressively depleted in Ni as olivine and other mafic minerals crystallize. Therefore, the lower Ni contents of the mega-olivines from volcanic angrites indicate that they likely originated from a more evolved mantle source than NWA 8535. All three mantle-derived and olivine-rich samples (bulk NWA 8535 and mega-olivines in A 881371 and A 12209) show elevated δ60/58Ni values, ranging from 0.422 ± 0.008‰ to 0.655 ± 0.012‰ (Fig. 2D), in contrast with the chondritic average of ~0.24‰ (31, 32, 40, 41). Experimental evidence shows that olivine crystallizing from melt fractionates Ni isotopes, with a fractionation factor: Δ60/58NiOl–melt of –0.142 ± 0.031‰ at 1,000 K (66), causing the melt to become isotopically heavier in Ni as its Ni content decreases. However, olivine from the later stage of differentiation (volcanic angrites with lower [Ni]) exhibits similar- or slightly heavier δ60/58Ni values (0.422 ± 0.008‰ and 0.655 ± 0.012‰) than the earlier-formed dunite (NWA 8535 with higher [Ni]) with a δ60/58Ni value of 0.469 ± 0.013‰, suggesting that the suprachondritic δ60/58Ni values in these samples may not stem from magmatic processes. In addition, the matrix samples of A 881371 and A 12209 contain higher [Ni] (89 to 108 ppm) than expected for silicate melts equilibrated with the associated mega-olivines in volcanic angrites (86 to 83 ppm). This mismatch in Ni concentration provides strong evidence that the mega-olivines did not crystallize directly from the matrix melt.

A plausible alternative explanation for the heavy Ni isotope signatures observed in APB mantle samples is Ni evaporation, which preferentially enriches the residue in the heavier isotopes of Ni (Fig. 3). For example, early Zn isotope studies of lunar samples revealed that evaporation processes left the residual material enriched in heavy Zn isotopes (15). Several potential mechanisms on the APB could have induced Ni evaporation: 1) surface eruptions and lava flow outgassing, 2) nebular processes, 3) primary magma ocean degassing, and 4) impact events. Volcanic angrites with old ages and rapid cooling rates (6) represent surface lavas on the APB surface. But they do not exhibit heavy Ni enrichment like that found in olivine-rich samples, indicating that volcanic outgassing likely did not drive Ni evaporation in the APB’s mantle. Nebular processes, which can fractionate elemental and isotopic abundances in the protoplanetary disk (23, 67), would alter the Ni isotope composition globally, which is inconsistent with the observed differences in δ60/58Ni between the APB’s core, mantle, and crust (discussed in the following section). Thus, the APB’s mantle did not inherit its heavy δ60/58Ni values from precursor materials.

Schematic illustration of volatile element depletion on the APB inferred from Ni stable isotopes. (A) Heating from the decay of 26Al generated a global magma ocean (e.g., ~1,500 K), leading to evaporation of volatile elements, while refractory elements such as Ni, Mg, Fe, and Si were largely retained. At this stage, the APB preserved chondritic δ60/58Ni values. Similar processes likely occurred on other differentiated bodies, e.g., Earth, Mars, Vesta, and ureilite parent body, of which the mantle has chondritic or subchondritic δ60/58Ni values (30, 32, 35, 38, 41). (B) During and after the magma ocean stage, the APB underwent core–mantle–crust differentiation. Because core formation does not significantly fractionate Ni isotopes, the mantle retains a chondritic δ60/58Ni. The evaporated vapor likely recondensed to the APB crust. (C) A subsequent high-energy impact caused extensive mantle remelting at very high temperatures (up to ~4,000 K). Moderately refractory elements such as Ni, Mg, Fe, and Si were partially volatilized, leaving behind isotopically heavy residues. At the same time, exchange of Ni between the molten mantle and the core may have acted to buffer the isotopic composition of the mantle. This impact might also have triggered leakage of core material (purple triangles) into the mantle, later incorporated into plutonic angrite melts. (D) Rapid cooling of the impact-induced melts was followed by renewed mantle–crust differentiation. Vapors enriched in compatible elements, e.g., Ni and Mg, and their isotopically light components could have recondensed onto the crust rich in incompatible elements (e.g., Fe and Si). Subsequent volcanism produced surface lavas (e.g., volcanic angrites) that mixed with this recondensed vapor material, resulting in lighter δ60/58Ni signatures.

Magma oceans generated by 26Al decay are thought to have formed early in the solar system due to 26Al decay, which provided enough heat to melt planetary bodies (68–70). However, unlike the APB, Earth and other achondrites (e.g., HEDs, Martian, and lunar meteorites, ureilites, and aubrites) do not show suprachondritic δ60/58Ni values in their mantles (30, 31, 41, 71), suggesting that Ni could not escape during the magma ocean (68).

Impact processes are common in the early Solar System. High kinetic energies associated with impacts can potentially introduce sufficient heat to form a secondary magma ocean and reach significant temperatures (e.g., 4,000 K) to cause Ni evaporation (72) (Fig. 3). High-precision mass-independent O isotope analyses recently revealed mass-independent 17O/16O variations in some angrite matrices, potentially caused by high-temperature impact mixing (25). We undertook thermodynamic modeling to better understand the Ni isotope fractionation during high-temperature evaporation from silicate melts. The method is detailed in SI Appendix, Table S2 and is predicated on determining the relative volatilities of the elements from their free energies of vaporization from silicate melts. By computing relative volatilities, the difficulty of computing absolute losses, which depend on poorly constrained physical parameters such as the mass of the APB and the timescale of evaporation, are circumvented. To do so, we fix the Mn depletion experienced by the APB to 50% of the CI chondritic value, as indicated by its high Fe/Mn ratio and low Mn/Mg ratio (SI Appendix). We apply the method to the vaporization of Mn, Ni, Fe, Si, and Mg, each of which proceeds according to an n = 2 stoichiometry (36). That is, the formal charge of the metal oxide (e.g., Ni2+O) is two higher than that of the gas species (e.g., Ni0).

This exercise shows that among the quintet of elements, Ni is always the most volatile under any temperature condition, followed by Mn < Fe < Si < Mg (Fig. 4), which is consistent with the larger isotope differences between APB and chondrites for Ni compared to Si, Mg, and Fe (Fig. 1). As detailed in ref. 73, the reactions become less discriminating as temperature inceases. At 1,500 K, 99.3% of the initial Ni budget in the silicate liquid is lost for 50% loss of Mn, whereas as at 2,500 K, this decreases marginally to 98.6% [i.e., fvap(Ni) ~ 0.99]. Given the similarity in evaporation stoichiometry and relative isotopic mass difference to Fe, the fractionation factor is taken to be Δ60/58NiNi(g)-NiO(l) ~ −1.5 × 106/T2 (16). For a simple Rayleigh distillation process, this translates to a Δ60/58NiNi(g)-NiO(l) of −0.67‰ (1,500 K) to −0.24‰ (2,500 K), and given fvap(Ni) = 0.99, the resultant δ60Ni in the residues ranges from 3.1‰ (1,500 K) to 1.1‰ (2,500 K). Such fractionationed values are not observed among magmatic products from the APB, suggesting that; i) such significant Mn (and therefore Ni) loss did not occur and/or ii) the effective value of fvap(Ni) is much lower than calculated. The degeneracy between the unknown values of fvap(Ni) and Δ60/58NiNi(g)-NiO(l) precludes accurate determination of the temperature of Ni loss.

Thermodynamic modeling shows the fraction remaining of Mn, Fe, Mg, Si, and Ni (i.e., 1-fvap), for a fvap(Mn) = 0.5 at three discrete temperatures (1,500 K, blue; 2,000 K, green; 2,500 K, red). Ni is more volatile than Mn, Fe, Si, and Mg at different temperatures.

Both scenarios are plausible. For example, some Mn loss from the APB may have occurred in the solar nebula, for which the relevant Ni vaporization/condensation reaction becomes Ni(s) = Ni(g) (21), and it remains less volatile than Mn. Another possibility is that Ni loss from the APB occurred from silicate melts, but the Ni content of its mantle was buffered by equilibrium with the core. Because the vast majority (>99%) of the Ni is hosted in the core, any evaporative loss from the mantle buffers its isotopic composition to that of the core (δ60/58Ni = 0.202 ± 0.028‰). Fe isotopes should likewise be affected by core–mantle equilibrium, but because most Fe partitions into the core, any fractionation is mass-balance diluted in the mantle. This, together with intrinsically smaller metal–silicate fractionation, explains why Fe shows a weaker isotope effect than Si (Fig. 1), despite its slightly higher volatility (Fig. 4). Nevertheless, this exercise serves to demonstrate the greater proclivity for Ni to be lost by vaporization, relative to Mn, Fe, Mg, and Si, even if only locally during emplacement of APB-derived magmas onto the surface. Due to the unknown initial Ni content in the APB’s mantle, we cannot apply further models to simulate the actual Ni loss and Ni isotope fractionation on APB.

Notably, the APB core composition (represented by Ni-rich plutonic angrites) is chondritic, unlike the mantle’s suprachondritic δ60/58Ni. Since core formation does not fractionate Ni isotopes (30, 32–34), the chondritic and nonheavy δ60/58Ni value of the core suggests that impacts only remelted the APB’s mantle, and the core survived from melting. Hf-W dating indicates APB core formation occurred within ~2 Ma after calcium-aluminum-rich inclusions (7), suggesting that the impact event postdated core formation. The 53Mn–53Cr dating of angrites (~4,563.2 ± 0.3 Ma, ~4.1 Ma after solar system formation) may record this impact-triggered, second-stage magma ocean that remelted the APB mantle (8). This scenario also aligns with models suggesting that core fragments could mix with the mantle due to turbulence and heating from such impacts (74). These impacts and subsequent evaporation could also lead to isotopically heavy Fe (16) and Si (18) in angrites, distinct from magmatic or nebular processes (22, 23).

Subchondritic δ60/58Ni in the Crust of APB: A Partial Recondensation.

On average, volcanic angrites display considerable variability in δ60/58Ni values (ranging from −0.470 ± 0.018‰ to 0.262 ± 0.012‰), which are systematically lighter than those found in chondrites and the core and mantle of the APB. This wide δ60/58Ni variation is evident in three separate dissolutions of bulk D’Orbigny (−0.258 ± 0.009‰, −0.122 ± 0.019‰, and 0.262 ± 0.012‰, respectively). In volcanic angrites, two matrix samples from A 12209 and A 881371 also exhibit subchondritic δ60/58Ni values (−0.094 ± 0.003‰ and −0.073 ± 0.011‰). Thus, considering the higher δ60/58Ni values in megacrystic-olivines within these two angrites, the δ60/58Ni variability among D’Orbigny and other volcanic angrites likely results from the mixing of megacrystic-olivines (with δ60/58Ni values of ~0.42 to ~0.65‰) and matrix material, possibly characterized by δ60/58Ni values of −0.08 to −0.26‰, as represented by D’Orbigny and the matrices of A 12209 and A 881371.

In the matrix of volcanic angrites, our LA-ICP-MS data indicate that olivines and pyroxenes in A 12209 serve as the primary Ni-hosting minerals, with [Ni] concentrations of 70 to 230 ppm and 32 to 37 ppm, respectively (Datasets S1 and S2), compared to the bulk angrites containing 30 to 80 ppm Ni. Minor metal and sulfide phases (Fe-Ni-S) within the matrix, as observed in D’Orbigny (51), also contribute to the Ni budget in these volcanic angrites. The subchondritic δ60/58Ni values in volcanic angrites cannot be attributed to magmatic processes alone, as the crystallization of olivine and other mafic minerals typically leads to progressively heavier Ni isotopic compositions in the melt. This is because silicate minerals such as olivine are expected to preferentially partition the lighter isotopes of Ni when equilibrated with silicate melts (66). Previous studies on high-precision, mass-independent O isotope data for volcanic angrite matrices indicate the potential admixture of chondritic materials by microimpacts (25). However, no known bulk solar system body has δ60/58Ni values as low as −0.20‰ (30–32, 40, 41, 71), suggesting that the light Ni isotopic composition in volcanic angrites is unrelated to the addition of exotic materials.

The isotopically light Ni is also unlikely to be the result of evaporation, as this typically leaves behind a heavy Ni isotope residue and a vapor with a lighter isotopic composition. Instead, it is possible that this vapor itself contributed to the light Ni isotopic composition in volcanic angrites. Following a high-energy impact, a Ni-rich vapor would have formed from the melted silicate portion of the APB, primarily reflecting mantle composition. As the temperature of the magma ocean wanes, this vapor may cool and recondense onto a crust enriched in incompatible, lithophile elements (e.g., Ca, Fe, Al, and Si) but poor in compatible elements (e.g., Ni, Mg). This recondensation could have occurred under kinetic conditions similar to those in evaporation processes, where light Ni isotopes, which have higher velocities, preferentially condensed. Consequently, Ni in the condensates became isotopically lighter relative to the APB residue.

This vapor partially recondensed onto the APB’s surface and subsequently mixed with the volcanic angrite melts, resulting in the low δ60/58Ni values observed in the angrite matrices. Notably, the volcanic angrites do not exhibit light Fe and Si isotope compositions. This can be explained by 1) Fe and Si being less volatile than Ni (Fig. 4); 2) the APB potentially having a Ni-rich source, as suggested by the Ni enrichment observed in plutonic angrites (Fig. 2C); 3) Relative to Ni, Fe and Si are incompatible elements that are rich in APB surface crust. This potential vapor recondensation process also suggests that the size of the APB could have been larger than a Vesta-sized asteroid (~260 km), which is required for the APB to have sufficient gravity to retain the vapor. Such requirement of a relatively large parent body size is consistent with previous experimental studies (42).

Materials and Methods

Except olivine grain samples, other angrite meteorite samples were first powdered, using a boron carbide mortar and pestle. The resulting sample powders were accurately weighed and placed in Teflon beakers. 2 mL conc. HF and 1 mL conc. HNO3 were added into the Savillex teflon beakers (7 mL or 15 mL). Plutonic, intermediate, and dunite samples were processed in Parr Bombs for ensuring full dissolution, e.g., chromites. For other samples, these were placed in Savillex teflon beakers, which were in turn housed in bombs in order to help to minimize chemical blanks. After cooling, the solution was dried down, and then 3 mL conc. HNO3 was added to redissolve the sample and heated to 180 °C in an oven for another 2 d. After drying down again, the sample solution was finally dissolved in 1 mL of 4 M HNO3.

Before the addition of a 61Ni-62Ni double spike (41), a 1 to 5% v/v aliquot of the sample solutions were analyzed to determine elemental concentrations on an Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometer (ICP-MS), housed at Wuhan SampleSolution Analytical Technology Co., Ltd. (e.g., ref. 45) or at the University of Bristol.

Sample solutions with around 400 to 800 ng of Ni were mixed with the double spike. The samples and spikes were heated in 6 N HCl in Teflon beaker at 120 °C overnight for complete homogenization. Separation of Ni from matrix elements was achieved using a four-step ion-exchange procedure. In the first step, AG50-X8 cation exchange (2 mL) resin was used to separate Ni from the lithophile elements such as Al, Mg, Ca, and Ti with acetic acid. Samples were completely dissolved in 0.3 mL 10 M HCl, and then 1.7 mL 100% acetic acid was added to the sample before loading onto the resin. Nickel has no affinity for resin sites in strong HCl, and elutes immediately following loading, and was collected with an additional 10 mL 1.5 M HCl/85% (v/v) acetic acid (66, 75). The second and the third columns in this study are the same as the first and second columns described in ref. 41. Sample solutions were dried down from the first column and dissolved in 1 mL 1 N HNO3, then they were loaded onto the precleaned and preconditioned same columns (2 mL AG50-X8 cation exchange resin) in the first step. The matrix elements were eluted by 10 mL 0.5 M HF (Fe, Ti, V, and Al) and 15 mL 1 M HNO3 (Na and K), respectively. Then Ni was collected with 10 mL 6 M HCl. In the third column, we use dimethylglyoxime (DMG) to further purify Ni from most of other elements. DMG, a strongly Ni-specific chelating agent (76), has been widely used in Ni purifications in previous studies. The samples were dissolved in 50 μL 10 M HCl to which 950 μL acetone was added just before loading onto preconditioned columns filled with 250 μL AG50-X4 cation exchange resin. The matrix elements were eluted directly with the aid of an additional 1.1 mL 0.5 M HCl/95% (v/v) acetone. Nickel was subsequently recovered with 1.5 mL 0.5 M HCl/95% (v/v) acetone/0.1 M DMG and 0.5 mL 0.5 M HCl/95% (v/v) acetone, in beakers with ~200 μL MQ water (66). Acetone was evaporated and dried down on a hotplate at a low temperature of 80 °C. The addition of water and evaporation at low-temperature is to prevent Ni evaporation in acetone. The fractions were first treated with ~0.5 mL concentrated HNO3 at 130 °C, and, after drying down, conc. HNO3 (0.75 mL) + 30% H2O2 (0.25 mL) were added to redissolve the samples at 130 °C overnight. The aim of this step is to ensure any organics, largely resulting from DMG that may have been leached from the resin, are quantitatively broken down. Since Fe cannot be completely removed by the previous three columns (especially for the basaltic samples with high Fe/Ni ratios, >1,000) and DMG also contains some Fe itself, we used a final anion-exchange column with 0.5 mL AG1 X8 (200 to 400 mesh) to remove Fe (77). Samples were first dissolved in 0.5 mL 10 M HCl. After passing through the column, another 1.5 mL 10 M HCl was added for collecting Ni. The Ni yields of the entire purification procedure were >90%, and the total Ni blanks were 0.9 to 1.4 ng. After the column chemistry, conc. HNO3 (0.75 mL) + 30% H2O2 (0.25 mL) were added to the samples, which were then heated at 130 °C overnight, again in aid of destroying any organics that may have potentially been leached from the resin.

The Ni stable isotope measurements were performed on a Neptune Multicollector-ICP-MS housed at the University of Bristol. The signal intensities of 56Fe + 40Ar16O, 57Fe + 40Ar17O, 58Ni + 58Fe + 40Ar18O, 60Ni, 61Ni, and 62Ni were simultaneously measured using L3, L2, L1, H1, H2, and H3 cups in static mode, respectively. All amplifiers have 1011-ohm feedback resistors. We use a combination of Jet sample + H skimmer cones, an Aridus II desolvator (N2 was added to enhance the signal of Ni+ and suppress that of ArO+, especially for 40Ar18O+ that is isobaric with 58Ni+), and a ~60 μL min−1 microconcentric PFA nebulizer (ESI) for sample introduction. All the Ni isotope measurements were run in low-resolution mode at the peak center (31, 78). The interference of 58Fe+ on 58Ni+ was corrected by monitoring signal of 57Fe+ and calculating 58Fe+ assuming a natural 58Fe/57Fe ratio. As noted above, 40Ar18O+ is also a potential interference on 58Ni. However, the signal intensity for 40Ar16O+ is only 5 to 10 mV, which results in a 40Ar18O+ and 40Ar17O+ signal intensity of only up to ~0.02 mV and ~0.004 mV, calculated by assuming natural 16O/18O and 17O/18O ratio of ~500 and ~2,700, respectively. The very low abundance of ArO interferences were achieved by adding N2 to the Aridus II desolvator. Hence, the interference of 40Ar18O+ can be neglected relative to the 58Ni signal of 15,000 to 20,000 mV and 5 to 8 mV of the sample and blank solution respectively. Nickel solutions of ~200 ppb yielded 58Ni+ (L1 cup) signals of 15 to 20 V for both samples and standard solutions. Each measurement consisted of 60 cycles, with an integration time of 4.194 s for each cycle. Instrumental blank measurements with 10 cycles of 4.194 s were performed and subtracted from the signals of sample solutions (off-line). Each sample was measured at least three times. NIST SRM 986 was used as the isotope standard, and the spiked NIST SRM 986 was run every two to three samples. The Ni double spike data reduction procedure follows that described by Klaver et al. (41) and Klaver and Coath (79).

To characterize the precision and accuracy of our measurements, we also report δ60/58Ni values for the USGS standard rocks BCR-2 and AGV-1, which have chemical compositions similar to angrites (Table 1). Our results are consistent with published data and show comparable or better precision (32, 78, 80–82). Eleven independent dissolutions of BCR-2 yield a δ60/58Ni external reproducibility of 0.026‰ (2SD), which we adopt as the external uncertainty of our Ni isotope measurements.
