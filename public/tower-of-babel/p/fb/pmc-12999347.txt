Dataset for developing allometric models to estimate aboveground biomass in secondary tropical dry forests of western Mexico
Authors: Adrián Bojórquez, Angelina Martínez-Yrízar, Alberto Búrquez, Víctor J Jaramillo, Francisco Mora, Patricia Balvanera, Juan C Álvarez-Yépiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a dataset on tree size and aboveground biomass for 303 trees from 27 tropical dry forest species harvested along a chronosequence of secondary forests on the coast of Jalisco, Mexico. The studied species account for ∼80% of the total basal area across secondary forest stands in this landscape. Data were generated through destructive sampling of 9 to 14 individuals per species, covering their full diameter at breast height (DBH) distribution. Before harvesting, each tree was measured, cut at ground level, and its fresh mass was determined. For dry mass calculation, a wood sample taken from the base of each tree was oven-dried in the laboratory following a standardized protocol to estimate water content and total dry mass. This dataset includes the following variables for each harvested tree: DBH, total height (H), and aboveground dry biomass (AGB). This information provides valuable data for developing allometric models that can be applied to similar secondary tropical dry forests at broader spatial scales, including regional, national, or pantropical contexts.Keywords: Basal area, Diameter at breast height, Forest carbon stocks, Tree height, Tree phytomass, Tropical deciduous forest

Background

Tropical dry forests (TDF) represent about 42 % of the vegetation in tropical regions [1]. In the Neotropics, extensive areas previously converted to agriculture are abandoned, fostering the development of successional secondary forests with high potential for carbon accumulation. Currently, landscapes on the Pacific coast of Mexico consist of a mosaic of old-growth and secondary forests. However, methods to estimate biomass in secondary TDFs are scarce. Structural heterogeneity contributes to high uncertainty in estimates of aboveground biomass. Allometric models are widely applied in old-growth forest contexts but may not accurately represent the structure of trees in secondary forests. These trees commonly exhibit basal branching and multiple stems, which most likely alter their allometric relationships and may introduce bias in aboveground biomass estimates. Thus, in this work, we provide original data used to develop reliable allometric models for estimating aboveground biomass in secondary tropical dry forests of the Pacific coast of Jalisco, Mexico [2].

Data Description

Data were collected across a broad area of secondary tropical dry forest sites distributed outside the boundaries of the Chamela-Cuixmala Biosphere Reserve, on the coast of Jalisco in western Mexico (Fig. 1). Here, we provide direct measurements of aboveground biomass (AGB; dry mass), diameter at breast height (DBH) and total tree height (H), for individual trees of varying sizes, of the most important 27 woody species (see Supplementary Material) harvested along a chronosequence of secondary forest stands in this region. These data were used to develop and validate several allometric models, as described in detail in our previous study [2].

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

During the spring of 2012 (dry season), we conducted the destructive sampling of trees across 31 secondary tropical dry forest plots distributed over an area of 134,484 ha (Fig. 1). These plots, with successional ages between 5 and 45 years, were established to represent secondary tropical dry forests of the region, thereby capturing the structural and compositional diversity characteristic of these forests.

Selection of trees for harvesting

Following the approach of van Breugel et al. [3], we selected all woody species with DBH ≥ 1.0 cm contributing up to 80 % of the total basal area at the plot, successional stage and landscape level. This selection was based on the floristic inventory of our secondary forest plots provided by Mora et al. [4]. A total of 303 trees, representing 27 species across 11 families, were selected for harvesting (Supplementary Material). For each species, 9–14 individuals were sampled to represent the full natural DBH range (minimum to maximum) observed in secondary forest plots throughout the landscape.

Harvesting process and tree measurements

We followed a strict harvesting protocol. Before harvesting, DBH of each tree was measured to the nearest millimeter using a caliper or a measuring tape. Trees were cut down at ground level using a chainsaw (Fig. 2a). The total height of each felled tree was measured to the nearest cm along the main stem (Fig. 2b). Large stems were cut into smaller pieces (Fig. 2c) to facilitate field fresh mass determination (Fig. 2d). For multi-stemmed trees, only one stem corresponding to the target DBH size was measured and harvested. The fresh mass of branches and the whole stem was measured separately in the field using 20 and 50 kg scales with accuracies of 0.10 and 0.50 kg, respectively (Fig. 2d). To ensure accurate biomass estimation, sawdust produced during sawing was collected on a plastic tarp, weighed (Fig. 2e) and added to the total fresh mass of the tree. A stem wood sample was taken from every tree (Fig. 2f), stored in sealed plastic bags, and transported to the laboratory for moisture content determination, enabling the conversion of fresh mass to dry mass (Fig. 2g-h). Because direct measurement of whole‑tree dry mass was not feasible, we estimated it using the dry‑to‑fresh mass ratio derived from the corresponding wood sample and applied this ratio to the entire tree. Most trees were leafless at the time of harvest. When present, leaves were manually removed from a fraction of the crown (i.e., one quarter) and their fresh mass (Fig. 2i-j) was used to correct the total fresh mass of the tree. All logged trees were left in the field for further use by landowners as lumber or fuelwood.

Wood samples processing in the laboratory

The fresh mass of each wood sample (kept in sealed plastic bags to prevent moisture loss, Fig. 3a) was measured immediately after harvesting using an electronic balance with 0.001 g accuracy, after subtracting the known weight of the plastic bag (Fig. 3b). All samples were individually packed in labeled paper bags (Fig. 3c-d) and oven-dried at 105 °C during 75 h (stems), and 48 h (branches) to determine dry mass (Fig. 3e).

Fresh wood samples were individually stored in sealed plastic bags and transported to the laboratory (a). For fresh mass determination, samples were weighed (b) and subsequently prepared (c–d) for oven drying to constant weight (e). After drying, samples were weighed using an electronic balance with a precision of 0.001 g (b), and the values were used to convert fresh mass to dry mass.

Control of measurement errors

To minimize measurement errors, we followed standardized protocols in both field and laboratory (see Section 2). These included using a consistent cutting methodology, calibrated weighing scales, and standardized timing for fresh mass measurements. In the laboratory, dry mass was determined once constant weight was achieved. Outliers were checked by visually inspecting their position in fitted curves and evaluating R2 values; when detected, they were verified by double-checking the raw data. Furthermore, sample trees were deliberately selected to represent the full natural DBH range of each species, ensuring that only individuals within predefined DBH classes were harvested and thereby reducing potential bias in AGB estimates.

Limitations

Because patches of secondary forest stands were widespread across the landscape, the rugged terrain and limited accessibility posed major challenges for fieldwork. The dispersed distribution of these stands also required extensive travel to encompass the full DBH range of the 27 species included in the study. Furthermore, data collection was delayed because felling the sample trees required landowner permission, which restricted sampling areas and extended the duration of fieldwork.
