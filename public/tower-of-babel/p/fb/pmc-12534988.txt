Longitudinal changes in estimated glucose disposal rate and risk of cardiovascular disease among adults with cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome stages 0–3: a nationwide prospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaoming Zhang, Rui Zeng, Zhigang Wu, Yi Xiao, Siyi Wang, Yufei Zeng, Tenghui Fan, Huajing Wu, Kun Huang, Wan Zhu, Fayi Xie, Ke Zhu, Dongmei Ye, Zhiqi Jiang, Aizhang Zhu, Lihuan Chen, Mengxia Shi, Mingxing Lai, Ruohan Wu, Yunfeng Liu, Jiahui Bian, Xiaotong Sun, Jiang Wang, Wenwu Zhang, Yunzhi Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aging population and medical advancements have led to a rise in chronic conditions such as metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and chronic kidney disease, giving rise to the concept of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome. The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) is an important surrogate marker of insulin sensitivity; however, its longitudinal impact on CVD risk across CKM syndrome stages 0 to 3 remains unclear.MethodsData were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). After excluding participants lacking CKM stage 0–3 diagnostic indicators and those with incomplete data, a total of 3,503 eligible individuals were included. eGDR was calculated based on waist circumference, hypertension status, and HbA1c level. K-means clustering was applied to classify participants into five distinct trajectories based on eGDR dynamics. Cox proportional hazards models with increasing levels of adjustment were constructed to examine the association between eGDR and incident CVD. Additionally, restricted cubic spline (RCS) and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression analyses were conducted for further evaluation.ResultsDuring a three-year follow-up, 504 participants (14.39%) developed CVD. Compared with participants in the persistently high eGDR group (Class 1), the fully adjusted Cox models revealed significantly increased CVD risk in Class 4 (HR = 2.01; 95% CI 1.46–2.77; P < 0.001), and Class 5 (HR = 2.09; 95% CI 1.16–3.97; P < 0.05), while no significant association was observed for Class 2 and Class3. When cumulative eGDR was treated as a continuous variable, each unit increase in eGDR was associated with a 7% reduction in CVD risk (HR = 0.93; 95% CI 0.90–0.96; P < 0.001). Participants in the highest cumulative eGDR quintile exhibited a 66% lower risk of CVD compared to those in the lowest quintile in the fully adjusted model. RCS analysis showed an overall inverse relationship between cumulative eGDR and CVD risk, with a marked risk reduction when cumulative eGDR exceeded 28.5 (P for non-linearity > 0.05). WQS regression further identified waist circumference as the most influential component of eGDR in relation to CVD risk.ConclusionLower cumulative eGDR and unfavorable eGDR trajectories are significantly associated with increased CVD risk among individuals in CKM stages 0–3. These findings suggest that monitoring eGDR may enhance early CVD risk prediction and guide prevention strategies in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03286-9.Keywords: CKM syndrome, Estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), Cardiovascular disease, Longitudinal cohort, CHARLS

Introduction

With population aging and rapid advancements in biomedical technology, chronic conditions such as metabolic syndrome (MetS), cardiovascular disease (CVD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) have become pressing [1] global health concerns [2]. Recognizing the complex interplay among these conditions, the American Heart Association (AHA) recently proposed the concept of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome [3]. This framework underscores the bidirectional and synergistic interactions between metabolic dysfunction, CKD, and the cardiovascular system, which collectively contribute to an elevated risk of adverse cardiovascular events and multi-organ dysfunction. Mounting evidence has substantiated these interrelationships, positioning CKM syndrome at the forefront of modern clinical and epidemiological research [1, 4, 5].

Epidemiological studies have demonstrated a substantial increase in CVD risk as individuals progress from CKM stage 0 to stage 3. Between 2015 and 2020, approximately 25% of adults in the United States were estimated to be affected by CKM syndrome [6], with similar public health implications emerging in China. The management of CKM syndrome imposes a considerable burden on healthcare systems, exacerbating both healthcare costs and the risks of all-cause mortality and future cardio-cerebrovascular events [1]. Therefore, early identification of high-risk individuals within the CKM spectrum is essential for timely risk stratification and the implementation of preventive strategies.

The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) is a well-validated surrogate marker for insulin sensitivity, originally developed for use in individuals with diabetes. Derived from easily obtainable clinical variables such as waist circumference (WC), hypertension status, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), eGDR offers a non-invasive method for estimating insulin resistance. Accumulating evidence has linked lower eGDR values—reflective of greater insulin resistance—to heightened risks of CVD, stroke, and type 2 diabetes [7, 8]. These findings support the utility of eGDR as a prognostic indicator for adverse cardiometabolic outcomes, including in populations affected by CKM syndrome.

Despite the established cross-sectional associations between eGDR and CVD, most prior investigations have relied on single time point measurements, thereby failing to capture dynamic changes in insulin sensitivity over time [9]. Such static assessments may inadequately reflect the cumulative burden of metabolic dysfunction. To date, limited research has explored the longitudinal influence of eGDR trajectories on CVD outcomes, particularly among individuals in the early stages of CKM syndrome (stages 0–3) [10]. As a result, the long-term prognostic implications of evolving eGDR patterns remain insufficiently understood.

In light of these gaps, the present study utilizes nationally representative data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to examine the association between longitudinal changes in eGDR and the incidence of CVD among individuals in CKM stages 0–3. By investigating both cumulative eGDR exposure and distinct trajectory patterns, this study aims to provide novel insights into the temporal relationship between insulin resistance and cardiovascular risk, thereby informing targeted prevention strategies for this growing at-risk population.

Methods

Study design and data sources

Data were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [11], a nationally representative, prospective cohort study. The baseline survey (2011–2012) enrolled 17,708 participants across China using multistage probability sampling. Trained interviewers collected data on demographic characteristics, family structure, lifestyle factors, and health status through face-to-face computer-assisted personal interviews (CAPI) with standardized questionnaires. Follow-up assessments were conducted biennially, and subsets of participants also underwent anthropometric measurements and blood biomarker testing. As of now, four waves of follow-up data have been released. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University (Approval Number: IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Among 11,847 participants with complete baseline blood count data, 3,503 individuals were included in the final analysis based on the following exclusion criteria: (1) absence of diagnostic indicators for CKM stage 0–3 syndrome; (2) lack of complete blood count data in wave 3; (3) missing indicators for eGDR calculation; (4) missing age data or age < 45 years; (5) diagnosed with CVD prior to wave 3 or missing CVD information in wave 4; and (6) missing data on covariates. A detailed flowchart of the inclusion and exclusion process is shown in Fig. 1.

Definition of CKM syndrome stages 0–3

Cardiometabolic-kidney (CKM) syndrome was defined based on the 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) scientific statement [3], which classifies individuals into four stages reflecting cumulative risk for CVD.

Stage 0: No detectable CKM risk factors (i.e., normal BMI and waist circumference, normoglycemia, normotension, normal lipid profile, no kidney dysfunction, and no clinical or subclinical CVD);

Stage 0: No detectable CKM risk factors (i.e., normal BMI and waist circumference, normoglycemia, normotension, normal lipid profile, no kidney dysfunction, and no clinical or subclinical CVD);

Stage 1: Presence of abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 85 cm in women or ≥ 90 cm in men) and/or prediabetes (fasting glucose 100–124 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7%–6.4%);

Stage 1: Presence of abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 85 cm in women or ≥ 90 cm in men) and/or prediabetes (fasting glucose 100–124 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7%–6.4%);

Stage 2: Presence of overt metabolic risk factors (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, or metabolic syndrome) or renal dysfunction;

Stage 2: Presence of overt metabolic risk factors (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, or metabolic syndrome) or renal dysfunction;

Stage 3: Presence of subclinical cardiovascular disease.

Stage 3: Presence of subclinical cardiovascular disease.

Detailed diagnostic thresholds and criteria are presented in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Calculation of estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR)

In the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) was calculated using the following validated formula[12]: eGDR = 21.158 − (0.09 × WC) − (3.407 × HT) − (0.551 × HbA1c), where WC denotes waist circumference (cm), HT represents hypertension status (1 = diagnosed, 0 = not diagnosed), and HbA1c refers to hemoglobin A1c (%). eGDR values were computed at wave 1 (2011–2012) and wave 3 (2015). To assess long-term insulin sensitivity, cumulative eGDR was calculated as the time-weighted average between the two waves: Cumulative eGDR = [(eGDR₁ + eGDR₃)/2] × (2015 − 2012), a method adopted in previous research [13]. This approach reflects the average annual eGDR over the 3-year follow-up period (2012–2015), multiplied by the total duration to obtain a cumulative measure.

Definition of cardiovascular disease (CVD)

CVD events were identified based on self-reported physician diagnoses, including heart diseases (myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, heart failure, etc.) and stroke. During follow-up, new CVD events were assessed through the following questions: “Has a doctor ever told you that you had a heart attack, angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, or other heart problems?” “Has a doctor ever told you that you had a stroke?” [14, 15].

Participants were considered to have developed CVD if they reported a new physician-diagnosed event during follow-up. For those without CVD, follow-up time was defined as the duration from baseline to the last interview. For those with CVD events, the time-to-event was estimated based on the midpoint between interview waves or approximated as the integer year difference between the baseline and the wave with reported CVD.

Covariates

Baseline covariates included the following: Demographics: Age, sex, education level (lower secondary, upper secondary/vocational, tertiary), marital status (currently married: yes/no), and household registration (rural/urban). Health behaviors: Smoking status, drinking status, BMI, and sleep problems. Comorbidities and treatments: Systolic and diastolic blood pressure, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, kidney disease, and use of antidiabetic or lipid-lowering medications. Laboratory indicators: Fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C, LDL-C, C-reactive protein (CRP), HbA1c, and uric acid (UA).

Statistical analysis

K-means clustering, a rule-based unsupervised method with good scalability, was used to classify participants into five groups based on eGDR trajectories: Class 1: Persistently high (11.607–11.276), Class 2: Persistently moderate (9.949–9.996), Class 3: Moderately low-stable low (7.546–7.604), Class 4: High at baseline but decreased significantly over time (10.805–6.720), and Class 5: Markedly low-stable (6.092–5.403), as shown in Fig. 2. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables, median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables, and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Group differences were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to assess the association between eGDR and CVD incidence. Three models were constructed: Model I: Adjusted for age and sex; Model II: Further adjusted based on a directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), including education level, marital status, household registration, smoking, drinking, sleep problems, and BMI, as shown in Figure S1; Model III: Additionally adjusted for comorbidities [systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), diabetes, dyslipidemia, kidney disease], medication use (antidiabetic and lipid-lowering drugs), and baseline eGDR. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with knots at the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles were used to assess potential non-linear relationships between cumulative eGDR and CVD risk. Weights ranged from 0 to 1 and summed to 1, with higher values indicating greater relative importance. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.0 and EmpowerStats version 4.1. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Baseline characteristics of participants classified by changes in eGDR across CKM syndrome stages 0–3 are summarized in Table 1. A total of 3,503 participants met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The mean age of participants was 57.0 ± 6.0 years, with 46.07% being male. The mean eGDR was 9.46 ± 2.23 in 2012, decreasing to 8.77 ± 2.40 in 2015, while the average cumulative eGDR over the period was 27.34 ± 6.33.

When using the lowest mean eGDR group (Class 5) as the reference, participants in other groups exhibited higher rates of smoking and alcohol consumption, lower BMI, SBP, DBP, and WC, along with lower prevalence of diabetes, dyslipidemia, and heart disease. Additionally, they had lower levels of fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, C-reactive protein (CRP), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), HbA1c, and uric acid (UA), but higher levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) compared with class 5.

Association between eGDR and incident CVD

Over a three-year follow-up period, 504 participants (14.39%) developed CVD. Table 2 presents the Cox regression analyses examining the association between eGDR change/cumulative eGDR and the risk of incident CVD across CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Three covariate-adjusted models were constructed to evaluate this relationship. After adjusting for potential confounders, the risk of CVD was significantly higher in Class 4 (HR = 2.01, 95% CI 1.46–2.77, P < 0.001) and Class 5 (HR = 2.09, 95% CI 1.16–3.79, P < 0.05), compared with class 1. No significant association was observed for Class 2 and Class 3.

When cumulative eGDR was analyzed as a continuous variable, higher eGDR levels were associated with a decreased risk of CVD (HR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.90–0.96, P < 0.001). Further categorization into eGDR quintiles showed that participants in the highest quintile (Q5) experienced a 66% lower risk of CVD compared to those in the lowest quintile (Q1), in the fully adjusted model (Model III). A significant inverse trend in CVD risk with increasing cumulative eGDR was also observed.

Restrictive cubic spline analysis revealed a negative association between cumulative eGDR and CVD risk in the overall population (Fig. 3), with no evidence of non-linearity (P for non-linearity > 0.05). The risk of CVD notably decreased when cumulative eGDR exceeded 28.5. However, no significant linear or non-linear relationships were identified within any of the five subgroups defined by cluster analysis (all P for trend and P for non-linearity > 0.05).

Subgroup analyses

Stratified analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the eGDR–CVD association across various prespecified subgroups, including age, sex, education level, marital status, household registration type, smoking and drinking behaviors, BMI, and sleep disorder status. The association remained robust across all subgroups, with no significant interaction observed between changes in eGDR and any of the examined covariates (Table S3).

Weighted quantile sum (WQS) analyses

To assess the relative contribution of individual components of eGDR to CVD risk, WQS regression models were employed. The weights assigned to different components are illustrated in Figure S2. WC emerged as the most influential factor, with weights of 0.556 in wave 1 and 0.534 in wave 3, indicating its predominant role in the development of CVD.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between dynamic changes in eGDR and subsequent CVD risk among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. The principal findings are as follows: (1) individuals with persistently low eGDR or a rapid decline in eGDR had a significantly elevated risk of CVD compared to those with persistently high eGDR levels; (2) a linear inverse association was observed between cumulative eGDR and CVD risk, with a cut-off value of 28.5 (corresponding to an annual eGDR of 9.5); when cumulative eGDR was below 28.5, CVD risk increased (HR > 1), and conversely, risk decreased when above this threshold (HR < 1); (3) among the components constituting eGDR, WC contributed the most to CVD risk prediction, accounting for over 50% of the weighting.

Previous literature has consistently demonstrated that insulin resistance (IR) is closely linked to diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension—all recognized as major risk factors for CVD events [16–18]. The relationship between traditional IR markers such as HOMA-IR and the triglyceride glucose (TyG) index with CVD has been well established, with higher levels of these markers being predictive of increased CVD risk [19, 20]. However, their reliance on fasting glucose, insulin, or triglyceride levels limits their clinical utility in non-diabetic populations [21, 22]. Therefore, this study employed eGDR—a surrogate IR marker incorporating WC, hypertension status, and HbA1c—as a more practical and potentially more reliable predictor of CVD, in line with previous studies [23].

eGDR captures multiple aspects of metabolic health. WC, an anthropometric proxy for central obesity and visceral adiposity, is closely associated with IR [24, 25]. Elevated IR levels have been linked with increased hypertension risk in meta-analyses [26], and Mendelian randomization studies have identified elevated HbA1c as a causal factor for stroke [27]. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that eGDR may outperform traditional IR markers in predicting CVD risk among individuals in CKM stages 0–3 [28].

Several studies have reported that higher eGDR values are associated with a lower incidence of CVD [29, 30], while Liang et al. described a U-shaped relationship between eGDR and CVD risk in this population [10]. However, because eGDR components are variable over time, reliance on a single measurement may introduce bias [31]. In our study, eGDR trajectories were identified using K-means clustering based on repeated measures from the CHARLS dataset. Among the five identified trajectory classes, Classes 1–3 and Class 5 showed relative stability from 2011 to 2015. Persistently high eGDR levels were associated with a lower risk of CVD, while persistently low levels were linked to increased risk, suggesting a protective effect of higher baseline eGDR. Notably, Class 4 showed a substantial decline in eGDR over time, accompanied by a higher CVD risk, indicating that early detection of declining eGDR may help identify individuals who would benefit from preventive interventions.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis revealed a linear inverse association between cumulative eGDR and CVD risk, with an inflection point at 28.5 (average 9.5 per year), which contrasts with the previously reported U-shaped relationship [10]. Subgroup RCS analyses, however, did not show significant linear associations, possibly because extremely high eGDR values may indicate other conditions, such as wasting or sarcopenia [32, 33]. These findings underscore the importance of contextual interpretation of eGDR levels: for individuals with low eGDR, lifestyle modifications aimed at improving metabolic health may mitigate future CVD risk, whereas in those with very high eGDR, underlying comorbidities such as malnutrition or sarcopenia warrant further clinical evaluation. Personalized management strategies based on eGDR profiles may be an effective approach to reduce CVD incidence in the CKM population.

To assess potential effect modification, subgroup analyses were conducted across 11 variables, and no significant interactions were detected. However, no significant associations were observed in subgroups including males, unmarried individuals, urban residents, non-smokers, and non-drinkers. Previous studies have noted a higher incidence and mortality of CVD among men [34], and unmarried status has been associated with increased CVD risk [35]. Urban populations may experience greater exposure to environmental stressors [36], and smoking and alcohol consumption are well-known CVD risk factors [37]. These factors may attenuate the predictive value of eGDR trajectories for CVD in specific subgroups.

This study has several strengths. First, it is the first to investigate the association between dynamic eGDR changes and future CVD risk using a clustering approach in a large cohort of individuals with CKM stages 0–3. Second, it utilizes longitudinal data from a nationally representative study, with rigorous design, a large sample size, and extended follow-up. Third, confounding was addressed through adjustment based on a minimum sufficient set determined via DAGs, improving causal inference. Fourth, eGDR offers both clinical accessibility and predictive validity, making it a valuable tool in routine risk assessment for CVD in this population.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, the diagnosis of CVD in CHARLS was based on self-reported physician diagnoses, which may introduce information bias. However, previous studies have demonstrated acceptable validity of self-reported disease histories in large-scale longitudinal cohorts [38, 39]. Second, although cumulative eGDR has been increasingly applied in recent cohort studies, it has not yet been widely validated across diverse populations or clinical settings. Therefore, our findings based on this metric should be interpreted with caution.

Third, although we adjusted for baseline diabetes treatment, detailed information regarding the type, intensity, and duration of treatment was unavailable. Changes in diabetes management during the follow-up period may have influenced HbA1c levels, potentially confounding the observed associations between longitudinal changes in eGDR and CVD risk. Fourth, incomplete data resulting from missing physical or biochemical measurements may have introduced selection bias and affected the stability and generalizability of our estimates. Fifth, although individuals with baseline CVD were excluded, the potential influence of undiagnosed or subclinical CVD on eGDR trajectories cannot be entirely ruled out. This raises the possibility of reverse causation, thereby limiting causal inference. Finally, the study population consisted exclusively of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, which may limit the external validity of our findings. Further research is needed to validate these associations in more ethnically and geographically diverse populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that persistently low or rapidly declining eGDR levels were associated with a significantly increased risk of CVD among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. eGDR, as a simple, non-invasive, and clinically accessible indicator, holds promise for improving CVD risk stratification and prevention in this high-risk population. These findings provide novel insights into CVD pathogenesis and highlight the potential of using dynamic eGDR assessments to reduce the burden of CVD in the CKM 0–3 population.
