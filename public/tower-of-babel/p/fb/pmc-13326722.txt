Orthopedic preoperative evaluation and surgical strategies of hereditary multiple osteochondromas involving the spinal region
Authors: Shuzhong Liu, Jinyi Xing, An Song, Xi Zhou, Yong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hereditary multiple osteochondromas (HMO), previously known as hereditary multiple exostoses (HME), is a congenital skeletal developmental anomaly characterized by multiple osteochondromas that commonly grow outward from the metaphyses of long bones.Hereditary multiple exostoses is an autosomal dominant genetic disease characterized by multiple cartilage growth disorders, which affect the long bones of the limbs, scapula, and ribs, among others. Consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of spinal involvement is relatively lacking.For such patients, experience in terms of clinical screening, preoperative evaluation, surgical intervention indications and timing, surgical expertise and lessons learned, and follow-up is needed and critical.This review focuses on the clinical evaluation and orthopedic surgical treatment of HME patients with spinal involvement. It summarizes the patients’ diagnosis, clinical features, and treatment strategies based on evidence from the published literature.The clinical manifestations, location of onset, and surgical intervention are summarized in detail in this review. The above contents will help improve the clinical diagnosis and treatment level of HME patients with spinal involvement internationally.

The etiology and epidemiology of HME

Hereditary multiple exostoses (HME), also known as hereditary multiple osteochondromas (HMO), is an autosomal dominant genetic disease characterized by multiple cartilage-capped exostoses involving the metaphyses of long and flat bones (1, 2). The disease was first described in the medical literature by Boyer in 1814 (3). Solomon from the Royal National Orthopaedic Hospital and the Hospital for Sick Children in London was the first to study the disease from a genetic perspective in 1964 (4). Most HME patients can bear heterozygous inactivating mutations in the tumor suppressor gene EXT1 (exosomal mycosyltransferase-1) or EXT2 (exosomal mycosyltransferase-2) (5, 6). Genetics has shown that the EXT gene is the pathogenic gene of HME, and specific gene mutation sites have been associated with the severity of HME (5). However, EXT gene mutations cannot fully explain the occurrence of some HMEs, and HME cases without EXT1 or EXT2 pathogenic gene mutations account for 4–33% of the total (7). Another study found that the EXT gene family may contain the EXT3 gene, but it is unclear whether it serves as a pathogenic gene for HME (5).

Although some HME patients have mutations in the EXT1 and EXT2 gene loci, they have not shown corresponding multiple tumors. A pathogenic mechanism for HME, based on the second-hit hypothesis of tumors (second mutation theory), has been proposed (8, 9, 10); somatic mutations occur again after birth on the basis of genetic mutations during embryonic development. The first strike of HME formation may be related to germline mutations, which occur due to a deletion mutation in one allele of EXT in the chromosome arm (11, 12, 13); the second hit may be due to the complete loss of EXT1 or EXT2 alleles in somatic cells or the loss of heterozygosity (LOH) effect of EXT1 (14).

The osteochondroma is a bone neoplasm in the metaphysis, which can cause pain, deformity, and limit joint movement. The prevalence of the disease is approximately 1:50,000–1:100,000 (15, 16, 17). The incidence may be underestimated due to asymptomatic or mild patients who have not visited the hospital. The incidence of HME involving the spinal region accounts for 0.1–68% of all HME cases, and the literature is inconsistent (18, 19, 20, 21). In the literature, HME patients with lesions located within the spinal canal account for approximately 19–27% (18, 19). In contrast, neural impingement occurs in only about 4–7% of HME patients (18, 19).

Clinical manifestation

Hereditary multiple exostoses is a cartilage growth disorder characterized by abnormal growth of multiple osteochondromas, with the affected areas being long bones, scapula, ribs, pelvis, spine, etc. (22). HME exhibits a predilection for males, with a ratio of 1.5 to 3:1, and it is generally believed that male gender serves as a significant factor influencing the severity of HME (7, 15, 19). However, a recent study has indicated that gender does not affect the severity of HME in patients with spinal involvement (18). Most patients with HME have a family history of the disease, and the clinical manifestations mainly include multiple cartilage-capped exostoses of varying sizes on the bones, often bilateral and symmetrical. The tumor is usually adjacent to the growth plate of the bone and frequently grows continuously. This rare entity can be associated with a significant reduction in skeletal growth, shortened stature, skeletal deformity, restricted joint motion, premature osteoarthrosis, and compression of peripheral nerves (15). The clinical diagnosis of HME should be established primarily by characteristic clinical features and family history. Moreover, a molecular diagnosis should be established by identifying a heterogeneous pathogenic variant in EXT1 or EXT2 through molecular genetic testing. Patients with EXT1 mutations have more severe conditions, skeletal deformities, and shorter stature than those with EXT2 mutations (23, 24). To the contrary, patients carrying EXT2 mutations and those without EXT1 or EXT2 mutations may have milder disease symptoms (23, 24). Males experience more severe symptoms than females in HME patients carrying EXT mutations (23, 25). HME patients may experience various complications, including unsightly bone growth, skeletal curvature and deformity, limited mobility, and compression of nerves and blood vessels (26, 27). Spinal involvement is common in patients with HME, primarily affecting the spinal column. The literature reports varying proportions of cases where lesions are located within the spinal canal, potentially causing nerve impingement (18, 19). Once signs of neurological damage emerge, the consequences can be severe, often necessitating surgical intervention. Given the seriousness of this condition, thorough clinical screening and monitoring are essential for patients with HME. The clinical manifestations of HME in patients with spinal involvement include neck and back pain, low back pain, limb pain and numbness, urinary and fecal dysfunction, scoliosis, and Brown-Séquard syndrome (28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33) (Fig. 1). Do HME patients need regular monitoring of spinal lesions?

The primary radiological features of osteochondroma include the continuity of the cortex and medulla between the lesion and the underlying bone, as well as the presence of a cartilage cap (34). Osteochondroma is typically diagnosed through radiographic imaging, particularly when located at the metaphysis of a long bone. However, complex lesions or those in specialized anatomical areas, such as the pelvis, shoulder girdle, scapula, or spine, are better characterized using computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (35). When evaluating the morphology of osteochondromas, CT is superior to radiography, as it provides a clearer view of the continuity between the cortex, medulla, and the underlying bone (36, 37). One significant drawback of CT is the associated radiation dose, which should be minimized during screening and follow-up, particularly in young patients or those at risk of malignant transformation (35, 38). Ultrasound is effective for assessing the cartilage cap of superficial lesions and is especially beneficial for pediatric patients, where MRI options may be limited. However, ultrasound also has its limitations, such as operator dependency and an inability to evaluate deeper lesions or the bone composition of lesions (36). MRI offers an accurate assessment of the cartilage cap and excellent visualization of surrounding structures. Its main disadvantages include longer image acquisition times, contraindications for patients with claustrophobia or metal implants, and the potential need for sedation in children (17).

The use of MRI for routine screening of the entire spine and spinal canal is controversial for HME patients. There is a lack of consensus or expert recommendations to guide clinical decision-making. Montgomery et al. surveyed experts in the field of HME (104 were contacted, 26 participated, and 23 completed the survey); 72% of experts did not incorporate MRI screening regularly for spinal lesions (39). Screening is conducted within a variable/wide age range (4–18 years old) (39). Due to the rarity of this disease, it is challenging to reach a consensus on the screening plan for identifying spinal osteochondroma in HME patients (39). Experts display significantly different approaches; most respondents do not frequently undergo spinal MRI screening (39). Therefore, a national or international guideline is needed to assist in making decisions regarding HME patients. Fei’s study showed that annual MRI screening for patients with HME (between 20 and 40 years old) may be valuable (40). However, the scope of anatomical imaging remains controversial, and focusing on imaging from cervical spinal region to proximal femur may increase the clinical expenses (40). In a recent study, Carlos Monroig-Rivera et al. emphasized the importance for clinicians caring for HME patients to be aware that osteochondromas causing neural impingement can occur in any patient. They supported Roach et al.’s recommendation that HME patients should receive ‘advanced imaging, preferably MRI, as soon as they can cooperate and do not require general anesthesia’ (18, 19).

Early identification of spinal cord compression symptoms and timely imaging evaluation are effective strategies for determining the timing of surgery. Routine MRI screening should be performed if possible for children with a family history of HME during the rapid growth phase of the spine to prevent potential neurological complications (41). Some studies suggest that spinal imaging should be considered for patients with ‘harbinger lesions’ of rib and pelvic osteochondromas (20, 42). These ‘harbinger lesions’ may be related to the occurrence of spinal osteochondroma in HME patients, and it is recommended to limit advanced imaging examinations to patients with ‘harbinger lesions’ or patients with neurological symptoms to reduce unnecessary medical expenses (42). However, some studies have not fully confirmed the significance of ‘harbinger lesions’, indicating that further large-scale clinical research is needed to establish the clinical value of this phenomenon (1, 18). For patients with HME who have suspected spinal lesions, close follow-up is essential to minimize the risk of nerve impingement events. During this follow-up, clinicians should remain vigilant for skeletal-related events, such as spinal cord or nerve roots compression, as well as pathological fractures of the vertebral body and its appendages.

In HME patients with a potential for malignant transformation in the spine, the spinal region becomes a critical area for monitoring. Any patient who develops new pain near a previously existing osteochondroma should raise concerns about possible malignant transformation. Imaging findings that warrant suspicion of malignant tumors include growth of the osteochondroma after growth plate closure, irregular or lobulated edges, irregular or scattered calcifications, areas of internal dissolution, and adjacent bone erosion or destruction (43, 44). In such cases, an MRI examination is recommended for spinal lesions, if feasible. If an MRI cannot be performed, CT and ultrasound evaluations are acceptable alternatives (43, 45). On MRI, a key factor indicating the possibility of malignant transformation of osteochondroma is a cartilage cap thickness greater than 15–20 mm, which should raise suspicion. If malignant transformation is suspected, the patient should be referred to a specialized bone and soft tissue tumor center for further evaluation, including biopsy, resection, or other adjuvant therapies (46).

Surgical intervention

There is a knowledge deficit on HME and no standard for treatment methods and surgical timing. The lesions can involve many parts of the body, including long bones of limbs, pelvis, and axial bones. Some patients may undergo one or more surgeries for tumor resection (17, 47, 48). However, it is difficult to eliminate the condition through surgery due to its multiplicity. The optimal age at which HME patients should undergo surgery is related to the development of individualized surgical plans and is also an urgent topic for further discussion. Surgery before epiphyseal closure may lead to premature closure of the epiphysis, causing developmental abnormalities in children. Some scholars recommend postponing resection in children until their bones have matured, as younger patients may experience a higher recurrence rate (17, 37). If surgery cannot be delayed, it is essential to conduct close follow-up after the procedure to monitor for local recurrence or potential malignant transformation (17). Therefore, surgical intervention is not recommended for patients with insignificant surgical indications.

Only a portion of HME patients with spinal involvement exhibit clinical symptoms (18). For symptomatic patients, surgery mainly depends on the location of the lesion and the severity of the symptoms. The goal of surgery is to completely remove the lesion, relieve nerve compression, restore spinal function and stability, avoid postoperative spinal deformities, and reduce the risk of recurrence and malignant transformation (28, 49). For asymptomatic HME patients with lesions located in the spinal region, it is recommended to monitor the progression of the lesion rather than perform immediate surgery. Surgery should be considered to reduce the risk of nerve damage if the lesion expands over time or there is a risk of spinal cord or nerve root compression (1, 41). Surgical resection is crucial in preventing deterioration of the condition and restoring function when patients experience severe symptoms (such as neck and back pain, lower limb weakness, sensory abnormalities, difficulty walking, spasms, and dysfunction of urination and defecation). Surgical timing and indications

Surgical resection or orthopedic intervention is often required when limb deformities, pain, neurovascular compression, limited functional activity, and malignant transformation occur. Surgical resection can relieve space-occupying tumors, reduce compression on adjacent tissues, increase joint mobility, improve appearance, and delay or prevent further osteochondroma growth (54). The cartilage cap should be removed entirely, and epiphyseal plate protection should be ensured (15, 55). The normal development of the bone will be affected if the epiphyseal plate is damaged during tumor removal. However, if the tumor is not entirely removed to protect the epiphyseal plate, there may be a recurrence or even deterioration of the lesion. Orthopedic surgery should be considered for HME patients with spinal involvement when severe neck, chest, and back pain, spinal cord or nerve root compression symptoms, vertebral compression fracture caused by exostoses or high risk of compression, and malignant transformation of the lesion occur.

Surgical intervention for spinal lesions in HME patients

For HME patients with spinal involvement, surgical decision-making requires careful consideration of multiple factors, including the exostoses’ number, size, location, adjacent structures, and impact of spinal lesions on the spinal cord, and treatment should be individualized (Fig. 5). Surgery should be performed in the least invasive but most effective manner, ensuring complete removal of the lesion and protection of spinal cord and spinal stability (56). Surgery mainly involves decompression surgery (such as simple tumor resection, laminectomy, hemilaminectomy, and laminoplasty) to relieve spinal cord and nerve root compression, maintain spinal stability, and perform thorough lesion resection. Complete resection is recommended to reduce the disease recurrence risk if the exostosis is large and complex (57). Osteochondroma that causes spinal cord compression should be removed entirely to prevent recurrence and potential nerve damage (49). Efforts should be made to avoid damaging the spinal joint surface and prevent postoperative spinal deformities such as kyphosis (41). Microsurgical techniques and advanced imaging guidance may help complete resection of the lesion (41, 42). The use of ultrasonic osteotome for laminectomy can reduce surgical damage and bleeding, and improve the accuracy of tumor resection (58). Spinal fixation or fusion surgery is usually required to ensure the local and overall stability of the spine after extensive vertebral resection (49).

Post-surgical neurological function recovery should be closely monitored, and appropriate rehabilitation should be carried out. To accelerate postoperative recovery, it is important to assist patients in restoring normal motor function and improving their quality of life (56). Monitoring spinal development and disease recurrence after surgery is important for these patients (59). Regular postoperative imaging can help detect early signs of recurrence, allowing prompt interventions (60). There is a possibility of malignant transformation for cases with recurrence, and surgical resection should be reconsidered (49).

HME patients with cervical spinal involvement

Based on a summary and analysis of the literature, a detailed analysis of reported patients with HME involving the spine was conducted, and 62 patients had cervical spinal involvement (Supplementary file 1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article)), accounting for the majority of the cases. Hereditary multiple exostoses patients with combined cervical spinal lesions are prone to high-risk spinal cord compression and paralysis. Cervical spinal cord compression caused by tumor growth may rapidly lead to severe neurological dysfunction, directly threatening the HME patient’s motor function and quality of life. The timing of the surgery and whether to intervene in asymptomatic lesions remain common challenges in clinical decision-making (39). Due to the particularity of the cervical spinal region, earlier and more aggressive intervention may be needed for a lesion located within the spinal canal and with a risk of spinal cord compression, even if the lesion is asymptomatic in specific patients (61).

The anatomical structure of the cervical spinal region is complex, and slight errors may cause severe nerve or vascular damage. It is necessary to carefully assess the surgical risks, including potential impacts on large blood vessels, nerves, and respiratory tract or esophagus, and take necessary protective measures (62). For lesions in the cervical spinal region, the surgical approach and resection range need to be selected based on the specific location of the lesion. Posterior laminectomy is usually performed to avoid damage to the structure in front of the spinal cord and reduce the risk of direct injury to the spinal cord for tumors located in the posterior part of the spine (such as vertebral plates and pedicle). This surgical method can effectively relieve spinal cord compression while reducing intraoperative risks (39). The stability of the cervical spine should be maintained to prevent postoperative cervical instability or motor dysfunction. This is crucial during cervical laminectomy. Laminoplasty can prevent possible postoperative cervical kyphosis (63). Oga et al. implanted a hydroxyapatite spinous process spacer to increase support and ensure the stability of the spine in a 13-year-old boy with hereditary multiple exostoses (58). Ergün et al. removed tumors through posterior laminectomy (C3–C6) to relieve compression on the spinal cord (64). An anterior discectomy was performed at the C5–C6 segment, and bone was transplanted from the ilium for interbody fusion, restoring intervertebral height and stability (64). In addition, surgery can utilize microsurgical techniques to remove lesions while protecting surrounding nerve structures accurately. Imaging techniques, such as CT and MRI, can help accurately locate lesions and ensure their complete removal during surgery (61). For exogenic bone warts with cartilage caps, complete removal of the cartilage cap should be paid special attention during resection (65). Important local anatomical structures should be protected, especially the muscles and nerves around the spine. The attachment of some neck muscles can be preserved when removing the lesion; the muscles can be sutured after surgery to minimize postoperative neck pain and functional loss (65). Electrophysiological monitoring during surgery assesses spinal cord function in real-time, ensuring maximum spinal cord protection intraoperatively (30).

HME patients with thoracolumbar spinal involvement

A total of 36 patients with HME presented with thoracic lesions (Supplementary file 2), and 17 cases with lumbar lesions (Supplementary file 3). Only two patients with HME in the sacrococcygeal region are reported in the literature (Supplementary file 4). The thoracic region is closely related to the ribs and thoracic structure, with relatively low flexibility and high stability. The spinal cord in the thoracic region is relatively narrow, and the cardiac and pulmonary organs in front of the spine also increase the complexity of the surgery. The thoracic spine has a relatively abundant blood supply, and special attention should be paid to controlling hemorrhage during the surgery (30). The surgical plan for HME patients with multiple thoracic lesions should be formulated according to the specific condition of the lesions. Staged surgery may be necessary to reduce surgical time and the risk of complications. Spinal instability may occur after thoracic surgery, especially during extensive laminectomy or tumor resection. Spinal fixation or fusion surgery can prevent postoperative instability and secondary deformities effectively.

Surgeries of the lumbar spine can be more complex than in the upper spinal region due to the deep position and the surrounding rich, soft tissue structure. Care should be taken to avoid damaging the surrounding blood vessels, muscles, and nerves. In addition, the osteochondromas in the lumbar region may be located within the intervertebral foramen or other narrow anatomical spaces, increasing the complexity of the surgery, and resection usually requires more delicate procedures (66). It is necessary to closely monitor the lumbar spine’s stability and the recurrence of the osteochondroma after surgery. If required, physical therapy should be performed to strengthen the muscle support of the lumbar spine and ensure long-term rehabilitation. Pedicle resection can prevent direct exposure to the spinal cord and reduce the risk of spinal cord injury during surgery if the osteochondroma is located in the pedicle or compresses the spinal nerve root laterally (30). If surgery involves extensive laminectomy or pedicle removal, spinal fusion or fixation is usually required to avoid postoperative spinal instability. This surgery can maintain the stability of the lumbar spine and prevent postoperative deformity.

Malignant transformation

Multiple cartilage-capped exostoses develop during childhood and ossify until skeletal growth is complete (16, 67). The most severe complication of HME is malignant transformation of the osteochondroma, which usually occurs within the cartilage cap and leads to the development of a secondary chondrosarcoma (17, 68, 69). Chondrosarcoma transformation is undoubtedly the most serious and intractable complication in HME (70, 71, 72). The risk of malignancy transformation in HME is reported to be about 2.7–5% (24, 40, 73). Although studies have found that a few patients carrying EXT1 mutations have a higher malignant transformation rate in HME than those carrying EXT2, no relevant epidemiological reports exist (8, 21). Multiple studies have proposed a screening method using X-ray or MRI to reach the early identification of malignant transformation (21, 72, 74). The results of Fei’s study suggested that annual MRI screening for HME patients (between the ages of 20 and 40 years) may be significant (40). Nevertheless, the extent of anatomical imaging that should be performed still needs to be debated in depth. Moreover, a focused imaging protocol that scans from the cervical spine to the proximal femur may influence the cost-effectiveness to a certain extent (40).

Non-surgical treatment methods

Animal studies show that drugs such as the selective retinoic acid receptor gamma agonist palovarotene may be effective and worthy of further exploration (75, 76). Retinoic acid receptor (RAR-γ) belongs to nuclear hormone receptors, which regulate gene transcription and participate in essential processes such as embryonic and cartilage development. In mouse model, BMP signaling in chondrocyte mesenchymal progenitor cells is enhanced with abnormal activation of RAR-γ, leading to osteochondroma formation; decreased heparan sulfate (HS) levels do not affect this process (77, 78, 79). Palovarotene mediates the inhibitory effect on ectopic ossification by reducing BMP signaling and may become an effective drug for preventing HME (76, 80, 81).

There is currently no unified diagnosis and treatment standard or consensus, or specific drug for HME (15). Therefore, identifying drug targets and developing effective therapeutic drugs is required. Treatment for HME mainly includes replacing defective or missing proteins or genes and correcting abnormal signaling pathways. The enhancement of BMP signaling caused by HS reduction plays a dominant role in developing HME. The use of BMP inhibitor LDN-193189 can effectively reduce the growth of osteochondroma in EXT1 model mice with heterozygous deletion (77). Study has shown that LDN-193189 can effectively reduce the proliferation of mouse embryonic chondrocytes by inhibiting BMP signaling (82). Other potential drugs are under in-depth research for treating HME, such as the heparanase inhibitor SST0001, but their effectiveness and safety still need further validation (64, 83, 84).

Limitations and future directions

This review provides a comprehensive summary of clinical screening and surgical treatment strategies for HME patients with spinal involvement, based on the existing literature. However, several unresolved issues and limitations persist. First, HME with spinal involvement is rare in clinical practice and often suffers from misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis. This necessitates increased clinical attention to the disorder, as current analyses may not accurately reflect the actual situation. Second, there is a notable lack of large, multi-center, prospective clinical studies on HME patients with spinal involvement worldwide, which limits the advancement of treatment strategies and experiences. Third, variations in screening and treatment approaches among clinicians across different countries and regions contribute to inconsistencies. In addition, the differences in examination-related costs during follow-up significantly affect the development of an international consensus. Fourth, there is an urgent need for research on the mechanisms of HME and the development of new drugs. Emerging technologies, such as gene editing and innovative drug delivery methods, hold promise for benefiting HME patients in the future. Fifth, it is essential to enhance psychological health management for HME patients to alleviate their physical and mental burdens, helping them navigate challenges related to life, work, marriage, and childbirth. Finally, there is a lack of evaluation and treatment strategies tailored to patients of different ages internationally, which is crucial for refining clinical approaches. Moving forward, there is an urgent need for more research and the establishment of an international consensus on HME with spinal involvement. This will provide hope for patients with genetic disorders, improve their quality of life, and enable precise evaluation and treatment.

Conclusion

Hereditary multiple exostoses are rare in clinical practice. The diagnosis and treatment of HME involving the spinal region are especially complex, and multidisciplinary experts from orthopedics, pediatrics, oncology, genetics, radiology, pathology, and endocrinology are needed to participate together. So far, no international consensus on orthopedic surgical treatment strategies has existed. For HME patients with spinal involvement, it is crucial to grasp clinical monitoring strategies and surgical indications, and determine the optimal surgical timing and approaches. This review summarizes the surgical strategy for HME, including involvement in different spine segments, to improve diagnosis and treatment for these rare entities. Adequate data for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of HME patients with spinal involvement need to be compiled to improve therapeutic outcomes.
