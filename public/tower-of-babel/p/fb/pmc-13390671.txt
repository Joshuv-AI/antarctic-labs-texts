Admission D-Dimer-to-Albumin Ratio as a Predictor of Poor Prognosis in Patients with Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jiankang Zhang, Yu Yu, Nannan Feng, Jianguo Rao, Xiangjun Deng, Hongjuan Yin, Liangchen Wei, Xiaobo Ding, Lifen Hu, Ying Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a severe viral infection associated with rapid clinical deterioration and high mortality. This study evaluated the prognostic value of the D-dimer-to-albumin ratio (DAR) in hospitalized patients with SFTS.Patients and MethodsA total of 387 patients with SFTS were retrospectively included. Correlations between biomarkers and SFTSV RNA levels were evaluated by Spearman analysis. The prognostic performance of DAR and other candidate biomarkers was assessed using ROC analysis and DeLong’s test. Multivariable Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed to determine the independent prognostic value of DAR.ResultsOf the 387 included patients, 314 (81.1%) survived and 73 (18.9%) died within 28 days after admission. Among the evaluated biomarkers, DAR showed the strongest correlation with SFTSV RNA level (r = 0.438, P < 0.001). DAR also demonstrated the best discriminative ability for predicting 28-day mortality (AUC=0.810, 95% CI, 0.745–0.874). Its prognostic performance was significantly better than that of PLR, CRP, ALB/PLT, LDH/ALB, FAR, and SII, and was comparable to that of NLR and PCT. The optimal cutoff value for DAR was 0.087, with a sensitivity of 73.8% and a specificity of 79.4%, and the highest Youden index. In univariate Cox regression analysis, patients with DAR >0.087 had a significantly increased risk of 28-day mortality (HR = 7.026, 95% CI: 4.228–11.677; P < 0.001). In multivariable Cox regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, cardiovascular comorbidity, neurologic symptoms, bleeding manifestations, SFTSV RNA viral load, onset-to-admission interval, and year of disease onset, DAR remained independently associated with poor outcome (adjusted HR, 4.475; 95% CI, 2.635–7.600; P < 0.001). This association remained significant when DAR was analyzed as a continuous variable. Kaplan–Meier analysis further showed significantly lower survival probability in patients with DAR >0.087.ConclusionDAR was significantly associated with poor prognosis in hospitalized patients with SFTS. As a simple and readily available admission-based biomarker, DAR may be useful for early risk stratification and clinical monitoring.Keywords: severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome, D-dimer-to-albumin ratio, mortality, prognosis, risk stratification

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging viral infectious disease caused by severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV), also known as Dabie bandavirus (DBV). Since it was first identified in China in 2009, the number of reported cases has increased substantially in several East Asian countries.1 Clinically, SFTS commonly presents with fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, neurologic manifestations, and bleeding tendency. In severe cases, patients may rapidly develop disseminated intravascular coagulation, multiorgan dysfunction, and death, with reported case-fatality rates ranging from 12% to 30%.2,3 Because the clinical course can deteriorate quickly and specific therapeutic options remain limited, early recognition of patients at high risk for poor outcome is essential for timely monitoring, supportive care, and clinical decision-making.

Laboratory biomarkers provide objective and readily available information for prognostic assessment in infectious diseases. Previous studies have shown that several admission-based indicators, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR),4 platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR),5 alanine aminotransferase-to-aspartate aminotransferase ratio (ALT/AST),6 albumin-to-platelet ratio (ALB/PLT),7 and lactate dehydrogenase-to-albumin ratio (LDH/ALB),8 are associated with disease severity or mortality in SFTS. However, the predictive performance of these markers remains suboptimal in routine clinical practice, and a simple biomarker with better discriminatory ability would be of practical value for early risk stratification.

D-dimer is a well-established marker of coagulation and fibrinolytic activation and has been associated with both disseminated intravascular coagulation and adverse outcomes in severe infections.9 In SFTS, coagulation dysfunction is a common feature of severe and fatal disease, and elevated D-dimer has been linked to clinical deterioration.10,11 Serum albumin reflects nutritional and inflammatory status as well as overall physiologic reserve, and hypoalbuminemia has been associated with greater disease severity and worse prognosis in SFTS and other critical illnesses.11,12 The D-dimer-to-albumin ratio (DAR) integrates these two parameters and may therefore provide a more comprehensive assessment of coagulation dysfunction and host condition than either measure alone.

SFTS shares important pathophysiologic features with sepsis and severe COVID-19, including cytokine storm, endothelial dysfunction, and multi-organ failure.13–15 Despite its reported prognostic value in sepsis, COVID-19, and other infectious diseases, the clinical significance of DAR in SFTS remains unclear.16,17 Therefore, this study was designed to evaluate the prognostic value of DAR in hospitalized patients with SFTS and to determine whether it may serve as a practical admission-based marker for early risk stratification.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Enrollment

This retrospective study included patients with SFTS who were admitted to Lu’an Hospital of Anhui Medical University between January 2021 and December 2024. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1) a relevant epidemiologic history, including residence in, work in, or travel to an endemic area during the epidemic season, contact with patients with SFTS, or a recent tick bite; (2) compatible clinical manifestations, including fever or chills, malaise, and thrombocytopenia (platelet count <100 × 109/L); and (3) laboratory confirmation of SFTS by positive serum nucleic acid testing.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age younger than 18 years; (2) liver cirrhosis or liver failure; (3) pre-existing leukemia, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, or other hematologic disorders; (4) long-term anticoagulant therapy; (5) human immunodeficiency virus infection, ongoing radiotherapy or chemotherapy for malignancy, or persistent immunosuppression; and (6) incomplete clinical or laboratory data.

Patients were classified into a non-fatal group and a fatal group according to their 28-day outcome after admission. The non-fatal group comprised patients who survived through day 28, whereas the fatal group comprised those who died within 28 days of admission due to SFTS or its related complications. For Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, follow-up started on the date of hospital admission and ended at death or day 28, whichever came first; patients who were alive at day 28 were censored at that time.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical records of hospitalized patients. Collected variables included age, sex, comorbidities, clinical manifestations, and laboratory findings obtained within 24 hours after admission. Laboratory parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, platelet count, hemoglobin, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), D-dimer, fibrinogen, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio (INR), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), total bilirubin (TBIL), albumin, blood glucose (GLU), sodium, potassium, and SFTSV RNA level.

The following derived indices were calculated: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) = platelet count/lymphocyte count; lymphocyte-to-C-reactive protein ratio (LCR) = lymphocyte count/CRP; D-dimer-to-albumin ratio (DAR) = D-dimer/albumin; fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) = fibrinogen/albumin; and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) = neutrophil count×platelet count/lymphocyte count.

Statistical Analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for nonparametric comparisons across multiple groups when applicable. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to assess the associations between viral load and biomarker levels.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic performance of candidate biomarkers for poor outcome in SFTS. The corresponding area under the curve (AUC), optimal cutoff value, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, accuracy, and Youden index were calculated. AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test. To identify independent predictors of poor prognosis, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed, and Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was performed. In the Cox regression analysis, NLR, PLR, CRP, PCT, ALB/PLT, LDH/ALB, and SII were entered as categorical variables based on the cutoff values derived from ROC analysis. DAR was initially analyzed as a categorical variable using the ROC-derived cutoff to ensure a consistent grouping strategy in both the Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier analyses. To reduce the risk of effect-size inflation caused by dichotomization, a supplementary multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed with DAR entered as a continuous variable. Both models were adjusted for age, sex, cardiovascular comorbidity, neurologic symptoms, bleeding manifestations, SFTSV RNA viral load, onset-to-admission interval and year of disease onset. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 409 patients with SFTS were initially screened for inclusion in this study. After exclusion of 22 patients because of major underlying diseases or incomplete data, 387 patients were included in the final analysis. The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Among the 387 included patients, 314 (81.1%) were assigned to the non-fatal group and 73 (18.9%) to the fatal group. The sex distribution was comparable between the two groups. However, diabetes and cardiovascular disease were significantly more common in the fatal group than in the non-fatal group (both P < 0.05). In addition, bleeding manifestations and neurologic symptoms were more frequently observed among patients with fatal outcomes.

Association Between Biomarkers and Viral Load

To further evaluate potential prognostic biomarkers in SFTS, we examined the associations between SFTSV RNA level and a range of laboratory biomarkers, including WBC, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, platelet count, eosinophil count, procalcitonin, ALT, AST, creatinine, BUN, LDH, NLR, ALB/PLT, LDH/ALB, CK, CK-MB, D-dimer, fibrinogen, DAR, FAR, and SII.

Among the evaluated biomarkers, DAR showed the strongest correlation with SFTSV RNA level (r = 0.438, P < 0.001), followed by D-dimer, LDH, LDH/ALB, AST, PCT, ALT/AST, and PLT (all P < 0.001). Significant correlations were also observed for LYM%, ALB/PLT, UREA, ALT, CK, NLR, FIB, NEU, LYM, CK-MB, and EOS, whereas no significant correlation was found for WBC, FAR, or SII (all P > 0.05) (Table 2). These results showed that DAR had a stronger correlation with SFTSV RNA level than several other routinely measured laboratory indicators.

Comparison of ROC Performance Among Biomarkers

As shown in Figure 1, DAR showed good discriminative ability for predicting poor prognosis in patients with SFTS, with an AUC of 0.810 (95% CI, 0.745–0.874). Among the evaluated biomarkers, DAR had the highest AUC, followed by NLR (AUC = 0.785), PCT (AUC = 0.745), and LDH/ALB (AUC = 0.736). Pairwise comparisons using DeLong’s test showed that the AUC of DAR was significantly higher than those of PLR, CRP, ALB/PLT, LDH/ALB, FAR, and SII (all P < 0.05). By contrast, no significant difference was found between DAR and NLR or PCT (P = 0.586 and P = 0.088, respectively), indicating that DAR performed better than most candidate biomarkers, with predictive ability comparable to that of NLR and PCT (Table 3). As shown inTable 4 the optimal cutoff value for DAR was 0.087, with a sensitivity of 73.8% and a specificity of 79.4%. Among all evaluated biomarkers, DAR showed the highest Youden index (0.532), indicating the best overall balance between sensitivity and specificity. Although FAR had the highest specificity (99.2%) and accuracy (0.811), its sensitivity was very low (3.3%), limiting its usefulness for early identification of high-risk patients. LDH/ALB showed the highest sensitivity (91.8%) and the highest NPV (95.8%), but its specificity was relatively low (43.9%) and its Youden index was lower than that of DAR. NLR also showed high sensitivity (88.9%) and NPV (95.7%), but its specificity was modest (57.0%). Overall, DAR demonstrated a more balanced diagnostic performance than the other biomarkers, supporting its potential value for prognostic risk stratification in patients with SFTS.

Predictive Value of Biomarkers for Survival in Patients with SFTS

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to assess the prognostic value of NLR, PLR, CRP, PCT, ALB/PLT, LDH/ALB, DAR, and SII in patients with SFTS. Detailed HRs, 95% CIs, and P values are provided in Table 5, and the corresponding forest plot is shown in Figure 2. All biomarkers were significantly associated with mortality in the univariable analysis. After adjustment for age, sex, cardiovascular comorbidity, neurologic symptoms, bleeding manifestations, SFTSV RNA viral load, onset-to-admission interval and year of disease onset, NLR, PCT, LDH/ALB, DAR, and SII remained significant, whereas PLR, CRP, and ALB/PLT lost significance. Among these biomarkers, DAR showed the strongest independent association with mortality (adjusted HR, 4.475; 95% CI, 2.635–7.600; P < 0.001), followed by NLR (adjusted HR, 3.9; 95% CI, 1.82–8.34; P < 0.001), LDH/ALB (adjusted HR, 3.316; 95% CI, 1.582–6.952; P = 0.002), and PCT (adjusted HR, 2.244; 95% CI, 1.348–3.737; P = 0.002). Although NLR showed the highest HR in the univariate analysis (HR, 8.58; 95% CI, 4.11–17.89; P < 0.001), its association was attenuated after multivariable adjustment. The adjusted HR of DAR was higher than that of NLR (4.47 vs 3.90), suggesting that DAR had a stronger independent association with mortality and may provide more robust prognostic information for mortality risk stratification in patients with SFTS.

As a supplementary analysis, DAR was also entered as a continuous variable in the multivariable Cox regression model to reduce potential bias caused by dichotomization. The association between DAR and poor prognosis remained significant after adjustment for the same covariates (Supplementary Table S1). Additional analyses using D-dimer and albumin as separate continuous variables showed that D-dimer, but not albumin, was significantly associated with poor prognosis (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3).

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significantly lower survival probability in patients with DAR >0.087 than in those with DAR ≤0.087 (P < 0.001). Similar survival differences were also observed for NLR, PCT, LDH/ALB, SII, PLR, CRP, and ALB/PLT according to their respective cutoff values. The survival curves for DAR showed clear separation between the two groups, consistent with the ROC and Cox regression findings (Figure 3).

Discussion

SFTS is associated with high mortality, and early identification of patients at high risk remains clinically important because deterioration often occurs within the first two weeks after symptom onset.18,19 Given this narrow therapeutic window, early identification of patients at high risk for poor outcome is essential for closer monitoring, timely supportive care, and more appropriate clinical management.

In the present study, we found that older age, bleeding manifestations, neurologic symptoms, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and high viral load were associated with poor prognosis in patients with SFTS. These findings are consistent with previous reports identifying these variables as major predictors of poor outcome in SFTS.19–21 In addition, compared with non-fatal group, the fatal group had significantly higher levels of ALT, AST, CK, and CK-MB, suggesting more extensive tissue injury during disease progression, particularly involving the liver and myocardium.6,22,23 GLU levels were also higher in the fatal group. This finding may reflect stress hyperglycemia or possible pancreatic involvement in severe SFTS, both of which have been linked to worse clinical outcomes.21

Cytokine storm is thought to play a central role in the pathogenesis of severe SFTS. Previous studies have shown that elevated cytokine levels, including interferon-α, interleukin-10, and interferon gamma-induced protein 10, are associated with viral load and adverse outcome.23 These inflammatory responses may contribute to neutrophil activation, lymphocyte depletion, endothelial injury, and multiorgan dysfunction.24 In this study, several inflammatory markers and inflammation-related indices were associated with mortality, suggesting that early inflammatory disturbance may be linked to poor outcome in SFTS. We also observed an association between eosinopenia and poor prognosis, which is in line with prior reports in critically ill patients.25 However, the prognostic role of eosinophil-related indices in SFTS remains uncertain, as previous studies have reported inconsistent findings.26,27 Elevated PCT may reflect a more severe inflammatory state or possible secondary bacterial infection in patients with poor outcome.28 In contrast, CRP appeared to have weaker discriminatory value in this analysis. These findings suggest that inflammatory activation contributes to disease progression, although the prognostic value differs among inflammatory biomarkers.

Thrombocytopenia is a common finding in SFTS and may be partly related to macrophage-mediated clearance of virus-bound platelets.29 In severe disease, endothelial injury caused by viral infection and excessive inflammation may contribute to platelet consumption, coagulation abnormalities, and bleeding complications.30,31 Previous studies have suggested that prolonged APTT may help identify patients at higher risk of severe disease.32 Consistent with these findings, patients in the fatal group in this study had significantly prolonged APTT, prothrombin time, and INR, as well as higher D-dimer levels and lower fibrinogen levels.33,34 These findings support an important role of coagulation dysfunction in disease progression and provide a biological rationale for evaluating coagulation-related biomarkers such as DAR.35,36

Several routinely measured laboratory biomarkers have been associated with disease severity or mortality in SFTS. In our study, DAR and several composite biomarkers differed significantly between fatal and non-fatal patients. Previous studies have shown that early viral load is closely associated with disease severity and outcome in SFTS.37 Notably, DAR showed the strongest correlation with SFTSV RNA level, suggesting that it may better reflect disease activity related to viral burden.

DAR demonstrated favorable prognostic performance, with the highest Youden index and the strongest independent association with mortality after multivariable adjustment. Although its AUC was comparable to those of NLR and PCT, DAR showed higher sensitivity and stronger risk stratification ability. D-dimer remained independently associated with poor outcome, whereas albumin alone did not. As D-dimer reflects coagulation and fibrinolytic activation and albumin reflects overall host condition, their combination in DAR may provide more integrated prognostic information than either component alone.12 NLR primarily reflects systemic inflammation, whereas PCT may be influenced by secondary bacterial infection or other stress-related responses.4,38 Because D-dimer and albumin are routinely measured at admission, DAR is simple and clinically accessible. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significantly lower survival in patients with DAR >0.087, indicating that this cutoff may help identify patients at higher risk of death who require closer monitoring.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, and the cutoff value of 0.087 was derived from the same cohort without external validation. Although DAR was also analyzed as a continuous variable, no resampling method was used to assess result stability. Dynamic changes in DAR during hospitalization were not evaluated. Therefore, the proposed cutoff value should be interpreted cautiously until validated in prospective multicenter cohorts. Second, DAR alone may not fully capture the complexity of prognosis in SFTS, and future studies should assess whether combining DAR with other markers can improve predictive performance. In addition, the biologic mechanisms linking DAR to poor outcome were not directly investigated.

Conclusion

In summary, DAR was significantly associated with poor prognosis in hospitalized patients with SFTS and remained independently associated with mortality after adjustment for major clinical confounders. Compared with several routinely available biomarkers, DAR showed favorable overall prognostic performance, including a strong correlation with viral load, the highest Youden index, and a clear association with survival. By combining D-dimer and albumin, DAR may reflect both coagulation dysfunction and overall host condition. Given its simplicity, low cost, and routine availability at admission, DAR may serve as a practical adjunctive biomarker for early risk stratification and clinical monitoring. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify how DAR should be incorporated into routine clinical assessment.
