Clinical and Economic Burden of Obesity and Cardiovascular Disease Among Medicare Fee‐For‐Service Beneficiaries
Authors: Jiayin Xue, Jeffrey Anderson, Ahong Huang, Ray Gou, Haiyan Sun, Junhui Zhao, Catherine Allende, Hannah Hua, Andriana Hohlbauch, Chanadda Chinthammit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis retrospective cohort study described clinical outcomes, healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU), and costs among Medicare beneficiaries with or without obesity and stratified by cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk.Materials and MethodsUsing Medicare FFS claims (Jan 2016–Dec 2022), beneficiaries were indexed after 12 months of continuous enrollment and followed until death, disenrollment, or study end (≥ 12 months continuous enrollment post‐index required for HCRU and cost analyses). Outcomes were compared between beneficiaries with obesity (class ≥ 1: diagnosis/BMI ≥ 30; class ≥ 3: severe/morbid obesity/BMI ≥ 40) versus without obesity, and stratified by CVD risk (established, at‐risk and low‐risk). Time‐to‐event outcomes were assessed using Cox models, and HCRU and costs using generalised linear models and two‐part models, all adjusted for baseline covariates.ResultsA total of 21 306 251 beneficiaries were included in the study. Hazard ratios for clinical outcomes were higher among those with obesity versus without obesity, particularly for heart failure‐associated hospitalisation (class ≥ 1: 1.92; class ≥ 3: 3.40), type 2 diabetes (class ≥ 1: 1.65; class ≥ 3: 2.20) and MACE‐5 (class ≥ 1: 1.44; class ≥ 3: 2.27). Total costs (per‐patient per‐month $; cost ratio) were higher among those with obesity class ≥ 1 ($4113 vs. $2876; 1.43), and obesity class ≥ 3 ($5319 vs. $2929; 1.82), driven by high outpatient and inpatient utilisation. Results were consistent across CVD risk stratifications.ConclusionThese findings highlight the higher risk of negative clinical outcomes and costs associated with obesity among US Medicare beneficiaries. Further research is warranted to improve treatment and prevention of obesity to mitigate risks and costs for this population.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, obesity care, observational study, real‐world evidence

Introduction

Individuals with obesity are at risk for obesity related comorbidities (ORCs) such as cardiovascular disease (CVD), which together may result in poorer health outcomes [1]. ORCs complicate the management of chronic diseases, leading to increased healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU), higher costs, and long‐term patient and health system burden [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Among real‐world studies, annual per‐patient total healthcare costs are estimated to be 1.1 and 3.3‐fold greater among those with obesity class 1 and 3, respectively, when compared to those without obesity, and up to 5‐fold higher among those with ORCs [8].

While obesity affects individuals across all demographics, the Medicare population is a population with a high disease burden and costs, and they may exhibit unique challenges and disparities related to obesity. Obesity is associated with substantially elevated rates of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), with Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥ 65 with obesity bearing disproportionate burdens of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and ischemic heart disease compared to those without obesity [9, 10]. Older adults with obesity are at greater risk for multimorbidity, particularly type 2 diabetes (T2D) and cardiometabolic disorders [9, 11]. The prevalence of T2D among older adults with obesity (44.3%) is twice that of those without obesity (21.8%) [9], while obesity is an independent risk factor for CKD progression and renal outcomes in this population [12, 13, 14].

With a growing aging population, the number of Medicare beneficiaries is projected to increase significantly from 60 million in 2023 to 74 million by 2034 [15, 16]. With this growth in the Medicare population, the burden of CVD is also expected to rise [17]. While prior studies have characterised the association between obesity and clinical and economic burden, there is a lack of data regarding the contribution of CVD risk status, which is particularly relevant among older adults. Given the risk for obesity and CVD in this population, the goal of this study was to describe clinical outcomes, HCRU, and costs among Medicare beneficiaries based on obesity status and CVD risk status, specifically Medicare fee‐for‐service beneficiaries who may have a higher disease burden than Medicare Advantage beneficiaries.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a retrospective, observational, cohort study of Medicare fee‐for‐service (FFS) beneficiaries from Medicare Parts A, B and D (Figure S1). The study period was from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2022, with a patient identification period from January 1, 2017 through December 31, 2021, to allow for 12 months of baseline and ≥ 12 months potential follow‐up. The index date was the first date of the first month after 12 months of continuous medical and pharmacy enrollment (allowable gap ≤ 30 days) in the FFS data source. The baseline period was the 12‐month period prior to the index date (inclusive of index date). The follow‐up period was the period following the index date (exclusive of index date) to patient death date, continuous enrollment end date, or study end date (December 31, 2022), whichever occurred first.

The study was comprised of Medicare FFS beneficiaries aged 65 or older. Patients were excluded if they had ≥ 1 bariatric surgery procedure claim or ≥ 1 malignancy diagnosis claim during the baseline period, if gender was unknown or conflicting, or if key data quality issues were present (e.g., unknown or conflicting date of birth or multiple reported dates of death). To be included in the HCRU and cost analyses, patients were required to have ≥ 12 months of continuous medical and pharmacy enrollment post‐index, with an allowable gap of 30 days.

Additionally, analysis of time to end‐stage renal disease (ESRD)/renal death included patients with ≥ 1 diagnosis claim for CKD stage 3 or 4 at baseline and excluded those with ≥ 1 diagnosis or procedure claim for ESRD during the baseline period. For the analysis of time to type 2 diabetes (T2D), patients with ≥ 1 diagnosis claim for T2D during the baseline period were excluded.

Patients were classified based on obesity status and CVD risk (Table S1). Obesity status was defined in the baseline period and included 3 classifications: obesity class ≥ 1, obesity class ≥ 3 and without obesity. Obesity class ≥ 1 included those with ≥ 1 diagnosis claim for obesity or body mass index [BMI] ≥ 30 kg/m2. Obesity class ≥ 3 included those with ≥ 1 diagnosis claim for severe/morbid obesity or BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2, and was a subset of patients in the obesity class ≥ 1 group. Those with no diagnosis claim for obesity, nor a claim indicating BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 were classified as without obesity. CVD status during the baseline period included 3 classifications: established CVD, at‐risk for CVD and low‐risk for CVD. Established CVD included those with ≥ 1 CVD diagnosis claim. The at‐risk classification included those without a diagnosis for CVD but with ≥ 3 of the following CVD risk factors: age ≥ 70 years for females or ≥ 65 years for males, hypertension with an anti‐hypertensive medication claim, dyslipidemia, CKD of any stage, prediabetes or T2D, and history of tobacco use. The low‐risk classification included those with no CVD diagnosis and with < 3 CVD risk factors.

Outcomes

The 2 co‐primary aims of this study were to describe and compare during the follow‐up period (1) the incidence and risk of clinical outcomes and (2) HCRU and costs, based on obesity status (without obesity vs. obesity class ≥ 1 or obesity class ≥ 3), as well as stratified based on CVD risk status (Table S2). The full cohort of those with obesity was used for the comparison of obesity class ≥ 1 versus without obesity. For the comparison of obesity class ≥ 3 versus without obesity, those with BMI 30.0–39.9 kg/m2 were excluded from this comparison. Clinical outcomes included T2D, ESRD, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI) and nonfatal stroke (no associated claim within 30 days prior to death), coronary revascularization, heart failure hospitalisation, CVD‐related death (death within 30 days of a CVD claim), all‐cause death, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). MACE‐3 was defined as the earliest occurrence of nonfatal stroke, nonfatal MI, or CVD‐related death. MACE‐5 was defined as the earliest occurrence of nonfatal stroke, nonfatal MI, coronary revascularization, heart failure event (inpatient [IP] or emergency department [ED]), or all‐cause death. These clinical outcomes were selected as they represent clinically meaningful endpoints commonly used to evaluate cardiometabolic risk and are relevant to the burden of obesity. HCRU and cost outcomes were included to capture the economic dimension of this burden; together, these outcomes provide a comprehensive evaluation of the impact of obesity in the Medicare FFS population. Group‐level means for all‐cause and CVD‐related HCRU and costs for patients in the cohorts were described for the follow‐up period, with CVD‐related medical HCRU and costs defined using claims with CVD codes (Table S3). HCRU was reported as per‐patient per‐year (PPPY) visits and patient costs as per‐patient‐per‐month (PPPM) United States dollars (USD).

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics including Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [18] comorbidities and ORCs were summarised using descriptive statistics for the overall study population and stratified by obesity status and CVD status. For continuous variables, the mean, standard deviation (SD), median, first quartile (Q1), third quartile (Q3), and min/max were reported, while categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages.

Time‐to‐event clinical outcomes were summarised using the Kaplan–Meier method to estimate survival probabilities and median time to event, based on obesity status and further stratified by CVD risk status. Cox proportional hazards models were fitted to estimate the hazard of clinical outcomes associated with obesity status (obesity class ≥ 1 vs. without obesity, and obesity class ≥ 3 vs. without obesity). We assessed the proportional hazards assumption using scaled Schoenfeld residuals and Kaplan–Meier curves. The models were adjusted for clinically relevant or potentially confounding demographics (age, sex, race, region, low‐income subsidy) and comorbidities (dementia, pulmonary disease, rheumatoid disease and chronic liver disease). We examined effect modification by baseline CVD‐risk using stratified analyses because the resulting stratum‐specific estimates are readily interpretable and, under the proportional hazards model, are equivalent to estimates obtained from a model with an obesity × CVD‐risk interaction term. It is worth noting that the resulting stratum‐specific estimates represent conditional associations between obesity and outcomes within each CVD risk group. For each clinical outcome, hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

HCRU and costs were summarised using descriptive statistics, standardised as PPPY visits for HCRU (to account for low values when expressed as PPPM) and PPPM United States dollars (USD) for costs. Total HCRU and costs during follow‐up were calculated over each patient's follow‐up period and divided by their follow‐up time to derive patient‐level standardised values. Costs were reported as 2022 USD, adjusted for inflation using the Medical Care component of the Consumer Price Index [19].

Adjusted analyses of follow‐up HCRU and costs were performed using generalised linear models (GLMs) with a log link. Two sets of models were performed separately. In the first set, the obesity class ≥ 1 versus without obesity comparison, the full cohort was included. In the second set, the obesity class ≥ 3 versus without obesity comparison, those with BMI 30.0–39.9 kg/m2 were excluded. A Poisson distribution was used to estimate the relative risk ratio for binary outcomes such as the first occurrence of any inpatient visit. A negative binomial distribution was applied to HCRU count outcomes, and a gamma distribution to cost outcomes. The log of follow‐up time was included as an offset to account for unequal follow‐up time. For costs of 0, a value of 0.01 was added to enable log transformation in the gamma models. For cost outcomes, values greater than the 99th percentile based on obesity class were truncated to minimise the influence of extreme values. For specific HCRU and cost outcomes with more than 10% of patients having no utilisation/cost, a two‐part model was used. The first part modelled the probability of any utilisation or cost using logistic regression, and the second part modelled nonzero outcomes using GLM (negative binomial with a log link for HCRU, and gamma with a log link for cost). The same covariates used in the clinical outcome models were included in the HCRU and cost models. Adjusted PPPY and PPPM estimates were derived from the models to represent group‐level mean outcomes (PPPY visits and PPPM costs), and adjusted means with 95% CIs and mean ratios with 95% CIs were reported.

Forest plots were used to display HRs with 95% CIs for clinical outcomes and mean ratios with 95% CIs for HCRU and cost outcomes.

All data analyses were conducted using Databricks (14.4.x‐cpu‐ml‐scala2.12) and SAS software (version 9.4) [20, 21].

Results

Baseline Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 21 306 251 patients were included in the overall study population. This included 17 708 656 (83.1%) patients without obesity, 3 597 595 (16.9%) patients with obesity class ≥ 1, and 580 026 (2.7%) patients with obesity class ≥ 3. Stratified by CVD risk status, the study included 6 425 874 (30.2%) patients with established CVD, 6 152 172 (28.9%) patients at‐risk for CVD, and 8 728 205 (41.0%) patients with low‐risk for CVD.

Among the overall population, median follow‐up time was 49.0 months, with the longest follow‐up duration observed for those without obesity (50.7 months), followed by those with obesity class ≥ 1 (47.0 months), and shortest among those with obesity class ≥ 3 (40.0 months) (Table 1). Mean age was 73.2 years overall, and most beneficiaries were White (84.0%), from the South (37.2%) or Midwest (23.9%), and classified as non‐dual eligible/low‐income subsidy (76.8%). Females were more represented overall (59.3%); proportions were similar for those without obesity and those with obesity class ≥ 1, but higher for those with obesity class ≥ 3 (68.1%).

Clinical Outcomes

Clinical outcomes were assessed among 21 306 251 beneficiaries for nonfatal MI and stroke, coronary revascularization, heart failure‐associated hospitalisation, MACE‐3 and MACE‐5, and all‐cause and CVD‐related death; in 15 243 447 beneficiaries for T2D; and in 322 624 beneficiaries for ESRD (Figure S2). The unadjusted incidence and hazard rates for clinical outcomes are available in Tables S5 and S6. The scaled Schoenfeld residuals as well as Kaplan–Meier curves did not indicate meaningful departures from proportional hazards.

After adjustment, those with obesity class ≥ 1 had higher relative hazards compared with those without obesity, particularly for heart failure‐associated hospitalisation (adjusted HR [95% CI]: 1.92 [1.92–1.93]), T2D (1.65 [1.65–1.66]) and MACE‐5 (1.44 [1.43–1.44]) (Figure 1A). Likewise, those with obesity class ≥ 3 had higher relative hazards compared with those without obesity for heart failure‐associated hospitalisation (3.40 [3.38–3.41]), MACE‐5 (2.27 [2.26–2.28]) and T2D (2.20 [2.18–2.22]). Hazards for heart failure‐associated hospitalisation, MACE‐5, and T2D were also higher among those with obesity versus without obesity across all CVD risk stratifications (Figure 1B–D).

All‐Cause and CVD‐Related PPPY HCRU

HCRU and cost outcomes were assessed among patients with ≥ 12 months of continuous medical and pharmacy enrollment during follow‐up (n = 19 699 272). This included 3 317 276 individuals with obesity class ≥ 1, 522 529 individuals with obesity class ≥ 3 and 16 381 996 individuals without obesity.

Unadjusted HCRU is available in Table S7. Among the overall population, individuals with obesity class ≥ 1 had higher adjusted HCRU (adjusted rate ratio [95% CI]) versus those without obesity for all‐cause IP visits (1.40 [1.38–1.41]), ED visits (1.28 [1.27–1.29]) and OP visits (1.28 [1.28–1.29]) (Figure 2A). Likewise, those with obesity class ≥ 3 had higher all‐cause IP visits (1.84 [1.81–1.86]), ED visits (1.56 [1.54–1.58]) and OP visits (1.43 [1.42–1.43]) versus those without obesity (Figure 2A). Across all CVD risk stratifications those with obesity class ≥ 1 and class ≥ 3 had higher adjusted all‐cause HCRU versus those without obesity (Table S8).

CVD‐related HCRU was also higher among those with obesity compared with those without obesity. After adjustment, those with obesity class ≥ 1 had higher CVD‐related IP visits (1.58 [1.54–1.61]), ED visits (1.46 [1.42–1.49]), and OP visits (1.39 [1.38–1.40]) versus those without obesity (Figure 2B). Likewise, those with obesity class ≥ 3 had higher CVD‐related IP visits (2.06 [2.01–2.12]), ED visits (1.74 [1.69–1.80]), and OP visits (1.53 [1.51–1.55]) versus those without obesity (Figure 2B). Across CVD risk stratifications, those with obesity class ≥ 1 and class ≥ 3 had higher adjusted CVD‐related HCRU for most outcomes versus those without obesity (Table S8).

All‐Cause and CVD‐Related PPPM Costs

Unadjusted PPPM all‐cause and CVD‐related patient healthcare costs are available in Table S9. Among the overall population, adjusted all‐cause healthcare costs (mean PPPM USD; cost ratio [95% CI]) were higher among those with obesity class ≥ 1 versus without obesity for both total costs ($4113 vs. $2876; 1.43 [1.43–1.43]) and total medical costs ($3109 vs. $2221; 1.40 [1.40–1.40]) (Figure 3A). Higher costs were observed across all outcomes including IP costs ($1071 vs. $755; 1.42 [1.40–1.43]), ED costs ($94 vs. $76; 1.24 [1.23–1.25]), OP costs ($1118 vs. $848; 1.32 [1.32–1.32]), other medical costs ($729 vs. $525; 1.39 [1.38–1.40]) and pharmacy costs ($852 vs. $525; 1.62 [1.62–1.62]). Likewise, those with obesity class ≥ 3 had higher all‐cause total costs ($5319 vs. $2929; 1.82 [1.81–1.82]) and total medical costs ($4048 vs. $2253; 1.80 [1.79–1.80]), versus those without obesity (Figure 3A). Costs were higher across all outcomes including IP costs ($1422 vs. $756; 1.88 [1.86–1.90]), ED costs ($112 vs. $76; 1.47 [1.46–1.49]), OP costs ($1265 vs. $853; 1.48 [1.48–1.49]), other medical costs ($1139 vs. $539; 2.11 [2.09–2.13]) and pharmacy costs ($1085 vs. $541; 2.01 [2.00–2.01]). Across all CVD risk stratifications those with obesity class ≥ 1 and class ≥ 3 had higher adjusted all‐cause costs versus those without obesity (Table S10).

Outcomes were adjusted for clinically relevant or potentially confounding demographics (age, sex, race, region, low‐income subsidy) and comorbidities (dementia, pulmonary disease, rheumatoid disease, and chronic liver disease). Adjusted PPPM estimates were derived from the models to represent group‐level mean outcomes, and adjusted means with 95% CIs and mean ratios with 95% CIs were reported. CI, confidence interval; CVD, cardiovascular disease; ED, emergency department; IP, inpatient; Med, medical; OP, outpatient; PPPM, per‐patient per‐month; Rx, pharmacy; USD, United States dollars.

Higher adjusted CVD‐related total medical costs (mean PPPM USD; cost ratio [95% CI]) were also observed for those with obesity class ≥ 1 versus without obesity ($904 vs. $612; 1.48 [1.46–1.49]), and likewise for those with obesity class ≥ 3 versus those without obesity ($1159 vs. $620; 1.87 [1.85–1.89]) (Figure 3B). Those with obesity class ≥ 1 had higher CVD‐related IP costs ($469 vs. $292; 1.60 [1.57–1.64]), ED costs ($19 vs. $13; 1.51 [1.47–1.54]), OP costs ($118 vs. $83; 1.42 [1.41–1.44]), and other medical costs ($172 vs. $103; 1.67 [1.61–1.72]) versus those without obesity. Likewise, those with obesity class ≥ 3 had higher CVD‐related IP costs ($614 vs. $293; 2.09 [2.04–2.15]), ED costs ($24 vs. $13; 1.84 [1.78–1.89]), OP costs ($124 vs. $83; 1.49 [1.47–1.51]), and other medical costs ($277 vs. $105; 2.64 [2.54–2.74]) versus those without obesity. Across all CVD risk stratifications, those with obesity class ≥ 1 and class ≥ 3 had higher adjusted CVD‐related costs for most outcomes versus those without obesity (Table S10).

Discussion

Individuals with obesity had higher adjusted HRs for all major clinical outcomes compared with individuals without obesity, particularly for the outcomes of heart failure‐associated hospitalisation, T2D, and MACE‐5. For these outcomes, those with obesity class ≥ 1 had a relative risk between 1.5 and 2 times greater versus those without obesity. In those with obesity class ≥ 3, the relative risk was roughly 2–3.5 times greater for these outcomes compared to those without obesity. This association between obesity status and greater risk for clinical outcomes was also observed across all CVD risk stratifications (established CVD, at‐risk for CVD and low‐risk for CVD). It is important to note that these stratified findings represent conditional associations within each CVD risk group rather than an independent causal relationship between obesity and cardiovascular outcomes.

In addition to the association between obesity and risk for clinical outcomes, those with obesity consistently showed higher HCRU and costs compared with individuals without obesity. In all beneficiaries, regardless of CVD risk or obesity class, the biggest contributors to total medical costs were OP costs (~30%–40%), followed by IP costs (~30%–35%). Pharmacy costs accounted for approximately 20% of total costs and were greater among those with obesity. Those with obesity class ≥ 1 had 30%–40% greater all‐cause IP, OP, and ED utilisation rates yearly and roughly 40% higher monthly total medical costs compared to those without obesity. In those with obesity class ≥ 3, yearly all‐cause utilisation rates were 40%–80% greater versus those without obesity, along with 80% higher monthly total medical costs. The association between obesity and greater HCRU and costs has been observed across different population groups, including commercially insured individuals and Medicare Advantage (MA) enrollees [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7], highlighting the broad impact of obesity from both a clinical and economic perspective. However, the impact of CVD risk status on these outcomes among those with and without obesity has yet to be described. At all CVD risk stratifications, there was an association between obesity and higher costs, with similar adjusted mean ratios, regardless of CVD risk status. However, costs increased numerically with CVD risk status and obesity status, with the highest total medical costs overall observed among those with established CVD and obesity class ≥ 3. A recent cross‐sectional analysis of adult Medicare beneficiaries with BMI ≥ 25 estimated that among those with 1 or more comorbid conditions, a reduction of 5% in weight could reduce annual spending by 7% and a 25% reduction in weight could reduce annual spending by 31% [22].

The clinical and economic outcomes observed in this study are strengthened by the study design and statistical methods, with a key strength being the use of Medicare FFS data. In addition to the large sample sizes and ability to report CVD‐related death afforded by this data source, the economic outcomes use actual cost data from Medicare FFS beneficiaries. While this association of higher costs with obesity has been previously reported using a variety of data sources, including MA enrollees [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7], this study is among the first to characterise the burden of illness from both a clinical and economic perspective among the Medicare FFS population, which differs from the MA population in characteristics, coverage, utilisation and costs [23, 24, 25]. In addition, this is to our knowledge the first study to report these outcomes among the Medicare population with the additional characterisation by CVD risk status, which highlights the broad impact of obesity. These results should however be considered within the context of certain limitations, namely as an observational study, our analysis was not designed to estimate causality. Obesity diagnosis measured by International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes are generally underreported and underutilised in the real‐world, and this may result in misclassification bias, whereby patients who meet clinical criteria for obesity are not correctly identified. Likewise, obesity classes based on BMI codes may also be misclassified. Together, this may lead to underestimation of the study's sup‐population of Medicare beneficiaries with obesity. However, if the reference group includes individuals with undocumented obesity, then the outcomes of this study, which are of significant magnitude, may also be underestimated. Likewise, there is a potential for misclassification of covariates or study outcomes, including cause‐specific death, in administrative claims data. Confounding variables such as baseline comorbidities could also contribute to clinical outcomes and healthcare costs. While the study was designed to avoid directly adjusting for mediators, stratification by CVD risk groups, which inherently contain some mediation effects, could introduce bias and is a limitation [26, 27]. Multivariable regression (MVR) analysis was used to address for confounding variables which is a standard and well‐established method for observational RWE studies, and can produce comparable results to propensity score based methods when the outcome model is correctly specified and the number of events is sufficient relative to the number of covariates [28]. In our analysis the large sample size and adequate event count supported MVR as the primary approach. The outcomes are adjusted means from the GLM modelling where the baseline characteristics are fixed to the entire population mean. This approach ensures that group comparisons are not driven by differences in baseline characteristics between the groups. However, residual or unmeasured confounding remains a limitation inherent to the nature of claims data based analyses. The lower unadjusted all‐cause mortality hazard in those with obesity is consistent with the ‘obesity paradox’ in older adults, which has been attributed to frailty bias and reserved causation [29]. As frailty and functional status are not directly captured in administrative claim data, residual confounding from unmeasured frailty cannot be fully excluded, even after adjustment for dementia and other confounders. Additionally, due to the large sample sizes afforded by the data source, confidence intervals are small, increasing the likelihood of statistical significance even when differences are minor. The low event rates (~3%) observed in our study for certain outcomes (e.g., non‐fatal MI and stroke) contribute to overall lower effect sizes, and future studies with longer follow‐up time are warranted to assess these outcomes. This study was limited to only those individuals with Medicare FFS insurance coverage and results may not be generalizable to patients who are younger than 65, or patients with other insurance types, or those who are uninsured. Lastly, the continuous enrollment requirements may have created a selection bias for those who are continually enrolled in Medicare FFS.

Conclusion

Obesity was associated with substantially greater long‐term clinical burden, use of healthcare services, and costs in both those with and without established CVD among Medicare FFS beneficiaries. Further research is warranted to improve treatment and prevention of obesity to reduce the complications and costs associated with serving the US Medicare beneficiaries.
