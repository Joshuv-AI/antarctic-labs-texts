OX40/OX40L modulation: A target for regulating T cells in cutaneous inflammatory disorders
Authors: Aditya K. Gupta, Julia Steriopoulos, Vasiliki Economopoulos, Paradi Mirmirani, Vincent Piguet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aetiology of many inflammatory cutaneous diseases is T cell‐driven and involves dysregulation of the immune response. The complex nature of the immune system's involvement in these diseases has made the development of effective, safe, and durable treatments challenging, resulting in a continued demand for the development of novel therapeutics. OX40 and its ligand OX40L have been previously identified as a crucial axis in T cell differentiation and function, and are promising targets for the treatment of T cell‐driven disease. Development of OX40/OX40L agonists and inhibitors has potential uses in atopic dermatitis, alopecia areata, psoriasis, and more. While trials of these drugs have mainly been conducted in atopic dermatitis, they show promise in treating other dermatological conditions. Studies of amlitelimab and rocatinlimab have shown some efficacy but have demonstrated increased durability of response. Current concerns with the class are the potential for broad immune suppression with antagonists and the potential for worsening disease with agonists. Additional studies will enable us to determine their benefit–risk ratio in the real‐world setting.

INTRODUCTION

The pathogenesis of many skin disorders, such as atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, involves dysregulation of the immune response. Immunomodulatory therapies, such as disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and corticosteroids, have been used to treat many of these disorders, but the broad immunosuppressive properties of these drugs warrant more targeted approaches. Biologic drugs and JAK inhibitors are a recent advancement that have greatly improved patient outcomes. Even so, not all patients respond to these treatments.

Biologics used to treat skin disorders target specific cytokines (interleukin (IL)‐17, IL‐4, IL‐12 and IL‐23) as well as tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), 1 , 2 inhibiting specific immune responses, which include Th1 (TNFα and IL‐12/23 targeted), 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Th2 (IL‐4 and IL‐13 targeted) 7 , 8 , 9 and Th17 (IL‐17 targeted), 6 , 10 and possess a risk of possible infection. JAK inhibitors, on the other hand, are less specific and target different members of the Janus Kinase (JAK) family. The inhibition achieved with these types of drugs is broad and affects multiple pathways, with the potential to have unwanted adverse events. Even though these drugs possess boxed warnings for major adverse cardiac events (MACE), infections, and malignancy, 11 , 12 , 13 these drugs have been shown to be relatively safe in recent meta‐analyses and in cohort studies. 13 , 14 , 15 However, the complex nature of these dermatological disorders, combined with the complexity of the immune response and individual patient variation, leads to varied treatment responses. 2 , 16

The OX40/OX40L signalling pathway is a relatively new therapeutic target and presents an approach that may be an effective treatment option for some dermatological disorders, as its inhibition would reduce immune activation upstream from current immunomodulating therapies, thereby impacting multiple activation pathways. OX40/OX40L is a co‐stimulatory pair, where OX40 stimulation promotes the differentiation of naïve CD4+ T cells, the survival of effector T cells and the formation of memory T cells. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 OX40L stimulation on antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) has been shown to promote the maturation of these cells and secretion of various factors, including TNFα, IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6 and IL‐12. 21

In this review, we discuss the role of OX40/OX40L signalling in various skin disorders and the impact that inhibiting this pathway may have on disease progression. We will also discuss current therapeutics and their stage of development, as well as potential adverse events.

OX40/OX40L SIGNALLING PATHWAY

OX40/OX40L are a pair of co‐stimulatory immune checkpoint proteins. 22 OX40 is a member of the TNF superfamily which is widely expressed on activated CD8+, CD4+, and regulatory T cells. 19 , 23 The corresponding ligand, OX40L, is expressed on antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages and B cells, and some activated T cells. 23 , 24 , 25 OX40 signalling is necessary for the survival of effector T cells and formation of memory T cell populations, and directs naïve CD4+ T cells to differentiate into Th1 or Th2 cells.

OX40 signalling supports effector T cell survival by increasing production of several pro‐survival and anti‐cell death molecules. In CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, OX40/OX40L signalling initiates nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) activation via interactions with tumour necrosis factor receptor‐associated factors (TRAFs) (Figure 1), 17 , 26 leading to expression of the anti‐apoptotic proteins Bcl‐2 and Bcl‐xL. 27

OX40/OX40L signalling improves T cell survival via activation of anti‐apoptotic signalling. (a) OX40/OX40L signalling can indirectly promote cell survival via activation of NF‐κB which results in the transcription of anti‐apoptotic molecules Bcl‐xL and Bcl‐2, and (b) directly via the activation of PKB which inhibits the activity of pro‐apoptotic molecules BAD and pro‐apoptotic transcription factors such as FOXO.

Additionally, OX40/OX40L signalling activates the PI3K/PKB pathway within T cells (Figure 1), 28 which suppresses pro‐apoptotic molecules such as BAD and transcription factors such as FOXO. 29 Simultaneously, PKB will phosphorylate and activate β‐catenin, increasing survivin transcription, blocking caspase‐mediated apoptosis and promoting clonal expansion. 30 , 31

OX40/OX40L signalling supports memory T cell development, directly impacting some chronic skin disorders. Tissue resident memory T cells release proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines upon stimulation and in disorders such as vitiligo and psoriasis (PsO), these memory cells recognize self‐antigens and elicit an inappropriate immune response, furthering disease progression. 32 The absence of OX40/OX40L signalling, through OX40 knockout or antagonism, reduces the generation and survival of memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 while OX40 agonists increase memory T cell generation and survival in vivo. 33 , 36 , 37 This effect on T cell memory may offer an advantage to currently used biologics which have limited longevity of response. 1 , 16

In the polarization of naïve CD4+ T cells, 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 OX40/OX40L signalling typically favours Th2 differentiation, particularly in the presence of IL‐4 (Figure 2), but can result in Th1 polarization when IL‐12 and/or IFNγ are present. 20 , 23 , 38 OX40/OX40L signalling will cause the proliferation of existing T regulatory cells (Tregs) (Figure 2), but seems to prevent naïve T cells from polarizing to Tregs even in the presence of tumour growth factor β1 (TGF‐β1), highlighting the complexity of OX40/OX40L signalling. 18 , 19 , 39

OX40/OX40L Signalling Modulates T Cell Proliferation and Polarization Dependent on the Cytokine Milieu. OX40/OX40L signalling can result in a variety of different effects on T cell proliferation and polarization depending on the existing microenvironment.

OX40 and OX40L display distinct expression profiles and modulation of each has distinct effects. Antagonism of OX40 impairs the survival and expansion of many T cell populations. OX40 knockout mice develop fewer antigen‐responsive T cells and have less surviving memory and cytotoxic T cells. 36 , 40 This dampening could result in susceptibility to infection, as shown in clinical trials of OX40‐inhibiting drugs where upper respiratory infections, fever, and other minor illnesses commonly occurred. 22 , 41 , 42 , 43

OX40L is expressed mainly on APCs, where stimulation of OX40L promotes maturation and the release of inflammatory cytokines from these cells. 19 , 38 In a murine model, blockage of OX40L was more effective at reducing inflammation in arthritis than OX40 blockade. 44 Targeting OX40L avoids T cell depletion, therefore preventing broad immune suppression. Amlitelimab, an OX40L inhibitor, showed fewer minor illness‐related adverse events than OX40 inhibitor Rocatinlimab. 45 Amlitelimab has also shown a sustained response in atopic dermatitis (AD) patients, both continuing and discontinuing the drug for 28 weeks. 46 , 47

SKIN DISORDERS, CURRENT TREATMENTS AND ROLE OF OX40/OX40L SIGNALLING

As mentioned above, OX40 and OX40L form a co‐stimulatory immune checkpoint pair that promotes T cell differentiation, polarization, and survival. 22 , 25 This pair of molecules plays a key role in the formation and progression of autoimmune and autoinflammatory skin disorders. Many of these skin disorders, which include alopecia areata (AA), hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), vitiligo, AD, PsO, and chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), are mediated by T cells, but with differing and often mixed immune activation phenotypes (Figure 3).

T cell activation phenotypes and skin disorders. (a) Th1 activation occurs in alopecia areata, hidradenitis suppurativa, and vitiligo. In this phenotype, OX40/OX40L signalling in the presence of IL‐12 or IFNg leads to the Th1 phenotype, with expression of IL‐2, IL‐12, TNFα, and IFNγ. TNFα and IL‐12 targeted biologics inhibit downstream cytokine signalling. (b) Th17 activation occurs in psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa. In this case, IL‐23, IL‐1β, and IL6 with OX40/OX40L co‐stimulation leads to the expression of IL‐17A/F, IL‐21, and IL‐22. Biologics targeted to IL‐17A or F inhibit downstream cytokine signalling, while IL‐23 targeted biologics inhibit Th17 differentiation. (c) IL‐4 leads to a Th2 phenotype, where IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 are produced. Dupilumab, which is targeted to IL‐4R, leads to the inhibition of downstream signalling as well as downregulating differentiation into the Th2 phenotype.

OX40/OX40L targeted treatments act upstream from many currently available biologics at the point of T cell activation and may provide more durable results than targeting only one cytokine pathway. Targeting OX40/OX40L in combination with currently available treatment options may increase the durability of drug responses. 48 It is important to keep in mind that even though OX40/OX40L signalling seems to be central to many aspects of T cell activation, proliferation, and survival, it is not the only mechanism, and inhibition may not be beneficial in all disorders.

Alopecia areata

AA is a T‐cell‐mediated autoimmune disorder characterized by loss of immune privilege in the hair follicle. 49 This loss of immune privilege involves an increase in IFNγ and IL‐15 within the hair follicle's microenvironment, with a decrease in protective factors (TGF‐β1, IL‐10, as well as others), allowing for the infiltration of immune cells. 49 , 50 IFNγ expression within the microenvironment promotes a Th1 phenotype (Figure 3a), however, Th2 and Th17 cells may also be present in some patients. 49 , 50 , 51

There are currently few effective therapies for AA, with patients receiving topical diphenylcyclopropenone (DPCP) immunotherapy, local injection of corticosteroids into lesions, or systemic therapies such as systemic corticosteroids, DMARDs, and more recently JAK inhibitors (baricitinib, tofacitinib, ritlecitinib, and deuruxolitinib). 52 , 53 , 54 Many of these drugs are broadly immunosuppressive, with the potential for significant adverse events, particularly increased infection risk, and for JAK inhibitors, this also includes an increased risk of MACE events. 11 , 12 , 55 Some biologics are currently under investigation for use in AA which include dupilumab (IL‐4Ra targeted) and ustekinumab (IL‐12 and IL‐23 targeted). 52 Modulating OX40/OX40L signalling has the potential to target several aspects of the immune microenvironment in AA. There is an increase in the number of OX40L+ mast cells within the skin in AA, in addition to increased CD8+ T cells, potentially supporting the activation and expansion of these T cells. 18 Additionally, Kim et al. have shown that OX40/OX40L is upregulated within hair follicles of AA‐affected skin, 56 while a proteomic study by Glickman et al. found that OX40 expression was associated with more significant disease. 57 As various Th phenotypes are likely involved in AA pathology with variations between patients, inhibiting OX40/OX40L signalling may impact the expansion of all immune phenotypes, potentially providing more effective control of the disorder. Additionally, as inhibiting OX40/OX40L interferes with the proliferation and differentiation of T cells into various Th populations and memory T cells as well as interactions with B cells, the potential for loss of efficacy over time due to anti‐drug antibodies may be lower. 56

Agonizing OX40/OX40L may be another therapeutic approach within AA. As AA is typically Th1 dominant, agonism of OX40 with Th2‐stimulating factors may shift the activation phenotype from Th1 to Th2 and allow for hair regrowth. In fact, topical DPCP immunotherapy utilizes this approach indirectly to shift the activation phenotype. 58 , 59 When agonizing OX40, care must be taken to ensure that the appropriate immune phenotype is generated, as there is a very real potential that the inflammatory processes underlying AA become magnified.

Vitiligo

Vitiligo is a Th1‐dominant disorder, where oxidative stress in melanocytes results in apoptosis and leads to the infiltration of Th1‐type helper and cytotoxic T cells into the skin. 9 , 60 These infiltrated cells can produce IFNγ and TNFα, furthering the type 1 immune response. 2 , 9 , 61 TNFα‐targeted biologics, such as adalimumab, have been tested in vitiligo but have had limited efficacy as well as reports of alopecia areata as an adverse event. 2 Additionally, IL‐17 and Th17 cells have been found within skin lesions, but their role in vitiligo pathogenesis has not been fully elucidated. 2 Th17 cells have been observed to transdifferentiate to Th1‐type cells in vitiligo, potentially explaining the lack of efficacy of IL‐17‐targeted biologics such as secukinumab. 2 Currently, vitiligo is treated with topical corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors and JAK inhibitors, mainly ruxolitinib. 60 Oral treatments include pulsed courses of corticosteroids, some DMARDs and possibly JAK inhibitors such as baricitinib and tofacitinib, where trials are underway. 60 , 61 OX40L has been shown by Gay‐Mimbrera et al. to be upregulated in monocytes present in patients with non‐segmental vitiligo, where they may support T cell activation. 62 Treg cells are also dysregulated within vitiligo, which may present a therapeutic target for OX40 modulation (Figure 2). 61

As with AA, targeting of OX40/OX40L signalling may be advantageous compared to currently available treatment options, particularly compared to oral therapies, where the risk of adverse events is higher, most notably that JAK inhibitors are known to have an increased risk of MACE events, malignancies and infection which are listed as boxed warnings. 11 , 12 , 60

Hidradenitis suppurativa

Both Th1 and Th17 phenotypes are thought to play a role in HS pathogenesis. In early phases of the disorder, it is thought that a dysbiosis occurs within the follicular unit, leading to the infiltration of a variety of immune cells, including Th1 and Th17 cells (Figure 3a,b) and leading to the formation of inflammatory nodules and eventually absesses. 16 , 63 Tunnels and bands of scar tissue occur as the disorder progresses. Currently, HS is treated with antibiotics (clindamycin, rifampicin, doxycycline and tetracyclines), corticosteroids and DMARDs, anti‐androgens, as well as with surgical procedures. 16 More recently, biologics have been approved for use in HS, including adalimumab (TNFα), secukinumab (IL‐17A) and bimekizumab (IL‐17A/F) which have proved invaluable for patients with significant disease. 63 However, these treatments are not without risk; all three drugs have been associated with tuberculosis reactivation and other opportunistic infections and malignancy, with adalimumab being associated with the development of antibody‐mediated autoimmune disorders. 3 , 10 , 63 , 64 Secukinumab and bimekizumab have been associated with an increased risk of fungal infections, particularly candidiasis. 10 , 63 , 64 As with any biologic, there is a risk that the patient will develop anti‐drug antibodies, decreasing efficacy.

OX40/OX40L could be a promising alternative to current therapeutic targets. As mentioned earlier, OX40/OX40L acts at an upstream point in the immune activation pathway compared to TNFα and IL‐17, and is involved in both Th1 and Th17 activation. 65 OX40 expression has been shown to be upregulated within HS lesions. 66 Targeting OX40/OX40L may allow for Th1 and Th17 phenotypes to be downregulated simultaneously, and may also reduce the risk of anti‐drug antibodies as T cell‐B cell interactions would be reduced.

Psoriasis

Psoriasis is a predominantly Th17‐driven disorder, with infiltration of both lymphoid and myeloid immune cells. DCs within the skin produce IL‐12 and IL‐23 which are the main drivers of Th1 and Th17 activation, respectively. 1 IL‐17 is secreted by Th17 cells and this secreted IL‐17 promotes the release of chemokines by keratinocytes, leading to additional infiltration of immune cells, creating a feed‐forward loop. 1 , 49 Current effective therapeutic options, in addition to traditional immunosuppressive treatments, include TNFα inhibitors (etanercept, infliximab and adalimumab), IL‐17 inhibitors (secukinumab, bimekizumab and ixekizumab) and IL‐23 inhibitors (risankizumab, guselkumab and tildrakizumab) and the IL‐12/23 inhibitor ustekinumab. The IL‐17 inhibitors have shown higher efficacy in trials compared to the TNFα and IL‐23 inhibitors, but all have clinical benefit. 16 The risks with these drugs are generally low, but, as mentioned above, there are risks of infection and malignancy. 16 As multiple immune activation phenotypes are involved in psoriasis, targeting OX40/OX40L could be an alternative by downregulating the activation of each of these phenotypes. Currently, an OX40‐targeted antibody, KHK4083, has been trialled in psoriasis, where a decrease in disease activity was observed. 65 , 67 However, more trials of OX40‐targeted biologics are needed to assess efficacy and safety in this condition.

Atopic dermatitis

In AD, IL‐4, as well as alarmin cytokines (IL‐25 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin), are produced in response to tissue damage and drive Th2 differentiation through OX40/OX40L, allowing for allergic disease to develop. 22 , 45 , 49 These Th2 cells will produce IL‐4 and IL‐13 which disrupt normal skin barrier function and promote pruritis by direct action on peripheral sensory neurons. 8 IL‐31 is also produced by Th2 cells and may also contribute to pruritis. 22 , 45 More recent evidence suggests that Th17 and Th22 responses may also contribute to AD pathology and that chronic AD may involve Th1 response as well. 22 , 68 There is additional evidence that tissue resident memory T cells (TRMs) may contribute to the severity of AD. TRMs have been shown to infiltrate chronic AD lesions and will produce IL‐4 and IFN‐γ when stimulated. 69 , 70 Mehta et al. demonstrated that TRM infiltration correlated with AD disease severity, highlighting the importance of this cell population in AD pathology. 70

Given the potential for several different inflammatory phenotypes to be involved in AD pathology, inhibiting OX40/OX40L signalling may lead to more robust responses than current therapy options. 68 Glickman et al. demonstrated through proteomic analysis that OX40 expression was associated with increased AD severity. 57 Currently, patients with significant AD burden are treated with conventional non‐targeted immunosuppressive treatments or receive targeted therapies such as dupilumab, which targets the IL‐4R alpha subunit (Figure 3c), which inhibits signalling through IL‐4 and IL‐13, and tralokinumab, which targets IL‐13 directly, helping to alleviate symptoms. However, not all patients respond to these treatments, and many of the traditionally immunosuppressive therapies can have significant risks when taken long‐term. 68 Dupilumab and tralokinumab both act downstream of OX40/OX40L, only impacting one aspect of AD's immune dysfunction. Exploiting the upstream signalling of OX40/OX40L would target more pathways in dysfunctional immune processes that occur in AD and prevent their sustained activation compared to current treatment options.

Chronic spontaneous urticaria

This disorder is caused by the inappropriate degranulation of mast cells (MCs) within the skin, leading to the formation of hives. OX40L is one of a myriad of receptors and ligands that can activate MCs, as well as IL‐4 and TNFα. 71 These cells can interact with many other cells including Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells, which may occur directly through OX40/OX40L. Currently, CSU is treated with antihistamine to eliminate symptoms, 72 but refractory cases require cyclosporine or omalizumab (anti‐IgE IgG antibody). 71 OX40 may potentially provide benefit to patients with refractory disease by not only downregulating the expansion and activation of Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells, but also directly through interactions with OX40‐expressing cells.

Pemphigus

This disorder is caused by an inappropriate immune response to desmoglein (DSG) 1 or DSG3 within the skin, leading to the development of large blisters. 12 Follicular helper T cells (Tfh cells) interact with B cells, and through OX40/OX40L co‐stimulation, DSG1 or DSG3 targeted antibodies are produced. These Tfh cells express high levels of OX40 and, therefore, may strongly activate the B cells, leading to increased auto‐antibody production. 18 By inhibiting this co‐stimulatory interaction, the production of auto‐antibodies is potentially reduced and may provide relief for the patient. 18 , 73 There is potential for targeting OX40 or OX40L given the role of OX40/OX40L signalling in this disorder.

Alopecia areata, vitiligo, hidradenitis suppurativa, chronic spontaneous urticaria and pemphigus

There currently are very few trials, if any, of OX40/OX40L targeted biologics in these disorders. Amlitelimab, an OX40L antagonist which is currently being developed by Sanofi, is being tested in AA (NCT06444451), where the phase 2 trial is currently active and no longer recruiting. 74 Additionally, amlitelimab is being tested within HS in a phase 2, multicentre trial (NCT06118099) which is ongoing. 75

Psoriasis

Trials of OX40/OX40L modulating drugs in psoriasis are in early phases of development. A phase I randomized trial was conducted in patients with psoriasis using rocatinlimab at varying doses delivered by intravenous infusion or subcutaneous injection. This study observed no serious AEs and some improvement in PASI and sPGA scores at doses of 1 and 10 mg/kg delivered by intravenous injection and at a dose of 1 mg/kg delivered subcutaneously. 67 Specifically, a reduction in PASI scores by 50% was achieved in approximately 50% of patients receiving 1 mg/kg subcutaneously and by approximately 18% of patients receiving 10 mg/kg intravenously by day 57 of treatment. 67 More trials of OX40‐targeted biologics are needed to properly assess efficacy and safety.

Atopic dermatitis

The vast majority of clinical trials of OX40/OX40L modulating drugs in treating diseases of the skin have been conducted in patients with AD. There are currently several clinical trials in progress for Amlitelimab in atopic dermatitis (NCT05769777, NCT06130566, NCT06181435, NCT06407934 (recruiting), and NCT06224348 (recruiting)). 121 , 122 , 123 , 124 , 125 , 126 Amlitelimab is an antagonistic antibody targeted to OX40L present on APCs and is non‐depleting of T cells. Two clinical trials have been completed for Amlitelimab in AD; trial NCT03754309 found a significant reduction in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI) by 16 weeks of treatment in both dosages of Amlitelimab (200 mg loading dose then 100 mg qw4, or 500 mg loading dose then 250 mg q4w) administered intravenously (59% on low dose and 52% on high dose vs. 25% on placebo for EASI‐75 – proportion achieving 75% reduction in EASI from baseline), 76 while trial NCT05131477 (STREAM‐AD) found a significant reduction in EASI by week 24 at all doses (500 mg loading dose then 250 mg q4w, or no loading dose then 250 mg q4w, 125 mg q4w, or 62.5 mg q4w; mean decrease of 51.6%–61.5% in treated vs. 29.4% in placebo) 77 of subcutaneously administered Amlitelimab with maintenance of this response at week 52 in patients continuing or stopping treatment. 78 , 127 The COAST1 (NCT06130566) study results were shared via press release in September 2025 and demonstrated an EASI‐75 of 46.0% for q4w dosing and 50.3% for q12w dosing compared to 27.6% in placebo. 128

The efficacy of Amlitelimab is lower than upadicitinib (61%–80% EASI‐75; Measure Up, AD Up and Heads Up trials), but higher than baricitinib (24%–31% EASI‐75; BREEZE‐AD trials) and comparable to abrocitinib (44.5%–72.0% EASI‐75; JADE trials). 129 Amlitelimab performed better than tralokinumab (25.0%–56.0% EASI‐75; ECZTRA trials) but comparable to dupilumab (38.1%–69.7% EASI‐75; LIBERTY trials). 129 The same trials also found Amlitelimab to be well tolerated, with low rates of adverse events (AEs) being reported in experimental groups. The most commonly reported AEs were headache, upper respiratory infections, hyperhidrosis, pyrexia, increased aspartate aminotransferases and iron deficiency. Conjunctivitis was reported as an AE in studies; however, one study deemed these cases to be unrelated to the treatment. 76 , 77

A large phase III trial of rocatinlimab is underway, referred to as the ROCKET programme which contains eight clinical trials (HORIZON, IGNITE, SHUTTLE, VOYAGER, ASTRO, ORBIT, ASCEND and OUTPOST) and examines the safety and efficacy of Rocatinlimab in treating atopic dermatitis in adults and adolescents. 41 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 93 , 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 An initial phase 2b study (NCT03703102) demonstrated an EASI‐75 of 39% to 54% in treated groups versus 11% in placebo. 48 NCT05651711 (ROCKET‐HORIZON), which was designed to test rocatinlimab monotherapy, reported a reduction in EASI by 75% or more (EASI‐75) in 32.8% of patients treated with rocatinlimab compared to 13.7% of those in the placebo group. 130

NCT05398445 (ROCKET‐IGNITE), designed to evaluate two different doses of rocatinlimab, reported achievement of EASI‐75 in 42.3% of patients in the high‐dose rocatinlimab group and 36.3% of the low‐dose group, compared to 12.8% and 12.9% in the respective placebo groups. 130 NCT05724199 (ROCKET‐SHUTTLE), which examined the use of rocatinlimab in combination with topical corticosteroids and/or topical calcineurin inhibitors, reported achievement of EASI‐75 in 52.3% of high‐dose group participants and 54.1% of the low‐dose group, compared to 23.6% and 23.7% in the respective placebo groups. 91 NCT05899816 (ROCKET‐VOYAGER), which assessed the impact of Rocatinlimab usage on vaccine responses, is currently underway. 92

No results are available from the ASTRO and ORBIT studies (adolescents), 95 , 96 the ASCEND study (maintenance of response in withdrawal and extension cohorts), and the OUTPOST study (self‐administration). 94 , 97 Amgen has released a statement reporting the most frequent AEs to be similar to those reported in previous ROCKET trials, including upper respiratory infections, aphthous ulcers, headache, influenza, cough, and rhinitis. 131

Trial NCT03096223 found that 55% of patients administered 10 mg/kg q2w of Rocatinlimab intravenously for 6 weeks, then followed for an additional 16 weeks, achieved EASI‐75. While 17/22 patients in the trial experienced treatment‐related AEs, all events were considered minor and did not result in discontinuation. The most frequent AEs were pyrexia and chills. 98 Trial NCT03703102, 48 conducted subsequently to trial NCT03096223, found a significant reduction in EASI by 16 weeks of treatment at all doses (150 mg q4w, 600 mg q4w, 300 mg q2w, 600 mg q2w) administered subcutaneously in atopic dermatitis patients. 48 The AEs in this study occurred more frequently in the experimental groups versus placebo and include pyrexia, chills, headache, nasopharyngitis, nausea, and aphthous ulcer. 48 These patients experienced improved durability of response compared to placebo, which may be partially attributed to the depletion of antigen‐specific T cell populations. 48 Overall the efficacy of rocatinlimab is similar to that of other biologics currently used clinically, while upadicitinib outperforms rocatinlimab. 129

There are two completed clinical trials of telazorlimab in patients with atopic dermatitis; Trial NCT03568162 found a significant reduction in EASI by week 16 of treatment delivered subcutaneously in the 300 mg q2w and 600 q2w groups with a 57.6% (300 mg dose) and 59.7% (600 mg dose) drop in EASI compared to 42.1% and 43.2% in the respective placebo groups, 43 and trial NCT02683928 found a significant reduction in EASI after 10 weeks (67.4% drop in treated vs. 38.2% in placebo) of treatment delivered intravenously (two infusions of 10 mg/kg 28 days apart). 42 However, there was a high rate of discontinuation in all trial arms for both trials (18% to 45% in NCT03568162; 45% to 50% in NCT02683928). 42 , 43 In NCT02683928, reported AEs were most commonly headache, nasopharyngitis, and upper respiratory infection, while in NCT03568162 there was a slightly higher rate of AEs in the treatment groups, with both treatment and placebo having high rates of non‐serious adverse events. 42 , 43

Trial NCT06094179 is currently recruiting, with the aim of evaluating the efficacy and safety of BAT6026 in AD. 101 BAT6026 is an OX40 agonist which is also being tested within solid tumours (NCT05105971). 102

Malignancies

As OX40/OX40L is a co‐stimulatory immune checkpoint pair, targeting these molecules, particularly OX40 through agonism, may provide an effective treatment approach. Many tumours have escaped immune surveillance, allowing for the growth of not only the primary tumour but also dissemination to secondary sites. OX40 agonism can help lift the immune checkpoint blockade that is generated by the tumour cells' expression of PD‐L1.

Currently, there are several agonists in development for both solid and non‐solid tumours. BGB‐A445 is an OX40 agonist that is currently in clinical trials for various solid tumours, which include melanoma and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, with several trials underway. 104 , 105 , 106 A phase 1 trial that was recently completed and published did not show significant responses in patients when BGB‐A445 was administered alone, but responses were seen when the drug was administered with the PD‐1 inhibitor tislelizumab. 104 , 132 This trial did observe AEs in their patients, with the most common being fatigue, nausea and pyrexia, however, there was no placebo control group for comparison. 132

Another OX40 agonist, BAT6026 is currently under investigation in solid tumours, with one active trial. 102 A second trial was terminated early, but the reasons for the termination are not clear. 103 HLX51, being developed by Shanghai Henlius Biotech, was approved to start clinical trials within China in 2023 and will be investigated in solid tumours and lymphomas. 109 , 133 Ivuxolimab, which is under development by Pfizer, has completed phase 1 trials and phase 2 trials are currently underway. 117 , 134 In phase 1 trials which focused on safety and tolerability, the most common side effects were fatigue (46.2%), nausea (28.8%), and loss of appetite (25%) and only 1 AE was a grade 3 event with no dose‐limiting toxicities observed. 117 , 118 INBRX‐106, a hexavalent OX40 agonist, is currently in phase 2 trials. 110 Detailed analysis of this antibody in vitro, in vivo, and in patients demonstrates enhanced activation of T cells. 111 , 112 Even though many of these trials have demonstrated overall safety with these drugs, the majority of available results are from dose escalation studies where small numbers of patients were studied and did not possess placebo controls.

Safety of OX40/OX40L modulation

The safety of any drug targeting OX40/OX40L signalling needs to be considered. As with antibodies that antagonize downstream cytokines (interleukins and TNFα), there is a risk of infection with those that antagonize OX40. As mentioned earlier, OX40/OX40L signalling supports the proliferation, survival and differentiation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and is essential in forming memory T cells. Rocatinlimab and telazorlimab both deplete T cells through antibody‐dependent cell cytotoxicity, leading to possibly even further suppression compared to currently available treatments which target cytokines. 135 , 136 In fact, no further trials of telazorlimab have been conducted since 2022; however, the reasons for this are not clear, as the results of these trials were promising. This signalling is not specific to one immune phenotype but rather functions in all contexts, with suppression of OX40 potentially leading to broad immunosuppression. This leads to a depletion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells of any immune phenotype and prevents the formation of memory T cells which are necessary for mounting an immune response. 137 Similar to other biologics and JAK inhibitors, this broad suppression could potentially leave patients susceptible to infections or cancer, as this immunosuppression would also have an impact on tumour immune surveillance. Work by Byun et al. demonstrated that the development of Kaposi sarcoma could be linked to an OX40 mutation leading to a deficiency in OX40 signalling. 138 Additionally, the ability of patients to form an immune memory after vaccination may also be hindered. Caution is needed to ensure that patients remain healthy.

When OX40L is antagonized, there will still be inhibition, but the same depleting effect is not observed. 135 OX40L on APCs is blocked from interacting with OX40, but this does not necessarily deplete the T cell pool and still allows for the patient to form an immune response. These biologics will still lead to a broad suppression of T cell responses, but it would not be as pronounced as OX40 antagonists, which currently deplete T cells.

In AD and in cancer, OX40 agonists are being tested. 101 , 102 The goal of these drugs is to shift to a more proinflammatory immune phenotype. In cancer, this shift will hopefully allow the immune system to overcome the tumour's inhibitory signals, 139 while in AD, the goal would be to shift the immune phenotype to halt the disease process. This has the potential to be quite dangerous, as triggering an increase in the immune response runs the risk of patients experiencing cytokine storms, leading to poor outcomes. Additionally, even though AD is primarily Th2 driven, there are still Th1 aspects of inflammation in the disorder, and agonizing OX40 could lead to an increase in disease activity in susceptible patients. 22 , 61 , 140

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

In recent years, there has been a drive towards precision medicine, leading to several targeted therapies that have been revolutionary for patient care in dermatology. The recent development of OX40/OX40L targeted therapeutics may further impact patient care, potentially through increased efficacy and inhibition of multiple immune phenotypes. Targeting OX40/OX40L signalling, particularly in AD, may provide an effective treatment option for patients where multiple immune phenotypes may be active and who have chronic, treatment‐resistant disease, where memory, helper and regulatory T cell depletion may be beneficial.

There are currently several OX40/OX40L modulating drugs in development to treat a variety of inflammatory and autoimmune skin disorders (Table 1). Several drugs have shown promise in the treatment of Th1, Th2, and Th17‐driven disorders, with most data available for atopic dermatitis (Th2), while only early‐phase data are available for PsO, HS (Th17), and AA (Th1). Studies in AD with larger numbers of patients are needed to determine the true efficacy of these drugs with varied mechanisms of action in these patients. Later phase trials are also needed in other skin disorders to determine the feasibility, efficacy, and safety of these drugs. Additionally, the safety of these drugs, which would suppress all OX40/OX40L interactions, needs to be thoroughly studied, as OX40 inhibition has been linked to cancer development. 138 This limitation of the currently available data could be addressed through larger trials and trials across more disorders.

The development of OX40/OX40L modulating drugs for use in the treatment of inflammatory dermatological disorders is only beginning, and given the mechanism of action, this therapeutic category may be pivotal in providing both effective and durable results. In the future, the development may include a wider variety of T cell‐driven dermatological pathologies.
