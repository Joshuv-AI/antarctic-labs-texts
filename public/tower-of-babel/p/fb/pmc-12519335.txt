Impact of adding an airway safety prompt to the A–F bundle on unplanned extubation in the intensive care unit
Authors: Maged Tanios, Huan Mark Nguyen, John W Devlin, Hyunsoon Park, Michael Tanios, Meera Lakshmi Mahidhara, Jay Patel, Sasanapirath Vong, Jeff Cupino, Mark Cordia, Antonio Beltran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEndotracheal intubation is a life-saving intervention for adults with acute respiratory failure (ARF) but may result in unplanned extubation (UE). The success of UE prevention efforts has varied. We describe the development, implementation and impact of an airway safety quality improvement programme (ASQIP) embedded in an existing ABCDEF (A–F) bundle on UE occurrence.DesignBefore-and-after evaluation of an ASQIP.Setting42-bed mixed intensive care unit (ICU) at a university-affiliated teaching hospital.PatientsConsecutive adult patients, endotracheally intubated for ARF.InterventionThe ASQIP, developed from a literature review, the results of a national clinician survey, local clinician focus group input and root cause analyses of prior UE events, included interprofessional rounding scripts and was embedded into an existing A–F bundle. Multiple implementation strategies were employed, including didactic education to all ICU nurses (registered nurse, RN) and respiratory care therapists (RTs), the daily posting of signs of the ASQIP on the doors of rooms with a patient deemed to be at high risk for UE, and daily reminders from managers to bedside RTs and RNs.MeasurementsASQIP implementation was effective and was associated with a significantly lower incidence of UE per 100 MV days (before 0.43 vs after 0.29; p=0.04).ConclusionsA multidisciplinary quality improvement initiative that incorporates airway safety within the A–F bundle may help reduce UE rates in critically ill adults. Future research is needed to validate standardised communication and assess the long-term sustainability of such interventions.Keywords: Quality improvement, Patient safety, Critical care, Team training, Airway Management

Introduction

Endotracheal intubation and invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) are lifesaving interventions for patients with acute respiratory failure (ARF) in the intensive care unit (ICU). Unplanned extubation (UE), often categorised as either accidental or patient-initiated, refers to the inadvertent removal of the endotracheal tube (ETT) and occurs at an estimated rate of 0.3–4.2 events per 100 days of intubation.1 2 This complication of IMV may lead to hypoxia, pneumonia, prolonged mechanical ventilation and extended ICU stays.2,4 Published efforts to prevent UE have generally focused on addressing individual risk factors rather than developing a bundled, multicomponent UE prevention strategy that can be adopted for use by the interprofessional ICU team on daily rounds.4,7

The ABCDEF bundle (A–F bundle), a multicomponent strategy shown to reduce delirium, hasten liberation from mechanical ventilation, shorten hospital stays and lower mortality rates,8,11 is widely used in ICUs worldwide.10,13 With previous initiatives to reduce UE in our ICU proving ineffective, we felt the A–F bundle was the ideal platform for implementing a multidisciplinary quality improvement (QI) initiative focused on enhancing airway safety and preventing UE in our ICU. We describe the development, implementation and impact of an airway safety quality improvement programme (ASQIP) integrated into the existing A–F bundle on the incidence of UE and identify potential factors associated with UE.

Methodology

Setting

A 42-bed mixed medical-surgical ICU at a 420-bed university-affiliated, community teaching hospital.

Participants

Consecutive critically ill patients requiring IMV were compared before (January 2022 to May 2022) and after (June 2022 to December 2022) implementation of an ASQIP.

Usual care

The A–F bundle14 has been in place to guide ICU daily rounds for more than a decade, and multiple audits of A–F bundle use in our ICU have demonstrated that daily use of all bundle elements exceeds 90%. The A–F bundle elements include: to Assess, prevent and manage pain; Both daily Spontaneous Awakening Trial (SAT)/Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT); Choice of analgesia and sedation; Delirium: Assess, prevent and manage; Early mobility and exercise; Family engagement and empowerment was well-established in the study ICU before the development and implementation of the ASQIP.

Development of the airway safety QI initiative

We used best practice approaches for developing QI projects in healthcare and identifying key dimensions for the project.15 16

Literature review and expert input: To develop and implement an ASQIP that the ICU multidisciplinary team will deliver daily, we used a literature review by conducting a systematic search of common English-language databases (ie, PubMed, Embase and Scopus) using relevant search terms including ‘unplanned extubation, airway safety and quality improvement’. Articles were screened for relevance to our planned QI interventions, clinical outcomes and planned implementation strategies.1,35 7 17 We also used results from a recent national survey of ICU clinician airway safety practices,45 46 input from local ICU clinician focus groups, and a review of data from root cause analyses of UEs at our institution. The key findings from the root cause analyses were the identification of variability in sedation documentation, concerns over the effectiveness of commercial ETT holders and the need for standardised education on the appropriateness of using physical restraints.

Implementation of the ASQIP

Education and preimplementation preparation for the project were conducted in June 2022. During the preimplementation phase, critical care nurses and respiratory care practitioners received a didactic in-service on the ASQIP from their practice leaders. To ensure providers’ familiarity with existing protocols and unit best practices, introduce the initiative, and engage and address concerns from front-line staff, a unique didactic in-service was provided to each clinician group (ie, nurses, RTs, intensivists), that covered the ASQIP and their specific roles as part of the interprofessional ICU team. The ASQIP airway safety prompt was addressed during daily ICU bedside rounds while the A–F bundle goals and completion were discussed. Daily screening was implemented to identify patients at high risk for UE (as per the ASQIP). In addition, a door sign indicating the current phase of ventilation weaning was posted on the door of all patients. For patients with high-risk airways (HRAs), an additional door sign alert was posted. RTs were responsible for daily documentation of ETT securement, clinical findings, safety discussions and daily plans in the electronic medical record. Nurses and physicians were encouraged to include their discussions and plans regarding airway safety and ASQIP use in their daily progress notes. Compliance with the ASQIP was monitored daily by the ICU clinical nurse specialist/charge nurse and lead RTs. When gaps in compliance were detected, they provided real-time reminders to bedside RNs and RTs, respectively, under their charge. While this report focused on outcome evaluation, relevant process measures—such as adherence to securement checklists, documentation of sedation goals and daily risk reviews—were tracked internally to guide iterative improvements. These measures were not collected systematically for analysis, and ongoing daily monitoring on a per-shift basis was used to maintain stable bundle adherence and address any drift in real-time. The initiative was conducted in California, USA, where the state’s nursing ratio law requires a 1:2 RN-to-patient ratio in the ICU.

Data collection and management

All UE episodes were reported per the institution’s policy for unusual occurrences and were collected by the Quality Department. Data on the occurrence of all UE incidents and the use of ETT securement devices in the before-and-after group were obtained. Additionally, data on potential factors associated with UE were collected for all patients in each group who experienced UE. Based on the literature review,1 7 31 39 48 49 this included whether the patient was at the target Richmond Agitation and Sedation Scale (RASS) goal before the UE, whether the patient was agitated (RASS≥1) at the time of the UE, the use of restraints, the use of an ETT securement device, the presence of the RT in the room at the time of UE, any RN interventions during the UE, the number of patients under the nurse’s care, the nursing shift (night vs day), whether the patient was visible from the nursing station, and whether the nurse was on a strategic assignment at the time of the UE. Relevant demographic characteristics were collected for patients who experienced UE. Two initiative members performed data entry to improve accuracy and efficiency. Data were acquired from the postevent debriefing record, the electronic medical record (Epic Systems, Verona, WI), and nursing assignment records. Data verification and validation were conducted to resolve missing records, improperly formatted outputs or outliers. The deidentified dataset was stored on a secure server with access control in accordance with Good Clinical Practice (ICH E6).

Analysis

A convenience sample of patients admitted to the unit with IMV during the initiative periods was used. A χ2 test with the Yates continuity correction, Fisher exact or the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between two independent groups and test for associations between categorical variables. Statistical testing was two-sided with a type I error rate of <0.05. IBM SPSS (version: 30.0) Statistics (IBM SPSS) was used for all analyses. The QI was approved for 6 months (June 2022 to December 2022), and a convenience sample of invasively intubated patients admitted to the ICU during the quality initiative was used. Earlier studies have reported the incidence of UE as a per cent of IMV, introducing a bias for density of days at risk, necessitating obtaining details on all IMV patients for contextual interpretation. To reduce the confounding effect of intubation duration and provide a suitable comparison incidence among units, we report the outcome of interest for this study as UE per 100 mechanical ventilation days. Current literature recommends the incidence density of days at risk as the accepted metric for reporting UE.41

Patient and public involvement

Our study was conducted using deidentified data. There was no direct interaction with any individual participant, and therefore, members of the public were not involved in the study design, recruitment or conduct.

Results

The Airway Safety Quality Improvement Protocol

The final ASQIP protocol is presented in figure 1. It included: (1) the embedment of an airway safety prompt in the multidisciplinary A–F bundle. The prompt was added to the ‘Both SAT and SBT’14 element of the A–F bundle; (2) a mandatory discussion during interprofessional ICU rounds on airway safety and UE) prevention while RT presented A–F bundle goals and compliance; (3) ensuring daily sedation (RASS) goal was established and documented for each IMV patient; (4) reevaluating (and documenting) the rationale for physical restraint use every nursing shift; (5) inspecting and documenting appropriate ETT securement and position every nursing shift; (6) applying an intensified airway monitoring policy to all IMV patients identified as high risk for UE by implementing a HRA patient screening tool component to the ASQIP (online supplemental appendix A); (7) strategic nursing assignment guidelines for optimising monitoring of intubated and HRA patients included avoiding placing HRA patients in low-visibility rooms, pairing HRA patient assignments into ‘Pods’ or next door to each other, avoiding pairing HRA patients in the bay rooms (room numbers omitted) with other private rooms, and avoiding assigning HRA patients to temporary RN staff and (8) finally, a UE audit tool was routinely applied to all IMV patients (online supplemental appendix B).

ASQIP compliance

Although formal daily compliance with the protocol wasn’t collected as part of this ASQIP implementation and evaluation project, the ASQIP itself (as outlined above) required nursing and RT managers to evaluate daily staff compliance and provide real-time reminders to staff when compliance gaps were identified. The implementation monitoring by local shift leaders confirmed stable performance across bundle elements, with no sustained drift requiring system-level intervention. Anecdotal feedback from clinical staff indicated an improvement in interdisciplinary communication. In addition, based on strong team engagement, the updated A–F bundle was adopted by the night shift team following the intervention.

Outcomes compared between all before-and-after patients

The incidence of UE per 100 MV days was significantly reduced after the implementation of the ASQIP ((0.43 (before) vs 0.29 (after); p=0.04). ETT securement devices in the ICU were also significantly reduced after ASQIP implantation (before 65.9% vs after 34.1%, p=0.003). Internal tracking of process measures, including checklist adherence, sedation goal documentation and daily UE risk review, showed improvement over the intervention period, though formal analysis of these metrics was beyond the scope of this report.

Characteristics of patients who experienced a UE

Across the 41 patients across both groups who experienced a UE, their median age was 44 (IQR: 30, 63) years, and 65% were male. A total of 79.4% of the UE patients were visible from the nursing station. The goal RASS (0 to −2) was achieved in 60.5% of the UE patients before the UE event occurred; 31% reported agitation (RASS>1) before the UE event. Nearly all (86%) of the UE patients were restrained at the time of the event. Commercial ETT securement devices were in place for most (76.7%) UE events.

Discussion

Our QI initiative, which focused on a multidisciplinary approach to airway safety, resulted in a 32.5% reduction in UE. These results occurred despite the similarity in risk factors and the limited effect on practice improvement patterns during the implementation phase. Important risk factors for UE, such as young age, male gender and the presence of agitation,29 50 remained constant. Furthermore, practice patterns related to sedation adequacy, appropriateness of restraints and nurse–patient ratios did not differ significantly between the usual care and post-ASQIP implementation, apart from the higher proportion of UE events associated with commercial ETT securement devices. Although no events occurred among high-risk patients during the intervention period, the initiative did not gather risk factors for all IMV patients in the unit; other unmeasured risk factors may have influenced the outcome.

The institutional policy for airway securement states that taping the ETT is the primary securement method. After consulting with the intensivist, commercial securement devices may be considered for patients where taping is not feasible (eg, patients with facial oedema or injuries). The decreased usage of commercial securement devices may have resulted from greater adherence to the ETT taping hospital policy and suggests that adding the airway safety prompt to the A–F bundle improved interdisciplinary communication regarding airway securement. The trend of reduced UE risk following the ASQIP implementation may reflect the heightened awareness of risks communicated during the multidisciplinary rounds and the effect of subsequent preventive efforts.

Established UE risk factors for UE include both patient factors (eg, male sex, delirium, greater severity of illness, agitation) and nursing care system-related factors (eg, lack of physical restraint use, greater nurse–patient care ratios, method of endotracheal securement and sedation management approach used).1 2 27 28 While many studies have analysed risk factors,2 7 37 51 52 few have examined prevention strategies (eg, use of physical restraints, tracheal tube securement methods), and the results have been inconsistent. Therefore, the literature to support standardised preventative approaches remains limited.1 5 30 39 53 QI initiatives can be a robust tool to evaluate preventive measures and interdisciplinary practice gaps.54,56 Developing a successful QI initiative involves overcoming obstacles such as limited resources, resistance to change and the need for multidisciplinary collaboration to implement evidence-based practices. The success of our QI effort required providers’ buy-in, the careful maintenance of patient care standards, careful planning, continuous monitoring and adaptability to unforeseen challenges.

The A–F bundle’s widespread adoption as a rounding tool in adult critical care makes it an ideal facilitator for other improvement initiatives. Also known as the ICU Liberation Bundle, the A–F Bundle has become a globally recognised standard of care in ICUs, guiding interprofessional teams in improving patient outcomes. Its implementation has been associated with significant enhancements in patient outcomes, such as reduced ICU length of stay, shorter durations of mechanical ventilation and a decreased incidence of delirium. The bundle comprises six key elements: assessing, preventing and managing pain; conducting both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials; choosing analgesia and sedation; assessing, preventing and managing delirium; promoting early mobility and exercise; and fostering family engagement and empowerment.10,1257 The decision to include the airway safety improvement initiative within the A–F bundle was based on the fact that the A–F bundle is the standard of care in our ICU, ensuring a consistent approach to QI implementation. Our study did not aim to examine the impact of the A–F Bundle, as it has already been implemented in the unit, but sought to assess the effect of a customised, multifaceted airway safety improvement protocol on reducing UE rates.

Identifying and modifying UE risk factors requires a multidisciplinary team approach and regular communication to routinely discuss interventions such as daily goals for analgesia and sedation,29 30 48 58 59 optimal management of delirium,60,62 judicious use of restraints,4 22 29 50 63 increased staff surveillance, implementing ventilator weaning protocols64 65 and standardising tube fixation.2 3 51 54 66 Institutional factors, such as nursing staffing ratios and patient visibility from the nursing station, can contribute to airway safety and should also be considered when designing safety initiatives.67 68 Bundling effective practices and coordinated interdisciplinary interventions can be effective and promote institutional culture and practices, while, when implemented individually, they may not yield consistent benefits.1 7 31 36

The demanding nature of the ICU environment can introduce role confusion arising from poor team communication or inadequate handoffs, adversely affecting patient safety. The Joint Commission’s National Patient Safety Goal69 highlights this concern. Therefore, including multidisciplinary airway safety prompts in a widely adopted rounding tool such as the A–F bundle allows for better communication and improved assignment of roles and responsibilities.70 Mitigating role confusion stemming from inadequate team communication might have contributed to our finding of reduced UE.

Despite the challenge of having a lower baseline rate when demonstrating a marginal effect of implementing new interventions, our results were positive and support the concept behind this initiative. Our experience suggests that a multidisciplinary approach targeting various aspects of airway management may be more effective than applying individual interventions. While incorporating an airway safety check into the A–F bundle appears feasible, future research should focus on validating the most effective multidisciplinary communication tool within a standardised approach. Healthcare institutions should consider designing and implementing QI initiatives to enhance airway management safety practices.

Our results apply only to adult ICUs, as airway practices in paediatric ICUs may differ and would necessitate distinct interventions. A survey of airway safety practices uncovered significant variations between adult and paediatric ICUs, including methods for securing and monitoring the ETT across different ICU environments, assigning responsibilities and reporting adverse events.46 This underscores the necessity to investigate and implement standardised airway management safety guidelines for both adult and paediatric ICUs.

Our initiative focused on implementing the ASQIP as an adjunct to the existing A–F bundle discussions during daily rounds in the adult ICU, aiming to promote and reinforce airway safety considerations within the established workflow, rather than modifying or evaluating compliance with the broader bundle. As such, we did not systematically collect process or outcome measures specific to A–F bundle adherence. While gathering specific data on the entire cohort of mechanically ventilated patients would have allowed for a more in-depth analysis, this was not the goal of this initiative. The primary objective of the initiative was the pragmatic implementation of enhanced interdisciplinary communication guided by the A–F bundle over a 6-month period. While a pragmatic approach reflects routine ICU practice, it limits reproducibility and generalisability. The adoption of the revised A–F bundle format by the night shift team suggests feasibility, perceived value and early signals of sustainability.

Our findings suggest that embedding UE prevention strategies within the A–F Liberation framework is feasible and may be associated with reduced UE incidence. However, as a proof-of-concept study, this report has several limitations. As a single-centre ICU QI project, our findings are based on a convenience sample constrained by the study timeline rather than a formal a priori power analysis. Given the relatively low prevalence of UE observed during the study period, we could not conduct more advanced statistical analyses, such as statistical process control or formal time-series methods, as these require larger sample sizes to produce reliable inferences. As a result, we employed a simple preimplementation versus postimplementation comparison, which, while informative, does not account for potential confounding factors or secular trends. The relatively low sample size may have introduced a type II error when comparing the impact of risk factor modification on outcomes. Confirming the sustainability of our results will require a long-term analysis that includes the characteristics of all IMV patients during the initiative. Given our lack of a comparator group who did not experience an UE, we cannot determine whether the observed changes were specifically attributable to the ASQIP or other concurrent changes in clinical practice or patient characteristics. The lack of difference in risk factors between usual care and the QI implementation phase may be related to previous QI efforts. Lastly, we did not consider balancing the measures, given the potential unintended consequences of the UE intervention, which could reduce adherence to and sustainability of the A–F elements. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness and sustainability of UE prevention strategies through multicentre collaborations that use formal time-series methods and a priori planning for process and balancing measure collection to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study suggests that a multidisciplinary QI initiative incorporating airway safety within the A–F bundle may reduce UE rates in critically ill adults. Future research should focus on validating standardised communication and practices to prevent UE and assess the long-term sustainability of such interventions.
