Photographic determination of bubble departure diameter in subcooled flow boiling of aqueous calcium sulfate solution
Authors: Ali Taft Ashian, Shiva Falak Dini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a comprehensive experimental investigation of bubble departure diameters during subcooled flow boiling of aqueous calcium sulfate (CaSO4) solutions. The impacts of heat flux, bulk liquid temperature, volumetric flow rate, and salt concentration were methodically investigated utilizing a laboratory-scale flow boiling loop. Bubble growth and departure processes were recorded by high-speed photography and analyzed with digital image processing techniques. The results demonstrate that increasing heat flux and solute concentration lead to larger bubble departure diameters, while higher flow rates tend to suppress bubble growth. The number of bubbles per unit of volume (Population) also increases with heat flux and concentration, but decreases with flow rate. A new correlation regarding heat flux, Reynolds number, Jakob number, and salt concentration has been developed, which predicts bubble departure diameters within ± 10% of the observed values. This study contributes to the understanding of boiling heat transfer performance for saline solutions and has direct implications for the design and operating conditions of heat exchangers exposed to a scaling fluid. Introduction

Boiling heat transfer is significantly important in several industrial processes, which include, but are not limited to, desalination, chemical production, nuclear reactor cooling, and energy generation. The departure process of the vapor bubbles from the heated solid can be important in controlling both the heat transfer rate and the critical heat flux. The bubble departure diameter is one of the many parameters controlling the departure process. The bubble departure diameter can also be considered important because it controls the bubble frequency, interfacial area, and surface rewetting behavior. While there have been many studies addressing the dynamic behavior of bubbles in pure fluids, electrolyte and saline solutions, such as NaCl, KCl, and CaSO4, complicate matters further1–7. The dissolved salts affect the physical properties of the liquid, such as surface tension, viscosity, density, and it modifies bubble nucleation, growth and detachment4. Rationally, an additional complexity arises from salts with inverse solubility, such as CaSO4, precipitate with heating and then scale or foul an industrial heat exchanger8.

The deposition of CaSO4 leads to a reduction in thermal efficiency and operational reliability. It is important to understand the bubble behavior in scaling environments and systems, as they are so relevant in various industrial processes. While systems which suffer from thermal scaling are directly relevant to industry, relatively few publications have studied bubble departure diameters from an evaporative surface in aqueous CaSO4 solutions, under subcooled flow boiling conditions, or by primarily studying aqueous solutions of either de-ionized pure water and/or simple electrolytes (e.g., NaCl). Due to its inverse solubility—in other words, its solubility increases with decreasing temperature—it is important to understand what occurs as a whole, when the heat flux, flow rate, and solute concentration all influence the bubble behavior in CaSO4 mixtures to develop predictive correlations and/or reduce fouling in thermal processes. The current study aims to address this gap, by conducting a systematic, photometric study of bubble departure diameters during the subcooled flow boiling of aqueous CaSO4 solutions. This paper describes the investigations of high-speed imaging and digital image processing, to measure and quantify the heat flux, flow rate, bulk liquid temperature, and concentration on bubble release processes while boiling in aqueous CaSO4. The study provides basic understanding into boiling heat transfer within saline media, and direct implications in thermal process design/operation with scaling fluids1–10. To guarantee the reproducibility and reliability of the reported trends, a thorough statistical analysis and uncertainty quantification were applied to all measured quantities. Online Appendix B contains information on the statistical framework and uncertainty analysis.

Literature review

The formation and departure of vapor bubbles during the process of subcooled flow boiling has been the subject of numerous studies due to their significant impact on overall heat transfer performance and stability of the flow. The preliminary experimental studies focused on pure water and basic geometries in order to develop primary correlations for bubble growth, diameter of departure, and frequency11–18 (Figs. 1 and 2).

Experimental setup schematic.

Bubble diameter measurement from high-speed images using ImageJ software.

Ünal and Klausner et al. provided semi-empirical models for bubble departure diameter as correlated to wall superheat, liquid subcooling (ΔTsub), and flow velocity. Later, Situ et al. accomplished high-speed visualization and CFD approaches to characterize the near-wall bubble dynamics and correspond the two-phase models to real engineering applications19–21.

More recent experimental studies3,4,22 have characterized mass flux, liquid subcooling, and heat flux on bubble departure size; however, the interaction between buoyancy, drag, surface tension, and inertial forces together seem less established.

In addition to pure fluids, the effect of dissolved substances on boiling phenomena has drawn recent interest. Peyghambarzadeh et al. showed that dissolved salts can affect bubble nucleation and departure by altering surface wettability and thermophysical properties of the fluid. However, previous investigations mostly feature regular soluble salts like NaCl and KCl, while the inverse-solubility salts like CaSO4 (where precipitation and interfacial phenomena are more convoluted) have been under investigated2.

Recent studies have improved the understanding of bubble dynamics and interfacial effects in subcooled flow boiling. Yang et al.23 reviewed experimental approaches and key mechanisms governing bubble behavior, while Xanthopoulou et al.24 showed that changes in liquid composition can significantly influence bubble growth and detachment. In addition, Hallaji et al.25 examined calcium sulfate fouling under subcooled flow boiling conditions. However, the combined effects of inverse solubility, surface deposition, and bubble departure dynamics in CaSO4 solutions are still not well understood23,24.

The goal of the present work is to investigate bubble departure diameter experimentally in aqueous CaSO4 solutions at a range of operating conditions; a viable correlation is suggested to be used in future heat-exchanger designs. Although bubble departure in pure water and common electrolytes has been extensively studied, recent high-resolution studies by Colin et al. and Baglietto et al. highlighted the role of near-wall forces, surface condition, and local hydrodynamics in forced-convection boiling. Nevertheless, inverse-solubility salts like CaSO4, where simultaneous bubble dynamics and surface deposition coexist, were not taken into account in these studies, which were restricted to non-scaling fluids.

Novelty and contribution

This research includes a systematic experimental analysis of subcooled flow boiling in calcium sulfate (CaSO4) solutions. Calcium sulfate is a salt with an inverse solubility where solubility decreases with temperature. This behavior results in fundamentally different bubble nucleation and growth behavior than NaCl or water, with considerable coupling of surface deposition of the solid with bubble dynamics. High-speed photographic measurements were conducted to measure bubble departure diameters and bubble population density while changing heat flux capacity, flow rate, and solute concentration systematically. Although bubble behavior in electrolyte solutions has been widely studied, experimental work on scaling-prone CaSO4 systems remains relatively limited, especially under subcooled flow boiling conditions. Based on the present experiments, a new empirical correlation was developed that incorporates a Scaling Concentration factor (Cs) alongside classical non-dimensional parameters (Re, Ja). This correlation captures the main trends observed in the data within the investigated range and highlights the role of solute concentration in influencing bubble departure behavior8–10,26,27.

Methodology and experimental setup

The experimental apparatus was an annular boiling loop utilizing a concentric cylindrical heating arrangement with stainless-steel heater rods (SS316) installed inside a transparent PMMA tube. The stainless-steel rods served as the heating candidate and the PMMA wall provided optical access to conduct high-speed pictorial visualization of bubble behavior. The experimental conditions were at atmospheric pressure (1.0 bar), hence the saturation temperature of water is likely 100 °C. The inlet temperatures of bulk liquid were 25 °C and 45 °C, which correspond to subcooling condition levels (ΔTsub = Tsat − Tin) of roughly 75 °C and 55 °C, respectively. The wall heat flux applied ranged between the range of 28.4 to 94.5 kW·m−2, by heating the inner rod with electrical means. Heat losses were minimized through insulation of the test section. Under steady-state conditions, no measurable drift in temperature or power input was observed, indicating stable thermal behavior. Based on this stability and consistency of the energy balance, heat losses are expected to be small relative to the applied heat flux and within the overall experimental uncertainty. Based on insulation design and previous similar systems, heat losses are expected to remain within a few percent of the input power.

The respective working fluids were deionized water and aqueous CaSO4 solutions, supplied by circulating the respective working fluid at volumetric flow rates of 2, 5, and 10 L·min−1, with concentrations of 1.7 g·L−1 and 2.2 g·L−1, for the proposed salt working fluid and corresponding fluid properties. The reported bubble counts are directly comparable across tests because the bubble count was defined as the total number of bubble departure events observed within a fixed image acquisition time window (Δt), recorded under identical camera settings (frame rate and recording d uration) for all experimental conditions.

All measured quantities were reported using SI units (°C, kW·m−2, L·min−1, g·L−1). To enhance repeatability, each operating condition was investigated through three independent experimental runs. On the order of 20–50 of distinct bubble departure events were extracted from the captured image sequences within each run and used as statistical samples. As explained in Online Appendix B, statistical analysis was used to quantitatively evaluate the measurements’ repeatability and the related uncertainty. High-speed imaging was performed by using a digital camera operating at 1000 frames per second with a spatial resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels. A stainless-steel micrometer scale was placed at the same focal plane as the test section to calibrate the pixel-to-length ratio under the same optical conditions, and led to eliminating parallax and depth-of-field errors. This calibration factor provided a spatial resolution of approximately 0.008 mm per pixel. To identify the bubble boundaries in ImageJ software, grayscale threshold was applied, and the bubble departure diameter was determined by averaging three orthogonal axes to reduce perspective error.

Before analysis, image sequences were preprocessed by background subtraction and contrast enhancement in order to reduce reflections. In this measurement overlapped or partially visible bubbles were excluded by means of binary segmentation and size filtering. Each experimental condition was repeated three times, and the average values were reported. After confirming linear correlation (R2 ≈ 0.999), flow rate calibration was performed through the gravimetric method. Thermocouples were re-examined by a mercury thermometer, and also heater calibration was verified via the Wilson plot method. The combined uncertainty in bubble diameter measurement was estimated within ± 0.8 pixels (± 0.6%)1–7. As explained in Online Appendix B, the uncertainty related to the image-based calculation of the bubble departure diameter, including pixel resolution and calibration effects, was specifically measured and included in the overall uncertainty budget.

The system provided precise control of heat flux, flow rate, and bulk liquid temperature. A 20 L storage tank was used to provide working fluids (distilled water, CaSO4 solutions at concentrations of 1.7 and 2.2 g·L−1) and a centrifugal pump to circulate liquid through the loop. A rotameter with a needle valve was used to control and measure liquid volumetric flow rates of 2, 5, and 10 L·min−1. The inlet liquid temperatures were set to 25 °C and 45 °C utilizing the pre-heater, resulting in subcooling (ΔTsub) of 75 °C and 55 °C, respectively. The vertical cylindrical test section was 15 mm inner diameter × 200 mm long and was heated electrically to wall heat fluxes of 28.4—94.5 kW·m−2.

Wall and bulk liquid temperatures were monitored using K-type thermocouples with an accuracy of ± 0.5 °C. Temperature measurements were primarily conducted along the horizontal direction due to geometric constraints of the test section. However, the system was designed to approximate one-dimensional heat transfer, and radial temperature gradients are expected to be small under the present operating conditions.

Fresh solutions of CaSO4 at concentrations of 1.7 g·L−1 and 2.2 g·L−1 were prepared1–7. At these relatively low concentrations, variations in thermophysical properties (ρ, μ, σ, k) are expected to be small. Based on previous studies28–30, such variations are typically within approximately 2–3% over this concentration range. This level of variation is smaller than the overall experimental uncertainty (~ 7%), and therefore, fluid properties were approximated as those of pure water at the corresponding bulk temperature. However, it should be noted that even small changes in interfacial properties, particularly surface tension, may have nonlinear effects on bubble dynamics. Therefore, the potential influence of thermophysical property variations cannot be completely excluded and is acknowledged as a limitation of the present study (Figs. 3 and 4).

The protocol outlined above was uniformly executed for each experimental condition. The procedures included loop filling; bulk liquid temperature setting; flow rate adjustment; the application of electrical heating; and bubble imaging after the experimental condition had achieved a state of steady-state. These conditions were repeated three times to allow for reproducibility, and the reported results were the average values of all the repetitions.

The uncertainty of the measurements was calculated using the Kline–McClintock method, which estimated the uncertainty based on the accuracy of the thermocouples (± 0.5 °C), the accuracy of the flowmeter (± 2%), power-supply fluctuation (± 1%), image resolution (± 1pixel), and measurement repeatability (that is, standard deviation over repetitions). The overall experimental uncertainty is on the order of ± 7%, with condition values are quantified and reported in Online Appendix B. This level of uncertainty is within the range reported in previous boiling visualization experiments31,32, which confirms the reliability of the present measurements (Figs. 5 and 6).

Results

The observed variations were significantly larger than the measurement uncertainty (typically exceeding it by an order of magnitude), indicating that the reported trends are statistically meaningful.

Although wall temperature was monitored for system control, it was not recorded with sufficient resolution to construct a reliable boiling curve (qʺ–ΔT) and was not used for quantitative boiling curve analysis. Therefore, boiling behavior in this study is interpreted based on bubble dynamics and visual observations rather than macroscopic boiling curves.

In the case of both CaSO4 concentrations, increasing heat flux increased the bubble departure diameter monotonically at a given flow rate and bulk temperature2,6,7. Effect of CaSO4 concentration

As shown in Fig. 8, increasing concentration of CaSO4 from 1.7 to 2.2 g·L−1 slightly increases the bubble departure diameter. This trend suggests that an impure solution affects bubble dynamics by modifying surface properties. Unlike ordinary electrolytes such as NaCl, CaSO4 shows inverse solubility; consequently, increasing concentration promotes micro-deposition on the heating surface, which enhances surface roughness and reduces wettability, leading to slightly larger bubble departure diameters1,18,28,32 (Figs. 13, 14 and 15).

Discussion

Mechanistic interpretation

The observed changes in bubble departure behavior can be interpreted through two concurrent mechanisms: (i) modification of thermophysical properties due to dissolved ions (ion effect), and (ii) surface modification caused by CaSO4 deposition (scaling effect). The ion effect primarily influences surface tension and bulk liquid properties, thereby affecting bubble growth dynamics and nucleation behavior. In contrast, the scaling effect alters surface roughness and may modify surface wettability, which can delay bubble detachment and increase departure diameter.

In the present study, these two effects could not be fully decoupled due to the absence of direct measurements of surface characteristics such as contact angle, roughness, and deposition layer thickness. However, the observed trends are consistent with previous studies on scaling-prone systems, suggesting that surface modification due to deposition likely contributes to the increase in bubble departure diameter.

Bubble departure can also be interpreted in terms of a force balance acting at the moment of detachment. Bubble growth is promoted by vapor generation, which increases buoyancy and internal pressure, while surface tension acts to retain the bubble on the surface. In flow boiling conditions, fluid motion introduces additional hydrodynamic forces, particularly drag, which influence the detachment process. Within this framework, the Jakob number reflects vapor generation, the Reynolds number represents hydrodynamic shear, and the concentration factor (Cs) captures surface modification effects associated with scaling.

In the present experiments, increasing heat flux enhances vapor generation and thus increases buoyancy forces, leading to larger bubble departure diameters. Conversely, increasing flow rate intensifies the surrounding flow field and increases hydrodynamic forces acting on the bubble, particularly drag, resulting in earlier detachment and smaller bubble sizes.

The increase in bubble size observed in CaSO4 solutions is therefore likely influenced by a combination of surface modification due to deposition and changes in interfacial properties caused by dissolved ions. While deposition may alter surface characteristics and delay detachment, dissolved ions may also influence surface tension and nucleation behavior, contributing to increased bubble population density.

These observations are consistent with ion-specific bubble behavior reported in electrolyte solutions1–7,34,35, although the combined influence of ion effects and surface deposition cannot be fully separated in the present study.

Although long-term surface aging was not the focus of this study, the experiments were conducted over relatively short durations, and the heating surface was cleaned and reconditioned between test series to minimize aging effects. Therefore, the observed trends are primarily attributed to short-term bubble–surface interactions rather than progressive surface degradation.

It should be noted that the present analysis is qualitative in nature, as no direct measurements of interfacial forces or detailed flow field characterization were performed. Therefore, the proposed interpretation is intended to provide physical insight into the observed trends rather than a rigorous mechanistic model.

Comparison with literature

The outcomes of this research are consistent with reported behaviors in both pool boiling and subcooled flow boiling. For example, bubble departure diameters are seen to increase with increasing electrolyte concentration in NaCl, KCl, and KNO₃ solutions6. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the subcooled flow boiling of NaCl solutions produces larger bubbles compared to deionized water. This study corroborates such behaviors but has expanded the data base to include CaSO4, which is particularly relevant to scaling-type heat exchangers7–10,26,27 (Fig. 18).

Proposed correlation for bubble departure diameter

To overcome the limitations of existing models, a new empirical correlation was developed using dimensional analysis and regression of the present experimental data. The bubble departure diameter normalized by the hydraulic diameter is correlated with the Jakob number (Ja), Reynolds number (Re), and solute concentration (Cs)31,32,36–38 The exponents of Ja and Re were determined empirically and are close to those reported in previous works (0.4–0.5 for Ja and 0.2–0.3 for Re)28,32,39.

Since the temperature difference appears in the Jakob number, both Celsius and Kelvin scales lead to identical results. In this study, temperatures are reported in °C, but all property evaluations and correlations were performed in SI base units.

Compared to traditional pure-fluid models, this correlation provides an improved representation of the experimental trends within the investigated conditions of the present study in saline and scaling-prone settings by explicitly taking solute concentration into account8–10,27,31,32,36–38. The estimated variation of thermophysical properties due to CaSO4 addition was below 3%, which is smaller than the overall experimental uncertainty.

Practical implications

The larger bubble diameters and higher nucleation site densities observed in CaSO4 solutions indicate enhanced local heat transfer but an increased risk of surface scaling and fouling8–10,27. In industrial systems such as desalination units, power plant condensers, and petrochemical heat exchangers, these effects can accelerate performance degradation. The proposed correlation provides a practical framework for interpreting these phenomena and supporting mitigation strategies, including surface modification, flow control, and scheduled cleaning8–10,26. The observed trends exceed the experimental uncertainty, confirming their statistical significance within the investigated operating range (Online Appendix B).

Limitations and future work

While the present study provides new insights into bubble departure behavior in CaSO4 solutions, it is limited to relatively low concentrations (≤ 2.2 g·L−1) and a single test section geometry. At higher concentrations, precipitation and scaling effects are expected to become more pronounced, requiring coupled investigations of hydrodynamics and deposition kinetics8–10,27. Future work should therefore extend the experimental database to higher concentrations, different geometries, and other relevant salts (e.g., MgSO₄, NaCl). In addition, numerical simulations and CFD modeling could provide further mechanistic insight into the observed trends and help validate the proposed correlation8–10,19–21,26,33,40,41.

The potential influence of surface aging on bubble departure behavior was not directly quantified in this study. Although the heating surface was periodically cleaned and reconditioned to maintain repeatable conditions, no direct measurements of surface roughness, contact angle, or wettability evolution were performed. Therefore, the effect of surface aging is acknowledged as a limitation of the present work. Similarly, the relative contributions of dissolved ion effects and deposition-induced surface modification could not be quantitatively distinguished. Future studies should incorporate direct surface characterization, such as roughness measurements, contact angle analysis, and deposition layer thickness, to better isolate and understand these mechanisms.

Conclusion

A comprehensive investigation was undertaken in an optically-accessible boiler to characterize the bubble departure diameter during subcooled flow boiling of aqueous calcium sulfate (CaSO4) solutions. The experiments systematically scrutinized the influence of heat flux, volumetric flow rate, bulk liquid temperature, and solute concentration using high-speed photography and digital image processing analysis. The main findings are as follows:

Effect of heat flux

Effect of heat flux

Higher heat flux produced larger bubble departure diameters. Higher wall superheat increased the vapor formation and resulted in larger bubbles prior to detaching2,6,7.

2. Effect of flow rate

Effect of flow rate

Higher volumetric flow rates reduce the bubble departure diameter due to stronger hydrodynamic shear forces that detach bubbles earlier from the heated surface33.

3. Effect of bulk liquid temperature

Effect of bulk liquid temperature

At a constant heat flux and flow rate, increases of bulk liquid temperature (associated with decreased subcooling (ΔTsub)) lead to modestly smaller bubble departure diameters.

4. Effect of solute concentration

Effect of solute concentration

Solutions containing CaSO4 generate bubbles that are larger in diameter than pure water. Furthermore, the higher concentration (2.2 g·L−1) results in larger departure diameters compared to the lower concentration (1.7 g·L−1) due to the combination of lowered surface tension and altered surface wettability4,5,8,10.

5. Bubble population density

Bubble population density

The number of active nucleation sites and bubble population density raise by heat flux and solute concentration , while the bubble population density decreases as the flowrate increases2–4.

6. Proposed correlation

Proposed correlation

A new empirical correlation was developed, which incorporates the effects of heat flux, Reynolds number, Jakob number, and solute concentration. It can predict the bubble departure diameter with an average deviation of ±10%, representing good agreement with experimental data31,32,36–38.

The reliability of the experimental findings is supported by a comprehensive statistical analysis and uncertainty quantification, reported in detail in Online Appendix B.

Overall, this study’s results improve the knowledge of bubble behavior and boiling heat transfer in environments prone to scaling and containing saline fluids. The suggested correlation serves as a valuable tool for predicting and optimizing heat exchanger performance when working with electrolyte or scaling fluids like CaSO4 solutions. Future research should explore higher solute concentrations, various geometries, and different salt types to broaden the applicability of this predictive model8–10,26,27.
