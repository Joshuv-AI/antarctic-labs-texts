Association between metabolic score for insulin resistance and risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity: an analysis based on two national prospective cohorts
Authors: Zongren Zhao, Luming Wei, Huqiang Dong, Guisheng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) has become a major global health concern, carrying substantial mortality risk. This study investigated the association between the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR) and the incidence of CMM.MethodsThis prospective analysis utilized data from two nationally representative cohorts (CHARLS and ELSA). The association between the baseline METS-IR and incident CMM was assessed using logistic regression models. Restricted cubic splines and subgroup analyses were employed to examine the relationship pattern and consistency.ResultsIn the follow-up period, there were 149 cases of CMM in CHARLS and 287 cases in ELSA. Every unit increase in METS-IR correlated with a 4% elevated risk of CMM in both groups. Individuals in the highest METS-IR quartile exhibited a more than threefold elevation in CMM risk compared with those in the lowest quartile. A linear dose–response relationship was identified, exhibiting similar relationships across the majority of categories, albeit influenced by hypertension and chronic lung disease status.ConclusionIn CHARLS and ELSA, elevated baseline METS-IR independently predicted higher incident CMM risk in midlife and older adults, with a linear dose–response. As a routine-test-based metric, METS-IR can support early high-risk screening for targeted prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12986-026-01170-9.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the coexistence of two or more cardiometabolic conditions, such as heart disease, diabetes, and stroke, is a highly prevalent and severe multimorbidity pattern [1]. The annual prevalence of CMM is rising, and the mortality risk for CMM patients is considerably more than that of individuals with a singular condition, leading to a significantly diminished life expectancy [2]. At age 60, those with two chronic medical disorders (CMDs) suffered a 12-year decrease in life expectancy, but those with three CMDs encountered a 15-year decrease [3, 4]. Timely identification and control of risk factors are crucial to mitigate the worldwide burden of CMM.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a major risk factor for multiple metabolic disorders and has been associated with cardiovascular disease and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [5, 6]. IR is a notable risk factor for numerous metabolic illnesses and has been demonstrated to forecast cardiovascular diseases and negative cardiovascular outcomes [7]. Due to the widespread occurrence of IR, the prompt detection and identification of patients with CMM, along with proactive measures to enhance insulin sensitivity, may be essential for averting the advancement of cardiovascular disease and enhancing clinical outcomes. The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC) is widely considered the benchmark for evaluating insulin resistance [8]. Nonetheless, its invasive characteristics, substantial expense, and operational intricacies restrict its use in extensive clinical and epidemiological research. Moreover, alternative insulin-based insulin resistance measures are similarly limited in practical application due to apprehensions about their precision and reliability [9].

The Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR) is a non-insulin-based surrogate marker of insulin resistance calculated from fasting plasma glucose, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and body mass index. Compared with insulin-based indices such as HOMA-IR, METS-IR does not require fasting insulin measurement and is therefore more feasible for large-scale epidemiological studies and routine clinical settings. Compared with simpler lipid-glucose indices, METS-IR also incorporates adiposity and HDL-C, allowing it to reflect multiple metabolic dimensions related to insulin resistance. Previous studies have reported associations between METS-IR and individual cardiometabolic diseases, including coronary artery calcification, heart failure, and prediabetes [10]. A cross-sectional study of 1,576 participants without cardiovascular disease found that an increase in METS-IR correlated with a greater frequency of coronary artery calcification [11]. A separate study identified a positive association between METS-IR and the risk of heart failure [12]. METS-IR has been demonstrated to be an independent and positive predictor of pre-diabetes risk in Chinese populations [13]. Nonetheless, these investigations have predominantly concentrated on the association between METS-IR and specific CMD. No study has yet prospectively assessed the relationship between METS-IR and CMM. Therefore, this study used two nationally representative longitudinal aging cohorts, CHARLS and ELSA, to examine the association between baseline METS-IR and incident CMM. This study strategy seeks to furnish advanced epidemiological evidence for early risk alerts and focused prevention approaches for cardiometabolic multimorbidity.

Materials and methods

Study population and ethical approval

Baseline waves were selected according to the availability of biomarker and anthropometric data required to calculate METS-IR, including fasting plasma glucose, triglycerides, HDL-C, and BMI. CHARLS Wave 1 (2011) and ELSA Wave 4 (2004–2005) were used as baseline because these waves contained the required laboratory measurements. Follow-up waves were selected based on the availability of incident CMM information. CHARLS Wave 3 (2015) and ELSA Wave 8 (2016–2017) were used as follow-up endpoints because they provided the relevant follow-up information on diabetes, heart disease, and stroke for the present analysis. Surveys continued till the latest data: CHARLS Wave 3 (2015) and ELSA Wave 8 (2016–2017). Participants in both studies were required to be 50 years or older with complete baseline data to calculate the METS-IR. For CHARLS, the final cohort included 2,023 participants after exclusion criteria were applied, which included missing METS-IR data, pre-existing CMM, and inadequate covariate information. Similarly, the ELSA cohort included 2,163 participants after screening for missing METS-IR values, baseline CMM, and relevant covariates (see Fig. 1). Participants were excluded if they were younger than 50 years, had CMM at baseline, lacked the laboratory or anthropometric information required to calculate METS-IR, or had missing information on key covariates or follow-up CMM status. The final analytic samples were therefore based on complete-case analysis, and no statistical imputation was performed. Ethical approval was obtained from independent ethics committees in accordance with national and institutional guidelines, and informed consent was obtained from all participants [14, 15].

Assessment of METS-IR

The METS-IR were calculated according to the following formula: METS-IR = ln [2 × FPG (mg/dL) + fasting serum TG (mg/dL)] × BMI (kg/m2)/ln [HDL − C (mg/dL)] [16].

Definition of cardiometabolic multimorbidity

The principal aim of this study was to investigate a CMM event, defined as the presence of two or more chronic medical disorders, such as diabetes, heart disease, and stroke [17]. The criteria for diagnosing diabetes encompass fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c levels of ≥ 6.5%, current utilization of anti-diabetic medications, or self-reported diagnosis (e.g., “Have you ever been diagnosed by a physician with diabetes or hyperglycemia?”). Individuals with heart disease or stroke were identified by self-reported data from baseline and follow-up questionnaires, as well as the use of cardiovascular medications [18]. Standardized inquiries were posed on cardiovascular disease and stroke: “Have you ever received a diagnosis from a physician for coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or any other cardiac conditions?” The event duration was the interval between the final interview and the subsequent CMM diagnosis. The follow-up duration was the interval between the initial assessment and the final survey for patients who did not report CMM [19].

Collection of covariate information

This study included age, sex, smoking, alcohol, education, marital status, and comorbidities as variables. The CHARLS dataset classified educational level as college or above, high school, or primary school or below, smoking status as never smoked, former smoker, or current smoker, alcohol consumption as frequent, occasional, or never, and marital status as unmarried or married. The ELSA dataset characterized alcohol intake as none or yes, smoking and marital status as comparable to the CHARLS dataset, and educational level as having obtained education or never having gotten education. We included three factors to the ELSA database: job status (Managerial and professional professions, Intermediate occupations, Routine and manual occupations, Not in work), physical activity (No/Yes), and cancer.

Four characteristics defined hypertension: (1) Systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, (2) Diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg, (3) Physician-diagnosed hypertension according to self-report, or (4) Antihypertensive drug usage [20]. Baseline diabetes mellitus used as a covariate was defined according to the same criteria described in Sect. "Definition of cardiometabolic multimorbidity" [21]. Cancer included self-reported chemotherapy or surgery [22]. Chronic lung disease was assessed using cohort-specific self-reported questionnaire information. In CHARLS, chronic lung disease was identified based on self-reported physician diagnosis of chronic lung disease, including chronic bronchitis or emphysema. In ELSA, chronic lung disease was identified based on self-reported physician diagnosis of chronic lung disease or related respiratory conditions available in the questionnaire [23].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Student’s t-test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Fisher’s exact test was applied for low-prevalence variables or when the assumptions of the chi-square test were not satisfied. The association between METS-IR and incident CMM was examined using logistic regression analyses, with METS-IR modeled both as a continuous variable and according to quartiles. The crude model was unadjusted. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and cohort-specific basic demographic covariates. Model 2 was further adjusted for education, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and chronic lung disease. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to evaluate potential non-linear associations between METS-IR and incident CMM. The RCS models were fitted with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the METS-IR distribution. The P value for non-linearity was calculated by testing the coefficients of the non-linear spline terms. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to age group, sex, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic lung disease. Interaction P values were calculated by adding a multiplicative interaction term between METS-IR and each subgroup variable to the fully adjusted logistic regression model and using the likelihood ratio test. To address the potential overlap between METS-IR and FPG-based diabetes ascertainment, we performed sensitivity analyses using an alternative CMM definition in which diabetes was defined without FPG. In this alternative definition, diabetes was identified based on self-reported physician diagnosis, antidiabetic medication use, and/or HbA1c where available. In addition, baseline heart disease and baseline stroke were included in the fully adjusted model to account for pre-existing single cardiometabolic disease burden. R software (version 4.2.2) and related packages were used for all statistical analyses. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants across CMM status

Two independent elderly cohorts, the CHARLS (total n = 2023, non-CMM group n = 1874, CMM group n = 149) and the ELSA (total n = 2163, non-CMM group n = 1876, CMM group n = 287), were included in the stratified analysis of baseline characteristics by CMM status. Both cohorts showed consistent characteristics: CMM group was older (CHARLS: 61.72 ± 8.77 years vs. 58.65 ± 9.19 years; ELSA: 64.40 ± 7.26 years vs. 62.54 ± 6.79 years; all P < 0.0001), had higher BMI (CHARLS: 24.96 ± 4.47 kg/m² vs. 22.79 ± 3.58 kg/m²; ELSA: 29.80 ± 4.95 kg/m² vs. 27.44 ± 4.77 kg/m²). The CMM group had higher rates of hypertension (73.83% vs. 29.40%; ELSA: 72.47% vs. 39.45%; all P < 0.0001) and DM (26.17% vs. 8.96%, P < 0.0001; ELSA: 2.79% vs. 0.64%, P < 0.01). No significant differences were found in education or smoking status between the two groups (all P > 0.05) (see in Tables 1 and 2).

The CMM group had lower TC levels (214.23 ± 46.57 vs. 226.68 ± 42.00, P < 0.0001), more individuals without physical activity (39.02% vs. 24.52%, P < 0.0001), and higher prevalence of chronic lung disease (4.88% vs. 2.35%, P = 0.02). Compared to CHARLS, the CMM group had a greater number of non-married persons (20.13% vs. 12.01%, P < 0.01), with no significant relationships with TC, or chronic lung disease (all P > 0.05). In ELSA, the CMM group had a higher proportion of males than the non-CMM group, whereas no statistically significant sex difference was observed in CHARLS. There were no significant differences between the two groups in CHARLS residential location (urban/rural), ELSA occupation, income quartile, and cancer status (all P > 0.05)(see in Tables 1 and 2).

Association analysis Between METS-IR and CMM Risk

The association between METS-IR and incident CMM was examined using logistic regression models in both cohorts. The results are presented in Table 3 for CHARLS and Table 4 for ELSA. The dose-response relationships between METS-IR and incident CMM were further evaluated using restricted cubic spline analyses in CHARLS and ELSA, respectively (Figs. 2, 3). In the CHARLS cohort, METS-IR was positively associated with incident CMM across all models. In the fully adjusted model, each one-unit increase in METS-IR was associated with a 4% higher risk of incident CMM (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05, P < 0.001). When METS-IR was analyzed by quartiles, participants in the highest quartile had a markedly higher risk of incident CMM than those in the lowest quartile (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 3.81, 95% CI: 2.85–5.09, P < 0.001). A significant increasing trend was observed across METS-IR quartiles (P for trend < 0.001).

Similar findings were observed in the ELSA cohort. In the fully adjusted model, each one-unit increase in METS-IR was associated with a 5% higher risk of incident CMM (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.03–1.06, P < 0.0001). Compared with participants in the lowest METS-IR quartile, those in the highest quartile had a higher risk of incident CMM (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 4.03, 95% CI: 2.60–6.25, P < 0.001). The dose–response trend across quartiles was also significant (P for trend < 0.001). In sensitivity analyses using an alternative CMM definition in which diabetes was defined without FPG, the association between METS-IR and incident CMM remained generally consistent in both cohorts. After further adjustment for baseline heart disease and baseline stroke, each one-unit increase in METS-IR was associated with a higher risk of incident CMM in CHARLS (Model 2: OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.03–1.08, P < 0.0001) and ELSA (Model 2: OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.06, P < 0.0001). Compared with participants in the lowest METS-IR quartile, those in the highest quartile had a higher risk of incident CMM in CHARLS (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 3.22, 95% CI: 1.61–6.43, P < 0.001; P for trend = 0.002) and ELSA (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 3.76, 95% CI: 2.40–5.88, P < 0.0001; P for trend < 0.0001) (Tables S1 and S2). In conclusion, both cohorts showed that a higher METS-IR level represents a robust, linear, dose-response risk factor for CMM (Figs. 2, 3).

Subgroup analysis

We examined demographic, lifestyle, and clinical strata in both cohorts to see if the METS-IR-CMM risk link was consistent across subgroups. Most subgroups showed a strong positive correlation, whereas hypertension and (in ELSA) Chronic lung disease subgroups showed heterogeneity. The connection was significant across age, sex, marital status, smoking, drinking, Chronic lung disease, and DM subgroups in the CHARLS cohort (all P for interaction > 0.05). In hypertension subgroups, non-hypertensives had a stronger correlation (P for interaction = 0.027). The ELSA cohort showed consistent relationships in age, sex, married status, smoking, drinking, and DM subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05). Heterogeneity was found in hypertension (stronger in non-hypertensive participants, P for interaction = 0.035) and Chronic lung disease (significant association exclusively in non- Chronic lung disease participants, P for interaction = 0.126). Discussion

This study examined METS-IR and CMM risk in two separate cohorts from China (CHARLS) and England (ELSA), with subgroup analysis finding potential effect modifiers. These findings are consistent with previous evidence on metabolic scoring systems and suggest that METS-IR may capture metabolic disturbances related to CMM risk.

Participants in the highest METS-IR quartile had a substantially higher risk of incident CMM than those in the lowest quartile after adjustment for relevant covariates. A large-scale investigation in hypertensive patients found that the highest METS-IR quartile predicted 7.89-fold greater CMM risk than the lowest quartile, surpassing METS-VF (OR = 3.11) and CMI (OR = 11.51) [24]. RCS analyses suggested an approximately linear dose–response relationship between METS-IR and CMM risk in both cohorts, supporting the nonlinear dose-response relationship between METS-IR and CMM risk established in prior study [24, 25].

Furthermore, hypertension and Chronic lung disease subgroups showed heterogeneity, which pathophysiological relationships and current mechanistic findings help explain. Hypertensive participants may have a weaker association between METS-IR and CMM due to “risk saturation”: long-term hypertension is associated with vascular remodeling and endothelial dysfunction, which dominate CMM, masking the independent effect of insulin resistance [26]. Non-hypertensive people rely more on metabolic homeostasis, making METS-IR-related insulin resistance may show a more apparent association with CMM risk, consistent with the fact that metabolic indices better predict early-stage comorbidities in metabolically intact populations [27]. In ELSA’s Chronic lung disease subgroups, persistent systemic inflammation and oxidative stress may explain the lack of a meaningful correlation. A concept relating gut microbiota-derived TMAO to CMM suggests that Chronic lung disease-related inflammation may activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, favoring inflammatory damage above metabolic failure and altering the insulin resistance-CMM relationship [28]. According to UK Biobank, systemic inflammation (e.g., C-reactive protein) largely mediates the METS-IR-CMM connection [29].

The potential clinical relevance of METS-IR for CMM stems from its incorporation of insulin resistance, obesity, and dyslipidemia, which are fundamental pathogenic components of CMM. Previous mechanistic studies suggest that insulin resistance may be linked to endothelial dysfunction, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system activation, and chronic low-grade inflammation, which may partly explain the observed association between METS-IR and CMM risk. [30, 31], which corresponds with the finding that liver (γ-GGT) and kidney (eGFR, UACR) functions mediate the METS-IR-CMM relationship [31, 32]. These findings suggest that METS-IR may reflect a broader metabolic disturbance related to the progression from early metabolic impairment to subsequent organ dysfunction. Besides, Potential molecular mechanisms may involve visceral adipose tissue-derived extracellular vesicles carrying specific miRNAs that impair organ function [33], or the induction of hepatic FGF21 resistance disrupting cardiorenal protection [34].

From a clinical standpoint, METS-IR has a notable practical benefit: it is based on standard, inexpensive laboratory and anthropometric assessments. The application may improve the early detection of high-risk people in primary care, allowing prompt and specific lifestyle or pharmaceutical therapies to boost insulin sensitivity and avert the progression from a single illness to multimorbidity. This is especially vital considering the increasing global prevalence of CMM.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the observational design precludes definitive causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., dietary patterns, genetic predispositions) may persist. Second, the diagnosis of certain diseases (e.g., heart disease, stroke) relied partly on self-report, which might introduce misclassification bias, though this was mitigated by combining self-report with medication use. Third, the composition of CMM differed between CHARLS (higher prevalence of diabetes) and ELSA (higher prevalence of heart disease), which might influence the comparability of risk estimates. Also, METS-IR includes FPG as one of its components, while diabetes diagnosis was partly based on FPG in the primary analysis. Therefore, partial overlap between the exposure and one component of the outcome cannot be completely excluded. To reduce this concern, we performed sensitivity analyses using an alternative CMM definition in which diabetes was defined without FPG. The association between METS-IR and incident CMM remained generally consistent in both cohorts, suggesting that the observed relationship was unlikely to be solely explained by the shared FPG component. Finally, despite the large overall sample size, some subgroups had limited numbers, potentially affecting the stability of subgroup estimates.

This bi-national cohort research shows a linear dose–response relationship between high METS-IR and cardiometabolic multimorbidity in middle-aged and older persons. The connection is constant across most demographic and clinical categories, but hypertension and Chronic lung disease status modify it. METS-IR may help identify individuals at higher risk of CMM and could support risk stratification in epidemiological and primary-care settings. However, further studies are needed to determine whether METS-IR-guided interventions can reduce the risk of CMM.

Conclusion

This study found that higher METS-IR was independently associated with an increased risk of incident CMM in two national aging cohorts. The observed linear dose–response relationship and generally consistent subgroup findings suggest that METS-IR may serve as a simple and accessible marker for early CMM risk stratification. Further studies are needed to validate its clinical utility and to clarify the biological pathways underlying this association.
