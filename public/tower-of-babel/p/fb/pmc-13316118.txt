Effect of Al₂O₃ nanoparticle reinforcement and annealing on PMMA composite performance for prosthetic feet
Authors: Thamer Albahkali, Hany S. Abdo, Ahmed Algahtany, Talal T. Alshammari, Ahmed Fouly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) is a biocompatible polymer widely used in prosthetic applications, yet its limited stiffness and strength restrict its performance under the cyclic loads imposed by lower limb prosthetic feet. This study investigates the combined effect of Al₂O₃ nanoparticle reinforcement and post-fabrication heat treatment on the mechanical and structural performance of PMMA-based nanocomposites for prosthetic foot applications. Nanocomposite samples containing 0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 wt% α-Al₂O₃ nanoparticles were fabricated using a silane-assisted compounding route and subsequently annealed at 80 °C, 110 °C, and 140 °C for 15 min. XRD confirmed the phase stability of the α-Al₂O₃ reinforcement and the absence of new crystalline reaction products, while scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed progressive densification of the particle–matrix interface up to 110 °C. The optimal formulation, P7.5 A annealed at 110 °C, in which the optimal Young’s modulus and a compressive yield strength have been achieved, representing improvements of + 71.1% and + 22.2%, respectively, over neat PMMA. A finite element model (FEM) of a prosthetic foot was developed in ANSYS. FEM results showed that P7.5 A/110°C reduced total structural deformation by 40.2% relative to neat PMMA. These findings demonstrate that PMMA reinforced with 7.5 wt% Al₂O₃ and annealed at 110 °C offers a well-balanced combination of stiffness, strength, and structural safety suitable for lower limb prosthetic foot applications.

Introduction

Commercially available lower limb prosthetic feet are a crucial component in rehabilitation medicine, providing significant support to individuals with disabilities. Nevertheless, the materials commonly utilized in these prostheses may not always provide optimal comfort on a personal level. The selection of a prosthetic foot plays a key role in replicating a natural walking pattern and enabling ankle mobility. This choice is typically guided by the user’s daily functional needs and specific personal factors, such as age and body weight. Among the available options, energy-storing feet like the Flex-Foot offer enhanced performance by providing up to six degrees of freedom at the ankle joint. Despite these advancements, current prosthetic foot designs often fall short in offering the flexibility required for certain everyday tasks, such as kneeling or putting on footwear1. Therefore, the materials chosen for these devices are of critical importance, as they can significantly influence user performance and comfort.

In the early development of lower limb prosthetics, materials such as metals and wood were commonly used for manufacturing. However, these materials posed several practical challenges. Their relatively high weight made them cumbersome for everyday use, and they exhibited poor resistance to environmental factors like moisture and corrosive elements2. Wooden components, in particular, were prone to swelling and degradation when exposed to damp conditions, while metals often suffered from rust and fatigue over time3. These material limitations significantly hindered the prosthetic’s functional capabilities, especially in terms of dynamic performance and user adaptability. As a result, individuals relying on these prosthetic limbs were often restricted to basic, non-strenuous movements and had limited mobility in more demanding physical activities.

To address these shortcomings, the introduction of polymer matrix composites marked a pivotal advancement in prosthetic technology4. These composite materials brought a range of beneficial properties that enhanced both the functionality and comfort of lower limb prostheses. Notably, their lightweight nature reduced user fatigue and made the prosthetics easier to maneuver. In addition, polymer composites demonstrated superior resistance to corrosion and moisture, effectively eliminating issues related to environmental degradation. Their high fatigue resistance contributed to improved durability and performance over time, while the simplicity of their fabrication allowed for more customizable and complex designs. Consequently, the use of polymer matrix composites has become a standard in modern prosthetic engineering, offering users improved comfort, greater reliability, and enhanced mobility5.

Beyond conventional formulations, recent advances in thermoplastic composites have emphasized improved processing, recyclability, and performance6, including additively manufactured fiber-reinforced systems such as continuous sisal-fiber 3D-printed PLA composites7. In parallel, bio-based composites derived from plant fibers are increasingly investigated for their favorable specific properties and circular, low-carbon end-of-life pathways8. These developments motivate the present effort to enhance a thermoplastic acrylic (PMMA) matrix through a low-loading ceramic nano-reinforcement combined with a simple post-fabrication heat treatment.

Despite these advantages, a key challenge in the application of polymer composites lies in the optimization of their material and processing parameters. Achieving a composite with satisfactory mechanical properties such as strength, and stiffness requires careful selection and balance of constituents, including fiber type, matrix material, fiber orientation, and manufacturing technique9. The complexity of these interacting variables makes it difficult to consistently produce composites that meet al.l the performance demands of a lower limb prosthesis10. As such, ongoing research is focused on fine-tuning these parameters to enhance the reliability, efficiency, and biomechanical compatibility of polymer composite prosthetic limbs.

Polymeric materials intended for biomedical use must exhibit excellent biocompatibility and avoid triggering any chemical interactions that could lead to toxicity within the human body. Among these materials, polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) has held a prominent role in biomedical applications since its first introduction in 193711. The widespread use of PMMA can be attributed to several beneficial characteristics, including its lightweight nature, aesthetic appeal, ease of handling and polishing, cost-effectiveness, and structural stability during prosthesis use12. These attributes have made PMMA a preferred choice among researchers and clinicians for decades.

Traditionally, PMMA components are produced through a process that involves mixing pre-polymerized PMMA powder with liquid methyl methacrylate (MMA) monomer to create a moldable paste, which is then shaped into the desired form using casting techniques13. Despite its popularity, PMMA is not universally ideal for all medical applications. Its surface properties are relatively poor, making it unsuitable for use in conditions where frictional forces are prominent. Furthermore, its mechanical characteristics—particularly its flexural strength and impact resistance—are relatively limited14. When subjected to mechanical stresses such as tension, compression, or shear, PMMA is prone to surface damage in the form of scratches and cracks, and it tends to exhibit a high wear rate over time. These limitations have prompted ongoing research into ways to enhance PMMA’s mechanical performance for broader biomedical use15.

To overcome the limitations of PMMA, many researchers have worked on improving its properties through different methods. One approach involves using plasma technology to modify the surface chemistry of PMMA, which helps improve its resistance to impacts16. Scientists have also looked into adding cross-linking agents to enhance the strength and overall performance of the material17. In addition to these strategies, some researchers have applied heat treatment to PMMA as a way to improve its wear resistance18. Another widely used method is reinforcing PMMA by mixing it with different additives, such as particles or fibers. These fillers help improve the mechanical properties of the composite19. Kanie et al.20 highlighted that using fillers is one of the most effective ways to enhance the performance of polymer composites. Asar et al.21 examined the effect of adding metal oxide powders—like titanium dioxide (TiO₂), zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂), and aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃)—to PMMA. Their results showed noticeable improvements, especially in fracture toughness.

Further studies tested combinations such as ZrO₂ with Al₂O₃ and ZrO₂ with TiO₂22. Alhareb et al.23 evaluated the impact of various additives, including nitrile butadiene rubber (NBR) and ceramic materials like Al₂O₃ and yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), combined with a silane agent. Their best-performing blend included 2.5% YSZ, 2.5% Al₂O₃, and 7.5% NBR. It’s also worth mentioning that adding a significant amount of ceramic fillers caused noticeable differences in sample weight, due to their higher density compared to pure PMMA.

Aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) is widely used in different types of nanocomposites due to its impressive set of properties. This metal oxide stands out for its high stiffness, excellent resistance to wear, scratches, and corrosion, as well as its strong mechanical traits like fracture toughness, long-term durability, and resistance to deformation under stress24. In its nanoparticle form, Al₂O₃ also offers high hardness, great thermal stability, good dielectric behavior, and the ability to withstand high temperatures. Several studies have looked at how adding Al₂O₃ nanoparticles affects the behavior of composites. Wu et al.25 investigated epoxy behavior with 3% weight of nano-Al₂O₃ and found that the material’s tensile strength, impact resistance, and stretchability at break improved by 82%, 63%, and 33%, respectively. Along with better mechanical performance, the composite also showed improved thermal stability and decent dielectric properties. Souza et al.26 examined the effect of adding different amounts—2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, and 10%—of nano-Al₂O₃ to epoxy. They reported significant gains in thermal resistance as the loading increased. Ash et al.27 developed alumina/PMMA nanocomposites by incorporating alumina nanoparticles into methylmethacrylate using sonication and free radical polymerization. They also produced a comparison set using micron-sized alumina particles. They found that nanocomposites with alumina nanoparticles showed a 600% increase in strain-to-failure and developed a clear yield point under tension.

Heat treatment is a well-established method in the metal industry, often used to adjust and improve the properties of metals by relieving residual stresses that develop during manufacturing. Interestingly, researchers have proposed that similar heat treatment techniques can also be applied to polymers, not just metals28. Bhandari et al.29 applied heat treatment to a PLA-carbon fiber composite. They found that it significantly boosted the mechanical strength of the composite when compared to untreated PLA. In another study, Jayanth et al.30 tested the effect of annealing at different temperatures—specifically 0 °C, 90 °C, 100 °C, and 120 °C—on 3D-printed PLA parts. Their results showed that heating the material to 100 °C led to an 80% improvement in tensile strength and a 73% gain in heat resistance. Fouly et al.31 applied heat treatment to PLA-based green composites. They reported more than a 25% increase in compressive strength and a 25% reduction in wear volume. These findings highlight how effective heat treatment can be for polymers. However, the existing research on how this method affects the mechanical and wear behavior of polymers and their composites is still quite limited.

This study focuses on developing a polymer composite suitable for use in prosthetic devices, specifically, prosthetic feet designed for individuals with lower limb loss. Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) is combined with different weight percentages of aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) nanoparticles, 0%, 2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, and 10%, to create a range of composite samples. These samples are then subjected to heat treatment (annealing) at three different temperatures: 80 °C, 110 °C, and 140 °C, each for 15 min. To understand how heat treatment affects performance, both untreated and annealed composites are tested for their mechanical properties. The mechanical tests evaluate several key factors including hardness, relative toughness, elongation, yield strength, and elasticity. Additionally, ANSYS software is used to model a prosthetic foot under typical human weight-bearing conditions. This simulation helps estimate the stress levels experienced during use. Furthermore, this allows for a deeper understanding of how the material behaves under pressure. The study uses a well-rounded approach by combining hands-on testing with computer simulations to assess how adding Al₂O₃ affects PMMA. To complete the analysis, techniques like X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) are used to examine the structural and surface features of the nanocomposites.

Experimental work

Materials and fabrication process

In this study, a self-curing acrylic resin was used, sourced from Protechno Famadent S.L.U., a Spanish company. This specially formulated resin is commonly used for repairs and relining, making it suitable for biomedical applications. One of its main advantages is that it cures at room temperature, without requiring heat or additional curing agents. The resin comes in two parts: a white PMMA powder with a density of 1.18 g/cm³, which represents the primary polymer, and a liquid monomer, transparent, made of methyl methacrylate (MMA), with a density of 0.94 g/cm³. The aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) nanoparticles used in this study were sourced from US Research Nanomaterials, Inc. (USA). These nanoparticles were pure white in color and had a high purity level of over 99%. They were composed entirely of the alpha phase, with an average particle size of 80 nanometers and a density of 3.97 g/cm³. Figure 1(a) presents a TEM image of the Al₂O₃ nanoparticles. This TEM micrograph is reproduced from the supplier’s technical datasheet32 and is included only as a reference for the as-received primary particle size specified by the manufacturer (alpha phase, ≈ 80 nm, > 99% purity); it is not used for any quantitative analysis in this study. The morphology of the actual powder employed here was independently verified by SEM, as shown in Fig. 1(b). The SEM analysis confirmed that the nanoparticles are approximately equiaxed (near-spherical), and a tendency to form loose soft aggregates typical of high-surface-area ceramic nano-powders.

(a) TEM image of Al₂O₃ nanoparticles32, and (b) SEM of Al₂O₃ nanoparticles.

To prepare the nanocomposite samples, precise amounts of PMMA and Al₂O₃ nano-powders were weighed to achieve specific weight percentages of Al₂O₃, ranging from 0% to 10%, in 2.5% increments. Various researchers have explored the use of surface-modifying agents to improve the interaction between nanoparticles and polymer matrices33,34. One of the most commonly used agents for this purpose is (3-glycidoxypropyl)trimethoxy silane, which is known to enhance bonding between Al₂O₃ nanoparticles and the surrounding resin35–37. In this study, the same silane coupling agent was added to modify the surface of Al₂O₃ nanoparticles, promoting better adhesion and interface strength with the PMMA matrix.

The silane surface treatment was performed prior to compounding following an established hydrolysis–condensation route35–37. (3-Glycidoxypropyl)trimethoxysilane was added at 2 wt% relative to the mass of Al₂O₃ nanoparticles. The silane was first pre-hydrolyzed in a 95:5 (v/v) ethanol–deionized-water mixture, with the pH adjusted to approximately 4–5 using a few drops of acetic acid, and stirred for 30 min at room temperature to generate reactive silanol groups. The Al₂O₃ nanoparticles were then dispersed in this solution under magnetic stirring (1 h) followed by ultrasonication (30 min) to break up loose agglomerates and promote uniform adsorption of the silane onto the particle surfaces. The treated nanoparticles were separated, washed with ethanol to remove physiosorbed (un-grafted) silane, and dried in an oven at 110 °C for 2 h to complete condensation of the silanol groups with the surface hydroxyls of the alumina and to remove residual solvent. Successful grafting was inferred from the consistent and reproducible enhancement of interfacial load transfer reflected in the hardness and compressive data; the terminal epoxide group of the glycidoxy-silane is known to react with the surrounding methacrylate matrix during curing, yielding a covalently coupled, chemically compatible particle–matrix interphase34,35,37.

After the surface treatment, the MMA liquid monomer was mixed in, with solid-to-liquid weight ratios varying from 5:1. The total solid mass was calculated by combining the weights of both PMMA and Al₂O₃ powders. The mixture was manually stirred under controlled conditions 26 °C temperature and 35% relative humidity until it reached a thick, moldable consistency. Once ready, the viscous paste was poured into cylindrical molds measuring 25 mm in height and 8 mm in diameter. These molds were then compressed under a pressure of 15 MPa. The 15 MPa compaction pressure was applied uniaxially through a flat, polished steel plunger that matched the internal bore of the cylindrical mold, so that the load was distributed uniformly over the full 8 mm cross-section of the specimen. The pressure was held constant for the entire one-hour room-temperature curing period to suppress void formation, expel entrapped air, and ensure full densification before demolding. To confirm repeatability, five specimens were fabricated independently for every composition and heat-treatment condition under identical mixing, molding, and holding parameters; the low scatter observed in the hardness (six readings per specimen) and compression (five specimens per condition) measurements confirms the consistency and reproducibility of the compaction procedure. After one hour of compression, the solidified nanocomposite specimens were removed from the molds. All preparation steps followed the guidelines provided by the PMMA manufacturer. Figure 2 shows a step-by-step schematic of the process used to fabricate the PMMA/Al₂O₃ nanocomposite samples. The resulting PMMA/Al₂O₃ samples, which contained varying Al₂O₃ contents of 0%, 2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, and 10% by weight, were labeled as P0A, P2.5 A, P5A, P7.5 A, and P10A, respectively. The fabricated nanocomposite specimens were subsequently placed in a furnace for thermal treatment, where they underwent annealing at three distinct temperatures, 80 °C, 110 °C, and 140 °C, for 15 min. Upon completion of each heat treatment cycle, the specimens were allowed to cool gradually inside the furnace. The samples that underwent heat treatment at temperatures of 80 °C, 110 °C, and 140 °C were named P80, P110, and P140, respectively. To avoid any ambiguity between composition and thermal condition, a two-part nomenclature is adopted throughout this work. The composition is denoted by the prefix “PxA”, where x is the Al₂O₃ content in wt% (P0A, P2.5 A, P5A, P7.5 A, P10A), while the annealing condition is appended as a suffix indicating the treatment temperature: the un-annealed (as-prepared) state is written “P00”, and the annealed states are written “P80”, “P110”, and “P140” for 80 °C, 110 °C, and 140 °C, respectively. For example, P7.5 A/P110 designates the 7.5 wt% Al₂O₃ composite annealed at 110 °C. The temperature suffix never refers to composition, and the composition prefix never refers to temperature.

Characterization and testing

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out to investigate the crystalline structure and phase composition of the pure PMMA and Nanoalumina and the prepared PMMA/Al₂O₃ nanocomposites. XRD analysis was employed to identify the characteristic diffraction peaks of the alumina nanoparticles, to index the corundum (α-Al₂O₃) reflections, to assess their structural stability after incorporation into the polymer matrix, and to detect any possible phase transformations or new crystalline phases resulting from the processing conditions. Furthermore, XRD can reveal whether any new crystalline phase has formed; the retention of the original α-Al₂O₃ pattern, with no peak shifts and no additional reflections, indicates that the ceramic reinforcement is not consumed by a bulk solid-state reaction with the matrix. It should be noted that XRD probes long-range crystalline order and therefore confirms phase stability rather than excluding localized interfacial chemistry; the silane coupling agent is, by design, intended to form a thin interfacial layer through physicochemical bonding, which lies below the detection limit of laboratory XRD. In addition, XRD was used to verify the amorphous nature of the PMMA matrix and to evaluate the influence of increasing alumina content on the overall diffraction behavior of the composites.

The hardness of the prepared specimens was measured using a Shore D durometer in accordance with the ASTM D2240 standard38. Measurements were performed six times on different areas of each sample, and the mean hardness value was reported. For the evaluation of compressive mechanical properties, the test samples were fabricated according to the ISO 604 standard for plastics39, with dimensions of 16 mm in length and 8 mm in diameter. Compression testing was conducted using an Instron 5582 microtester at a constant strain rate of 2 mm/min. From the resulting stress–strain curves, key mechanical parameters such as the elastic modulus and maximum compressive strength were determined. Each test was repeated five times across five different specimens of each composite formulation, and the average values were documented for all mechanical assessments.

All reported mechanical values represent the arithmetic mean of these replicate measurements (six readings per specimen for hardness; five specimens per condition for the compressive properties). The associated dispersion is expressed as the standard deviation (SD), which is shown as error bars on the corresponding figures and reported alongside the mean values.

Finite element analysis

The maximum load a material can endure, its load-carrying capacity, can be assessed by analyzing the stresses generated during the application of external forces40. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the stress distribution throughout the loading process to determine the performance limits of the PMMA/Al₂O₃ nanocomposites under human body weight. To examine the potential of these nanocomposites for use in prosthetic legs, a finite element model (FEM) was developed. Constructing this model requires a thorough understanding of the anatomical structure of the human lower limb to ensure realistic simulation and accurate analysis of stress response.

The anatomical structure of the human lower leg comprises the calf muscles, skeletal elements such as the tibia, and supportive components including tendons, muscles, and the foot, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. The calf muscles play a key role in facilitating foot movement during locomotion and in absorbing the loads generated by such movements. The tibia, as the primary load-bearing bone, supports the body’s weight and sustains the reaction forces encountered during various activities. The structural design of the human foot also significantly contributes to balance and mobility, ensuring effective ground force distribution during standing, walking, or running. These biomechanical features must be taken into account when developing a lower limb prosthetic device. In a study by LaPre et al.41, a biomimetic model replicating the lower leg, comprising calf, bone, and foot-like elements, was developed to enhance stability and closely mimic human gait mechanics. A notable aspect of their foot-ankle design is its capacity to effectively dissipate ground reaction forces while maintaining anatomical resemblance to a natural foot, as shown in Fig. 3b. The prosthetic limb design consists of three main components: a socket, a pylon, and a foot. Dimensions were determined based on anthropometric data, in alignment with the referenced study. The socket interfaces with the residual limb (stump), while the pylon links the socket to the foot section. In the current study, an analogous model was developed (Fig. 3c), closely reflecting the configuration in Fig. 3b, and subsequently imported into ANSYS software for mechanical analysis.

Following the experimental determination of the mechanical properties of the PMMA/Al₂O₃ nanocomposite, these values were input into ANSYS for finite element analysis. It was assumed that the entire prosthetic leg structure was fabricated using the developed nanocomposite. The model was discretized using tetrahedral elements, resulting in a mesh composed of 2,240 elements and 4,687 nodes, as shown in Fig. 4. To apply realistic boundary conditions, the scenario of a person standing on one leg, specifically, the prosthetic leg, was considered. In this condition, the entire body weight is assumed to be concentrated at the interface between the residual limb and the prosthetic socket. Based on the study by LaPre et al.41, the subject was modeled as a male with a height of 1.88 m and a mass of 104 kg, representing the worst-case scenario. That same study also included lighter subjects, including a 73 kg male and a 68 kg female. For the boundary constraints, the bottom surface of the prosthetic foot was fixed in all directions to simulate ground contact. The material was modeled as linear-elastic, homogeneous, and isotropic, which is an appropriate idealization for PMMA-based glassy nanocomposites at the small strains relevant to the elastic stiffness of a load-bearing structure. For each formulation and heat-treatment condition, the experimentally measured Young’s modulus was assigned as the elastic input, a Poisson’s ratio of 0.37 (representative of PMMA) was used, and the rule-of-mixtures density of the corresponding composite was specified. A static structural analysis was performed under the applied body-weight load. The boundary conditions reproduce the single-limb support phase of gait, during which the contralateral foot is off the ground and essentially the entire body weight is transmitted through the prosthetic limb; this phase generates the largest sustained axial and bending loads on the device and therefore constitutes the governing worst-case static scenario for structural screening41. The assumption that the entire prosthesis is fabricated from the developed nanocomposite is adopted deliberately as a comparative, material-screening idealization: by keeping the geometry and loading identical across all simulations and varying only the material properties, the relative structural benefit attributable solely to the Al₂O₃ reinforcement and annealing can be isolated and ranked. In a practical device, the nanocomposite would most plausibly be used for the structurally demanding pylon/keel region, while the socket and cosmetic components could employ complementary materials; the homogeneous-material model therefore provides a conservative, application-agnostic basis for down-selecting the optimal material rather than a final multi-material design.

Mesh independence study

To ensure that the reported results are independent of the spatial discretization, a mesh-convergence study was performed prior to the parametric simulations. The neat-PMMA model under the 104 kg single-limb load was solved using four progressively refined tetrahedral meshes, and the maximum equivalent (von Mises) stress and maximum total deformation were monitored. As summarized in Table 1, refining the mesh beyond the selected density (2563 elements/5305 nodes) changed the maximum equivalent stress by less than 1%. The selected mesh therefore lies in the converged regime, providing a numerically accurate solution at a reasonable computational cost; this mesh density was subsequently retained for all formulations and heat-treatment conditions to guarantee a consistent basis for comparison.

Results and discussions

Figure 5 compiles the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the neat constituents and the PMMA/Al₂O₃ nanocomposites (P2.5 A–P10A). The as-received alumina powder exhibits a set of sharp reflections at 2θ ≈ 25.6°, 35.1°, 37.8°, 43.4°, 52.6°, 57.5°, 66.5°, and 68.2°, which are indexed to the (012), (104), (110), (113), (024), (116), (214), and (300) planes of rhombohedral corundum-type α-Al₂O₃ (space group R3̄c; JCPDS card No. 46–1212), confirming the single-phase nature of the reinforcement. In contrast, neat PMMA (P0A) shows only a broad diffuse halo centered near 2θ ≈ 13–15°, confirming its amorphous nature under the present processing conditions. The composite diffractograms reproduce the α-Al₂O₃ peak positions with shifts smaller than the instrumental angular resolution (Δ2θ < ±0.05°), while no additional peaks associated with transition-alumina polymorphs (γ-, δ-, or θ-Al₂O₃), boehmite, or other crystalline reaction products are observed. As the alumina loading increases from P2.5 A to P10A, the alumina reflections grow in relative intensity against the polymer background, consistent with the larger crystalline weight fraction and the reduced dominance of the amorphous matrix signal. The invariance of the peak positions and the absence of new reflections indicate that the ceramic phase remains structurally stable after compounding and that no detectable crystallization of the PMMA matrix is induced. It should be emphasized, however, that XRD probes long-range crystallographic order; the preservation of peak positions therefore establishes phase stability of the corundum lattice rather than the complete absence of localized interfacial chemistry.

Figure 6 shows that the Shore D hardness increased with both Al₂O₃ loading and annealing temperature up to 110 °C, followed by a small yet consistent decrease at 140 °C while remaining above the as-prepared state. For neat PMMA (P0A), hardness rose from 87.4 to 88.6 at 110 °C (+ 1.37%), then declined to 88.3 at 140 °C (− 0.34% from the 110 °C maximum). The same trend occurred at all filler levels: P2.5 A (89.6 → 91.1 → 90.6, + 1.67% then − 0.55%), P5A (91.5 → 92.9 → 92.0, + 1.53% then − 0.97%), P7.5 A (93.4 → 94.8 → 94.2, + 1.50% then − 0.63%), and P10A (95.1 → 96.2 → 95.3, + 1.16% then − 0.94%). At fixed temperature, hardness increased monotonically with filler; for example, relative to neat PMMA, P10A was higher by + 7.7 (as-prepared), + 7.4 (80 °C), + 7.6 (110 °C), and + 7.0 (140 °C). The maximum hardness of 96.2 was obtained for P10A at 110 °C (≈ + 10.1% vs. neat PMMA as-prepared). The rise in hardness up to 110 °C is attributed to sub-/near-Tg physical aging that reduces free volume and enhances polymer–particle load transfer, whereas exposure to 140 °C (> Tg) partially resets this aging and may relax interfacial constraints, leading to a modest but systematic reduction from the 110 °C optimum. Overall, the data indicate effective rigid-filler reinforcement coupled with a temperature-dependent balance between densification (≤ 110 °C) and rejuvenation (> Tg), yielding the highest indentation resistance near the glass-transition region. Furthermore, the improvement in hardness may arises from strong interfacial adhesion between the PMMA matrix and the alumina nanoparticles, which enables efficient stress transfer during indentation. Because hardness is sensitive to matrix–filler interactions, the observed increase indicates that the alumina is well dispersed throughout the polymer. Raising the nanoparticle content from 2.5 to 10 wt% shortens the interparticle spacing and expands the effective bonding area, producing a more integrated network that resists local plastic deformation.

Figure 7 illustrates the change in the surface occurred due to the heat treatment process for P10A sample. SEM examination of the P10A composite reveals a clear heat-treatment dependence that mirrors the hardness results. The as-prepared surface shows a relatively rough relief with scattered pits and exposed particle edges, indicative of incomplete local wetting and residual stresses, and is associated with a hardness of 95.1 Shore D. Annealing at 80 °C reduces the population of micro voids and smooths the matrix, consistent with a modest increase to 95.5. The micrograph at 110 °C near the glass-transition region of PMMA exhibits the most compact and continuous morphology, with nanoparticles well embedded and minimal pull-out features, and delivers the maximum hardness of 96.2. After treatment at 140 °C, the surface develops faint flow lines and isolated shallow cavities along the particle–matrix boundary, consistent with partial rejuvenation above Tg and slight interfacial relaxation; accordingly, the hardness decreases marginally to 95.3, yet remains higher than the as-prepared value. Overall, the SEM evidence of progressive densification and a strengthened interphase up to 110 °C, followed by minor relaxation at 140 °C, provides a microstructural rationale for the observed temperature hardness trend.

Figures 8 and 9 illustrate the mechanical properties based on compression test. Compression testing reveals that both the Young’s modulus and the yield compressive strength increase with alumina loading up to ~ 7.5 wt% and then decline slightly at 10 wt%. At a fixed composition, properties improve with annealing to 110 °C and decrease modestly at 140 °C, remaining above the as-prepared values. The best overall performance is obtained for P7.5 A at 110 °C, where the modulus reaches 3.20 GPa and the strength 74.2 MPa, representing gains of + 71.1% and + 22.2%, respectively, relative to neat PMMA (1.87 GPa, 60.7 MPa). Relative to neat PMMA at the same temperature (110 °C), P7.5 A still shows substantial enhancements (+ 68.9% E, + 6.9% σy). The improvement up to 110 °C is attributed to near-Tg physical aging that reduces free volume and strengthens the particle–matrix interphase, thereby improving stress transfer. The slight decrease at 140 °C is consistent with partial rejuvenation above Tg and limited interfacial relaxation on cooling. The drop in strength at 10 wt% (e.g., 66.5 MPa at 110 °C) despite the higher filler content is attributed to the onset of nanoparticle agglomeration and micro-defects, which reduce the effective bonded area and introduce local stress concentrators. Collectively, these results indicate an optimal balance between dispersion and interphase quality at ~ 7.5 wt% Al₂O₃ with annealing near 110 °C.

Because alumina is substantially denser than PMMA (3.97 versus ≈ 1.18 g/cm³), the absolute mechanical gains must be weighed against the accompanying increase in mass, which is particularly relevant for a wearable prosthetic. The composite density estimated from the rule of mixtures rises only modestly with filler content, from 1.18 g/cm³ for neat PMMA to approximately 1.20, 1.22, 1.25, and 1.27 g/cm³ for P2.5 A, P5A, P7.5 A, and P10A, respectively—an increase of only about 5.6% at 7.5 wt%. When the elastic modulus and strength are normalized by density, the optimal P7.5 A/110°C formulation still exhibits a pronounced improvement in specific modulus (≈ 2.57 versus 1.59 GPa·cm³·g⁻¹ for neat PMMA, + 62%) and in specific compressive strength (≈ + 16%). The reinforcement therefore delivers a genuine, weight-efficient enhancement rather than a benefit obtained merely by adding dense material, confirming that the 7.5 wt% formulation is optimal on a mass-normalized as well as on an absolute basis.

It is worth clarifying the criterion used to identify the optimal formulation, since the Shore D hardness and the bulk mechanical properties peak at different filler contents. Although hardness increases monotonically with alumina loading and is therefore maximized at 10 wt% (P10A), surface hardness reflects only the near-surface resistance to localized indentation and is not the property that governs the structural integrity of a load-bearing prosthetic component. The relevant design drivers are the bulk elastic modulus, and the compressive yield strength, all of which peak at 7.5 wt% before declining at 10 wt% owing to nanoparticle agglomeration. Because the finite element analysis (Sect. 3) confirms that only the P7.5 A/110°C formulation simultaneously maximizes the generated stresses, this formulation—rather than the hardest one—is selected as the optimal material.

The non-monotonic effect of annealing temperature is fully consistent with the physical-aging behavior of glassy polymers. PMMA has a glass-transition temperature (Tg) of approximately 105 °C; annealing at or just below Tg (80–110 °C) drives the non-equilibrium glass toward a denser, lower-enthalpy configuration through free-volume relaxation, a well-documented physical-aging process that increases stiffness, yield stress, and indentation resistance42. The accompanying contraction of free volume tightens the matrix around the silane-grafted nanoparticles, enhancing interfacial load transfer. Conversely, annealing above Tg at 140 °C partially rejuvenates the glass—erasing prior aging and re-expanding free volume on cooling—and promotes localized interfacial relaxation, which together account for the small reductions in hardness and mechanical properties observed at the highest temperature, although the values remain above the as-prepared state.

Figure 12 presents the maximum equivalent von Mises stress for all composite formulations and heat treatment conditions. A consistent and progressive decrease in equivalent stress is observed as Al₂O₃ content increases from 0 to 7.5 wt%, followed by a slight increase at 10 wt%. For the as-prepared condition (P00), the maximum equivalent stress decreases from 75.0 MPa for neat PMMA (P0A) to 72.4 MPa for P7.5 A, a reduction of 4.0%, before rising marginally to 73.0 MPa for P10A. This non-monotonic behavior at 10 wt% is fully consistent with the nanoparticle agglomeration evidenced by the cross-sectional SEM in Fig. 10, which introduced local stress concentrators and reduced the effective bonded area, limiting the mechanical gains at the highest filler loading. The effect of heat treatment on the equivalent stress mirrors the experimental mechanical trends: annealing at 110 °C produces the lowest equivalent stress at every composition, the 80 °C treatment provides an intermediate improvement, and the 140 °C treatment leads to a slight recovery toward higher stresses relative to the 110 °C optimum, yet remains below the as-prepared values. The absolute minimum equivalent stress of 72.0 MPa is achieved for P7.5 A/P110, precisely the composition and temperature that also delivered the highest Young’s modulus (3.20 GPa) and compressive yield strength (74.2 MPa) in the experimental tests. This is the only condition for which the experimentally measured yield strength (74.2 MPa) exceeds the maximum equivalent stress (72.0 MPa), yielding a structural safety factor above unity (SF ≈ 1.03). All other compositions produce equivalent stresses that exceed their respective yield strengths, underscoring the critical synergy between nanoparticle reinforcement and annealing.

Figure 13 presents the total structural deformation of the prosthetic foot for all composites and heat treatment conditions. In contrast to the equivalent stress, which varies only modestly across formulations (∼4%), the total deformation is highly sensitive to both Al₂O₃ content and annealing temperature, making it the more discriminating performance indicator in this study. For neat as-prepared PMMA (P0A/P00), the maximum total deformation has been recorded under the 104 kg body weight. Incorporating Al₂O₃ nanoparticles progressively reduces this value: P2.5 A/P00 by 9.8%, P5A/P00 by 24.4%, and P7.5 A/P00 by 32.9%, before rising for P10A/P00, again reflecting the agglomeration-induced stiffness penalty at 10 wt%. The influence of annealing on deformation is substantially more pronounced than its effect on the equivalent stress. For P7.5 A specifically, total deformation decreases compared with the as-prepared state after 80 °C treatment, and after 110 °C annealing, and rises slightly after 140 °C treatment. The absolute minimum total deformation is achieved for P7.5 A/P110, representing a 40.2% reduction compared with neat as-prepared PMMA. This dramatic structural improvement is in direct quantitative agreement with the 71.1% increase in Young’s modulus measured experimentally for the same formulation, confirming a strong and consistent correlation between laboratory-measured stiffness and FEM-predicted structural compliance.

The combined interpretation of Figs. 12 and 13 reveals a fundamental distinction in how the two structural outputs respond to material improvement. The equivalent stress field is governed primarily by the applied load magnitude and the structural geometry; since neither change between simulations, the stress distribution is only weakly sensitive to variations in elastic modulus, and the small reductions observed reflect a minor load redistribution as the structure stiffens. In contrast, total deformation is directly and strongly controlled by stiffness through the elastic constitutive law, so the large gains in Young’s modulus achieved experimentally translate directly into large reductions in structural deflection. This explains why deformation is the more informative FEM performance indicator in this study, and why the experimental stiffness results and the FEM deformation results are in such close quantitative correspondence. From a clinical and biomechanical perspective, the 40.2% reduction in prosthetic foot deformation for P7.5 A/110°C implies less energy lost to elastic deflection per gait cycle, better maintenance of foot geometry under load, and a more stable and energy-efficient prosthesis for the user. The convergence of all performance metrics — highest modulus, highest yield strength, lowest equivalent stress, only safety factor above unity, and minimum total deformation — at P7.5 A/110°C establishes this formulation as the unambiguously optimal material combination for lower limb prosthetic foot construction from the PMMA/Al₂O₃ system investigated in this work.

To place these results in the context of prosthetic-device requirements, it is useful to compare them with the relevant structural standards and with incumbent materials. Lower-limb prosthetic components are qualified against ISO 10,328 and ISO 22,675, which prescribe static proof loading and cyclic fatigue loading representative of level walking. At the high-performance end, energy-storage-and-return feet rely on carbon-fiber-reinforced polymers with effective moduli of roughly 70–150 GPa, whereas lower-cost, lower-activity devices commonly use thermoplastics such as polypropylene (≈ 1.0–1.5 GPa). The optimized PMMA/Al₂O₃ nanocomposite (E ≈ 3.2 GPa) more than doubles the stiffness of neat PMMA and exceeds that of polypropylene, while retaining the favorable processability, low cost, and biocompatibility of acrylics. With a finite element under the 104 kg worst-case single-limb load, the present material is best positioned for low-to-moderate-activity (K1–K2) users rather than for the high-impact, dynamic-response applications currently dominated by carbon fiber.

The constituent materials are intrinsically well suited to the biomedical environment. PMMA has a long clinical history as a bone-cement, denture-base, and intraocular-lens material, while α-Al₂O₃ is a bioinert ceramic widely used in orthopedic and dental implants; neither constituent raises cytotoxicity concerns, and the absence of new crystalline phases in the XRD patterns indicates chemical stability of the system. Because the device is intended for service within a cosmetic cover rather than in direct, permanent tissue contact, and because acrylic prostheses exhibit good resistance to moisture and corrosion, the long-term environmental stability of the nanocomposite is expected to be governed primarily by mechanical fatigue rather than by chemical degradation. Dedicated cytocompatibility and accelerated-ageing tests nonetheless remain advisable prior to clinical deployment. It should also be noted that polymeric composites exposed to service environments can undergo measurable changes in their mechanical, thermal, and viscoelastic response under accelerated weathering, so environmental-durability testing of the optimized formulation is recommended alongside the mechanical evaluation43.

The novelty of this work lies in the specific combination of three elements that, to the best of our knowledge, have not previously been integrated for prosthetic-foot design: a low-loading α-Al₂O₃ nanoparticle reinforcement of a self-curing PMMA matrix, an optimization of post-fabrication annealing in the immediate vicinity of the glass transition, and a coupled experimental–finite element evaluation referenced directly to a prosthetic-foot load case. While the individual aspects, ceramic-reinforced acrylics, polymer annealing, and finite element analysis of prostheses, have each been reported separately6,7,44–48, the present study uniquely links a mass-efficient, near-Tg-optimized nanocomposite to a quantitative structural safety assessment of a prosthetic limb, thereby providing a transferable material-screening methodology rather than a single isolated formulation result. In relation to the most recent literature, comparable studies have advanced polymer- and ceramic-reinforced composites, additive-manufacturing process optimization, and finite element analysis of biomedical and structural components44,49–57; the present work is distinguished from these by jointly targeting a mass-efficient, near-Tg-optimised PMMA/Al₂O₃ system and a quantitative prosthetic-foot structural-safety assessment within a single experimental–numerical workflow.

Finally, the present analysis is deliberately bounded to quasi-static behavior and a worst-case static load case, and several limitations define the scope for future work. Flexural and tensile responses, tribological performance, and most importantly is the fatigue life under the cyclic, multi-axial loading of repeated gait (in which dynamic ground-reaction forces can reach 1.2–1.5 times body weight per step over millions of cycles) were not measured and should be evaluated against the cyclic protocols of ISO 22,675. Likewise, the finite element model employed a static, linear-elastic, single-material idealization; transient dynamic-gait simulation, viscoelastic material modelling, and experimental strain-gauge validation of the predicted deformation field are recommended to confirm the in-service durability of the optimized formulation before clinical translation. Finally, given the relatively large formulation and processing space (filler content, silane chemistry, and annealing schedule), data-driven and machine-learning approaches offer an efficient route to predict and optimize the mechanical properties of such thermoplastic nanocomposites and to guide future experimental design58.

Conclusions

This study examined the mechanical behavior and structural suitability of PMMA/Al₂O₃ nanocomposites for lower limb prosthetic feet by combining experimental characterization with finite element analysis. XRD confirmed the phase stability of the α-Al₂O₃ reinforcement and the absence of new crystalline reaction products. Hardness, stiffness, and compressive strength all increased with Al₂O₃ content up to 7.5 wt% and with annealing up to 110 °C, while agglomeration at 10 wt% and polymer rejuvenation above 140 °C caused modest property reductions. The optimal formulation, P7.5 A annealed at 110 °C, achieved a Young’s modulus of 3.20 GPa (+ 71.1%) and a compressive yield strength of 74.2 MPa (+ 22.2%) relative to neat PMMA. FEM of the prosthetic foot under a 104 kg body weight showed that this formulation reduced total structural deformation by 40.2%, confirming adequate structural safety. A strong correlation between the experimentally measured Young’s modulus and FEM-predicted deformation validates the combined approach. Future work should address tribological performance and fatigue life under cyclic gait loading.
