Large-scale intermittent larviciding intervention and associations with key malaria epidemiological parameters in Tanga Region, Tanzania
Authors: Denis Kailembo, Tegemeo Gavana, Elizabeth Kasagama, Jubilate Bernard, Fabrizio Molteni, Noela Kisoka, Best Yoram, Stella Kajange, Samwel Lazaro, Charles Dismas, Amanda Ross, Prosper Chaki, Christian Lengeler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains one of the major public health problems both globally and in Tanzania. In addition to existing malaria control interventions, the country plans to introduce larviciding in its efforts towards malaria control and elimination. To guide a possible national upscaling of the intervention, a pilot larviciding study using biolarvicides produced in-country was implemented and evaluated to generate a body of evidence on operations and the impact of the intervention. The current manuscript reports on the association between larviciding and three major malaria epidemiological parameters.MethodsLarviciding was conducted in three councils in Tanga Region in the North-East of the country and covered a population of over 1.1 million. The councils represented different malaria risk strata: high, moderate, and low. Larviciding was conducted between June 2022 and April 2024 through a community-based approach using existing local government structures. Larviciding was applied intermittently in the intervention councils to all identified breeding habitats for three rounds per year, based on the local rainfall seasons. Both Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis and Bacillus sphaericus were used and each round comprised of 8 consecutive weeks of biolarvicide application. Epidemiological data, including malaria incidence, laboratory malaria test positivity rate and antenatal malaria test positivity rate, were collected from routine health facility data. Each intervention council was paired with multiple control councils based on malaria risk and rainfall patterns, allowing a controlled before-after study design. The association between larviciding and malaria incidence was assessed using negative binomial regression while logistic regression was used for the other epidemiological outcomes.ResultsThe intervention did not have a significant association with the incidence of reported clinical malaria among all age-groups. The estimated incidence rate ratio (IRR) was 1.16 (95% CI 0.98; 1.37) in the high, 0.86 (95% CI 0.72; 1.02) in the moderate, and 0.96 (95% CI 0.76; 1.22) in the low stratum. Similar results were observed across other epidemiological parameters. There was a slight tendency for estimates in the moderate and low strata to be in the direction of a reduction.ConclusionUnder the current design of intermittent larviciding application, there was no strong evidence of an association between larviciding and malaria epidemiological indicators. More studies considering continuous year-round implementation and randomizing a large number of clusters could generate further evidence on the contribution of larviciding to malaria control in similar transmission settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05548-7.

Background

Malaria remains one of the major global public health problems. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that there were 263 million malaria cases worldwide in 2023 [1], of which around 246 million (94%) were from the WHO African Region. In Tanzania, malaria is a major cause of morbidity and mortality, with over three million clinical cases reported through routine health facilities in 2024 [2].

Insecticide-treated mosquito nets (ITNs), and indoor residual spraying (IRS) have been shown to have a major impact in reducing the burden of malaria [3, 4]. Following large advances achieved by scaling up interventions between 2000 and 2015, reductions in the malaria burden have stagnated over the past decade [1]. In Tanzania, the malaria prevalence among children under 5 years of age remained relatively unchanged between 2017 and 2022 at 7.3% [5] and 8.1% [6], respectively. Emerging resistance to insecticides used in ITNs and IRS has been documented [7–14], as well as concerns about the durability of standard ITNs [15–18]. Moreover, changes in vector behavior from indoor to outdoor biting and resting have also been reported [19–22]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for additional preventive measures.

Larval Source Management (LSM) is one of the supplementary malaria control measures recommended by the WHO. It is defined as the management of potential mosquito breeding habitats to prevent the maturation of mosquito larvae to the adult stage [23]. LSM can be implemented through environmental management (i.e. habitat modification and habitat manipulation), biological control, and larviciding. To date, LSM implementation in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has been primarily through larviciding. The WHO does not currently recommend the implementation of larviciding in all areas, but only where breeding habitats are few, fixed and findable [23].

The impact of LSM on malaria control through environmental management and larviciding, conducted either alone or in combination, was assessed in a 2013 Cochrane Systematic Review, whereby the findings showed mixed results [24]. A more recent Cochrane Systematic Review, in 2019 which included four studies assessed only the impact of larviciding on malaria control and also found mixed results [25]. While one study in Sri-Lanka used insect growth regulators, the studies in Tanzania, Kenya and The Gambia used microbial larvicides [26–29]. The study in Sri-Lanka was the only cluster randomized controlled trial (cRCT) and larviciding was associated with significant reductions in adult mosquito populations, malaria prevalence and incidence. Pooled results from the Tanzanian and Kenyan studies using controlled before-after (CBA) designs found that larviciding was associated with a lower malaria prevalence [risk ratio 0.79, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.71 to 0.89]. In addition, the Kenyan study found a borderline association between larviciding and a reduction in the incidence of malaria cases (risk ratio 0.62, CI 0.38 to 1.01). The Gambian study also used a CBA design but it was evaluated separately because larviciding was only applied in breeding habitats with large surface areas (> 1 km2). There was no evidence of an association with the incidence of malaria cases (risk ratio 1.58, 95% CI 0.94 to 2.65) or parasite prevalence (risk ratio 1.15, 95% CI 0.40 to 3.20). Both reviews showed mixed results and called for further research to generate high-quality evidence [24, 25]. There is increasing global interest to elevate LSM and larviciding as a key preventive strategy and to generate further evidence on its contribution towards malaria control and elimination [30, 31].

In the early 2000 s, a large LSM programme was implemented in urban areas in Dar es Salaam Region, Tanzania. The results suggested larviciding was associated with a reduction in the human biting rate and the entomological inoculation rate of Anopheles gambiae sensu lato (s.l.) mosquitoes, i.e. a 31% reduction (95% CI 22% to 38%) [32], and lower odds of malaria infection among all individuals (odds ratio 0.79, 95% CI 0.66; 0.93) [27], and among children under 5 years (odds ratio 0.28, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.80) [33].

Since then, few relatively small-scale larviciding operations have taken place in the country and the impact of larviciding has mainly been assessed through entomological parameters. Larviciding was found to be associated with reductions in larvae abundance [34–36], and densities of adult mosquitoes [34, 37].

In 2014, the government of Tanzania, with the technical support from the Cuban government, built a factory in Kibaha, Pwani Region, to produce biological larvicides: Tanzania Biotech Products Limited (TBPL) [38]. The factory produces two products: Bacillus thuringiensis var israelensis (Bti, Bactivec™) and Bacillus sphaericus (Bs, Griselesf™), the most widely used biolarvicides [23, 33, 39]. Tanzania is now planning to implement larviciding in all councils across the country. To guide the scale-up of this programme, a pilot larviciding project was implemented and evaluated in three councils in Tanga Region. The aim of the pilot study was to generate information about the larviciding intervention on operational feasibility, community acceptability, costing data, and the association with entomological and epidemiological outcomes.

This paper, reports on the association between larviciding and three epidemiological outcomes derived from routine health facility data: malaria incidence, laboratory-reported malaria test positivity rate among patients with fever, and malaria positivity rate among pregnant women during their first antenatal clinic visit.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Tanga Region, one of the 26 regions in the country. Tanga is a coastal region located in the north-east part of Tanzania. Tanga has 11 administrative units, referred as councils, representing both urban and rural settings, and has substantial heterogeneity in malaria risk across its councils [40, 41]. In Tanzania and within Tanga Region, there has been a shift from An. gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.) and Anopheles funestus as the dominant malaria vector species to Anopheles arabiensis, which predominantly feeds and rests outdoors (Gavana et al. pers. commun.) [21, 42].

Study design

Because of the need to act as a pilot for the planned large-scale national upscaling, the intervention was implemented entirely through the existing government system. As a result, a cRCT was not feasible since the intervention was implemented at the level of the council. Instead, the evaluation was a quasi-experimental study, with three intervention councils and a CBA design to assess the association between larviciding and key malaria epidemiological parameters stratified by malaria risk strata. No epidemiological data were collected specifically in the frame of this pilot study, since the health system data are available. The evaluation used routine data collected between January 2020 and April 2024. The intervention period was between June 2022 and April 2024, whereas data collected before the start of the intervention, between January 2020 and May 2022, referenced the baseline data highlighting the pre-intervention trends.

Intervention councils

Selection of the larviciding implementation councils was based on a programmatic decision after consultative meetings between the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP), the President’s Office-Regional Administration and Local Government (PO-RALG), two major research institutions (the National Institute of Medical Research-NIMR and the Ifakara Health Institute-IHI) and the Swiss Tropical and Public Health Institute (Swiss TPH)—Towards Elimination of Malaria in Tanzania (TEMT) project.

Based on the available resources, three councils from Tanga Region were selected as intervention councils: Handeni District Council (DC), Tanga City Council (CC), and Lushoto DC. The intervention councils represented a high, a moderate and a low malaria risk stratum respectively as defined in the National Malaria Strategic Plan (NMSP) 2021–2025 [40, 41]. In both Handeni DC and Tanga CC, the intervention was conducted across all wards while in Lushoto DC, a programmatic decision was made to conduct the intervention in 22 out of 33 wards, as the 11 remaining wards were at a high altitude and malaria transmission was extremely low.

Operationalization of the larviciding intervention

Implementation of larviciding followed a community-based approach, with coordination and supervision conducted through the existing local government structure (Minja et al. unpublished manuscript) [43]. Community volunteers, known as Community-Owned Resource Persons (CORPs) were selected from within their communities. They were responsible for the identification of mosquito breeding habitats and the application of the larvicide. Two CORPs were selected per village. Village Executive Officers (VEOs) directly supervised CORPs, and they were in turn supervised by Ward Executive Officers (WEOs). The Regional and Council Health Management Teams (R/CHMTs) supervised the intervention in their respective areas. The CHMT was led by the District Medical Officer (DMO), District Malaria Focal Person (DMFP) and the District Vector Control Officer (DVCO) while the RHMT was led by the Regional Medical Officer (RMO), the Regional Malaria Focal Person (RMFP) and the Regional Health Officer (RHO). In order to save on costs and to avoid the risk of washout during periods of heavy rains, larviciding was not applied continuously through-out the year, but intermittently, based on rainfall patterns. Tanga Region has a bimodal rainfall pattern, with heavy rains in April–May and shallow rains in November–December. Thus, the larvicide was applied in three rounds: before the heavy rains; after the heavy rains; and before the shallow rains (Fig. 2). In each round, the larvicide was applied weekly for 8 consecutive weeks. Hence, in one calendar year, the larvicide was applied for three rounds, roughly in 46% (24/52) of the weeks. Application of the larvicide was conducted for six rounds across 2 years from June 2022 to April 2024. Biolarvicides

Two biolarvicide products were used: Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis (Bti, Bactivec™) and Bacillus sphaericus (Bs, Griselesf™). The products are in liquid form and are produced by TBPL, a subsidiary company of the National Development Corporation. Both products are selective for mosquito larvae and have been shown to be safe to humans, other organisms and the environment [23, 39, 44]. Both biolarvicides have a short residual efficacy, lasting between 1 and 2 weeks and thus require frequent reapplication [23, 29, 33, 45].

Bactivec™ was applied in the first 6 weeks, and Griselesf™ was applied in the last 2 weeks during each round since it has a longer residual effect than Bactivec™ [46]. Additionally, the larvicides were rotated to mitigate against any potential development of resistance. Larviciding application was conducted by CORPs using backpack sprayers, following the instructions of the manufacturer and standard operating procedures on application of larvicides. Local government officers and the national team offered on-site support during supervisory visits to ensure that identified breeding habitats were applied with larvicides and that all activities were carried out in accordance with the standard operating procedures.

Study variables and data collection

Data for all the study variables were collected monthly from January 2020 to April 2024 from different sources.

Outcome variables

Three key epidemiological indicators were used in the evaluation:i.Malaria incidence, as reported in health facilities; subcategorized as all ages, among children under 5 years, and individuals aged 5 years and olderii.Laboratory-reported malaria test positivity rate among patients with fever (Fever-TPR)iii.Malaria positivity rate among pregnant women during their first ante-natal clinic visit (ANC-PR)

Malaria incidence was defined as the number of confirmed malaria positive cases reported per month by the health facilities and confirmed either through malaria Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT) or microscopy per 1000 population at risk. The population in the council was given by the 2022 Tanzania National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) census report [47]. Fever-TPR was defined as the proportion of confirmed malaria positive cases per month out of all malaria tests performed in the routine laboratories on patients with fever. ANC-PR was defined as the proportion of confirmed malaria positive cases per month among pregnant women screened routinely for malaria during their first ANC visit.

The data were collected monthly from health facilities reporting to the National District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2). All health facilities with at least an 80% reporting rate across the study period were included [40] for uniformity of the data before and after the start of the intervention. All health facility data were downloaded from DHIS2 across the intervention and control councils listed in Table 1. The total dataset had 1,334 health facilities. There were 289 health facilities with no data, mostly from the private sector. After removing these, as well as 453 health facilities with less than an 80% reporting rate across the study duration, and two health facilities in Lushoto DC from non-intervention wards, the dataset remained with 590 health facilities. Thereafter, the health facility data were aggregated at the council level.

Covariates

Data on the out-patient department reporting rate were obtained from DHIS2. The malaria epidemiological risk strata were defined as in the NMSP 2021–2025 [40, 41]. Rainfall and temperature data were obtained from the Tanzania Meteorological Authority (TMA), while data on the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) were obtained from Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) [48]. ITN access was derived from Koenker et al. [49] where the researchers used a stock-and-flow model to estimate surviving nets in each council, based on nets distributed through all available channels and assuming a median life span of the nets of 2.15 years [18]. The total of surviving nets was divided by population estimates based on NBS data to calculate available nets per capita per annum and quarter.

Larviciding programmatic data

CORPs collected programmatic data on key intervention parameters during each week of larvicide application across all six rounds. Data were collected at the village level and aggregated at the council level. Data were collected on the following parameters: number and size of breeding habitats, number of habitats with Anopheles and Culex larvae, number of Anopheles larvae, Culex larvae and pupae, number and proportion of breeding habitats treated with larvicide, and amount of larvicide (in litres) used. A breeding habitat was defined as any standing water lasting more than 1 week. Standard dippers were used to collect mosquito larvae and pupa and the number of dips taken per habitat was based on the size of the habitat. A companion manuscript provides the full details of how the programmatic data were collected and presents the findings (Minja et al. unpublished observations).

Data on programmatic treatment coverage were based on the routine CORPs records which were routinely checked by the national team during supportive supervision visits. Programmatic data were used to inform operational decisions before and during every round of larvicide application.

Data analysis

The epidemiological indicators across both intervention and control councils were visualized over time using plots, marking the timing of the intervention. The assessment was conducted in each risk stratum separately. The association between the intervention and each of the epidemiological outcomes was estimated stratifying by risk stratum. Two sets of controls were used: firstly, the analysis was conducted by pairing each intervention council to the one control council that was also used for entomological evaluation, i.e. Handeni DC vs Muheza DC, Tanga CC vs Pangani DC, and Lushoto DC vs Bumbuli DC. This allowed later to assess both the epidemiological and entomological evaluations using the same locations. The second set of analysis was conducted between each of the three intervention councils using as many control councils that met the matching criteria, as listed in Table 1.

Malaria case incidence was modeled as counts of cases per council per month using negative binomial regression, with an offset for population with a log transformation. Fever-TPR and ANC-PR were modelled using logistic regression. The covariates rainfall, temperature, NDVI, and ITN access were lagged by 1 month, because the values of the covariates for the previous month affected the epidemiological outcomes in the current month. Time was modelled as a continuous variable. An autoregressive term of the log of the outcome at a lag of 1 month was added to account for the time series nature of the data. Despite the use of multiple covariates, there could be residual confounding due to the small number of intervention and control clusters and the non-random nature of the intervention allocation. Therefore, the estimate is referred to as an association. All analyses were conducted in R using R Studio version 4.4.1.

Ethical approval

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from both the Ifakara Health Institute Institutional Review Board (IHI/IRB/No: 32-2022) and the Ethics Review Board of the National Institute of Medical Research (NIMR/HQ/R.8a/Vol.IX/4158). In this study, individual level data was not collected since data sources included anonymized routine health facility data, and secondary data sources.

Results

Programmatic results

The population distribution across the three intervention councils was fairly similar as per 2022 census [47] (Table 2). Handeni DC has the largest surface area (in km2) followed by Lushoto DC and then Tanga CC. Handeni DC has 91 villages (21 wards), Tanga CC has 181 streets/villages (27 wards) and Lushoto DC has 127 villages (33 wards), and larviciding was implemented in all villages/streets in Handeni DC and Tanga CC. In Lushoto DC, larviciding was implemented in 88 out of 127 villages, covering approximately 89% (3129 km2) of the surface area and 75% (261,971) of the total population. Across the six rounds in all three councils, 63,900 L of larvicide were used: Bactivec™/Bti—46,160 L (72%) and Griselesf™/Bs—17,740 L (28%); at a total cost of United States dollars (USD) 350,045 or Tanzanian Shillings (TZS) 843,480,000 (Diarra et al. accepted manuscript). Handeni DC used the highest proportion of the total larvicide at 53%, while Lushoto DC and Tanga CC used 28% and 19%, respectively.

Epidemiological results In Handeni DC (high risk stratum, Fig. 3a), the incidence of confirmed malaria cases in health facilities per 1000 population among all age groups was higher in the intervention compared to control councils in both the pre- and post-intervention phases. No obvious patterns emerged except: (1) that the seasonality of the incidence rates is marked, and (2) that there is a clear downward secular trend over the period 2020–2024 for all councils. There was variation in the patterns over time among the control councils. The trends of incidence rates stratified by age-groups (children under 5 years and individuals aged 5 years and older) showed a similar pattern (Additional file 1, Figs S1 and S2). In Tanga CC (moderate risk, Fig. 3b) and Lushoto DC (low risk, Fig. 3c), the intervention councils had lower malaria incidence rates (among all age groups) compared to most control councils both in the pre and post intervention phases. The trends were also similar to the other epidemiological parameters: incidence rates among children under 5 years and individuals aged 5 years and older, Fever-TPR and ANC-PR (Additional file 1, Figs S1–S4). No consistent pattern was observed between larviciding and malaria epidemiological parameters.

For both the univariate and multivariable analyses, two sets of analyses were conducted for each risk strata, according to the number of controls as described in the methods section. In the first analysis, each intervention council was paired with the one control council used also for the entomological evaluation (Table 3). There was no evidence of an association between the larviciding intervention and the three malaria epidemiological indicators in the high-risk stratum (Handeni DC). In the moderate (Tanga CC) and low risk strata (Lushoto DC), the Incidence Rate Ratio (IRR) estimates were consistently in the direction of a reduction of malaria epidemiological indicators, but without being statistically significant. There was only one significant result of lowering the malaria positivity rate among pregnant women attending the first ANC visit in the moderate stratum, with an estimated decrease by 53% (95% CI 18%; 74%). However, given that 15 different associations were calculated, there was a risk that such a single significant result had arisen through chance. To account for the risk of a Type 1 error, the p-value was adjusted using Bonferroni’s correction based on the total number of tests performed. Discussion

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between larviciding and malaria epidemiological parameters, stratified by malaria risk epidemiological strata. All three intervention councils successfully conducted the planned six rounds of larvicide application. The success of the implementation was likely due to the involvement of the community through a community-based approach using CORPs that were selected from within the villages, the involvement of local, council and regional authorities at all levels in supervising the intervention, and the role of the national level in coordinating the activity. The use of CORPs with the local, council, and regional level authorities was also noted as a key factor for the successful implementation of a previous larviciding project conducted in Tanzania [43]. This highlights that countries can implement larviciding through a community-based approach using existing government structures for supervision and coordination. This aspect is reported in more detail in a companion paper (Gavana et al. unpublished manuscript).

Handeni DC, a rural district with the largest geographical area of the three councils, had the most breeding habitats, the highest estimated surface area of these habitats (in m2) and the largest variation in weekly identified breeding habitats. Handeni DC is a fairly representative rural district for the North of the country and has also much higher malaria indicators than the two other intervention councils (Fig. 3a and Additional file 1, Figs S1–S4).

While the reported weekly treatment coverage of identified breeding habitats was high, the rise in the number of breeding habitats following the rainfall seasons increased massively the workload of the CORPs in reaching all breeding habitats. It undoubtedly also increased the risk of missing breeding habitats, but this was not ascertained systematically through a validation exercise. As one conclusion from this pilot project, it became obvious that the number of CORPs per village should be regularly revised based on seasonality, to adjust for the number of breeding habitats to be treated.

In Handeni DC (high risk stratum), there was no evidence of an association between larviciding and the malaria epidemiological indicators in the two multivariable analyses. A similar result was also noted in a previous study in a malaria high-risk setting in Kenya [50]. It is well possible that given the large geographical area with a high number of potential breeding sites, especially during the wet months of the year, there was a higher risk for CORPs to miss the breeding habitats. Based on the WHO recommendation, Handeni DC does not fulfill the criteria for implementation of larviciding only in areas where the breeding habitats are few, fixed and findable [23].

In addition, the modality of applying the larvicide was intermittent, with roughly 2 months of application followed by 2 months of no application. As shown by the entomological evaluation (Gavana et al. submitted manuscript), this led in many settings to a rebound in mosquito larvae in-between larviciding rounds, possibly resulting in a rebound in adult mosquitoes and malaria transmission. One recommendation from the current work is therefore to consider continuous larviciding application.

Tanga CC, representing an urban setting, was the intervention council in the moderate risk stratum. A previous study in Tanzania demonstrated the impact of larviciding in reducing malaria prevalence in Dar es Salaam, a large urban setting [27, 33]. The results of the current study did not suggest an association with the malaria epidemiological indicators, but the direction of the results tended towards a reduction without being very strong or statistically significant with one isolated exception: malaria positivity rates among pregnant women attending their first ANC visit (ANC-PR). This could be a chance finding since it is an isolated significant result in one risk stratum similar to the result in the other direction for one age-group in the high-risk stratum, and was not significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons. The variations overall in the findings between the current evaluation and that conducted in Dar es Salaam could have be partly due to the differences in epidemiological outcomes (i.e. between incidence and prevalence), and the data sources used (i.e. between routine health facility data and household surveys).

Lushoto DC (low risk stratum) is a rural setting but with the lowest initial levels of malaria incidence, fever-TPR and ANC-PR. In the multivariable analyses, the intervention showed again a modest trend towards a reduction in malaria epidemiological indicators, but none of the results were statistically significant. A study in Zanzibar (another low malaria risk setting), also did not report significant findings or associations between the intervention and malaria incidence cases [51]. The researchers postulated that the lack of a significant association could have been influenced by other existing interventions, like IRS, and the timing of the intervention, which was conducted during the dry season with low levels of malaria cases. On the other hand, another study conducted in Ethiopia (Tolay Region) showed an impact of larviciding in low malaria prevalence settings [50]. In the Ethiopian setting, a different biological larvicide (i.e. granules) was used and larviciding was conducted continuously during the study period. Further, impact assessment was conducted through malaria prevalence surveys in primary schools. These variations may contribute to the differences with the current study results.

While studies conducted in Kenya [28] and in Sri-Lanka [26], together with the Dar es Salaam study [27], generally reported an impact of the intervention in the latest Cochrane review, the study conducted in the Gambia [29] showed no association. The studies in Kenya and Sri-Lanka covered relatively small surface areas compared to the current study, 2–4 km2 in each of the six settings in the Kenyan study and a total of 23 km2 across eight study villages in Sri-Lanka. In addition, while the Kenyan study used microbial larvicides, the application was conducted continuously and weekly across all identified breeding habitats. The Sri-Lankan study used pyriproxyfen which has a longer residual effect allowing for a spaced application schedule. As above, these variations might have contributed to the reported differences with the current study results.

Strengths and limitations

The current study had the following strengths. Firstly, the intervention was conducted through a community-based approach using community members to identify breeding habitats and apply the larvicide. The supervision was performed by government employees. This approach has been shown to increase community ownership, acceptance and effectiveness [43] and will necessarily be the model for future upscaling. Secondly, the study was conducted at the level of a whole council (the natural administrative unit for programmatic management of routine larviciding) and in three different malaria risk settings. Thirdly, the study included multiple control councils to account for variability within control councils and did an adjusted analysis to control for potential confounders.

However, it is important to also consider the study limitations. Firstly, while the study had multiple control councils per stratum, it only had one intervention council per stratum, and the number of clusters for comparisons was low. Thus, the study could not account well for naturally occurring variation between the intervention and control councils over time. Secondly, the selection of intervention and control councils was not randomized, and this might have also affected the results by introducing a selection bias. Thirdly, the larvae breeding habitats were only identified by CORPs, and there was no independent assessment conducted by either a more intensive search by an expert team or using new technologies such as remote sensing or drones. Hence, a substantial number of breeding habitats were likely missed, particularly during the wet months of the year, and it is unclear what percentage of actual breeding habitats were detected by CORPs and then applied with larvicide. In the future, the authors recommend that expert support is provided to CORPs for this part of their work. Fourthly, intermittent larviciding resulted in a rebound of larvae and adult mosquitoes of the different Anopheles species few weeks after completion of each round of application (Gavana et al. pers. commun.) also affecting the impact of the intervention. The authors advocate to implement larviciding continuously in the future, except at the peak of the rains when the risk of washout of the larvicide is too high. Lastly, epidemiological results relied on malariometric data collected from health facilities, which can be biased by healthcare seeking behavior. Moreover, the analysis included health facilities with over 80% reporting rates, which might have introduced selection bias and affect the generalizability of the findings. Fortunately, the quality and completeness of routine data have increased massively over the past decade, and there is good evidence that these data are suitable for a routine programme assessment [41, 52, 53].

A last consideration with regard to the evidence described above, as well as the authors experiences and the limitations of any observational evaluation, is the urgent need for high-quality cluster-randomized controlled trials (cRCTs) conducted under different field circumstances in Africa to put the evidence base concerning larviciding on a firmer basis.

The pilot larviciding project provides some evidence on the operational, cost and impact of larviciding as a supplementary malaria control intervention. At a time when other supplementary vector control interventions are only slowly emerging, generating more experience with larviciding is important.

Conclusion

The larviciding intervention was conducted successfully in all three intervention councils in Tanga Region in north-east Tanzania. Overall, there was no strong evidence of an association between larviciding and the epidemiological indicators, despite a tendency for estimates being in the direction of a reduction observed in the moderate and low malaria risk strata. The results might have been influenced by operational designs adopted during the implementation, including the intermittent model of larvicide application, as well as the operational challenges including missed breeding habitats. The inclusion of more clusters and randomization would provide a stronger study design. Learning from these findings, the authors recommend conducting more rigorous implementation studies across different malaria epidemiological risk strata to generate further evidence on the effect of larviciding on malaria transmission.
