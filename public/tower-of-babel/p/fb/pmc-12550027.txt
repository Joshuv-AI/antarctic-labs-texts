Streptococcus pneumoniae infection is associated with matrix metalloproteinase-9 in lung cancer progression and brain metastases
Authors: Lu Gao, Pushpa Dhilipkannah, Feng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor progression and metastasis, particularly brain metastases, are major causes of mortality in lung cancer. Emerging evidence indicates that microbial infections can influence tumor biology. We previously identified Streptococcus pneumoniae (SP) as a contributing factor to lung cancer progression. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are key enzymes involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and have been implicated in promoting metastatic dissemination. This study investigated the association between SP infection, MMP expression, and clinical outcomes in 126 lung cancer tissues. SP DNA levels were quantified using droplet digital PCR, and MMP protein expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry. Elevated SP abundance was significantly correlated with increased MMP-9 expression, advanced tumor stage, presence of brain metastases, and reduced overall survival (P < 0.05), whereas no significant association was observed with MMP-2. Therefore, SP infection is associated with lung cancer progression and metastasis through its correlation with MMP-9-mediated extracellular matrix remodeling. This association highlights a potential therapeutic target for limiting metastatic spread and improving patient outcomes; however, functional studies are required to confirm its mechanistic role in lung tumorigenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-10297-3.Keywords: Lung cancer, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Matrix metalloproteinase-9, Extracellular matrix remodeling, Brain metastasesSubject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Lung cancer, Metastasis, Tumour biomarkers

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, resulting in millions of deaths each year among both men and women1,2. 85% of lung cancer cases are classified as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which primarily includes adenocarcinoma (AC) (40–50%) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (25–30%)1,3. Disease progression and metastasis—particularly brain metastases (BM)—are among the most devastating complications for lung cancer patients, contributing substantially to mortality and severely impairing quality of life4,5. Despite substantial advances in therapeutic strategies, including targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the molecular mechanisms underlying lung cancer progression and metastasis are not fully understood. Elucidating these mechanisms is essential for identifying novel therapeutic targets and reducing lung cancer–related mortality.

Emerging evidence suggests that microbial infections play a significant role in cancer biology6. These infections can contribute to tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis through various mechanisms, including the activation of pro-inflammatory and oncogenic pathways6–9. Our recent studies demonstrate that Streptococcus pneumoniae (SP), a prevalent respiratory pathogen, can promote lung cancer development and progression through its surface protein PspC binding to the platelet-activating factor receptor (PAFR) on lung cancer cells8–11. This interaction stimulates cellular proliferation and migration and induces pro-inflammatory cytokine production. It also activates key oncogenic signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT and NF-κB, which are known to drive tumor growth and inflammation-associated progression11. In vivo, SP-infected cancer cells formed larger tumors in xenograft models, and co-administration with a tobacco carcinogen significantly increased tumor burden and reduced survival in mice. Clinically, elevated levels of SP and PAFR in human lung tumor specimens were associated with advanced disease stage and poorer disease-specific survival. These findings suggest that SP may act as a microbial contributor to lung tumorigenesis via PspC–PAFR–mediated inflammatory and oncogenic mechanisms, offering a potential therapeutic target11.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases that regulate extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling12,13. Among them, MMP-9 and MMP-2 are key enzymes that degrade ECM components such as collagen and gelatin12,13. This enzymatic activity facilitates tumor cell migration and colonization, which are critical steps in cancer invasion and metastasis associated with poor survival outcomes13. The role of MMP-9 and MMP-2 in the development of BMs is of particular interest, as BM is a leading cause of death in lung cancer patients13. However, the upstream regulators of MMP activity, including potential contributions from microbial pathogens, remain poorly understood13. Given the important role of SP in promoting inflammation and tumor progression, and the critical involvement of MMPs in cancer metastasis, we hypothesize that SP may contribute to lung cancer progression and metastasis by modulating the MMP activities. In this study, we aim to assess the abundance of SP in lung cancer tissues, evaluate its association with MMP expression, and determine its impact on survival outcomes in lung cancer patients.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Maryland (HP-00040666). Surgically resected lung tumor tissues were collected from 126 patients with histologically confirmed NSCLC (Table 1). Histologically, the patient population was nearly evenly split between adenocarcinoma (AC, 51.6%) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC, 48.4%). T-status classification was based on the AJCC 8th edition lung cancer staging criteria14, with tumors stratified into five subgroups: T1a (≤ 1 cm), T1b (> 1–2 cm), T2 (including T1c and T2a/b, > 2–5 cm or associated features), T3 (> 5–7 cm or direct invasion of the chest wall or pericardium), and T4 (> 7 cm or invasion of mediastinal structures). In our cohort, T-status ranged from T1a to T4, with T2 being the most common (41.3%), followed by T3 (17.5%), T1a (20.6%), T1b (13.5%), and T4 (7.1%). Among the 126 patients, 61.1% exhibited nodal involvement (N-positive), while 38.9% were node-negative. BMs were identified in 20.6% of patients, whereas the remaining 79.4% showed no evidence of brain involvement. Patients were diagnosed across all stages, with 26.2% at stage I, 47.6% at stage II, and 26.2% at stage III-IV. In addition, tumor grade distribution showed that 12.0% of tumors were well differentiated (Grade 1), 43.7% were moderately differentiated (Grade 2), and 44.4% were poorly differentiated (Grade 3), reflecting a substantial proportion of biologically aggressive tumors. This distribution underscores the diversity of the cohort across histologic subtypes, tumor grades, and clinical stages, making it suitable for investigating molecular and pathological correlates of progression and metastasis15.

SP DNA abundance and MMP-9 expression are both significantly associated with tumor characteristics in advanced lung cancer

Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) was successfully performed on 126 lung cancer tissue specimens to quantify the DNA copy number of SP, expressed as copies per microliter. In parallel, immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses were also effectively conducted to assess MMP-9 and MMP-2 protein expression in the same specimens. Both MMP-2 and MMP-9 were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of lung cancer cells, with staining intensity varying across histological subtypes and metastatic sites (Fig. 1). The Kruskal-Wallis test showed that SP abundance and MMP-9 expression were significantly higher in tumors with advanced T-status, stage III–IV disease, BMs, SCC histology, higher tumor grade, and in smokers (all P < 0.05) (Table 2; Fig. 2). SP abundance and MMP-9 expression were also considerably elevated in cases with positive lymph node involvement (all < 0.05). In contrast, MMP-2 expression did not show statistically significant variation across these tumor or demographic variables (Table 2) (Fig. 2). To further explore these associations, a Spearman rank correlation analysis was conducted (Table 3). This analysis confirmed strong positive correlations between SP abundance and T-status (ρ = 0.78 for T4, P = 0.004), N-status (ρ = 0.67, P = 0.003), BM (ρ = 0.79, P = 0.001), and tumor grade (ρ = 0.68 for high-grade, P = 0.002).

Correlation of SP abundance with MMP-9 and MMP-2 expression across clinical and pathological features

Spearman rank correlation analysis demonstrated a strong stage-dependent association between SP DNA abundance and MMP-9 expression in lung cancer tissues (Table 3). Specifically, SP abundance was moderately correlated with MMP-9 in Stage I (ρ = 0.38, P = 0.037) and Stage II (ρ = 0.52, P = 0.049), and showed a robust correlation in advanced disease (Stage III–IV: ρ = 0.76, P = 0.001). This trend is further supported by scatter plot analysis (Fig. 3A), where a significant linear correlation was observed between SP and MMP-9 expression (Pearson’s r = 0.612, P < 0.001). In contrast, no significant association was observed between SP abundance and MMP-2 expression at any disease stage (P > 0.05). SP abundance also showed significant correlations with MMP-9 expression across several clinicopathological categories. Specifically, both SP and MMP-9 levels were significantly higher in SCC compared to AC (P = 0.005 and P = 0.034, respectively). Smokers exhibited elevated SP abundance and MMP-9 expression compared to non-smokers (P = 0.041 and P = 0.046, respectively). Significant correlations between SP and MMP-9 were observed across tumor sizes, particularly in T2 (ρ = 0.55, P = 0.039), T3 (ρ = 0.72, P = 0.008), and T4 tumors (ρ = 0.78, P = 0.004). SP abundance was also strongly correlated with nodal involvement (ρ = 0.67, P = 0.003), accompanied by higher MMP-9 expression (P = 0.016). Patients with BM demonstrated a robust association between SP and MMP-9 (ρ = 0.79, P = 0.001; P = 0.026 for MMP-9 expression). Regarding tumor differentiation, SP abundance was positively correlated with MMP-9 expression in Grade 2 (ρ = 0.51, P = 0.041) and Grade 3 tumors (ρ = 0.68, P = 0.002), with significantly higher MMP-9 expression in poorly differentiated cases (P = 0.019). In contrast, no statistically significant associations were observed between SP abundance and MMP-2 expression across these categories, despite modest correlation coefficients in certain subgroups (e.g., T4: ρ = 0.58, P = 0.215) Fig. 3B). In addition, smokers had significantly elevated SP abundance (P = 0.041) and MMP-9 levels (P = 0.046).

Collectively, these findings underscore a specific and stage-dependent relationship between SP abundance and MMP-9 expression, especially in higher-grade, advanced-stage tumors with nodal involvement or BMs. The absence of a similar pattern with MMP-2 suggests the SP–MMP-9 axis may play a more pivotal role in lung cancer progression and metastasis.

SP abundance and MMP-9 expression as predictors of survival outcomes in cancer patients

To assess the prognostic relevance of SP abundance and MMP expression in lung cancer, we performed Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses (Table 4; Fig. 4). Patients with high SP abundance had significantly shorter median overall survival (OS) compared to those with low SP levels (12 vs. 20 months; hazard ratio [HR] = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.3–3.4; P = 0.02). Similarly, elevated MMP-9 expression was associated with worse survival (15 vs. 30 months; HR = 2.8, 95% CI: 1.8–4.3; P < 0.01). In contrast, MMP-2 expression showed no significant relationship with OS, indicating a limited prognostic role in this context.

Additionally, T-status, analyzed as an ordinal variable, was strongly associated with poorer prognosis (HR per one-level increase = 1.68; P < 0.001; FDR = 0.002). Together, these results support the prognostic value of SP and MMP-9 in lung cancer progression and underscore the critical role of tumor burden in shaping patient outcomes.

To further explore the clinical utility of these biomarkers, we stratified the cohort into four groups defined by combined SP and MMP-9 expression levels. The thresholds—3.5 copies/µL for SP DNA and > 60% for MMP-9–positive tumor cells—were selected based on their alignment with tumor stage distributions. SP levels above 3.5 copies/µL exceeded the median observed in Stage II tumors, while MMP-9 expression above 60% captured the elevated expression characteristic of Stage II–IV disease. As summarized in Table 5, patients with both low SP and low MMP-9 expression exhibited the most favorable prognosis, with a median OS of 35 months. In contrast, those with high SP and high MMP-9 expression had the poorest survival (median OS = 10 months), with an adjusted HR of 4.0 (95% CI: 2.3–7.0; P < 0.001). Intermediate outcomes were observed in patients with elevated expressions of either SP or MMP-9 alone. A statistically significant interaction effect (P = 0.004) supports a potential synergistic role of SP and MMP-9 in tumor progression. This observation is further supported by both multivariable-adjusted outcomes and unadjusted survival comparisons (Table S2). Together, these findings highlight the potential for developing a clinically actionable biomarker panel to improve patient risk stratification.

Discussion

There is growing evidence that microbial dysbiosis and the presence of intratumoral bacteria contribute to cancer initiation and progression, as well as influence therapeutic responses16. We have previously demonstrated that SP promotes lung tumorigenesis by binding PspC to PAFR on lung cancer cells, thereby activating PI3K/AKT and NF-κB signaling pathways and enhancing tumor growth8,9,11. Building on our previous findings8–11the current study aimed to continuously investigate the role of the respiratory bacterial pathogen SP in lung cancer progression and metastasis, with a particular focus on its association with MMPs. Our findings demonstrate a strong positive correlation between SP abundance and MMP-9 levels. Furthermore, both SP abundance and MMP-9 levels are significantly associated with advanced lung cancer stages, BMs, and poor survival outcomes. Additionally, SP abundance was more strongly associated with SCC than AC, implying that SP may play a subtype-specific role in lung cancer pathogenesis. However, SP abundance did not correlate with MMP-2 levels, which were not significantly associated with advanced tumor stage, BMs, or survival outcomes in our cohort. These findings suggest that not all MMPs contribute equally to cancer progression and highlight the specificity of SP’s interaction with MMP-9 in promoting metastatic disease. Additionally, MMP-2 may play a distinct and possibly less prominent role in SP-mediated tumor biology or may be activated under different pathophysiological conditions. We also confirmed that SP abundance is associated with smoking status, supporting our previous findings and highlighting smoking as a potential enhancer of SP-related pathways in tumor progression8–11. The close, stage-dependent correlation observed between SP abundance and MMP-9—but not MMP-2—suggests that MMP-9 may be selectively induced by microbial and immune-related stimuli. Therefore, based on our current and previous findings8–11we propose a model in which SP infection promotes MMP-9 upregulation through both direct interaction with lung cancer cells and the induction of immune-mediated inflammatory signaling (Fig. 5). Elevated MMP-9 expression facilitates ECM, thereby enhancing tumor invasion, progression, and dissemination to distant organs such as the brain.

Proposed mechanism by which SP infection promotes lung cancer progression and metastasis via MMP-mediated extracellular matrix degradation, based on our previous and current studies. SP binds to the platelet-activating factor receptor (PAFR) on lung cancer cells, initiating downstream inflammatory signaling and immune cell activation. This leads to increased expression of matrix metalloproteinases, particularly MMP-9 and potentially MMP-2, which mediate extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation. The resulting ECM breakdown facilitates tumor cell invasion, progression, and dissemination to distant sites such as the brain.

The pronounced association between SP abundance and BMs is particularly noteworthy, given that BMs are a leading cause of mortality among lung cancer patients and their underlying mechanisms remain inadequately understood1. Our data suggest that SP infection may promote BMs by enhancing tumor invasiveness and ECM degradation. From a clinical standpoint, our findings may open promising avenues for therapeutic intervention. For example, future strategies targeting SP colonization in lung cancer patients could potentially disrupt the SP-MMP-9 axis, thereby limiting BMs. Moreover, MMP-9 inhibitors, which are currently under investigation for various malignancies17, may offer heightened efficacy in patients with elevated SP levels.

Nevertheless, our study has some limitations. The reliance on tissue specimens from a single institution may limit the generalizability of our findings; therefore, larger, multicenter studies are necessary to validate and extend these results. Further functional studies using animal models are warranted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the contribution of SP abundance to MMP-9-mediated tumor progression.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that elevated SP abundance is significantly associated with MMP-9-mediated ECM, contributing to lung cancer progression and metastases. This study also suggests the broader importance of tumor–microbe interactions in cancer biology. Although further validation and functional studies are warranted, these findings underscore the significance of microbial infections in cancer biology and highlight the therapeutic potential of combining antimicrobial agents with MMP-9 inhibitors to mitigate metastasis and improve survival outcomes in lung cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Patients and clinical specimens

This study was carried out in strict adherence to ethical standards, following protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Maryland Baltimore (IRB number HP-00040666). All research procedures and methods were conducted in compliance with the approved institutional and ethical guidelines. Prior to participation, all individuals enrolled in the study were required to provide written, informed consent, confirming their voluntary involvement. Furthermore, participants were fully informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without any consequences. A total of 126 patients diagnosed with NSCLC were included in the study. These individuals had undergone either lobectomy or pneumonectomy surgeries at the University of Maryland Medical Center. From these patients, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) lung tumor tissue samples were collected and used for downstream analyses. FFPE samples are widely utilized in clinical research due to their stability and preservation of morphological features, making them suitable for retrospective pathological and molecular investigations. Comprehensive clinical and pathological data were retrieved from patient medical records to support the analysis. The data collected included key demographic and disease-related variables such as age, sex, and smoking status. In addition, tumor-specific characteristics were recorded, including the stage of the tumor at diagnosis, histological subtype, and the presence or absence of BMs. Furthermore, to minimize potential confounding factors, we confirmed that none of the patients included in this study had evidence of recent infections or had received antibiotic treatment at the time of surgical tissue collection. This information was verified through clinical records and preoperative evaluations.

Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR)

DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) lung tissue specimens using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Cat# 56404; Germantown, MD), following the manufacturer’s protocol and as previously described12,16. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), and only samples with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were included. ddPCR was performed using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). Specific primers and probe targeting the lytA gene of SP were used: forward primer 5′-ACG CAA TCT AGC AGA TGA AGC A-3′, reverse primer 5′-TGT CTC GAA TTC CAA CCC TTG-3′, and FAM-labeled probe 5′-TGC CGA AAA CGC TTG ATA CAG GGA G-TAMRA-3′. Each 20 µL ddPCR reaction contained 10 ng of template DNA, 900 nM of each primer, 250 nM of probe, and 10 µL of 2× ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTP) (Bio-Rad, Cat# 1863024). Droplets were generated using the QX200 Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad, Cat# 1864002), and thermal cycling was carried out on a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler under the following conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s and 60 °C for 1 min, with a final step at 98 °C for 10 min and a 4 °C hold, using a ramp rate of 2 °C/second. Droplets were read using the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad, Cat# 1864003), and data were analyzed with QuantaSoft software (Bio-Rad, v1.7). SP DNA abundance was reported as copies/µL and normalized to the total input DNA. Each experiment included no-template controls and positive controls using purified SP genomic DNA (ATCC 49619).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and analysis

IHC analysis of MMP-2 and MMP-9 was performed on FFPE lung tumor tissue sections, as previously described18. 5-µm sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a pressure cooker for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MMP-2 (rabbit polyclonal, Abcam, Cat# ab37150; dilution 1:200) and MMP-9 (rabbit monoclonal, Abcam, Cat# ab76003; dilution 1:300), prepared in antibody diluent (DAKO). The following day, sections were washed and incubated with a biotinylated secondary anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Abcam, Cat# ab6720; dilution 1:500) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by treatment with the VECTASTAIN® ABC kit (Vector Laboratories) and visualization using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (Abcam, Cat# ab64238). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Microscopic evaluation was performed using an Olympus BX51 microscope (Olympus, Center Valley, PA). For quantitative assessment, five representative high-power fields (400×) encompassing both tumor and stromal compartments were selected per section. Positive cells displaying strong, well-defined brown cytoplasmic staining were manually counted using AnalySIS Pro software (Olympus). Each region was independently assessed three times by two blind observers. Staining intensity and frequency were scored semi-quantitatively as follows: 0 (0–10% positive cells), 1 (11–25%), 2 (26–50%), and 3 (> 50%). All image analyses were conducted in a blind manner to minimize observer bias.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed to assess associations between SP DNA abundance, MMP-9 and MMP-2 expression, clinicopathological variables, and patient outcomes. Descriptive statistics summarized patient demographics, tumor characteristics, and biomarker levels. Differences in SP abundance and MMP expression across clinical subgroups (e.g., stage, histology, smoking status) were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test for overall comparisons, followed by Mann–Whitney U tests for pairwise comparisons. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess relationships between SP, MMP expression, and tumor features. Pearson’s correlation analysis was applied for selected scatter plots of SP and MMP expression levels. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to determine hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), both in univariate and multivariable contexts, adjusting for potential confounders such as age, stage, and treatment. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method for multiple comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using AnalySIS Pro (Version 5.2), R software (Version 4.3.2), and GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5.1) for data visualization.

SP binds to the platelet-activating factor receptor (PAFR) on lung cancer cells, initiating downstream inflammatory signaling and immune cell activation. This leads to increased expression of matrix metalloproteinases, particularly MMP-9 and potentially MMP-2, which mediate extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation. The resulting ECM breakdown facilitates tumor cell invasion, progression, and dissemination to distant sites such as the brain.
