A novel scoring algorithm for chest pain can effectively support the diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome in prehospital settings: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Keita Iyama, Shuntaro Sato, Ryohei Akashi, Kensho Baba, Koichi Hayakawa, Satoshi Ikeda, Koji Maemura, Osamu Tasaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly identification of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) in prehospital settings is crucial for optimal patient outcomes. However, existing risk assessment tools require laboratory data, making them unsuitable for prehospital use. Therefore, emergency medical technicians (EMTs) lack appropriate tools for prehospital ACS assessment and must rely on individual diagnostic skills, despite the importance of reducing prehospital time. To address this issue, a novel scoring system—Nagasaki Prehospital Chest Pain Assessment & Risk Determination (N-CARD)—was developed using only prehospital information and validated for use by EMTs, with the aim of improving patient outcomes and optimizing healthcare resource utilization.MethodsIn total, 584 participants with chest pain or suspected cardiac etiology who underwent a prehospital 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) between April 2023 and March 2024 were analyzed. The prehospital diagnostic score for ACS, N-CARD score, was developed using logistic regression based on the following variables: age, pain location, pain type, pain duration, coronary risk factors, and 12-lead ECG findings. Modeling was performed separately for high-risk and low-risk groups based on prior coronary artery disease (CAD) history. The model’s performance was internally validated using bootstrap methods.ResultsThe N-CARD scoring system was developed separately for participants without (N = 433) and with (N = 151) a history of CAD. The score ranged from − 1 to 10 for those without a history of CAD and 0 to 32 for those with a history of CAD. For participants without a history of CAD, scores ≥ 6 suggested ACS (specificity > 90%), whereas scores ≤ 3 suggested non-ACS (sensitivity > 90%), with an optimism-corrected area under the curve (AUC) of 0.90. For participants with a history of CAD, scores ≥ 24 suggested ACS, whereas scores ≤ 6 suggested non-ACS, with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.69.ConclusionsThe N-CARD scoring system, based solely on prehospital information, provides EMTs with an effective tool for assessing ACS risk in prehospital settings, potentially optimizing transport decisions and improving patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-025-01019-7.Keywords: Acute coronary syndrome, Chest pain, Emergency medical technicians, Optimizing patient transport, Risk assessment

Background

The appropriate management of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), which is one of the most common causes of mortality from ischemic heart disease, is an urgent global issue [1]. Around 35% of patients with chest pain have ACS, a critical, life-threatening condition [2]. Given the difficulty in determining the presence of ACS among patients with chest pain, various risk assessment tools, such as the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) risk, History, ECG, Age, Risk factors and Troponin (HEART) score, and Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) score have been used [3–7]. Early revascularization, which can be achieved through procedures such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), is required in ACS treatment, especially in patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), considering that a minimum acceptable level of reperfusion is required within 90 min, preferably within ≤ 60 min, of hospital arrival [2, 8]. Given the importance of early transport to an appropriate medical facility (PCI-capable facility) in ACS treatment, determining whether or not a patient with chest pain has ACS is crucial.

Although early reperfusion after hospital arrival is critical in ACS treatment, studies have shown that simple shortening of the door-to-balloon time (DTBT) alone does not improve outcomes in ACS patients and that a reduction in the prehospital time (i.e., from onset to hospital arrival) is required [2, 9]. The Japan Resuscitation Council Guidelines recommend a prehospital 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) for patients with ACS to reduce the total ischemic time, highlighting the importance of prehospital activities [10]. Emergency medical technicians (EMTs) play an important role in prehospital activities by identifying whether or not a patient with chest pain has ACS so that they can be transported to the appropriate medical facility. However, previous surveys have shown that EMTs have insufficient knowledge or confidence in interpreting 12-lead ECG findings [11]. Furthermore, ACS risk assessment tools after hospital arrival, such as the TIMI risk score, HEART score, or GRACE score, require blood testing, with no ACS risk assessment tools currently being available for prehospital settings. Hence, individual EMTs often select the transport destination based on their diagnostic skills. In Japan, emergency medical services (EMS) are operated by local fire departments, and EMTs are unauthorized to make definitive diagnoses or treatment decisions. Basically, there are no emergency physicians outside hospitals. Only EMTs come in contact with patients in prehospital settings. They transport patients to hospitals that can perform emergency cardiac catheterization if ACS is suspected based on ECG findings or physical examination or to general emergency hospitals if ACS is not suspected. Therefore, accurate risk stratification tools that can support the decisions of EMTs during patient transport are important in the Japanese context, wherein transport destination is selected based on preliminary field assessment and physician consultation [12].

During prehospital activities, EMTs are required to efficiently classify patients with chest pain as having either ACS or non-ACS. Therefore, a new ACS risk assessment tool specifically designed for prehospital use is needed to help improve the accuracy at which EMTs classify patients with chest pain as ACS or non-ACS. Efficient suspicion of ACS in prehospital settings can reduce the total ischemic time for ACS treatment and improve the prognosis of ACS patients. Moreover, some cardiology facilities in certain regions accept all patients with chest pain, though not all of these patients have cardiac disease. Therefore, the accurate transfer of non-ACS patients to non-cardiac facilities may help reduce the burden on cardiac facilities that generally accept all patients with chest pain, and task shifting may help to maintain/improve the performance of cardiac facilities. The importance of the current study lies in its focus on not only ACS treatment but also optimization of patient transport in regions with limited healthcare resources.

In the present study, we developed a novel ACS risk assessment tool, termed the Nagasaki Prehospital Chest Pain Assessment & Risk Determination (N-CARD) scoring system, for use in prehospital settings, confirmed its internal validity, and proposed scores to identify ACS or non-ACS patients.

Methods

This study was designed as a cross-sectional observational study.

Data source

The N-CARD scoring system was developed using research registry data. Data were collected between April 2023 and March 2024. All data except for outcomes were collected by EMTs and consisted of information that could be measured before hospital arrival from the time of initial patient contact. Outcome data were based on the final diagnoses from the receiving medical institutions. The data were anonymized at the point of outcomes and prehospital data integration and utilized for statistical analysis. All collected data were used in the development of the N-CARD scoring system.

Participants

This study included all participants within Nagasaki City, Japan, for whom EMS were dispatched for chest pain and who underwent a prehospital 12-lead ECG. All patients who underwent prehospital 12-lead ECG due to suspected cardiac etiology even without chest pain as the primary complaint (e.g., syncope or dyspnea) were included. The exclusion criteria were as follows: participants without 12-lead ECG acquisition, those under 18 years of age, and those without a final diagnosis from the receiving medical institutions.

Outcomes

Participants diagnosed with acute myocardial infarction or unstable angina by the receiving medical institutions were classified as having ACS.

Predictors

The N-CARD scoring system was developed using only information that can be collected in prehospital settings, with reference to the TIMI risk score and HEART score [3, 4]. It consists of the following seven components: history of coronary artery disease (CAD), age, pain location, pain type, pain duration, coronary risk factors, and 12-lead ECG findings. Each component was collected as categorical data, with the details being provided in Table 1. The findings of the 12-lead ECG used in this scoring system were determined by the EMTs in prehospital settings and not by physicians. Many of them had taken our 12-lead ECG training course for EMTs and were able to interpret basic 12-lead ECG findings [13, 14].

Age was stratified into three levels according to the HEART score classifications [4]. Pain location and type were categorized as high, middle, or low risk with reference to the Chest Pain Guidelines [7]. Pain duration was classified based on the ACS guidelines [2]. Risk factors were categorized according to the HEART score, with high risk being defined as having two or more of the seven coronary risk factors and low risk being defined as having zero or one risk factor. Participants with 12-lead ECG findings indicating ST-elevation or depression, T-wave inversion or left bundle branch block, and no abnormalities were classified as high, middle, and low risks, respectively. The risk stratification of findings is described in Table 1.

Sample size

Between April 2023 to March 2024, a total of 656 patients with chest pain or suspected cardiac disease were transported by ambulance and underwent 12-lead ECG testing, among whom 584 participants with a confirmed diagnosis were included in the analysis.

Missing

Among the participants with confirmed diagnosis, 1% of participants had missing data for predictors. Given their small proportion, these participants were excluded from model development.

Analytical methods

This study developed separate N-CARD scoring systems for individuals with and without a history of CAD considering that the contribution of other predictors to the outcomes might differ between these groups. Background characteristics were summarized using numbers and percentages (Table 1).

Scoring system development

Some categories for the analyzed predictors contained few patients, resulting in unstable estimates. Therefore, all predictors were classified into two categories, and the following model development was conducted. First, the adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using logistic regression models with ACS as the response variable and six predictors (excluding history of CAD) as explanatory variables were estimated. Next, a scoring system was from the mentioned model. Subsequently, a base predictor was selected and used to divide the log ORs of other predictors by the log OR of this base predictor, rounding to create integer scores. For details, see Supplementary method 1.

To distinguish between ACS and non-ACS, cutoff values for diagnostic probabilities need to be set. In many patients, a diagnostic probability of 0.5 or more would indicate ACS. However, it was determined that high specificity was needed for ACS identification (i.e., scores indicating ACS should have a high probability of physician confirmation), whereas high sensitivity was needed for non-ACS identification (i.e., scores indicating non-ACS should have a high probability of physician confirmation). Therefore, the required sensitivity and specificity were both set at 90% or higher for the N-CARD score. For more details, see Supplementary method 2. Subsequently, the sensitivity and specificity for each diagnostic probability was calculated, and the score cutoff values for ACS or non-ACS identification were examined.

The model diagnostic performance measures were calculated, including the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC), and created calibration plots to verify the agreement between diagnostic probabilities and actual probabilities of ACS occurrence.

Internal validation of the scoring system

Owing to the short registration period and single-region data, external validation was not possible. Therefore, all data were used for model development and internal validation. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap methods to calculate optimism and compute optimism-corrected sensitivity, specificity, and AUC [15]. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) or higher.

Results

Summary of participant characteristics

Among the 584 participants analyzed, 433 had no history of CAD, whereas 151 had such history (Table 1). Regardless of CAD history or ACS status, over 70% of the participants were 65 years or older. For pain location, very few participants fell into the middle risk category (< 2% in all groups), with most being either high or low risk. Regarding pain duration, over 60% were high risk even in non-ACS participants, regardless of past medical history, considering that the inclusion criteria specifically targeted EMS dispatch cases (walk-in cases were excluded). Over 30% of the ACS group and around 25% of the non-ACS group had two or more risk factors, regardless of medical history.

Results for the models and N-CARD score

Table 2 summarizes the adjusted ORs with 95% CIs and scores for each predictor. Among patients without a history of CAD, those 64 years of age or younger were less likely to be diagnosed with ACS (OR 0.64, 95%CI 0.30–1.33), whereas among those with a history of CAD, those 64 years of age or younger were more likely to be diagnosed with ACS (OR 2.83, 95%CI 1.04–7.76). For other predictors, regardless of CAD history, higher risk categories were more likely to be diagnosed with ACS, consistent with previous studies.

Diagnostic probabilities for ACS and model performance

As indicated in the ROC curves of Fig. 2, patients without a history of CAD had an original (i.e., using raw data) AUC of 0.92 (without optimism correction) and a corrected AUC (i.e., using scores) of 0.90. Meanwhile, those with a history of CAD had an original and corrected AUC of 0.73 and 0.69, respectively. The difference in AUC between the original data and the score-based models was minimal, with higher discriminative ability having been observed among those without a history of CAD. As shown in Fig. 1c and d, the overlap in the diagnostic probability of ACS based on the developed scoring system between participants diagnosed with ACS and non-ACS was smaller among those without a history of CAD, explaining the difference in discriminative ability. The calibration plots presented in Supplementary Fig. 1 show that regardless of a history of CAD, the diagnostic probabilities based on the scoring system generally aligned well with actual proportion of ACS diagnosis. Figure 3 shows the N-CARD scores for the ACS and non-ACS groups. Notably, 114 patients without a history of CAD had an N-CARD score ≤ 3, among whom only one was a false negative case of ACS (false negative rate, 0.88%). Meanwhile, 37 patients with a history of CAD had an N-CARD score ≤ 6, including two false negative cases of ACS (false negative rate, 5%).

Discussion

In the current study, the N-CARD score was developed to assist EMT decision-making by calculating ACS risk using only information available in prehospital settings for patients with suspected ACS presenting with symptoms such as chest pain. Findings suggest that using the N-CARD scoring system could significantly improve ACS patient outcomes in the future by enabling early transport to PCI-capable facilities and rapid catheterization laboratory activation based on field assessment.

Applying the N-CARD score

The N-CARD scoring system can be used to optimize emergency transport decisions for patients with chest pain. The system was designed with EMTs as the primary users, making it highly practical for field use. Typically, EMTs attempt to transport patients with a history of CAD to the facility where they had been previously treated. Therefore, EMTs most likely face transport decision challenges in patients without a history of CAD. For this reason, the current study separated subjects based on a history of CAD. The proposed process involves an initial assessment for a history of CAD, such as myocardial infarction or angina after which the N-CARD score is applied in prehospital settings (Fig. 4). For patients without a history of CAD, a N-CARD score ≥ 6 suggests prioritizing transport to facilities capable of emergency PCI, whereas a score ≤ 3 more likely suggests non-ACS, allowing for transport to general emergency hospitals without PCI capabilities. Figure 5 depicts a flowchart for determining the transport hospital based on the N-CARD scoring system. There was only one false negative case in the group without CAD history and two in the group with CAD history (Fig. 3), which appears to be an acceptable criterion in prehospital practice.

For patients with a history of CAD, clinical practice typically prioritizes transport to facilities where previous cardiac catheterization procedures were performed. However, when such facilities are unavailable or in particularly challenging cases, the N-CARD scoring system can guide transport decisions. Study findings showed that a N-CARD score ≥ 24 suggests prioritizing transport to emergency PCI-capable facilities, whereas a score ≤ 6 suggests considering non-PCI facilities (general emergency hospitals). However, given the wide score ranges obtained in the data, this scoring system alone may not be sufficient to guide transport decision-making in this group. Additionally, considering the complexity of score calculation, its use might be limited to a reference tool at present. Nonetheless, further follow-up studies are anticipated.

These cutoff values were statistically derived and can be adjusted based on regional emergency medical systems. For example, if PCI-capable facilities in the region have capacity for non-ACS patients, the cutoff values could be lowered. Conversely, if prompt transfer to PCI facilities is possible when ACS patients are transported to non-PCI facilities, regional cutoff values could be raised. Regardless, implementation should ideally follow discussion and approval by regional medical control councils.

Age reversal phenomenon

Although the current study included all patients for whom ambulances were dispatched for chest pain, it did not account for pain severity (e.g., numerical rating scale). Therefore, the threshold for calling an ambulance might differ between younger and older patients. According to the Cabinet Office’s public opinion survey on emergency services, younger people tend to support actively restricting ambulance use for minor symptoms, whereas older people support a more cautious approach to limiting minor symptom use [16]. This suggests that younger people might only call for an ambulance when experiencing severe chest pain, whereas older people might have a lower threshold for calling, potentially explaining why the present study found opposing ORs for younger and older patients among those with a history of CAD unlike in past studies. However, as this data represents real-world data from the Nagasaki region, it was incorporated directly into the N-CARD scoring system.

Contribution to ACS patient care

ACS, particularly STEMI, requires early intervention [2, 10]. This intervention should begin not only after hospital arrival but also during the prehospital phase. In fact, the Resuscitation Council Guidelines emphasize the importance of prehospital intervention in ACS care [10]. Thus, when EMTs strongly suspect ACS, they can further save time by not only transporting the patient to PCI-capable facilities but also proposing emergency cardiac catheterization preparation in advance. However, given that EMTs cannot directly suggest a treatment plan in practice, decisions are made by the physician after hearing the case report from the EMTs over the phone. The N-CARD score can be useful for physicians to make such a decision. Therefore, it is important to develop regional systems where EMTs can communicate N-CARD scores to hospitals, enabling receiving facilities to activate the catheterization laboratory in preparation.

ECG transmission

Although 12-lead ECG transmission systems are gradually becoming ubiquitous, allowing physicians to interpret ECGs and provide instructions after transmission rather than requiring EMTs interpretation, their effectiveness remains debated [17–19]. Some reports indicate that 12-lead ECG transmission and prenotification reduced ischemic time [18], whereas others suggest no clear benefits in time reduction [20]. This suggests that simply transmitting ECGs is insufficient and that comprehensive management including post-transmission hospital processes significantly impacts outcomes. Given that only a few regions throughout Japan have fully implemented 12-lead ECG transmission systems, most still rely on EMT personnel’s interpretation abilities. Therefore, the N-CARD scoring system appears valuable considering that it allows EMTs to visualize necessary information for easy selection of transport destinations and preactivation of catheterization laboratories.

Transport optimization

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, difficult transport cases for both COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 patients have increased across Japan [21], with inappropriate transport decisions being one factor for such an increase. Epidemiologically, although 50% of chest pain cases presenting to the emergency department are non-cardiac in nature [2], general hospitals often refuse such patients due to their limited capacity to treat cardiovascular diseases, leading to concentration at cardiac centers. However, given that 50% of patients with chest pain are non-cardiac in nature, specific cardiac care is not always necessary [2]. Nonetheless, a spike in admissions for non-cardiac cases at cardiac hospitals can cause capacity issues, potentially preventing acceptance of true ACS cases and creating transport difficulties. The proposed N-CARD scoring system could help optimize patient transport and reduce regional transport difficulties.

EMS education

Although more studies are needed for verify the N-CARD scoring system’s contribution to DTBT reduction and improvement of ACS patient outcomes, existing evidence has suggested that ECG transmission systems alone may not improve ACS patient outcomes, highlighting the need for education and awareness activities for both EMS personnel and receiving hospitals alongside the N-CARD scoring system, the implementation of which cannot succeed without receiving hospital understanding. Furthermore, in the Nagasaki region, 12-lead ECG training courses have been conducted for EMTs, leading to a significant improvement in their ability to interpret ECGs [13, 14]. Therefore, in the present study, data on ECG findings interpreted by EMTs were used directly. Moving forward, it is essential to continue raising awareness among EMTs and various medical facilities.

Limitations

First, ECG interpretation was performed by EMTs rather than cardiologists, potentially introducing discrepancies. However, given that regions without 12-lead ECG transmission systems require EMTs to interpret ECG, which represents a more realistic scenario, EMT-interpreted 12-lead ECG findings were used. Second, participants without a confirmed diagnosis were excluded when developing the model considering that such participants lacked predictor variable information, making it impossible to perform statistical imputation for missing data. However, this exclusion may have influenced the model’s results. In future work, eligible participants with confirmed diagnoses and complete predictor information will be recruited. Third, external validation of the scoring system was not performed. Nonetheless, external validation by implementing it in other regions and facilities, potentially updating the model as needed. Fourth, whether this scoring system improves outcomes could not be verified. Hence, future cluster randomized controlled trials or natural experiment design studies are needed. Fifth, the analyzed sample size in this study was determined based on the number of participants accumulated over a certain period rather than using the statistical sample size calculation recommended by TRIPOD + AI. In subsequent studies, the sample size will be calculated considering the prediction accuracy and number of variables used in the model [22, 23]. Sixth, model evaluation from a fairness perspective as described in TRIPOD + AI was not completed. Although the system was designed for use in Japan with a predominantly Japanese population, making evaluation across different ethnicities challenging, sex-based evaluation is feasible. However, the registry did not collect sex data, leaving this as a future consideration.

Conclusions

The N-CARD scoring system offers a promising tool for prehospital ACS risk stratification that could substantially improve emergency medical care through optimized transport decisions and reduced treatment delays. With further validation and refinement, this system has the potential to become an integral component of prehospital chest pain management protocols.
