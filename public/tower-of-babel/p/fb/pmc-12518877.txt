Corrected population grid data using multisource high precision data
Authors: Shuang Wang, Chun Dong, Rong Zhao, Yu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPopulation grid data provide a fundamental scientific basis for ecological planning and management, environmental monitoring and assessment, ecological early warning, and emergency response. Traditional population allocation methods based on administrative units frequently introduce cross-scale errors, and current population grid datasets require enhancement regarding regional fine-grained characteristics and population distribution. This study presents a methodology to correct public population grid data utilizing Multisource high precision data at the grid scale. WorldPop serves as the baseline data for correction. The original data undergoes enhancement through a new model, incorporating Multisource high precision auxiliary data. The results indicate that compared to the original data, the RMSE of the corrected population grid data decreased by more than seven times, with an R2 of 0.997, demonstrating substantial improvement in data quality.Keywords: Corrected population, Population grid, WorldPop, Multisource data, High precision geographic, Public population gridSubject terms: Ecology, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

As a critical indicator for assessing the environmental impact of human activity, population grid data exhibits strong interactions with the ecological environment1,2. High-quality population grid data has become increasingly vital given the severe challenges of global climate change and the pressing need for sustainable development goals. It represents an essential information foundation for achieving carbon neutrality, carbon peak, and sustainable development objectives3. Additionally, it proves indispensable in various domains, including environmental monitoring and assessment, resource allocation, and disaster management4–6. Consequently, the demand for high-quality population grid data has become particularly urgent.

The population grid technology fundamentally integrates traditional demographic and Multisource data. Macro-statistical data are refined to fine-scale grid units7–9 through data mining and model construction technology. While this approach aims to spatially refine low-resolution demographic data to improve spatial accuracy10it inherently contains cross-scale errors. Current population gridding research primarily relies on Multisource auxiliary data and complex model algorithms to generate high precision population grid data11–13. However, this approach often involves high production costs, extended production cycles, and complex operational processes. Consequently, most high precision population grid data are not freely accessible, limiting their universality and practicality, which partially impedes comprehensive implementation in scientific research and practical applications.

The publicly available population grid data on a global scale can effectively address the above challenges. Currently, there are five main types of free accessible global population grid datasets: the Gridded Population of the World (GPW) data set is the earliest population grid data covering the world, with a spatial resolution of 30 arc seconds (about 1 km at the equator), a period of 2000–2020, and an interval of 5 years14; LandScan data set is produced and updated by the Oak Ridge National Laboratory of the United States Department of Energy15. The spatial resolution is 30 arc seconds (about 1 km at the equator), and the period is from 2000 to 2022. United Nations Environment Programme/Global Resource Information Database (UNEP/GRID) is a research project established by the United Nations Environment Programme16. The spatial resolution is about 5 km, and the temporal resolution varies from region to region; GHS-POP is a population grid dataset developed by the Joint Research Centre of the European Commission in cooperation with Columbia University in the United States17. The spatial resolution is 100 m and 1 km, and the period is 1975 to 2020 (predicted to 2030); the University of Southampton released the WorldPop dataset with spatial resolutions of 100 m and 1 km, and the period was from 2000 to 202018. These datasets have their characteristics, such as WorldPop, which has shown broad application potential in many fields with its excellent accuracy and resolution19. Lin Danchun et al.20 compared WorldPop, GPW v4, and two Chinese kilometer-grid population datasets, and found that WorldPop had the highest overall accuracy, especially in densely populated areas. Jouego et al.21 modeled based on WorldPop data to map and estimate diagnostic gaps in regions with low tuberculosis notification in Cameroon. Zhao et al.22 refined the spatial distribution of population data based on WorldPop data to generate high precision population grid data. Nethery Rachel C. et al.23 used WorldPop data to estimate real-time disease incidence rates in the Census Bureau.

Spatial population distribution methodologies currently face dual challenges. First, pronounced error propagation persists due to discrepancies in statistical units and scale effects during cross-scale modeling processes. Second, existing approaches fail to fully exploit the intrinsic value of publicly available spatial population datasets. These limitations become particularly acute when analyzing regions with underdeveloped statistical infrastructures, where the accuracy and reliability of spatial population products are further compromised by three persistent issues: inconsistent national census standards, low-accuracy ancillary data, delayed data updates in local statistical systems. This investigation presents a methodology for enhancing publicly available spatial population distribution data through the integration of Multisource high precision geographic data, utilizing the open-source WorldPop dataset as a case study. The proposed method employs grid-based units to correct WorldPop datasets through an updating model. By analyzing correlations between multi-dimensional high precision datasets (DEM, water, POI, building) and population spatial distribution, this framework determines weight coefficients of influencing factors and performs zonal correction on spatial population distribution data. Compared to existing approaches, the proposed method not only effectively resolves the persistent challenge of cross-scale error propagation and maximizes the utilization value of open datasets to achieve significant improvements in both precision and reliability, but also substantially reduces redundant data collection efforts. The methodology demonstrates remarkable cost-efficiency in data production by decreasing both temporal and financial expenditures, thereby establishing a novel and reliable pathway for the precise acquisition and efficient utilization of global spatial population distribution data.

Study area and data

Study area

This study takes Yimen County, Yuxi City, Yunnan Province, China (24°27’ − 24°57’ N, 101°54’ − 102°18’ E) as the research area. Yimen County is selected as the study area for the study of the corrected population grid data method, mainly considering the following points: First, it lies within the Yuanjiang Red River Basin at the junction of Kunming, Yuxi, Chuxiong, and adjacent counties, serving as a critical node for regional transportation and economic activities. Second, its highly diverse topography—ranging from mountainous zones with dispersed settlements to valley areas with concentrated yet capacity-constrained populations—poses significant challenges to the accuracy of population grid data. This complexity establishes the county as an exemplary region for studying population-terrain relationships. Third, substantial variations in township-level population density (Fig. 1) present distinctive challenges for population distribution research.

Modeling and methodology

The publicly available WorldPop population grid dataset exhibits significant discrepancies between the updated model corrections and demographic data in the study area. Through analysis of correlations between Multisource high precision data and grid population, the methodology selects correction factors strongly related to population grid by eliminating water grids and comprehensively evaluating factors including elevation, slope, POI, and building construction. To validate CPD accuracy, this study implements multiple accuracy evaluation indicators and conducts visual comparative analysis with other datasets to assess the modified and finely divided population grid data.

Flowcharts

The methodology comprises four primary stages: update, spatial analysis, correction, and verification. Figure 2 illustrates the overall technical approach of this study.

Flowchart of the population grid data correction method.

During the data update stage, the updated model rapidly refreshes the WorldPop data in the study area to ensure data timeliness and accuracy.

In the spatial analysis phase, the WorldPop grid population undergoes analysis, and the influence weight values of grid elevation and slope on population distribution are calculated through clustering-type partition.

During the data correction phase, water area grid cells were eliminated based on water area data. Building data underwent reclassification according to building height, and residential density weights for different housing types were calculated using the ordinary square method. POI data was processed and categorized to examine correlations between population and POI types. POI categories showing strong correlation with population distribution were identified as influential factors. Kernel density analysis was applied to these highly correlated POI types to determine their weighted impact on population distribution.

The experimental results were validated through both quantitative and visual analytical methods during the accuracy verification phase.

Population grid update model

The Population Grid Update Model refers to a method for rapidly updating population distribution when administrative unit population data changes within an existing gridded population data system. This approach leverages spatial relationships between original grid data and geographic features. Based on the spatial correspondence between administrative units and grid cells, it adopts a regional processing strategy that integrates the advantages of both the Simple Update Model and the Environmental Factor Area Model. Two distinct scenarios govern the relationship between administrative unit populations and grid cell populations: (1)Grid cells are completely contained within administrative unit boundaries. (2)Grid cells intersecting administrative unit boundaries24,25.

Simple update model

For Scenario (1) (grid cells not intersecting boundaries), population updates employ the Simple Update Model. Each grid cell’s population maintains a fixed proportional relationship to the total population. This model updates grid populations independently of environmental factors, offering operational simplicity suitable for global grid updates but inadequate for boundary-intersecting cells. Weight determination

Elevation/slope weight

Population distribution exhibits strong correlation with elevation and slope, both significantly influencing settlement pattern25. Elevation and slope data indicate topographical influences on population distribution. In mountainous or hilly regions, population distribution is constrained by terrain conditions, which can be identified and adjusted using elevation and slope data. Population density typically decreases with increasing altitude. Areas with steep slopes present challenging living conditions and limited agricultural and transportation capabilities, resulting in sparse population distribution. Conversely, regions with gentle slopes provide favorable living conditions conducive to human habitation and various economic activities, leading to higher population concentration. Through spatial clustering analysis of population data from each grid in Yimen County, population aggregation grids were identified to determine high-high clustering grids, those exhibiting high attribute values both individually and in surrounding neighboring grids. These grids were utilized to identify areas with high population density and favorable topographic conditions, thereby determining elevation and slope impacts on population distribution. Building weight

Building data provides comprehensive information about residential environments, including building typology, density, height, and spatial distribution. Building characteristics demonstrate strong multi-dimensional and multi-scale correlations with population distribution, establishing them as essential explanatory variables in population spatialization modeling, as they directly reflect residential preferences and living patterns26. This study refers to the detailed criteria for classifying housing buildings in the Geographical Conditions Census Contents and Indicators27. The study incorporates area-specific housing characteristics to establish a refined building classification system. Residential structures are categorized into three types: multi-story and higher buildings, low-rise buildings, and abandoned buildings. The multi-story and higher category encompasses high-density multi-story complexes, independent multi-story structures, mid-high-rise buildings, high-rise buildings, super-high-rise buildings, and low-density multi-story areas. The low-rise category includes high-density and low-density low-rise areas, standard low-rise zones, and independent low-rise structures. Abandoned buildings primarily consist of contiguous rural structures that have become uninhabited due to population migration and have lost their residential function through prolonged vacancy and maintenance neglect, resulting in a residential density of 0. Population correction method

The correction methodology implements precise adaptation to diverse geographical and socioeconomic conditions through differentiated data filtering and dynamic parameter optimization. In mountainous and plateau regions, the method emphasizes topographic factor weights (elevation, slope) to quantify terrain-based population constraints while excluding uninhabitable zones. For urbanized areas, the approach focuses on POI data and building classification, determining residential population density through building function analysis and POI-density correlations. Water bodies are excluded from consideration. Through analysis of relationships between topography, POI distribution, residential density, and population patterns, the method establishes a population correction weight layer, enabling parameter optimization across varying geographical and socioeconomic contexts.

The methodology establishes a population spatialization research framework suitable for global complex geographical and socioeconomic environments through comprehensive integration of model construction and adaptive mechanisms. The zonal update model’s modular design utilizes the relationship between administrative units and grid unit populations, implementing proportional methods and environmental factor ratio methods for cross-scale error correction across different zones. The dynamic factor weight adjustment module enables flexible weight configuration for key influencing factors (e.g., topography, POI, buildings) according to regional characteristics. The data adaptation interface incorporates Multisource data compatibility, effectively combining high-resolution remote sensing imagery with low-precision open-source data to address regional disparities in data acquisition capacity.

These integrated design components enable the method to produce high precision population spatial distribution data in regions characterized by limited data infrastructure, complex geographical environments, and substantial socioeconomic development disparities. This approach provides a standardized framework for global sustainable development assessment, offering significant scientific value and practical applications in cross-regional human-environment relationship research.

Precision verification method

This study employs three accuracy evaluation indices to assess model performance: Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), and Coefficient of Determination (R2. MRE represents the relative difference between predicted and actual values, RMSE indicates the deviation between expected and actual values, and R2 measures the estimation results’ goodness of fit. A MAPE value of 0% indicates optimal model performance (perfect model), while values exceeding 100% indicate poor model performance (inferior model). Population distribution data accuracy verification indicators lack absolute “good or bad” standards; their appropriateness should be evaluated holistically considering research objectives and data characteristics. These indicators demonstrate notable scale dependence: MAPE tolerances range from ± 20% at global scales to ± 15% for urban scales, and ± 10% for building-scale analyses; RMSE constraints vary by data magnitude and resolution, with acceptable ranges of < 50 persons/km2 globally, < 20 persons/km2 in urban areas, and < 10 persons/km2 at building scales; R2 requirements differ by discipline, with > 0.3 supporting social science trend analysis, > 0.6 necessary for urban modeling, and > 0.8 required for high precision applications.

Accuracy verification and analysis

To comprehensively evaluate CPD accuracy, verification procedures were conducted at both county and township levels. Calculations and comparative analyses reveal that WorldPop’s RMSE value was 7763.5015, while CPD’s RMSE value was 1174.1334. The corrected data’s root mean square error decreased by more than sevenfold, achieving a fitting degree of 0.997, as illustrated in Fig. 8(a). Township-level administrative unit verification was performed for more comprehensive accuracy assessment. As shown in Fig. 8(b), CPD demonstrates lower relative error values compared to WorldPop across all townships. Overall, CPD exhibits superior accuracy to WorldPop, validating the effectiveness and advantages of this study’s modified population grid data methodology.

Errors of CPD and WorldPop at different scales. (a) and (b) represent the county and the township scale, respectively.

The significant optimization of CPD over WorldPop is primarily attributed to three improvements: First, the deep integration of Multisource data, including 12.5 m resolution DEM, 21 categories of POI data, and building height vector data. By reclassifying building types into multi-story residences, low-rise residences, and abandoned buildings, the study achieves a refined estimation of residential density. Second, the application of a zonal update model: a simple proportional model for grids without administrative boundaries, and an environmental factor area ratio model incorporating administrative polygon features for boundary-intersecting grids, effectively addressing cross-scale errors in traditional methods. Third, dynamic adjustment of factor weights: POI, elevation, and slope weights are adjusted according to population density to accurately capture population aggregation hotspots.

In practical applications, this accuracy enhancement is of critical significance: it supports population carrying capacity assessment in high-altitude nature reserves for ecological planning, and enables robust estimation of affected populations in landslide-prone areas for disaster emergency response.

Through comparative analysis of the local grid population distribution in Fig. 9, this investigation reveals that while WorldPop partially reflects spatial characteristics of population distribution, it lacks detail and precision. Following correction, the population grid data quality demonstrates significant improvement. The revised model accurately identifies land types with minimal population activity, such as cultivated areas, forests, and grasslands, and appropriately adjusts population distribution across these land types. Specifically, the modified algorithm reduces the population density in these areas and redistributes it more accurately to adjacent residential and commercial service lands, better reflecting actual population distribution. This enhancement is evident in both the population distribution pattern and the characterization of population distribution in small areas. Furthermore, when comparing CPD with remote sensing imagery data, which directly reflects surface conditions, the analysis reveals strong consistency between CPD and remote sensing data in areas of both high and low population density, validating the effectiveness and advantages of the proposed model.

Conclusions and prospects

This study introduces an innovative multi-source high-precision data integration framework specifically designed to enhance public population grid data. With WorldPop as the baseline dataset, the core of this framework lies in a grid-scale correction mechanism that integrates high-precision auxiliary data to refine the characterization of population distribution. This mechanism effectively mitigates the cross-scale errors inherent in traditional allocation methods based on administrative units. Experimental results indicate that the proposed CPD achieves significantly higher accuracy and quality compared to other mainstream public population grid datasets, including GPW, LandScan, GHS-POP, WorldPop, and China’s kilometer-grid population datasets. These findings validate the effectiveness of the correction method in improving the accuracy and reliability of public population grid data, addressing existing limitations in spatial distribution data of public populations, and providing more precise data support for related research and practical applications.

While this study has achieved specific outcomes in population grid data correction methodology, several areas warrant further refinement and development. Future research should focus on enhancing model update algorithms and parameter optimization. Advanced technologies and algorithms can improve the efficiency and accuracy of data updates. Additionally, exploring the integration of Multisource auxiliary data with public population grid data through analysis of various dataset characteristics could lead to more precise and efficient correction methods. Comprehensive utilization of auxiliary data characteristics may enable more accurate population data extraction and high precision spatiotemporal dynamic analysis, adapting to increasingly complex data environments and application requirements.
