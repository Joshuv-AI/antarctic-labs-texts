Asthma is associated with increased Streptococcus pneumoniae carriage in the upper airway: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Raeni Dwi Putri, Chrysanti Murad, Kuswandewi Mutyara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAsthma is a chronic respiratory condition characterized by airway inflammation and hyperreactivity. Previous studies have suggested a potential association between asthma and bacterial colonization of the upper respiratory tract. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare bacterial carriage rates, specifically focusing on Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Staphylococcus aureus, among individuals with and without asthma.MethodsWe followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) in reporting this meta-analysis.ResultsA total of 619 records were initially identified, leading to the inclusion of eight studies that met predefined inclusion criteria. The pooled prevalence rates of bacterial carriage were analyzed using a fixed-effect model. The risk of bias assessment was conducted using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. A total of 5,399 subjects were included, comprising 1,572 (29.11%) individuals with asthma and 3,827 (70.88%) without asthma. The prevalence of S. pneumoniae carriage was significantly higher in individuals with asthma (30.8%, 95% CI: 19.5–42.1%) compared to those without (19.2%, 95% CI: 8.3–30%) with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.35 (95% CI: 1.10–1.66, p = 0.005; I2 = 29%). In contrast, H. influenzae carriage rates were similar between groups (18.6% in individuals with asthma vs. 21.4% with no asthma; OR = 0.93, p = 0.84; I2 = 0%). The prevalence of M. catarrhalis was low in individuals with asthma (4.7%), and the data for S. aureus was limited, showing a prevalence of 36.67% among individuals with asthma compared to 26.67% with no asthma.ConclusionThis systematic review and meta-analysis showed that asthma may increase the risk of S. pneumoniae carriage. The similar carriage rates of H. influenzae between the two groups suggest distinct mechanisms of bacterial colonization, underscoring the need to further investigate asthma’s role in predisposing individuals to specific bacterial infections.

Introduction

The upper respiratory tract is home to various commensal bacteria, which can become pathogenic under certain conditions [1,2]. Among these bacteria, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis and Staphylococcus aureus are of particular concern as they are known to colonize the nasopharynx and oropharynx and contribute to invasive diseases like pneumonia [3]. Carriage, is an important epidemiological marker as it precedes infection and plays a key role in bacterial transmission between individuals [4,5]. Pneumonia impacts people at the extremes of age, those with compromised immune systems, and individuals with chronic respiratory conditions such as asthma [6].

Asthma, which affects millions worldwide, may further exacerbate the risk of bacterial carriage and subsequent infections [6–8]. Individuals with asthma have a 90% increased risk of developing pneumonia compared to individuals without asthma [9]. Asthma-related inflammation alters the micro-epithelial environment of the airways, making individuals more susceptible to bacterial colonization [6]. The use of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), a common asthma treatment, has been associated with increased carriage of pathogenic bacteria such as S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae [10–13]. ICS suppress local immune responses and can alter the mucosal barrier in the oropharynx, making it easier for bacteria to adhere to and proliferate. This weakened defense, along with reduced mucociliary clearance, allows pathogenic bacteria to persist and potentially increases upper respiratory tract bacterial carriage [13]. However, data on the effect of asthma on the carriage of pathogenic bacteria in the upper respiratory tract are still varied. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to compare the bacterial carriage in the upper airways of individuals with and without asthma.

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [14]. The meta-analysis protocol was registered with PROSPERO on 17 September 2024 (PROSPERO ID: CRD42024587909). Comprehensive searches were conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, ScienceDirect, and Scopus, from their inception to September 2024 by three authors (RDP, CM, KM). The search was limited to English-language articles, using keywords such as ‘carriage,’ ‘upper airway,’ ‘nasopharynx,’ ‘oropharynx,’ and ‘asthma’ (Table S1). In addition to the database searches, reference lists from included studies were manually reviewed. Duplicate citations were removed, and the titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles were independently screened by RDP and CM, with any disagreements resolved through discussion with KM. The full texts of the remaining articles were then carefully examined. The screening process is visually summarized in a PRISMA flowchart, presented in Figure 1. Eligibility criteria

Studies meeting the following criteria were included. The population consisted of children and adults (all ages) from community and healthcare settings. The exposure of interest was asthma, with individuals without asthma serving as the comparator group. The primary outcome was the carriage of S. pneumoniae in the upper airway (nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal). Secondary outcomes included the carriage of other respiratory microbials in the upper airway (nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal), specifically H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, and S. aureus. Cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort studies were included. Only English-language publications were considered, and both published articles from electronic databases and unpublished studies were included. Animal studies were excluded, as well as abstracts, case reports, editorials, and letters to editors. No specific time frame was applied.

Data extraction

Two authors (RDP, KM) independently extracted data using a standardized form. The extracted data included study characteristics such as author, year of publication, country, study design, sample size, and methods of detection (e.g. culture, or polymerase chain reaction (PCR)). Subject characteristics included age, gender, asthma diagnosis criteria, severity of asthma, ICS usage and parental smoking status. The primary outcome of interest was the prevalence of S. pneumoniae carriage. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved by consensus by involving a third author (CM).

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment for the included studies was conducted using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort and case-control studies, and a modified NOS for cross-sectional studies [15]. This assessment was performed independently by two authors (RDP, CM), with any disagreements resolved by involving a third author (KM).

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis provided a summary of study characteristics and results. For the quantitative analysis, a meta-analysis was conducted if sufficient data for each outcome were available. A fixed-effect model was used when there was low (I2 = 0–40%) heterogeneity, whereas a random-effects model was applied in cases of moderate (I2 = 41–75%) to high (I2 = 76–100%) heterogeneity. Pooled prevalence estimates of S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, and M. catarrhalis carriage among individuals with asthma were calculated using 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The comparison of carriage prevalence between individuals with and without asthma was expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Additional analyses were performed based on age group, detection methods, and site of sampling. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots.

Results

Characteristics of studies

As shown in Figure 1, our search initially identified 619 records, of which 150 were duplicates. After reviewing the titles and abstracts, 469 articles were found to be irrelevant and excluded. The remaining 17 articles underwent a detailed screening process. Additionally, one study was identified through citation searching. Following evaluation against the inclusion and exclusion criteria, eight studies [10,16–22] were included in this systematic review. Reasons for exclusion included overlapping study data (n = 1), different populations (n = 3), different outcomes (n = 3), insufficient outcome data (n = 1), and different sources for outcome samples (n = 2). In final, a total of eight studies with 5,399 subjects were included in this study, among whom 1,572 (29.11%) were individuals with asthma, while the remaining 3,827 individuals without asthma (70.88%). S. pneumoniae carriage

The prevalence of Streptococcus pneumoniae carriage among individuals with asthma was 30.8% (95% CI, 19.5–42.1%), compared to 19.2% (95% CI, 8.3–30.0%) among individuals without asthma (Figure 2A,B). The difference in the pooled rate of S. pneumoniae carriage between the two groups resulted in an OR of 1.35 (95% CI, 1.10–1.66), p = 0.005; I2 = 29%, indicating that the difference was statistically significant (Figure 2C). However, these data are predominantly derived from pediatric populations. Figure 2.S. pneumoniae carriage prevalence in children and adults population among (A) individuals with asthma, (B) individuals without asthma, (C) comparison of S. pneumoniae carriage between individuals with and without asthma by subgrouping children and adults population.

S. pneumoniae carriage prevalence in children and adults population among (A) individuals with asthma, (B) individuals without asthma, (C) comparison of S. pneumoniae carriage between individuals with and without asthma by subgrouping children and adults population.

Of the eight included studies, seven focused on children (< 18 years), with four providing comparative carriage data between asthma and no-asthma groups. The remaining one study involved adults (≥18 years) and also reported carriage prevalence in both groups. To address the potential confounding effect of age, we conducted separate analyses for children and adult populations. Among children, the pooled prevalence of S. pneumoniae carriage was 34.3% (95% CI: 19.5–45.1%) in individuals with asthma and 23.3% (95% CI: 12.0–34.6%) in those without asthma, as shown in Figure 3. In the adult population, based on a single study involving military recruits (n = 224 individuals with asthma), the carriage prevalence was 8.5% (19/224), compared to 4.3% (29/668) in individuals without asthma (Table 4). An age group-stratified analysis comparing children and adult populations (Figure 2C) showed that the increased S. pneumoniae carriage among individuals with asthma remained statistically significant in the children subgroup with an OR of 1.26 (95% CI, 1.01–1.57), p = 0.004; I2 = 0%. Culture was used as the detection method in four studies, while one study used polymerase chain reaction (PCR). In an additional analysis focused on studies using only culture detection, we still observed a significant difference in the pooled rate of S. pneumoniae carriage between individuals with and without asthma with an OR of 1.35 (95% CI, 1.10–1.67), p = 0.004; I2 = 46% (Figure S1).

In nasopharyngeal samples (four pediatric studies), individuals with asthma had higher S. pneumoniae carriage than those without asthma (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.01–1.57; p = 0.04; I2 = 0%). In oropharyngeal samples (one adult study), the association was (OR 2.49 (95% CI 1.34–4.63); p = 0.04). The test for subgroup differences was p = 0.04. A sensitivity analysis restricted to nasopharyngeal sampling yielded the same pooled effect (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.01–1.57) (Fig. S1). Culture-based studies showed a significant association (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.10–1.67; I2 = 46%), whereas the single PCR study was inconclusive (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.15–6.85); there was no evidence of a difference by method (p = 0.76). Data on ICS were inconsistently reported; only Zhang et al. (2013) reported ICS-stratified carriage (27.1% vs 8.3%; OR 4.09, 95% CI 1.74–9.58), so a pooled analysis by steroid exposure was not feasible.

Secondary outcomes

The H. influenzae carriage prevalence for individuals with and without asthma was 18.6% (95% CI, 2.2–34.9%), and 21.4% (95% CI, 16.5–59.2%), respectively (Figure 4A,B). The difference in the pooled rate of H. influenzae carriage between the two groups yielded an OR of 0.93 (95% CI, 0.49–1.79), p = 0.84; I2 = 0%, indicating that the difference was not statistically significant (Figure 4C). The M. catarrhalis carriage prevalence for individuals with asthma was 4.7% (95% CI, 7.0–24.9%); I2 = 88.35% (Figure 5). Only two studies reported on the prevalence of M. catarrhalis carriage among individuals with asthma, while only one study provided data for individuals without asthma, showing a prevalence of 3%. For S. aureus carriage, only one study was available, which found a prevalence of 36.67% among individuals with asthma and 26.67% among individuals without asthma (Table 4). Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of bacterial carriage, particularly S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, and S. aureus, in individuals with and without asthma. Our analysis revealed a statistically significant higher rate of S. pneumoniae carriage among individuals with asthma compared to individuals without asthma. This finding highlights the potential for increased susceptibility to respiratory infections in individuals with asthma, which aligns with existing literature that indicates a greater burden of infections in this population [6,9,23,24].

The significantly higher carriage rate of S. pneumoniae among individuals with asthma may involve the altered immune response and airway inflammation characteristic of asthma, which could create a more favorable environment for S. pneumoniae colonization [6,25–28]. Asthma is associated with chronic airway inflammation, which may disrupt the epithelial barrier and change the local immune landscape [29–31]. S. pneumoniae exploits the Th2-skewed immune environment and epithelial disruption characteristic of asthma to establish colonization [30,32]. This altered environment can enhance the susceptibility of individuals with asthma to respiratory pathogens like S. pneumoniae, potentially leading to increased colonization rates.

A key consideration when interpreting these findings is the age distribution of the included populations. Seven of the eight studies in this meta-analysis focused exclusively on children, while only one study involved adult participants. This pediatric predominance means that the overall estimate of S. pneumoniae carriage primarily reflects pediatric data and cannot be directly generalized to adults. The single adult study was conducted among young male military recruits with mild asthma, and individuals with severe asthma were excluded. Previous studies have shown that pneumococcal carriage rates decrease with age [4,33], which further supports the need for caution in applying these results to older populations.

In contrast, the lack of significant difference in H. influenzae carriage between the two groups suggests that H. influenzae might function more as a common colonizer of the upper respiratory tract [34], independent of asthma status. Unlike S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae may not engage in the same pathogenic mechanisms that could promote its increased colonization in individuals with asthma [35–37]. The differences in the pathogenic potential of S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae are shaped by their distinct biological characteristics, interactions with the host immune system, and their behavior within the airways of individuals with asthma. S. pneumoniae, a Gram-positive encapsulated bacterium, relies on its polysaccharide capsule to evade immune responses and uses pneumolysin to damage epithelial barriers, facilitating colonization and potential invasion [38]. In individuals with asthma, the chronic airway inflammation, disrupted epithelial integrity, and altered immune responses create a favorable environment for S. pneumoniae colonization [39]. Conversely, H. influenzae, a Gram-negative bacterium, particularly in its non-typeable form (NTHi), adapts to the airway environment through biofilm formation and localized immune evasion strategies. It has the ability to persist and replicate intracellularly. It predominantly localizes within large macrophage-like mononuclear cells in the subepithelial layer and, to a lesser extent, within the reticular crypt epithelium [37]. H. influenzae might thrive in the inflamed airway, but its pathogenic mechanisms may be less influenced by asthma-specific changes, potentially functioning more as a chronic colonizer than an invasive pathogen.

Environmental factors may also contribute to these findings. Shared exposures to allergens, pollutants, and other respiratory irritants could affect both individuals with and without asthma, complicating the relationship between asthma and bacterial carriage [40–43]. The prevalence of M. catarrhalis was notably low among individuals with asthma at 4.7%, with high heterogeneity (I2 = 88.35%) observed across studies. This variability may stem from differences in study populations, methodologies, and geographic locations. Additionally, the limited data for individuals without asthma further restricts comparative analysis, highlighting the need for more extensive research to clarify the relationship between asthma and M. catarrhalis carriage.

Lastly, the solitary study on S. aureus found a higher carriage prevalence of 36.67% among individuals with asthma compared to 26.67% among individuals without asthma. While this finding suggests a potential association between asthma and S. aureus carriage, the reliance on a single study underscores the necessity for further research to confirm these results.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis assessing the bacterial carriage among individuals with asthma compared to those without asthma. The analysis revealed low heterogeneity in the carriage comparisons for S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae, indicating consistent findings across studies and reinforcing the reliability of these results. Additionally, the inclusion of diverse study designs – cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional – provides a well-rounded perspective on bacterial carriage prevalence. A key distinction is that our analysis addresses colonization rather than infection. For example, Kama et al. [16] investigated pharyngeal colonization during acute asthma exacerbations, reporting both bacterial carriage and clinical variables. In our review, only the colonization data were extracted and synthesized, and the results should therefore be interpreted strictly in the context of bacterial carriage.

This review has important limitations. First, potential confounding by ICS usage could not be addressed adequately: only one study reported ICS-stratified carriage, precluding a pooled analysis and leaving residual confounding likely. Second, the evidence base is age-imbalanced (seven pediatric studies, one adult study), limiting generalizability to adults despite our age-stratified reporting. Third, sampling site and detection method varied: subgroup analyses suggested differences between nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal sampling and most data were culture-based, with only one small PCR study, these factors may influence sensitivity and effect sizes. Fourth, the heterogeneity of the included studies particularly for M. catarrhalis, limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding its carriage prevalence in individuals with asthma. Our review also assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using the NOS. The scores indicated varying levels of bias, with most studies exhibiting low to moderate bias. This variability highlights the importance of considering study design and methodology when interpreting results. Future research should consider longitudinal studies to explore the causal relationships between asthma and bacterial carriage.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that S. pneumoniae carriage is significantly higher among individuals with asthma compared to those without asthma. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the pediatric-dominated dataset. These findings suggest the need for targeted prevention strategies, such as vaccination against S. pneumoniae, particularly in high-risk groups, including those with asthma [44,45]. Understanding the dynamics of bacterial carriage can inform clinical strategies aimed at reducing respiratory complications in individuals with asthma and highlight areas for future research. Future studies should include broader, more representative adult cohorts and prospectively measure inhaled (and intranasal) corticosteroid exposure, capturing dose, duration, and adherence, with stratified and adjusted analyses to clarify the independent effect of corticosteroids on carriage.
