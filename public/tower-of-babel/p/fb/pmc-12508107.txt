Research on the mechanism of initial explosion electromagnetic radiation under different vacuum degrees
Authors: Yu Hao, Xuchao Pan, Hong Chen, Hancheng Wang, Wei Du, Yuanpei Meng, Junjie Jiao, Zhong Fang, Yong He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExplosion electromagnetic radiation (EEMR), as an accompanying phenomenon during the explosion processes, has attracted widespread academic attention. However, a specific theoretical model characterizing its generation mechanisms remains unestablished. Addressing this gap, this study developed a theoretical model for atmospheric environments through integrated theoretical and experimental approaches, innovatively constructing research encompassing three core elements: (1) A customized EEMR testing platform with controllable vacuum conditions; (2) An advanced signal processing algorithm integrating signal denoising with electric field strength reconstruction; (3) A theoretical model linking EEMR with detonation transmission. The results indicate: The initial EEMR originates from the process in which the detonation wave transmits into the air. calculated electric field strength decreased from 0.72 V/m to 0.45 V/m as vacuum degrees increased from 0 to 90% (maximum prediction deviation: 4.3%). The established model quantitatively correlates EEMR with key shockwave parameters (temperature, pressure, density, thickness, velocity etc.), revealing significant correlations between measurement results and calculation results (R2 > 0.99). This work presents the first physics-based explanation model for EEMR analysis, providing crucial theoretical support for explosion diagnostics and energetic material characterization.Keywords: Explosion, Electromagnetic radiation, Explosives, Vacuum degreeSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Explosion electromagnetic radiation (EEMR) is an accompanying phenomenon in explosion processes. Since Kolsky1 reported the phenomenon of electromagnetic radiation noise generated by explosions, it has attracted wide attention. Many researchers have conducted experimental studies, varying explosion conditions2–5 and analyzing the peak strength6,7, spectral distribution3,8, and other aspects9–11 of EEMR from multiple perspectives. Furthermore, some scholars have explored the application of the EEMR phenomenon in ore extraction12 and the aerospace pyrotechnic industry13, used for the detection of explosion events and electromagnetic compatibility design. Given the strong correlation between EEMR and the explosion process, research on EEMR can significantly advance the study of the physical changes and chemical reactions during explosions and expand its potential applications. Thus, investigating the generation mechanism of EEMR is highly necessary.

Based on experimental results, many researchers have proposed different mechanisms underlying the generation of EEMR. From the perspective of experimental phenomenon, G. P. Chambers et al.14 suggested that EEMR originates at the moment when the shock wave interacts with the explosive crystals. Bernard Hayes15 proposed that EEMR is generated at the gaps between two explosive charges. Huilan Ren et al.16 suggested that the three stages of EEMR originate from the generation of explosion plasma, the interaction of the reflected ground shock wave with the forward shock wave, and the arrival of the shock wave at the measurement equipment. Lijun Zhao et al.17 proposed that EEMR originates from the generation of explosion plasma; From the perspective of macroscopic charge distribution, many researchers have suggested that EEMR generated by detonation plasma asymmetry forming a non-steady capacitor-like structure1,12,18–28, with some using these hypotheses to calculate the radiation produced by the flowing detonation plasma4,5,29–31. These calculations are suitable for the diffusion processes of the detonation products on the microsecond to millisecond scale; From the perspective of charged particle motion, researchers have proposed that EEMR generated by molecular ionization and neutralization32, bremsstrahlung33,34, and the acceleration of charged particles6,11, as well as their motion within the geomagnetic field5. Calculations have also been conducted to model dipole radiation produced by charged particles as they cross the shock wave front35 or impact the ground7,11,36. These calculations effectively describe the radiation of single particles. However, these studies often do not distinguish the origins of EEMR and associate it with the specific physical processes.

Given the current situation of research, this study aims to investigate the generation mechanism of EEMR. First, an EEMR measurement system is constructed, and by varying the degree of vacuum, we alter shockwave parameters such as pressure, temperature, and density during the explosion process. Corresponding EEMR data are then measured and using a method of denoising and reconstruction to obtain the electric field strength of the EEMR. Next, the experimental results are analyzed to identify the specific physical processes related with generating EEMR. A simplified physical model is subsequently developed to compute the expectation, which are then compared with the measurement results to verify the accuracy of the proposed model. EEMR experiments

Experimental arrangements

Figure 2 illustrates the schematic of the EEMR measurement system, which consists of three core components: the initiation trigger system (depicted by the orange line), the optical-electrical measurement system (represented by the green line), and the negative pressure generation system (indicated by the blue line).

Layout diagram of the EEMR experiment.

The initiation trigger system includes a blaster (FD100), a high-voltage probe (Tektronix P6015A), a signal generator (Tektronix AFG1062), an initiation line, and additional components. The operational principles of this system are detailed in Fig. 3. It primarily facilitates the synchronization of the initiation process with the commencement of the EEMR experiment. During operation, the device latencies ( and ) are maintained below 50 ns, while the transmission time of the electrical signal across a cable shorter than 60 m does not exceed 300 ns. The initiation line, approximately 100 m in length, exhibits an initiation delay of less than 500 ns. The time difference between the high-voltage pulse produced by the blaster and the trigger zero point of the detonator is under 1 µs, which is compatible with the minimum temporal resolution of 1.3 µs for the high-speed cameras utilized in the experiment.

Schematic of the Operating Principle of the Initiation Trigger System.

The optical-electrical measurement system comprises high-speed cameras (Phantom VEO410L and TMX6410), antennas, oscilloscopes (Tektronix 4104 C and Tektronix 64B), and associated instrumentation. Electromagnetic signals are acquired using oscilloscopes with a bandwidth of 1 GHz, and a trigger delay of less than 10 ns. The high-speed cameras operate at frame rates of 150,000 frames per second (fps) and 750,000 fps, corresponding to temporal resolutions of ± 6.67 µs and ± 1.33 µs, respectively. Correlation between the optical frames and the electromagnetic waveforms allows precise mapping of the explosion’s temporal evolution to distinct stages of Electromagnetic Emission Radiation (EEMR) events. The negative pressure generation system comprises a vacuum tank, a vacuum pump (Feiyue V-i280SV), and auxiliary components, designed to achieve a controlled vacuum environment with a pressure range between 0% and 90% of atmospheric pressure. The vacuum tank is a PMMA cylinder with a diameter of 500 mm, a height greater than 500 mm, and a wall thickness of 10 mm.

Standard detonators are the main explosives in this paper. During the explosion process, the bridge wire, lead azide, and RDX are sequentially detonated, with RDX being the main explosive with a mass of approximately 500 mg and a diameter of 7 mm. The cylindrical explosives are aligned vertically to ensure radial symmetry in the distribution of detonation products, shock waves, and the EEMR. Three antennas are positioned equidistantly in a circular arrangement, 5 m from the detonation point. Both the explosives and antennas are positioned at a height of 1.2 m. To reduce interference during electromagnetic wave measurements, all equipment used in the experimental setup is made from non-metallic materials.

Initial EEMR signals from Φ20*20 RDX charge under negative pressure environment.

In high-speed photography, three intense reactive processes can generate plasma within the first bright frame duration: detonation wave propagation, detonation wave transmission, and air shock wave propagation. Detonation wave propagation is the process of detonation waves propagating inside explosives, while the detonation wave transmission is the process of detonation waves transmitting to the air from explosives. Analysis of the EEMR signal characteristics helps find the radiation source. The waveform reveals that the amplitude of the initial EEMR signal first increases and then decreases, so the steadily propagating detonation wave is excluded. The overall duration of the initial EEMR signal is about 0.1 µs, which can exclude shock waves with attenuation time on the order of milliseconds. The shape and transmission time of the detonation wave matches the initial EEMR signal. As the pressure of the detonation wave increases, ionization in the transmitted shock wave air increases sharply, and when the pressure decreases, the ionization decreases sharply, leading to significant changes in field strength. Consequently, the pulse width of the initial EEMR signal is smaller than that of the detonation wave. Additionally, the radiation from the side surface tends to cancel each other out during far-field EEMR measurements. During the detonation process, the detonation wave expands outward through a circular cross-section at each time instant. Although this expansion is not perfectly symmetric due to the complexity of detonation, it remains approximately centrosymmetric. As a result, the EMR generated from opposite directions tends to be cancelled out in the far field, leading to no observable signal in the measurements. Based on the similarity of waveforms, time scales and physical processes, this study suggests that the initial EEMR primarily originates from the accelerated motion of charged particles during the detonation wave transmission of charge end surface.

Data processing methods

The time-domain signal from the optical-electrical measurement system is transformed into the E-field strength using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and the Friis transmission equation. First, the signal is transformed into a complex frequency-domain signal via FFT. Then, the frequency-domain signal is converted into the frequency-domain E-field amplitude considering the antenna factor (AF), impedance matching loss, polarization matching loss, and cable transmission loss. The E-field strength in the time-domain is then derived by performing an inverse FFT (IFFT) on the frequency-domain amplitude. The AF is calibrated and interpolated to match the frequency axis of the FFT results. The impedance matching loss was calculated using the S11 parameters from a vector network analyzer (VNA). The cable transmission loss is calculated using the S21 parameters from the VNA. The EEMR measurements are conducted using vertically and horizontally polarized biconical antennas. The equivalent AR of the EEMR is calibrated against the peak value of the experiment results, which is crucial for determining polarization matching loss. Radiation process and calculation

Based on the analysis in Sect. 3, this study makes three simplified assumptions about the detonation wave transmission process: the material at the shock wave front is uniformly distributed, the gas is in thermal equilibrium after the shock wave, and the particles directly reach a stable equilibrium state. Based on the above assumptions, this study first calculates parameters of detonation and shock wave, then obtains the ionization parameters of shock wave and radiation parameters and finally compares the calculation results with measurement results. This calculation process is shown in Fig. 12. It is also worth noting that the detonation wave transmits gradually into the air over a period of approximately 0.1 µs, which corresponds to the entire initial EEMR waveform. The calculations presented in the following text, however, are only intended to model the radiation generated by the peak of the detonation wave, that is, only the peak point of the EEMR waveform.

As the vacuum degree increases from 0 to 90%, the expected electromagnetic field strength radiated by a single particle decreases slightly due to differences in velocity distribution, reducing from 2.044 × 10−9 to 2.035 × 10−9, corresponding to a variation of merely 0.44%. Simultaneously, the shockwave front temperature increases, and the density decreases, resulting in the overall increase in the degree of ionization. However, due to the reduction in effective volume, the total ion number density decreases, leading to a reduction in the total number of particles contributing to radiation, from 3.54 × 108 to 2.21 × 108. Consequently, the overall expected electric field strength decreases from 0.72 V/m to 0.45 V/m. As observed from Fig. 18, the trend of the expected electric field strength gradually becomes smooth as the vacuum degree increases from 0 to 80% but exhibits a rapid decline beyond 80%. Compared with the previous measurement results, the relative deviations are under 4.3%, and the coefficient of determination is more than 0.99, indicating a high level of accuracy.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a measurement system for measuring explosion electromagnetic radiation (EEMR) under different vacuum degrees. A method of denoising and construction was used to obtain the electric field strength. Measurement results demonstrate that as the vacuum degree increases from 0 to 90%, there is a corresponding decline in EEMR peak strength from 0.75 V/m to 0.45 V/m.

Through a comparative analysis of high-speed photography results and EEMR waveforms, we suggested that the initial EEMR primarily originates from the process of detonation wave transmitting into air. In this process, air particles are ionized and accelerated. The incoherent radiation emitted by electrons is difficult to detect, while the coherent radiation from positive ions serves as the main source of the initial EEMR. Based on this, we developed a simplified physical model. This model first calculated the detonation parameters and the shock wave parameters to obtain the pressure, temperature, density, velocity of particles, et al. and then calculated the degree of ionization, volume of efficient gas, collision time, and finally calculated the expected electric field strength. The results showed that the expectation decreased from 0.72 V/m to 0.45 V/m, the relative deviations with the measurement results are under 4.3%, and the coefficient of determination is more than 0.99. Thus, this model has a high level of accuracy.

This study enhances our understanding of the correlation mechanism between initial EEMR and detonation wave transmission, illustrates the influence of various intermediate physical quantities on EEMR, and establishes a foundation for further advancements in both experimental and theoretical investigations of EEMR. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the experiment used only one type of explosive component, and the model’s accuracy was not validated by testing with different components. Secondly, the theoretical model incorporates certain equilibrium assumptions, which may introduce errors into the results. Despite these limitations, the study provides a valuable starting point for future research in this area.
