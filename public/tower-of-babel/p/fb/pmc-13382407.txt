Distal nerve transfers for median nerve reinnervation and hand function restoration
Authors: Jovan Grujić, Andrija Savić, Svetozar Stanković, Aleksandar Djurdjević, Aleksa Mićić, Dragoslav Nestorović, Aleksandra Stojiljković, Milan Lepić, Nenad Novaković, Lukas Rasulić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionProximal and extensive median nerve median nerve injuries cause profound disability, impairing daily activities and quality of life. Outcomes after autologous nerve grafting are often suboptimal, due to long regeneration distances and complex branching. Distal nerve transfers offer a more direct reconstructive approach.Research questionMay combined distal nerve transfers (ECRB-AIN, SUP-FDS, ADM-TE, and DDN-PDN) restore hand function and quality of life in patients with proximal or extensive median nerve injuries?Material and methodsA retrospective series of 13 patients (9 males, 4 females; mean age 35 years (17–62)) who underwent distal nerve transfer procedure(s) between 2011 and 2022 for proximal or extensive median nerve injury. All patients were followed for at least 2 years with standardized pre- and postoperative assessment of motor function, grasp and pinch strength, thumb opposition (Kapandji score), and quality of life (PNSQoL).ResultsAll patients initially presented with absent finger flexion (M0), of which: 11 achieved M4 recovery, while one reached M2 and M1. Grasp strength improved from 22.8% (0–45%) to 62.8% (13–87%), pinch strength from 27.5% (0–43%) to 62.0% (20–90%), Kapandji score from 4.2 (1–6) to 7.5 (2–10), and PNSQoL from 41.3 (30–47) to 67.4 (45–79). All improvements were statistically significant (p < 0.01).Discussion and conclusionDistal nerve transfers provided consistent functional recovery, supporting their role as a primary reconstructive strategy in proximal median nerve injuries. Combined motor and sensory transfers yielded meaningful improvements in grasp, pinch, thumb opposition, and quality of life.

Introduction

Injuries to the median nerve (MN) with sequential hand dysfunction significantly impair patient's quality of life (QoL), compromising basic everyday activities like feeding, housework, and personal hygiene maintenance. Such injuries usually affect the working-age population, inducing job losses, causing significant individual and societal handicap (Bailey et al., 2009; Chemnitz et al., 2013; Jaquet et al., 2001; Stonner et al., 2017; Wiman et al., 2022; Tapp et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2022; Bertelli et al., 2016).

In proximal and extensive MN lesions autologous nerve grafting yields inconsistent outcomes, often resulting in insufficient functional recovery for many patients (Kim et al., 2001; Roganovic, 2005). Poor results are mostly due to the long distance that regenerating fibers need to pass to the effector muscles and their dispersion caused by the extensive MN branching in the proximal third of the forearm (Caetano et al., 2020). In such cases, indirect repair with the use of distal nerve transfer techniques remains a viable option, more favorable than the commonly used muscle-tendon transfer procedures (Bertelli et al., 2024a; Brown and Mackinnon, 2008).

Due to the rarity of the lesion, and a variety of treatment options, only a limited number of papers with a limited number of cases are available. Regarding the possible distal nerve transfer options for MN reinnervation, various donor-to-recipient combinations have been attempted, generally yielding satisfactory functional outcomes, although still lacking confirmation within larger cohorts (Bertelli, 2015; Socolovsky et al., 2011; Bertelli and Ghizoni, 2011; Zhao et al., 2004; Medina, 2022; Garg et al., 2022). To date, there is no consensus on the best or the most effective donor-to-recipient combination, optimal timing for reinnervation, or outcome prognosis.

This series evaluates functional outcomes and QoL in patients treated for proximal or extensive MN injuries and lesions using a combination of distal nerve transfer procedures including: (1) extensor carpi radialis brevis to anterior interosseous nerve (ECRB-AIN), (2) supinator to flexor digitorum superficialis (SUP-FDS), (3) abductor digiti minimi to thenar eminence (ADM-TE), and (4) dorsal digital nerve to palmar digital nerve (DDN-PDN) transfers, for hand function restoration.

All 13 patients included in this series underwent distal nerve transfer(s) for MN reinnervation. Associated injuries were present in 6 patients, including: 5 patients with vascular injury requiring emergency vascular reconstruction at the time of initial presentation, 1 patient with humerus fracture managed with appropriate emergency osteosynthesis, and 2 patients with associated ulnar nerve (UN) injury, when ADM-TE transfer was not performed.

Preoperative evaluation

The preoperative evaluation consisted of a thorough patient history, neurological examination (thumb and index flexion strength, grasping and pinch strength, and the degree of thumb opposition), and QoL assessment. Nerve lesions were confirmed with electrophysiology (Electromyoneurography - EMNG, Somatosensory Evoked Potentials - SSEP) and imaging studies (Ultrasonography - USG, Magnetic Resonance Imaging - MRI).

Thumb and index flexion strength

During the assessment, the seated patients were asked to extend their arm and lean it on the table in the supine position and then to flex their thumb and index finger, while the examiner applied resistance at the level of the distal finger phalanges. The assessed strength was graded using the British Medical Research Council (MRC) Muscle Strength Scale.

The grasping and pinch strength

The grasping and pinch strength were measured using ‘Jamar’ hand dynamometer (Lafayette Instrument, USA). The numerical values obtained for the injured side were divided by the numerical values obtained for the healthy side and multiplied by 100. The result was expressed as a percentage.

The degree of thumb opposition

The degree of thumb opposition was examined by asking the patients to perform maximum thumb opposition and graded using a visual method according to Kapandji (1992)

Quality of life

The quality of life was assessed using the PNSQoL questionnaire (Peripheral Nerve Surgery Quality of Life) questionnaire - an original questionnaire designed at the Clinic for Neurosurgery, University Clinical Centre of Serbia (Appendix B). The score values range from 0 to 80 with lower values indicating worse QoL.

Surgical procedure

All patients (n = 13) were treated with the use of distal nerve transfers, while additional autologous nerve grafting was performed in 11 cases, including the MN defects longer than 20 cm (n = 9) and cases associated with malignancy (n = 3). In 2 cases with associated UN injury, the ADM-TE transfer was not performed (Appendix A).

Surgical procedure for ECRB-AIN and SUP-FDS transfer

Exploration of the radial nerve (RN) and MN

Following skin incision over the marked site, the dissection and retraction of the skin and subcutaneous adipose tissue provide extensive access to the anterior compartment of the forearm. The dissection should be performed carefully to prevent damage to the medial or lateral cutaneous nerves of the forearm, or cephalic vein.

Resection of the biceps brachi muscle (BB) tendon aponeurosis provides an opening in the fascia between the anterior and lateral compartments of the forearm. Retraction of the brachioradialis muscle (BRR) laterally provides quick identification of the RN superficial branch, situated laterally, and the radial artery, situated medially. Next, the space between these two structures is entered deeply, until reaching the pronator teres (PT) tendon and its junction on the outer side of the radius.

Step lengthening of the PT tendon provides relaxation of the PT and makes it feasible for an easy retraction medially, which is necessary to access the MN branches in the proximal part of the forearm. The blunt separation of the PT from the radial artery exposes many transversely positioned arterial branches, which penetrate the PT and have to be electro-cauterized. After that, the PT is retracted medially providing the identification of the MN trunk which is then traced distally until exposing all its branches.

Identification and preparation of recipient branches (FDS branch, AIN)

Knowing the MN branching pattern in the proximal third of the forearm is necessary for the precise identification of its side branches (Fig. 2). In most cases, the first branch innervates the flexor carpi radialis muscle (FCR) and contains fibers for the palmaris longus muscle (PL), originating at the medial side of the MN. Then, a branch for the PT originates at the anterior side of the MN, followed by a branch for the FDS at the medial side, and another branch for the FDS more distally at the medial side. The AIN originates at the lateral side of the MN.Fig. 2Intraoperative view of the median nerve (MN) and its side branches in the proximal third of the forearm, including the branch to the flexor carpi radialis (FCR), the proximal and distal branches to the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS), and the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN)– flexor digitorum profundus, FCR – flexor carpi radialis.

Intraoperative view of the median nerve (MN) and its side branches in the proximal third of the forearm, including the branch to the flexor carpi radialis (FCR), the proximal and distal branches to the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS), and the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN)– flexor digitorum profundus, FCR – flexor carpi radialis.

The AIN and proximal branch for the FDS are completely exposed and released from their origin as proximally as possible to achieve their greater mobility to enable a direct and loose coaptation with the donor branches. It should be noted that during this preparation, smaller surrounding blood vessels are at risk of being damaged, which may cause profuse bleeding in a narrow space. In this case, a suction is necessary to localize the bleeding and perform precise electro-cauterization to prevent thermal injuries to the nerve structures. Therefore, the preparation needs to be carried out gently, meticulously, and precisely. The surrounding blood vessels need to be identified and, if needed, electro-cauterized and cut.

Identification and preparation of donor branches (ECRB branch, SUP branch)

Following the preparation of the recipient nerves, identification and exposure of the donor nerves follow. The superficial RN branch is traced proximally, ensuring the identification of the RN branch for the ECRB and the PIN (Fig. 3). Similarly to the surrounding area of the recipient branches, this region presents a narrow space containing many small blood vessels prone to damage and profuse bleeding. Therefore, equal care should be taken during the preparation to ensure control over hemostasis and prevent nerve injury.Fig. 3Intraoperative view of the terminal branches of the radial nerve (RN) in the proximal third of the forearm, including the superficial radial nerve (sRN), the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN), the branch to the supinator muscle (SUP), and the branch to the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB).

Intraoperative view of the terminal branches of the radial nerve (RN) in the proximal third of the forearm, including the superficial radial nerve (sRN), the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN), the branch to the supinator muscle (SUP), and the branch to the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB).

The branch for the ECRB is thinner than the superficial RN branch and extends almost parallel with it, while the PIN is thicker and extends deeper, more diagonally and laterally. These nerves are traced proximally until exposure of their origin from the RN trunk in the lateral bicipital groove and also released distally to allow for as much excursion as possible.

During the preparation of the PIN distally, it is necessary to partially resect the tendon of the ECRB which covers it. By doing this, the upper border of the fascia of the superficial layer of the SUP is exposed and also resected. By performing gentle preparation around the PIN, two branches of the SUP are usually exposed, and they are traced as distally as possible.

Coaptation of the targeted branches (ECRB-AIN, SUP-FDS)

The nerve transfer is complete by providing a direct coaptation of the selected branches without tension over the suture line. The ECRB branch is attached to the AIN, while the SUP branch is attached to the FDS branch (Fig. 4). A maintained coaptation stability is provided by a few microsurgical sutures and fibrin glue.Fig. 4Direct nerve coaptation for the ECRB-AIN and SUP-FDS transfers. The extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) branch is coapted to the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN), and the supinator (SUP) branch is coapted to the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) branch.

The technique for ADM-TE transfer

Identification and exposure of recipient and donor branches

The recurrent motor branch of the MN for the TE originates at the anterolateral surface of the MN and extends towards the TE, being very easy to identify (Fig. 5). The branch for the ADM is also easily identified because it originates at the medial side of the UN trunk, typically somewhat distally to the UN deep motor branch, which typically originates at its posterior side. While the ADM branch is traced and resected as distal as possible, internal neurolysis of the MN is necessary to expose and prepare the branch for the TE, followed by its resection as proximal as possible. The technique for sensory DDN-PDN transfer

The initial incisions are made in a projection of the proximal phalanges at the medial side of the thumb and lateral side of the index. The terminal sensory branches of the RN (DDNs) and MN (PDNs) for the first interdigital space are identified in the dorsal and palmar aspects of these incisions, respectively. Resection of the donor DDNs is performed as distally as possible, while resection of the PDNs, is performed as proximally as possible. Following this, a direct loose coaptation of the complementary DDNs and PDNs was performed and secured using fibrin glue (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Direct coaptation of the terminal sensory branches of the radial nerve (dorsal digital nerves, DDNs) with the terminal sensory branches of the median nerve (palmar digital nerves, PDNs) for the first interdigital space.

Direct coaptation of the terminal sensory branches of the radial nerve (dorsal digital nerves, DDNs) with the terminal sensory branches of the median nerve (palmar digital nerves, PDNs) for the first interdigital space.

The technique for autologous MN grafting

The MN nerve was explored through the standard supra- and/or infraclavicular BP approach. Following resection of the injured part of the nerve until reaching its healthy parts and adequate hemostasis, the length of the nerve gap is measured. The sural nerve is harvested through the standard approach and used for preparing the cable graft 0,5 cm longer than the nerve defect, to prevent tension over the suture line. Following the bridging of the nerve defect, a coaptation of the graft with the nerve stumps is maintained by circumferential epineurial micro-sutures and fibrin glue.

Outcome assessment

The outcome was assessed 24 months after surgery by evaluating motor strength (thumb and index flexion, grasping and pinch), degree of thumb opposition, and QOL. The postoperative values were compared with the preoperative.

Regarding the thumb and index flexion strength, the grades M0, M1, and M2 were considered unsatisfactory (without useful functional recovery), while M3, M4, and M5 were designated as satisfactory (useful functional recovery). The groups with useful functional recovery are further divided: M5 - excellent, M4 - very good, and M3 - good.

Results

This series included a total of 13 patients (9 males and 4 females) with a mean age of approximately 35 years (range 17–62 years). The majority of patients were male (69.2%). The most frequent cause of injury was sharp laceration, present in more than half of the cases (53.8%), followed by iatrogenic injuries (30.8%) and traffic accidents (15.4%). Isolated median nerve lesions predominated, affecting 11 patients (84.6%), while combined median and ulnar nerve injuries were in 2 patients (15.4%). Nearly half of the patients (46.2%) presented with associated injuries requiring additional procedures such as vascular reconstruction or bone fixation. The number of emergency surgeries per patient ranged from 1 to 8. The interval between injury and definitive surgery varied between 1 and 8 months. Overall, the cohort represents a relatively young, predominantly male population with severe traumatic and iatrogenic median nerve injuries, often complicated by associated vascular or orthopedic damage.

All patients initially presented with absent finger flexion (M0). At final follow-up, 11 patients (84.6%) regained M4 flexion, while one patient achieved M2 and another M1 recovery. Discussion

The present study demonstrates that distal nerve transfers are a powerful reconstructive strategy for patients with proximal and extensive MN injuries, providing substantial functional and QoL improvements. All patients in this cohort presented with complete loss of thumb and index finger flexion, yet the majority achieved useful functional recovery after surgery, with M4 flexion restored in most cases. Objective measures including grasp and pinch strength, thumb opposition, and PNSQoL all improved significantly, underscoring both the functional and patient-centered impact of these interventions.

Historically, autologous nerve grafting and tendon transfers have been the mainstay of treatment for proximal and extensive MN injuries. However, outcomes following grafting are often suboptimal in such lesions due to long regeneration distances, axonal misdirection, and complex intraneural branching patterns (Kim et al., 2001; Roganovic, 2005). Tendon transfers can restore partial function, but they carry donor site morbidity, risk of imbalance, and reduced finesse of hand movements (Sammer and Chung, 2009; Cooney, 1988; Wilbur and Hammert, 2016; Kozin, 2005). Distal nerve transfers overcome many of these limitations by directly reinnervating distal targets over short regeneration distances, thereby improving the likelihood of meaningful recovery and facilitating cortical re-education (Brown and Mackinnon, 2008; Bertelli et al., 2024b).

The results of this series support the growing body of evidence favoring distal nerve transfers over traditional methods. The majority of patients regained useful grasp and pinch strength, and high rates of thumb opposition recovery were achieved. These outcomes are consistent with previous reports advocating distal transfers as a first-line reconstructive option in proximal MN injuries (Bertelli, 2015; Bertelli et al., 2018). Our results also support the view that combining distal transfers with autologous grafting, when possible, can enhance outcomes. Patients who underwent both interventions generally exhibited superior recovery, particularly in forearm pronation and hand flexion. Nonetheless, this combined approach requires careful microsurgical planning and carries the risk of complications, especially in patients with associated vascular injury where vascular grafts are at risk during re-exploration.

Multiple donor-to-recipient nerve combinations have been described for MN reinnervation. The phrenic nerve has been proposed in brachial plexus settings but is unsuitable for isolated MN injuries due to the risk of respiratory compromise (Zhao et al., 2004). Transfers from the musculocutaneous (brachialis branch) or pronator teres branches may be effective in selected partial lesions (Palazzi et al., 2006; Ray et al., 2012; Tung and Mackinnon, 2001; Yang et al., 2014), though their utility is limited in high MN lesions where these branches are nonfunctional.

The radial nerve offers several valuable donors. The brachioradialis branch has been used, but its limited axonal load (about 23% of the AIN) raises concerns about reliable reinnervation (Garcia-Lopez et al., 2011, 2013). Similarly, ulnar nerve branches to the flexor carpi ulnaris have been described (Socolovsky et al., 2011), though sacrificing them risks significant wrist flexion weakness, especially problematic when the flexor carpi radialis is already nonfunctional in high MN lesions. In contrast, the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) branch is regarded as an ideal donor due to its pure motor composition, sufficient axonal count, and synergistic activation with finger flexion (Garcia-Lopez et al., 2011, 2013) (Caetano et al., 2020). This rationale underpinned its central role in our reconstructive strategy. The supinator (SUP) branch was also included in our series to augment reinnervation of the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS), although its axonal density is lower than the ECRB (Ukrit et al., 2009; Bertelli et al., 2009).

For thenar reinnervation, various donor options have been explored, including lumbrical and interosseous branches, but the abductor digiti minimi (ADM) branch remains the most promising due to its axonal load and ability to achieve direct coaptation (Bertelli et al., 2018; Ozcelik et al., 2020).

Finally, distal sensory transfers, particularly dorsal digital nerve (DDN) to palmar digital nerve (PDN) transfers, restore sensibility in the thumb–index pinch area, a crucial element of functional hand use (Bertelli, 2015; Bertelli and Ghizoni, 2011; Bertelli et al., 2018). The favorable sensory and motor outcomes in our series reflect the benefits of combining these strategies into a comprehensive reconstructive plan.

Despite their advantages, distal nerve transfers have some limitations in MN reinnervation. Successful restoration of median nerve function relies on cortical reorganization and motor relearning, as patients must develop new activation patterns to achieve coordinated thumb opposition, precision pinch, and independent finger flexion. Functional recovery may therefore be prolonged and requires dedicated rehabilitation. In addition, the functional outcome is influenced by donor nerve selection, donor-to-recipient axonal compatibility, especially when ulnar nerve serves as a donor due to fascicular inferiority (Jerome and Matsui, 2023; Bateman et al., 2023).

Regarding the etiology of MN lesion, iatrogenic injuries, though less frequent, posed unique challenge. Patients undergoing tumor resection required combined autologous grafting and distal transfers to achieve satisfactory results. This hybrid approach provided enhanced recovery of forearm pronation and flexion but carried greater surgical complexity. Importantly, patients with concomitant vascular injuries achieved comparable long-term outcomes provided that urgent vascular repair and timely referral were ensured, underscoring the importance of multidisciplinary acute management.

Of the 13 patients in this series, two did not achieve M4 flexion recovery, reaching M2 and M1, respectively, in contrast to the 11 patients (84.6%) who achieved M4. Given the small size of this subgroup, statistical comparison was not feasible; however, both patients had a combined median-ulnar nerve injury, reflecting the additional challenge of restoring multiple functional domains simultaneously, including the omission of the ADM-TE transfer due to concurrent ulnar nerve involvement. This is consistent with our broader observation that patients with combined median and ulnar injuries generally had less favorable recovery and may benefit from staged or adjunctive procedures, e.g. tendon transfers.

The relative contribution of distal nerve transfers and concurrent autologous nerve grafting to the functional recovery observed in this cohort might be debatable. Nevertheless, these reconstructive strategies act on different timescales and mechanisms of recovery are probably complementary rather than competing. In this respect, they function similarly to the “babysitter” concept by preserving target muscle viability while more proximal axons regenerate through nerve grafts (Beck-Broichsitter et al., 2014).

Distal nerve transfers provide early reinnervation through a short regeneration distance, typically resulting in measurable functional improvement within months thus serving as the main vehicle of reinnervation. Although graft repair alone yields inconsistent, frequently poor outcomes, its role in the recovery achieved by patients in our series can not be completely neglected.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and a relatively small sample size which reflects the rarity of such MN injuries, but it still reduces the power for subgroup analyses. Moreover, the heterogeneity of the cohort, which included both traumatic and iatrogenic etiologies, as well as cases with combined nerve injuries, complicates interpretation. Sensory outcomes after nerve transfers were assessed only indirectly through the PNSQoL questionnaire rather than with standardized objective sensory testing which limits the study ability to quantify sensory recovery. Finally, although the novel PNSQoL score provides useful QoL data, the lack of a standardized tool may limit comparability to other studies, and future prospective studies should incorporate validated objective sensory measures alongside patient-reported outcomes to allow better characterization of sensory recovery after distal sensory nerve transfer.

Future research should focus on larger multicenter cohorts, standardized outcome measures, and long-term follow-up to confirm the generalizability of these findings. Nonetheless, distal nerve transfers remain a primary reconstructive option in proximal and extensive MN lesions, offering patients a reliable and substantial functional recovery and improved QoL.

Conclusion

Distal nerve transfers represent a reliable reconstructive strategy for proximal and extensive MN injuries, providing significant improvements in motor function, thumb opposition, and patient-reported quality of life. In this series, the combination of ECRB-AIN, SUP-FDS, ADM-TE, and DDN-PDN transfers achieved consistent useful functional recovery, with additional autologous grafting further enhancing outcomes in selected cases. Although recovery was less favorable in elderly patients, delayed interventions, and combined median-ulnar nerve lesions, the overall results strongly support distal nerve transfers as a primary reconstructive strategy in proximal and extensive MN lesions.
