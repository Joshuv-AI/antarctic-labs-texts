Patient Characteristics and Mortality Trends Among Octogenarians With Heart Failure
Authors: Caroline Hartwell Garred, Mariam Elmegaard, Daniel Mølager Christensen, Deewa Zahir Anjum, Morten Malmborg, Emil L Fosbøl, Charlotte Andersson, Pardeep Jhund, John JV McMurray, Lars Køber, Mark C Petrie, Morten Schou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is uncertain whether recent advances in heart failure (HF) management have translated into improved survival among the very elderly.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to examine 20-year trends in 2-year mortality among Danish octogenarians with new-onset HF and identify-associated factors.MethodsThis nationwide cohort study included all Danish patients aged ≥80 years diagnosed with incident HF from 2002 to 2021. Two-year mortality rates were assessed overall and across subgroups defined by age (≥85 years), sex, frailty, and comorbidities. Use of evidence-based HF therapies—angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, β-blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists—was evaluated over time.ResultsAmong 46,943 octogenarians with incident HF, the crude 2-year mortality risk declined by 8% from 2002 to 2006 to 2017 to 2021. Adjusted analysis showed a 28% reduction in the hazard of mortality in the later period compared to the earlier period. Diagnoses increasingly occurred in outpatient settings, and patients had more recorded comorbidities over time. Mortality improvements were observed across all subgroups, although with variable magnitude. Use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, and β-blockers increased, whereas mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists use slightly declined (2002-2006: 31.6%; 95% CI: 30.7%-32.5%; 2017-2021: 27.4%; 95% CI: 26.6%-28.3%).ConclusionsOver the past 2 decades, 2-year mortality rates in octogenarians with HF have improved despite an increase in comorbidities recorded at the time of diagnosis.Key words: aging, heart failure, prognosis, temporal trends

Heart failure (HF) is a chronic condition closely associated with advancing age. With the elderly population rapidly expanding,1 HF prevalence continues to rise, placing considerable pressure on health care systems worldwide.2, 3, 4 Despite significant advancements in HF care, including pharmacological and device therapies, patient education, and multidisciplinary intervention including rehabilitation,5, 6, 7 previous research has shown little improvement in 5-year mortality among patients with new-onset HF who are 80 years or older8 However, recent major trials such as DAPA-HF, PARADIGM-HF, DELIVER, and FINEARTS-HF have not demonstrated age-related differences in treatment effects on clinical endpoints in both HF with reduced ejection fraction and preserved ejection fraction, suggesting outcomes in older patients may improve at the population level with implementation of these newer therapies.9, 10, 11, 12 However, elderly patients can present with very complex clinical profiles and often exhibit a wide range of functional capacity, from those who remain highly active to other with very limited functional independence.13 Moreover, the applicability of clinical guidelines to older patients have been questioned as their recommendations have usually been based on trials conducted in relatively younger participants, some of which even excluded patients over the age of 80 years.14, 15, 16 To date, it remains unclear if the persistently high mortality rates among the oldest patients with HF are primarily due to advanced age itself or stem from specific patient characteristics or the presence of certain comorbidities that drive mortality independently of HF. Identifying these characteristics could offer significant insights to create more effective treatment approaches and improve outcomes for older patients from both a patient-centered and socioeconomic point of view.

To explore this, we conducted a nationwide cohort study to assess the temporal trends in 2-year mortality among patients with HF aged 80 years or older, focusing on the impact of predefined patient characteristics over the past 2 decades. Moreover, to better understand how mortality risk has evolved over time, we evaluated the outcomes of patients with HF relative to those of the general population.

Methods

We used data from Danish nationwide administrative health registers. All Danish residents are allocated a unique and personal identification number at birth or immigration, which facilitates linking information across different national registries. The study was conducted as a register-based cohort study. In this study, data were sourced from 4 registries: the Danish Civil Registration System, which provided details on vital status, gender, and birth date; the Danish National Patient Registry, which offered information on HF diagnoses, comorbidities, and hospital visits (coded using International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision [ICD-10]); the Danish National Prescription Registry, which tracked prescription claims from pharmacies, categorized according to the Anatomical Therapeutical Chemical (ATC) classification system; and the Danish Income Statistic Registry, which contained information on annual income on an individual level. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines in the design and reporting of this study (Supplemental Table 1).

Study population

All patients with a first-time primary diagnosis of HF from both inpatient and outpatient records in Denmark at age 80 to 95 years between January 1, 2002, and December 31, 2021, were identified. HF was defined by a hospital admission lasting more than 6 hours or change in calendar date or an outpatient visit. Patients with HF were matched in a ratio of 1:2 with age- and sex-matched controls from the general population who did not have a HF diagnosis at baseline. To account for improvements in mortality related to comorbid conditions frequently coexisting with HF, additional control groups were created, matched on age and sex and either myocardial infarction (MI), chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD), atrial fibrillation/flutter (AF), or diabetes. Comorbidities were identified by hospital diagnoses based on ICD-10 codes within the 5 years preceding baseline. Diabetes was defined either through an ICD-10 diagnosis or a prescription for antihyperglycemic therapy. Frailty was defined using the electronic medical record–based score, the hospital frailty score,17 and categorized as either low (low risk of frailty) or intermediate-high (intermediate and high risk of frailty). The use of pharmacological treatments was defined by prescriptions based on ATC codes that were claimed within 180 days before baseline (see Supplemental Tables 2 and 3 for ICD-10 and ATC codes). The socioeconomic status was defined from annual income and categorized into 4 quartiles based on the mean income each year within the past 3 years from inclusion, a commonly used approach to capture socioeconomic differences in a population.18,19 The socioeconomic status was then simplified into 2 groups: the lowest quartile was designated as “low”, whereas the remaining 3 quartiles were grouped as “high”.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the change over time in the 2-year rates in all-cause mortality, in predefined subgroups of octogenarians with HF.

Additional outcomes included assessing changes in mortality over time for patients with HF compared with HF-free controls, as well as the proportion of patients with HF receiving evidence-based medical therapy 3 months after baseline and the subsequent discontinuation of these therapies.

Statistical analysis

The study period was divided into consecutive 5-year intervals from 2002 to 2021 (2002-2006, 2007-2011, 2012-2016, and 2017-2021). Patients and controls were followed until the occurrence of death, emigration, completion of 2-year follow-up, or end of the study (December 31, 2023), whichever occurred first. Baseline characteristics are presented as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables and median with IQR for age for each calendar group. P values for trend across calendar periods were calculated using logistic regression for categorical variables and linear regression for continuous variables.

We calculated the absolute 2-year risk of the primary outcome using the Kaplan-Meier estimator for all calendar periods, specifically reporting results for the first (2002-2006) and the latest (2017-2021) calendar group. We determined the absolute risk difference and the relative risk difference between these 2 periods. This was performed for both patients with HF and the HF-free controls. In addition, multivariable Cox proportional hazard models (adjusted for sex, age >85 years, frailty, socioeconomic status, MI, COPD, diabetes, AF, cancer, hypertension, and peripheral artery disease) were used to analyze the mortality ratios as HRs with CIs for chosen subgroups of patients with HF (all patients, age, sex, frailty, COPD, AF, hypertension, diabetes, and MI) comparing mortality rates from the first (reference) and the latest calendar period. In each subgroup analysis, the model included the same adjustment set as the main analysis, except the variable defining the subgroup.

Sensitivity analyses

To determine the difference in survival time between the first and latest calendar period in a metric of days, we calculated the adjusted restricted mean survival time (RMST) over 2 years as a time-based measure of average survival. The model was adjusted for sex, age >85 years, frailty, socioeconomic status, MI, COPD, diabetes, AF, cancer, hypertension, peripheral artery disease. Supplementary, we also stratified RMST by diagnostic setting (inpatient vs outpatient) to explore differences in survival across settings in the main cohort. In those who were alive 3 months after baseline, we calculated proportion of patients on evidence-based medical therapy for HF (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors [ACEI]/angiotensin II receptor blockers [ARB]/angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors [ARNI], beta-blockers [BB], or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists [MRA]) and their risk of discontinuing treatment hereafter until the end of follow-up, using the Aalen-Johansen estimator accounting for the competing risk of death. Discontinuation was defined as a break of at least 90 days without medication coverage. In addition, multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were used to examine factors associated with discontinuation, reporting ratios as HR with CI. Subgroup analyses compared discontinuation rates between the first (reference) and the latest calendar period. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors were excluded from the comparative analyses of the calendar periods as they were only recently incorporated into the clinical guidelines.20 Finally, to assess temporal differences between patients with HF and HF-free controls, we compared their baseline characteristics in the first (2002-2006) and the latest (2017-2022) calendar period.

All analyses and data management were carried out in R (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).21

Ethics

Studies in Denmark using register-based pseudonymized data do not require informed consent or ethical approval. This study was approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (approval number P-2019 to 348).

Results

We included a total of 46,943 patients with HF (Supplemental Figure 1). The median age was 83.3 [IQR: 80.0-95.0] years and 48% were men. The baseline characteristics are shown by the calendar period in Table 1. No missing data were present. Throughout the study period, the prevalence of comorbidities generally increased, as did the proportion of patients in the intermediate-high frailty risk group (30.0% in 2002-2006% to 42.6% in 2017-2021). The proportion of patients first diagnosed in an outpatient setting doubled from the first to the last calendar period, from 24.7% (2002-2006) to 49.5% (2017-2021).

Two-year mortality risk

The 2-year absolute risk of all-cause mortality declined across all 4 calendar periods (2002-2006, 2007-2011, 2012-2016, and 2017-2021), as shown in Figure 1. This decrease remained significant after adjustment for the prespecified potential confounders and was observed across all subgroups and their counterparts (Figure 2). However, the magnitude of mortality reduction varied across subgroups, with the most pronounced decline observed among those with a history of MI, who experienced the greatest decrease in mortality rates (reference period: 2002-2006; 2017-2021 HR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.53%-0.64%). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed for all Cox models using Schoenfeld residuals and was satisfied in each case. In the supplementary analysis, the RMST over 2 years increased by 65.1 [57.8-72.3] days from the first calendar period (2002-2006, reference) to the latest (2017-2021) for all patients. Patients with a history of MI has the greatest increase in RMST across all subgroups, rising by 103.8 [85.3-122.3] days (Supplemental Table 4). When stratified by diagnostic setting, RMST increased by 42.7 [33.1-52.6] days in inpatients and by 32.6 [21.6-43.6] days in outpatients.

We included 96,350 age-matched and sex-matched controls that were free from HF at baseline. The baseline characteristics of this control group are shown in Supplemental Table 5. We included a similar number of controls for each disease-match (MI, COPD, diabetes, and AF). The controls had fewer comorbidities over time and were less frail in the most recent calendar period than in the first calendar period compared to the patients with HF (Supplemental Table 6). The absolute risks of 2-year all-cause mortality from the period 2002-2006 to 2017-2021 are compared in Figure 3 for patients with HF and controls. From the first (2002-2006) to the latest (2017-2021) calendar period, the absolute mortality risk decreased in both patients and the controls that were matched on age and sex exclusively (patients with HF, 2002-2006: 53.1% [95% CI: 52-3%-54.0%], 2017-2021: 44.8% [95% CI: 44.0%-45.7%]; controls, 2002-2006: 23.7% [95% CI: 23.2%-24.1%], 2017-2021: 20.2% [95% CI: 19.7%-20.7%]). The absolute risk difference between the first and last calendar periods was 8% (95% CI: 7.9%-8.0%) in patients with HF and 3% (95% CI: 2.9%-3.0%) in controls. The relative risk difference for patients with HF was 15.6% (95% CI: 15.6%-15.6%) and 15.2% (95% CI: 15.2%-15.2%) for controls (Figure 3). In the controls that were matched and age, sex, and a specific premorbid condition, where the distribution of that condition was consistent with those in the HF study population, mortality only changed modestly over time (MI, 2002-2006: 23.1% [95% CI: 22.3%-23.6%], 2017-2021: 20.9% [95% CI: 20.4%-21.4%]; diabetes mellitus 2002-2006: 23.4% [95% CI: 22.9%-23.9%], 2017-2021: 22.7% [95% CI: 22.3%-23-3%]; AF, 2002-2006: 22.9% [95% CI: 22.4%-23.5%], 2017-2021: 22.3% [95% CI: 21.9%-22.9%]; COPD, 2002-2006: 23.3% [95% CI: 22.8%-23.8%], 2017-2021: 22.1% [95% CI: 21.6%-22.6%]) (Supplemental Figures 1A to 1D).

Sensitivity analyses

Among those who were alive 3 months after their HF diagnosis, the proportion of patients who were on evidence-based medical therapy for HF increased from the earliest to the latest calendar period: ACEI/ARB/ARNI (58.3% [95% CI: 57.2%-59.2%] in 2002-2006 to 72.7% [95% CI: 71.8%-73.5%] in 2017-2021) and BB (44.0% [95% CI: 43.0%-45.0%] to 72.3% [95% CI: 71.7%-73.4%]). However, a small decrease was observed for MRA use (31.6% [95% CI: 30.7%-32.5%] to 27.4% [95% CI: 26.6%-28.3%]) (Figure 4). In the latest calendar period, 107 (0.3%) patients received an ARNI, and 366 (1%) patients received a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors 3 months after their HF diagnosis. The cumulative incidence of discontinuation of the 3 types of pharmacotherapies among those who were alive and on treatment at 3 months after diagnosis, decreased significantly for both ACEI/ARB/ARNI (2002-2005: 20.0% [95% CI: 18.9%-21-0%], 2017-2021: 18.8% [95% CI: 17.9%-19.7%]) and BB (2002-2006: 18.5% [95% CI: 17.4%-19.7%], 2017-2022: 13.8 [95% CI: 13.0%-14.6%]). An opposite trend was observed for MRA, where drug discontinuation rose from the earliest to the latest calendar period (26.1% [95% CI: 24.6%-27.7%] to 34.9% [95% CI: 33.1%-36.6%]) (Supplemental Figures 3A to 3C). These trends were largely consistent with the adjusted analyses (Supplemental Table 7).

Discussion

In this nationwide study of almost 50,000 octogenarians with new-onset HF, we examined the changes in the 2-year mortality risk over a period of 20 years (2002-2006 to 2017-2021). Our analyses demonstrated a significant decrease in mortality over that time in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. However, patients <85 years of age, with lower frailty, and those with a history of hypertension or MI, exhibited significantly greater mortality reductions than their counterparts. The restricted mean survival time also increased in all subgroups, with the biggest improvement in those with a history of MI, corresponding to an absolute gain in survival of just over 2 months (Central Illustration).

Moreover, the setting of initial HF diagnosis gradually shifted from a hospitalization to an outpatient visit, likely reflecting better access to diagnostic tools and increased clinical awareness across care settings.22 When we compared the mortality risks over the same period in matched controls, the absolute risk reduction was smaller than in patients with HF, suggesting that advancements in HF management may have contributed more to improved survival than the treatment of comorbidities (assuming these were treated similarly in controls and patients with HF). Nevertheless, the relative risk reduction remained similar between controls and patients with HF.

As the elderly population continues to expand, managing HF in patients over 80 years has become increasingly central to medical practice.23,24 Despite the growing clinical problem, there remains a gap in our understanding of how mortality trends have evolved in this patient group. Improving our knowledge in this area is important as health care professionals increasingly encounter older patients with complex health profiles that require nuanced approaches to care.24 Most studies of older patients with HF have been conducted in clinical trial populations. To our knowledge, this study presents the first real-world nationwide data, specifically examining new-onset HF in octogenarians over 20 years, focusing on HF-related comorbidities. Our findings show an improvement in survival over time among octogenarians with HF, irrespective of comorbid status. However, deciding on the length of follow-up when analyzing temporal trends in mortality reduction may be important to consider especially in studies of older patients.

A previous study on Danish patients with HF showed that there were very minimal changes in the 5-year absolute risk of all-cause mortality compared to the general population over time among octogenarians.8 In the present study, we found that over a 2-year follow-up period, there was a pronounced absolute reduction in mortality compared to the general population. Although based on an association in an observational analysis, this is consistent with the possibility that evidence-based treatment approaches might have had a significant impact even on the oldest, at least in their first years after diagnosis, at a populational level. Several studies have explored the persistently high mortality rates among octogenarians with HF, identifying undertreatment compared to younger patients as a possible contributing factor to the lack of improvements in survival.25,26 Despite the decreasing mortality described above, our findings also raise a concern about persistence with treatment.

We observed a high risk of discontinuation for ACEI/ARB/ARNI, despite an increase in the proportion of patients receiving these treatments during the study period, when implementation of patient educational programs and follow-up visits in specialized HF clinics also became standard practice. In addition, over time, MRA use declined, whereas discontinuation increased. Elderly and often frail patients may be susceptible to adverse drug reactions due to age-related physiological changes and comorbidities. Clinicians may be cautious when prescribing or continuing certain therapies out of concern for side effects as well as concerns of limited futility of treatment.27 Interestingly, octogenarians with new-onset of HF who surpassed the age of 80 have already demonstrated better survival than the general population, where the average life expectancy for men in Denmark is 79 years.28 This suggests that there may be more potential to optimize care and pharmacological treatment for the oldest patients with HF, further improving their outcomes.

Given the heterogeneity among octogenarians and the complexity of aging of patients which involves a range of factors such as frailty, diminished resilience, and multiple chronic conditions, understanding which outcome is the most important to a given patient is important. Although our study primarily focused on all-cause mortality, which is a significant metric, it is important to recognize that other issues relevant to patient experience also warrant attention. Notably, the use of MRA has been shown to improve mortality rates and morbidity outcomes for older patients with HF.29 However, our findings indicated that octogenarians with HF were receiving MRAs less frequently and tended to discontinue treatment earlier than in the past. This trend highlights the need for continued efforts to improve medication adoption, particularly for MRAs, among the oldest patients. Ensuring that HF care is both evidence-based and aligned with the goals of older patients is the key to patient-centered management. Stevenson et al demonstrated patient preferences in HF can shift throughout the disease course,30 underscoring the importance of considering these factors in future studies and clinical practice. Investigating how and whether patients' preferences evolve with aging, in addition to the trajectory of their HF warrants further research.

Study limitations

The main strength of our study was the completeness of the registries used, which enabled us to include all patients ≥80 years of age diagnosed with HF in Denmark over a 20-year period, with minimal loss to follow-up or selection bias. The validity of the ICD codes used to identify HF diagnoses has been established in previous research, which reported a high positive predictive value of 81% to 88% in the Danish National Patient Registry.31,32 Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Our data lack important clinical details relevant for older patients, such as physical function, kidney function, and patient-centered measures such as quality of life wishes and care preferences. Moreover, data on echocardiographic measures, including left ventricular ejection fraction, and natriuretic peptide levels were not available. As a result, we are unable to distinguish between HF phenotypes. In addition, although we could track medication claims, we could not ascertain whether patients adhered to the prescribed pharmacotherapy, limiting insights into actual medication compliance. Use of device therapy was not examined in our analysis and may also have influenced outcomes. Finally, given Denmark’s relatively homogeneous population and universal health care system with equitable access may limit the generalizability of our findings to countries with more diverse populations or different health care structures. Differences in comorbidity prevalence, which can influence clinical outcomes and treatment patterns, should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusions

In octogenarian patients with new-onset HF during 2002 to 2021, 2-year all-cause mortality improved regardless of the presence of comorbidities, although some were associated with more or less improvement. Despite being modest, this improvement exceeded that of the general population in absolute numbers. Moreover, although the use of evidence-based medical therapy increased over time for ACEi/ARB/ARNI and BB, an opposite trend was seen for MRAs. These findings highlight the importance of focusing future clinical research on optimizing care strategies and identifying appropriate outcomes for the oldest patients with HF.
