Reducing Buprenorphine Misinformation as a Strategy to Improve Adoption in Primary Care
Authors: Sean Bogart, Cheyenne Fenstemaker, Elizabeth A Abrams, Katherine King, Ronak Tripathy, Jenna Shalash, Berkeley Franz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Buprenorphine is a highly effective medication for opioid use disorder, but it has not been widely implemented in primary care. Negative attitudes toward the medication are a well-known prescribing barrier. Health misinformation, a phenomenon which negatively informs attitudes and subsequent behavior, has been associated with lower willingness to treat OUD. Attitudinal barriers to buprenorphine prescribing may be due in part to misinformation about the medication. No studies have explored the link between buprenorphine misinformation and prescribing attitudes and behavior or assessed efforts to reduce buprenorphine misinformation among rural PCPs.Objective:To explore buprenorphine misinformation as a modifiable prescribing barrier.Methods:We analyzed pre-post survey data from 63 primary care providers (PCPs) who completed a brief buprenorphine prescribing support program and conducted semi-structured interviews among a subsample (n = 26) to explore how misinformation serves as a prescribing barrier. We assessed the most common forms of misinformation at baseline and post-intervention and used independent sample t-tests to assess whether post-intervention changes varied across subgroups. Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis.Results:A brief prescribing support intervention reduced endorsement of common misinformation about buprenorphine and was most effective among PCPs who had never prescribed it. Misinformation endorsement was higher for rural PCPs at baseline and after training. Three qualitative themes emerged: PCPs described that misinformation frequently focused on misunderstandings of how buprenorphine works, originated from different people, including patients and health care professionals, and that additional training helped address misinformation.Conclusion:Misinformation about buprenorphine is multifactorial and has clear consequences for implementation. Our findings suggest brief educational interventions can help primary care providers recognize misinformation that may otherwise undermine implementation efforts.Keywords: Opioid-related disorders, primary care, misinformation, buprenorphine, addiction medicine

Introduction

Opioid-related morbidity and mortality remain high, with over 79,000 overdose deaths nationally in 2023 (Garnett & Miniño, 2026). There are effective medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), such as buprenorphine, which can be prescribed on an outpatient basis, but access remains limited more than two decades after the medication was approved for use in the U.S. Access is particularly limited in rural areas, where workforce challenges (Haffajee et al., 2018), inadequate training (Fenstemaker et al., 2023), insufficient prescribing mentorship opportunities (King et al., 2026), and multifaceted stigma (Franz et al., 2025) have limited the number of prescribers. Despite efforts to expand access to buprenorphine via legislation and education, most providers still do not prescribe buprenorphine (Christine et al., 2024). Only a quarter of patients with OUD are prescribed MOUD (Dowell et al., 2024), and even among adults who receive OUD treatment, 30% do not receive evidence-based medication (Dowell et al., 2024). In rural areas, over one-third of residents live in a county without buprenorphine access (Andrilla & Patterson, 2022).

Buprenorphine is a partial μ-opioid receptor agonist that is an effective treatment for OUD. Extensive evidence demonstrates that buprenorphine treatment is associated with reduced illicit opioid use, reduced opioid-related mortality, decreased overdose risk, and improved retention in care compared to no treatment or psychosocial treatment alone (Larochelle et al., 2018; Sordo et al., 2017; Wakeman et al., 2020). Buprenorphine has a strong safety profile as it has a ceiling effect for respiratory depression and therefore a decreased risk of causing overdose as compared to full-opioid agonists, including methadone (Wang, 2019). Both buprenorphine and methadone cause physiological dependence and carry risks of withdrawal symptoms if rapidly discontinued (Zweben et al., 2021). Similar to other chronic conditions, long-term or maintenance treatment is often necessary, as short-term treatment or discontinuation of buprenorphine is associated with an increased risk of relapse and increased mortality (Mattick et al., 2014).

Primary care is a vital access point for individuals with OUD to receive buprenorphine given greater outpatient flexibility for this medication compared to methadone (42 C.F.R Part 8, 2024). Although methadone is also effective for the treatment of OUD and has higher treatment retention compared to buprenorphine, methadone treatment is highly regulated (42 C.F.R Part 8, 2024) and the medication can only be dispensed within specialized opioid treatment programs that require frequent dosing, sometimes on a daily basis (Braun & Potee, 2023). Buprenorphine, by contrast, can be prescribed in office-based settings, such as primary care, by physicians, nurse practitioners (NPs), and physicians associates (PAs) with standard Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) prescribing authority (42 C.F.R Part 8, 2024), making it more accessible for patients, especially in rural communities that lack opioid treatment programs for methadone (Jehan et al., 2024). Outpatient management for buprenorphine typically includes weekly appointments to start, followed by a stabilization phase with less frequent visits. Primary care supports longitudinal rapport building between patients and providers, offers ability for close follow up, and is itself more accessible compared to psychiatrists or addiction specialists; from 2010-2018, nearly twice as many people receiving buprenorphine treatment did so via primary care than from a specialist (Olfson et al., 2020). Scaling up buprenorphine access through rural primary care remains, therefore, a significant priority for reducing opioid overdose and associated morbidities.

Numerous barriers have limited the scale-up of buprenorphine in primary care (Fenstemaker et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2022; McGinty et al., 2020; Parchman et al., 2020). For example, clinicians were previously required to obtain a special DEA waiver, commonly known as the “X-waiver” to prescribe buprenorphine for OUD. The X-waiver, which was implemented under the Drug Addiction Treatment Act of 2000 (DATA-2000) required extra training to prescribe buprenorphine and imposed patient caps for individual prescribers (Rep. Bliley, 2000). Prior to its removal in 2023, only 10% of PCPs held an X-waiver (McBain et al., 2020). Although the elimination of the X-waiver reduced critical regulatory barriers (Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023), prescribing by PCPs has not meaningfully increased (Christine et al., 2024). The slow implementation of buprenorphine suggests additional concerns about the medication still must be addressed.

Provider-level barriers such as limited knowledge (Piscalko et al., 2024), inadequate training (Fenstemaker et al., 2023), and stigma toward treating addiction in primary care (Franz et al., 2025) are well documented. Negative attitudes toward MOUD are also common and include concerns that medication use is not compatible with abstinence-based recovery (Richard et al., 2020) and that medication prescribing is difficult to integrate into primary care (Austin et al., 2023). Buprenorphine’s unique regulatory history (Heidbreder et al., 2023), which treated the medication differently from other opioid agonist medications used primarily to treat pain, also likely contributed to beliefs that the medication is complex to prescribe and risky for patients (Franz et al., 2025).

Negative attitudes toward buprenorphine may at least partially reflect inaccurate information about the medication. Health misinformation, defined as health claims based on anecdotal, false, or misleading evidence (Suarez-Lledo & Alvarez-Galvez, 2021), acts as a conceptually distinct phenomenon, separate from similar constructs like stigma, which is often affiliated with an individual or characteristic (APA Dictionary of Psychology, 2018). Critically, health misinformation can have far-reaching and negative outcomes, sometimes resulting in harm (Do Nascimento et al., 2022). For example, the recent proliferation of misinformation about Vitamin A as a treatment for measles led to increased hospitalizations due to Vitamin A poisoning (Devitt, 2025). Among non-health professionals, exposure to health misinformation has been linked to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy (Carletto et al., 2025; Garett & Young, 2021; Neely et al., 2022), lower adherence to public health guidelines, such as masking (Roozenbeek et al., 2023), decreased HPV vaccinations (Milondzo et al., 2021), and a higher risk of mortality among patients with cancer who opt for non-evidence-based treatments (Johnson et al., 2018). Notably, though, this work has focused almost exclusively on non-medical professionals, with little attention given to how medical professionals may be exposed to and endorse health misinformation.

Previous studies have documented misinformed beliefs about MOUD, such as that it substitutes one harmful drug for another (Dickson-Gomez et al., 2022; Franz et al., 2024) or that it does not constitute “real” recovery (Monico et al., 2015; Quadri et al., 2025). But to our knowledge, no studies have assessed efforts to reduce buprenorphine misinformation among PCPs or have explored how misinformation shapes prescribing willingness among PCPs. In a previous study, we found that misinformation was common among PCPs and was associated with lower willingness to treat OUD in primary care, although this study did not measure willingness to prescribe buprenorphine specifically (Franz et al., 2024). A new study is needed to explore how misinformation shapes buprenorphine prescribing decisions and whether training to address misinformation is a promising implementation strategy to improve buprenorphine adoption in primary care.

The current study used mixed methods to build on this prior work. Our aims were to: 1) quantitatively examine the relationship between endorsement of buprenorphine misinformation and demographic characteristics at baseline and after a brief training intervention to identify the groups most at need of training; 2) qualitatively explore the most common forms of buprenorphine misinformation and whether endorsement of misinformation shapes prescribing behavior, to confirm and refine appropriate content for training; and 3) solicit feedback on a brief training intervention to understand how misinformation is best addressed. Understanding the extent of buprenorphine misinformation and its relationship to prescribing is critical for tailoring strategies to support the implementation of buprenorphine within primary care.

Methods

Intervention overview

We conducted pre-post surveys and interviews as part of a funded project to develop, refine, and pilot the Buprenorphine Prescribing Support Program (BPSP), an hour-long clinical training module for PCPs currently in practice (Franz et al., 2026). The BPSP provides clinical skills training on buprenorphine prescribing, addresses stigma related to patients with OUD, and also addresses common buprenorphine misinformation. PCPs who completed the full training as part of a clinical trial planning study were eligible to participate. A subset of participants who helped pilot test the BPSP completed interviews to better understand whether the training addressed barriers to buprenorphine prescribing.

Participant enrollment

We sought PCPs who did and did not currently prescribe and individuals working in both rural and urban settings to enroll a diverse sample to test the program. Participants were first recruited to test the BPSP and provide feedback before finalizing the intervention. These individuals were recruited through a partnership with the Ohio Association of Community Health Centers and through snowball sampling. An additional group of participants completed the BPSP as part of a pilot cluster randomized controlled trial reported elsewhere (Franz et al., 2026). All participants who completed the BPSP were aggregated into one sample for this study to increase statistical power for understanding the relationship between misinformation, willingness to prescribe buprenorphine, and provider characteristics.

Data sources

Pre-post surveys

We measured misinformation using an expanded version of the endorsement of buprenorphine misinformation scale, which was previously validated with a different sample of PCPs (Franz et al., 2024). This scale measures endorsement of correct information with 9 items using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1(strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) with 5 indicating more correct information. We also collected data on demographic characteristics, including training credential (physician, NP, or PA), whether the participant currently prescribed buprenorphine (yes/no), years in practice, and gender. We measured geographic location using the participants’ county of practice and the Ohio Department of Health map of urban, partially rural, and fully rural counties (Ohio Department of Health Primary Care Office, 2020). Partially and fully rural counties were collapsed into a single rural category.

Analyses

We first calculated descriptive statistics on misinformation at baseline and after the intervention. Using independent t-tests, we first evaluated whether misinformation scores at baseline were different for participants based on demographic characteristics such as urban or rural location, gender, years in practice, previous buprenorphine prescribing experience, or training credential. We also assessed whether misinformation scores at T2 were different for those who did and did not intend to start prescribing buprenorphine in the next size months. Finally, we assessed whether different demographic groups experienced greater changes in misinformation after the intervention. A mean difference variable was created for misinformation endorsement by subtracting Time 1 scores from Time 2 scores for each participant. Missing data were removed using listwise deletion. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 19.5 (Stata, 2026).

Interviews

To better understand whether PCPs perceived misinformation to be a buprenorphine prescribing barrier and whether brief training could influence misinformation, two members of the research team (BF and CF) conducted semi-structured interviews with a subset of participants who completed the BPSP (n = 26) between November 2023 and January 2024, using a standardized interview guide. Interviews lasted 30–60 min. In interviews, we explored the types of buprenorphine-related misinformation PCPs encountered, its relationship to buprenorphine prescribing, and whether and how training on misinformation changed views on buprenorphine. Interviews continued until no new themes emerged. After receiving a study description via email, PCPs indicated their interest in participating via an online form and were contacted via email. Participants were compensated with a $100 Amazon gift card. Approval was received from the Ohio University institutional review board.

Results

Participant characteristics

PCPs (n = 88) working in Ohio provided Time 1 (T1) data on misinformation and 61 provided Time 2 (T2) data (Table 1). Baseline participants included 63 NPs, 20 physicians, and 5 PAs. Approximately 72% (n = 63) of participants practiced in rural areas. Approximately 77% identified as female (n = 68). Fewer than a third (28%) of participants were currently prescribing buprenorphine (n = 25). Approximately 33% of participants previously had an X-waiver to prescribe buprenorphine (n = 29). A subset (n = 26) of this sample also completed in-depth interviews. Interview participants included 10 NPs, 14 physicians, and 2 PAs. Approximately 58% (n = 15) of interview participants practiced in rural areas and 73% identified as female (n = 19). Over half (62%) of interview participants were currently prescribing buprenorphine (n = 16) and previously held an x-waiver (62%) to prescribe buprenorphine (n = 16).

Misinformation, intentions to prescribe buprenorphine, and participant characteristics

Approximately 48% of participants endorsed one or more forms of buprenorphine misinformation. Believing that buprenorphine should be used only for a short period of time was the most commonly cited form of misinformation at baseline, and it also improved the most. The least commonly endorsed form of misinformation was that buprenorphine does not prevent withdrawal symptoms, and it also improved the least after the program. At baseline, we found differences in misinformation based on whether PCPs had previously prescribed buprenorphine (t(86) = −3.88, p < 0.001, d = 0.96). Participants who had previously prescribed buprenorphine reported more correct information (M = 4.40, SD = 0.54) than those who had not (M = 3.81, SD = 0.68). At baseline, we also found significant differences in misinformation based on geographic location (t(86) = 3.11, p = 0.003, d = −0.75), with rural PCPs reporting less correct information (M = 3.84, SD = 0.68) than urban PCPs at baseline (M = 4.33, SD = 0.63). There were no significant differences between baseline misinformation and gender, training credential, or years in practice.

Next, we assessed whether specific groups of PCPs demonstrated greater change in misinformation after completing the prescribing support program. We found greater changes based on buprenorphine prescribing status (t(59) = 2.69, p = 0.01, d = 0.75)), with individuals who did not currently prescribe buprenorphine showing greater improvements in correct information (M = 0.62, SD = 0.52) than those who had prescribed buprenorphine previously (M = 0.24, SD = 0.49). No significant differences in improvement in misinformation were found based on geographic location, credential type, years in practice, or gender.

Misinformation and willingness to prescribe buprenorphine

Qualitative data provided information on the most common types of buprenorphine misinformation participants had heard and how it influenced their own willingness to prescribe, detailed how buprenorphine misinformation was spread, and outlined how evidence, training, and exposure to patients with OUD helped correct misinformed beliefs and improve willingness to prescribe buprenorphine.

Types of misinformation endorsed

PCPs described commonly hearing misinformation about how buprenorphine works and its role in treating OUD. The most common myth shared about buprenorphine was that it simply substitutes one harmful drug for another. For example, one PCP described challenging conversations with patients who believed that taking buprenorphine is no different than any other opioids: “sometimes I hear my patients say it to me when I’m talking to them like, “Hey, let’s try the buprenorphine versus you taking Percocet from the street or wherever you’re getting it.” And then they’ll say, ‘Well, that’s just substituting…’” (#11, NP).

Another PCP similarly described reluctance to take buprenorphine among patients due to beliefs that buprenorphine was equally as risky as illicit opioids:

“I do have a patient who is really struggling with severe use, and we have attempted to do an induction with Suboxone. Her thing is, “I just don’t want to be addicted to another medication,” and it’s really hard for even her to break through that experience” (#1, NP)

A different PCP shared a similar experience: “Patients that were on it, they wanted to come off of it because everyone’s telling them they’re just substituting or their mom is like, ‘Well, you’re still using a drug’” (#18, PA). Although participants commonly described patient concerns about substitution, some PCPs shared that they too held similar concerns. For example, one PCP explained:

“you kind of think of it like, oh my gosh, it’s just tradingone drug for another. That was my, I guess, thought process before I started to learn and then also started to prescribe it. But you do think, how is this way helping them? It’s just keeping them on an opioid.” (#22, NP)

Other misinformation about buprenorphine included beliefs that the medication should only be prescribed short-term and at the lowest dose possible. For example, one PCP explained: “A lot of people still think, ‘Oh, you only need to be on it for a few weeks or a month and everything will be fine.’ And that is not true as you know from the literature” (#21, physician). Another PCP shared a similar concern about misinformation regarding the length and dose of treatment and their own change in practice once they understood how the medication worked:

One of the myths that I think I am honest with patients about and informs a lot of my discussions now is the ability to call this a long-term medicine…I think I always talked about short-term treatment. Let’s get you bridged to sobriety, and then the fight is in meetings and whatnot. I used to tell patients, ‘We got to try and get you down off. This is not sustainable to keep you at the doses of whatever medicine.’ Partly because I was so motivated to get so many patients off of the amount of opiates they were already on. There was that ability to not see these as different issues…I don’t try and wean people down on insulin. I don’t try and wean you down on blood pressure medicines” (#9, physician).

A final form of misinformation related to whether buprenorphine was safe and relatively straightforward to prescribe. For some PCPs, they previously had heard that buprenorphine was a complicated drug to manage for patients, which limited their willingness to prescribe it: “that kind of surprised me…oh, we can just start it. And wow, there’s not a lot of interactions or it’s just a pretty easy drug to prescribe. I think that was reassuring to hear and kind of surprising” (#14, PA). Another PCP added their concerns about whether the medicine was safe for them to prescribe: “It’s tons of messaging all the time. This is dangerous. You’re going to get sued, you’re going to kill somebody. And people just don’t, just don’t want to go there. I mean, it’s so hard” (#23, physician). For many PCPs, they had heard that buprenorphine was challenging to prescribe and potentially dangerous for patients, which limited willingness to offer the medication.

Origins and consequences of misinformation

When asked about how misinformation about buprenorphine is transmitted, participants described hearing myths about buprenorphine from different people. Several PCPs suggested that they heard misinformation from patients “often… it ‘s the patients themselves” (#1, NP). Another PCP described hearing myths from a range of health care professionals: “I’ve heard that from pharmacists… I’ve heard that from behavioral health professionals… that there is a substitution there and they’re not really working through the issues behind their addiction… but then I’ve definitely heard it from folks in primary care…(#19, physician).

In general, participants did not describe sharing misinformation themselves, but a few admitted that they had previously believed some of the myths and may have passed it on. Many participants suggested that misinformation was a result of limited training or exposure to people with substance use disorders. One PCP described this: “I have heard that [myths about buprenorphine] from staff that don’t have any experience treating the addiction” (#10, NP). Another PCP shared a similar concern: “Yeah, so some of it is nursing staff. They just don’t have the education necessary to make that more informed decision” (#15, NP). Others emphasized that myths could be spread by any member of a health care organization and that holding harmful information could translate to negative experiences for patients and limit willingness of health care professionals to support medication use. As one PCP explained, training to correct misinformation should extend to every member of the health care team:

Education should not and cannot only be towards the people who are prescribing. I mean, this sort of thing really needs to kind of come from the door, like from the moment people walk in the door to the moment people walk out the door. We’ve recognized from navigating different hospital systems that the first person that you meet is a registration person or a scheduler… How you interact with them is going to kind of impose an energy on the patient and how they kind of carry through to their appointment, how long that appointment is going to take, especially if they are kind of met with resistance at the door…” (#24, NP).

This same PCP expressed a similar concern that misinformation among staff ended up causing a patient harm:

I had a patient come in…his ratios were just super low. One of the staff used the word addict towards him. They kept him on a very short follow-up schedule, like week to week to week saying that he wasn’t taking his meds, and he swore that he was. And I took some time just to say, well, walk me through your process and come to find out that they had told him as the film dissolves, you don’t swallow it, you spit it out. And then he didn’t like the taste, so he was chasing it with coffee right afterwards or smoking a cigarette or just trying to do anything to get the taste out of his mouth. Well, of course his ratios are low, and of course he feels like crap because he’s not taking the medicine. And you walk him through it and he comes in within two weeks and his ratios are great and he feels better” (#24, NP).

Participants described misinformation coming from multiple sources and emphasized the consequences for patients if the entire health care team does not support evidence-based care for OUD because of misinformation.

Shifting understanding of buprenorphine through evidence and exposure

Some PCPs acknowledged formerly holding misinformed beliefs about buprenorphine. PCPs commonly attributed their past misinformation to a lack of education about OUD, as one participant noted: “I think there’s always myths out there about opiate use disorder in general. I think it most times boils down to lack of information, lack of education.” (#13, NP). Many PCPs described shifts in their attitudes toward buprenorphine, highlighting specific experiences that supported changes in their perception of the medication. Trainings that exposed them to clinical evidence about buprenorphine were especially impactful, as one PCP explained:

“I think I’ve really, my mindset has really changed…probably from the time I did the data waiver training…even though I don’t think that the data waiver training was done exceptionally well… The evidence-based side of it…and I’ve heard that from one of the other physicians in the office too, that when you just…look at the data, how can you argue with the data?” (#19, physician).

A different PCP also described how the x-waiver training changed their mind about buprenorphine, “…I used to be a lot more rigid in my thinking as far as, you know, there’s no time and place for buprenorphine. Then the more I educated myself and really broke it down, I changed my thinking…” For this PCP, exposure to patients with OUD was also a critical to correcting misinformed beliefs and emphasized the need for effective treatment, “I can’t remember what the defining moment was necessarily, but…when you see people continue to relapse, something’s not working.” (#13, NP)

Clinical training was key for many participants, and some wondered what training resources would fill the gap left by the X-waiver removal. One PCP expressed: “I’ve had a couple coworkers who have come to me and they’re like, “Well, where do I go to learn about this stuff? Because we don’t need this X-waiver anymore…so I felt like getting rid of that training was a disservice…” The same provider expressed that other training options may not effectively address prescribing concerns and now PCPs were left with just “scattered information.” (#12, NP)

PCPs also reflected on the BPSP and whether explicit training to address misinformation changed their views about prescribing buprenorphine. One PCP described the training as “very alleviating” and prescribing as “not that scary” anymore (#6, NP). Another PCP found it helpful to better understand how buprenorphine works: “I think understanding what the medicines do…Especially, I think dealing with a lot of the myths was helpful because they are out there, they are common things that you hear.” (#2, NP). A different PCP explained that “the ease of which it [starting to prescribe buprenorphine] could be done” and “the resolution of all the misinformation or outdated information” were important takeaways, emphasizing a shift in their attitude toward treatment: “I was excited to talk about treatment, and that doesn’t happen all the time… I think this has now become an idea to something that’s realistic. This is doable.” (#5, physician). Overall, there was a sense that short, targeted training can address misinformation and that this also changed attitudes toward prescribing buprenorphine.

Discussion

The results of this study suggest that buprenorphine misinformation may be common; nearly half of participants endorsed one or more forms of misinformation. Not surprisingly, training on misinformation reduced misinformation the most among PCPs with less experience prescribing buprenorphine, but even current prescribers endorsed significantly less misinformation afterwards. Most important, among non-prescribers, misinformation was higher among those not intending to start prescribing in the next six months which suggests a possible relationship between buprenorphine misinformation and willingness to prescribe the medication. At baseline, we found that misinformation was higher for rural PCPs as compared to their urban counterparts and though they improved significantly after the training, the gap between rural and urban PCPs persisted. This finding may be explained by the unique impact of overprescribing prescription opioids within rural communities (Franz et al., 2025; Monnat & Rigg, 2018) which may have contributed to mistrust of all opioid medications, including buprenorphine. This finding is important given that there are far fewer buprenorphine prescribers in rural areas (Andrilla et al., 2019). Misinformation may be an understudied barrier to buprenorphine prescribing and one factor that has limited buprenorphine adoption in critically important rural communities.

Qualitative interviews provided deeper insight into the most common types of misinformation encountered, as well as participants’ responses to training on misinformation. Importantly, previous studies have not assessed the origins of buprenorphine misinformation; PCPs described a range of sources, including patients and different types of healthcare professionals. The most common myth shared about buprenorphine in both survey data and qualitative interviews was that it substitutes one harmful drug for another. PCPs described hearing this myth from patients, which may be due to limited knowledge (Piscalko et al., 2024), or concern about the relative risk of a partial opioid agonist, especially among patients with lived experience in communities uniquely affected by the prescription opioid epidemic (Franz et al., 2025). Other misinformation about buprenorphine included beliefs that the medication should only be prescribed short-term. PCPs attributed this misinformation to a lack of education and experience treating patients with OUD. It is possible that changing regulations on the maximum dose (Modifications to Labeling of Buprenorphine-Containing Transmucosal Products for the Treatment of Opioid Dependence, 2024) may also play a role in beliefs that the medication is unsafe to use for very long. Previous studies have documented inappropriate prescribing in rural areas to limit the time on the medication or the total dose (Richard et al., 2020). Policy and regulatory confusion are known prescribing barriers (Moran et al., 2017) and may signal to less experienced providers shorter prescribing durations are preferred or even safer which is not in line with current prescribing guidelines by the American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM, 2020). Additional qualitative work to understand how policy and regulatory guidelines contribute to PCPs’ understanding of buprenorphine prescribing is warranted.

Some PCPs acknowledged holding misinformation about buprenorphine previously and discussed what factors made them think differently about the medication. For example, PCPs noted that exposure to clinical evidence about buprenorphine, including mandated X-waiver training, helped to correct misinformation. There is a dearth of literature describing the effectiveness of using clinical evidence to correct buprenorphine misinformation in the primary care setting, but educational messaging conveying the efficacy of MOUD and countering common myths has been found to be effective in criminal justice settings (Harrell-Webber et al., 2025). PCPs also described how contact with patients with OUD and experience treating OUD helped to shift their understanding of buprenorphine. This finding is consistent with a large body of evidence highlighting the importance of positive contact in the treatment of SUDs (Christ et al., 2014; Dhanani et al., 2023), including literature demonstrating that experience treating patients with OUD results in positive perceptions of the role of medication (James et al., 2021; Mazzarelli et al., 2024).

As buprenorphine prescribing becomes more common within the primary care setting, PCPs could play an important role in reducing misinformation by acting as knowledge brokers (Glegg & Hoens, 2016; Ward et al., 2009) within their clinical and professional networks, addressing bad information among patients, different healthcare professionals, and peers. As some PCPs noted, patient hesitation to start buprenorphine may provide an opportunity for PCPs to dispel myths through patient education (Bhattad & Pacifico, 2022), if hesitation is rooted in misinformation. In everyday practice, PCPs can model evidence-based OUD treatment (ASAM, 2020) by normalizing long-term maintenance-based prescribing and harm reduction practices (Jawa et al., 2023; Johnson et al., 2024; Samples et al., 2020). For non-prescribing PCPs, persistent misinformation may continue to inflate perceived prescribing risk and complexity. Our study shows that brief training interventions, even if low touch, can help to break this cycle by targeting and addressing key pieces of misinformation and other specific concerns.

Beyond attitudinal barriers at the provider level, implementation of buprenorphine in primary care is shaped by organizational and system level barriers. Participants in this study emphasized that misinformation must be addressed across organizations, including among nurses and front desk staff, and not just among prescribing clinicians. This aligns with previous research which emphasizes that buprenorphine prescribing barriers must be addressed at the organizational level including group practice structures (Janet Ho et al., 2022), staffing challenges (Andrilla et al., 2025; Lanham et al., 2022), time constraints (Pippin, 2026), and low organizational support (Dhanani et al., 2025). Misinformation may also persist in the community and legal system and may add additional system level barriers, such as state and federal regulations (United States Government Accountability Office, 2016)and insufficient reimbursement rates (Bowser et al., 2024) which shape how and whether primary care clinics incorporate buprenorphine prescribing into routine practice.

Limitations

This mixed methods manuscript was limited in part by a small sample size that was enrolled primarily to test the feasibility of a brief prescribing support program and further tailor it for future testing. Aggregating samples from two phases of pilot testing also may have introduced bias given that the two groups had different baseline buprenorphine prescribing rates. Given the small sample size, we were unable to control for possible confounding. Future studies should assess the relationship between misinformation and prescribing with a larger sample of PCPs. PAs were underrepresented in our sample, which may reflect the practice of PAs in specialty vs. primary care (NCCPA, 2025) relative to nurse practitioners and physicians. Additionally, it is unclear to what degree and for how long the effects reported will persist after the training. Finally, while prescribers were asked about intent to change behavior after the training, actual post-training prescribing data were not collected as part of the present study and therefore the impact of shifts in misinformation scores on actual prescribing behaviors cannot be assessed. Future studies should assess behavior change after completing training that addresses buprenorphine misinformation and assess the durability of effects.

Conclusion

Overall, this study found that there are common forms of misinformation related to buprenorphine that may serve as a barrier to prescribing within primary care. After receiving training on misinformation, PCPs held less misinformation related to buprenorphine, with the strongest changes occurring among PCPs who had not previously prescribed. These findings are important for tailoring future interventions to reduce misinformation, and this approach may be adapted for other stigmatized health care interventions. Future work should assess the specific mechanisms by which reducing misinformation changes willingness to prescribe buprenorphine and test whether holding less misinformation is associated with actual behavior change. Addressing misinformation may be an important strategy to improve buprenorphine adoption in the critically important primary care setting.
