From High-Risk to High-Reward
Authors: Kathrin Trame, Hazem Omran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has evolved from a therapy for inoperable patients to a standard of care across the surgical risk spectrum. As indications broaden and procedural volumes rise worldwide, the absolute number of rare but serious complications inevitably increases. Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) following TAVR is among the most challenging complications.

The reported incidence of TAVR-PVE ranges from 0.3 to 2.0 per 100 person-years,1, 2, 3 comparable to surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR)-PVE. However, registry data suggest that the clinical course of TAVR-PVE is frequently severe. Up to 70% of patients experience major adverse events during the index hospitalization, including acute heart failure, acute kidney injury, septic shock, acute myocardial infarction, or systemic embolization.1,2,4 These complications determine early and late mortality. In-hospital mortality has been reported between 16% and 36%,1,5,6 with 1-year mortality ranging from 41% to 59%.1,5,7 Even among patients who survive the index hospitalization, long-term prognosis remains limited, with approximately 60% mortality at 5 years in some cohorts.3,4

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Silva et al8 describe an 88-year-old man who developed early PVE 2 months after TAVR. Blood cultures grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis. Despite targeted intravenous vancomycin therapy, inflammatory markers failed to decline. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a vegetation on the aortic prosthesis, perivalvular abscess formation, a fistulous connection between the noncoronary sinus and the right atrium, and secondary tricuspid valve involvement. The patient underwent extensive surgical debridement, aortic root reconstruction with a Bentall procedure, and tricuspid valve replacement, with favorable early postoperative recovery.

This case illustrates a central dilemma in TAVR-associated IE: Patients selected for TAVR because of elevated surgical risk may ultimately require a complex open surgery for definitive source control. The decision to operate must balance operative mortality against the high lethality of uncontrolled infection. Redo aortic valve replacement after TAVR is associated with significantly greater procedural complexity and an in-hospital mortality rate approaching 12%.9

Although TAVR-PVE shares features with surgical PVE (SAVR-PVE), several aspects merit emphasis. First, serious complications during the index hospitalization are common and determine prognosis.1,2 Second, paravalvular extension appears relatively frequent and may be more common than after SAVR in some series.1,2 The interface between the transcatheter frame and the native, often heavily calcified annulus may facilitate abscess formation, pseudoaneurysm, or fistulization. The case presented here, characterized by abscess and aorto-right atrial fistula, exemplifies aggressive local behavior. Third, microbiological patterns are relevant. Enterococci and coagulase-negative staphylococci, including S epidermidis, are frequently implicated in TAVR-PVE, reflecting both procedural factors (transfemoral access vs sternotomy) and biofilm formation on prosthetic material.1,10 Biofilm-associated infection complicates eradication and increases relapse risk under conservative therapy. Finally, the changing demographic profile of TAVR recipients complicates interpretation of outcomes. Early TAVR cohorts consisted predominantly of elderly, frail, high-risk patients. Contemporary practice increasingly includes younger and lower-risk individuals.11 As baseline health status and life expectancy improve, both operative candidacy and potential long-term benefit of definitive treatment strategies evolve.

Current management of TAVR-PVE is largely extrapolated from guidelines for SAVR-PVE.12 Recommended antibiotic regimens typically extend for at least 6 weeks. However, no randomized trials specifically address the TAVR population, and comparisons between medical and surgical therapy are subject to significant selection bias.

In high-surgical-risk cohorts, surgery has not consistently improved mortality compared to medical therapy alone.1,7,13 Operated patients typically represent a selected subset with surgically amenable anatomy or overt indications such as uncontrolled infection or local extension.14 When complications like abscess, fistula, or dehiscence occur, or infection persists despite antibiotic therapy, surgery appears beneficial.1,2,14 The case by Silva et al8 exemplifies such a scenario with extensive paravalvular destruction and antibiotic failure.

As TAVR expands to younger and lower-risk patients, operative risk may be lower than once assumed, and the long-term value of definitive surgical source control gains importance. Thus, “higher surgical risk” at the time of index TAVR should not automatically preclude surgical consideration during a subsequent endocarditis episode. Risk is dynamic and must be reassessed in the context of infection severity, anatomical destruction, hemodynamic compromise, frailty, and patient preferences.

In contemporary practice, most TAVR-PVE cases are managed medically, reflecting comorbidity burden and perceived operative risk. Prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy, sometimes followed by chronic suppressive oral treatment, is frequently employed.7,12 Chronic suppressive antibiotic therapy may represent a pragmatic option in frail elderly patients without life-threatening complications or with limited life expectancy.4 Such an approach is rarely curative and should be framed within transparent shared decision-making.

Optimal care requires an interdisciplinary endocarditis team.12 Complication profile, not initial TAVR indication, should guide management. Uncontrolled infection, tissue destruction, heart failure, or embolization calls for strong surgical consideration. Biological rather than chronological age should weigh most.11,12

The case presented highlights that even elderly, high-risk patients may benefit from aggressive surgical intervention when infection is extensive or refractory. As TAVR use widens, balanced, individualized, team-based decision-making, avoiding both therapeutic inertia and unselective intervention, remains paramount.
