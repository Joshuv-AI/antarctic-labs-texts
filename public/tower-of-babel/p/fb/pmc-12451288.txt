The prognostic value of postoperative cardiac troponin I and T for major adverse cardiac events after coronary artery bypass grafting: a systematic review
Authors: Maaike S.Y. Thio, Peter G. Noordzij, Janice H. Klingenberg, Frieda Renkens, Lisette M. Vernooij, Olaf L. Cremer, Linda M. Posthuma, Thijs C.D. Rettig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis systematic review describes the prognostic value of postoperative cardiac high-sensitive troponin I and T as risk markers for major adverse cardiac events (MACE) after coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery and their predictive performance.MethodsPubMed and Embase were searched up to 14 October 2024. Studies were included if they reported either the association between cardiac troponin I and T and MACE (using risk-, hazard-, or odds ratios) or the accuracy of troponin I and T based prediction models for MACE (using c-statistics and calibration) within 72 h after CABG. MACE was defined as the composite of myocardial infarction, coronary artery revascularisation, cardiac death, non-fatal cardiac arrest, and heart failure. Risk of bias was assessed using the Quality in Prognostic Studies tool.ResultsOut of 2273 studies, 10 studies were included. Five studies reported on troponin I (1956 patients) and five on troponin T (5615 patients). MACE composites were heterogenous, with only two studies using an identical composite. Increased postoperative troponin I and T concentrations were associated with higher risk of MACE, with a hazard ratio range of 1.6–11.1 for troponin I (n=2) and an odds ratio range of 0.9–67.9 for troponin T (n=5). Discriminative performance was moderate to good for troponin I (n=3, c-statistics range 0.60–0.81) and moderate for troponin T (n=3, c-statistics range 0.72–0.77). No studies reported calibration for troponin I. For troponin T (n=1) calibration was good for in-hospital MACE (Hosmer–Lemeshow P=0.20). Risk of bias was moderate to high across domains.ConclusionsPostoperative cardiac troponin I and T biomarkers are associated with postoperative MACE after CABG. The current evidence on the predictive value for postoperative MACE is insufficient to use troponin I and T for risk stratification.

Patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) are at increased risk for postoperative major adverse cardiac events (MACE). Almost half of all mortalities after cardiac surgery are attributable to cardiovascular causes, such as myocardial infarction and heart failure.12 The risk of MACE is highest within 30 days after surgery.3 Therefore, identification of patients at high risk of MACE should occur in the early postoperative phase, as this allows for timely intervention such as increased (electrocardiogram) monitoring, revascularisation, and optimisation of guideline-directed medical therapy.

Cardiac troponins (cTn) are often routinely measured after cardiac surgery to diagnose perioperative myocardial injury and infarction. Recent studies revealed that myocardial injury, regardless of whether myocardial infarction was diagnosed, is prognostic of 30-day postoperative mortality in cardiac surgery patients.45 However, postoperative all-cause mortality usually follows after a series of potentially treatable complications, such as myocardial infarction.6 Predicting MACE rather than all-cause mortality might therefore provide more actionable insights. However, the prognostic and predictive value of myocardial injury for MACE in the postoperative period is unknown.

The primary aim of this report is to systematically review current evidence on the prognostic value and predictive performance of postoperative cTnI and cTnT for MACE within 30 days after CABG. Secondarily we aimed to identify prognostic threshold concentrations of cTnI and cTnT for postoperative MACE.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline, and using the checklist for critical appraisal and data extraction for systematic reviews of prediction modelling studies (CHARMS) checklist.78 The protocol was registered in PROSPERO on 30 August 2023 (PROSPERO CRD42023442570).

Study eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported original research on the prognostic or predictive value of postoperative cTn for MACE within 30 days after elective CABG. Because of the limited amount of available studies with a 30-day prediction horizon, we deviated from our PROSPERO protocol, and studies with an in-hospital prediction horizon were also included. Studies were included when either (high-sensitive) cTnI or cTnT was measured, within 72 h after CABG surgery.

For this review, MACE was defined as the composite of myocardial infarction, coronary artery revascularisation, cardiac death, heart failure, and non-fatal cardiac arrest, in accordance with the MACE definition proposed by the Standardized Endpoints in Perioperative Medicine (StEP) initiative, with the addition of heart failure.9 Heart failure was included in our definition owing to its clinical relevance in the cardiac–surgical setting as it is a major cause of cardiovascular death after CABG.2 Also, a previous systematic review revealed that more than a quarter of observational studies with the outcome MACE included heart failure as an endpoint.10 Studies reporting at least one of the individual endpoints were included. Studies including CABG procedures in combination with other procedures, emergency surgery, or salvage surgery, and animal studies were excluded.

Eligible study designs included cohort studies, case-control studies, and RCTs. Authors were contacted when full texts were not readily accessible. Studies written in languages other than English or Dutch or without available full text were excluded.

Literature search and screening process

PROSPERO was consulted on 01 August 2023 for any ongoing systematic reviews regarding this topic and yielded no relevant results. A literature search was conducted in PubMed and Embase from inception to 14 October 2024 to identify relevant studies. The complete search strategies are presented in Supplementary materials 1 and 2. We searched in the title, abstract, or keyword fields, for CABG, troponin, and MACE using terms such as ‘coronary artery bypass grafting’, ‘cardiac troponin’, ‘major adverse cardiac event’, and terms for the individual endpoints of MACE such as ‘myocardial infarction’ and ‘heart failure’. After deduplication, two reviewers (MT, JK) independently screened articles for eligibility on title and abstract. Full texts were identified and independently assessed for final inclusion by both reviewers. Disagreements on eligibility were solved through discussion or by involving a third reviewer (LP). Further, a cross-reference check was performed on all retrieved articles to check for additional relevant studies.

Data extraction and critical appraisal

A standardised data extraction form was developed containing items based on the CHARMS checklist. To ensure consistent data extraction, the data extraction form was piloted and independently executed by two reviewers. Disagreements were solved through discussion. For each study, the following data was extracted: bibliographic details, study design, study population, specifications of cTn assay, moment of cTn sampling, the metric used to include cTn in the model (e.g. area under the curve, peak), prediction horizon (i.e. in-hospital or 30-day), outcome definitions and incidence, predictive performance measures (c-statistic, calibration, O:E ratios), and predictor–outcome association measures (odds ratios [ORs], risk ratios [RRs], and hazard ratios [HRs]). The complete list of extracted items can be found in Supplementary material 3.

The risk of bias was assessed across six domains as defined by the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool: study participation, study attrition, prognostic factor measurement, outcome measurement, study confounding, and statistical analysis and reporting.11 Each domain consisted of several subsections which were adjusted in accordance with our research question ahead of the bias assessment (Supplementary material 4). Two reviewers (MT, JK) independently assessed the risk of bias in all publications. If all subdomains were scored as low risk of bias, the overall domain was judged as low risk of bias, in other cases the domain was scored as moderate or high risk of bias. Discrepancies between reviewers in bias assessment were solved through discussion.

Data analysis

Results were presented using descriptive statistics. Findings for cTnI and cTnT were presented separately, as their postoperative concentrations and release patterns differ significantly, possibly influencing their predictive value and prognostic thresholds.12 The prognostic value (the association between cTnI and cTnT release and increased risk for MACE) was assessed through the predictor–outcome associations as HR, OR, and RR. The predictive performance (the ability of cTnI and cTnT to differentiate between patients with and without MACE) was evaluated on two domains: discrimination, presented by the c-statistic, and calibration, presented by either a calibration plot or a Hosmer–Lemeshow test. As outlined in our PROSPERO protocol, a meta-analysis would have been performed if a minimum of three included articles were comparable with respect to patient characteristics, endpoint definition, and measure of effect. Unfortunately, owing to heterogeneity in the MACE composites, predictive performance and association measures reported, and cTn thresholds used in predictive modelling, statistical pooling was unsuitable and we decided not to conduct a meta-analysis.

Results

The search identified 2273 unique articles, of which 2218 were excluded based on title and abstract. Study design and patients

Four retrospective single-centre and six prospective observational single-centre cohort studies were included, with a total of 7571 patients. One study included two separate cohorts; thus, 10 studies were included describing 11 cohorts. Studies were mainly conducted in Europe (n=4)14151822 and North America (n=3).131921 Study patients were mainly men (64–100%), with average age between 62 and 73 yr. Surgical procedures included both on-pump and off-pump CABG. Detailed cohort and surgery descriptions can be found in Supplementary material 6.

Five studies reported on cTnI13, 14, 15, 16, 17 and five studies on cTnT.18, 19, 20, 21, 22 All studies measured cTn concentrations within 24 h after surgery. For cTnI, most measurements were conducted at a single time point, in contrast to cTnT for which most studies conducted repeated measurements. The composite of MACE differed between studies and none of the composites coincided with our pre-defined definition (Table 3, Table 4). Only two studies used an identical MACE composite of stroke, myocardial infarction, and all-cause mortality, but their endpoint definitions differed. The most commonly included endpoints in MACE were myocardial infarction (n=8), all-cause mortality (n=7), and heart failure (n=5). Incidences ranged 3–10% for myocardial infarction, 6% for non-fatal cardiac arrest, 3% for revascularisation, 11–15% for heart failure, 0.5–4% for all-cause mortality, and 0% for cardiac death. Prognostic value of cardiac Troponins for major adverse cardiac events

Two studies investigated the association between cTnI and MACE.1617 Univariable and multivariable HR for several cut-off values ranged from 1.6 to 11.1 (Table 3). Overall, increased cTnI concentrations were independently associated with an increased risk of MACE.

Four studies investigated the association between cTnT and MACE.18,20, 21, 22 Univariable and multivariable ORs for several cut-off values ranged from 0.9 to 67.9 (Table 4). Overall, increased cTnT concentrations were independently associated with risk of MACE and results suggest a proportional association between cTnT concentrations and risk of MACE. Specifications of the multivariable models for cTnI and cTnT can be found in Supplementary material 8.

Predictive performance of cardiac Troponins for major adverse caradiac events

Three studies reported the univariable discriminative performance of cTnI for postoperative MACE.131517 The c-statistics were 0.60, 0.78, and 0.81, with large confidence intervals (CIs). None of the studies investigated discriminative performance of cTnI in a multivariable model. None of the studies reported on calibration.

Two studies, including three cohorts, reported on the univariable discriminative performance of cTnT, hs-TnT, or both for MACE.1819 The c-statistics were 0.72 for cTnT and 0.77 for hs-TnT, calibration was not reported. One study reported the discriminative performance of cTnT in a multivariable model yielding a c-statistic of 0.75.21 This study reported good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P=0.20).

Prognostic thresholds of cardiac Troponins for major adverse cardiac events MACE

Four studies reported thresholds for the association between cTnI and MACE after CABG. One study validated a previously established threshold of 13 000 ng L–1,14 and three studies derived new thresholds from the data with the maximal Youden index value, respectively, 65 ng L–1,17 2600 ng L–1,16 and 7800 ng L–1.15 The large variety in thresholds and lack of validation studies prevented the identification of a threshold for cTnI that is prognostic for MACE.

Three studies reported thresholds for the association between cTnT and MACE after CABG. One study validated a threshold of 800 ng L–1,18 one study used the Youden index value to determine the optimal threshold of 800 ng L–1,21 and one study used the highest quintile of 1477 ng L–1.22 Furthermore, two studies categorised cTnT concentrations by steps of 200 ng L–1.1820 Though several studies found increased risk of MACE using a threshold of 800 ng L–1, increased risk was also observed at concentrations as low as 200 ng L–1 and the risk seems to be proportionally associated with cTnT concentrations. Thus, we were unable to distinguish a single prognostically relevant threshold for cTnT.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment is shown in Fig 2, Fig 3.23 All studies had at least one domain with a high risk of bias. None of the studies reported on missing data or imputation methods, leading to a high risk of attrition bias. Outcomes were often not defined in accordance with guidelines or definitions were not provided at all, leading to a high risk of misclassification bias of the outcome. Further, most studies did not report both discrimination and calibration, which led to an incomplete display of predictive performance. Discussion

Summary of findings

We systematically reviewed the prognostic and predictive value of postoperative cTnI and cTnT concentrations for MACE after CABG. We found a limited number of studies, with moderate to high risk of bias. Study results were difficult to compare owing to differences in MACE composites and endpoint definitions, and inconsistent reporting on the association between cTn and MACE, with some studies reporting the univariable and others the multivariable association. Furthermore, most studies failed to provide information on both c-statistic and calibration, which are essential elements to assess predictive performance. Subject to all limitations, the evidence consistently shows that elevated cTn concentrations are associated with MACE, indicating increased risk of MACE with higher cTn concentrations. The discriminative performance, its ability to discriminate between patients who will and will not experience MACE, was moderate, indicating at least some predictive value, but the evidence is insufficient to support the use of cTn for clinical risk stratification.

Comparison with previous literature

In cardiac surgery, myocardial injury has long been considered inevitable and the focus has been on cTn as a diagnostic test of perioperative myocardial infarction. Three earlier systematic reviews examined the association between myocardial injury and all-cause mortality after cardiac surgery.24, 25, 26 All found that elevated postoperative cardiac biomarkers were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. Similar to our review, these reviews encountered difficulties in comparability of results, owing to heterogeneity in cTn measurement and study designs. In contrast to our review, the other reviews focused on all-cause mortality while MACE might be a clinically more relevant endpoint in the cardiac–surgical population as mortality incidences are low and do not provide clear treatment objectives. Furthermore, our study investigated the predictive performance of cTn, which is essential for risk stratification, whereas other reviews solely assessed its prognostic value. A more recent review examined cTnI as a risk factor for surgical outcomes.27 They concluded that cTnI concentrations are prognostic of adverse clinical outcomes after cardiac surgeries. However, the review was limited to cTnI, whereas our review included studies on cTnI and cTnT. Moreover, their review included diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive studies, multiple surgical procedures (including CABG, valve replacement surgeries, and combination procedures), and heterogeneous endpoints (myocardial infarction, mortality, length of hospital and ICU stay). This limits the clinical applicability of the review as the large variety in study designs, surgery types, and outcomes fail to provide a clear target population and treatment objective.

Strengths and limitations

There were several limitations regarding the studies included in this review. Firstly, we identified only 10 studies, which is less than expected considering the widespread use of cTn in clinical practice. Secondly, the statistical analyses of the studies were often incomplete for the assessment of the predictive value. For example, studies described the discriminative performance, but not the calibration which provides information on correspondence between observed and expected MACE. Also, studies described adjustment for confounders, whereas in purely prognostic research confounding is not relevant. Furthermore, when evaluating new prognostic factors, it is common practice to investigate the added value to established risk factors. Only six studies evaluated prognostic value in addition to other clinical parameters, making it difficult to estimate if cTn adds prognostic value in addition to current practice. Also, differences in added prognostic factors might have resulted in large differences in ORs and HRs. Thirdly, comparing outcomes was difficult owing to the use of various composites of MACE. This may have led to differences in MACE incidences, which can affect prognostic and predictive performance. This problem has been recognised in the perioperative research community and several consensus statements on perioperative outcomes have been recently published.9,28, 29, 30 Fourthly, most studies relied on a single measurement of cTn. After CABG, cTnI and cTnT have distinct release profiles and prognostic value might be strongly dependent on the moment of measurement.31 An area under the curve or dynamic course of repeated cTn measurements might thus provide more prognostic information than a single or peak measurement. Lastly, the review included both on-pump and off-pump CABG surgeries, and the prognostic value of cTn may differ between these techniques, as cTn concentrations are typically lower in off-pump CABG than in on-pump procedures.32 However, as off-pump CABG has been associated with better outcomes, the lower cTn may reflect this more favourable prognosis, consistent with the findings of this review.33 Furthermore, including both on-pump and off-pump CABG procedures improves the generalisability of this review.

There were several limitations in our review process. Starting, six studies published only data for all cardiac–surgical populations combined. As our review focused on CABG patients, these studies were not included. For our outcome definition, we adhered to the StEP initiative, with the addition of heart failure. However, we also encountered papers that included all-cause mortality, stroke, ventricular arrythmias, and atrial fibrillation as a part of MACE. There is a possibility that because of our strict definition, relevant evidence was excluded. Nevertheless, given the increasing consensus on the definition of MACE, those studies might be irrelevant to the current research question.

Implications for practice and research

At the moment, postoperative cTn sampling is often routine practice after CABG. Our review shows that, despite the association between cTn and postoperative MACE, the evidence on the predictive value of cTn for MACE is very limited. Physicians should be careful with (subconsciously) marking patients as high risk for MACE based on cTn concentrations. Moreover, physicians should be cautious when exposing patients to interventions aimed at MACE prevention based on myocardial injury alone. In contrast, combined with clinical signs of ischaemia (e.g. pathological Q waves on ECG, new regional wall motion abnormalities on cardiac echo), myocardial injury can be used as a diagnostic for myocardial infarction, but this is outside the scope of this review.34

To generate definite evidence on the predictive value of postoperative cTn for MACE, future studies should adhere to established guidelines on prognostic research reporting as Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD).35 Prospective cohort studies are needed using outcome definitions in line with perioperative outcome consensus statements and incorporating cTn in multivariable prediction models. These models should assess the incremental predictive value of cTn when used alongside established clinical predictors, evaluating both discrimination and calibration. Preferably these studies should also include an internal or external validation.

Conclusions

Based on this systematic review, both cardiac Troponin I and cardiac Troponin T serve as a prognostic factor for major adverse cardiac events after CABG, but a prognostic threshold remains unclear. There is currently insufficient evidence on the predictive performance of cTnI and cTnT for postoperative MACE. As a result, their routine use for risk stratification in clinical practice after CABG cannot be recommended.
