Commentary: Molecular mechanisms of true and apparent antioxidant synergism in foods
Authors: Natalia Bolocan, Oxana Spinu, Igor Povar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntioxidant synergism in foods is widely reported, but its mechanistic interpretation remains inconsistent across experimental systems and assay conditions. This commentary clarifies the distinction between true synergism, driven by reversible regeneration and transient multicomponent associations in structured matrices, and apparent synergism arising from covalent product formation or additive effects. By integrating thermodynamic constants with kinetic and spectrometric evidence, we propose an analytical hierarchy for reliable classification of antioxidant interactions. This framework provides a mechanistic basis and a cautious path toward predicting the rational formulation and evaluation of antioxidant systems in complex food matrices.Keywords: Antioxidant synergism, Analytical evidence, Heterogeneous food matrices, Regeneration mechanism, Multicomponent complexes, Thermodynamic modeling

Introduction

Synergistic interactions among natural antioxidants are central to the preservation and nutritional quality of foods. Antioxidant synergy typically denotes a greater-than-additive effect on radical scavenging or inhibition of lipid oxidation. However, these interactions are not always interpreted consistently, particularly when assay responses are used without mechanistic verification. True synergism may arise from reversible redox regeneration and the transient formation of ternary or multicomponent complexes, particularly in heterogeneous matrices such as emulsions, lipid–aqueous interfaces, or solid–liquid boundaries. In these environments, radicals are stabilized, enabling cooperative electron and hydrogen atom transfer that enhances antioxidant efficiency. Computational and experimental studies have shown that solvent polarity, interfacial localization, and microenvironment structure significantly influence the bond dissociation enthalpy (BDE) and ionization potential (IP) of phenolic antioxidants, lowering BDE via interfacial stabilization and promoting radical scavenging (Leopoldini et al., 2011; Litwinienko & Ingold, 2007).

Mechanistic understanding of synergy is further informed by concepts established in chemical systems such as metal solvent extraction, surfactant mixtures, and leaching reactions (Povar et al., 2019; Povar and Spinu, 2015, Povar and Spinu, 2022, Povar and Spinu, 2025). In these systems, formation of transient ternary or multicomponent complexes produces efficiencies exceeding the sum of individual components due to favorable stability constants, ligand interactions, and local microenvironment effects. These principles extend to antioxidant systems, where reversible interactions among multiple components in heterogeneous matrices enhance regeneration cycles and prolong active lifetimes. Chemical synergy thus depends not only on molecular compatibility but also on local concentrations, polarity, pH gradients, temperature, and phase behavior.

Conversely, apparent synergism originates from additive behavior or formation of new stable antioxidant species through chemical transformation, most frequently observed in homogeneous systems. Such effects reflect independent activity of products rather than cooperative regeneration. Quantitative assessment of equilibrium constants for binary and ternary complexes, supported by kinetic and spectrometric evidence, is therefore essential to differentiate mechanistic pathways and ensure reproducible evaluation of antioxidant performance.

From a physicochemical perspective, antioxidant behavior spans a continuum of interaction types that includes additive, synergistic, and antagonistic effects. Additive behavior typically occurs in homogeneous systems where antioxidants act independently, whereas synergism and antagonism are often enhanced in heterogeneous environments where spatial organization, phase distribution, and reversible complexation modulate radical stabilization and regeneration. Establishing this continuum offers a consistent basis for mechanistic interpretation and avoids overestimating apparent cooperative effects observed in simplified model systems.

Mechanistic framework

Antioxidants interact through electron transfer, hydrogen atom transfer, radical addition, or complexation. The net effect may be additive, antagonistic, or synergistic. True synergism may arise when antioxidants participate in regeneration cycles or form reversible complexes, stabilizing intermediate radicals. Alpha-tocopherol and ascorbic acid exemplify this, where ascorbate regenerates α-tocopherol via electron transfer. Polyphenols can similarly reduce oxidized vitamins or chelate metals, prolonging antioxidant action. In heterogeneous systems, spatial co-localization and microheterogeneity are crucial; transient ternary or multicomponent associations may stabilize radicals and facilitate reversible regeneration, resulting in true synergistic enhancement beyond simple additive effects.

Apparent synergism, by contrast, arises from formation of new stable products, such as covalent adducts between phenols and peptides. These products exhibit antioxidant activity but reflect chemical transformation rather than cooperative interaction. While functionally beneficial, apparent synergy does not involve regeneration cycles, and its efficiency is less dependent on microheterogeneous conditions or spatial organization.

True and apparent synergy in antioxidant systems can be distinguished mechanistically. In true synergy, the oxidized antioxidant (AOX•) is reduced by a coantioxidant (COH), regenerating the parent AOX while forming a transient complex stabilized by the surrounding solvent or ions. This process is reversible and influenced by microheterogeneous conditions, allowing continuous antioxidant regeneration. In apparent synergy, AOX• and COH react to form a covalent adduct (AOX–CO), creating a new chemical entity with independent antioxidant capacity. Although this increases the overall antioxidant potential through additive effects, no regeneration of the original antioxidant occurs. This distinction emphasizes that cooperative regeneration is a defining feature of true synergy, whereas apparent synergy reflects chemical transformation without recovery of the parent antioxidant. This distinction should not be interpreted as a rigid binary classification. In real food systems, reversible regeneration and chemical transformation may occur simultaneously, and the dominant mechanism can change with reaction time, antioxidant ratio, oxygen availability, pH, water activity, and phase composition. Therefore, true and apparent synergism are best regarded as limiting mechanistic cases within a continuum of antioxidant interactions.

Evidence from recent studies

Representative food and biological systems provide experimental evidence supporting the mechanistic distinctions described above. Rather than behaving independently, antioxidants frequently interact through regeneration cycles, reversible association, or chemical transformation, resulting in synergistic, additive, or antagonistic outcomes depending on phase composition and microenvironment.

Bayram and Decker, 2023, Bayram, Laze and Decker, 2023 provided a clear example of true synergism in oil-in-water emulsions. Mixtures of α-tocopherol with myricetin or taxifolin exhibited antioxidant effects exceeding the sum of individual components. Mechanistic analysis indicated that reversible regeneration of oxidized tocopherol by flavonoids under compatible redox potentials and favorable phase distribution drives the synergistic effect. Importantly, no covalent transformation of the parent compounds occurred, distinguishing this as true synergism. By contrast, covalent modifications of proteins by phenolic compounds, such as caffeic acid forming adducts with myofibrillar proteins, illustrate apparent synergism, where new chemical entities are produced and regeneration of radicals is not possible.

In aqueous systems, Roriz et al. (2015) demonstrated synergistic antioxidant effects in herbal infusions combining Cymbopogon citratus with Pterospartum tridentatum or Gomphrena globosa. Among single-plant extracts, P. tridentatum exhibited the highest phenolic content and antioxidant activity, followed by C. citratus and G. globosa. The strongest synergistic response was observed for mixtures in a 40:60 proportion, which balanced complementary phenolic profiles, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, and chalcones. Mechanistically, these compounds may form transient noncovalent associations stabilized by hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking, facilitating electron transfer and radical stabilization without generating new chemical species. The heterogeneous aqueous medium enhanced interfacial co-localization and regeneration of active species, reinforcing cooperative effects.

Chen et al. (2022) systematically investigated interactions among curcumin, (−)-epicatechin, and catechin-rich green tea fractions using multiple antioxidant assays (DPPH, ABTS, CUPRAC, FRAP, and β-carotene bleaching). Their results confirmed that synergistic, additive, or antagonistic effects are strongly influenced by assay medium and phase composition. True synergy arose from reversible noncovalent complexation stabilized by π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electron delocalization, promoting cooperative radical scavenging and redox cycling without covalent modification. Apparent synergism was observed only when oxidative coupling or degradation generated new antioxidant species. These findings provide a mechanistic foundation for distinguishing true from apparent synergy in polyphenolic systems and emphasize the importance of transient molecular associations in enhancing antioxidant capacity.

Extending these concepts, Villanueva-Bermejo et al. (2024) developed a quantitative framework to evaluate synergistic effects in coextracted mixtures of marjoram (Origanum majorana), rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis), and parsley (Petroselinum crispum). Their approach combined experimental data with predictive modeling to separate cooperative physicochemical interactions from compositional changes in complex plant extracts. The study is consistent with the view that synergistic effects may involve reversible multicomponent association within heterogeneous microenvironments, while apparent synergism may occur when chemical transformations produce stable, non-dissociable species.

These examples highlight that antioxidant mixtures should be interpreted through both response magnitude and mechanistic evidence, rather than activity enhancement alone. This perspective can inform formulation strategies aimed at improving oxidative stability, shelf life, and bioavailability, while supporting more reliable assessment of synergistic activity in complex matrices. The same logic can be extended cautiously to other food matrices. In high fat foods, antioxidant behavior is strongly influenced by partitioning between lipid, aqueous, and interfacial regions, so regeneration may depend more on localization than on total antioxidant concentration. In fermented foods, microbial metabolites, peptides, organic acids, and phenolic transformation products can modify redox behavior, making apparent and true synergism difficult to separate without product profiling and kinetic monitoring. Synthetic antioxidants may also participate in mixed mechanisms, especially when combined with natural phenolics or metal chelators.

Traditional synergy evaluation usually relies on comparison with an additive expectation, antioxidant activity ratios, or concentration response models. These approaches are useful for identifying whether a mixture performs above or below expectation, but they do not distinguish regeneration from product formation, phase redistribution, or assay specific effects. Similar thermodynamic interpretations have been proposed for multicomponent systems under heterogeneous conditions, supporting the distinction between cooperative and non-cooperative contributions (Bolocan & Povar, 2026). The framework proposed here does not replace these empirical indices; rather, it adds a mechanistic layer that links the observed response to kinetic, spectrometric, and thermodynamic evidence.

True synergism may arise when antioxidants participate in reversible, noncovalent association within heterogeneous environments, forming transient ternary or multicomponent complexes. These complexes, stabilized by hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, or electrostatic forces, are characterized by measurable equilibrium constants and typically correspond to thermodynamically favorable processes. Such association enhances electron or hydrogen atom transfer and promotes continuous regeneration without formation of new chemical entities.

Apparent synergism may result from chemical transformation, including oxidative coupling or condensation, producing stable covalent products with independent antioxidant activity. In this case, the enhancement reflects product formation rather than cooperative regeneration.

Additive effects predominate in homogeneous systems where antioxidants act independently, whereas antagonism emerges when competitive reactions, unfavorable energetics, or microenvironmental disruption reduce overall efficiency.

Together, these behaviors define a continuum controlled by phase structure, complex stability, and redox compatibility, providing a quantitative basis for predicting antioxidant performance. Quantitative threshold criteria for classification remain system-dependent and cannot yet be universally defined, representing an important direction for future work. The present framework should therefore be regarded as a structured interpretative tool rather than a definitive mechanistic validation.

Rigorous analytical evaluation is required to distinguish true from apparent synergy. Evidence consistent with true synergism includes reversible recovery of parent antioxidants, transient detection of oxidized intermediates, and radical transfer observed through electron paramagnetic resonance. Consistent responses across multiple assays, including DPPH, ABTS, ORAC, and lipid oxidation measurements, in the absence of detectable new products, may be indicative of regeneration processes. However, commonly used antioxidant assays such as DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, CUPRAC, ORAC, and β-carotene bleaching primarily reveal non-additive behavior and do not, on their own, resolve the underlying mechanism. An observed enhancement may arise from regeneration, phase partitioning, metal chelation, formation of new products, or assay specific kinetics. These methods should therefore be interpreted as screening tools, and mechanistic conclusions should be supported by complementary kinetic, spectrometric, or structural evidence. Apparent synergism is indicated by new peaks in LC-HRMS or NMR corresponding to covalent products. Time-resolved spectroscopy provides the highest mechanistic certainty, while combining kinetic monitoring, spectrometric analysis, isotope labeling, and reaction modeling allows differentiation between transient regeneration and accumulation of new compounds. A practical classification may follow a stepwise logic. First, the mixture should be compared with the expected additive response calculated from individual antioxidants. Second, chromatographic or spectrometric analysis should determine whether new stable products are formed. Third, kinetic monitoring should test whether the parent antioxidant is regenerated during oxidation. Fourth, structural or spectroscopic evidence should be used to evaluate whether transient associations are plausible. Under this scheme, product formation supports apparent synergism, regeneration without product accumulation supports true synergism, and absence of both mechanisms indicates additive or weakly interacting behavior. Prioritization of analytical techniques depends on system complexity, with spectrometric detection of products and kinetic monitoring generally providing the most direct mechanistic evidence. Recognition of ternary or multicomponent interactions further explains enhanced functional efficiency in heterogeneous systems (Povar et al., 2019; Povar & Spinu, 2015; Povar and Spinu, 2022, Povar and Spinu, 2025).

Implications for food chemistry

Many reports describe antioxidant “synergy” without distinguishing reversible regeneration from irreversible product formation, which limits reproducibility and complicates formulation strategies. Apparent synergism should not be regarded as merely an artefact or an inferior form of interaction. In some foods, formation of new antioxidant products may be technologically useful, contributing to color stability, flavor development, radical scavenging capacity, or formation of bioactive compounds. The practical value of true or apparent synergism therefore depends on the intended application, product matrix, safety profile, and desired shelf life. Mechanistic clarity based on thermodynamic constants and analytical verification enables reliable translation from model systems to real food matrices. In heterogeneous foods, true synergism prolongs antioxidant lifetimes with minimal byproducts, whereas apparent synergism produces new compounds with distinct sensory or stability characteristics. Selection of antioxidants should therefore be guided by the intended function, whether sustained protection or generation of new bioactive species. Quantitative knowledge of redox potentials, equilibrium constants, and phase behavior is essential for rational formulation of effective antioxidant mixtures. It should also be recognized that antioxidant synergism observed in vitro does not necessarily translate directly into biological synergism in vivo. Bioavailability, metabolism, digestion, protein binding, compartmentalization, and microbial transformation may substantially alter antioxidant interactions. Therefore, chemical synergy in food matrices should be interpreted as a prerequisite for, but not proof of, biological synergy.

Conclusions

Antioxidant interactions in food systems comprise true synergism, apparent synergism, additive effects, and antagonism. True synergism is typically associated with reversible regeneration and transient multicomponent complex formation in heterogeneous matrices, whereas apparent synergism reflects covalent product formation or independent additive behavior. Distinguishing these mechanisms enables mechanistic interpretation, predictive modeling, and rational formulation of antioxidant mixtures for reproducible performance in complex food environments.
