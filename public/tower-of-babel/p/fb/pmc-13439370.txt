Experimental study of the sand deposition patterns on railway tracks under different subgrade structures in desert areas
Authors: Peng Wang, Ning Huang, Kan He, Guowei Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWind-blown sand hazards are a major threat to the safe operation of railways in arid regions. To systematically investigate how different subgrade structures affect sand transport and deposition around railway tracks and slopes, wind tunnel experiments and lagrange three-dimensional numerical simulations were conducted to simulate the airflow patterns and sand transport processes under three typical structural conditions: full subgrades, full bridges, and subgrade-bridge transition sections. The results indicate that the airflow velocity within the model height exhibits a typical “W” distribution along the flow direction, with significant differences in flow disturbance intensity and sand accumulation patterns. The full subgrade structure induces the strongest airflow interference, resulting in the highest sand deposition on the windward slope, followed by the leeward slope, with the most severe accumulation occurring in the track area. When a train model is introduced, sand accumulation on the leeward slope increases, with the accumulation on the windward side reaching 87% of that on the leeward slope, and sand deposition on the track increases by approximately 57%. In contrast, the bridge structure demonstrates superior ventilation, resulting in the least sand accumulation on the track, although this increases by about 56% with the addition of a train. The transition section exhibits intense wind-sand interaction, with the amount of sand accumulation on the track falling between those for the subgrade and bridge structures, and consistently higher deposition on the windward side than the leeward side. After introducing a train, the sand accumulation on the track in the transition section becomes comparable to that for the bridge structure.Keywords: Wind-blown sand, Subgrade, Bridge, Transition section, Sand accumulationSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Aeolian sand hazards are among the most significant natural challenges to the safe operation and sustainable development of railway infrastructure in Northwest China1–3. This issue is particularly pronounced in regions such as the margins of the Taklimakan Desert, the Hexi Corridor, and western Inner Mongolia, where railways are frequently affected by wind erosion, sand burial, and ballast cementation. These issues not only increase maintenance costs but also pose serious safety threats to train operations. With the continued advancement of China’s “Western Development” strategy and the “Belt and Road” Initiative, railway construction is rapidly expanding into sandy and desert regions. Consequently, the proportion of railway lines traversing aeolian landscapes is increasing, making the sand resistance performance and structural rationality of railway subgrades critical for ensuring engineering quality and operational efficiency4–7.

The formation and evolution of windblown sand hazards are governed by fluctuations in near-surface wind speed, turbulence intensity, and particle transport dynamics, all of which are critically modulated by the structural form of the railway8. Different cross-sectional configurations significantly disturb the incoming airflow, directly affecting the trajectories and spatial distribution of transported sand particles. In practice, the track zone and adjacent windward/leeward slopes are particularly susceptible to sand accumulation, especially where interactions between airflow and structures generate recirculation vortices and enhanced turbulence, leading to localized sedimentation9. Common subgrade types used in sand-prone regions include full subgrade, full bridges, and transition zones formed by alternating subgrade and bridges.

Previous studies have shown that full subgrade, characterized by tall and enclosed geometry, create extensive low-velocity zones on the windward side and vortex recirculation on the leeward side, making them highly susceptible to sand deposition10,11. In contrast, bridge structures facilitate smoother airflow through their open under-deck spaces, significantly reducing sand accumulation around the tracks. However, transition zones, due to abrupt geometric transitions, induce intense airflow disturbances and complex particle transport patterns, resulting in the most spatially heterogeneous and unpredictable sand deposition risks (Fig. 1).

Typical railway subgrade structures in desert areas.

Recent years have witnessed extensive research on wind-sand interactions across various subgrade structures, providing valuable insights into the coupled dynamics between infrastructure geometry and aeolian processes12–14. However, most previous studies have predominantly focused on static conditions, neglecting the critical influence of moving trains. In operational environments, train-induced aerodynamic disturbances, such as wake flows and pressure pulses, markedly modify near-ground wind fields and alter particle transport trajectories. These effects vary significantly across different subgrade types, thereby shaping the spatial patterns of sand accumulation. Accounting for train movement is thus essential for two key reasons: (1) to realistically simulate sand hazard dynamics under operational conditions, and (2) to improve the reliability of sand control engineering design. Addressing this gap is crucial for advancing predictive modeling and developing mitigation strategies for railway sand hazards.

This study addresses the mechanistic understanding and mitigation needs of aeolian sand hazards along desert railways by investigating three representative structural units, namely a standalone subgrade, a standalone bridge, and a transition section formed by a subgrade and bridge configuration. The selected inflow wind speeds represent typical field-observed conditions under which trains can still operate safely, rather than extreme sandstorm scenarios that would require traffic control measures such as speed reduction or suspension. Wind-tunnel experiments are integrated with three-dimensional numerical simulations to establish a unified comparative framework under identical inflow and sand-supply conditions. The flow-field evolution, sand accumulation characteristics, and particle transport pathways around each structure are examined under two operational scenarios, with and without a moving train, to elucidate how train-induced aerodynamics interact with structural form and thereby reshape along-stream wind-speed development and deposition mechanisms. The key novelty lies in treating the subgrade and bridge transition as the primary research object and in explicitly quantifying, under both train and no-train conditions, the respective and combined effects of geometric discontinuity and train-induced unsteadiness on near-surface airflow redistribution and sand deposition. Furthermore, design-oriented quantitative metrics are derived for engineering optimization and sand-hazard assessment, providing mechanistic evidence and actionable guidance for transition-section refinement and the configuration of sand-mitigation measures in desert railway corridors.

Research methods

Wind tunnel experiments

The experimental investigations were conducted at the Multifunctional Environmental Wind Tunnel of the State Key Laboratory of Desert and Desertification, Gansu Province, China. The wind tunnel has a total length of 38.9 m, with a test section measuring 16 m in length and a cross-section of 1.2 m × 1.2 m. The wind speed is continuously adjustable within a range of 4 to 35 m/s. The tunnel comprises four main sections, namely drive, flow straightening, test, and diffuser sections. To accurately reproduce the logarithmic wind velocity profile characteristic of a neutral atmospheric boundary layer, a wedge and roughness elements were installed upstream of the test section.

The layout of the wind tunnel experiment is illustrated in Fig. 2. A wedge and a series of roughness elements were placed at the entrance of the test section to generate a turbulent boundary layer, resulting in a boundary layer thickness of 0.3–0.4 m. A Pitot tube was installed approximately 1 m downstream of the roughness elements to monitor the inlet wind speed, with measurements taken along the central axis of the tunnel. Additionally, seven Pitot tubes were mounted on a vertical frame to measure wind velocity at heights of 2.0, 4.0, 7.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, 30.0, and 45.0 cm. Each measurement was recorded over a 3-minute interval. Measurement positions along the flow direction included windward locations at 5.0H, 4.0H, 3.0H, 2.0H, 1.0H, 0.1H, and 0H, and leeward locations at −0.1H, −1.0H, −2.0H, −3.0H, −4.0H, −5.0H, −7.0H, −10.0H, −15.0H, and − 20.0H, where H represents the height of the subgrade. Given the greater airflow variability on the leeward side, more measurement points were allocated there than on the windward side. Two portable sand collectors were positioned around the front and back of the model to quantify sand transport under different structural configurations. The tested models included three structural types: a subgrade, a bridge, and a subgrade–bridge transition section. The bridge used in this study has a clearance height of 20 m and is a simply supported beam bridge.

The wind tunnel experimental setup.

The experimental procedure consisted of two phases: baseline characterization and model testing. First, a reference condition was established by measuring the wind field and sand accumulation in the empty tunnel. Wind-blown sand tests were then conducted for the subgrade, subgrade–bridge transition, and bridge models at two representative inlet speeds: 10 and 12 m/s. Before initiating sand transport, wind velocity profiles at various heights were recorded using Pitot tubes, and the vertical wind speed distribution was fitted to obtain the boundary layer profile, as shown in Fig. 3a. After placing the subgrade, transition, and bridge models in the test section, wind speed variations at different locations and heights were again measured using Pitot tubes. A sand bed was positioned approximately 1 m downstream of the roughness elements, with a supply length of 3 m along the flow direction, a width of 1.3 m, and a thickness of 0.05 m. After each sand-blowing test, the sand bed was replenished and leveled to ensure a consistent and sufficient sand supply. The particle size distribution of the test sand is shown in Fig. 3b. The sand was collected from the Minqin Desert, Gansu Province, which is representative of the natural aeolian sand widely distributed in Northwest China. The maximum particle diameter is approximately 350 μm, and the mean particle diameter is about 200 μm. The testing protocol began with a 5-minute continuous sand-blowing phase under empty-tunnel conditions, during which baseline sediment transport rates were measured using the positioned sand collectors. The sand collector has a total height of 60 cm and is composed of 30 square sand-collecting tubes, each with a size of 2 cm × 2 cm. The collectors on both the windward and leeward sides are positioned at a distance of 5 H from the subgrade. Sand accumulation at the windward slope, subgrade surface, and leeward slope of the three subgrade configurations was systematically collected using a vacuum cleaner, and the collected samples were subsequently measured with a high-precision balance. The mass of sand at different locations was then quantified to evaluate the spatial variations in sedimentation under each structural configuration.

The wind velocity profiles and sand particle size distribution.

Following this, the three structural models (subgrade, transition, and bridge) were positioned 9 m downstream of the sand bed. Once stable wind speeds were established in the tunnel, the sand accumulation along the track centerline, windward slope, and leeward slope for each model was observed. Sand collected at different locations was weighed to quantify spatial variations in sedimentation under each structural configuration. Additionally, the sand from each collector was analyzed to assess the transport intensity and deposition characteristics associated with each structural configuration. The subgrade, bridge, and subgrade-bridge transition models all had a length of 1.0 m and a height of 15 cm. The subgrade side slope was set to 1:1.5. For the subgrade-bridge transition model, the subgrade and bridge segments were each 0.5 m in length, yielding a half-subgrade/half-bridge configuration.

Wind-tunnel modelling of wind-blown sand requires consideration of multiple similarity criteria. Here, geometric similarity and the kinematic and dynamic similarity of the undisturbed (clean-air) boundary-layer flow were prioritized to enable a mechanistic comparison of flow modification and sand transport among the railway structures15. The models were scaled proportionally to the prototype, with the model height constrained to be smaller than the boundary-layer thickness. Given a boundary-layer thickness of ~ 0.30 m and a model height of 0.15 m, a geometric scale of 1:25 was adopted (corresponding to a prototype height of ~ 1.5 m). The inflow direction was fixed and the mean velocity profile followed a logarithmic law, consistent with the atmospheric boundary layer. Dynamic similarity was addressed by keeping the air- and sand-phase inflow directions fixed and using near-surface shear characteristics as the primary control for sand entrainment and deposition.

It is also acknowledged that full similarity in wind-sand two-phase experiments is generally unattainable because particle size cannot be strictly scaled, and thus complete matching of all non-dimensional numbers (e.g., Reynolds number) is not feasible16–19. Therefore, the wind-tunnel results are used to derive mechanistic insights and robust relative comparisons-particularly for the transition section under train and no-train conditions-rather than for direct full-scale quantitative extrapolation. The revised manuscript now explicitly states the adopted similarity criteria and clarifies their applicability and limitations.

Numerical simulation

For the numerical simulations, three-dimensional geometric models of the railway subgrade structures were created using DesignModeler, with mesh generation performed within the same module. The ANSYS Fluent solver was employed to conduct the simulations using high-performance computing resources. The computational domain measured 240 m in length, 50 m in height, and 92.1 m in width. Geometric models representing three types of railway subgrade structures were established. Initially, simulations were conducted without structural models to determine the inlet wind velocity and sand particle release rates based on field observations. Once steady-state conditions were reached, the sand transport rate within the computational domain was evaluated.

The discrete phase model (DPM) was used to simulate particle motion, with a sand mass flow rate of 0.5 kg/s and an inlet wind velocity of 12 m/s. The airflow inlet boundary condition was set as Velocity-inlet, the outlet was set as Outflow, and all remaining surfaces were defined as non-slip Wall boundaries. For the discrete phase, the particle inlet condition was set to Escape, and particle injection parameters were derived from field measurements. Due to the presence of subgrade structures, the computational domain featured an irregular geometry. Hexahedral meshes were employed for discretization. To better resolve near-ground flow behavior and capture variations in flow and deposition around the subgrade, an inflation layer mesh refinement strategy was applied near the structures.

The numerical simulations were conducted using an unsteady Eulerian-Lagrangian framework, with the residual convergence criterion set to 0.0001. Near-wall resolution was achieved by applying 20 inflation layers with a growth rate of 1.1, resulting in a minimum first-layer thickness of approximately 0.001 m and y+ values of about 30–50. This meshing strategy produced roughly 20 million cells across the computational domain. Grid-independence was further assessed using meshes of approximately 12, 17, 20, and 25 million cells, and comparisons with wind-tunnel measurements showed that the 20 and 25 million-cell meshes yielded very similar results; therefore, the 20-million-cell mesh was adopted for all subsequent simulations. The simulation was based on a reference inlet wind speed of 10 m/s, 12 m/s and 15 m/s at a height of 10 m, with a surface roughness length of z₀ = 0.0013 m. The vertical wind speed profile was consistent with that observed in the wind tunnel experiments.

Validation of numerical simulation results

To validate the accuracy of the numerical simulation, a computational domain matching the wind tunnel dimensions was first established. Without particle release, the simulated clean wind speed at 5 m (Height 0.1 m) on the leeward side of the embankment was compared with the wind tunnel measurements, as shown in Fig. 4. The results indicate that the simulated wind speeds closely match the experimental data, confirming the reliability of the numerical simulation.

The validation of the numerical simulation results (15 m/s).

In both the wind-tunnel experiments and the numerical simulations, the train was modelled as a stationary body, and a lateral wind–sand inflow perpendicular to the train axis was imposed to represent crosswind-driven deflection and deposition. Accordingly, the “train effect” refers only to obstacle-induced aerodynamic interference (blockage, local acceleration, separation/recirculation, and wake development), rather than motion-induced unsteady phenomena associated with a passing train (pressure pulses, slipstream growth, and time-evolving wakes). This setup enables controlled comparisons of train presence effects on near-surface flow, surface shear, and deposition hotspots over subgrade, bridge, and transition sections under identical boundary conditions. However, transient peaks in disturbance and sand-transport rates during real train passage may be underestimated; thus, the results are intended for mechanistic insight and relative assessment rather than direct full-scale extrapolation.

Results and discussion

Wind field variations in the wind tunnel experiments

Figure 5 presents the leeward wind speed profiles within 0–60 cm at distances of 5, 10, 20, 30, and 50 m from the track centerline for the bridge, subgrade, and transition section, comparing the no-train and train-present conditions. Overall, wind speed generally increased with height in the 20–60 cm layer. At the same height, the bridge exhibited the lowest wind speeds, followed by the transition section, whereas the subgrade showed the highest values. In the near-surface zone (0–20 cm), the profiles were more complex, especially at 5 and 10 m where a distinct “elephant trunk” shape was observed, with wind speed first decreasing and then increasing with height. This feature was most pronounced for the subgrade, moderate for the transition section, and weakest for the bridge, reflecting structure-dependent leeward vortex formation and associated suction effects. With increasing leeward distance (20–50 m), wind speed fluctuations weakened for the bridge and transition section due to attenuation of the disturbance, whereas the subgrade maintained a slight post-plateau increase, indicating a more persistent aerodynamic influence and an extended negative-pressure region beyond 50 m. Under train-present conditions, the subgrade profiles closely resembled those without a train: wind speeds remained near zero within 0–5 cm and increased sharply between 15 and 40 cm. In contrast, the bridge and transition section exhibited greater train-induced variability than in the no-train case, particularly within the lower height range; however, in the 40–60 cm layer, the overall trends remained broadly similar between the train and no-train scenarios across all structural types. Collectively, these results indicate that leeward near-surface disturbances are highly sensitive to structural form, and that the transition from bridge to subgrade is associated with a more complex pressure gradient and strengthened lateral aerodynamic forcing under identical inflow conditions.

Wind speed variations at different positions from the leeward side. (wind speed: 12 m/s). (a) 5m, (b) 10m, (c) 20m, (e) 50m.

Figure 6 displays the wind speed variations at four horizontal planes located 0.01, 0.08, 0.3, and 0.6 m above the surface of each structure type. For the bridge case, the wind speeds at the 0.3 and 0.6 m planes remained relatively stable. However, at the 0.01 and 0.08 m planes, a sharp wind speed drop was observed near the windward edge of the bridge, followed by a gradual increase downstream. This trend was more pronounced under no-train conditions, indicating less flow disturbance and stronger near-surface pressure gradients in the absence of vehicles. For the subgrade case, the wind speeds at all planes were generally higher with a train present. At the 0.01 and 0.08 m planes, the wind speed dropped sharply to nearly zero about 5 m upwind of the subgrade edge. In contrast, at the 0.3 and 0.6 m planes, the wind speeds increased locally above the subgrade, particularly at 0.3 m. This is attributed to the deflection of crosswinds by the sloped embankment surface, causing the flow to ascend along the slope. This deflection created a low-pressure zone near the surface, resulting in wind speed reductions, while slightly higher regions experienced accelerated airflow due to compression effects along the slope. For the transition section, the near-surface wind speed trends were found to be similar to those of the subgrade. However, at the 0.01 and 0.08 m planes, the wind speed gradually increased on the leeward side beyond 20 m. This was because part of the airflow passed through the open bridge section, weakening the low-pressure zone compared to the full subgrade case. In the presence of a train, the magnitude of the wind speed drop and recovery was less pronounced than in the absence of a train. Moreover, at equivalent leeward positions, the wind speed was consistently higher with the train present, and the rate of increase was greater.

The wind speed variations at different locations under different subgrade types with and without the presence of a train. (wind speed: 12 m/s).

Sand deposition in wind tunnel experiments

Figure 7 shows the amount of sand collected by the sand traps positioned at 5H on the leeward side of the subgrade, bridge, and transition section models. As illustrated in Fig. 7a, sand accumulation generally increased with height for all three structural types. A noticeable inflection was observed around the height of 10 cm, where the influence of structural geometry led to divergent sand distribution patterns. Below 10 cm, the sand deposition under the subgrade and transition structures initially increased and then decreased, while the bridge case exhibited a continuous upward trend. Above 10 cm, the sand deposition trends across all three structural models became more consistent: the bridge showed the least accumulation, the subgrade exhibited the most, and the accumulation of the transition section fell in between those of the bridge and subgrade. This was primarily due to the stronger obstruction effect of the subgrade, which trapped more particles near the structure; in contrast, the bridge allowed more sand to pass through, resulting in higher leeward deposition. Figure 7b shows that the spatial distributions of sand with and without a train present were largely consistent. This was because adding the train effectively increased the height of the model, uniformly influencing the wind flow and sand deposition patterns on the leeward side. However, the overall quantity of deposited sand decreased in the presence of a train, likely due to changes in flow structure that reduced particle transport efficiency.

The weight of sand grains collected at different heights from a sand collector positioned on the leeward side of the models (wind speed: 12 m/s).

To further evaluate the effect of trains on sand accumulation, a comparative analysis was conducted using the sand collected by the traps in the presence and absence of a train. Figure 8 illustrates the variation in collected sand quantities for the subgrade, bridge, and transition section under both scenarios. As shown in Fig. 8a, for the transition section, a critical point appeared at a height of approximately 15 cm. Below this height, sand accumulation decreased in the presence of the train due to the increased structural height, which blocked part of the sand transport. Above 15 cm, however, the amount of collected sand increased; this was likely due to flow acceleration enhancing particle transport over the elevated structure. Figure 8b shows that, in the subgrade case, sand accumulation first increased and then decreased in the presence of the train. Overall, the total amount of sand was reduced to about 40% of that under the no-train condition, indicating both enhanced attenuation of sand transport and increased deposition on the track surface when a train was present. In contrast, Fig. 8c shows minimal change in the bridge case after adding the train, suggesting that the bridge structure offered little obstruction to wind-blown sand and that the presence of a train did not significantly alter sand deposition on the track.

The weight of sand grains collected at different heights from a sand collector in the presence and absence of a train (wind speed: 12 m/s).

Figure 9 presents the sand accumulation on the track, windward slope, and leeward slope for the subgrade, bridge, and transition section models. As shown in Fig. 9a, the subgrade exhibited the highest sand deposition on the track, while the bridge track in the transition section exhibited the lowest. The amount of sand on the bridge track alone was comparable to that on the subgrade portion of the transition section. Figure 9b indicates that, for the subgrade, sand accumulation on the windward slope was the greatest, with the leeward slope receiving about 77% of the amount on the windward slope. In the transition section, the leeward slope accumulated about 60% of the sand that was accumulated on the windward side. Compared to the subgrade structure, sand accumulation on both the windward and leeward slopes for the transition section decreased to approximately 41% and 31%, respectively. These findings suggest that the subgrade configuration led to greater sand deposition on both the track and adjacent slopes, emphasizing the need for targeted sand control measures in such sections.

The weight of sand grains accumulated on the track, windward slope, and leeward slope for the subgrade, bridge, and transition section models (wind speed: 12 m/s).

Figure 10 builds on the findings of Fig. 9 by examining the effect of the presence of a train on sand accumulation across different structural configurations. Compared to the no-train scenario, sand deposition on the subgrade track (B1) was increased significantly due to the reduced near-surface airflow caused by the added train body, sleepers, and ballast. Although the transition (A1, A2) and bridge (C) structures also exhibited slight increases in sand accumulation, their levels remained comparatively low, confirming their superior aerodynamic performance. On the windward and leeward slopes, the presence of the train altered the deposition pattern. In the subgrade structure, leeward sand accumulation (B3) surpassed that on the windward side (B2), reaching about 87% of the windward amount; this was contrary to the trend observed in the absence of the train. This shift resulted from the enhanced leeward recirculation caused by the increased model height. For the transition section, leeward deposition (A4) remained approximately 84% lower than windward deposition (A3), indicating sustained ventilation benefits. In summary, the presence of the train model intensified sand accumulation, particularly under subgrade conditions. However, the transition and bridge structures effectively mitigated this effect, highlighting the importance of aerodynamic design in railway environments prone to wind-blown sediment.

Numerical simulation results

Figure 11 presents the flow field distributions for the three structural configurations at a wind speed of 12 m/s. The full subgrade structure generated the most severe flow disturbance, characterized by a large low-velocity zone on the windward slope, significant recirculation on the leeward side, and a sharp wind speed reduction over the track, making it the most susceptible to sand accumulation. In the transition section, the abrupt change in structure caused complex velocity gradients and turbulence at the interface, resulting in strong flow disruption and uneven wind speed distribution along the track, with severe sand accumulation, especially on the windward side. In contrast, the full bridge allowed airflow to pass freely beneath the structure, producing a more uniform velocity field, minimal recirculation, and relatively high wind speeds over the track, which greatly reduced sand deposition. These results highlight the crucial influence of structural form on airflow patterns and the sand deposition distribution, with the bridge structure offering superior ventilation and sand mitigation under high wind conditions.

The flow field distributions at 0.1 m above the ground surface for the three structural configurations.

Figure 12a,b respectively illustrate the sand particle concentration along the track centerline for the three structural types in the up-track and down-track directions. In both cases, the full subgrade exhibited the highest sand concentration, ranging from 6 × 10− 6 to 9 × 10− 6 kg/m³, with sharp increases at both the windward and leeward edges, indicating a high deposition risk. The transition section exhibited slightly lower concentrations, following a similar but more moderate trend, suggesting reduced yet still significant flow disturbance and deposition potential compared to the subgrade structure. In contrast, the bridge structure maintained the lowest concentration, around 1 × 10− 6 kg/m³, with minimal fluctuations. This reflects the effectiveness of its open under structure in promoting ventilation and reducing sand deposition. These results align with the flow field patterns observed in Fig. 12 and further confirm the superior sand-control performance of the bridge structure, making it especially suitable for railway sections exposed to frequent wind-blown sand. Conversely, the subgrade structure posed the highest sand accumulation risk, necessitating enhanced protective measures.

The sand particle concentration along the track centerline for the three subgrade types in the (a) up-track and (b) down-track directions.

Figure 13 presents the visualized sand transport and concentration distributions at a wind speed of 12 m/s for the three structural configurations. In the sand cloud images, color intensity-from green to yellow to orange-red indicates increasing sand concentration. For the full subgrade (Fig. 13a), a large accumulation of high-concentration particles appeared above the windward slope, with visible vortex-induced deposition over the track and additional buildup on the leeward slope. This pattern reflects strong airflow disruption and forced sedimentation, making it the most deposition-prone structure. In the transition section (Fig. 13b), sand concentration intensified near the junction between the subgrade and bridge. Accumulation was primarily concentrated on the windward side, while the bridge portion provided partial airflow relief due to improved ventilation. However, the overall disturbance remained high, featuring a wide and dense deposition zone with a characteristic “concentrated-transition-reconcentrated” pattern. The bridge structure (Fig. 13c) allowed most particles to pass smoothly beneath via the open substructure. Particle concentration remained low (primarily green to light yellow), and no significant accumulation zones were observed, indicating minimal airflow disruption and the lowest deposition risk. These results align with those presented in Figs. 11 and 12, reinforcing that the bridge structure offered the best sand mitigation performance, while the subgrade structure exhibited the highest risk. Due to abrupt geometric transitions, the transition section was found to be a critical area for targeted sand control. These findings provide clear visual and quantitative evidence supporting optimized railway structural design in sand-prone environments.

Variations in the sand particle concentration under three types of subgrades. (a) Subgrade, (b) Transition, (c) Bridge.

The differences in sand accumulation among the three structural configurations are fundamentally governed by flow–structure interaction mechanisms. The full subgrade, acting as a high-blockage obstacle, generates a pronounced low-velocity zone on the windward side and a strong recirculation region on the leeward side, leading to rapid near-surface momentum loss and consequently the most significant sand deposition on the track and adjacent slopes. In contrast, the bridge structure, owing to its open under-deck geometry, effectively promotes airflow ventilation, weakens flow separation, and suppresses wake vortices, thereby maintaining near-bed momentum and resulting in minimal sand accumulation. The transition section, characterized by abrupt geometric variation, induces flow restructuring with shear layer distortion and vortex interactions, leading to complex particle transport pathways and highly heterogeneous deposition patterns.

On this basis, train operation further modulates these structural effects. Under no-train conditions, the full subgrade exhibits the most severe accumulation, the bridge the least, and the transition section shows intermediate but non-uniform deposition. With a train present, the induced aerodynamic disturbances alter the near-surface flow structure and amplify the differences among configurations. Specifically, sand accumulation on the subgrade track and leeward side increases significantly; the bridge shows a slight increase but still maintains the lowest accumulation; and in the transition section, track accumulation becomes comparable to that of the bridge, while the windward side remains distinctly higher than the leeward side. Overall, blockage effect, ventilation capacity, and geometric continuity collectively govern the evolution of wind-blown sand flow and the resulting deposition patterns under both train and no-train conditions.

Conclusions

Based on wind tunnel experiments, this study simulated airflow patterns and sand transport around three typical railway structures: subgrades, bridges, and subgrade–bridge transition sections. The main conclusions are as follows.

For the full-subgrade configuration, a distinct low-velocity zone developed along the windward slope, while recirculation and enhanced turbulence formed above the track, producing the highest near-track sand concentration. Sand deposition was primarily concentrated on the track and the windward slope, identifying this region as the main hotspot for wind-blown sand hazards. After introducing the train model, deposition on the leeward slope increased, and the windward-slope deposition reached 87% of that on the leeward slope. Meanwhile, track deposition increased by approximately 57%.

Sand deposition was primarily concentrated on the track and the windward slope, identifying this region as the main hotspot for wind-blown sand hazards. After introducing the train model, deposition on the leeward slope increased, and the windward-slope deposition reached 87% of that on the leeward slope. Meanwhile, track deposition increased by approximately 57%.

The bridge configuration provided effective ventilation beneath the track, substantially mitigating airflow disturbance and reducing sand accumulation. The sand-laden concentration within the track zone was markedly lower than that of the other two configurations, indicating superior sand-mitigation performance for desert railways. However, when a train was present, the sand deposition on the track increased by 56%.

The transition section was found to exhibit uneven airflow and sand concentration due to abrupt structural changes. The sand accumulation was most pronounced on the windward slope, with localized deposition along the track. With the addition of a train, the sand deposition on the subgrade portion of the transition section became comparable to that of the bridge structure, while the windward slope continued to accumulate more sand than the leeward side.
