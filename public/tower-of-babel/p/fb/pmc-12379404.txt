Statistical evaluation of body composition and aerobic/anaerobic power in young adults with generalized joint hypermobility: an ANCOVA-based study
Authors: Ayse Yildiz, Ramazan Yildiz, Onur Camli, Musa Eymir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneralized joint hypermobility (GJH) is a highly prevalent condition that affects a person’s exercise tolerance and is frequently associated with joint injuries. This study aimed to evaluate body composition and aerobic/ anaerobic power in young adults with asymptomatic GJH.MethodsSixty university students aged 18–25 years (30 with GJH, 30 controls) participated in the study. GJH was defined using a Beighton score ≥ 5. Body composition was assessed using the Tanita BC-601 device. Aerobic power was measured with the shuttle run test, while anaerobic power was calculated using vertical jump height and the Lewis Nomogram formula. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was applied to control for age and gender effects.ResultsANCOVA results revealed statistically significant differences between groups in mineral percentage (partial η²ₚ = 0.093, p =.020) and aerobic power (partial η²ₚ = 0.256, p <.001) after adjusting for age and gender. No significant group differences were found in other body composition variables or anaerobic power (p >.05). Gender significantly affected lean mass, muscle mass, fat percentage, and aerobic and anaerobic power (p <.05).ConclusionsThe study demonstrated that GJH has a distinct and independent impact on aerobic capacity. Additionally, mineral percentage differences regarding body composition were noted between groups.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01257-5.Keywords: General joint hypermobility, Body composition, Aerobic power, Anaerobic power, R programming, Statistical inference

Introduction

Generalized joint hypermobility (GJH) is an asymptomatic condition in which an individual has more than one joint with a greater-than-normal range of motion. The joint’s range of motion is determined by the tightness or laxity of the ligaments, and joint laxity is considered a prerequisite for hypermobility [1]. Beighton scoring, which evaluates five separate joints, is used to diagnose GJH, and people who score five and above according to the maneuvers are diagnosed with GJH [2].

Functional exercise capacity is reduced in young adults with asymptomatic GJH [3]. This reduction in functional capacity has been demonstrated by a significant decrease in walking distance and jumping capacity in young adults with GJH [4]. Body composition is a key indicator of functional capacity. Evaluating it in individuals with GJH helps us understand their physical performance. However, there is limited research on this population, and existing studies are based only on indirect measures such as body mass index (BMI) [5]. Body composition components such as muscle mass and fat percentage can influence an individual’s muscular strength and endurance [6, 7]. These assessments are also essential to identify additional health problems, such as obesity and metabolic disease, in individuals with GJH. High body fat may increase joint stress, causing joint pain and functional loss. Body composition directly affects physical performance indicators such as aerobic and anaerobic power, as muscle mass supports force production and endurance. In contrast, excess fat mass may hinder movement efficiency and cardiorespiratory function. Consequently, detailed body composition assessments in individuals with GJH will help us understand their physical performance and enable the development of the most appropriate interventions.

Muscular endurance and strength are critical to overall physical performance in these individuals. In particular, muscular strength and endurance provide key indicators for interventions that can improve body composition and overall health [8, 9, 10]. In this context, aerobic and anaerobic power are essential to understanding the muscular endurance and energy production capacity of individuals with GJH. Aerobic power refers to the ability of muscles to take in and use oxygen. It is commonly assessed through VO₂ max tests performed under increasing workload [11, 12]. Anaerobic power refers to the capacity of skeletal muscles to perform work using anaerobic energy systems in high-intensity physical activities [13].

In individuals with GJH, deterioration in body composition may negatively affect aerobic power, which determines the capacity of muscles to carry and use oxygen. Therefore, aerobic and anaerobic power assessments may guide the design of personalized exercise programs and support physical recovery processes. In addition, measuring these power parameters may allow the development of effective strategies for individuals with GJH to develop better posture and generally maintain a healthier lifestyle. Increasing aerobic and anaerobic power may improve physical performance while protecting joint and muscle health. However, no research has been conducted in this direction in the literature. This study, which aims to fill the gaps in the existing literature on this subject, aims to examine aerobic and anaerobic power parameters and body composition in asymptomatic individuals with GJH.

To ensure the validity of the GJH classification, Beighton scores were determined independently by two raters. Inter-rater reliability (ICC, 95% CI) was 0.88 (0.62 − 0.96). Joint hypermobility assessments based on the Beighton criteria were validated using a goniometer. Each joint (such as the elbow and the 5th metacarpophalangeal joint) was passively moved to its maximal range of motion, and the angle was measured in degrees with the goniometer placed at the joint’s center of rotation. To enhance reliability, two separate measurements were taken for each joint, and the average of the two was used for further analysis. If a discrepancy greater than 5° was observed between the two measures, a third measurement was performed, and the closest two were averaged. This procedure provided an objective confirmation of hypermobility alongside the Beighton criteria.

Participants were recruited from university students. To define “asymptomatic” GJH, participants were screened through a structured interview based on typical clinical manifestations of symptomatic hypermobility disorders (Supplementary File 1). Individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain (lasting longer than 3 months), recurrent joint dislocations or subluxations, increased skin elasticity or easy bruising, history of functional impairment due to joint instability, hypermobility spectrum disorder (HSD), hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS), or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) were excluded from the asymptomatic GJH group. These criteria were assessed via a checklist during the intake interview. The study did not include participants who met any of the exclusion criteria. This procedure aimed to ensure that only asymptomatic individuals with GJH were classified in the GJH group.

The sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2, Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). The test family was set to F tests, and the statistical test selected was ANCOVA: Fixed effects, main effects, and interactions. Based on a previous study [14], a priori power analysis was conducted to detect a significant difference between groups with a power of 90%, a significance level of 0.05, and an effect size of 0.88. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 58 participants (29 per group) was required. The study was completed with 60 individuals, including 30 in the GJH group and 30 in the control group.

Outcome measures

Joint Hypermobility

Five movements are evaluated: dorsiflexion of the fifth metacarpal joint, passive thumb touching the forearm, hyperextension of the elbow and knee, and trunk flexion. The first four items are evaluated symmetrically in all extremities, and separate scores are given for the right and left extremities. Since the fifth item includes the whole body, it is assessed as one point when the desired movement is performed. Those who score five or more out of 9 points are considered to have GJH [14, 15, 16].

Body composition

Participants’ total body weight (kg), body fat, water, and protein percentage (%) were measured on an empty stomach with the help of Tanita® BC-601 Body Composition Monitor. After entering the required individual data (height, gender, age), they were asked to grasp the electrodes on the device. Then, with the body analysis monitor, data such as total body weight (kg), body fat mass (kg), body fat (%) percentage, body muscle mass (kg), body muscle mass (%) percentage, and BMI were automatically calculated by the device [17].

Aerobic power

Aerobic power was assessed individually using the shuttle run test, where participants ran repeated 20-meter shuttles in response to a signal. The shuttle run test is often used to determine VO2 max indirectly, and the literature shows that this test is a reliable indicator of aerobic capacity, especially in young adults. Since this test involves repeated running sprints that measure the participant’s speed, endurance, and cardiovascular responses, it can accurately estimate an individual’s aerobic capacity without directly measuring VO2 max. The test was chosen because it is practical and easy to implement for field studies, rather than expensive equipment-based measurements in a laboratory setting [18, 19]. Participants were instructed to complete as many levels and shuttles as possible for the test. The test was concluded when a participant could not maintain the required pace for two consecutive shuttles and withdrew due to fatigue. The following formula was used to estimate VO2 max: VO2 max in ml/kg/min =-24.4 + 6 x maximal speed [19, 20].

Anaerobic power

Jump height obtained from vertical jump tests was used as a practical measure of anaerobic power [21]. Before the vertical jump test application, the participants participating in the measurement were allowed to warm up for 15 min. Before the measurement, the participants were shown how the test would be applied practically. They were told to jump as far as possible when they felt ready for the test. The vertical jump performance of the participants was measured using the wall measurement method. The test was repeated twice for each participant, and all results were recorded in cm. Anaerobic power was calculated using the Lewis Nomogram formula: Power (Watts)= √ 4.9 x body weight (kg) x √ jump distance (m) [22].

Physical activity level

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ) assessed participants’ physical activity levels. The short form of the questionnaire consists of seven questions. The duration of vigorous physical activity, moderate physical activity, walking, and average daily sitting times is questioned. However, sitting times are not included in the calculations [23]. The physical activity score is calculated by converting the questionnaire score to the Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET- min/week, 1 MET = 3.5 ml/kg/min), corresponding to the basal metabolic rate. Separate calculations are made for each activity. Walking is calculated as MET min/week score [3.3 x walking minutes x number of days walked], moderate activity as MET min/week score [4.0 x activity minutes x number of days the activity is done], and vigorous activity as MET min/week score [8.0 x activity minutes x number of days the activity is done]. All the results are collected, and the physical activity level is determined. Individuals with less than 600 METs of physical activity per week are classified as “inactive”, individuals with 600 to 3000 METs per week are classified as “minimally active”, and individuals with more than 3000 METs of physical activity per week are classified as “very active” [24].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2, R Core Team, 2024) [25]. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and summarized with medians (IQRs) or means (SDs), as appropriate. Group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test in case of non-normality. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and Fisher’s exact test was used for group comparisons. Boxplots were generated to visualize the distribution of primary outcome variables across groups. Boxplots represent median, IQR, and outliers. Group differences in fat-free mass (%), muscle mass (%), fat mass (%), body water (%), protein (%), mineral (%), anaerobic power, and aerobic power were analyzed using ANCOVA, adjusting for age and gender. Box-Cox transformations were applied where necessary; for aerobic power, a rank-based ANCOVA was used due to non-normality. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 [25].

Results

This section presents the findings of descriptive and inferential statistical analyses to compare the study groups’ demographic characteristics, body composition, and performance-related outcomes. Preliminary analyses evaluated distributional assumptions and group comparability across covariates, while the primary analyses focused on identifying group differences in outcome variables after adjusting for potential confounders. All results are reported with corresponding test statistics and significance levels.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate young adults’ body composition with GJH and determine aerobic and anaerobic power. As a result of the study, significant differences were found between the groups in terms of mineral percentage and aerobic performance. Hypermobility has also been found to be strongly associated with aerobic capacity, independent of age and gender.

After adjusting for age and gender, ANCOVA results revealed distinct patterns of association across the examined outcomes. A significant group effect was detected for aerobic power and mineral percentage, suggesting that group membership independently influenced these two variables, potentially reflecting physiological or training-related distinctions. These findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that group-based differences—whether due to athletic status, clinical conditions, or lifestyle factors—can differentially impact aerobic performance and mineral metabolism [26, 27]. In contrast, no significant group differences were observed for other body composition or performance metrics, indicating that group affiliation did not broadly impact these domains.

In the limited number of studies examining BMI in individuals with GJH, it has been reported that there is no difference in BMI between young adults with and without hypermobility [28, 29]. In a recent study evaluating the body composition of adults with GJH in terms of fat mass, free fat mass, and total body water, it was found that body composition was no different from that of healthy participants [5]. BMI, a measure based on weight and height, is a critical parameter for individuals with GJH [30]. However, BMI alone may not fully reflect body composition differences because it does not account for changes in muscle mass, fat distribution, or protein levels. Therefore, in this study, detailed body analysis was performed. In line with the literature, no differences were found between the groups regarding BMI, fat-free mass percentage, muscle mass percentage, fat mass percentage, body water percentage, and protein percentage; only the mineral percentage was lower in individuals with GJH. Altered loading patterns in individuals with GJH may influence bone adaptation processes. As suggested in previous studies [31], changes in movement control during gait could potentially reduce the mechanical stimulus required for optimal bone mineralization. However, further research is needed to confirm this association. Additionally, although a lower mineral percentage was found in individuals with GJH, it should be noted that this value may be sensitive to factors such as hydration status and time of measurement.

Individuals with GJH avoid dynamic activities and prefer more stationary activities like cycling [31]. Adolescents and young adults with asymptomatic GJH reported lower physical function levels than non-hypermobile controls [4, 29]. Alghadir et al. demonstrated that the occurrence of GJH is linked to low levels of physical activity and abnormal cellular oxidative stress [32]. Varol et al. have shown that functional capacity and pulmonary function may affect young adults with GJH [3]. Although different parameters, such as exercise and functional capacity, have been evaluated in individuals with GJH, aerobic power has yet to be investigated. Therefore, in this study, the shuttle run test, a popular way of measuring aerobic power in assessing cardiovascular endurance and estimating VO₂ max, was used, and the aerobic power of the participants was calculated. As a result of the study, it was concluded that aerobic power was worse in individuals with GJH compared to the control group. Due to joint instability in individuals with GJH, muscles work harder to stabilize. This extra demand may prevent the muscles from working efficiently in a continuous aerobic exercise environment, limiting both the duration and intensity of exercise and causing aerobic power to decrease. Although direct comparisons are limited due to the novelty of this approach in the GJH population, previous studies have reported impairments in postural control and balance and increased fatigue perception in individuals with GJH. These findings support the plausibility that compromised neuromuscular control and reduced fatigue resistance may contribute to lower aerobic test performance in this group [33, 34, 35]. In addition, some studies suggest that there may be subtle cardiovascular differences in individuals with joint hypermobility, such as lower stroke volume or altered blood flow regulation [36]. These differences may have affected aerobic power by limiting the efficiency of oxygen delivery and utilization during exercise. However, it should be noted that the shuttle run test is effort-dependent, and individuals with GJH may adopt more cautious movement strategies due to joint instability, potentially biasing performance outcomes. This may have influenced the results and should be considered when interpreting aerobic power findings.

There is limited knowledge regarding the capabilities of individuals with GJH during dynamic performance assessments, such as vertical jump tests that replicate elements of high-intensity sports. Junge et al. discovered that jump length was comparable between children aged 10–15 with GJH and their control counterparts [37]. Scheper et al. found that GJH was associated with decreased functional status as measured by walking distance and jumping capacity [4]. The current study used the vertical jump test to calculate anaerobic power. No difference was found between the groups in terms of anaerobic power. Anaerobic power involves short, intense bursts of activity that place less demand on the joints over time than continuous aerobic activity. Individuals with GJH may better tolerate these short, intense workouts because they do not require long-term joint stability or cardiovascular endurance. This may facilitate rapid, high-intensity force production.

Considering that age and gender may affect joint hypermobility and other clinical outcomes, these parameters were included as covariates in subsequent analyses. In this regard, Age was significantly associated only with mineral percentage, while a nearly significant trend was observed for aerobic power. While its influence was not widespread across all variables, these findings align with known age-related changes in bone density and aerobic capacity, which begin to decline in early adulthood and continue progressively with age [38, 39]. The inclusion of age as a covariate thus remains methodologically justified to account for its potential confounding role.

Gender emerged as a strong and consistent predictor across most outcome variables, including lean and fat mass components, water and protein distribution, and anaerobic and aerobic performance measures. These results align with the established literature documenting gender-based physiological differences in muscle mass, fat distribution, and cardiorespiratory capacity [40, 41]. Specifically, males typically exhibit greater lean mass and performance capacity, whereas females tend to have relatively higher fat mass and different water distribution patterns.

Together, these results highlight the importance of adjusting for age and gender when comparing physiological parameters between groups and underscore aerobic power as the most distinguishing variable in this sample.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Participants’ motivation and effort may influence the use of field tests to assess aerobic and anaerobic power during testing, and these measures may not fully reflect their actual physical capacities. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design may prevent us from drawing causal inferences about the relationships between GJH and the observed differences in body composition and physical performance. Another limitation is using the Tanita BC-601 device for body composition assessment. Although it offers practical and non-invasive measurement, it is less accurate than gold standard methods such as DEXA and may be influenced by hydration status or recent food intake. The limited representation of male participants may also affect the generalizability of the findings, and future studies should aim for a more balanced sex distribution. To address these limitations, future research should consider employing laboratory-based assessments and longitudinal designs to clarify causality and mechanisms better.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights significant differences in body composition and aerobic power between young adults with GJH and those without. The results suggest that individuals with GJH exhibit a lower mineral percentage and reduced aerobic power than controls, with age and gender playing essential roles in shaping these differences. Despite no significant differences in anaerobic power, the findings underscore the importance of considering GJH as a potential factor influencing physical performance, particularly aerobic capacity. In this context, individualized and targeted exercise programs such as low-impact aerobic exercise may be useful to improve aerobic capacity in individuals with GJH. However, it should be noted that all results represent asymptomatic adults with GJH aged 18–25 years, and the effects of these approaches may differ in older age groups or in symptomatic conditions such as Ehlers-Danlos. Future research should incorporate laboratory-based assessments and controlled exercise interventions to explore these relationships further and develop tailored recommendations.
