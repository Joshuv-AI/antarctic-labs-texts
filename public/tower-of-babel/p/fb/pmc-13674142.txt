Deep intronic ANK1 variants causing pseudo‐exon inclusion in hereditary spherocytosis: Whole‐genome sequencing and functional assessment
Authors: Victor Marin, Alexandre Janin, Céline Renoux, Yoann Huguenin, AURAGEN consortium, Stéphanie Dulucq, Louis Lebreton, Philippe Joly, Julian Boutin
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Hereditary Spherocytosis (HS) is a congenital haemolytic anaemia characterized by osmotically fragile, spherical red blood cells (RBCs). 1 It results from defects in RBC membrane–cytoskeleton proteins. Reduced surface‐to‐volume ratio and cellular deformability lead to premature splenic sequestration and destruction, causing anaemia, jaundice and splenomegaly of variable severity. While genetically heterogeneous, HS is most commonly caused by ANK1 mutations, responsible for approximately 50% of cases 1 , 2 , 3 . ANK1 encodes ankyrin‐1, a multi‐domain structural protein linking the spectrin‐actin cytoskeleton to the transmembrane anion exchanger 1 (Band 3), thereby ensuring the mechanical stability and biconcave morphology of the RBC 4 . Ankyrin‐1 deficiency destabilizes this vertical interaction, leading to HS. The established ANK1 mutational spectrum predominantly consists of coding region variants or canonical splice‐site mutations 2 , 5 . Broad access to whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) has revealed deep intronic variants (located >100 bp from splice junctions) as an emerging cause of monogenic disorders often missed by standard panels 6 , 7 . Their pathogenicity typically arises from the creation of cryptic splice sites, which could result in the aberrant inclusion of an intronic sequence (a ‘pseudo‐exon’ [PE]) into the mature messenger Ribonucleic acid (mRNA). This event frequently disrupts the reading frame, introduces a premature termination codon (PTC) and ultimately leads to a significant reduction in the level of functional protein 8 . While this mechanism was established in the hema6 murine model, 9 human deep‐intronic ANK1 variants causing pseudo‐exon inclusion have never been documented in HS. Here, we describe two cases of HS associated with heterozygous deep intronic ANK1 variants identified by WGS and functionally assessed by diagnosis splice assays, highlighting the importance of considering non‐coding regions in the genetic diagnosis of unresolved cases of haemolytic anaemia.

The first case was a young man, now 18, who was diagnosed with HS at the age of two after presenting with asthenia. The clinical presentation was consistent with a de novo or recessive form of HS, with no family history. His clinical course was marked by significant haemolytic anaemia (haemoglobin [Hb] 5.7–8.7 g/dL), requiring three transfusions at age four after Parvovirus B19 aplastic crisis and splenectomy at age six for major splenomegaly. Post‐splenectomy, his Hb levels stabilized above 13.0 g/dL, with only biochemical evidence of compensated residual haemolysis (Table S1). Complications included a history of cholelithiasis, which resolved with ursodeoxycholic acid treatment. Initial diagnostic tests, including osmotic gradient ektacytometry, the presence of spherocytes on the blood film and eosin‐5′‐maleimide (EMA) binding, confirmed the HS diagnosis (Table S1). A first‐tier genetic analysis by a short‐read Next‐Generation Sequencing (NGS) panel was inconclusive, identifying only a heterozygous variant of unknown significance (VUS) in the EPB42 gene (encoding erythrocyte membrane protein band 4.2; NM_001114134.2: c.1115G>A), a finding that did not explain the severe phenotype (Figure 1A).

Similarly, a second patient (Case 2) presented with a comparable diagnostic challenge. This boy was diagnosed 15 days after birth with severe haemolytic anaemia (Hb = 6.0 g/dL), requiring three blood transfusions before the age of 6 months and weekly erythropoietin therapy during the first 9 months of life. A strong paternal family history of autosomal dominant HS was noted, with his father, paternal grandmother and one brother also affected. A maternal family history of glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency was present, and the patient was confirmed to have a hemizygous G6PD variant (Class B) (Viangchan, NM_001360016.2:c.871G>A). The blood film showed characteristic spherocytes and the EMA binding test confirmed the HS diagnosis. Baseline Hb fluctuated between 8.0 and 8.5 g/dL, and he experienced significant clinical repercussions, including marked asthenia, exercise intolerance and persistent jaundice. He suffered a severe aplastic crisis secondary to a Parvovirus B19 infection with a Hb nadir of 3.2 g/dL requiring two further transfusions at the age of 5. A total splenectomy was performed at the age of six, resulting in overall clinical improvement, with Hb levels normalizing above 12.0 g/dL (Table S1). Initial genetic testing identified two VUS in the SPTA1 gene (encoding erythrocytic spectrin alpha 1; NM_003126.4:c.1982G>A and c.3357G>C), all maternally inherited (Figure 1B). Given these remaining molecular diagnostic dilemmas, WGS was performed for both patients within the framework of the French genomic medicine programme and written informed consent for genetic analysis and publication of de‐identified clinical and molecular data was obtained (Supporting Information: Methods). In both patients, rare deep intronic single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs) in ANK1 were identified as strong candidate causes of HS. These variants, located far from the canonical splice sites, would not have been detected by standard exome or panel‐based sequencing and can result in cryptic splice site modulation leading to various functional effects (Figure 1C).

In Case 1, we identified a de novo deep intronic ANK1 variant (NM_000037.4:c.909+1181T>G) absent from GnomAD v4 population database. In silico prediction tools suggested a relative probability of aberrant splicing through the creation of a novel acceptor splice site (acceptor gain, AG = 0.40) and a novel donor splice site (donor gain, DG = 0.35) according to SpliceAI 10 10 k score, while SPiP 11 predicted a 30.67% risk of PE creation. The predicted consequence is the inclusion of a 197 bp PE between exons 9 and 10, leading to a PTC 12 codons downstream (Figure 1D).

In Case 2, trio analysis revealed the deep intronic variant ANK1 NM_000037.4:c.1998+900C>T, which was inherited from the father and segregated with the disease phenotype. This variant is absent from GnomAD v4 population database. In silico analysis predicted two possible outcomes. A major one (SpliceAI 10 k score AG = 0.56/DG = 0.79, SPiP 96.7% risk of PE creation), with the creation of a 103 bp pseudo‐exon between exons 17 and 18, leading to a PTC 24 codons downstream or a possible minor one (96 bp) with a PTC 16 codons downstream (AG = 0.14) (Figure 1E). Pangolin 12 and AlphaGenome 13 supported the SpliceAI‐prioritized pseudo‐exon model. AlphaGenome modelling further predicted, for the ALT allele, a directional increase in donor/acceptor splice‐site probability and in cryptic‐junction usage. This predicted increase was observed in haematopoietic and erythroid/progenitor‐relevant tracks (e.g. K562) and was consistent across all cellular contexts examined (Figure S1, Supporting Information: Methods).

Functional minigene assays were performed as previously described 14 (Figure 2A, Supporting Information: Methods). In case 1, the mutated construct showed a near‐complete disappearance of the normally spliced transcript (247 bp) and the appearance of two aberrant transcripts, a major one of 444 bp (197 bp PE insertion) and a minor one of 282 bp (35 bp PE insertion). This second PE was not predicted by SpliceAI and leads to a PTC 5 codons downstream (Figure 2B). This result supports a splice‐disruptive effect of the c.909+1181T>G variant through activation of cryptic splice sites, providing functional evidence for pathogenicity. In case 2, the mutated construct produced a single aberrant transcript of 350 bp, with a complete absence of the normally spliced 247 bp product. This indicates that the c.1998+900C>T variant results in complete loss of the canonical product in the minigene assay, through inclusion of the 103 bp pseudo‐exon predicted by SpliceAI (Figure 2C). A reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) on patient blood Ribonucleic acid (RNA) confirmed the presence of the same aberrant transcript with similar ratios of expression on gels and sashimi‐plots, supporting the in vitro findings and validating the splicing defect in vivo (Figure 2D,E).

Both patients harbour rare heterozygous deep intronic variants that generate pseudo‐exon inclusion and ankyrin‐1 haploinsufficiency. The pseudo‐exon insertions are predicted to introduce PTCs, consistent with loss of canonical ANK1 function. Together with rarity, segregation/de novo status, phenotype specificity and functional evidence, these findings support the American College of Medicale Genetics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) pathogenic or likely pathogenic classification 15 . Minigene transcript ratios may provide a hypothesis‐generating explanation for disease severity differences: Case 2 showed complete loss of the canonical product in vitro and severe familial HS, whereas Case 1 retained a minor canonical fraction and achieved near‐normal Hb levels after splenectomy.

Given the two‐patient cohort, the non‐erythroid minigene context and the complexity of genotype–phenotype correlations in HS, this interpretation remains exploratory. Splice predictors provide supportive evidence but do not replace patient‐derived transcriptomic validation. Patient‐derived CD34+ progenitors differentiated along the erythroid lineage would constitute an alternative relevant validation approach, while patient‐derived whole‐blood RNA, when available, provides complementary in vivo evidence from the haematopoietic compartment.

From a diagnostic standpoint, these cases underscore three critical insights. First, they demonstrate the superiority of genome‐wide analysis over exome‐based strategies since both variants lie >900 bp from the nearest exon and would have been missed by ‘exon‐centred’ targeted panels. Complete intronic targeted panel or WGS coupled with dedicated splice‐prediction pipelines is therefore key for ‘unsolved’ membranopathies. Second, functional validation remains imperative, as minigene assays can rapidly convert a VUS into a fully characterized pathogenic variant, with results that may differ from in silico predictors. Third, molecular diagnosis has direct clinical relevance, offering families clarity on recurrence risks and guiding long‐term management, including transfusion planning and the assessment of splenectomy efficacy. These cases represent first examples of human ANK1 variants located several 100 base pairs from canonical splice junctions and functionally shown to activate pseudo‐exon inclusion in HS. They extend the ANK1 mutational spectrum and highlight the contribution of cryptic splicing to RBC disorders. We propose that deep‐intronic ANK1 variants should be systematically considered in the molecular work‐up of unresolved hereditary haemolytic anaemia, particularly when first‐tier short‐read NGS panels or exomes are inconclusive.
