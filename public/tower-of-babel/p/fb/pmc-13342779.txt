Effect of Intramuscular Adrenaline on Histamine‐Induced Hypotension: A Randomised Placebo‐Controlled Pilot Trial
Authors: Matthias Weiss‐Tessbach, Al Medina Dizdarevic, Thorsten Bischof, Christa Firbas, Alexander Taschner, Eva‐Luise Ritter‐Hobl, Barbara Steinlechner, Eva Fischer, Lorenz Sonnberger, Georg Gelbenegger, Jolanta M. Siller‐Matula, Christian Schoergenhofer, Bernd Jilma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) is the first‐line treatment for anaphylaxis. This recommendation is based purely on consensus, not randomised controlled trials. In humans, hypotension and shock strongly correlate with plasma histamine concentrations. The efficacy of intramuscular adrenaline in reversing histamine‐induced hypotension remains unproven. This trial evaluated whether intramuscular adrenaline reverses histamine‐induced shock.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory open‐label trial and a confirmatory randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, crossover trial. Healthy volunteers received a 15‐min continuous intravenous histamine infusion to induce hypotension. At 5 min, participants received either intramuscular adrenaline (300–500 μg) or placebo. A second adrenaline dose was administered if the mean arterial pressure (MAP) remained below 60 mmHg at 10 min. The primary endpoint was area under the effect curve for change in MAP.ResultsDuring histamine‐induced hypotension, intramuscular adrenaline failed to produce a pressor response and was indistinguishable from placebo in the majority of participants. Excluding two subjects who needed a simultaneous dose reduction of histamine, only 5 of 20 participants (25%; 95% CI 8.7%–49.1%) demonstrated a transient MAP recovery after a single dose. A second adrenaline dose administered to participants who did not reach a MAP > 60 mmHg also failed to reverse hypotension. The primary outcome in the confirmatory cohort, excluding two subjects with histamine dose adjustments, was not different between treatments (adrenaline‐placebo: 7 mmHg·min, 95% CI −2 to 25; p = 0.06).ConclusionIn this study of a limited number of healthy human subjects, intramuscular adrenaline injections did not provide a sustained response in severe histamine‐mediated hypotension in a majority of recipients.Trial RegistrationCTIS (2022–003591‐16) and ISRCTN (Nr. 11335622; https://doi.org/10.1186/ISRCTN11335622)

Introduction

Anaphylaxis, a serious generalised or systemic allergic or hypersensitivity reaction [1], is characterised by the potential for life‐threatening circulatory compromise or airway obstruction [2]. The incidence of anaphylaxis appears to have risen in recent decades ago, with fatal outcomes documented in approximately 0.5%–1% of hospitalisations [3]. The updated World Allergy Organisation (WAO) grading system outlines a 5‐grade scale to classify the spectrum of severity in allergic reactions, where Grade 1 covers mild symptoms (e.g., localised urticaria, nasal symptoms), and Grades 4 (systolic blood pressure below 90 mm Hg) and 5 (respiratory or cardiac arrest) cover the most severe reactions [4]. Guidelines widely recommend intramuscular adrenaline as the first‐line treatment of anaphylaxis, administered at 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg, with doses repeated every 5 to 15 min if symptoms persist [2]. The recommendation for adrenaline is largely based on expert consensus, retrospective clinical observations, and extrapolation from its use in other emergency conditions [2, 5]. Controlled interventional studies in anaphylaxis have not been conducted, because of the unpredictable course of anaphylaxis, low incidence, and the need for immediate treatment. Experts question the feasibility of conducting a randomised controlled trial in anaphylaxis [6, 7]. Due to the absence of data from controlled clinical trials, it is difficult to impossible to perform a definitive risk–benefit assessment for the use of adrenaline in anaphylaxis [8].

Insect sting challenge models have provided valuable insights into the pathophysiology of human anaphylaxis three decades. They demonstrated a rapid rise in histamine and a close correlation between histamine plasma concentration and the degree of hypotension in Grade 4 anaphylaxis [9]. However, inter‐individual variability in responses among sensitised individuals is substantial. Sting challenges carry significant safety concerns due to the unpredictable severity of reactions, which can include profound and protracted hypotension, with a mean arterial pressure (MAP) reduction of up to 100 mmHg [9], or cardiac arrest [10]. Consequently, there is a need for an alternative experimental model which replicates key features of anaphylaxis such as hypotension, which would allow investigation of therapeutic interventions under controlled and reproducible conditions with minimal safety risk for trial participants.

Histamine, a central mediator in anaphylaxis [11], induces flushing, urticaria, bronchoconstriction, and hypotension. An intravenous histamine infusion is a safe and controllable method to induce hypotension in humans. Infusion rates of 0.5 to 1.33 μg/kg/min dose‐dependently reduce blood pressure, with symptoms resolving within minutes after cessation of the infusion due to the short half‐life of histamine [12, 13, 14].

Controlled human histamine infusion studies have mainly defined symptom profiles including effects of H1 and H2 receptor blockers on headache [15, 16], characterised haemodynamic responses and their modulation by H1 and H2 receptor blockers [13, 17, 18], measured catecholamine responses [18], and reported alterations of electrocardiograms [19].

The present work is using this controllable model to evaluate the efficacy of intramuscular adrenaline in reversing histamine‐induced hypotension in humans.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This single‐centre study was conducted in two parts at the Medical University of Vienna (MUV), Austria. There was an open‐label exploratory cohort, followed by a randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled confirmatory cohort. The exploratory phase tested a 300 μg dose of intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) to evaluate whether a lower dose, potentially associated with fewer adverse effects, would be sufficient to counteract histamine‐induced hypotension. The confirmatory phase used a blinded 500 μg dose to assess efficacy at the guideline‐recommended dosage under placebo‐controlled conditions and minimise bias in outcome assessment.

Eligible participants were adults of any sex, without significant medical comorbidities, not receiving antihypertensive therapy or histamine receptor blockers, with no contraindications to adrenaline, and no history of severe anaphylaxis. Race and ethnicity were self‐reported by participants to describe cohort representativeness; no race‐based algorithms were used.

All participants provided written informed consent before any trial‐related activity. This report focuses on the results of the pharmacological intervention period.

Procedures

Details on study‐related procedures are described in the Appendix S1.

Participants first underwent a dose‐finding period to determine the individualised histamine infusion rate required to achieve a MAP of approximately 50 mmHg. Histamine infusion solutions were prepared by diluting 1 mL of 10 mg/mL histamine dihydrochloride (ALK‐Prick, ALK‐Abelló Arzneimittel GmbH, Austria; corresponding to 6.04 mg/mL histamine base) in 500 mL of saline (B. Braun, Germany), resulting in a final concentration of 0.012 mg/mL histamine base. Infusion rates were initiated at 1.0 μg/kg/min and titrated downward as needed to 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, or 0.125 μg/kg/min. Adjustments were made based on haemodynamic responses with a target MAP of approximately 50 mmHg and tolerability. Infusion rates were reduced if excessive hypotension or clinical symptoms indicative of haemodynamic compromise were experienced, for example, visual field restrictions. Infusions were continued throughout each 15‐min period, including during injections at 5 and 10 min.

The first six participants in the exploratory group received four 15‐min histamine infusions, each separated by a 45 min washout period. Given the short plasma half‐life of infused histamine (approximately 2–3 min), this washout duration was considered sufficient for plasma concentrations to return to baseline before subsequent infusions.

All subsequent participants underwent two 15‐min periods with a 45 min washout period, followed by a third infusion 4 h after the second.

In the exploratory group, participants received 300 μg adrenaline (Suprarenin, Cheplapharm, Germany) in 0.3 mL saline intramuscularly in either the second or third period, administered 5 min after the start of the histamine infusion, using a 25‐gauge needle. The second dose of adrenaline was injected when the MAP remained below 60 mmHg at 10 min after start of the histamine infusion in the same thigh. The anterolateral thigh was used for all intramuscular injections.

In the confirmatory group, participants were randomised to receive either 500 μg intramuscular adrenaline in 0.5 mL saline or 0.5 mL saline as placebo, with the respective alternate treatment administered during the subsequent period, according to the randomisation schedule.

The investigational treatment was administered 5 min after the start of the histamine infusion and repeated 5 min later if MAP remained below 60 mmHg.

Outcomes

The primary objective of the exploratory phase was to evaluate the feasibility and tolerability of a histamine infusion and to characterise the variability in MAP response. This analysis focused on establishing a predictable dose–response relationship and the infusion rates required to consistently achieve the target level of hypotension. In the confirmatory randomised placebo‐controlled crossover phase, the primary endpoint was area under the curve (AUC) for change in MAP over 7.5 to 12.5 min, corresponding to 15 MAP measurements, after histamine infusion start (2.5 min after adrenaline administration) expressed in mmHg·min.

The response rate to intramuscular adrenaline was defined as achieving a MAP above 60 mmHg with or without the need for a second dose. Participants requiring an adjustment of the histamine dose within a treatment period were excluded, and the response was assessed in the modified intention‐to‐treat population. Secondary endpoints included plasma concentrations of histamine and safety parameters. The rationale for the chosen MAP target and time window, as well as details of histamine and adrenaline analyses, are provided in the Appendix S1.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with the 95% confidence interval (CI), estimated by bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations), as specified for each analysis. Details of the sample size calculation are described in the Appendix S1.

The primary endpoint was the AUC for change in MAP over 7.5 to 12.5 min after infusion start (2.5 to 7.5 min post‐adrenaline), calculated by the trapezoidal rule and expressed in mmHg·min using the 7.5‐min time point as a baseline. Additionally, the median MAP increase was calculated.

The intention‐to‐treat population was defined as all participants enrolled, analysed according to assigned treatment, irrespective of protocol deviations.

The modified intention‐to‐treat population was defined as all participants enrolled excluding two participants requiring histamine dose adjustments during the treatment period.

The per‐protocol population was defined as confirmatory crossover participants who received both treatments as randomised and had no histamine dose adjustment during the respective study period.

Between‐treatment comparisons used the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for paired data and the Mann–Whitney U test for unpaired data. The primary endpoint (per‐protocol MAP‐AUC, 7.5–12.5 min) was analysed without multiplicity adjustment. For the family of three post hoc MAP‐derived endpoints (intention‐to‐treat MAP‐AUC, per‐protocol MAP change, and intention‐to‐treat MAP change; all 7.5–12.5 min), multiplicity was addressed using the Holm‐Bonferroni method (two‐sided family‐wise α = 0.05).

Responder analysis was conducted by combining participants from both cohorts who received adrenaline without histamine dose adjustment during the treatment period. Response was defined as achieving at least a temporary MAP above 60 mmHg. No participant in the placebo treatment met this response criterion. A 95% confidence interval for the proportion of responders was calculated based on the binomial distribution using the Clopper–Pearson exact method. Statistical analyses and data visualisation were conducted using R Studio (version 2025.05.0), Python (version 3.11.5), and Microsoft Excel (version 2505).

Primary Outcome

In a combined analysis of both dose groups (300 and 500 μg), only 5 of 20 participants (25%; 95% CI 8.7%–49.1%) achieved a MAP above 60 mmHg after a single intramuscular adrenaline injection. Two subjects were excluded because they needed a simultaneous dose reduction of histamine (see Appendix S1). A transient response to adrenaline was observed in 3 of 10 participants from the exploratory cohort and 2 of 10 participants from the confirmatory cohort. However, this effect was not sustained, as MAP subsequently fell below 60 mmHg again, reaching levels comparable to those in non‐responders or those who had received a placebo injection. MAP response was similar in women and men (Figure S3). A sensitivity analysis including the two subjects who required a reduction of the histamine infusion rates indicates a slightly higher responder rate (7 of 22 participants or 32%; 95% CI 14%–55%).

There was no significant change in MAP in the placebo treatment (Figure 2). AUC for change in MAP in the per‐protocol analysis, excluding two subjects with histamine dose adjustments and one who did not receive placebo (n = 9), was borderline different in a paired analysis between treatments: adrenaline median 9.7 mmHg·min (95% CI 1.5 to 22.3) and placebo −1.3 mmHg·min (95% CI −5.8 to 11.7) with a between‐treatment median difference of 7 mmHg·min (95% CI −2 to 24.5, p = 0.06; Wilcoxon signed‐rank test) (Table 2).

Secondary Outcomes

The median baseline plasma histamine concentration across all periods was 0.2 ng/mL (95% CI 0–0.7; n = 22). During the 15‐min histamine infusion, median peak plasma histamine concentration (C max) was 12.5 ng/mL (95% CI 10.6–14.2; range 5.2–19) at a median time of 15 min (range 5–15) after the start of infusion.

In the confirmatory group the median plasma concentration of adrenaline 5 min after intramuscular adrenaline injection was 399 pg/mL (95% CI 267–759). In the placebo group it was 150 pg/mL (95% CI 86–287) (Figure 3).

Safety

A total of 285 adverse events (AEs) were reported across all individual periods of this study. Most AEs were mild and transient, and no serious adverse events occurred. Adverse events were most frequently classified under nervous system disorders (104 events in 20 participants) and vascular disorders (60 events in 22 participants). The most commonly reported AEs included flushing (100%), headache (86%), dysgeusia (64%), and palpitations (45%). The median time to onset for flushing was 1 min. No participant developed urticaria or angioedema. Safety outcomes and a detailed description of adverse events are provided in the Table S1.

Discussion

This trial is the first to rigorously evaluate intramuscular adrenaline in established histamine‐induced hypotension. Despite achieving plasma adrenaline concentrations recently regarded as ‘therapeutic’ [20], the standard adrenaline doses failed to produce a clinically meaningful haemodynamic response in the majority of participants. The brief, transient response seen in a minority of individuals was not even sustained for 10 min. Even a second adrenaline dose did not increase blood pressure in non‐responders.

The controlled histamine infusion rapidly induced hypotension, closely mimicking the haemodynamic collapse after insect sting challenge in grade 4 anaphylaxis [9].

Our pharmacokinetic analysis aligns with prior research demonstrating similar absorption profiles of intramuscular adrenaline injections [21, 22]. Although plasma adrenaline concentrations vary by dose and device [21], plasma levels achieved 5 min after injection were comparable to previous reports testing the Epipen and Anapen devices [21, 22]. In healthy, normotensive individuals, standard intramuscular doses only modestly and inconsistently increase systolic blood pressure [23]. Importantly, our results mirror preclinical observations in which intramuscular adrenaline failed to reverse anaphylactic or histamine‐induced shock across different animal models [24, 25].

These data are consistent with emerging clinical recommendations that emphasise escalation to advanced therapies when two intramuscular doses fail and caution against over‐reliance on auto‐injectors as definitive management [26]. As antihistamines are fairly ineffective at high histamine concentrations [13, 17, 27], the haemodynamic findings underscore the need for alternative interventions capable of rapidly restoring vascular tone during severe, histamine‐mediated hypotension [28].

Furthermore, our findings challenge the validity of pharmacokinetic targets currently used in the regulatory assessment of novel adrenaline formulations.

Regulatory bodies, such as the US FDA, have adopted plasma adrenaline concentrations of 100 to 200 pg/mL as arbitrary efficacy benchmarks, extrapolated from studies showing haemodynamic changes after intravenous adrenaline infusions in healthy volunteers [20, 29]. Although the agency explicitly acknowledges the limitations and uncertainty of extrapolating these thresholds to patients experiencing anaphylaxis, they serve as regulatory reference points.

However, our experimental results reveal a critical discrepancy. Following placebo injection, the body's intrinsic adrenergic response to histamine‐induced hypotension resulted in endogenous plasma adrenaline concentrations that reached levels currently regarded as ‘therapeutic’ for new adrenaline formulations. Despite these concentrations, haemodynamic compromise persisted. After adrenaline injections, its plasma concentrations exceeded those thresholds but were still insufficient to reverse established, histamine‐mediated hypotension. This observation questions the utility of using those arbitrary pharmacokinetic thresholds as proxies for clinical efficacy during an episode of histamine‐induced shock.

The controlled histamine infusion offers several strengths. The plasma histamine concentrations achieved (~12.5 ng/mL) are highly comparable to the median levels reported in patients experiencing Grade 4 anaphylaxis after insect stings [9]. Furthermore, the reproducibility of this hypotension across all study periods (Figure S2) confirms the robustness of our experimental setup for evaluating acute cardiovascular interventions. The correlation between the blood pressure drop and histamine levels is comparable between histamine infusion in healthy volunteers and patients with Grade 4 anaphylaxis (Figure S2). Thus, the observed degree of MAP decline is consistent with clinical data from anaphylaxis, implying that histamine is the major if not sole driver of the hypotension observed during severe anaphylactic shock.

The model also offers high controllability and safety. In accordance with histamine's short half‐life [30], hypotension and the majority of symptoms resolved rapidly, typically within 2 min of stopping the infusion. Finally, the predictable adverse event profile, with symptoms like flushing, headache, and palpitations clustering into distinct early and late phases, aligns well with the known effects of histamine [15, 31].

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the sample size was modest, but the confidence intervals indicate no clinically relevant benefit in a majority of recipients of adrenaline. Using the per‐protocol crossover data (MAP‐AUC 7.5 to 12.5 min, n = 9 pairs), the observed within‐subject mean difference was 10.8 mmHg·min (approximately 2.2 mmHg over 5 min), requiring 16 participants for 80% power. However, a clinically meaningful 10 mmHg sustained increase over 5 min (50 mmHg·min AUC) would require only two participants.

Second, our cohort consisted of relatively young and healthy adults, which may limit the generalizability of findings. We deliberately excluded elderly participants, a population at highest risk of fatal anaphylaxis [32], due to the potential for histamine‐induced coronary spasms [33]. We also excluded patients with asthma due to their heightened sensitivity to histamine‐induced bronchoconstriction, which induces an immediate severe reduction of vital capacity even at a fraction of the currently used histamine doses [34]. In addition, anaphylaxis in younger, otherwise healthy individuals may be more likely to be self‐limited and accompanied by stronger endogenous compensatory responses, potentially reducing the generalizability of our findings to higher‐risk patient populations. Nevertheless, reduced β‐adrenergic responsiveness with ageing [35] and concomitant medications (e.g., β‐blockers) could further attenuate the haemodynamic response to intramuscular adrenaline; therefore, effectiveness in more vulnerable patient populations may be even lower than observed in this study.

Third, the histamine infusion itself, while effective at inducing reproducible hypotension, does not fully replicate the complex pathophysiology of clinical anaphylaxis, which may involve other mediators with uncertain relevance in humans [11]. However, huge amounts of histamine (100 mg) are pre‐stored in mast cells, which can be immediately released upon activation. In contrast, clinical data show that other mediators such as leukotrienes do not correlate with the severity of hypotension in hypersensitivity reactions [36, 37]. This underscores the central role of histamine in causing anaphylactic shock. Our data shows a clear exposure‐response relationship of histamine with MAP in our dataset and in the context of a comparison with historic experimentally induced insect sting anaphylaxis (Figure S2). Supporting the model's relevance for studying the haemodynamic component of median histamine levels observed in grade 4 anaphylaxis after hymenoptera stings [9].

Nevertheless, we observed no angioedema or urticaria, although these cutaneous manifestations occur in up to 58% of anaphylaxis cases [38]. The absence of angioedema (particularly laryngeal edema) is beneficial from a safety perspective for a model. Urticaria is due to extremely high local histamine concentrations [39] at the sites of mast cell degranulation, which cannot be expected when histamine is infused directly into the blood stream. Fourth, although this model was used to study the haemodynamic component of anaphylaxis and reproduces severe hypotension comparable to Grade 4 reactions, intravenous histamine infusion cannot replicate real‐world anaphylaxis with ongoing or repeated antigen exposure and route‐dependent kinetics that vary widely across triggers such as intravenous drugs, foods, or venoms; therefore, extrapolation to heterogeneous clinical anaphylaxis should be made with caution. Finally, the impact of adrenalin as a potentially life‐saving measure has not been studied in the extreme situation of Grade 5 reactions. Yet, non‐survivors of anaphylaxis do not even respond to an average of 5 mg of adrenaline before admission [40], or cumulative infusions of 15 mg epinephrine in anaphylaxis induced by neuromuscular blocking agents [41].

A long‐standing hypothesis posits that adrenaline's efficacy is partly due to a mast cell‐stabilising effect mediated via β‐adrenergic receptors. This concept probably originates from in vitro research, showing that suprapharmacological adrenaline concentrations, 100 to 1000 times higher than the arbitrary threshold suggested by the FDA, inhibited histamine release [42, 43, 44]. Despite an extensive literature search, we found no evidence that adrenaline at standard therapeutic doses may inhibit mast cell degranulation in vivo in humans. Ideally, the hypothesis that intramuscular adrenaline prevents further mast cell degranulation should also be tested in a well‐controlled trial in humans.

Conclusions

In summary, intramuscular adrenaline—despite achieving plasma concentrations ‘supposedly therapeutic’ —was ineffective in reversing histamine‐induced hypotension in the majority of subjects. This finding highlights a critical limitation of the recommended first‐line therapy and underscores the need for more potent and targeted interventions. Given that intramuscular adrenaline remains the guideline‐endorsed first‐line treatment worldwide, these results warrant careful re‐evaluation of its role in established, histamine‐mediated shock.
