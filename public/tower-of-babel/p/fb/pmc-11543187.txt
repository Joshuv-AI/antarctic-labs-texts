Rhabdomyolysis is the breakdown of skeletal muscle and subsequent release of intracellular materials into the bloodstream, potentially leading to life-threatening complications such as acute kidney injury, hepatitis, hypovolemia, cardiac arrhythmias, and cardiac arrest. Inciting events are categorized as either traumatic or non-traumatic. Examples of traumatic etiologies are crush injuries, direct injuries, prolonged immobilization, severe burns, and resistance to physical restraints. Non-traumatic causes include seizures, severe exertion, heat stroke, prolonged fasting, malignant hyperthermia, and adverse drug reactions to neuroleptics and statins. The release of intracellular components of muscle such as creatine phosphokinase, myoglobin, potassium, and lactic acid, into the bloodstream results in the clinical presentation and adverse outcomes of rhabdomyolysis. Patients with rhabdomyolysis often present with generalized weakness, muscle pains, and dark urine ranging from brown to red, in addition to other nonspecific symptoms such as nausea, fever, and vomiting1. Although the presenting symptoms are often generalized and systematic, a clinically significant manifestation of rhabdomyolysis and one of the early complications is acute renal injury2. The released creatine phosphokinase and myoglobin result in pigment nephropathy, which causes acute necrosis of the kidney tubules ultimately leading to the patient presenting with signs of intrinsic acute renal injury. Other possible complications are the release of lactic acid could result in metabolic acidosis, and the release of potassium can, in severe cases, result in cardiac arrhythmias or even arrest.

This case report focuses on a patient presenting with acute kidney injury from statin-induced rhabdomyolysis. Clinical cases of statin-induced rhabdomyolysis are highly significant, as they are higher than those seen in clinical trials since clinical trials often exclude patients with predisposing risk factors, and as such do not encompass the entire patient population on statins3.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 69-year-old female with a significant medical history, including hypertension (HTN) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) for 15 years. One month prior to admission, she experienced a non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). She was admitted to our hospital due to a recent onset of increased shortness of breath over the past week, generalized body aches persisting for a week, and decreased urine output for the last 3 days. She had no history of fever, rash, joint pain, altered sensorium, chest pain, cough, and expectoration. The patient’s current medications included aspirin 75 mg, clopidogrel 75 mg, metoprolol-XL 12.5 mg, metformin-SR 500 mg, linagliptin 5 mg, amlodipine 5 mg, and rosuvastatin 40 mg, all administered once daily for 10 days during the hospital stay and discharged with the continuation of all these medications with regular follow-up. Following her NSTEMI diagnosis, she opted for conservative management and declining coronary angiography (CAG) and was discharged with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) and a statin. At the time of discharge, her serum creatinine was 0.904 mg/dl.

Upon admission, her vital signs were recorded as follows: blood pressure 150/90 mmHg, respiratory rate 20/min, oxygen saturation 95% with nasal cannula, and pulse rate 86 beats per minute. The patient was conscious and well-oriented to time, place, and person. Systemic examination revealed normal vesicular breath sounds in the chest without any crackles and rhonchi; normal heart sounds without any murmur or other adventitious sounds. Neurological evaluation disclosed paraparesis with proximal muscle involvement greater than distal muscles. Power at the hip joint was graded at 3/5, while at the knee and ankle, it was 4/5. Plantar reflex was flexor, bilateral knee jerks and ankle jerks were absent, with no upper limb involvement. Sensory function, cranial nerve, and higher function were within normal limits. The patient complained of no band-like sensation. Her oxygen saturation at room air was 100%. Abdominal examination revealed a soft abdomen without tenderness or organomegaly.

Laboratory investigations unveiled impaired renal function with a serum creatinine of 9.28 mg/dl and normal serum potassium levels. Liver function tests were also impaired aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 804 international unit (IU) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 379 IU. Urine routine examination showed no red blood cells but 3+ albumin. Total creatine phosphokinase (CPK) level was elevated at 33 775 IU and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was 1290 IU. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis indicated high anion gap metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.28, PCO2 of 22.5, and HCO3 of 14. Electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed a normal sinus rhythm, while echocardiography (ECHO) displayed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 40%, mild mitral regurgitation (MR), and mild tricuspid regurgitation (TR). USG abdomen showed normal kidney size and echotexture, normal liver size and echotexture, and no evidence of free fluid in the abdomen.

Initial conservative management included intravenous fluids, which is normal saline (0.9%); however, due to inadequate improvement in urine output, hemodialysis was initiated as continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). A total of four hemodialysis sessions were performed, the first three sessions were performed at an interval of 24 h, then the last maintenance hemodialysis after 48 h of three sessions. The patient weakness of the bilateral lower limb returns to normal condition with a power of 5/5 after three sessions of hemodialysis. Rosuvastatin was discontinued. She was treated at the general ward. After two sessions of hemodialysis, her power improved to 4/5 at the hip joint and 5/5 at the knee and ankle joint. After the third hemodialysis, power returned to normal 5/5 at all hip, knee, and ankle joints. Her condition improved. The patient was discharged at the time of writing the article.

Clinical discussion

Statins are one of the most commonly prescribed and most effective drugs for the management of hypercholesterolemia. They inhibit the hydroxymethylglutaryl coenzymes A (HMG CoA) reductase enzyme and block the synthesis of cholesterol. This, in turn, leads to an upregulation of low-density lipid (LDL) receptors, increasing the uptake of LDL by the liver from the blood. In addition to statins’ effect of reducing cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides, statins also have anti-inflammatory, anti-antioxidant, antiproliferative, and immunomodulatory effects. These effects are due to the inhibition of the synthesis of isoprenoid intermediates required for the activation of intracellular signaling pathways4. Examples of statins include rosuvastatin, simvastatin, atorvastatin, lovastatin, pravastatin, etc. Statins are used in several conditions, for example, hyperlipidemia, dysbetalipoproteinemia, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, familial hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia. Despite their broad acceptance and generally favorable tolerability profile, statins are not without adverse reactions. Adverse effects include myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, hepatotoxicity, and an association with diabetes mellitus.

Rhabdomyolysis is characterized by the death of muscle cells and the release of intracellular contents into systemic circulation5. Rhabdomyolysis is an uncommon adverse effect of statins. The rate of statin-induced rhabdomyolysis, as per the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System, is 0.3–13.5 cases per 1 000 000 statin prescriptions6. The mechanism of statin-induced rhabdomyolysis deals with the inactivation of Rab GTPase, which interrupts intracellular vesicle trafficking. This impairs mitochondrial respiration, causing a depletion of intracellular ATP, which results in cell death7. Risk factors for statin-induced rhabdomyolysis can be either endogenous or exogenous. Endogenous risk factors include advanced age (80 years), small body frame, multisystem disease (e.g. renal dysfunction, hepatic dysfunction, thyroid disorders), hypertriglyceridemia, and metabolic muscle diseases. Exogenous risk factors include alcohol consumption, heavy exercise, and surgery with severe metabolic demands. Taking drugs that affect the CYP-450 system is an important risk factor for statin-induced rhabdomyolysis. Examples of these drugs include fibrates, cyclosporine, azole antifungals, macrolide antibiotics, HIV protease inhibitors, verapamil, amiodarone, and warfarin3.

Rosuvastatin is an HMG CoA reductase inhibitor that was approved by the FDA in 2003. Rosuvastatin has a unique chemical structure, which is responsible for its increased affinity and tight binding with the HMG CoA reductase. This is why it has the lowest IC50 among all statin drugs. Trials have shown that the reduction in LDL-C by rosuvastatin is greater than that of atorvastatin, simvastatin, and pravastatin. Studies have also shown that rosuvastatin is more effective at a starting dose of 10 mg when compared to 10 mg atorvastatin, 20 mg pravastatin, and 20 mg simvastatin. Rosuvastatin is a well-tolerated drug with minimal adverse effects. The most common adverse effects reported by patients in phase II and III clinical trials were pharyngitis, pain, headache, flu syndrome, and myalgia8.

Most cases of statin-induced rhabdomyolysis are due to simvastatin and atorvastatin6. Rosuvastatin is hydrophilic, so its distribution into extrahepatic tissues is very low. This is one proposed reason why the incidence of rhabdomyolysis amongst patients taking rosuvastatin is much less compared to other statins7. Even then, most cases of rhabdomyolysis are characterized only by lab abnormalities and rarely progress to acute renal failure requiring hemodialysis for treatment5. In our case, the patient did not have any risk factors predisposing her to statin-induced rhabdomyolysis. She was appropriately treated with i.v. (intravenous) fluids, which are normal saline (0.9%) and immediate cessation of rosuvastatin therapy. The patient was not using any medication that can cause drug reactions with rosuvastatin. So, it is an isolated condition only with rosuvastatin not because of any interaction with any of the drugs that the patient was using. A total of four hemodialysis sessions were performed; first three sessions were performed at an interval of 24 h, then the last maintenance hemodialysis after 48 h of three sessions. The patient weakness of the bilateral lower limb returns to normal condition with a power of 5/5 after three sessions of hemodialysis. Rosuvastatin was discontinued. She was treated at the general ward. After two sessions of hemodialysis, her power improved to 4/5 at the hip joint and 5/5 at the knee and ankle joint. After third hemodialysis, power returned to normal 5/5 at all hip, knee, and ankle joints. Her condition improved. Rosuvastatin was discontinued with cardiac consultation for 1 month with risk benefits assessment. The patient was advised to follow up after 2 weeks on an OPD basis.

Conclusions

Physicians need to be aware of the potential of developing severe rhabdomyolysis in patients taking rosuvastatin to prevent morbidity, extended hospital stay, and financial loss.
