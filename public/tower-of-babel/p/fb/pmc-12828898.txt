Milestones in Clostridium perfringens research since 1995
Authors: Bruce A. McClane, Jihong Li, Francisco A. Uzal, Julian I. Rood
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clostridium perfringens is an important human and veterinary pathogen, as well as a common member of the normal intestinal microbiota. By applying molecular approaches, substantial progress has been achieved since 1995 in understanding the pathogenicity and biology of this gram-positive, anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium. For example, there is now a much improved understanding of the structure and action of its “legacy” toxins (i.e., CPA, PFO, ETX, CPB, ITX and CPE) and the pathogenic importance of many of those toxins has now been clearly demonstrated. In addition, several new toxins have been discovered since 1995 and at least one of those new toxins (NetB) has been clearly linked to disease. The importance and diversity of mobile genetic elements, particularly conjugative plasmids, for pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance is now established. Several regulators controlling virulence gene expression have been identified and, in some cases, their regulatory mechanisms have been clarified. For some regulators, their importance for virulence has also been demonstrated. Lastly, there is also improved knowledge of sporulation and germination mechanisms for C. perfringens, as well as how sporulation contributes to disease transmission and pathogenesis for this bacterium. Despite these advances, some important questions about C. perfringens remain to be explored.

Introduction

After briefly describing the biology and pathogenic importance of Clostridium perfringens, this review will highlight major research advances for understanding C. perfringens genetics, physiology, and virulence since ~1995. This is an appropriate timespan to consider for two reasons. First, 1995 coincides with publication of the initial issue of Anaerobe, a journal that often publishes C. perfringens research. Second, 1995 marks the date of the first International Conference on the Molecular Biology and Pathogenesis of the Clostridia (Clostpath). Clostpath-1 was heavily C. perfringens-oriented and included the first presentations, in an international setting, describing the revolutionary progress in C. perfringens research that was being achieved using modern molecular techniques.

Brief summary of the biology and pathogenic importance of C. perfringens

C. perfringens is a gram-positive spore-former that grows incredibly quickly, with a doubling time that can be as little as ~10 min [1]. While classified as anaerobic because it does not grow in the presence of oxygen, C. perfringens is relatively aerotolerant with respect to its survival [1]. This bacterium lacks classical flagella-mediated motility but does exhibit gliding motility mediated by type IV pili [2]. It has a broad natural distribution, including a presence in soil, decaying organic matter, wastewater and the normal intestinal microbiota [1].

C. perfringens is also a very significant human and animal pathogen [1]. It ranks among the major causes of histotoxic diseases, most notably the highly-lethal muscle infection clostridial myonecrosis (gas gangrene). Additionally, C. perfringens is responsible for several important diseases originating in the intestines, including both enterocolitis and enterotoxemia (where a toxin(s) produced in the intestines is absorbed into the circulation to damage other organs such as the brain). In humans, the most prevalent C. perfringens intestinal infections are type F food poisoning, which affects millions of people annually, and antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD) [1,3]. This bacterium also causes costly, and often fatal, diseases of livestock such as avian necrotic enteritis and enterotoxemia of ruminants [1].

The pathogenicity of C. perfringens largely involves its formidable >20 toxin arsenal, as discussed later. However, isolate-to-isolate differences exist in toxin production. This variability is exploited in a toxin typing classification scheme (Table 1) that currently uses a multiplex PCR to assign C. perfringens isolates to one of seven types (A-G), based upon which of six toxin genes is carried by an isolate [4]. There are well-established toxinotype; disease associations, e.g., C. perfringens type F food poisoning is caused by type F isolates, while gas gangrene is usually caused by type A isolates [1].

C. perfringens research highlights since 1995

Before addressing major post-1995 C. perfringens research milestones, it deserves mention that excellent, groundbreaking research on this bacterium had been conducted prior to 1995. For example, genes encoding several C. perfringens toxins had already been cloned, which was not trivial in the pre-PCR era. Also, some C. perfringens toxins had been purified and their biochemistry and action studied. A prime example is the landmark 1941 study by MacFarlane and Knight [5] reporting that C. perfringens alpha toxin (CPA) possesses phospholipase C activity, marking the first identification of any bacterial toxin with enzymatic activity. Some progress towards understanding C. perfringens genetics/regulation of toxin production had also been achieved. For example, in the 1970s Duncan’s group determined that production of C. perfringens enterotoxin (CPE) was sporulation-associated [6]. Also, conjugative transfer had been demonstrated for C. perfringens plasmids IP401, a 52 kb plasmid encoding chloramphenicol and tetracycline resistance, and pCW3, a 47 kb plasmid that confers tetracycline resistance [7]. Lastly, during the 1980s-early 1990s, technical breakthroughs such as methods to introduce plasmid DNA into C. perfringens and the development of C. perfringens-E. coli shuttle vectors [8,9] provided the tools/techniques necessary for the post-1995 molecular revolution in C. perfringens research.

With that acknowledgement, a subjective list of major post-1995 research milestones (and important subsequent findings) for understanding C. perfringens genetics, virulence-related physiology and pathogenicity is presented in Fig. 1 and briefly discussed and expanded upon below. Please note that space limitations prohibit mention of all C. perfringens research achievements during this period and require citation of many reviews rather than all relevant original articles.

C. perfringens genomics and toxin genetics:

In 2002, the Shimizu laboratory determined the complete genome sequence of C. perfringens type A strain 13 [10]. This was a tour de force accomplishment given the available technology (shotgun cloning and Sanger sequencing) and represented the first completely sequenced genome of any Gram-positive pathogenic anaerobe. It provided many novel insights, e. g., C. perfringens lacks genes needed for biosynthesis of several amino acids and has an ~3.5 Mb genome. A subsequent 2006 study by Myers et al. [11] reported complete sequencing of genomes for two additional C. perfringens strains, including SM101, which is a transformable derivative of type F food poisoning strain NCTC8798 that has a chromosomal enterotoxin (cpe) gene and is often used in studies of type F food poisoning strains. With the more recent application of next generation technology to whole genome sequencing, hundreds of C. perfringens genomes are now available (e.g., Refs. [12,13]). Compiling those sequencing results with findings obtained using multilocus sequencing typing (MLST) has established that, i) C. perfringens possesses a relatively diverse pangenome consisting of 4–5 different clades and ii) type F strains isolates carrying a chromosomal cpe gene (and a few related isolates) belong to a single clade, helping to explain their unusual phenotypic features, e.g., they often produce spores with extreme resistance to heat, cold and other stresses (more on this later).

Genome sequencing studies also established that CPA and perfringolysin O (PFO) are both chromosomally-encoded. However, a series of pulsed-field gel Southern blot approaches (mainly conducted from 1995 to the 2010s) mapped the genes encoding most other toxins of this bacterium to plasmids [7,14]. An exception is the cpe gene, which can be either chromosomal or plasmid-borne [7,14]. C. perfringens toxin plasmids are often large, ranging up to >135 kb in size [7,14]. Interestingly, a single C. perfringens isolate can possess multiple toxin plasmids and individual plasmids can carry up to four different toxin genes [7,14].

With the technology available in the 2000s, sequencing C. perfringens toxin or antibiotic resistance plasmids was challenging due to their size, low copy number, and the presence of multiple copies of other very similar plasmids in individual C. perfringens isolates. Nevertheless in 2006, Miyamoto et al. and Bannum et al. independently sequenced, respectively, the cpe plasmids of two type F strains (F4969 and F5603) and pCW3 [15,16]. This work revealed that these three plasmids share an ~35 kb conserved region but also possess variable regions encoding, among other genes, beta2 toxin (CPB2, for F5603), bacteriocins (for F4969) or tetracycline resistance (for pCW3). Subsequent sequencing and overlapping PCR studies [7,14] of many other C. perfringens toxin plasmids showed that, i) most (but not all) other cpe plasmids of type F isolates resemble the F4969 or F5603 cpe plasmids and ii) a similar conserved region is present in plasmids carrying the beta toxin (cpb), epsilon toxin (etx), iota toxin (itx) or necrotic enteritis B-like (netB) toxin genes in other toxinotypes.

Plasmids carrying the ~35 kb conserved region are conjugative and transfer at high frequency to other C. perfringens isolates [7,14], as first demonstrated in the 1970s for the antibiotic resistance plasmids. The conjugative transfer ability of toxin plasmids with this conserved region was first shown in 2001 by Brynestad et al. for the cpe plasmid [17]. Conjugative toxin or antibiotic resistance plasmids carrying this ~35 kb conserved region belong to the Tcp (transfer of clostridial plasmids) plasmid family. The Rood laboratory has shown, i) this conserved region contains oriT (origin of transfer) and 12 tcp genes that encode homologues of a Gram-positive type IV secretion system [18] and ii) these tcp genes mediate conjugative transfer of pCW3 (and presumably other tcp plasmids). Those researchers also conducted mechanistic analyses identifying the function of each tcp gene in C. perfringens plasmid transfer [18–20].

Certain toxin gene combinations have not been observed in individual C. perfringens isolates, e.g., no isolate carrying both the itx and etx genes has yet been identified. The Rood lab showed that plasmid incompatibility explains this observation. In a series of studies [21–23] they, i) identified at least 10 different parMRC partioning loci that can be present in the ~35 kb conserved region of Tcp plasmids, ii) demonstrated that plasmids with different parMRC homologues are compatible and iii) determined that plasmids with identical parMRC homologues are incompatible [21–23].

Another wrinkle in the association between C. perfringens toxin genes and mobile genetic elements is that many of these toxin genes reside adjacent to insertion sequences. In 1997, Brynestad and Granum first noted this association when they determined that the chromosomal cpe gene of type F strain NCTC8239 is flanked by IS1470 sequences and suggested this may represent an integrated transposon [24]. Supporting this possibility, those researchers detected a cpe-carrying circular form in C. perfringens that may represent a transposition intermediate [25]. Subsequent studies showed the plasmid cpe gene, and nearly all other toxin genes located on tcp plasmids, are also located adjacent to various IS elements [14]. However, isolate-to-isolate variability exists regarding, i) the IS elements associated with a particular toxin gene and ii) the arrangement of those IS elements within toxin loci [3,7,14]. Circular forms containing many of those toxin genes have also been detected [1,14], which may explain how C. perfringens accumulates multiple toxin genes on a single plasmid. However, no insertion of excised sequences carrying any toxin gene into the C. perfringens chromosome or plasmids has been experimentally demonstrated.

More recently, another C. perfringens plasmid family named the Pcp plasmids was identified [26]. Pcp plasmids can be large (>100 kb) and some carry the bec genes encoding binary clostridial enterotoxin (BEC) or the cpb2 gene encoding CPB2, i.e., beta2 toxin (note that cpb2 is also carried by some Tcp plasmids). Pcp plasmids are also conjugative and transfer at high frequency using homologues of a type IV secretion system that differ from those found in Tcp plasmids. There is a third group of non-conjugative plasmids that are related to the bacteriocin plasmid pIP404 [12]. Diagrams of the three major C. perfringens plasmid types are depicted in Fig. 2.

In addition, nonconjugative plasmids carrying the cpe gene have recently been identified [22], adding further to toxin plasmid diversity in C. perfringens. This bacterium can also carry Bcp family plasmids, which appear to have a novel conjugation locus, although conjugative transfer has not yet been demonstrated [12].

Regulation of Toxin Production:

While slightly preceding the post-1995 main focus of this review, it should be mentioned that the Shimizu and Rood laboratories independently identified the C. perfringens VirS/VirR two component regulatory system (TCRS) in 1994 [27,28]. This TCRS is comprised of the VirS transmembrane sensor and the VirR transcriptional regulator. The Rood lab later determined that VirS phosphorylates VirR on a conserved Asp residue located in the VirR N-terminal region and VirR ~ P then binds to a pair of VirR boxes located upstream of a VirR regulated gene [29].

The VirS/VirR system was first shown to regulate CPA and PFO production [27,28,30]. Subsequent studies demonstrated that this TCRS also controls expression of genes encoding NetB, CPB2, and beta toxin (CPB), along with some nontoxin proteins [1]. The regulation of PFO, NetB and CPB production involves VirR ~ P binding to VirR boxes located in promoter regions of genes encoding those proteins. Seminal work by the Shimizu lab identified several VirR-controlled genes encoding small regulatory RNAs, the most important of which is a gene named vrr that encodes VR-RNA [31,32]. VirR ~ P binds to VirR boxes in the vrr promoter to increase the expression of VR-RNA, which then enhances production of CPA and CPB2, as well the enzyme collagenase. For collagenase (and perhaps CPA and CPB2), VR-RNA increases the stability and processing of collagenase mRNA to enhance translation [33]. Lastly, the importance of the VirS/VirR system for virulence has been directly demonstrated; it is required for intestinal production of CPB and for type C isolates to cause enterocolitis in rabbit small intestinal loops or enterotoxemia in mouse models [34].

In 2009 the Shimizu and McClane labs independently identified an Agr-like quorum sensing system (QSS) in type A strain 13 [35,36]. This C. perfringens QSS involves an operon with an agrD gene (encoding AIP, the autoinducing peptide) and agrB (encoding AgrB, a protein involved in processing and exporting the AIP). Using strain 13 agrB mutants, those initial studies showed that the C. perfringens Agr-like QSS controls CPA and PFO production. Follow-up studies using agrB mutants of other C. perfringens strains demonstrated this QSS also regulates NetB production by type G strains, CPB production by type C or B strains and CPE production by type F strains [1]. Furthermore, using animal models, it was shown this Agr-like QSS is important for type A strains to cause gas gangrene, type G strains to cause avian necrotic enteritis and type C strains to cause enterocolitis or enterotoxemia [1].

In 2015, Ma et al. employed a series of synthetic peptides corresponding to C. perfringens AgrD sequences to identify a 5-mer thiolactone ring peptide that can induce CPB production by C. perfringens type B or C mutants lacking a functional Agr-like QSS [37]. Although unpublished due to his untimely death, Shimizu and colleagues reported at a Clostpath meeting that the natural AIP purified from C. perfringens has a similar molecular mass as the 5-mer cyclic peptide identified by Ma et al. More recent studies with the AIP of the Listeria monocytogenes Agr-like QSS suggest that, at neutral pH, this 5-mer thiolactone ring peptide is labile but is the active AIP [38]. Since the anagolous S. aureus AIP receptor is part of a TCRS and both VirS/VirR and the Agr-like QSS co-regulate expression of many C. perfringens toxins (including CPA, PFO, CPB2, NetB and CPB), it was hypothesized that VirS is the receptor for the C. perfringens AIP. This postulate received substantial support from a study by Li and McClane demonstrating that this AIP physically binds to VirS [39].

Compiling these findings enables development of a model for Agr-like QSS and VirS/VirR TCRS regulation of C. perfringens toxin production (Fig. 3). AIP activates VirS to phosphorylate VirR. VirR ~ P then binds to VirR boxes upstream of VirR-regulated genes, including the genes encoding PFO, CPB and NetB. Alternatively, VirR ~ P binds to VirR boxes upstream of vrr to produce VR-RNA, which then regulates production of CPA, CPB2 and collagenase.

C. perfringens also possesses other TCRS or QSS virulence gene regulators, although they are not as well studied. The genome of this bacterium encodes more than 20 other TCRS besides VirS/VirR and at least two of those, i.e., RevS/RevR and EutV/EutW, are important for virulence [40,41]. RevS/RevR controls expression of genes encoding several degradative enzymes such as clostripain and sialidases [40]. RevR is important for development of gas gangrene in a mouse model [40].

In the EutW/EutV TCRS, EutW senses the presence of ethanolamine from host cells and then activates the EutV transcriptional regulator [41,42]. Working together with VirS/VirR and the Agr-like QSS, this activation leads to enhanced growth, increased production of CPA and PFO, and virulence in the mouse gas gangrene model [41,42]. A similar effect is likely involved in upregulation of toxin production when type C strain CN3685 senses the presence of Caco-2 cells [43].

C. perfringens also has another QSS named the autoinducer-2 (AI-2) QSS. This QSS may be important for virulence since it regulates CPA and PFO production in vitro [44]. However, inactivation of the luxS gene (part of the AI-2 QSS) in type C strain CN3685 did not affect enteric virulence [43].

A few other regulators of C. perfringens virulence gene expression have also been identified. CodY is a global transcriptional regulator in Gram-positive bacteria. In C. perfringens type D strain CN3718, CodY binds to the etx promoter and positively regulates etx expression, although it does not affect pfoA or cpa expression by that strain [1]. While CodY decreases sporulation in CN3718, it increases sporulation and CPE production by type F strain SM101 [45]. C. perfringens also produces CcpA (catabolite control protein), another global regulator, which belongs to the LacI/GalR family [1]. In the presence of increasing glucose concentrations, CcpA represses numerous genes but also positively controls production of ETX and CPE, as well as sporulation in C. perfringens [1,46]. Interestingly, CodY, CcpA and NanI sialidase (discussion later) interact together to boost ETX production by type D strain CN3718 [1]. A few other C. perfringens regulators, e.g., NanR and virX RNA, will be discussed later.

C. perfringens toxins:

C. perfringens ranks among the most copious bacterial toxin producers. Since 1995, at least seven new C. perfringens toxins have been added to its already potent arsenal, including CPB2, NetB, NetE, NetF, NetG, Toxin C. perfringens large (TpeL), and binary enterotoxin Clostridium perfringens (BEC).

Because each C. perfringens isolate typically produces multiple toxins, fulfillment of Molecular Koch’s postulates is the most definitive approach to demonstrate virulence contributions by an individual toxin. The first application of molecular Koch’s postulates to study disease importance for C. perfringens toxins was a 1995 study by the Rood group showing that CPA is important when this bacterium causes gas gangrene [47]. Later studies by that group used this approach to demonstrate that CPA and PFO act synergistically during clostridial myonecrosis [48]. The initial application of molecular Koch’s postulates to probe toxin involvement in C. perfringens intestinal diseases was a 1999 study from the McClane laboratory demonstrating that CPE production is necessary for type F strains to cause enteritis [49]. These early Molecular Koch’s postulates studies used C. perfringens toxin null mutants generated by homologous recombination. This same approach later showed that, i) ETX is necessary for enteritis and enterotoxemia caused by type D isolates in sheep, goats and mice [50] and ii) NetB is required for avian necrotic enteritis [51].

In 2005, Chen et al. reported a major technical advance i.e., modification of the Targetron insertional mutagenesis system for use in C. perfringens [52], which opened efficient targeted mutant construction for many other pathogenic clostridia and is still often used to construct C. perfringens mutants. Employing this Clostridium-modified Targetron approach, molecular Koch’s postulates analyses demonstrated the importance of CPB, but not CPA or PFO, for enteritis and enterotoxemia caused by type C isolates [53]. Targetron-generated mutants also showed that CPE and CPB can cause synergistic intestinal damage by CPE-producing type C isolates [54].

Using a toxin typing classification scheme developed in the early 1960s [1], C. perfringens isolates had traditionally been classified into five types (A-E) based upon production of four toxins (CPA, CPB, ETX and iota toxin [ITX]). By the 2010s, it had become apparent this system needed updating to address important toxins discovered since the early 1960s. In 2018, a committee of C. perfringens researchers revised the toxin typing system [4] by adding two additional toxinotypes to accommodate new toxins with proven disease roles, based upon epidemiology and fulfillment of molecular Koch’s postulates. The toxins meeting those criteria in 2018 were CPE and NetB, which are responsible for, arguably, the currently most impactful C. perfringens infections i.e., type F intestinal disease of humans and avian necrotic enteritis, respectively. For ethical and practical reasons, it was also decided this revised system would rely upon PCR detection of toxin genes rather than neutralization of mouse lethality using type-specific antitoxins, as per the 1960s classification system. Isolates carrying the cpe gene encoding CPE, but not genes encoding NetB, CPB, ETX or ITX, are now classified as type F, while isolates carrying the netB gene are now classified as type G (Table 1). The C. perfringens research community left open the possibility of future expansion of this typing system when linkage of a toxin to disease can be clearly demonstrated by epidemiology and molecular Koch’s postulate analyses.

C. perfringens toxins fall into two broad categories, i.e., enzymes or pore-forming proteins. Since 1995, remarkable progress has been made in solving the structure of these toxins, identifying their receptors, dissecting their molecular actions and describing how they contribute to disease. A brief comparison of these toxins is shown in Table 2 and current understanding of these toxins will now be briefly described, starting with the enzymatic toxins.

Toxins with enzymatic activity

Alpha toxin (CPA): This 43 kDa single peptide has cytotoxic, hemolytic and lethal properties [55,56]. It is a zinc-dependent metalloprotease comprised of two domains [55]. The α helix-rich, catalytic N-terminal domain (residues 1–246) shares partial homology with other bacterial phospholipase C enzymes. This enzymatic domain is connected, via a flexible linker (residues 247–255), to a β sheet-rich, membrane-binding C-terminal domain (residues 256–370).

Alpha toxin (CPA): This 43 kDa single peptide has cytotoxic, hemolytic and lethal properties [55,56]. It is a zinc-dependent metalloprotease comprised of two domains [55]. The α helix-rich, catalytic N-terminal domain (residues 1–246) shares partial homology with other bacterial phospholipase C enzymes. This enzymatic domain is connected, via a flexible linker (residues 247–255), to a β sheet-rich, membrane-binding C-terminal domain (residues 256–370).

CPA possesses both phospholipase C and sphingomyelinase activity mediated by a single catalytic site [55,56]. This toxin has complicated effects on host cells, effects that are influenced by toxin dose, host species and cell type. For erythrocytes of some host species, CPA promotes hemolysis by increasing ceramide levels and sphingosine 1-phosphate but it also interacts with a GTP-binding protein to activate host phospholipases to produce similar end-products. In neutrophils, CPA binds to ganglioside GM1 and interacts with neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor type 1 (TrkA) to activate phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1 and also interacts with a GTP binding protein to activate endogenous phospholipase C and generate diacylglycerol. Those effects trigger signal transduction changes leading to generation of O2− which (along with other CPA-induced factors like ceramide) can induce apoptosis or IL-8 release.

As already mentioned, CPA plays a major role in C. perfringens clostridial myonecrosis [47,48]. During gas gangrene, this toxin inhibits polymorphonuclear leucocyte influx into lesions and causes tissue necrosis. It also induces thrombosis formation, which is important since this effect reduces tissue oxygen tension to favor anaerobic growth of C. perfringens [55]. Lastly, recent studies indicate CPA (or PFO) can kill a differentiated muscle cell line (as an in vitro model for gas gangrene) to provide nutrients for C. perfringens growth [42].

Iota toxin (ITX): ITX is a binary toxin consisting of the Ia enzymatic component and the Ib binding component, which are encoded, respectively, by the iap and iab genes [1,56,57]. Ib is initially produced as an inactive 100 kDa protein but becomes proteolytically-activated when a 20 kDa N-terminal fragment is removed by trypsin or chymotrypsin (or perhaps C. perfringens lambda toxin). Those proteases also cleave 9 to 13 amino acids from the N-terminus of the Ia precursor, also thereby activating this ITX component.

Iota toxin (ITX): ITX is a binary toxin consisting of the Ia enzymatic component and the Ib binding component, which are encoded, respectively, by the iap and iab genes [1,56,57]. Ib is initially produced as an inactive 100 kDa protein but becomes proteolytically-activated when a 20 kDa N-terminal fragment is removed by trypsin or chymotrypsin (or perhaps C. perfringens lambda toxin). Those proteases also cleave 9 to 13 amino acids from the N-terminus of the Ia precursor, also thereby activating this ITX component.

ITX action starts with the binding of activated Ib to its receptor, which is lipolysis-stimulated lipoprotein (also known as angulin-1) [57]. Bound Ib then oligomerizes into a heptamer, which itself has pore, and some cytotoxic, activity. However, classical ITX forms when Ia binds to the Ib oligomer, triggering endocytosis and translocation of Ia into the cytoplasm. Ia then covalently attaches an ADP-ribose to Arg residue 177 of actin. This modification leads to actin filament depolymerization into actin monomers, causing a collapse of the cytoskeleton that can lead to caspase-3 activation and apoptosis.

It has been suggested that type E isolates cause enteritis in some animals, but this has not been rigorously established. Nor have Molecular Koch’s postulates been performed for ITX.

Binary Enterotoxin of Clostridium perfringens (BEC): BEC [58], another C. perfringens binary toxin, is comprised of BECa (enzymatic component) and BECb (binding component). Those proteins have ~40–45 % sequence identity with Ia and Ib of ITX.

Binary Enterotoxin of Clostridium perfringens (BEC): BEC [58], another C. perfringens binary toxin, is comprised of BECa (enzymatic component) and BECb (binding component). Those proteins have ~40–45 % sequence identity with Ia and Ib of ITX.

The receptor for BEC is unknown. At the cellular level, BEC shares with ITX the ability to ADP-ribosylate actin. The toxin itself has enterotoxic activity in suckling mice. In this animal model, a BEC-producing C. perfringens strain also has enterotoxic activity but a BECb mutant is attenuated. A complementing strain has not yet been tested for virulence, so molecular Koch’s postulates remain to be proven.

Clostridium perfringens toxin large (TpeL): This ~205 kDa single polypeptide belongs to the large clostridial glycosylating toxin (LGT) family that includes, among others, Clostridioides difficile toxins A and B [59]. Like other LGT family members [1,59], TpeL contains a N-terminal glycosyltransferase domain (GTD), a cysteine protease domain (CPD), and a translocation and receptor binding domain (T). However, unlike other LGT toxins, TpeL lacks a C-terminal CROPS (combined repetitive oligopeptides) domain.

Clostridium perfringens toxin large (TpeL): This ~205 kDa single polypeptide belongs to the large clostridial glycosylating toxin (LGT) family that includes, among others, Clostridioides difficile toxins A and B [59]. Like other LGT family members [1,59], TpeL contains a N-terminal glycosyltransferase domain (GTD), a cysteine protease domain (CPD), and a translocation and receptor binding domain (T). However, unlike other LGT toxins, TpeL lacks a C-terminal CROPS (combined repetitive oligopeptides) domain.

The TpeL receptor is lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) [60]. TpeL binds to LRP1 via the T domain and is then internalized by endocytosis. The CPD domain liberates the enzymatic GTD domain, which enters the cytosol and causes N-acetylglucosaminylation of Ras proteins at threonine 35 [59]. This modification inactivates Ras and induces multiple effects, including cell death by apoptosis.

As mentioned earlier, it has been suggested that TpeL-producing type G strains are more virulent than other type G strains in avian necrotic enteritis models [61]. However, this hypothesis has not been tested since molecular Koch’s postulate analyses have not been performed to assess possible TpeL contributions to avian necrotic enteritis.

Pore-forming toxins

In contrast to the enzymatic activities of CPA, ITX, BEC and TpeL, many other C. perfringens toxins act via pore formation. These pore-forming toxins include a cholesterol-dependent cytolysin (CDC), two aerolysin family toxins and several members of the S. aureus α-hemolysin family. Considerable progress in understanding the action of C. perfringens pore-forming toxins has occurred since 1995, as will now be briefly summarized.

Perfringolysin O (PFO): PFO is the archetype pore-forming toxin, in large part due to the pioneering work of Tweten and colleagues [62,63]. PFO, a single polypeptide of ~55 kDa, is a CDC pore-forming toxin. PFO was the first C. perfringens toxin whose structure was solved [64], which revealed this toxin has a four domain architecture (D1–4), with D1, D2 and D4 arranged in a linear arrangement rich in β-sheet. D3 consists of β-sheet and two α helical bundles.

Perfringolysin O (PFO): PFO is the archetype pore-forming toxin, in large part due to the pioneering work of Tweten and colleagues [62,63]. PFO, a single polypeptide of ~55 kDa, is a CDC pore-forming toxin. PFO was the first C. perfringens toxin whose structure was solved [64], which revealed this toxin has a four domain architecture (D1–4), with D1, D2 and D4 arranged in a linear arrangement rich in β-sheet. D3 consists of β-sheet and two α helical bundles.

PFO action starts when it binds, mainly via the D4 domain, to cholesterol [62,63]. Bound monomers oligomerize on the membrane surface to form a prepore. Two α helical bundles in the D3 domain of each PFO monomer in a prepore then refold into β-hairpins that puncture the membrane. Those β-hairpins then interact with β-hairpins from other PFO monomers in the same prepore to create a β-barrel pore. The PFO pore consists of up to 50 monomers and has an enormous 150–300 Å diameter.

The major proven disease role for PFO concerns its synergism with CPA during gas gangrene caused by type A isolates [48]. Interestingly, PFO is not necessary for type F food poisoning since many type F food poisoning strains naturally lack the pfoA gene [3] or for type C enteritis, where a pfoA null mutant retains full virulence [53].

C. perfringens enterotoxin (CPE): CPE, a 35 kDa single polypeptide, has a unique amino acid sequence, although it is an aerolysin family member [1,3]. It consists of an N-terminal domain (residues 1–197) and a C-terminal domain (residues 198–319). The C-terminal region mediates receptor binding, while the N-terminal domain region is responsible for oligomerization and pore formation. Once produced in the intestines, CPE is rapidly processed by intestinal proteases, including trypsin, but this processed toxin retains in vivo and in vitro toxicity [65].

C. perfringens enterotoxin (CPE): CPE, a 35 kDa single polypeptide, has a unique amino acid sequence, although it is an aerolysin family member [1,3]. It consists of an N-terminal domain (residues 1–197) and a C-terminal domain (residues 198–319). The C-terminal region mediates receptor binding, while the N-terminal domain region is responsible for oligomerization and pore formation. Once produced in the intestines, CPE is rapidly processed by intestinal proteases, including trypsin, but this processed toxin retains in vivo and in vitro toxicity [65].

The cellular action of CPE begins when it binds, via the C-terminal domain, to certain members of the claudin family of tight junction proteins [1,3,66]. Claudins vary considerably in their CPE binding ability, with some claudins (e.g., claudin-3 and −4) binding CPE with strong affinity but other claudins having reduced (e.g., claudins-8 and −14) or negligible (e.g., claudin-1) CPE binding ability [1,3].

Binding of CPE to a claudin receptor forms a small (~90 kDa) complex that can also contain a nonreceptor claudin such as claudin-1 [1,3]. Approximately six small complexes then oligomerize into an ~450–500 kDa prepore [67]. An α–helix (amino acids 80–110) in the N-terminal domain of each CPE in a prepore unwinds into a β-hairpin loop [68]; all 6 β–hairpins then interact to form a β-barrel pore. This pore has an ~1.4 nM diameter and exhibits cation-selectivity, leading to Ca2+ influx [1,3]. Low CPE doses form small numbers of pores and cause limited Ca2+ influx, resulting in modest calpain activation that leads to classical caspase-3 mediated apoptosis in human enterocyte-like Caco-2 cells [1,3]. Higher CPE doses form many pores that result in massive Ca2+ influx, causing strong calpain activation and necroptosis [69].

Type F isolates are major human enteric pathogens, causing the very common type F food poisoning and 3–15 % of all AAD cases [1,3]. As mentioned earlier, CPE production in the intestines is necessary for the enteric virulence of type F isolates [49]. CPE cytotoxicity initiates intestinal damage, which includes epithelial desquamation and villus blunting [1,3]. Interestingly, in the small intestine, CPE binds mainly to receptor-rich villi tips yet damages the entire villus [1,3]. This observation may involve a bystander killing effect, as noted in vitro, where CPE-sensitive intestinal cells release factors that kill CPE-insensitive intestinal cells [70]. This toxin can also affect the colon [1,3]. CPE-induced intestinal damage appears necessary to induce intestinal fluid transport changes (diarrhea) in animal models [1,3]. In people with medication-induced constipation side-effects, type F infection can be fatal [1,3]. Studies with mice [71] indicate this process involves absorption of CPE from the intestinal lumen into the circulation, where it induces a fatal hyperkalemia.

Epsilon toxin (ETX): This protein is initially produced by type B and D isolates as a nearly-inactive 33 kDa (296 amino acid) prototoxin [72]. By using intestinal contents from an adult goat (a natural host for ETX-mediated disease) and specific protease inhibitors, Freedman et al. determined that prototoxin processing is initiated by intestinal serine proteases (trypsin and chymotrypsin), followed by carboxypeptidase cleavage [73]. This proteolytic processing generates three different ~27 kDa ETX species, each cytotoxic but with a different C-terminal amino acid.

Until recently it was believed that neonatal animals were not susceptible to type D enterotoxemia because of their low intestinal trypsin activity resulting from the trypsin-inhibiting properties of colostrum. However, there have been recent case reports of type D enterotoxemia in goat kids [74]. The type D strains involved carried the lambda toxin gene, and it was demonstrated that, in sufficient quantities, this toxin can activate ETX [75]. However, not all type D strains carrying the lambda toxin gene can activate their ETX in vitro and one lambda toxin gene-negative C. perfringens isolate has been identified that could activate ETX [76]. Therefore, further study is warranted to determine whether ETX activation to cause enterotoxemia in kids involves type D isolates producing large amounts of lambda toxin and/or involves other bacterial or intestinal proteases.

Although ETX does not have amino acid sequence homology with CPE, it is another member of the aerolysin toxin family [72]. ETX is β sheet-rich and consists of three domains. Domain I is the receptor-binding domain, while domains II and III are the pore-forming portion of the toxin. Notably, domain II has an amphiphilic loop that insets into membranes to create the β-barrel pore [72].

ETX action starts with binding to its receptor which, for cultured cells and mice, is the myelin and lymphocyte (MAL) protein [77,78]. Bound ETX monomers then oligomerize to form a heptameric prepore, followed by active pore formation [79]. This pore is 1.2 nm at its narrowest and causes K+ loss and ATP depletion, which triggers necrosis [78–80].

During disease, ETX is produced in the intestines and then promotes its self-absorption into the circulation by opening tight junctions and increasing intestinal permeability [78]. Type D strains are the leading cause of enterotoxemia in sheep, goats and possibly other species. As mentioned earlier, molecular Koch’s postulate analyses [50] established a key role for ETX for type D strain virulence in natural hosts (goats and sheep) and laboratory animal models (mice). Interestingly, type D infection has varied presentations in different natural hosts. In goats, type D infections involve predominantly gastrointestinal disease, although systemic changes sometimes occur [78]. However, type D disease in sheep mainly involves systemic effects such as brain and lung edema and hydropericardium, with limited gastrointestinal effects [78].

With respect to intravenous LD50 in mice [78], ETX is the third most potent clostridial toxin, ranking behind only the botulinum toxins and tetanus toxin. One reason for the exceptional potency of ETX compared to other pore-forming toxins is that ETX binds to endothelial cells in the brain vasculature and then breaks down the blood brain barrier [78]. Once present in brain parenchyma, this toxin causes demyelination and neuronal cell necrosis. Interestingly, some studies suggest ETX can trigger multiple sclerosis in humans [81,82], although this is controversial [83].

Beta toxin (CPB): CPB, a single 35 kDa polypeptide, belongs to the S. aureus α hemolysin family of pore-forming toxins. CPB is extremely sensitive to proteases, especially trypsin, so it is only active in hosts with low trypsin levels due to age, malnutrition and/or a diet rich in foods containing a trypsin inhibitor [1,84].

Beta toxin (CPB): CPB, a single 35 kDa polypeptide, belongs to the S. aureus α hemolysin family of pore-forming toxins. CPB is extremely sensitive to proteases, especially trypsin, so it is only active in hosts with low trypsin levels due to age, malnutrition and/or a diet rich in foods containing a trypsin inhibitor [1,84].

Posthaus et al. recently identified CD31(PECAM-1) as the endothelial cell receptor for CPB [85]. Endothelial cells are the proposed primary target cells for CPB [84] but it is unclear how CPB crosses the intestinal epithelium to access those cells. Once bound to CD31, CPB oligomerizes (apparently into an octomer) and then forms a pore [86]. This results in Ca2+ entry into cells, which activates calpain. This effect causes necrostatin-inhibitable cell death, which could involve either necroptosis or apoptosis [87].

CPB is produced by both type B and C isolates. Type B isolates are associated with fatal hemorrhagic enteritis in sheep, while type C isolates cause enterocolitis or enterotoxemia in ruminants and horses [1]. Type C isolates are also responsible for an uncommon human foodborne disease named enteritis necroticans (or PigBel), which occurs in people with low intestinal trypsin levels due to diet, malnutrition or disease [1,84]. As mentioned earlier, molecular Koch’s postulate approaches demonstrated the importance of CPB for type C isolate pathogenicity [53]. Furthermore, purified CPB is sufficient to cause enteritis or enterotoxemia in animal models [53]. However, at low toxin concentrations, CPB and CPE can act synergistically to cause intestinal damage [54].

Necrotic enteritis B-like toxin (NetB): This 33 kDa single polypeptide is another member of the S. aureus α-hemolysin pore-forming toxin family [1,88,89]. It has 38 % identity with CPB, including some key residues involved in pore-formation [88]. NetB pores are heptameric with an estimated pore diameter of ~1.7 nm [90]. This pore allows ion influx that can lead to osmotic lysis.

It has 38 % identity with CPB, including some key residues involved in pore-formation [88]. NetB pores are heptameric with an estimated pore diameter of ~1.7 nm [90]. This pore allows ion influx that can lead to osmotic lysis.

Avian necrotic enteritis is a major agricultural problem, costing billions of dollars per year [1,88,89]. This disease involves formation of necrotic lesions in the small intestinal mucosa. The clinical form usually occurs in younger birds and involves diarrhea, decreased feeding and death within hours. The subclinical form affects birds of any age and involves formation of macroscopic intestinal lesions; this form often involves decreased feed conversion, so it affects productivity. Originally this disease was thought to be mediated by CPA but, as mentioned earlier, molecular Koch’s postulate analyses established NetB as the major toxin involved [51]. While a large amount of evidence indicates that NetB is the main virulence factor for type G isolates causing avian necrotic enteritis, there are also reports of chickens with this disease from which no NetB isolates can be isolated. Whether this is attributable to netB plasmid instability or some other reason requires further study.

C. perfringens produces several other pore-forming toxins related to CPB, NetB and other toxins in the S. aureus α toxin family, including NetF, NetE, NetG and delta toxin.

Delta toxin, made by some type B and C isolates, has a structure similar to NetB [91–93]. Ganglioside GM2 has been proposed as its receptor [93]. This toxin is cytotoxic for a broad array of cell culture lines, forming a medium size pore of ~4 nm [91–93]. This pore causes ATP depletion and swelling that apparently induces necrosis. Delta toxin also affects the barrier function of human enterocyte-like Caco2 cells. In mouse intestine, it causes histologic damage, villus shortening and shedding of intestinal epithelial cells, apparently via ADAM10 mediated degradation of cadherin in adherences junctions [91]. The role of this toxin in disease remains to be defined.

NetF toxin shares 48 % homology with NetB toxin [94]. This ~32 kDa toxin may bind to a sialyoprotein receptor [95]. Binding is followed by formation of an ~4–6 nm diameter pore containing 6–8 monomers [96]. NetF is cytotoxic for some cell lines [95,96]. NetE and NetG have not been well studied but are predicted to have a molecular mass of 36 kDa or 34 kDa, respectively. The netE and netG ORFs have 79 % and 52 % homology, respectively, with the NetB ORF.

C. perfringens isolates producing NetF have been linked to foal and canine intestinal disease [94], although molecular Koch’s postulates have not been fulfilled so the role of NetF in animal disease has not been definitively proven. This is relevant since many C. perfringens isolates causing foal necrotizing enteritis or canine diarrhea carry three different plasmids encoding, i) NetE and NetF, ii) CPE and CPB2 or iii) NetG. Therefore, which of the toxin(s) in this myriad mixture cause or contribute to foal or canine intestinal disease is not yet clear [96].

Beta2 toxin (CPB2) is proposed to be a pore-forming toxin (1). All C. perfringens types can carry a cpb2 gene. There are two major cpb2 gene variants, which are named “atypical” or “consensus”. Details about the receptor or intestinal action of CPB2 are currently lacking, as is the role, if any, of this toxin in disease.

C. perfringens degradative enzymes:

This bacterium produces a slew of degradative enzymes, such a proteases, hyaluronidases, collagenases, and sialidases [1]. The semantics of describing these proteins as degradative enzymes vs. toxins is not always clear, e.g., lambda toxin is a protease that can activate ETX and ITX in vitro yet purified lambda toxin also causes hemorrhagic edema when injected intravenously [97]. In addition to lambda toxin, C. perfringens produces other proteases including clostripain, a potent endoprotease, and collagenase. Neither of those proteases are required for C. perfringens to cause gas gangrene in a mouse myonecrosis model [98,99].

There is evidence for virulence contributions by glycosidases. EngCP is an endo α-N-acetylgalactosaminidase that is regulated by the RevS/RevR TCRS [100]. An EngCP mutant was significantly attenuated for virulence in a mouse myonecrosis model and this effect was reversible by complementation [101].

C. perfringens also produces three sialidases, named NanH (43 kDa), NanI (77 kDa) and NanJ (120 kDa) [102]. These enzymes share a family 33 carbohydrate binding module, with NanI and NanJ possessing additional carbohydrate-binding modules. These sialidases exhibit different substrate preferences, but all release sialic acid (neuraminic acid) from glycoconjugates. NanI and NanJ are both secreted from C. perfringens, while NanH is cytoplasmic, at least early during growth. Most C. perfringens isolates produce all three sialidases, but NanI is predominantly responsible for their sialidase activity. Interestingly, type F isolates with a chromosomal cpe gene, and some related type C human disease strains, consistently lack the nanI gene. Those type F chromosomal cpe strains produce NanH during sporulation and this sialidase is released (together with CPE) when the mother cell lyses at the completion of sporulation (more later) [3].

NanI and NanJ production is not necessary for type A strain 13 to cause myonecrosis in the mouse model [103]. However, substantial evidence suggests sialidases may contribute to type F intestinal disease. Using nanI mutants, it was demonstrated that NanI enhances the growth of F4969 (a type F plasmid cpe, AAD strain), particularly in the presence of mucus, and increases adherence to enterocyte-like cells in vitro [104]. This sialidase also increases growth and colonization of this strain in a mouse small intestinal loop model [104]. In addition, NanH, NanI and NanJ each increase binding and cytotoxicity of CPE for Caco-2 cells [3]. Furthermore, at levels produced in the intestines NanI also increases CPE-induced intestinal damage [105]. NanI also enhances the cytotoxicity of CPB, ETX, and NetB in vitro [106–108].

C. perfringens can grow by using sialidases to cleave sialic acid from glycoconjugates and then internalizing and metabolizing this liberated sialic acid using transporters and enzymes encoded by the nan operon [109]. In type F strain F4969, glucose and high sialic acid levels repress nanI expression. In this strain, nanI and nan operon transcription is regulated by NanR, a member of the RpiR transcriptional regulator family [109]. In sporulation medium, NanR significantly increases sporulation and CPE production by F4969, but this effect is strain variable, i.e., for two chromosomal cpe type F strains, NanR also increases sporulation and CPE production by SM101 but has no effect on sporulation or CPE production by strain 01E809 [3].

Adhesins:

Adherence is an important, although still understudied, aspect of C. perfringens virulence (particularly in the intestines) since it assists colonization by enhancing the persistence of this bacterium in the host. Several candidate adhesins, including collagen adhesion protein (Cna) and fibrinogen binding proteins (FbpA and FbpB) are reported to function as adhesins [110,111]. For example, a cna mutant of a type G strain has reduced virulence in an avian necrotic enteritis model [110]. Also, as mentioned earlier, NanI (and perhaps other) sialidases increase F4969 adherence to mucus-producing enterocyte-like cells in vitro and enhance intestinal colonization [112].

Sporulation and germination:

Most C. perfringens isolates can sporulate and this ability plays a vital role in their pathogenicity. Spores are critical for transmission of C. perfringens to cause either gas gangrene (where spores contaminate a wound) or AAD (which often occurs after spores are ingested from the environment).

Another important virulence contribution of sporulation involves the environmental resistance of spores. Spore resistance likely helps type F AAD strains survive in the nosocomial environment so they can then be ingested to cause AAD. Spores also help type F strains, particularly chromosomal cpe strains, survive food environment stresses such as heat, cold and preservatives so those spores can later germinate into vegetative cells and then be ingested to cause food poisoning [113]. Compared to most other C. perfringens strains, type F chromosomal cpe strains often produce remarkably resistant spores, e.g., their spores can exhibit only a one log reduction in viability after an hour of boiling [114]. In a 2008 study, Li and McClane identified a small acid soluble protein (SASP)-4 variant made by most type F chromosomal cpe strains as a major contributor to this exceptional spore resistance phenotype [115]. SASPs are DNA binding proteins that protect spore DNA from stresses such as heat. The protective mechanism of the SASP-4 variant present in extremely resistant spores also requires the presence of the other SASPs made by C. perfringens [116]. Other factors such as other SASPs, spore coat thickness, spore core size, and spore dipicolinic acid and metal content also influence spore resistance properties [1,117–119].

Since 1995, there has been substantial progress in understanding sporulation in C. perfringens. In the paradigm spore-forming bacterium Bacillus subtilis, sporulation starts with activation of a phosphorelay whose terminal phosphate receptor is Spo0A, the master regulator of sporulation [120]. Spo0A~P then activates a sporulation-associated sigma factor cascade that temporally regulates production of proteins needed at various steps in sporulation. Studies from several groups have shown that Spo0A and the same four sporulation-associated alternative sigma factors (SigE, SigF, SigG and SigK) mediating B. subtilis sporulation are also necessary for C. perfringens sporulation, with SigF controlling production of the other three sigma factors [121–123]. A small RNA named VirX has been shown to repress sporulation and CPE production [124], although the mechanism is unclear.

However, it was surprising when the early genome studies of C. perfringens revealed the absence of the B. subtilis phosphorelay [125]. It was hypothesized that, rather than a phosphorelay, C. perfringens and other pathogenic clostridia use an orphan histidine kinase (OHK) to directly phosphorylate Spo0A [125]. This postulate was confirmed for C. perfringens by Freedman et al., in 2019 [126] when they showed that an OHK named CPR0195 is necessary for SM101 to sporulate (and produce CPE, more below) in sporulation medium; they also determined that another OHK, named CPR1055, is not required for sporulation (or CPE production). This study also demonstrated that CPR0195 can directly phosphorylate Spo0A in vitro.

Laboratory sporulation media do not resemble the intestinal conditions where type F strains sporulate during disease (see below), so Mehdizadeh-Gohari et al. recently compared [127] sporulation by wild-type SM101 vs. those two SM101 OHK mutants in mouse small intestinal contents (MSIC). Surprisingly, the CPR0195 mutant sporulated at wild-type levels in this experiment, demonstrating that induction of C. perfringens sporulation is environmentally-influenced. When mutants of the five additional OHKs in C. perfringens were constructed in SM101, the cpr1953 or cpr1954 OHK null mutants made virtually no spores (or CPE) in either MSIC or sporulation media, which was reversible by complementation. Those two OHK mutants produced decreased amounts of Spo0A and the cpr1954 mutant phosphorylated less Spo0A compared to wild type SM101. Overexpression of cpr1953 by the cpr1954 null mutant, or vice versa, restored wild-type sporulation (and CPE production) strongly suggesting these two OHKs do not function in a novel phosphorelay [123]. Food poisoning and AAD caused by type F isolates begins when these bacteria sporulate in the intestines, resulting in production of CPE [3]. Zhao and Melville showed that CPE production is sporulation-dependent because the cpe promoters are SigE- and SigK-dependent [128]. CPE is not a secreted protein; instead, CPE accumulates inside the mother cell and is released when the mother cell lyses at the completion of sporulation to release its spore [1].

C. perfringens spores are essentially inert and lack metabolism so they remain viable indefinitely. However, spores must eventually germinate back to vegetative cells to complete the life-cycle. Spore germination is also important for pathogenicity [123]. C. perfringens spores contaminate wounds but then must germinate to cause gas gangrene. Similarly, spores are considered the major transmissible form when type F strains cause AAD, so those ingested spores must germinate in the intestines. Germination is also important for type F food poisoning, i.e., spores surviving stresses (such as cooking) must germinate in foods so the resultant vegetative cells can multiple before being ingested and later sporulating to produce CPE in the intestines.

The process of germination starts when C. perfringens senses small molecules named germinants, which are strain-variable and include factors such as KCl, amino acids and phosphate [123]. Germinants bind to receptors on the spore surface, the most prominent of which is GerKC for type F food poisoning strains [123]. Germinant binding induces CspB-mediated proteolytic activation of SleC. SleC is a cortex hydrolase that removes cortex layers of the spore, allowing hydration of the spore core to resume metabolism [123]. Germination also involves removal of calcium dipicolinic acid from the spore core and removal of SASPs bound to spore DNA so transcription can resume [123].

Antimicrobial resistance:

Numerous surveys have identified an increasing prevalence of C. perfringens antibiotic resistance, sometimes including multidrug resistance. One explanation for this pattern has already been mentioned, i.e., the tetracycline plasmid pCW3 is conjugative, which assists in the spread of tetracycline resistance [16]. Other resistance elements encoding, for example, chloramphenicol, bacitracin and lincomycin, are also associated with conjugative plasmids related to pCW3. While the TetP resistance genes are not present on a transposable element, other resistance determinants are, e.g., the chloramphenicol resistance determinant is associated with the mobilizable genetic element Tn4451 [129]. This situation is reminiscent of toxin gene carriage by C. perfringens, where many toxin genes are associated with mobile genetic elements that have inserted onto a conjugative plasmid [7,14].

Summary and future directions

This review has described some of the momentous advances achieved since 1995 in understanding C. perfringens pathogenicity and biology. Some of these findings have impact beyond C. perfringens. For example, identifying the pore-forming mechanism of PFO has opened understanding of how many other pore-forming toxins work. Similarly, elucidating the mechanisms and virulence contributions of the VirS/VirR:Agr-like QSS of C. perfringens should be informative since many other pathogenic clostridia also possess an Agr-like QSS. Progress since 1995 also has the promise of translational value, e.g., identifying pathogenically-important antigens should improve vaccine construction for diseases such as avian necrotic enteritis and describing CPE interactions with claudins may allow use of CPE, or nontoxic CPE derivatives, for cancer identification or therapy, immunization and drug delivery [3]. Despite this progress, many questions remain for future C. perfringens research. To cite only a few examples: which of the newly-identified C. perfringens toxins are important for disease? Are there other toxins still to be discovered? Can other toxins besides CPA and PFO, or CPB and CPE, act synergistically? What triggers intestinal sporulation by C. perfringens? We await the answers to these and other questions with considerable excitement.
