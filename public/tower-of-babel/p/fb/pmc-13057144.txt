Natural low methoxyl pectin extracted from sunflower heads serves as an efficient biosorbent for lead removal
Authors: Xiaoxia Peng, Qiang Gong, Ruirui Gao, Xiaorui Ci, Zilu Jin, Zhifang Dou, Jianfang Song, Lijuan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study demonstrated that extraction temperature serves as a critical parameter for modulating the structural characteristics and Pb2+ adsorption performance of natural low methoxyl pectin derived from sunflower (Helianthus annuus) heads, an abundant agricultural byproduct. Pectin extracted at 100 °C for 3 h (AHP) exhibited a lower molecular weight (Mw, 78.2 kDa), higher galacturonic acid (GalA) content (83.32%±3.52%), and lower degree of esterification (DE, 26.34%±1.52%) compared to its low-temperature counterpart (LHP, extracted at 80 °C for 1 h, 84.68%±3.86% GalA, 29.32%±2.18% DE, and bimodal Mw of 316.4 and 115.7 kDa) and commercial citrus pectin (CP, 76.83%±3.74% GalA, 83.24%±3.22% DE, and Mw of 573.1 kDa). Consequently, AHP achieved a superior Pb2+ adsorption capacity of 295.86 mg/g, exceeding that of LHP and CP by 25.7% and 76.6%, respectively. The adsorption process was best described by the Langmuir isotherm and pseudo-second-order kinetic models (R2 > 0.99), indicating a monolayer, chemisorption-dominated mechanism. Integrated characterization using FT-IR, XPS, SEM-EDS, BET and competitive adsorption experiments revealed that the enhanced performance of AHP originated from the synergistic effect of a high content of accessible carboxyl groups and a reduced Mw that minimized chain entanglement. This combination enhanced active-site accessibility and promoted the formation of an open, permeable, ion-crosslinked network upon Pb2+ coordination. Introduction

Heavy metal contamination of water resources, particularly by lead ions (Pb2+), represents a persistent environmental challenge due to their acute toxicity, bioaccumulation potential in food chains, and non-biodegradability, which collectively pose serious risks to human health even at trace levels1–3. Conventional remediation technologies, including chemical precipitation, ion exchange, membrane filtration, and electrocoagulation, are often constrained by high operational costs, technical complexity, and the generation of secondary waste, with diminished efficacy at low metal concentrations4–6. In contrast, adsorption has emerged as a promising alternative, offering advantages in operational simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and typically high removal efficiency7. Within this framework, biosorbents derived from renewable biomass align closely with circular economy principles, providing a sustainable strategy for valorizing waste materials in water treatment applications8.

Natural biopolymers derived from agricultural and industrial byproducts, including cellulose, alginate9, starch10, chitosan11, and pectin12, represent promising candidates for biosorbent development. Among these, pectin has attracted considerable attention due to its anionic polysaccharide backbone enriched with carboxyl groups, which serve as effective metal-binding sites8. Structurally, pectin consists primarily of linearly linked α-(1→4)-D-galacturonic acid (GalA) residues interspersed with rhamnose, forming well-defined domains including homogalacturonan (HG), rhamnogalacturonan-I (RG-I), rhamnogalacturonan-II (RG-II), and xylogalacturonan (XGA)13. The adsorption capacity of pectin is intrinsically governed by its macromolecular architecture, with the degree of esterification (DE) of GalA carboxyl groups serving as a critical structural determinant14. Notably, low methoxyl pectin (LMP, DE < 50%) generally exhibits superior metal-binding performance compared to high methoxyl pectin (HMP, DE > 50%), primarily through mechanisms such as ion exchange and the egg-box coordination model15. Currently, most commercial pectin is HMP, predominantly sourced from citrus peels and apple pomace16. In contrast, LMP is typically produced via post-extraction chemical or enzymatic de-esterification, processes that are often energy-intensive, generate additional waste, and may compromise polymer integrity or functionality17. Accordingly, the identification of natural sources yielding intrinsic LMP, such as sunflower (Helianthus annuus) heads, an abundant agricultural byproduct with reported DE values frequently below 30%, offers a more sustainable and efficient alternative18–20.

Despite this potential, the adsorption performance of pectin-based materials is governed not only by DE but also by broader physicochemical structural features, including molecular weight and chain conformation8,21. These characteristics are not intrinsic constants but are decisively shaped by the extraction conditions applied to the raw biomass22. Although considerable progress has been made in engineering pectin into composites and hydrogels to enhance its stability and functionality23–25, the foundational potential of extraction conditions, particularly temperature, as a direct and deliberate means to modulate the macromolecular architecture of pectin, and thereby govern its adsorption function, remains underexplored. Consequently, a systematic investigation that explicitly links controlled extraction parameters to the structural evolution of sunflower head pectin and the resulting mechanisms of Pb2+ adsorption is presently lacking. We therefore hypothesized that controlled thermal extraction serves not merely as an isolation procedure but also a deliberate structural engineering tool to tailor the molecular weight and chain entanglement state of sunflower head pectin, thereby governing its Pb2+ adsorption performance through the synergistic optimization of carboxylate site content and physical accessibility.

To test this hypothesis, the present study aimed to investigate extraction temperature as a critical parameter for tailoring the physicochemical structure and Pb2+ adsorption performance of natural low methoxyl pectin derived from sunflower heads. By comparing pectins extracted under two distinct thermal regimes (LHP at 80 °C for 1 h and AHP at 100 °C for 3 h) with commercial citrus pectin (CP) as a reference, we systematically evaluated key structural parameters (including molecular weight distribution, DE and GalA content) and their correlations with adsorption capacity, kinetics, and isotherm behavior. Complementary analytical techniques, including FT-IR, XPS, SEM-EDS, and BET, were employed to elucidate the underlying adsorption mechanisms. The findings demonstrate that extraction serves not merely as an isolation step, but as a critical parameter for tailoring the macromolecular architecture of pectin. This work provides a sustainable, structure-driven strategy for transforming sunflower head residues into high-performance biosorbents for Pb2+ removal.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Sunflower (Helianthus annuus) heads were obtained from Baoding City, Hebei Province, China, and supplied by Anguo Guangshang Trading Co., Ltd. The heads were deseeded and crushed prior to use as the raw material for pectin extraction. A standard lead (II) solution (1 g/L Pb2+ in 1% HNO3) was sourced from the National Steel and Iron Material Testing Center (Beijing, China). All chemical reagents used were of analytical grade, including Pb(NO3)2, CaCl2, NaCl and AlCl3 (Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36–38%, Beijing Chemical Works), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, Beijing Chemical Works). Deionized water was used to prepare all solutions, which were subsequently filtered through 0.22 µm membranes (Beijing Huabodeyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) prior to use. Commercial citrus pectin (CP), purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), was used as a reference material.

Preparation of pectin from sunflower heads

Pectin was extracted from deseeded and crushed sunflower heads using an oxalic acid-assisted method. To systematically investigate the influence of macromolecular architecture on adsorption behavior, two distinct thermal extraction protocols were deliberately employed to produce pectin samples18,26. These samples were designated as low-temperature pectin (LHP) and high-temperature pectin (AHP). The selection of 80 °C and 100 °C as extraction temperatures was based on preliminary screening across a range from 40 to 100 °C, which indicated that extractions conducted at 80 °C and 100 °C yielded pectin with high and stable GalA content and comparable monosaccharide profiles, while also inducing a significant and reproducible difference in molecular weight (Mw). These conditions were therefore chosen to isolate the effect of Mw on adsorption performance while minimizing variation in core chemical composition.

Specifically, LHP was extracted using 0.2% (w/v) oxalic acid solution (pH 2.1) at 80 °C for 1 h. In contrast, AHP was obtained under a more stringent thermal regime using the same oxalic acid solution at 100 °C for 3 h. After each extraction, the combined supernatant was neutralized to pH 5.0, concentrated under reduced pressure, and precipitated with ethanol. The precipitated pectin was collected, redissolved in deionized water, and extensively dialyzed (MWCO: 3500 Da) against frequent water changes to remove low-molecular-weight impurities and salts. The purified retentate was finally lyophilized to obtain a dry powder. A detailed, stepwise description of the complete extraction and purification procedure is provided in the Supplementary Information (Section S1).

Compositional analysis

The galacturonic acid (GalA) content of pectin samples (LHP, AHP, and CP) was determined spectrophotometrically at 520 nm using the m-hydroxydiphenyl method, with D-GalA as the standard27. Ash content was determined gravimetrically after combustion at 500 °C for 8 h in a muffle furnace (SX2-4–10N, Shanghai Yixin Experimental Instrument Co., Ltd.). The degree of esterification (DE) was measured by titration according to the Chinese National Standard GB 25533—201018.

Molecular weight distribution analysis

The molecular weight distributions of the pectin samples were determined by high-performance gel permeation chromatography (HPGPC) using a TSK-gel G-4000PWXL column (7.8 mm × 300 mm, Tosoh Bioscience, Japan) coupled to a Shimadzu HPLC system28. Pectin solutions (5 mg/mL) were prepared in 0.2 mol/L NaCl, filtered through 0.22 µm membranes, and injected (20 µL) into the system. Isocratic elution was performed with 0.2 mol/L NaCl as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. Detection was carried out using a refractive index detector (RID-10A, Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan). Calibration was performed with a series of dextran standards (5, 50, 150, and 410 kDa). It should be noted that the reported molecular weights were relative estimates, as differences in hydrodynamic volume and conformation between dextran and pectin might introduce systematic bias29. Therefore, the data were used primarily for comparative analysis of relative molecular size among samples under identical analytical conditions.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

The monosaccharide composition of the pectin samples was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) following an established method30. Briefly, pectin samples (5 mg) were subjected to sequential methanolysis (1 mL anhydrous methanol containing 1 mol/L HCl, 80 °C, 16 h) and acid hydrolysis (0.5 mL of 2 mol/L trifluoroacetic acid, 120 °C, 1 h). The released monosaccharides were derivatized with 1-phenyl-3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (PMP) and analyzed using a Shimadzu LC-10ATvp system with UV-Vis detection at 245 nm. PMP-derivatized monosaccharides (20 µL) were injected and eluted with a mobile phase consisting of PBS (0.1 mol/L, pH 7.0) and acetonitrile (82:18, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Pb2+ adsorption experiments

Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of pH (2.0–7.0), temperature (20–80 °C), initial Pb2+ concentration (50–1000 mg/L), and contact time (2–90 min) on the adsorption performance of the pectin samples. In a typical procedure, 0.100 g ± 0.001 g of pectin was added to 50 mL of Pb2+ solution at a predetermined concentration in a polypropylene centrifuge tube. The mixture was agitated at 120 rpm in a temperature-controlled orbital shaker (THZ-300, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.) until adsorption equilibrium was attained. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane. The residual Pb2+ concentration was measured by flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS, ZEEnit 700P, Analytik Jena, Germany). To avoid introducing extraneous ions, the pH was monitored throughout the experiment and manually adjusted to the target value (± 0.2) using small volumes of 0.1 mol/L NaOH or HCl as required. Characterization

FT-IR spectroscopy analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were collected using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples were prepared as KBr pellets containing 1% (w/w) pectin, and spectra were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm–1 with a resolution of 4 cm–1.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

Surface elemental composition and Pb binding states of AHP before and after adsorption were examined by XPS using a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250 Xi spectrometer (UK) equipped with an Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV). The binding energy scale was calibrated with the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV.

Scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)

Morphological and elemental analyses were performed using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, JSM-7900F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) before and after Pb2+ adsorption. Multiple regions were examined at various magnifications to ensure representative sampling. Areas showing typical surface morphology and free of artifacts were selected for detailed EDS point analysis and elemental mapping.

BET surface area and porosity analysis

Textural properties of the pectin samples were evaluated by nitrogen adsorption-desorption at 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyzer (USA). Prior to measurement, samples were degassed at 105 °C for 6 h under vacuum. Specific surface area was calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method, and pore characteristics were derived from the desorption branch of the isotherm using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n ≥ 3). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between groups was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05). Batch reproducibility was evaluated via the coefficient of variation (CV = SD/mean × 100%).

Results and discussion

Physicochemical characterization of pectin samples

Comparison of extraction yields and physicochemical properties

The extraction yields and key physicochemical properties of the two sunflower head pectins (AHP and LHP) and commercial citrus pectin (CP) are summarized in Table 1. The extraction yield was significantly influenced by the processing conditions. AHP, obtained under a more stringent thermal regime (100 °C for 3 h), exhibited a yield of 18.35% ± 1.08%, which was significantly higher than that of LHP (14.58% ± 1.35%, extracted at 80 °C for 1 h). This result aligns with previous studies in which elevated temperatures improved pectin extraction efficiency, likely due to enhanced polymer solubility and cell wall disruption18. The low coefficients of variation (CV = 5.9% for AHP, 9.3% for LHP) indicated good reproducibility across batches. Compositional analysis revealed that the two sunflower head pectins were fundamentally similar, with no statistically significant differences in GalA content, protein content, ash content, or DE. The principal and intended structural distinction between AHP and LHP resided unequivocally in their molecular weight distributions. In contrast, CP exhibited a distinctly different profile typical of high methoxyl pectin, with significantly higher DE (83.24%±3.22%) and molecular weight, and correspondingly lower GalA content (76.83%±3.74%). The high GalA content and low DE of the sunflower head pectins afforded a high density of free carboxyl groups, which were well-established prerequisites for efficient heavy metal binding via ion exchange and coordination31, thereby providing a favorable structural foundation for adsorption.

Molecular weight distribution analysis

The molecular weight (Mw) distribution profiles of the pectin samples, determined by HPGPC, are presented in Fig. 1. All samples exhibited broad and polydisperse distributions, consistent with the structural heterogeneity typical of plant-derived pectins32. Both AHP and CP displayed unimodal elution profiles, whereas LHP showed a distinct bimodal distribution, indicating the presence of two predominant Mw populations. Quantitative analysis revealed significant differences in average molecular weight among the three pectins. CP exhibited the highest Mw (573.1 kDa), while LHP displayed an intermediate bimodal distribution with peaks centered at 316.4 and 115.7 kDa. In contrast, AHP possessed the lowest Mw (78.2 kDa) with a unimodal profile. This pronounced reduction in Mw for AHP was attributed to β-elimination reactions, which are known to cleave glycosidic linkages under prolonged high-temperature extraction conditions. The lower Mw of AHP was identified as a key contributor to its enhanced adsorption performance. Reduced Mw minimized intermolecular chain entanglement, thereby improving the accessibility of carboxylate binding sites. Furthermore, it facilitated more facile conformational rearrangements upon metal ion coordination, promoting the formation of an open and permeable ion-crosslinked network.

FT-IR spectroscopy analysis before adsorption

The structural and functional features of the pectin samples were characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy prior to Pb2+ adsorption (Fig. 4A). All spectra exhibited a broad band in the 3600–3000 cm–1 region, attributable to O–H stretching vibrations associated with the the hydrogen-bonding network of polysaccharide33. Peaks in the 3000–2800 cm–1 range corresponded to C–H stretching vibrations of pyranose rings34. Distinct differences among the pectin samples were observed in the carboxylate-related spectral regions. Characteristic bands were identified at approximately 1737 cm–1 (esterified carboxyl, –COOR), 1615 cm–1 (asymmetric stretching of free carboxylate, –COO–), and 1413 cm–1 (symmetric vibration of –COO–)35. The relative intensities of the bands at 1737 cm–1 and 1615 cm–1 provided direct spectroscopic validation of the DE36. Both AHP and LHP exhibited a stronger –COO– signal relative to the –COOR signal, consistent with their low methoxyl nature as determined chemically (Table 1). In contrast, the spectrum of CP was dominated by the intense –COOR band at 1737 cm–1, characteristic of its high DE (83.24%). The FT-IR spectrum of AHP further revealed features indicative of extraction-induced structural modification. Specifically, a decrease in the relative intensity of the –COOR band at 1740 cm–1 was accompanied by enhanced absorption in the 1650–1600 cm–1 region and a more pronounced band near 1030 cm–137. These spectral signatures were diagnostic of the formation of unsaturated structures resulting from β-elimination reactions. This observation provided spectroscopic evidence linking the high-temperature extraction conditions to the chain-scission mechanism responsible for the significantly lower molecular weight of AHP, as quantified by HPGPC (section “Molecular weight distribution analysis”).

Monosaccharide composition analysis

The monosaccharide compositions of the pectin samples are detailed in Table 2. Galacturonic acid (GalA) was the predominant monosaccharide, accounting for 83.26%, 72.72%, and 69.37% of the total sugar content in AHP, LHP, and CP, respectively. Neutral sugars constituted the minor fraction, including rhamnose (Rha, 2.21%–5.70%), galactose (Gal, 4.57%–18.58%), arabinose (Ara, 2.79%–5.56%), glucose (Glc, 2.51%–6.08%), mannose (Man, 0.46%–1.48%), and fucose (Fuc, 0–1.00%). These compositional profiles reflected variations governed by both botanical origin and extraction parameters. The high GalA/Rha ratios (16.89 for AHP, 12.76 for LHP, and 31.39 for CP) confirmed that homogalacturonan (HG) domains were dominant across all samples, comprising over 90% of the pectin structure, with rhamnogalacturonan-I (RG-I) constituting only a minor fraction (3.78%–9.33%)38. This finding is critically relevant, as the linear HG backbone, composed primarily of GalA residues, serves as the principal structural platform for metal ion binding in pectin15. Furthermore, the (Ara + Gal)/Rha ratio, an indicator of side-chain branching complexity within RG-I domains, was markedly higher for CP (9.67) compared to the sunflower head pectins AHP (1.68) and LHP (2.35). This result is consistent with the documented presence of more extensive arabinogalactan side chains in citrus pectin39. The sunflower head pectins (AHP and LHP) are therefore characterized by an architecture dominated by linear, GalA-rich HG domains with simplified, sparsely branched side chains. This combination, a high density of potential carboxylate ligands coupled with minimal steric hindrance, establishes a favorable structural foundation for subsequent metal ion adsorption.

Adsorption properties of AHP, LHP, and CP toward Pb2+

Effect of initial pH on Pb2+ adsorption

The initial solution pH exerts a significant influence on Pb2+ adsorption, as it governs both the surface charge of the pectin adsorbents and the aqueous speciation of the metal ion40. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted within the pH range of 2.0–7.0 to avoid precipitation of Pb (II) hydroxides. As shown in Fig. 2A, all pectins exhibited a characteristic pH-dependent adsorption profile. Adsorption capacity increased sharply from pH 2.0 to 4.0, plateaued between pH 4.0 and 6.0, and gradually declined at pH > 6.0, with an optimum around pH 5.0. This behavior is dictated by the acid-base equilibrium of pectin carboxyl groups, which possess an apparent pKa typically between 3.5 and 5.041. Under strongly acidic conditions (pH < 4.0), carboxyl groups remain predominantly protonated (–COOH), exhibiting weak affinity for Pb2+, while high concentrations of H3O+ compete effectively with Pb2+ for binding sites. As pH increases through the carboxyl pKa region, progressive deprotonation generates anionic –COO– groups, enhancing electrostatic attraction and enabling specific multidentate coordination via mechanisms such as the egg-box model42,43. The observed decline in adsorption above pH 6.0 is attributed to the competitive complexation of Pb2+ by OH– ions, leading to the formation of soluble hydroxo complexes and the incipient precipitation of Pb(OH)2, which reduces the concentration of free Pb2+ available for sorption. AHP consistently exhibited the highest adsorption capacity across the tested pH range, along with the most pronounced response to pH variation. In contrast, CP exhibited the weakest pH dependence and the lowest capacity. This differential behavior correlates with the structural profiles detailed in sections “Molecular weight distribution analysis” and “FT-IR spectroscopy analysis before adsorption”. The lower DE and lower molecular weight of AHP create a chemical environment that favors the generation of deprotonated carboxylate groups as pH increases, which likely contributes to its enhanced adsorption performance and stronger pH response relative to CP and LHP8. Based on these results, pH 5.0 was identified as the optimal condition for Pb2+ removal across all samples and was selected for all subsequent adsorption experiments.

Effect of initial Pb2+ concentration on adsorption

The equilibrium adsorption capacity of the pectin samples as a function of the initial Pb2+ concentration (50–1000 mg/L) is presented in Fig. 2B. All materials exhibited a characteristic three-phase adsorption trend, reflecting the progressive occupancy of available binding sites. At low initial concentrations (50–200 mg/L), the adsorption capacity increased in an approximately linear manner, driven by a high concentration gradient and a large surplus of unoccupied carboxylate sites, which facilitated efficient Pb2+ uptake44. As the concentration increased to an intermediate range (200–600 mg/L), the adsorption rate decelerated, indicating a nonlinear transition phase due to the gradual saturation of the most accessible sites. At concentrations above 600 mg/L, the adsorption isotherms approached a plateau, signifying that available sites were largely occupied and the materials were nearing their practical adsorption capacities under the given conditions. Throughout the entire concentration range, AHP consistently demonstrated the highest adsorption capacity at each equilibrium point, followed by LHP and then CP. Based on these comprehensive adsorption profiles, an initial Pb2+ concentration of 250 mg/L was selected for all subsequent kinetic and mechanistic studies to ensure robust and comparable measurements.

Effect of contact time on Pb2+ adsorption

The temporal evolution of Pb2+ adsorption by the pectin samples is shown in Fig. 2C. All materials exhibited a characteristic triphasic kinetic profile: a rapid initial uptake within the first 20 min, a subsequent decelerating phase from 20 to 40 min, and a final equilibrium plateau beyond approximately 40 min. This pattern is consistent with a coordination-driven adsorption process subject to evolving mass transfer constraints. The initial rapid phase corresponds to the fast complexation of Pb2+ with the most accessible carboxylate groups on the pectin surface. As adsorption proceeds, accumulating bound ions promote localized interchain cross-linking, consistent with ionotropic gelation phenomena reported for pectin8,45. The subsequent deceleration in rate likely reflects the increasing diffusion resistance as Pb2+ must penetrate this developing coordination network to access interior binding sites. The final plateau signifies the practical saturation of available sites under the experimental conditions. Although the time required to reach equilibrium was comparable (40 min) for all pectin samples, the equilibrium adsorption capacity of AHP was significantly higher than those of LHP and CP. These results indicate that while the overall kinetics are governed by common steps, surface coordination followed by limited ingress, the ultimate capacity is determined by the total number of accessible and utilizable binding sites, which varies among pectin samples. Based on the kinetic profiles, a contact time of 40 min was selected for subsequent equilibrium studies to ensure the attainment of adsorption equilibrium under the experimental conditions.

Effect of adsorption temperature on Pb2+ adsorption

The influence of temperature on Pb2+ adsorption was evaluated over the range of 20–80 °C (Fig. 2D). For all pectin samples, adsorption capacity increased with rising temperature up to an optimum near 60 °C, beyond which a gradual decline occurred. The capacities increased from 85.45, 68.52, and 50.36 mg/g at 20 °C to maximum values of 222.20, 181.95, and 132.41 mg/g at 60 °C for AHP, LHP, and CP, respectively. The initial increase in capacity with temperature is primarily attributed to enhanced diffusion kinetics of Pb2+ ions and potentially favorable conformational adjustments of the pectin chains, both of which improve access to binding sites. The decline observed above 60 °C is likely due to the increasing dominance of opposing effects, such as thermal destabilization of coordination complexes and an elevated tendency for desorption46. Notably, AHP exhibited the highest capacity at each temperature point, with values 1.22 to 1.68 times greater than those of LHP and CP. These results indicate that a moderate temperature range of 40–60 °C is conducive to optimizing Pb2+ adsorption for these pectin-based biosorbents.

For all pectin samples, the Langmuir model provided the best fit to the experimental data (R2 = 0.9991–0.9996, Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating that Pb2+ adsorption predominantly occurred as a monolayer on a surface with relatively homogeneous binding sites12,51. This finding is consistent with the structural uniformity of the pectin samples, wherein carboxyl groups are regularly distributed along the GalA backbone, particularly within the homogalacturonan regions. In contrast, the Freundlich model (R2 = 0.8633–0.8708), while still providing a reasonable fit, suggested a lower degree of surface heterogeneity compared to many composite adsorbents34. This distinction underscores that the homogeneous adsorption mechanism is fundamentally dictated by the intrinsic macromolecular architecture of pectin. The derived Langmuir parameters are summarized in Table 3. The theoretical maximum adsorption capacity (qm) followed the order: AHP (295.86 mg/g) > LHP (235.29 mg/g) > CP (167.50 mg/g). This hierarchy correlates with the structural properties of the pectins. The low DE of AHP (26.34%±1.52%) and LHP (29.32%±2.18%) ensures a high content of free carboxyl groups (–COO⁻), which serve as the primary ligands for Pb2+ coordination via mechanisms such as the egg-box model8,15. The significantly lower qm of CP is consistent with its high DE, wherein methyl esterification reduces the number of available anionic sites49. Moreover, molecular weight emerged as the key factor differentiating AHP and LHP. Despite their similar low DE values, the substantially lower Mw of AHP (78.2 kDa) compared to the bimodal, higher Mw distribution of LHP (316.4 and 115.7 kDa) has profound implications for adsorption performance. The lower Mw minimizes intermolecular chain entanglement in solution, thereby reducing diffusion barriers for Pb2+ ions and promoting a more open, accessible polymer network. This enhanced physical accessibility allows a greater proportion of the carboxylate groups in AHP to participate in binding, effectively translating its theoretical site density into a higher practical adsorption capacity. This interpretation is corroborated by the observed higher solution viscosity and greater gelation propensity of LHP, which serve as macroscopic indicators of a more entangled network state. The favorability of adsorption under the studied conditions was confirmed by the separation factor (RL), which ranged between 0 and 1 for all samples, indicating favorable adsorption52. Influence of co-existing cations on Pb2+ adsorption

The practical applicability of an adsorbent depends considerably on its performance in multicomponent systems containing competing ions. The competitive adsorption of Pb2+ onto the pectin samples in the presence of Na+, Ca2+, and Al3+ is shown in Fig. 3. The results indicate a distinct interference hierarchy: Al3+ > Ca2+ > Na+. At an equimolar concentration of 10 mmol/L, Al3+ reduced Pb2+ uptake by 34.27%–43.38%, while Ca2+ caused a 31.36%–33.94% reduction. In contrast, Na+ exhibited negligible influence across the tested concentration range (0–20 mmol/L). The pronounced competition from Al3+ can be attributed to its high charge density, which favors strong electrostatic attractions with the anionic carboxylate (–COO–) sites, as well as its ability to form stable, multidentate carboxylate complexes that effectively displace bound Pb2+. The significant interference from Ca2+, despite its lower charge density than Pb2+, confirms that both divalent cations compete for the same primary coordination sites, likely through similar inner-sphere complexation pathways. Conversely, the minimal effect of Na + is consistent with its low charge density and its tendency to participate only in weak, outer-sphere interactions, which are readily outcompeted by multivalent ions. These results substantiate that Pb2+ adsorption extends beyond simple ion exchange34 and involves specific, inner-sphere coordination to carboxylate groups12. The observed cation affinity series reflects the combined influence of ionic charge, hydration energy, and complexation15,21. Consequently, the efficiency of pectin-based adsorbents in real aqueous systems may be modulated by the background concentrations of hard cations such as Ca2+ and Al3+. This selectivity pattern therefore outlines a rational pathway for future surface functionalization to enhance Pb2+ specificity53, building upon the high-capacity foundation established by the intrinsically optimized structure demonstrated in this study.

FT-IR spectroscopy analysis after adsorption

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was employed to examine structural changes in the pectin samples following Pb2+ adsorption (Fig. 4B). Comparative analysis revealed distinct spectral shifts consistent with metal coordination. In the hydroxyl region, the broad O–H stretching band centered at 3316 cm–1 shifted to lower wavenumbers (approximately 3280 cm–1) and broadened, indicating participation of hydroxyl groups in Pb–O coordination51. More definitive evidence was observed in the carboxylate region. The asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the free carboxylate group, located at approximately 1615 cm–1 and 1413 cm–1, respectively, shifted to lower frequencies (1604 cm–1 and 1406 cm–1) with increased intensity after adsorption12. This red shift and intensification confirm that carboxylate groups transitioned from an ionic or solvated state to a coordinated state with Pb2+, supporting inner-sphere complexation34. Additionally, the ester carbonyl band near 1737 cm–1 exhibited a slight shift and a decrease in intensity, suggesting possible involvement of esterified carboxyl groups in metal binding. This observation provides spectroscopic evidence that the content of accessible carboxyl groups is a key determinant of adsorption performance. Collectively, the FT-IR results confirm that Pb2+ adsorption proceeds primarily through specific coordination to carboxylate and hydroxyl groups.

XPS analysis

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to investigate the surface chemical state changes of AHP before and after Pb2+ adsorption. The survey spectrum after adsorption (Fig. 5A) confirmed the presence of a distinct Pb 4f signal at approximately 139 eV. High-resolution analysis of the Pb 4f region (Fig. 5B) revealed a doublet at binding energies of 143.6 eV (4f5/2) and 137.8 eV (4f7/2)51. The position of the Pb 4f7/2 peak is consistent with Pb in a coordination environment involving oxygen donors, such as in carboxylate-Pb complexes (e.g., –COOPb+ or (–COO)2Pb), confirming a specific chemical interaction beyond physical deposition52. The C 1s spectrum before adsorption (Fig. 5C) exhibited peaks at 284.8 eV (C–C), 286.2 eV (C–O), 287.6 eV (C=O), and 289.3 eV (O–C=O)34. After Pb2+ adsorption (Fig. 5E), these components shifted to 284.8, 285.7, 286.9, and 288.8 eV, respectively, accompanied by a noticeable decrease in the intensity of the C=O and C–O contributions. Similarly, the O 1s spectrum before adsorption (Fig. 5D) was fitted with three peaks at 531.2 eV (C=O), 532.5 eV (C–O), and 534.3 eV (–OH)12. Following adsorption (Fig. 5F), these peaks shifted to 530.8, 532.1, and 533.4 eV, respectively. The decrease in intensity of the component near 532–533 eV suggests that oxygen atoms from hydroxyl or ether groups participated in the coordination with Pb2+, consistent with the proposed synergistic role of –COO– and –OH in metal binding51. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Pb2+ adsorption onto AHP involves the formation of inner-sphere coordination complexes, wherein Pb2+ is chelated by oxygen atoms originating predominantly from carboxylate and hydroxyl groups.

SEM-EDS analysis

SEM-EDS analysis was conducted to correlate morphological and elemental changes in the pectin samples before and after Pb2+ adsorption. Prior to adsorption, SEM images (Fig. 6A, C and E) revealed surface morphologies consistent with the textural properties derived from BET measurements. AHP exhibited an interconnected macroporous structure, LHP displayed visible porosity, and CP appeared relatively smooth and dense. Elemental analysis confirmed that carbon (36.60–41.77 wt%) and oxygen (56.25–60.16 wt%) were the predominant elements. The calculated C/O atomic ratios (0.81 for AHP, 0.90 for LHP, and 0.99 for CP) correlated well with their respective degrees of esterification. Trace sodium (1.98–3.82 wt%) was detected as exchangeable counter-ions, with no Pb signals present. After Pb2+ adsorption, all samples underwent significant morphological transformation (Fig. 6B, D and F), including increased surface roughness and the appearance of wrinkled or aggregated features, most prominently in AHP. These textural changes are indicative of surface-mediated structural reorganization, likely driven by ion-induced cross-linking51. Quantitative EDS analysis provided evidence of the adsorption process. A strong Pb signal was detected post-adsorption, with measured Pb contents directly correlating with adsorption capacity: 43.29 wt% for AHP, 35.45 wt% for LHP, and 7.19 wt% for CP. Concurrently, the sodium signal vanished entirely. This stoichiometric replacement of Na+ by Pb2+ provides direct support for an ion-exchange mechanism as a primary uptake pathway55. The preservation of carbon and oxygen signals indicates the structural integrity of the pectin backbone throughout the process. The more pronounced textural evolution and higher Pb loading observed for AHP align with its optimized macromolecular architecture. The lower molecular weight and more open porous morphology of AHP facilitate greater accessibility for Pb2+ ions and enable more extensive, stable network formation upon metal binding.

BET analysis

The textural properties of the pectin samples were characterized by N2 adsorption-desorption before and after Pb2+ adsorption (Table 6). Although N2 adsorption at 77 K may underestimate the absolute surface area of polymeric materials due to diffusion limitations, the data provided reliable comparative trends. Following Pb2+ adsorption, a consistent and mechanistically significant evolution was observed: the specific surface area (SSA) and total pore volume (PV) increased, while the average pore size (PS) decreased for all samples. This combination of increased SSA/PV and decreased PS is counterintuitive for simple pore-filling and provides evidence for a dynamic, metal-ion-driven structural reorganization within the pectin matrix. Coordination between Pb2+ and carboxylate groups on adjacent pectin chains, consistent with the egg-box model8,15, acts as ionic cross-links. This cross-linking induces polymer network contraction while simultaneously creating new internal voids and interfaces within the consolidating gel structure, leading to the observed increase in SSA and PV. Consequently, the adsorption capacity is not merely dictated by the initial surface area but can be dynamically enhanced during adsorption52. This mechanism, which contrasts with the pore-blocking behavior typically observed in rigid inorganic adsorbents, underscores the adaptive and responsive nature of polysaccharide-based biosorbents. The most pronounced textural changes were observed for AHP, aligning with its highest Pb2+ uptake and its favorable macromolecular structure characterized by lower DE and lower Mw, which promote extensive and accessible network formation.

Pb2+ adsorption mechanisms on sunflower head pectin

Integrating evidence from adsorption equilibria, kinetics, thermodynamics, spectroscopy, and microscopy, a coherent mechanism for Pb2+ removal by sunflower head pectin is proposed, as illustrated in Fig. 7. The process begins with the electrostatic migration of Pb2+ ions to the anionic pectin surface, followed by immobilization through specific inner-sphere coordination. This chemisorption is characterized by Pb2+ binding to oxygen donors, primarily carboxylate (–COO–) groups with additional involvement of hydroxyl (–OH) groups12, consistent with an extended egg-box model15,51. The efficacy of this process is governed by the synergy between chemical affinity and physical accessibility. A low DE directly maximizes the content of available carboxylate ligands, thereby enhancing chemical affinity. More critically, a lower molecular weight, as exemplified by AHP, dictates favorable solution-state behavior12,56. Reduced intermolecular chain entanglement, supported by comparative rheological data of AHP and LHP, minimizes mass transfer resistance, promotes superior molecular dispersion, and facilitates the dynamic conformational rearrangements necessary for efficient multidentate coordination8. Consequently, upon interaction with Pb2+, AHP undergoes ionotropic gelation, forming a more open, permeable, and extensively cross-linked network. This active structural reorganization rationalizes the multi-stage kinetics, the endothermic and entropy-driven thermodynamics, and the post-adsorption increases in surface area and microporosity. Thus, the superior adsorption capacity of AHP stems from the optimal integration of high binding-site density and high site accessibility, enabling the formation of an efficient adsorption network before diffusion-limited saturation occurs.

Performance benchmarking and future implications for application

The superior Pb2+ adsorption capacity of the prepared sunflower head pectin AHP, as presented in Table 7, exceeds that of most reported natural pectin-based adsorbents and is comparable to several chemically modified or composite pectin materials. This performance originates from its optimized macromolecular structure, achieved through controlled thermal extraction, wherein a low degree of esterification maximizes carboxylate site density, while a reduced molecular weight ensures enhanced site accessibility. However, the water-soluble nature of the native pectin presents practical challenges for solid-liquid separation and reusability in conventional treatment systems. Laboratory-scale regeneration experiments involving EDTA-assisted desorption, dialysis, and lyophilization demonstrated material stability and reusability, with approximately 85% of the initial adsorption capacity retained after five cycles. Nevertheless, this multi-step regeneration process is operationally complex, time-consuming, and energy-intensive, limiting its scalability for practical water treatment. Future efforts should therefore focus on immobilizing the optimized pectin structure into robust, insoluble, and readily recoverable forms. Promising strategies include the fabrication of covalently cross-linked hydrogels9,25,45, incorporation into porous carriers to form composite beads7,23,24, or development of magnetically responsive hybrid materials52,57. The performance of such engineered adsorbents should then be systematically evaluated under technologically relevant conditions, including continuous-flow operation, long-term stability, selectivity, and fouling resistance in complex wastewater matrices. Finally, a comprehensive techno-economic analysis encompassing scaled-up biomass processing, adsorbent fabrication, and regeneration cycles will be essential to assess commercial feasibility.

Conclusions

This study established extraction temperature as a critical parameter for modulating the macromolecular architecture of sunflower head pectin, thereby governing its efficacy as a biosorbent for Pb2+ removal. Through two distinct thermal extraction protocols, structurally differentiated natural low methoxyl pectins were obtained: AHP (100 °C, 3 h) and LHP (80 °C, 1 h). Although both pectins exhibited comparable GalA content (83–85%) and DE (26–29%), AHP possessed a substantially reduced molecular weight (78.2 kDa, unimodal) versus the bimodal distribution of LHP (316.4 and 115.7 kDa). This structural divergence enabled AHP to achieve a Langmuir maximum adsorption capacity of 295.86 mg/g for Pb2+, exceeding LHP by 25.7% and CP by 76.6%. Adsorption isotherm, kinetic, and thermodynamic analyses confirmed that the process followed the Langmuir monolayer model and pseudo-second-order kinetics (R2 > 0.99), proceeding via spontaneous, endothermic chemisorption. Comprehensive characterization (FT-IR, XPS, SEM-EDS, and BET) revealed that Pb2+ immobilization involved a synergistic mechanism of ion exchange, electrostatic attraction, and inner-sphere coordination with carboxylate and hydroxyl groups, consistent with the extended egg-box model. Critically, adsorption performance was governed by the synergy between chemical affinity and physical accessibility. While low esterification provided abundant carboxylate sites, it was the reduced molecular weight and consequent minimization of chain entanglement that rendered these sites accessible. This enhanced accessibility accelerated Pb2+ diffusion, facilitated ionotropic gelation, and promoted structural reorganization into an open, cross-linked network, as evidenced by post-adsorption increases in surface area and pore volume. These findings demonstrate that controlled thermal extraction modulates the molecular weight and chain entanglement state of sunflower head pectin, governing the physical accessibility of binding sites and the efficiency of ion-induced network formation during adsorption. Consequently, this work provides a rational, reagent-free strategy for transforming agricultural residues into high-performance biosorbents by concurrently optimizing chemical affinity and physical accessibility through extraction conditions for polysaccharide-based adsorbents for heavy metal remediation.
