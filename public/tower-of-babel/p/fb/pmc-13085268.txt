PCDSOS: a novel clinical predictive tool for screening primary ciliary dyskinesia in adult bronchiectasis patients—a multicenter derivation and external validation study
Authors: Wangji Zhou, Lin Wang, Qiaoling Chen, Yaqi Wang, Haiyu Pang, Anhui Guo, Aohua Wu, Xueqi Liu, Jinrong Dai, Shuzhen Meng, Rongchun Wang, Danhui Yang, Yaping Liu, Weiguo Zhu, Kai-Feng Xu, Xue Zhang, Hong Luo, Xinlun Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a rare but underdiagnosed genetic cause of adult bronchiectasis, with current predictive tools (e.g., PICADAR, NA-CDCF) primarily validated in children and lacking adult-specific predictors (e.g., subfertility). This study aimed to develop and validate a practical tool (PCDSOS) for PCD screening in adult bronchiectasis.MethodsDerivation group (n = 287) from Peking Union Medical College Hospital (2013–2025) and validation group (n = 107) from The Second Xiangya Hospital (2016–2024) were included. All patients completed ≥ 1 PCD diagnostic test (nasal nitric oxide, whole-exome sequencing, transmission electron microscopy, or high-speed video microscopy analysis). Logistic regression was used to develop PCDSOS, with performance assessed by AUC, calibration curve, and decision curve analysis.ResultsExisting tools showed reduced accuracy in adults (AUC: 0.76–0.85 vs. 0.84–0.98 in original studies). PCDSOS included 6 predictors: pulmonary atelectasis/lobectomy in middle lobe/lingula (P, 2 points), neonatal chest symptoms (C, 2 points), organ laterality defects (D, 5 points), chronic sinusitis (S, 2 points), chronic otitis media/hearing loss from childhood (O, 1 point), and subfertility (S, 1 point). At cutoff = 3, PCDSOS had sensitivity 0.86, specificity 0.76 (derivation cohort, AUC = 0.90) and sensitivity 0.90, specificity 0.67 (validation cohort, AUC = 0.92). A free web-based version of PCDSOS for automated scoring is available to facilitate clinical application.ConclusionsPCDSOS outperforms existing tools in adult bronchiectasis, providing a cost-effective screening strategy to identify patients requiring further PCD diagnostic testing—critical for preventing irreversible lung damage and guiding genetic counseling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04780-5.Keyword: Primary ciliary dyskinesia; Predictive tool; Diagnostic score

Background

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a rare, heterogeneous genetic disorder characterized by motile ciliary dysfunction. The global PCD prevalence is estimated to be at least 1 in 7554 [1]. The primary clinical manifestations of PCD include neonatal respiratory distress, chronic sinusitis, recurrent otitis media, lateral organ defects, subfertility, and recurrent chest infections leading to bronchiectasis [2, 3]. Although patients with PCD exhibit clinical manifestations from a young age, PCD is often diagnosed later and is not uncommon in adulthood. According to data from the European Reference Network’s ERN-LUNG international PCD Registry, approximately 25% of patients were diagnosed in adulthood (age at diagnosis, median: 10 years, interquartile range: 4.4–17 years) among 947 documented cases [4]. Similarly, as of May 2021, 244 patients with PCD have been reported in China, among whom still 55 (22.5%) are adults [5].

The diagnosis of PCD remains challenging. According to the guidelines of the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and European Respiratory Society (ERS), current methods for diagnosing PCD include genetic sequencing or transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [6–8]. Kartagener syndrome, characterized by the triad of situs inversus, chronic sinusitis, and bronchiectasis, is recognized as a highly indicative clinical syndrome for PCD [9, 10]. In addition, nasal nitric oxide (nNO) and high-speed video microscopy analysis (HSVA) can assist in the diagnosis of PCD [6, 7, 11]. These diagnostic methods require expensive infrastructure and an experienced team of clinicians, geneticists, and microscopists, which are typically available in few PCD diagnostic centers. Accordingly, several predictive tools have been developed to guide the referral of patients with suspected PCD; these tools include the PICADAR, North American Criteria Defined Clinical Features (NA-CDCF), and modified PICADAR [12–14].

All of these tools performed reasonably well in the original publications describing their application. However, their accuracy and effectiveness in adults are unclear. First, PICADAR and NA-CDCF were primarily developed and validated in pediatrics, and it is necessary to validate them in adults [12, 13, 15]. Second, the items of the above two scores included a number of predictors based on early life, such as gestational age, admission to a neonatal unit, and cough or nasal congestion beginning before 6 months of age, which may be difficult to recall in adulthood [12, 13, 16]. Based on this, Rademacher et al. modified the PICADAR by omitting the gestational age and integrating neonatal respiratory symptoms and the new tool showed good clinical performance in an adult bronchiectasis cohort [14]. But this modified scoring system has not yet been validated in other cohorts. Third, subfertility is a commonly reported feature of PCD and may be a powerful predictor of adult diagnosis [17]. However, subfertility has not been included as a predictor in previous predictive tools.

Bronchiectasis is an age-related finding in PCD, with 50% of children having bronchiectasis by 8 years of age and nearly universal presence in adults [3]. In adults with non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis, the proportion of those with PCD can range from 0.6% to 9% [18–20]. According to the guidelines of the British Thoracic Society (BTS), the combination of bronchiectasis with supporting clinical features of PCD, including a history of neonatal distress, symptoms from childhood, recurrent otitis media, rhinosinusitis, or infertility, may serve as an effective strategy for identifying adults with PCD [21]. Our preliminary study demonstrated that utilizing bronchiectasis combined with other PCD-supporting clinical features as key search terms in electronic medical record (EMR) systems can facilitate the identification of patients with PCD who were previously missed [22]. Multivariate analysis by Ewen et al. demonstrated that chronic rhinosinusitis/nasal polyps, age < 53 years, middle/lower lobe involvement, bronchiectasis duration > 15 years, and prior P. aeruginosa infection were independent predictors of PCD in adult bronchiectasis. However, since not all patients underwent PCD-related diagnostic testing, confirmation bias may exist, and the study ultimately did not develop a predictive tool [19]. Furthermore, a previous systematic review of 244 Chinese patients with PCD revealed atelectasis in 88 cases (37.8%) [5]. The PCD-CT score also identifies atelectasis or prior lobectomy as a significant predictive indicator for PCD [23]. These consistent findings underscore the diagnostic utility of this imaging feature, though further exploration is warranted.

PCD is characterized by progressive respiratory impairment, with lung function declining steadily with age. Severe cases may eventually require lung transplantation due to end-stage respiratory failure. Notably, delayed diagnosis is closely associated with compromised baseline forced expiratory volume in 1 s, as adults diagnosed later in life often present with established bronchiectasis and irreversible parenchymal lung damage [24]. Conversely, mounting evidence indicates that timely diagnosis and initiation of guideline-recommended management can stabilize or even improve lung function and reduce acute exacerbation rates. Moreover, the advent of newer PCD therapies and ongoing clinical trials necessitate accurate and early diagnosis to ensure eligible patients can access these emerging treatments [25]. Beyond clinical management, definitive PCD diagnosis enables critical genetic counseling. It allows for carrier screening in family members, risk stratification for inherited PCD in offspring, and personalized fertility guidance for affected individuals with subfertility.

Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) and The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (The Second Xiangya Hospital) are two PCD diagnostic centers focusing on adults. In this study, we used a derivation group from PUMCH to validate the performance of existing PCD predictive tools and develop a new diagnostic predictive tool (PCDSOS). We then conducted external validation in a validation group from The Second Xiangya Hospital. We aimed to develop a tool to assist clinicians in identifying adult bronchiectasis patients with suspected PCD who should be referred for PCD diagnostic testing, thereby facilitating the early diagnosis of PCD.

Methods

Study population

The derivation group was selected from a suspected hereditary bronchiectasis cohort at PUMCH enrolled between January 2013 and May 2025. This cohort comprised patients with bronchiectasis in whom genetic etiology was suspected during initial clinical assessment. According to the ERS and BTS guidelines, eligible patients presented with at least one of the following clinical features: chest symptoms from childhood, rhinosinusitis or otitis media, organ laterality defects, subfertility (including a history of infertility, assisted reproduction, spontaneous abortion, or decreased sperm motility with less than 32% progressive motility [26]), a family history of bronchiectasis, or a history of neonatal respiratory distress [7, 21]. Patients later diagnosed with PCD or cystic fibrosis (CF) were classified into the respective PCD or CF cohorts. The diagnostic method for PCD is determined by the level of clinical suspicion, referring to the ATS/ERS guideline-recommended algorithm [6, 7]. Initial screening involves nNO. Patients with positive nNO results (< 77 nl/min) undergo further testing, including genetic sequencing or TEM. For those with negative nNO results but strong clinical suspicion, further evaluation with HSVA, TEM, or genetic sequencing is performed. Cases with negative nNO results and low clinical suspicion require no further testing.

The validation group was selected from the similar cohort at The Second Xiangya Hospital enrolled between September 2016 and August 2024. The most significant difference from PUMCH is that The Second Xiangya Hospital incorporates HSVA as the initial step in its PCD screening process. For bronchiectasis cases with suspected PCD, the diagnostic protocol initiates with nNO and HSVA. Cases demonstrating positive results in any of these initial tests or those with strong clinical suspicion undergo subsequent confirmatory testing through genetic sequencing or TEM.

The inclusion criteria comprised the adult patients (age at diagnosis ≥ 18 years) who had completed at least one of the following PCD-related diagnostic testing: nNO, whole-exome sequencing (WES), TEM, and HSVA (details in Additional file 1). Patients without detailed medical records (refer to the “Clinical data” section for details) were excluded.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of PUMCH (approval no. I-24PJ1848) and The Second Xiangya Hospital (approval no. LYEC2024-K0225) and was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Diagnostic categories

Based on the results of PCD-related diagnostic testing, the patients were divided into definite PCD, possible PCD, and non-PCD groups. Definite PCD was diagnosed when patients met one of the following criteria: (1) class 1 defects identified by TEM, (2) biallelic pathogenic variants in PCD-associated genes [6–8]. Possible PCD was diagnosed when patients met one of the following criteria: (1) low nNO levels (< 77 nL/min, excluding CF), (2) abnormal HSVA results, (3) class 2 defects identified by TEM, or (4) Kartagener syndrome only [7, 8, 10]. Patients with no clearly abnormal PCD-related diagnostic test results may have other etiologies of bronchiectasis and were categorized into the non-PCD group.

Clinical data

We retrospectively collected the following clinical data from medical records. For the derivation group: (1) basic information (sex, age at diagnosis, family history, consanguinity); (2) respiratory symptoms (chronic wet cough, dyspnea, onset of the above symptoms from childhood); (3) neonatal symptoms (term status, neonatal chest symptoms, neonatal pneumonia, neonatal unit admission); (4) upper airway and ear symptoms (chronic rhinitis, chronic sinusitis, nasal polyps, chronic otitis media, hearing loss, ear surgery, onset of the above symptoms from childhood); (5) other clinical characteristics (organ laterality defects, congenital heart disease, hydrocephalus, subfertility); (6) chest CT characteristics (atelectasis or lobectomy in middle lobe or lingula). In addition, we collected other clinical information to evaluate existing predictive tools (PICADAR, NA-CDCF, and modified PICADAR). For the validation group, data collection included sex, age at diagnosis, and clinical information related to existing tools and PCDSOS.

Statistical analysis

Evaluation of existing PCD predictive tools

Scores for the three existing PCD predictive tools were calculated for the derivation cohort. Predictive performance was assessed by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). AUC values obtained from the derivation cohort were compared with those reported in the original publications to evaluate tool performance. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated for various total scores of each predictive tool.

Development of the PCDSOS score

A total of 25 potential binary predictor variables, including those derived from the PICADAR and additional clinically relevant features, were identified in the derivation cohort [12]. Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare characteristics between patients with definite PCD and non-PCD. Variables with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) or strong clinical relevance (e.g., variables from the PICADAR) were retained for further modeling. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 5 indicating significant collinearity. Forward stepwise logistic regression was performed to construct a simplified and clinically practical model. The final logistic model was used to derive the PCDSOS score, with each predictor assigned a score based on its regression coefficient rounded to the nearest integer. The probability of a PCD diagnosis was estimated using the total score [27]. Model performance was compared between the original regression model and the PCDSOS using AUC values.

Performance of the PCDSOS score

The diagnostic performance of the PCDSOS score was evaluated using ROC analysis. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated for different total scores. Discrimination was interpreted as moderate for AUC values between 0.7 and 0.9, and good for values > 0.9. The optimal cutoff score was determined by jointly considering sensitivity, specificity, and Youden’s index. AUC values for the PCDSOS and existing tools were compared to assess relative discrimination. Overall model performance was evaluated using the Brier score, with values < 0.25 indicating good model fit. A calibration curve was plotted to assess agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, and decision curve analysis was used to evaluate net clinical benefit.

Validation in an external population

The model’s performance in the validation cohort was assessed using the same procedures applied to the derivation cohort. AUC values for the PCDSOS were compared between cohorts.

Comparisons of AUCs were performed using DeLong’s test for correlated datasets or publicly available software for independent datasets [28]. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM SPSS, USA) and R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Web-based tool availability

A user-friendly, free web-based version of the PCDSOS score is available to clinicians and researchers for automated calculation. It can be accessed at: http://pcd.www.rareld.cn/. The tool requires input of the predictors and generates the total score and corresponding PCD risk prompt instantly.

Results

Study population

The enrollment process for patients in the derivation and validation group is illustrated in Additional file 2: Fig. S1. Of 287 patients in the derivation group, 81 (28.2%) had definite PCD, 32 (11.1%) had possible PCD, and 174 (60.6%) had non-PCD. Among them, 213 (74.2%) underwent WES, 209 (72.8%) underwent nNO measurements, 36 (12.5%) underwent TEM analysis, and 18 (6.3%) underwent HSVA. The two most common pathogenic variants were DNAH5 (22.5%) and DNAH11 (15.5%). Of the 107 patients in the validation group, 29 (27.1%) had definite PCD, eight (7.5%) had possible PCD, and 70 (65.4%) had non-PCD. Among them, 43 patients (40.2%) underwent WES, 105 (98.1%) underwent nNO measurement, seven (6.5%) underwent TEM analysis, and 36 (33.6%) underwent HSVA. The diagnostic results for the patients in the two groups are shown in Additional file 2: Table 1, Fig. S2 and Fig. S3.

Median age at diagnosis was 32 years (interquartile range, 24–42) in the derivation group versus 48 years (35–55) in the validation group. The proportions of female sex were 60.3% and 64.5%, respectively. Basic information and a comparison of patients with definite PCD and non-PCD in the two groups are shown in Table 1.

Evaluation of existing PCD predictive tools

In the derivation group, ROC curves were plotted for the three existing predictive tools (Additional file 2: Fig. S4), with AUCs of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.74–0.87) for PICADAR, 0.85 (95% CI: 0.79–0.90) for the modified PICADAR, and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.69–0.83) for the NA-CDCF. In a comparison of the AUCs of the three existing predictive tools between their original publications and this study, we found that the PICADAR scores (0.91 versus 0.81, respectively, P = 0.011), the modified PICADAR scores (0.98 versus 0.85, P < 0.001), and the NA-CDCF score (0.84 versus 0.76, P = 0.044) were statistically different. At the recommended cutoff value, the sensitivity and specificity of the three existing predictive tool scores were 0.52 and 0.97 (PICADAR score), 0.73 and 0.82 (modified PICADAR score), and 0.27 and 0.98 (NA-CDCF), respectively (Additional file 2: Table 2).

Development of the PCDSOS score

The clinical characteristics of the derivation group are summarized in Table 2. Among the 25 potential variables, 12 showed significant differences between patients with definite PCD and non-PCD. Before entering the logistic regression model, multicollinearity analysis confirmed that there was no significant collinearity among the variables. The best logistic regression model included six significant predictors. In order of importance (based on odds ratios), these predictors were organ laterality defects, atelectasis or lobectomy in middle lobe or lingula, neonatal chest symptoms, chronic sinusitis, subfertility, and ear symptoms (chronic otitis media or hearing loss) from childhood (Table 3). Patients with an unknown fertility status during the analysis were considered to have normal fertility.

Performance of the PCDSOS score

The likelihood of a patient having PCD was estimated by comparing the total score with a probability curve (Fig. 2). The highest combined sensitivity and specificity scores (0.74 and 0.92, respectively) were at the cutoff value of 4 points (Table 5). However, when the cutoff value was set at 3 points, the sensitivity is relatively high while the Youden’s index slightly decreases (sensitivity: 0.86; specificity: 0.76), making it more suitable for PCD screening. This corresponded to a 31.93% probability of having PCD.

A comparison of the PCDSOS score with the three existing predictive tools revealed that the AUC of the PCDSOS score was greater than that of the PICADAR (0.90 versus 0.81, P < 0.001), modified PICADAR (0.90 versus 0.85, P = 0.005), and NA-CDCF (0.90 versus 0.76, P < 0.001).

The Brier score in the derivation group was 0.098. Calibration curve for the best model in the derivation group was very close to the diagonal gray line (for the ideal model), indicating that the model was well calibrated (Fig. 3A). Decision curve analysis indicated that when the threshold was almost any range, the use of the PCDSOS to determine whom to refer for PCD diagnostic testing provided greater net benefit than the “test all” or “test none” strategies, supporting PCDSOS’s clinical utility (Fig. 3C).

Validation in an external population

The ROC curve of PCDSOS in the validation group is shown in Fig. 1B. The AUC for the best prediction model was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.85–0.99) and that for the PCDSOS score was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.85–0.99); these values were not significantly different from those in the derivation group (P = 0.645). When the total score was 3 points, the sensitivity and specificity were 0.90 and 0.67, respectively.

The Brier score in the validation group was 0.094. Calibration curve for the best model in the validation group was always lower than the diagonal gray line (for the ideal model), indicating that the model may have overestimated the probability of PCD in the validation group (Fig. 3B). Decision curve analysis (Fig. 3D) showed that when the threshold probability exceeded 6.0%, using the PCDSOS to determine who should be referred for PCD diagnostic testing had good clinical practicality.

Sensitivity analysis

Patients with possible PCD were excluded from PCDSOS development and validation. However, when these participants were reassigned to either the definite PCD or non-PCD groups, AUC values remained similar (Additional file 2: Table 3).

Additionally, patients with incomplete medical records were excluded during enrollment. Sensitivity analysis showed comparable demographics between excluded and included cases. However, idiopathic bronchiectasis was overrepresented among excluded patients, likely due to insufficient etiological documentation (Additional file 2: Table 4).

Discussion

This study was the first to validate three existing PCD predictive tools in adult patients with bronchiectasis. Our results showed that the performances of these predictive tools were not as good as in the original publications. Therefore, we developed the PCDSOS, which demonstrated superior performance compared with previous predictive tools in adults with bronchiectasis and was well validated in an external validation group.

When evaluating adult bronchiectasis patients, physicians should prioritize PCDSOS screening if the patient has any of the following red flags: (1) organ laterality defects (e.g., dextrocardia); (2) chronic sinusitis or otitis media; (3) a history of neonatal respiratory distress; (4) chest symptoms from childhood; (5) subfertility; (6) a family history of bronchiectasis. For scoring, physicians can easily obtain data via routine history-taking (neonatal chest symptoms, subfertility, chronic sinusitis or otitis media), physical examination (organ laterality defects), and chest CT review (middle lobe atelectasis or lobectomy) without additional tests. Patients with a score ≥ 3 should first undergo nNO measurement (if available in primary care settings) or be referred to respiratory specialists for further genetic or TEM testing. For patients with scores < 3, the possibility of a PCD diagnosis is very low, and other etiologies (e.g., post-infectious bronchiectasis) should be considered as a priority. Clinicians can access the online PCDSOS scoring tool (details in the “Web-based tool availability” subsection of the “Methods” section) to calculate scores quickly without manual computation.

PCDSOS is recommended for use in (1) outpatient settings for adult bronchiectasis patients with red flags and (2) EMR screening—integrating PCDSOS into EMR systems could automatically flag high-risk patients (as shown in our preliminary study [22]). For centers without specialized PCD testing, PCDSOS can prioritize referrals to tertiary centers, optimizing resource allocation.

Our cohort included patients from PUMCH and The Second Xiangya Hospital, the two largest adult PCD diagnostic centers in China strategically located in northern and southern regions, respectively. This multicenter design enhances the generalizability of our findings within the Chinese adult PCD population. While the consistency of prevalent genotypes between our cohort (DNAH5: 22.5%; DNAH11: 15.5%) and European populations (DNAH5: 22%; DNAH11: 11%) hints at PCDSOS’s preliminary cross-population utility, regional heterogeneity in PCD phenotypes, especially organ laterality defects, must be acknowledged [4, 5]. Epidemiological data show laterality defects are far less common in certain regions: 28% in Turkey, 31% in the Netherlands, 37% in Germany, 39% in Spain among European cohorts, and only 40% in Japan and 14.6% in Korea in East Asian populations [4, 29, 30]. As organ laterality defects carry the highest weight (5 points) in PCDSOS, this regional variability may theoretically lower the tool’s sensitivity in populations with rare laterality anomalies. However, PCDSOS retains diagnostic value for non-laterality-defect PCD patients, given its 3-point recommended cutoff (well below the 5 points for laterality defects). Combinations of other predictors can easily reach or exceed this cutoff, ensuring the tool can identify high-risk patients even in regions with low laterality defect prevalence.

Inclusion criteria required at least one PCD-related diagnostic test, ensuring robust classification. Not all patients underwent confirmatory testing (e.g., TEM or genetic sequencing), in accordance with ATS/ERS guidelines recommending initial screening with nNO or HSVA, followed by confirmatory testing only if results are abnormal [6, 7]. In our study, most patients completed nNO, and it remains a reliable diagnostic tool given its high sensitivity (97.5%) and specificity (96.4%), and favorable cost-effectiveness [6]. Since isolated low nNO (excluding CF) is no longer recommended for diagnosis, we classified such cases as possible PCD [11]. Although enrollment spanned extended periods (12 and 8 years, respectively), ongoing follow-up allowed for diagnostic confirmation over time.

The PCDSOS incorporated six significant predictors and showed improved performance over PICADAR. Notably, our study confirmed superior performance of the modified PICADAR in adults (AUC 0.85 vs. 0.81, P < 0.001), supporting its key adaptations: (1) removal of gestational age, (2) consolidation of rhinitis/sinusitis as chronic rhinosinusitis, and (3) merging neonatal indicators into a single “neonatal respiratory distress” variable [14]. These modifications were validated, as excluded variables, prematurity, isolated rhinitis, and neonatal unit admission, did not significantly differ between groups. While the Clinical Index offers utility without specialized tests, its dependence on retrospective recall of infant symptoms and treatments limits its adult applicability and thus precluded its evaluation in our study [31].

Second, we incorporated a novel predictor, subfertility, which was not included in earlier predictive tools. Subfertility is frequently reported in PCD and is considered a strong predictor of diagnosis in adults [12]. Although the exact prevalence remains unclear, data indicate it is more common in men (up to 83%) than in women (up to 61%) [17]. Earlier tools excluded this variable because most participants were children. In this study, subfertility included decreased sperm motility, allowing for the inclusion of adult male patients. Individuals with unknown fertility status were assumed to have normal fertility. While this may have underestimated PCD probability in some cases, it simplified the model’s clinical use.

Third, the PCDSOS also incorporates a chest CT–based variable: atelectasis or lobectomy in the middle lobe or lingula. In a previous review of 244 Chinese PCD patients, 88 (37.8%) had atelectasis, consistent with our findings [5]. Robinson et al. reported that 75% of atelectasis among pediatric PCD cases involved the middle lobe [32]. Among adults, the lung resection rate reached 8.9%, with 48.8% involving the right middle lobe [33]. A similar variable was used in the PCD-CT score to predict PCD in adults with bronchiectasis, atelectasis, or prior middle/lower lobe resection [23]. These findings supported the inclusion of this CT-based predictor in PCDSOS.

A key strength of this study is that it is the first to develop and validate a clinical predictive tool for PCD tailored specifically to adults with bronchiectasis. Notably, we identified and incorporated subfertility and atelectasis, two predictors not used in previous tools, marking their first inclusion in such a model for this population.

This study has some limitations. First, the PCDSOS score is dependent on chest CT imaging for the definitive diagnosis of bronchiectasis, a resource largely restricted to tertiary medical centers. This significantly limits its applicability in remote, rural, or low-resource settings where such imaging is unavailable. In these contexts, clinical symptom- and medical history-based tools including NA-CDCF, PICADAR, and modified PICADAR remain the preferred PCD screening options. Second, its retrospective design required the exclusion of patients with incomplete medical records. However, demographics were comparable between excluded and included cases. Third, although our sample met the empirical 10-events-per-variable rule, it fell short of Riley et al.’s stricter requirement of 530 participants for six predictors at an event fraction of 0.3 [34]. Still, given the rarity of PCD, this represents the largest multicenter study of Chinese adults with PCD to date, achieved via collaboration between two centers and rigorous phenotyping. Fourth, we retained neonatal chest symptoms despite the potential for recall bias, as this simplified variable remains clinically valuable. Neonatal symptoms strongly suggest PCD, even in infants without chronic respiratory issues, highlighting the need for careful birth history documentation [6]. Lastly, while PCDSOS was developed in specialized centers, future prospective studies involving larger, community-based internal medicine cohorts would further validate its utility. Future studies should also validate PCDSOS in cohorts from regions with distinct PCD phenotypic profiles (e.g., East Asian populations with low laterality defect rates) to confirm its cross-population generalizability.

Conclusions

We proposed a well-validated PCD diagnostic predictive tool. Compared with previous PCD predictive tools, this tool demonstrated better performance in adult patients with bronchiectasis. We believe that the PCDSOS will help identify adult patients with bronchiectasis who require further PCD diagnostic testing and look forward to its validation in different clinical institutions.

Research in context

Knowledge before the study

We searched PubMed from database inception to May 31, 2025, for articles related to the development and validation of diagnostic tools for primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), using the search terms “primary ciliary dyskinesia” AND “diagnostic score”. The search identified 141 articles. These articles described several PCD diagnostic prediction scores, primarily including the PICADAR, North American Criteria Defined Clinical Features (NA-CDCF), and modified PICADAR, PCD-CT score, and Clinical Index. Among these, the PICADAR and NA-CDCF have been recommended by European Respiratory Society or American Thoracic Society guidelines. However, the diagnostic performance of these tools in adult populations remains suboptimal. First, these two scores were mainly developed and validated in pediatric populations and have not yet been validated in adult patients. Second, a number of predictors in these tools based on early life, such as gestational age, admission to a neonatal unit, and cough or nasal congestion beginning before 6 months of age, may be difficult to recall in adulthood. Finally, subfertility is a commonly reported feature of PCD and may be a powerful predictor of adult diagnosis. However, subfertility has not been included as a predictor in previous predictive tools.

Added value of the study

We conducted this study focusing on adult bronchiectasis patients with suspected PCD in two hospitals (Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) and The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (The Second Xiangya Hospital)). We firstly validate three existing PCD predictive tools (PICADAR, NA-CDCF, and modified PICADAR) in a derivation group of 287 patients from PUMCH. As expected, the results showed that the performances of these predictive tools were not as good as in the original publications. Thus, we developed the PCDSOS score, which demonstrated superior performance compared with previous predictive tools in adult patients with bronchiectasis and was well validated in an external validation group of 107 adult patients from The Second Xiangya Hospital. The PCDSOS included six predictors: pulmonary atelectasis or lobectomy in middle lobe or lingula (P, 2 points), neonatal chest symptoms (C, 2 points), organ laterality defects (D, 5 points), chronic sinusitis (S, 2 points), chronic otitis media or hearing loss from childhood (O, 1 point), and subfertility (S, 1 point). At a cutoff score of 3 points, the PCDSOS showed sensitivity of 0.86 and specificity of 0.76. The AUC were 0.90 and 0.92 for the derivation and validation groups, respectively.

Implications of all the available evidence

Early diagnosis and treatment of PCD are critical to reducing long-term pulmonary complications and preventing irreversible lung damage. Accurate diagnosis also enables essential genetic counseling and assessment of infertility risks. However, PCD diagnosis remains challenging due to limited access to specialized diagnostic services, low clinician awareness, and frequent under recognition of its characteristic clinical features. With an estimated global prevalence of 1 in 7554, PCD is significantly underdiagnosed worldwide. Bronchiectasis is an age-related finding in PCD, with nearly universal presence in adults. Notably, among adults with non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis, PCD accounts for 0.6% to 9% of cases, suggesting that bronchiectasis populations may harbor many undiagnosed patients with PCD. We hypothesize that systematic screening of bronchiectasis cohorts could uncover a substantial reservoir of undiagnosed PCD cases. The PCDSOS score guides clinicians in determining which adult bronchiectasis patients with suspected PCD should undergo confirmatory PCD diagnostic testing based on specific clinical manifestations. This approach facilitates prompt identification of PCD patients while preventing excessive utilization of costly or invasive diagnostic modalities such as genetic testing and electron microscopy, thereby optimizing the allocation of limited healthcare resources. We look forward to its validation in further studies conducted at different clinical institutions.
