Optimizing ovarian dose sparing for young cervical cancer patients after oophoropexy: a dosimetric and radiobiological comparison of three radiotherapy techniques (IMPT, TOMO, and VMAT)
Authors: Meiling Xu, Kailian Kang, Man Zhao, Suyan Bi, Jun Ma, Zhijian Chen, Miao Peng, Zhitao Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo systematically compare the dosimetric performance and model-based radiobiological estimates of intensity-modulated proton therapy (IMPT), helical tomotherapy (TOMO), and volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) for ovarian dose sparing in young cervical cancer patients after oophoropexy.MethodsTen premenopausal cervical cancer patients who received postoperative pelvic radiotherapy following bilateral ovarian transposition were retrospectively enrolled. Three treatment plans (IMPT, TOMO, and VMAT) were designed for each patient with a uniform prescription of 45 Gy in 25 fractions. IMPT plans incorporated robust optimization accounting for setup uncertainty (5 mm) and range uncertainty (3.5%). Dosimetric parameters evaluated included target coverage (), homogeneity index (HI), ovarian mean dose () and maximum dose (), and dose-volume metrics for organs at risk (OARs: rectum, bladder, small intestine, sigmoid, femoral heads). Radiobiological analyses included equivalent uniform dose (EUD), normal tissue complication probability (NTCP), and excess absolute risk (EAR) estimation for secondary malignancies using Schneider’s mechanistic model.ResultsAll three techniques achieved clinically acceptable target coverage. IMPT showed superior dose homogeneity and the most favorable planned ovarian dose-sparing profile, reducing ovarian by approximately 95% compared with photon-based techniques (0.11–0.35 Gy vs. 4.44–5.24 Gy, p = 0.002) and substantially lowering low-dose exposure ( cc vs. 2.86–7.68 cc, p < 0.05). Both IMPT and TOMO achieved significantly lower to the rectum, bladder, and small intestine than VMAT (p < 0.05). IMPT also reduced small intestine , mean bladder dose, and integral dose to normal tissue compared with both photon techniques (p ≤ 0.039). Model-based NTCP analysis showed that IMPT yielded lower estimated complication probabilities for the small intestine, rectum, and bladder compared with TOMO and VMAT (p ≤ 0.006), while femoral head estimates were comparable between IMPT and VMAT and lower than TOMO. EAR modeling suggested organ-specific and endpoint-dependent differences across pelvic OARs. VMAT had the shortest beam-on time (2.20 ± 0.05 min vs. TOMO 8.18 ± 0.99 min, p < 0.001).ConclusionIMPT achieved the lowest ovarian radiation exposure and the most favorable overall dosimetric profile for ovarian dose sparing after ovarian transposition. TOMO provided competitive sparing for several high-dose pelvic OAR endpoints, whereas VMAT offered the shortest photon beam-on time. Overall, IMPT appears to be the most favorable dosimetric option for ovarian dose sparing when available, while VMAT and TOMO remain reasonable alternatives depending on clinical priorities and resource availability. These findings should be interpreted as dosimetric and model-based advantages rather than direct evidence of improved ovarian endocrine function or reduced clinical toxicity.Keywords: Ovarian transposition, Ovarian dose sparing, Intensity-modulated proton therapy, Helical tomotherapy, NTCP, Secondary cancer risk

Introduction

Cervical cancer ranks among the most prevalent gynecological malignancies worldwide, posing a significant health burden to women globally [1]. Adjuvant radiotherapy plays a critical role in the comprehensive management of young cervical cancer patients, particularly those who have undergone radical hysterectomy with ovarian transposition [2, 3]. While advanced radiotherapy techniques such as helical tomotherapy (TOMO), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and intensity-modulated proton therapy (IMPT) have demonstrated superior organ-at-risk (OAR) sparing over conventional methods [4], the optimal technique for ovarian dose sparing in this clinical setting remains undefined.

The preservation of ovarian function during pelvic radiotherapy represents a paramount clinical consideration for premenopausal patients. Iatrogenic premature menopause induced by ovarian failure has been associated with substantial long-term health risks, including an approximately 2.4-fold increase in dementia risk and a 68% increase in depression risk, with the magnitude of harm increasing at younger ages of exposure. Critically, even standardized estrogen therapy cannot fully compensate for the endocrine and non-reproductive functions of ovaries [5]. Ovarian function preservation is therefore indispensable for endocrine homeostasis and long-term health in this population [6, 7]. Importantly, ovarian preservation is oncologically safe in early-stage cervical cancer. Studies have confirmed that ovarian conservation does not compromise survival in stage IB patients without gross extrauterine spread [8], underpinned by the low incidence of ovarian metastasis ranging from 2 to 4.95% in cervical adenocarcinoma [9, 10]. Although ovarian transposition (OT) is a key protective procedure, its efficacy is not absolute. Evidence shows that even with OT, ovarian function was preserved in only 61.4% of patients after pelvic radiotherapy, underscoring that the radiation dose delivered remains a major determinant of functional outcomes [11]. Moreover, long-term survival benefits extend beyond endocrine health, with Matsuo’s cohort study revealing a 6.7% absolute increase in 20-year overall survival with ovarian conservation [12]. Therefore, minimizing radiation dose to the transposed ovaries is an important treatment-planning objective.

Although previous investigations have addressed ovarian protection during pelvic radiotherapy, notable limitations persist. Marnitz et al. [4] conducted a dosimetric comparison of TOMO, VMAT, intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), and IMPT in patients with locally advanced cervical cancer, confirming IMPT advantages in sparing OARs such as the small intestine, bladder, and rectum. However, this study did not specifically quantify ovarian dose sparing in the context of post-transposition ovaries. Du et al. [13] highlighted the insufficiency of conventional IMRT dose constraints for functional ovarian protection, proposing optimized constraints () based on receiver operating characteristic analysis, yet the differential capacities of advanced techniques (TOMO, VMAT, or IMPT) in achieving these objectives remain unexplored. Vyfhuis et al. [14] specifically compared VMAT and IMPT in whole pelvic radiotherapy with ovarian preservation, demonstrating IMPT superiority in meeting ovarian dose goals; however, this investigation was limited by a small sample size (n = 10) and exclusive focus on unilateral ovarian protection. Huang et al. [15] compared ovarian-sparing IMRT with conventional IMRT in early-stage cervical cancer patients after ovarian transposition but did not include TOMO or IMPT for comparative analysis. While studies by Husseinzadeh [7] and Gubbala [3] have validated the clinical feasibility of ovarian transposition combined with ovarian dose limitation, their primary focus did not center on the dosimetric advantages of specific modern radiotherapy technologies. Currently, comprehensive head-to-head dosimetric studies systematically comparing the ovarian dose-sparing capabilities of IMPT, TOMO, and VMAT in post-ovarian transposition patients are lacking. Additionally, the long-term carcinogenic risk of scattered/low-dose radiation to pelvic organs represents an equally critical concern for young survivors, yet this dimension is rarely incorporated into comparative analyses.

To address these gaps, this study performed a comprehensive planning-based dosimetric comparison with model-based radiobiological estimates of IMPT, TOMO, and VMAT for young cervical cancer patients after ovarian transposition. The primary objective was to quantify and compare the planned ovarian dose-sparing capabilities of these three techniques under the same prescription dose and contouring conditions. In addition to conventional dose–volume parameters, equivalent uniform dose (EUD), normal tissue complication probability (NTCP), and Schneider model-based excess absolute risk (EAR) estimates were incorporated to provide complementary radiobiological information [16]. By jointly assessing target coverage, dose homogeneity, ovarian and pelvic organ-at-risk (OAR) sparing, NTCP estimates, EAR estimates, and photon treatment efficiency, this study provides a multidimensional dosimetric and model-based framework to inform radiotherapy technique selection in this specific clinical scenario.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

This retrospective study included 10 premenopausal gynecological malignancy patients (details are shown in Table 1) who had undergone external beam radiotherapy at our hospital. While ovarian preservation is typically reserved for early-stage cervical cancer, selected advanced-stage patients (e.g., FIGO IIB–IIIC) with confirmed absence of ovarian involvement and strong desire to minimize ovarian radiation exposure were also included. This reflects real-world clinical scenarios where young patients prioritize quality-of-life preservation despite advanced disease. All patients underwent bilateral ovarian transposition prior to radiotherapy with ovaries fixed at vertebral levels ranging from L4 to S1. Specifically, 65% were positioned at L5, and radiopaque markers were placed intraoperatively in 9 out of 10 cases for identification during radiotherapy positioning. The distance from the ovarian center to the body midline ranges from 7.3 to 10.1 cm (mean: 8.45 cm). The prescription dose was 45 Gy/25 fractions for PTV_LR. Five patients were originally treated with TOMO, and five with VMAT.

CT simulation and contouring

All patients underwent CT simulation using a GE CT simulator (Discovery 590 RT, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA). The scanning range extended from the T10 vertebra to 5 cm below the ischial tuberosity. Images were reconstructed with a slice thickness of 5 mm. Three patients were scanned in the supine position, and seven in the prone position, with appropriate immobilization devices (such as vacuum pads and thermoplastic masks) used to ensure reproducibility of positioning.

The delineation of CTV_LR encompasses the superior 3–4 cm of the vaginal cuff along with the adjacent paravaginal tissues and the pelvic lymph node regions (including the common iliac, internal iliac, external iliac, obturator nodal groups, and the presacral nodal region approximately at S1–S3 levels), while accounting for variations in bladder and rectal filling, as specified in the updated 2020 NRG oncology and RTOG consensus guidelines [17] for target delineation in postoperative radiotherapy for cervical and endometrial cancer. PTV_LR is a 5-mm three-dimensional expansion of CTV_LR. The ovaries are contoured based on the markers after ovarian transposition and magnetic resonance imaging. Ovarian contours were delineated by a senior attending physician and subsequently reviewed and confirmed by another senior chief physician; however, inter-observer variability was not formally assessed. Contouring of OARs, including the rectum, small intestine, bladder, sigmoid, femoral heads, bone pelvis, and spinal cord, was performed using the automatic contouring software AccuContourTM (Manteia Medical Technologies Co. Ltd, Xiamen, China).

Treatment planning

Utilizing identical CT datasets and structure sets, three treatment plans were developed for each patient. To facilitate comparison, all plans were prescribed the same nominal dose of 45 Gy in 25 fractions. For photon plans, the prescription dose was normalized such that 100% of the prescription dose () covered at least 95% of the PTV_LR volume. For IMPT plans, the nominal dose distribution was required to deliver to at least 99% of the CTV_LR volume. All treatment plans were generated by a single experienced physicist. Formal planner blinding was not feasible because the three techniques required different planning systems and modality-specific optimization workflows. Dose constraints were defined based on EMBRACE studies [18], previous research [19], and the clinicians’ experience at our institution, as detailed in Table 2. For transposed ovaries, the EMBRACE II study [18] recommends a hard constraint of Gy and a soft constraint of Gy. In our clinical practice, an intermediate soft constraint of Gy was adopted as the planning goal.

Dose objectives and constraints for treatment planning

IMPT plans were generated using the RayStation planning system (Version 2024B, RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) on an RSL_PBS_CYCL machine (for evaluation purposes only), employing a Monte Carlo dose calculation algorithm with multi-field optimization (MFO). Robust optimization incorporated setup uncertainty (5 mm) and range uncertainty (3.5%), evaluating 21 scenarios. For supine patients, beam angles were set at 180, 140, and 220, while for prone patients, angles were 40, 0, and 320, to spare the ovaries. The nominal plan was required to achieve 100% dose coverage (D) to 99% of the CTV_LR volume. The robustness evaluation required that the second worst-case scenario must attain 99% prescription dose coverage (D) to 99% of the CTVLR volume. TOMO plans were generated using the Accuray Planning Station (TomoH™, Version 2.1.9, Accuray, Sunnyvale, CA) on a TomoH™ system, with jaw width of 2.5 cm, pitch of 0.287, and modulation factor of 2.3. VMAT plans were generated using Pinnacle3 version 16.2.1 (Philips Medical Systems, Fitchburg, WI) on a Varian TrueBeam linac. Dual full arcs (181–179) with 6 MV Flattening-Filter-Free (FFF) photons were employed.

Data processing

All analyses were performed using Python 3.13.3 (SciPy 1.17.1). Given the intra-patient paired design (each patient had three plans: IMPT, TOMO, and VMAT), the Friedman test was first used to detect overall differences among the three techniques for each parameter. When the Friedman test was significant (p < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm correction for multiple comparisons. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All reported p-values are corrected and rounded to three decimal places.

For the remaining OARs, both IMPT and TOMO demonstrated significantly superior sparing of the rectum, bladder, and small intestine at high-dose levels () compared to VMAT (p ≤ 0.012). Distinct advantages among the techniques emerged in low-to-intermediate dose regions: IMPT significantly reduced the small intestine volume by approximately 30% (176.13 ± 72.89 cc) relative to photon-based techniques (VMAT: 245.33 ± 79.68 cc; TOMO: 253.90 ± 94.91 cc; p łe 0.039), while VMAT achieved the lowest rectal (64.63 ± 11.58% vs. 90.43 ± 5.59% for IMPT and 83.41 ± 12.48% for TOMO; p = 0.002). Furthermore, IMPT resulted in a 35% reduction in mean bladder dose (22.33 ± 4.27 Gy) compared to VMAT and TOMO (32.33 ± 1.11 Gy to 35.24 ± 2.79 Gy; p=0.002). IMPT also substantially lowered the mean dose to the femoral heads (left: 4.78 ± 1.33 Gy; right: 5.16 ± 1.71 Gy) compared to VMAT (left: 11.26 ± 1.22 Gy; right: 10.73 ± 1.19 Gy) and TOMO (left: 16.18±2.14 Gy; right: 16.33±2.55 Gy); all three pairwise comparisons yielded p = 0.002. IMPT also reduced the integral dose to normal tissue by 54% vs. TOMO (p łe 0.002) and 47% vs. VMAT (p łe 0.002).

Radiobiological evaluations

Table 5 summarizes the radiobiological comparison among IMPT, TOMO, and VMAT. For the small intestine and bladder, IMPT yielded significantly lower NTCP than both TOMO and VMAT (all p = 0.002). For the rectum, IMPT also showed significantly lower NTCP than TOMO (p = 0.002) and VMAT (p = 0.006). For the femoral heads, NTCP was comparable between IMPT and VMAT (left: p = 0.846; right: p = 0.695), while both were significantly lower than TOMO (p = 0.002–0.027). EUD results were consistent with the NTCP findings across all structures, as shown in Fig. 4(a)–(e). EAR modeling suggested technique- and organ-specific differences: IMPT yielded the lowest bladder EAR (0.10 ± 0.05), significantly lower than TOMO (0.15 ± 0.08, p = 0.002) and VMAT (0.14 ± 0.07, p = 0.002), whereas VMAT yielded the lowest rectal EAR (9.13 ± 1.14 vs. IMPT 10.42 ± 1.02, p = 0.004; vs. TOMO 10.57 ± 1.41, p = 0.006).

Beam delivery time

Since our RayStation treatment planning system does not support the estimation of beam delivery time for proton therapy, here we focus on comparing the beam delivery time between TOMO and VMAT plans. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive dosimetric and radiobiological comparison of IMPT, TOMO, and VMAT for ovarian dose sparing in young cervical cancer patients after ovarian transposition. The principal finding is that IMPT achieved the most favorable ovarian sparing profile among the three techniques, with substantially lower ovarian mean dose, maximum dose, and low-dose exposure than either photon-based approach. In addition, IMPT was associated with lower integral dose to normal tissues and more favorable NTCP and bladder EAR estimates for several pelvic organs. By contrast, VMAT offered the shortest photon beam-on time, whereas TOMO provided competitive sparing for some high-dose OAR endpoints but was less effective in limiting low-dose exposure to the transposed ovaries. These findings suggest that, within the context of this planning study, IMPT offers the most advantageous dosimetric balance between ovarian dose reduction and overall pelvic normal-tissue sparing when available.

The substantial ovarian dose reduction observed with IMPT is physically plausible and consistent with the dosimetric characteristics of proton therapy. The sharp distal fall-off of the Bragg peak allows better avoidance of structures located beyond the target, which is particularly relevant for transposed ovaries situated cranially and laterally outside the pelvic target volume. In the present study, this translated into approximately 95% lower ovarian mean dose with IMPT than with TOMO or VMAT, together with significantly lower V. From a planning perspective, these findings indicate that proton therapy may be especially advantageous when minimizing ovarian radiation exposure is a major priority after oophoropexy. At the same time, the present data remain dosimetric and model-based; they support improved ovarian sparing rather than direct proof of preserved ovarian endocrine function.

Among photon techniques, VMAT exhibited superior treatment efficiency, with a significantly shorter irradiation time than TOMO (2.20 vs. 8.18 minutes), consistent with the characteristics of VMAT’s rapid rotational irradiation. However, the mean ovarian dose observed with VMAT approaches or exceeds the estimated follicular LD (∼4 Gy) proposed by Wallace et al. [27] and the suggested clinical threshold of < 3 Gy for preserving function observed by Husseinzadeh [7]. Consequently, selecting photon techniques necessitates a careful balance between efficiency and dosimetric risks, particularly the risk of ovarian functional impairment. VMAT can be applied in situations where treatment efficiency is a high priority, provided that strict ovarian dose constraints are applied and image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) is used to facilitate ovarian localization and reduce discrepancies between planned and delivered dose [28–30]. Compared with VMAT, TOMO’s spiral irradiation mode resulted in a 9–18% increase in the low-dose bath volume (V) to the ovaries, limiting its application for ovarian protection. Nonetheless, TOMO is comparable to IMPT in sparing high-dose regions of OARs (D) such as the rectum, bladder, small intestine, and sigmoid colon, and is superior to VMAT in this regard. TOMO may remain a valuable option for cases with highly complex target shapes. These modality-specific trade-offs underscore that technique selection should be individualized rather than based on a single endpoint.

An additional strength of the present study is the incorporation of radiobiological modeling, including EUD, NTCP, and Schneider EAR estimation, which extends the evaluation beyond conventional DVH metrics. The NTCP results were broadly consistent with the dosimetric findings and supported the advantage of IMPT for sparing the bladder, rectum, and small intestine. The EAR findings should be interpreted as complementary model-based estimates rather than a direct visual extension of the DVH curves. Although Fig. 2 shows the average DVH curves across the 10-patient cohort, the EAR values in Table 5 were calculated separately from each patient’s complete DVH and then summarized across the cohort. Therefore, the numerical ranking of EAR may not always exactly match the visual ordering of the averaged DVH curves. For example, the small-intestine EAR values for TOMO and VMAT were numerically close and not significantly different (p = 0.557), consistent with the broadly comparable small-intestine dose exposure observed in Fig. 2 and Table 4. Accordingly, small numerical differences in EAR ranking should not be overinterpreted as definitive clinical risk differences.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective planning study with only 10 patients, which limits statistical power and generalizability. Although the intra-patient design reduces between-patient variability and strengthens pairwise comparisons across techniques, the small cohort size requires cautious interpretation of the observed differences. Second, a small number of selected advanced-stage patients were included. While this reflects real-world practice in highly selected young patients with strong motivation for ovarian conservation and no evidence of ovarian involvement, the applicability of these findings beyond early-stage disease remains uncertain. Because only a few advanced-stage cases were available, a formal stage-stratified analysis was underpowered and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. Third, no direct clinical ovarian outcome data, such as menstrual status, hormonal assays, or long-term endocrine function, were available. The present study therefore evaluates ovarian dose sparing rather than actual ovarian function preservation. Furthermore, IMPT delivery time could not be assessed because the planning platform used in this study did not provide a validated estimate of proton beam delivery time, making the efficiency comparison incomplete. Finally, the planned ovarian dose may not fully represent the delivered dose because transposed ovaries may be affected by intra- and inter-fractional motion, residual setup uncertainty, bowel peristalsis, and changes in bladder or rectal filling. These effects may cause discrepancies between planned and delivered ovarian doses, particularly in steep dose-gradient regions and potentially more so for IMPT because of its sharp distal dose fall-off. Although robust optimization was applied in the present IMPT plans, future studies incorporating image-guided ovarian verification, repeated imaging, dose accumulation, or adaptive assessment are needed to better quantify delivered ovarian dose [28, 30].

Despite these limitations, the present study provides clinically relevant comparative information for a relatively uncommon but important treatment scenario. In young patients undergoing ovarian transposition, the choice of radiotherapy technique should ideally consider not only target coverage and conventional OAR constraints, but also ovarian dose exposure, integral dose, and the potential long-term implications of low-dose irradiation. Taken together, these findings support IMPT as the most favorable dosimetric option for ovarian dose sparing in this planning context, while VMAT and TOMO remain reasonable alternatives in selected settings. These findings warrant further validation in larger series incorporating both dosimetric and clinical endocrine outcomes.

Conclusion

IMPT achieved the lowest planned ovarian radiation exposure and the most favorable overall dosimetric profile for ovarian dose sparing after ovarian transposition. TOMO provided competitive sparing for several high-dose pelvic OAR endpoints, whereas VMAT offered the shortest photon beam-on time. Overall, IMPT appears to be the most favorable dosimetric option for ovarian dose sparing when available, while VMAT and TOMO remain reasonable alternatives depending on clinical priorities, treatment efficiency, and resource availability. Because this was a retrospective planning study, these findings should be interpreted as dosimetric and model-based results rather than direct evidence of improved ovarian endocrine function, reduced clinical toxicity, or reduced secondary cancer incidence.
