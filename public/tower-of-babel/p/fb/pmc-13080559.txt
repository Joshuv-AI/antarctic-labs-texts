History of Presentation

An 18-year-old woman was admitted after experiencing an episode of massive hemoptysis. On admission, vital signs showed heart rate of 70 beats/min, respiratory rate of 16 breaths/min, temperature of 36 °C, blood pressure of 119/30 mm Hg, and peripheral oxygen saturation of 93% on room air.

The physical examination revealed multiple carious lesions in the oral cavity, a mesodiastolic heart murmur in the aortic area, a holosystolic murmur in the mitral area, and a continuous murmur in the left infraclavicular region. Right-sided crackles were present on lung auscultation, along with purpuric lesions on the plantar surfaces of both feet. Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm and left ventricular hypertrophy (Figure 1A), while chest radiography showed localized right-lung consolidations with hilar enlargement (Figure 1B). Blood tests were notable for anemia (hemoglobin 7.5 g/dL; reference 12.2-16.2 g/dL), a normal leukocyte count, elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (4,797 pg/mL; reference 5-178 pg/mL), and markedly increased C-reactive protein (162 mg/dL; reference <5 mg/dL).

Past Medical History

The medical history included poor dental hygiene and a previous hospitalization 1 month earlier due to fever, anemia, and dyspnea, during which a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) was detected by echocardiography. Clinical improvement was achieved with a levofloxacin-based regimen and blood transfusions, and at that time, infective endocarditis (IE) or other infectious foci were ruled out.

Differential Diagnosis

Considering the patient's young age, medical history, chest radiographic findings, massive hemoptysis, and a strong clinical suspicion of IE, the differential diagnosis included septic pulmonary embolism with cavitary nodules resulting from recurrent embolic events, infection-related pulmonary artery aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm, pulmonary infarction secondary to embolic disease, pulmonary vasculitis (including granulomatosis with polyangiitis), and infectious processes with vascular involvement such as tuberculosis (Rasmussen aneurysm), aspergillosis, or actinomycosis. Consequently, diagnostic imaging was central to the differential diagnostic process, enabling accurate distinction among vasculitic, vascular, and primary infectious pulmonary entities.

Investigations

Echocardiographic evaluation revealed a filamentous image on the posterior leaflet associated with moderate mitral regurgitation, severe aortic regurgitation, and a PDA with a left-to-right shunt (Figures 1C to 1F, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3).

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) revealed segmental pulmonary thromboembolism with pulmonary infarctions in the upper lobes, as well as a mycotic pulmonary artery aneurysm (MPAA) arising from a lateral basal subsegmental branch of the right lower lobe with apparent posteroinferior rupture and a saccular formation measuring 50 × 49 × 50 mm (Figure 2).

Management

Given fulfillment of the criteria for a definitive diagnosis of IE, blood cultures were obtained, and treatment with levofloxacin 500 mg/d and clindamycin 500 mg intravenously every 8 hours was initiated immediately afterward. Considering the size of the MPA aneurysm, the severe aortic valve disease associated with heart failure, the moderate mitral valve disease, and the underlying congenital heart disease, a combined, staged approach was preferred, beginning with endovascular embolization, followed by valvular surgery and PDA closure, after prior extraction of all carious teeth.

Blood cultures isolated G. adiacens, which was sensitive to ceftriaxone, penicillin, and vancomycin. Accordingly, antimicrobial therapy was adjusted to ceftriaxone 2 g every 24 hours and amikacin 500 mg every 24 hours, resulting in clinical improvement without further episodes of hemoptysis or fever.

Endovascular embolization was performed using an 8-mm Amplatzer Vascular Plug II (Abbott Structural Heart, St Paul) deployed in the lateral basal segmental artery, achieving complete occlusion with no residual defects (Figure 5, Videos 9 and 10). Subsequent surgical exploration revealed vegetations on the aortic valve, vegetations with leaflet perforations on the mitral valve, and a mycotic aneurysm involving the descending aortic wall. The patient subsequently underwent mitral and aortic valve replacement with 31-mm and 23-mm St Jude mechanical prostheses (Abbott, St Paul), respectively, followed by resection of the descending aortic aneurysm along with division and suture closure of the PDA.

Outcome and Follow-Up

During hospitalization, the patient remained clinically and hemodynamically stable. Intravenous therapy was continued with ceftriaxone for 32 days and amikacin for 12 days, leading to normalization of laboratory parameters and a favorable clinical course. On hospital day 40, the patient was discharged asymptomatic with oral amoxicillin–clavulanate 875/125 mg every 12 hours for 3 months and weekly acenocoumarol. At the 7-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable, and echocardiography confirmed prosthetic valve integrity without recurrence.

Discussion

Granulicatella species are anaerobic Gram-positive nutritional variants of streptococci that require pyridoxal or cysteine for growth. In most cases, these organisms are found in the oral cavity and other mucosal surfaces.1, 2, 3 The genus Granulicatella comprises 3 species, G. adiacens, Granulicatella elegans, and Granulicatella balaenopterae, of which only G. adiacens and G. elegans are recognized as uncommon causes of IE.1 G. adiacens account for an estimated 5% to 6% of IE cases globally and may cause valvular damage, heart failure, embolization, or extension of vegetations into the atrium.2

Mycotic aneurysms are infection-related arterial dilations most commonly involving the aorta, visceral arteries, or cerebral circulation and frequently associated with hematogenous dissemination from septic emboli. In contrast, pulmonary artery involvement remained exceedingly rare with only limited cases reported.4 No cases of G. adiacens IE complicated by MPAA have been reported to date in the literature.

For many years, Granulicatella species were underreported in IE, particularly in culture-negative cases, although detection has improved through the introduction of advanced microbiological techniques.5 More recently, the Duke 2023 International Society of Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases criteria have listed Granulicatella species as typical IE pathogens.6,7 In patients with predisposing factors, these organisms can penetrate local barriers and cause severe disease. The thin and fragile cell walls, nutritional dependence, slow replication, and satellite growth around Staphylococcus aureus may contribute to negative or inconsistent culture results.3

Clinical presentation is often insidious, contributing to delays in diagnosis. Most reported cases involve the aortic and mitral valves, and those with prosthetic or structurally abnormal valves appear particularly susceptible. Symptoms are often limited to fever, malaise, or fatigue, and classical manifestations may emerge only in the later stages.3

Slow growth and fastidious behavior continue to complicate detection of G. adiacens; however, obtaining multiple blood cultures in supplemented media (eg, with pyridoxal) increases diagnostic sensitivity, and tests such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay or polymerase chain reaction can support rapid identification.2

The development of MPAAs can be caused by intraluminal septic thromboembolism extending into the vessel wall, by direct extension from adjacent pulmonary infection, or by hematogenous seeding of the vasa vasorum by infectious microemboli, all of which contribute to progressive arterial weakness and dilatation.4 The presence of bacteria or fungi in the arterial wall can result in focal dilation of pulmonary arterial branches. Vegetation fragments may embolize into the pulmonary circulation or systemic circulation.8,9 Clinical manifestations are rare unless rupture occurs, with massive hemoptysis being the most common symptom.4

Around half of the cases documented with MPAAs are associated with congenital heart disease. A left-to-right shunt may increase the shear stress of the pulmonary artery and lead to dilation, while congenital abnormalities may increase the risk of bacteremia, IE, and septic embolism.8

In our patient, despite prior antibiotic therapy, an untreated dental infectious focus persisted and contributed to the ongoing bacteremia, whereas the left-to-right shunt from the PDA likely promoted its dissemination to the pulmonary arterial branches, resulting in the development of MPAA.

In addition to detecting MPAAs at any site within the vascular tree, CT can also detect ischemic or hemorrhagic neurological lesions. CT scan provide information on the number, location, and size of MPAAS, as well as monitor interval changes, allowing for a precise diagnosis and preventing life-threatening complications such as rupture.8,10 Imaging findings reveal peripheral nodules with cavitation and a feeding-vessel sign.8,9 Three-dimensional reconstruction enables delineation of the arterial configuration and feeding artery.8 Whole-body imaging, including magnetic resonance imaging, can detect peripheral lesions and provide additional minor criteria for diagnosing IE.10

Clinical manifestations and disease duration provide critical insight into the etiologic diagnosis of septic pulmonary embolism, facilitating appropriate management and ensuring favorable clinical outcomes. A rapidly progressive septic pulmonary embolism with cavitary lesions developing within 12 weeks is typically associated with an infectious etiology, whereas a more chronic or indolent evolution beyond 12 weeks is more suggestive of malignancy or vasculitis.9

G. adiacens has also been associated with systemic embolic events requiring prolonged antibiotic treatment, embolectomy, or valve replacement.3 Consequently, a multimodal imaging approach is required for the diagnosis of IE, exclusion of associated complications, and planning of treatment.8 Urgent surgical intervention is recommended for uncontrolled infections, such as abscess, pseudoaneurysm, fistula, enlarging vegetations, or new atrioventricular block.10

Antimicrobial therapy for G. adiacens endocarditis is guided by susceptibility testing. Reported regimens typically include ampicillin, penicillin, or ceftriaxone combined with an aminoglycoside, although gentamicin resistance may necessitate adjustments. Vancomycin is used when beta-lactam resistance is suspected. Treatment usually lasts 4 to 6 weeks and may be prolonged in cases of persistent bacteremia or embolic complications.2,3

Valve preservation should be considered only when durable repair and complete eradication of infected tissue are achievable; in children, valve repair remains an important option given the limited options for valve replacement. Aortic valve replacement is usually required in acute IE, whereas aortic valve repair may be considered for isolated aortic regurgitation after a healed infection. Mitral valve repair may be feasible in certain cases with limited leaflet involvement and preserved chordae, typically in subacute or healed mitral endocarditis, although evidence is limited to suggest that the repair will be durable in more complex cases.10

In the case of MPAAs, the management is dependent on clinical stability and rupture risk. Antibiotic therapy remains crucial, although there are no standardized guidelines. In clinically stable patients without hemoptysis, conservative treatment with serial CT follow-up may be considered to monitor changes in the aneurysm size or status despite the absence of a universally accepted follow-up interval. More invasive approaches, including lobectomy, pneumonectomy, or surgical ligation, are reported when the rupture risk is high. Endovascular therapy provides a less-invasive option for saccular aneurysms, while fusiform aneurysms may require surgical intervention depending on the anatomy.8

This case illustrated a septic pulmonary embolism secondary to an infectious etiology, as evidenced by the acute clinical presentation and identification of a pulmonary infectious focus. Furthermore, multimodal cardiac imaging enabled detection of the source of infection and associated complications, thereby facilitating timely treatment and resulting in a favorable clinical outcome.

Conclusions

G. adiacens can produce aggressive IE with severe valvular injury and exceptionally rare complications such as MPAAs. Early multimodal imaging and coordinated medical–surgical treatment allowed timely diagnosis and successful recovery.
