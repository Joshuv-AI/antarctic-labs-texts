Copy number variant scores are associated with cerebrovascular pathology in aging
Authors: Jun Ni Du, Earvin S Tio, David A Bennett, Julie A Schneider, Cigdem Sevim Bayrak, Aaron Alexander-Bloch, Bin Zhang, Daniel Felsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLate-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) is the most common cause of dementia in older adults, with specific genomic copy number variants (CNVs) implicated in its pathology. However, the aggregate burden of genome-wide CNVs in dementia and age-related neuropathologies is uncharacterized. This study investigated the association between genome-wide CNV scores (CNV-S) and dementia, as well as LOAD-related neuropathologies, in 1011 elderly participants (mean age 88.06) from two ongoing US-based longitudinal clinical-pathological cohort studies who were initially dementia-free and consented to brain donation upon death. Participants exhibited varying cognitive statuses at death (429 dementia, 258 mild cognitive impairment, 324 cognitively normal). We evaluated effects of (1) eight individual-level CNV-S based on gene loss intolerance and dosage sensitivity; (2) a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based LOAD polygenic score (PGS-LOAD) calculated using Bayesian continuous shrinkage; and (3) covariates (age, sex, and education). Outcomes included cognitive scores across 19 tests, clinical diagnoses of Alzheimer’s disease or mild cognitive impairment, and four LOAD-related neuropathologies assessed postmortem. Analyses identified 4867 CNVs (3918 deletions, 949 duplications) mapped to 3211 genes. Higher deletion CNV-S were significantly associated with increased cerebrovascular pathologies (pLI: β = 0.14, 95% CI [0.08, 0.21]; LOEUF: β = 0.14, 95% CI [0.08, 0.20]; pHI: β = 0.15, 95% CI [0.08, 0.21]; binarized pHI: β = 0.14, 95% CI [0.08, 0.21]). Models predicting cerebral atherosclerosis that included deletion CNV-S significantly outperformed models based on only PGS-LOAD (R2 increase: 0.02). These findings suggest that genome-wide CNV burden, particularly deletions in dosage-sensitive genes, contributes to cerebrovascular pathology in aging. CNV-S may augment existing LOAD genetic risk models by capturing vascular pathways distinct from traditional SNP-based risk.Subject terms: Genetics, Neuroscience

Introduction

Late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) is the most common cause of dementia in the elderly and is clinically characterized by progressive decline in cognition, particularly in domains of memory [1]. Neuropathologically, LOAD is defined by the accumulation of amyloid-β protein aggregates, which form plaques that damage synapses [2], along with neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein [3]. Beyond these core pathologies, factors like chronic neuroinflammation and cerebrovascular conditions including arteriolosclerosis, atherosclerosis, and cerebral infarction can also contribute to the neuropathological and clinical manifestations of LOAD [1]. These neuropathologies have complex interactions and temporal relationships with known genetic and environmental contributors.

Copy number variations (CNVs), including genomic insertions, deletions, and duplications, have been implicated in LOAD through several recurrent pathogenic loci such as the 22q11.2 deletion [4], 6p21.3 deletion [5], and 15q11.2 duplication [5]. However, most clinically relevant pathogenic CNVs are ultra-rare, with frequencies too low for generalization [6], and studies testing their associations have mostly considered coarse clinical outcomes [7] (e.g. probable diagnosis of LOAD present or absent) rather than dimensional measures of symptomatology or specific neuropathologies. In addition, many CNVs exhibit variable penetrance and expressivity [8], and the impacted genes can have a wide range of phenotypic effects [9]. Further contributing to the challenges of studying CNVs at scale, traditional approaches, such as comparative genomic hybridization, quantitative PCR, or single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays are limited by their target region sizes, genomic coverage, and overall accuracy [10]. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) offers a way to overcome these limitations and facilitates more precise and efficient characterization of CNVs for genome-wide associations in large human samples [10].

Recent studies [11, 12] have examined the cumulative impact of common CNVs on clinically important phenotypes by analyzing the total burden of individual-level genome-wide CNVs and their phenotypic profiles. This method is conceptually similar to the generation of polygenic scores (PGS) in that both aggregate genome-wide genetic variation into a single numeric score per individual that reflects cumulative genetic burden and can be used for phenotypic prediction. The key distinction lies in the source and nature of their variant weights: PGSs are typically computed as linear combinations of common single nucleotide variants weighted by their estimated effects on a specific trait, derived from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [13], whereas CNV scores weight genes within each CNV by population-level metrics of intolerance to copy-number change (e.g., loss intolerance, dosage sensitivity) rather than trait-specific associations. The PGS approach has been fruitful for exploring pleiotropy on a large scale and developing predictive models, though its predictive value has not yet reached clinical utility owing in part to the limited scope of genetic variation and lack of mechanism-specific information captured [14]. By contrast, CNV scores aggregate estimated sensitivities to increases or decreases in copy number for genes that are encompassed within each insertion or duplication locus, respectively. Two studies [11, 12] have derived genome-wide aggregated CNV scores (CNV-S) to reflect the total intolerance or dosage sensitivity across all CNVs in an individual and evaluated their association with autism spectrum disorder, early-onset psychosis, and cognition in youth. However, despite the known role of structural genetic variation in aging and cognitive decline [15], these scores have yet to be tested in the context of risk for LOAD.

Here we calculated CNV-S based on WGS data from a large, deeply phenotyped late-life cohort using both existing pipelines and novel approaches, incorporating weights to account for CNV multiples and adding scoring criteria for both intolerance to loss-of-function mutations and dosage sensitivity. We aimed to (1) evaluate the associations between CNV-S, cognitive decline, and LOAD-related neuropathologies, and (2) test whether CNV-S improved predictive performance over an existing state-of-the-art PGS for LOAD. Given differing etiologies and prevalence of cerebrovascular disease in males and females [16], we also performed analyses stratified by biological sex.

Subjects and methods

Study participants

We analyzed data for 1011 participants from the Religious Orders Study/Memory and Aging Project (ROS/MAP), which are two ongoing longitudinal clinical-pathological cohort studies of older adults in the United States with complete antemortem clinical, postmortem neuropathological, and whole-genome sequencing data types [17]. Participants were recruited at age 65 and over without dementia (mean age = 88.06, SD = 6.52), were assessed annually for clinical and cognitive study outcomes, and had agreed to brain donation at the time of death. At death, clinical diagnoses were distributed as follows: 429 (42%) with dementia, 258 (26%) with mild cognitive impairment, and 324 (32%) cognitively normal. Community-based estimates of dementia prevalence among adults aged 85 + typically range from 18–38% [18]. Our observed prevalence of 42% is therefore high but still within expected limits, likely reflecting the prolonged follow-up and the survival of participants to very advanced ages at autopsy. Importantly, this distribution reflects cumulative incidence among individuals enrolled dementia-free and is not directly comparable to cross-sectional community-based prevalence estimates, particularly given that incident dementia is associated with a high risk of death [19, 20]. The distribution is also influenced by several selection factors. Consent for brain donation at enrollment may attract individuals with greater health engagement, and ROS/MAP are volunteer-based cohorts rather than population-based samples. However, several study procedures are designed to mitigate healthy volunteer bias, including in-home recruitment and evaluation, minimal exclusion criteria, and high follow-up (>90%) and autopsy (>85%) rates [21, 22]. All protocols were approved by an Institutional Review Board of Rush University Medical Center and participants provided informed consent and repository consent and a signed Anatomical Gift Act [22].

Antemortem cognitive performance

The global cognitive function score was derived from a series of 19 cognitive tests (Supplementary Table 1) that encompassed episodic memory, semantic memory, working memory, perceptual speed, and visuospatial ability [17, 23–26]. Raw scores were converted to z-scores based on corresponding baseline cohort values and averaged to yield a global cognitive function summary [26]. When individual test scores were missing, the composite was calculated as the mean of the available scores and treated as missing only if more than half of the 19 component tests were unavailable [27]. Cognitive performance from the last study visit was used. Two additional cognitive tests (Mini-Mental State Examination and Complex Ideational Material), separate from the 19 tests used for the composite cognitive score, were administered to aid in the clinical diagnosis of dementia.

Clinical diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease

Final clinical diagnosis was determined by a neurologist following review of select clinical data after death. Results from 11 neuropsychological tests, listed in Supplementary Table 2 [28], were first scored by computer using a decision-tree approach to approximate clinical judgment and generate ratings of impairment across five cognitive domains (episodic memory, semantic memory, working memory, perceptual speed, and visuospatial ability) in four education strata. All tests, selected clinical data, and the algorithm results were reviewed by a study neuropsychologist who confirmed or declined the proposed rating to determine the presence of cognitive impairment. Separately, a study neurologist reviewed these and additional clinical data to make a diagnosis of dementia and its causes [29]. A neuropsychologist, blinded to participant demographics, then assessed cognitive impairment (CI) and dementia based on the computer output and clinical data. Finally, a neurologist reviewed all study visit data to assign a diagnosis proximate to death [28–30]. Dementia and Alzheimer’s dementia diagnoses followed the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke (NINCDS) Alzheimer’s Criteria. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was defined as cases where the neuropsychologist identified cognitive impairment, but the clinician determined they did not meet dementia criteria [28].

The variable representing these clinical groups was classified into 6 categories, numbered from 1 to 6: (1) no CI; (2) MCI with no other CI-contributing condition; (3) MCI with other CI-contributing conditions; (4) Alzheimer’s dementia with no other CI-contributing condition; (5) Alzheimer’s dementia with other CI-contributing conditions; and (6) other dementia with no clinical evidence of Alzheimer’s dementia [30]. For analyses of clinical AD diagnosis, we restricted the sample to two groups to minimize confounding from mixed or non-AD etiologies: those in category 1 or 2 (no CI or MCI with no other contributing conditions; n = 566) served as the reference group, and those in category 4 (Alzheimer’s dementia with no other CI-contributing conditions; n = 380) as the AD case group. Categories 3, 5, and 6 were excluded because they include other CI-contributing conditions (e.g., vascular disease, Lewy body pathology, or non-AD dementia), which could confound associations between CNV-S and LOAD-specific pathways.

Postmortem neuropathology

Postmortem neuropathological measures included neuronal paired helical filament (PHF) tau tangle and amyloid-β peptide load. Following death, brains were removed (median PMI: 6.1 h) and hemisected, with one hemisphere having undergone MRI before and after fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for one month, then sectioned into 1 cm coronal slabs [31]. Tissues from eight regions (anterior cingulate cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, superior frontal cortex, inferior temporal cortex, hippocampus, entorhinal cortex proper, angular/supramarginal gyrus, and primary visual cortex) were sectioned, fixed, and processed with immunohistochemistry to quantify amyloid-β plaque area (%) and PHF tau tangle density. Amyloid-β plaques were labeled with monoclonal antibody 10D5 (Elan Pharmaceuticals, Dublin, Ireland; 1:1000), and PHF tau tangles were detected with phosphorylated tau antibody AT8 (Innogenetics, San Ramon, California, USA; 1:1000). A systematic random sampling scheme with specialized software and microscopy quantified amyloid-β plaque area, while a stereological mapping station assessed PHF tau tangles [31]. Raw pathology counts per region were standardized and averaged into composite measures for each patient, with the square root of the mean used in this study.

Vascular measures included large vessel cerebral atherosclerosis rating and arteriolosclerosis. After fixation, atherosclerosis was assessed at the Circle of Willis by visually inspecting major cerebral arteries and proximal branches [32]. Severity was graded by visual examination of the extent of involvement of each artery and number of arteries involved, on a semiquantitative scale (0–6), later collapsed into four levels: 0 (no significant atherosclerosis), 1 (mild atherosclerosis, <25% vessel involvement without significant occlusion), 2 (moderate atherosclerosis, ≤50% of visualized major arteries affected with < 50% occlusion in any single vessel), and 3 (severe atherosclerosis, >50% of arteries affected and/or > 75% occlusion in one or more vessels). Arteriolosclerosis was evaluated histologically in the anterior basal ganglia and graded based on concentric hyaline wall thickening (lipohyalinosis), using a scale from 0 (no occlusion) to 7 (complete small vessel occlusion). Scores were then grouped into four levels: 0 (absent), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), and 3 (severe) [32].

Whole genome sequencing

A subset of ROS/MAP participants underwent whole genome sequencing using DNA from brain tissue (n = 806), whole blood (n = 389), or EBV virus-transformed lymphocytes (n = 5) (synapse ID: syn10901595). In the final analytic sample (n = 1011), DNA was derived from brain (n = 681), blood (n = 327), or unspecified source (n = 3). Brain tissue was associated with higher deletion CNV-S and lower duplication CNV-S than blood (Supplementary Table 3). Full methodological details have been previously published [22]. Briefly, DNA was fragmented, underwent size selection, end-repair, adenylation, and ligation to Illumina sequencing adapters. Final WGS libraries were assessed using fluorescent assays and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X sequencer using 2 × 150 bp cycles. Sequencing reads were aligned to reference and duplicate reads were detected and marked, and additional processing such as indel alignment, base quality score recalibration, and variant calling and annotation were performed to extract additional insights from the WGS data [22]. Out of the 1200 individuals, 71 outliers were excluded because they contained over 6000 deletions or 1000 duplications, as well as two individuals of non-European ancestry due to cross-population constraints of PGS [10]. This led to a final count of 1127 individuals, out of which 1011 had complete phenotype data and were used for CNV calling [10].

CNV calling

Human autosomal CNVs were identified from WGS data using four complementary calling pipelines: CNVnator [33], Pindel [34], MetaSV [35], and Delly2 [36]. Individual-level calls from the four approaches were integrated into a set of population-level CNVs, and only CNVs detected by three or more approaches were used for subsequent analyses, as previously published [10]. The consensus requirement substantially reduces false positive calls; replication testing on technical replicates yielded a concordance rate of 97–99%, and more than half of consensus CNVs overlap with public reference datasets (e.g., GnomAD, 1000 Genomes Project, DGV, Decipher) [10]. With respect to resolution, CNVs smaller than 500 bp or larger than 500 Mb were excluded during integration, establishing a lower detection limit of approximately 500 bp [10]. Further, only deletion and duplication CNVs are kept. A final total of 4867 CNVs (3918 deletions and 949 duplications) were identified. The predominance of deletions over duplications is consistent with population-level observations, where deletions typically outnumber duplications due to stronger purifying selection against gene-disrupting losses and differences in mutational mechanisms [10]. CNVs were annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) [37] with default parameters and mapped to Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 37 [38].

CNV score calculation

Eight scoring metrics were used to generate eight CNV-S quantifying the cumulative effects of CNV burden for each study participant. Scoring criteria were divided into two categories: measures of intolerance and measures of dosage sensitivity. Measures of intolerance included (1) the probability of loss intolerance (pLI), which is the probability that a gene is intolerant to a loss-of-function mutation, ranging from 0 to 1 with a relatively bimodal distribution; and (2) the loss of function observed/expected upper bound fraction (LOEUF), which is a more conservative measure of loss intolerance with a continuous scale, where low scores indicate strong selection against loss-of-function variants, and high scores indicate relatively higher tolerance to inactivation [39]. In this study, the inverse LOEUF score (1/LOEUF) was used to align with other scoring criteria, with higher scores indicating greater intolerance risk. The pLI and LOEUF data were obtained from the Genome Aggregation Database (141,456 humans: 125,748 exomes, 15,708 genomes) [39].

Measures of dosage sensitivity included (3) the probability of haploinsufficiency (pHI) and (4) the probability of triplosensitivity (pTS), which are the probabilities that a gene is sensitive to copy number loss or copy number gain, respectively [40]. The pHI and pTS data were sourced from a study based on genomes from 753,994 humans across 30 primarily neurological disease phenotypes [40]. A separate, binarized representation of pHI and pTS was determined using a threshold (pHI: 0.86, pTS: 0.94) derived from the study [40], where values that surpassed the threshold indicated an average deletion/duplication effect size equivalent to loss-of-function mutations in genes susceptible to protein-truncating variants (odds ratio ≥ 2.7) [39]. Probabilities greater than or equal to their corresponding thresholds were rounded up to 1; otherwise, they were rounded down to 0. For each criterion, individual-level cumulative scores were calculated by summing the scores of genes within each CNV region, multiplied by the copy number change of that CNV in that particular individual. Cumulative pLI and LOEUF scores were computed separately for deletions and duplications, while pHI scores included only deletions and pTS scores only duplications. A score of 0 signified that the individual did not have any coding-gene-containing CNVs called. Of 3211 annotated genes, 2542 of 4867 CNVs contained at least one gene. Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates CNV distribution by size, chromosomal location, and gene content. Among the annotated genes, 2032 had associated pLI and LOEUF scores, and 1990 had associated pHI and pTS scores. Supplementary Fig. 2 displays individual-level CNV-S distributions. Notably, no CNVs in the dataset overlapped the APOE gene body (GRCh37: chr19:45,409,011–45,412,650 [41]) or any region within 500 kb of it; the nearest CNV used in the analysis is a deletion CNV on chromosome 19 that lies approximately 866.0 kb away from the APOE locus. In total, eight CNV-S measures were derived for analysis: pLI (DEL/DUP), LOEUF (DEL/DUP), pHI, pTS, and their respective binarized forms.

Polygenic score calculation

PGS represents the cumulative effects of common SNPs on an individual’s risk for a phenotype or disease. Here, we calculated a PGS for late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (PGS-LOAD) using summary statistics from a large GWAS (n = 94,437) [42], the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European reference panel for linkage disequilibrium (LD) [43], and SNP calls from WGS data of the 1011 participants involved in this study [22]. The Kunkle et al. summary statistics comprised approximately 11.5 million variants and included information on the association between each SNP and LOAD diagnosis (e.g., p-values, effect sizes, and sample size). After overlap with the LD reference and target genotypes, approximately 1.1 million SNPs were used for score computation. The LD reference panel provided complementary information on the extent to which alleles are correlated with each other in our European-ancestry cohort. PGS-LOAD was computed using PRS-CS (polygenic risk score with continuous shrinkage) [13], a Bayesian regression method that applies continuous shrinkage priors to SNP effect sizes. These priors enabled adaptive, marker-specific shrinkage based on GWAS signal strength, accommodating diverse genetic architectures. They also supported joint multivariate updates of SNP effect sizes within LD blocks, improving LD pattern modeling and computational efficiency. PRS-CS assigned posterior effect sizes to all included SNPs, with most receiving substantial shrinkage toward zero; the final score was a weighted sum of genotype dosages across these SNPs. Note that established LOAD risk loci, including APOE, were not modeled separately and were included within the genome-wide PGS to evaluate the additional predictive power of CNV-S beyond an all inclusive PGS-LOAD. For validation, we computed a secondary PGS using summary statistics from an independent GWAS of proxy LOAD (based on parental diagnoses; total n = 788,989) [44] to confirm the robustness of our findings across different GWAS inputs.

Statistical analysis of CNV-S associations with phenotypic outcomes

Association analyses between CNV-S and phenotypic outcomes were performed using the stats package in R (v4.2.1) [45]. Outcomes included four post-mortem neuropathological measures (PHF tau tangles, overall amyloid load, cerebral atherosclerosis rating, arteriolosclerosis) and two ante-mortem cognitive scores (global cognitive function proximal to death, final consensus clinical diagnosis). For each outcome k (4 neuropathological, 2 cognitive), four regression models were fitted (linear for continuous outcomes and logistic for binary), each of which featured two CNV-S, according to the following representative formulae:

outcomek~pLIDEL+pLIDUP+covariates

outcomek~pLIDEL+pLIDUP+covariates

outcomek~LOEUFDEL+LOEUFDUP+covariates

outcomek~LOEUFDEL+LOEUFDUP+covariates

outcomek~pHI+pTS+covariates

outcomek~pHI+pTS+covariates

outcomek~pHIbinarized+pTSbinarized+covariates

outcomek~pHIbinarized+pTSbinarized+covariates

Neuropathological models included covariates for sex, age at death, and post-mortem interval; cognitive models were adjusted for sex, years of education, and age at cognitive evaluation. All models incorporated the top 10 ancestry genetic principal components as covariates to account for fine population structure. The p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method [46], with significance at PFDR < 0.05 across all 48 models (6 outcomes × 8 CNV scores per outcome). Power calculations were performed in R using the pwr package.

Comparison with benchmark common variant polygenic score model

To test CNV-S’s predictive power beyond PGS-LOAD, an established polygenic score for LOAD [13, 22, 42–44], we constructed linear regression models to assess whether the inclusion of CNV-S significantly improved the overall model fit. Two linear models were developed for each outcome using the following representative formulas:

outcomek~PGS−LOAD+covariates

outcomek~PGS−LOAD+covariates

outcomek~PGS−LOAD+CNV−SDEL+CNV−SDUP+covariates

outcomek~PGS−LOAD+CNV−SDEL+CNV−SDUP+covariates

The primary analysis utilized PGS derived from the Kunkle et al. study [42], while PGS from the Bellenguez et al. study [44] were used for validation. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were performed between the two models, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Adjusted R-squared values were examined for each model pair to assess the improvement in model fit.

Results

The study included 1011 adults (65.6% female, mean age 88.06 ± 6.52 years), all self-identified as White, with tissue sources from blood (n = 327), brain (n = 681), and unspecified (n = 3). A total of 4867 high-quality CNVs (3918 deletions and 949 duplications) were identified, mapping to 3211 genes. The distribution of CNV based on size and chromosomal location, and the distribution of CNV-S can be found in Supplementary Figs 1 and 2. Of these, 2032 had associated pLI and LOEUF scores, and 1990 had associated pHI and pTS scores. Phenotypic outcomes measured included PHF tau tangles (n = 1001), overall beta-amyloid load (n = 1005), cerebral atherosclerosis rating (n = 1004), arteriolosclerosis (n = 1003), global cognitive function (n = 1011), and final consensus clinical diagnosis (n = 946).

Association of CNV risk scores with cognition and neuropathology

After correction for multiple comparisons, significant associations were found between cerebral atherosclerosis and all four deletion CNV-S (pLI: β = 0.14; 95% CI, 0.08 to 0.21; PFDR = 1.39 × 10−4; LOEUF: β = 0.14; 95% CI, 0.08 to 0.20; PFDR = 1.39 × 10−4; pHI: β = 0.15; 95% CI, 0.08 to 0.21; PFDR = 1.39 × 10−4; binarized pHI: β = 0.14; 95% CI, 0.08 to 0.21; PFDR = 1.39 × 10−4; Fig. 1). Fisher’s exact tests comparing top and bottom CNV-S tertiles (no vs. severe atherosclerosis) yielded significant associations for all deletion scores (pLI: P = 0.02; OR = 2.16; 95% CI, 1.08 to 4.41; LOEUF: P = 0.03; OR = 2.21; 95% CI, 1.07 to 4.64; pHI: P = 0.004; OR = 2.85; 95% CI, 1.36 to 6.12; binarized pHI: P = 0.001; OR = 3.06; 95% CI, 1.49 to 6.41; Supplementary Table 4). Individuals in the highest tertiles of CNV-S (based on pLI, LOEUF, pHI, and binarized pHI) had 2–3 times higher odds of severe atherosclerosis compared to those in lower tertiles.

Analysis of CNV-S in relation to source tissue category and read depth revealed significant associations with source tissue (Supplementary Table 3): brain tissue was associated with higher deletion CNV-S and lower duplication CNV-S compared to blood, but adjusting for source tissue as a covariate did not alter the associations between CNV-S and the primary or secondary outcomes. Sex-stratified analyses

Given sex-specific risk pathways for LOAD and known differences in vascular disease prevalence between sexes [16, 47], we repeated our association analyses separately for males (n = 348) and females (n = 663). Although CNV-S was significantly associated with cerebral atherosclerosis in both sexes (Fig. 3), stratified analyses revealed sex-specific differences. For example, cognitive performance, amyloid-β load, and PHF tau tangle density showed marginal associations in females but not in males. To test for sex-dependent effects, we included interaction terms (CNV-S * sex) for each outcome. However, no significant interaction effects remained after correction (Supplementary Table 7).

Augmentation of PGS-LOAD models by CNV-S

To test whether CNV-S explained additional variance in outcomes beyond established SNP-based PGS-LOAD, our analyses involved fitting PGS-LOAD models for each outcome. In a supplementary step, we additionally assessed correlations between PGS-LOAD and CNV-S. Several nominal associations were observed (e.g., LOEUF (DEL), pHI, pTS, binarized pTS; r≈0.04–0.08), but none survived FDR correction (Supplementary Fig. 5). As expected and shown previously in this cohort [48–50], PGS-LOAD was significantly associated with all pathological and cognitive outcomes. The strongest association was observed for global cognitive function at the last study visit (t = −20.30; PFDR = 1.37 × 10−75), and the weakest association was for arteriolosclerosis (t = 2.52; PFDR = 0.048). Adding CNV-S to these models significantly improved model fits for cerebral atherosclerosis, evidenced by an increase in adjusted R-squared values and significant ANOVA p-values (pLI (DEL): ΔR2 = 0.02; P = 6.51 × 10−5; LOEUF (DEL): ΔR2 = 0.02; P = 3.89 × 10−5; pHI: ΔR2 = 0.02; P = 9.10 × 10−6; pTS: ΔR2 = 0.02; P = 1.03 × 10−4; Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 8). A suggestive improvement was also observed between pHI CNV-S and arteriolosclerosis (ΔR2 = 0.01; P = 0.04). CNV-S did not enhance other outcome models. These improvements in model fit were consistently observed when using the validation PGS based on the proxy-LOAD GWAS (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

The results of this study shed light on the intricate associations between CNV-S and a spectrum of neurological, cerebrovascular, and cognitive outcomes in aging. One key observation is the positive and significant relationship between deletion CNV-S and cerebrovascular pathology, specifically large vessel cerebral atherosclerosis ratings. Previous studies [15, 51] examined CNVs and copy neutral loss of heterozygosity in vascular tissues and peripheral blood leukocytes of atherosclerosis patients, revealing increased CNV numbers and lengths in atherosclerotic coronary arteries compared to other tissues. Our findings provide (1) supporting evidence for a role of genome-wide structural variation in the pathogenesis of vascular disease in brain tissue and (2) a novel scoring procedure for predicting a portion of this neuropathological risk non-invasively in humans. While we also found suggestive evidence for associations between specific duplication CNV-S and neuropathological and cognitive outcomes, including PHF tau, amyloid-β protein density, clinical diagnosis of LOAD, and global cognitive function, as well as between deletion CNV-S and arteriolosclerosis, these associations did not survive multiple testing correction.

The four selected CNV scoring metrics (pLI, LOEUF, pHI/pTS, and binarized pHI/pTS) show varied performance across different models. Metrics based on dosage sensitivity (pHI, pTS) generally performed better than those based on loss intolerance (pLI, LOEUF) in models of neuropathological and cognitive phenotypes. The differing performance could be in part due to the fact that loss intolerance-based score metrics are derived from statistical modeling of the expected and observed prevalence of loss-of-function variants of a gene in a population [39], which often overlooks phenotypes emerging after reproductive age, since population prevalence is primarily determined by whether the gene variants affect an individual’s ability to reproduce. Late-onset phenotypes, like LOAD and related cognitive traits, might not be accurately represented by pLI and LOEUF metrics as they may not significantly impact reproductive success. On the other hand, metrics that measure dosage sensitivity (pHI, pTS) are based on a list of 54 coarse disease phenotypes [40]. Although each phenotype is not specific, e.g., the term “impaired cognition” covers cognitive impairment from all causes, our results show that they were able to better model an individual’s CNV composition to neuropathological and cognitive phenotypes. Nevertheless, the findings demonstrate the utility of applying multiple variant effect metrics when evaluating cumulative CNV risk.

Differing model performance was observed between the deletion and duplication CNV-S, even with the same weighting metric. For example, cerebrovascular pathology was associated with all four deletion-based CNV-S but not duplications. Deletion CNVs are typically more damaging than duplications [8], and mechanisms of pathogenicity are likely more variable for duplications [52]. The skewed distribution of CNVs in our sample (approximately 4:1 deletions to duplications) may also affect statistical inference: deletion CNV-S benefit from greater variant density and may have higher power to detect associations, whereas duplication CNV-S, with fewer variants, may be underpowered for some effects. We model deletions and duplications as separate predictors in each regression, so we do not directly compare their effect sizes; the observed pattern of stronger deletion associations may reflect both biological differences and differential statistical power. Previous research also proposed variations between haploinsufficient and triplosensitive genes in terms of size, proximity to other genes, and the precision of developmental regulation [40]. However, a consistent trend is that the binarized version of the pHI and pTS score, with threshold at odds ratio ≥ 2.7 [39], improves the model’s fit across all score metrics and outcomes. The differences in the impact of deletion and duplication CNV-S on cerebrovascular pathology highlight the complexity of genomic variation in influencing disease susceptibility. An important area of future work will be to expand and ultimately refine the selection of scoring metrics to better reflect a gene’s pathogenicity.

Building on existing methods for aggregating CNV alleles for calculating individual-level CNV-S, which previously counted CNVs with a binary “present vs. absent” indicator, we introduced a weighted sum approach where the effect of each CNV (as defined by each scoring metric) is multiplied by the copy number multiple to reflect individual-specific dosage effects of annotated genes. Gene expression often correlates directly with gene copy number. For example, if a gene is duplicated, triplicated, or more, there is often a proportional increase in the amount of mRNA produced by that gene [53]. Incorporating copy number into the aggregation process enhances the sensitivity of our analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4), enabling a more detailed assessment of the overall impact of CNVs on gene expression and phenotypes. Future work should explore different weighting schemes systematically and quantify their influence on both intermediate gene expression and downstream phenotypic measures.

Comparative analyses with PGS-LOAD showed a specificity of CNV-S to cerebrovascular phenotypes. The difference in the magnitude of score effect on cerebrovascular phenotypes compared to other canonical LOAD-related neuropathologies and cognitive performance is consistent with prior analyses [42], where PGS-LOAD was only marginally associated with measures of cardiovascular health, such as family history of high blood pressure, heart disease, and vascular/heart problems. Importantly, the addition of CNV-S to PGS-LOAD models resulted in significantly improved predictions of vascular phenotypes, but not of other neuropathological or cognitive phenotypes. These findings were further validated using a secondary PGS derived from an independent GWAS of family history-derived proxy LOAD [44], which consistently showed improved prediction of vascular phenotypes without significant effects on other neuropathological or cognitive outcomes. LOAD is a multi-faceted disorder characterized by a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors [1]. Different genetic factors may contribute to distinct risk pathways toward the same diagnosis. Our results suggest that cerebrovascular pathology may be particularly susceptible to influence by aggregate genomic structural variation rather than common SNPs, and that individual-level CNV-S may represent accessible in vivo metrics for predicting this pathology in late life. Future work will replicate this approach in other datasets to validate the generalizability of these findings.

There are several limitations to our study. First, the methods used in this research were adapted from previous studies that relied on SNP-array-based CNV calls, while this study utilized CNV calls from WGS data. This resulted in some differences in the distribution of CNV calls, making it infeasible to directly replicate the quality control measures outlined in the reference paper. We followed the criteria established in a previous study [10] to adapt our own set of quality control procedures tailored to the unique characteristics of our dataset. Second, our approach included copy number count as a weighted multiplier for the CNV scores, which differs from prior studies that assigned equal weights to each CNV. This approach assumes a linear relationship between copy number count and loss intolerance, as well as dosage sensitivity, which may not hold true in practice, and introduces an additional parameter that requires fine-tuning. Third, the clinical diagnoses at death (42% dementia, 26% MCI, 32% cognitively normal) reflects cohort-specific selection: participants were dementia-free at enrollment, consented to brain donation, and are volunteer-based rather than population-representative, limiting generalizability. This distribution reflects cumulative incidence over long-term follow-up rather than cross-sectional prevalence and should be interpreted in that context, particularly given the strong association between dementia and mortality [19, 20]. Fourth, combining cognitively normal individuals and MCI participants as a single reference group improves phenotypic specificity by excluding possible AD (categories 3 and 5) and non-AD dementias (category 6), but MCI is not a neutral baseline since neurodegenerative processes are often already underway, which may reduce effect size estimates. Future analyses using a fully stratified design would better characterize effects across the cognitive spectrum. Fifth, WGS was performed on DNA from mixed tissue sources. Brain tissue showed higher deletion CNV-S and lower duplication CNV-S than blood, consistent with known tissue-specific differences: somatic CNVs can accumulate in brain during development [15, 51], and read-depth-based CNV callers may detect tissue-specific copy number changes. EBV transformation of lymphocytes has been discussed as a potential source of structural variation artifacts, although low-passage cell lines may remain faithful to the donor genome [54]; only five participants had EBV-derived DNA. Including source tissue as a covariate in all primary models did not attenuate the significant associations with cerebrovascular pathology, suggesting the CNV-S–atherosclerosis relationship is not driven by tissue source. Nevertheless, future studies using a single tissue source would help clarify whether the observed associations reflect germline variation, tissue-specific somatic changes, or technical confounds.

This study identifies significant associations between genome-wide CNV scores and post-mortem cerebrovascular pathology. This type of pathology is an important component of neurological changes found in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, and not indexed by existing PGS-based modelling approaches. However, further improvements in quality control and scoring methods are necessary to enhance the generalizability of these associations. These results emphasize the importance of considering rare structural variation when mapping heritable contributors to late-life cognitive decline.
