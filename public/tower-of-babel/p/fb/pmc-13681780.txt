Development and validation of a prediction model for subsequent fractures after hip fracture surgery in older adults: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Linlin Zhang, Yanling Zhou, Jia Jin, Wenping Xue, Wei Wang, Shuqiu Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubsequent fractures after hip fracture surgery are associated with substantial morbidity, functional decline, and increased mortality in older adults. Early identification of high-risk patients is therefore essential for implementing targeted preventive strategies. This study aimed to develop and temporally validate a prediction model for subsequent fractures within 2 years after hip fracture surgery in older adults.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included older adults who underwent surgery for a first unilateral fragility hip fracture between January 2019 and May 2022. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and lifestyle-related variables were collected from electronic medical records and structured telephone interviews. LASSO Cox regression was used for predictor selection, followed by multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression to identify independent predictors. A nomogram was constructed to estimate individualized risks of subsequent fractures at 1 and 2 years after surgery. Model performance was evaluated using C-index, AUC, calibration plots, and DCA.ResultsDuring the 2-year follow-up period, 190 patients (8.9%) experienced subsequent fractures. Based on discharge date, 1,735 patients discharged between January 2019 and December 2021 were assigned to the development cohort, whereas 415 patients discharged between January 2022 and May 2022 constituted the temporal validation cohort. Six independent predictors were identified, including age, hip function, neurodegenerative disease, eye disease, and living alone, whereas exercise was identified as a protective factor. The nomogram demonstrated good discriminative performance, with C-index values of 0.891 and 0.861 in the development and temporal validation cohorts, respectively. The 2-year time-dependent AUCs were 0.925 (95% CI: 0.899–0.952) in the development cohort and 0.896 (95% CI: 0.832–0.941) in the temporal validation cohort. Calibration plots demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, and DCA indicated favorable clinical utility across a wide range of threshold probabilities.ConclusionsWe developed and temporally validated a nomogram for predicting subsequent fractures within 2 years after hip fracture surgery in older adults. This model may facilitate early postoperative risk stratification and support individualized secondary prevention strategies aimed at reducing the risk of secondary fragility fractures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10258-5.

Introduction

Hip fractures represent a major public healthf concern among older adults and are associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden worldwide [1]. With the rapid aging of the global population, the incidence of hip fractures continues to increase, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide. It is estimated that the number of hip fractures will reach 6.3 million by 2050 as global life expectancy increases [2]. Although advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management have improved short-term survival, older adults remain at high risk of adverse postoperative outcomes after discharge. Among these complications, subsequent fractures are particularly concerning [3].

Subsequent fractures following an initial hip fracture are associated with poorer functional recovery, increased disability, and higher mortality [4, 5]. Previous studies have reported substantial heterogeneity in the incidence of subsequent fractures across different countries and populations, which may be related to differences in study design, patient characteristics, and healthcare systems [5–7]. Importantly, subsequent fractures are often preventable, and early identification of high-risk individuals is critical for implementing targeted interventions, such as fall prevention, osteoporosis management, and rehabilitation programs [8–10]. Therefore, early identification of prognostic factors associated with subsequent fractures is essential for identifying high-risk patients and guiding preventive interventions.

Recent studies have identified several factors associated with subsequent fractures, including age, sex, comorbidity burden, and neurodegenerative disease [8–10]. However, t most previous studies focused primarily on conventional clinical characteristics and were conducted in highly selected populations, thereby limiting their generalizability to broader older adult populations. In recent years, predictive models have increasingly been used to estimate fracture risk and support individualized clinical decision-making [11, 12]. However, currently available models are often limited by relatively small sample sizes, inadequate validation. Moreover, most existing prediction tools were developed to estimate the risk of primary osteoporotic fractures rather than subsequent fractures after hip fracture surgery. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate predictive model for subsequent fractures within two years after hip fracture surgery using a retrospective cohort. By incorporating demographic characteristics, clinical variables, and modifiable lifestyle factors, we sought to construct a clinically applicable nomogram to facilitate early risk stratification and guide targeted preventive strategies in older adults.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study included older adults with hip fractures admitted to two affiliated hospitals of Soochow University between January 1, 2019, and May 31, 2022. Eligible patients met the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) first unilateral fragility hip fracture; and (3) surgical treatment with internal fixation or arthroplasty [13]. Patients were excluded if they: (1) died during hospitalization; (2) had pathological fractures or high-energy traumatic fractures; (3) had substantial missing baseline data; or (4) were lost to follow-up.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (SUDA20230115H07). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and minimal risk to participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines.

Predictor variables

Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were extracted from the electronic medical record system during the index hospitalization. Demographic variables included age, sex, and living status. Clinical variables included fracture type, cause of injury, comorbidities, postoperative complications, and hip function at discharge using the Harris Hip Score [14]. Comorbidities included hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure, arrhythmia, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatoid arthritis, neurodegenerative diseases (including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease) [15], and physician-diagnosed eye diseases such as cataract and glaucoma. Laboratory variables included hemoglobin level and albumin level. Anemia and hypoalbuminemia were defined according to institutional laboratory reference standards. Exercise was defined as self-reported participation in regular moderate-intensity physical activity or rehabilitation exercise after discharge. These variables were collected through structured telephone interviews conducted by trained investigators after discharge.

Follow-up and outcome definition

Patients were routinely followed up after discharge through outpatient visits and structured telephone interviews conducted by trained investigators. Follow-up assessments were performed between May 2023 and May 2025. All included patients completed at least 2 years of follow-up after the index hip fracture surgery. Information regarding survival status, mobility, and occurrence and data of subsequent fractures was collected through medical record review and telephone interviews. nformation regarding survival status, mobility, and dates of subsequent fractures was collected through medical record review and structured telephone interviews. Time-to-event was calculated from the date of index surgery to the date of subsequent fragility fracture or the end of the 2-year follow-up period, whichever occurred first. Patients without subsequent fractures were treated as censored observations at the end of follow-up. The primary outcome was subsequent fragility fracture within 2 years after hip fracture surgery. Subsequent fragility fractures were defined as new low-energy fractures occurring after discharge and confirmed by orthopedic physicians based on clinical presentation and imaging examinations, including radiography, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging when necessary.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent-samples t-test, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Missing data were assumed to be missing at random and were handled using multiple imputation with predictive mean matching [16]. Ten imputed datasets were generated, and pooled estimates were obtained according to Rubin’s rules to reduce potential bias and preserve statistical efficiency. Time-to-event analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression [17]. The time scale was defined as the interval from index hip fracture surgery to the occurrence of the first subsequent fragility fracture or the end of the 2-year follow-up period. Patients without subsequent fractures were censored at the end of follow-up.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression with 10-fold cross-validation was applied to identify candidate predictors and reduce potential overfitting [18]. The optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was selected according to the 1-standard-error criterion. Variables with non-zero coefficients identified by LASSO regression were subsequently entered into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify independent predictors. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated [19]. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant violations were observed.

A nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariable Cox regression model to estimate individualized probabilities of subsequent fractures at 1 and 2 years after surgery [20]. Patients discharged between January 2019 and December 2021 were assigned to the development cohort, whereas those discharged between January 2022 and May 2022 constituted the temporal validation cohort. Bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations was performed for internal validation and optimism correction in the development cohort [21]. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves with corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values [22, 23]. Calibration was evaluated using calibration plots comparing predicted and observed probabilities [24]. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA) [25]. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were additionally performed to compare fracture-free survival across predictor strata [26]. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and R software version 4.3.3. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and follow-up

A total of 2,866 older adults with hip fractures were initially screened for eligibility. After excluding 343 patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria and 373 patients who were lost to follow-up, 2,150 patients were ultimately included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). All included patients completed at least 2 years of follow-up after index surgery. Missing data were primarily observed in exercise variables collected during telephone interviews. The overall proportion of missing data was relatively low and was handled using multiple imputation before model development.

Predictor selection and model development

S Six predictors with non-zero coefficients were identified using LASSO Cox regression and subsequently included in the multivariable Cox regression model. Poor hip function, advanced age, neurodegenerative disease, eye disease, and living alone were associated with an increased risk of subsequent fractures, whereas exercise was identified as a protective factor (Fig. 2; Table 2). Kaplan–Meier survival analyses further demonstrated significant differences in fracture-free survival across different predictor strata in both cohorts (Additional File 2).

Model performance and validation

A nomogram incorporating the six independent predictors was constructed to estimate individualized probabilities of subsequent fractures at 1 and 2 years after hip fracture surgery (Fig. 3). The nomogram demonstrated good discrimination performance in both the development and temporal validation cohorts. The Harrell’s C-index of the model was 0.891 in the development cohort and 0.861 in the temporal validation cohort. In addition, the time-dependent AUCs for predicting subsequent fractures were 0.925 (95% CI: 0.889–0.952) in the development cohort and 0.896 (95% CI: 0.832–0.941) in the temporal validation cohort (Fig. 4). Calibration plots showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities in both cohorts (Fig. 5). Decision curve analysis demonstrated favorable net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities in both the development cohort (0.01–0.85) and the temporal validation cohort (0.01–0.75) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and temporally validated a prediction model for subsequent fractures within 2 years after hip fracture surgery in older adults. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on contralateral hip fractures, our outcome included postoperative incident fragility fractures across multiple skeletal sites. The overall incidence of subsequent fractures was 8.9%, which is generally consistent with previous reports in Asian populations [7, 27]. Differences in incidence across studies may be related to variations in patient characteristics, healthcare systems, follow-up duration, and postoperative management strategies.

Six predictors were ultimately incorporated into the final model, including age, hip function, neurodegenerative disease, eye disease, living alone, and exercise. Advanced age and poor postoperative hip function were strongly associated with increased fracture risk, which is consistent with previous evidence linking functional decline and frailty to recurrent falls and skeletal vulnerability [28–30]. In addition, neurodegenerative disorders and visual impairment may increase the likelihood of falls through impaired balance control, gait instability, reduced visual-spatial perception, and diminished environmental hazard recognition [31, 32]. Behavioral and social factors also appeared to play an important role. Patients living alone may have reduced supervision and lower adherence to rehabilitation or osteoporosis treatment after discharge [27]. Conversely, exercise was identified as a protective factor, possibly because regular physical activity may improve muscle strength, balance function, and mobility, thereby reducing fall risk [33]. These findings highlight the multifactorial nature of subsequent fractures after hip fracture surgery.

Compared with existing tools such as the Fracture Risk Assessment Tool (FRAX) and QFracture, which are primarily designed to estimate the risk of primary fractures [34], our model specifically targets postoperative hip fracture patients and predicts subsequent fractures across multiple skeletal sites. Moreover, the predictors included in the model are routinely available in clinical practice, which may facilitate practical implementation in orthopedic and geriatric settings.

From a clinical implementation perspective, the present nomogram may serve as a practical tool for early postoperative risk stratification and individualized secondary prevention planning [35]. Because all predictors can be obtained during hospitalization or early discharge follow-up, the model may be integrated into perioperative assessment workflows and discharge management pathways. Patients identified as high risk may benefit from intensified multidisciplinary interventions. These interventions may include structured rehabilitation, fall prevention strategies, medication optimization, and osteoporosis management. Furthermore, the model may help clinicians prioritize follow-up resources and identify socially vulnerable patients who require additional community or family support after discharge. Given the increasing emphasis on transitional care and long-term fracture prevention in aging populations, individualized prediction models may contribute to more efficient allocation of healthcare resources and improved continuity of care.

The nomogram also demonstrated good discrimination and calibration in both the development and temporal validation cohorts. Decision curve analysis further suggested that the model may provide clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective study and may therefore be subject to selection bias and information bias. Second, some predictors, including age and hip function, were analyzed as categorical rather than continuous variables, which may have resulted in information loss. Third, although temporal validation was performed, the validation cohort was derived from the same institutions, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, several potentially important variables, such as bone mineral density, medication adherence, and detailed rehabilitation intensity, were not available in the retrospective database and therefore could not be incorporated into the model. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to further validate and optimize the model.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and temporally validated a nomogram prediction model for subsequent fractures within 2 years after hip fracture surgery in older adults. The model incorporated routinely available clinical and lifestyle-related variables and demonstrated good discrimination, calibration, and potential clinical utility. This clinically accessible tool may facilitate early postoperative risk stratification and support individualized secondary prevention strategies for older adults at high risk of recurrent fragility fractures.
