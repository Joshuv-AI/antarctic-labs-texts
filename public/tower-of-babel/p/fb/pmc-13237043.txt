Sequential ethane filling induced structural transitions in a flexible–robust metal–organic framework for ethylene purification
Authors: Lu Zhang, Shan Liu, Weixiang Zuo, Yongheng Ren, Zilin Wu, Yong Wang, Yang Chen, Yue-Biao Zhang, Lin-Bing Sun, Libo Li, Jinping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe preferential identification of inert ethane molecules and the corresponding pore-filling mechanism are fundamentally challenging. Although flexible metal–organic frameworks are considered promising candidates to enhance ethane selectivity, their gate-opening pressures for ethane and ethylene are usually similar, which limits further improvement in their separation performance. Here, we report a flexible–robust metal–organic framework, TYUT-18, whose binary pore system combined with the flexible deformation of the framework produces significantly different gate-opening pressures for ethane (0.18 bar) and ethylene (0.48 bar) at 298 K, effectively reducing competitive adsorption in the same pore and improving overall separation efficiency, and achieves the purification of ultra-high purity ethylene (99.995%) with a separation productivity of 15.7 L kg−1. More importantly, we have systematically elucidated the dynamic filling behavior of ethane molecules in the flexible–robust framework system, a process of preferentially occupying the large pore cavities then filling into the small ones was uncovered using a combination of extensive single-crystal X-ray diffraction studies and density functional theory calculations. The evolution of this sequential guest-filling mechanism demonstrated the merit of binary pore networks for challenging gas separation tasks and thereby offers valuable insights for the design of highly selective separation materials.Subject terms: Energy, Organometallic chemistry, Chemical engineering

Introduction

Ultra-high purity ethylene (C2H4) is a critical feedstock in the petrochemical industry, which is extensively employed during the production of special type high-end polyolefins, including metallocene polyethylene, ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene fibre, and others, with a typical purity requirement exceeding 99.995%1–3. Currently, the critical purification of ultra-high purity C2H4 in industry is predominantly achieved using an energy-intensive multistage distillation process4–7. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop highly selective adsorbents capable of the targeted removal of trace ethane (C2H6), thereby markedly simplifying the current industrial purification process and minimizing energy consumption8–10.

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have emerged as promising candidates for the efficient separation of C2H4/C2H6 due to their highly tuneable architectures and versatile functionalization strategies11–18. However, the preferential recognition of the relatively inert and larger C2H6 molecules from high-concentration C2H4 mixtures remains a major challenge19–21. In general, robust MOFs offer excellent structural stability, while their inherent porous structures often make it difficult to simultaneously achieve both a high C2H6 adsorption capacity and high selectivity6,7,22–27. Flexible MOF with rich supramolecular interactions can achieve the selective recognition of specific gas molecules via the “gate-opening” effect, while they have defects such as closely gate-opening pressures and low adsorption capacity under low-pressure conditions28–32. In contrast, flexible–robust MOFs combine the structural advantages of their robust pore cavities and flexible pore windows, which are expected to achieve high adsorption capacity at low pressure and improved separation selectivity33–38. Subsequently, we successfully synthesized a flexible–robust MOF, TYUT-17, which achieves high C2H6 adsorption capacity (62.6 cm3 g−1) and C2H6/C2H4 selectivity (6.4) under low-pressure conditions (0.1 bar)19. Nevertheless, its single-pore structure concentrates the flexible response and molecular recognition function within the same pore, causing C2H6 and C2H4 molecules to exhibit similar pore-opening behaviors during adsorption, resulting in competitive adsorption within the same pore.

We propose engineering two distinct pore cavities (large and small) within the framework in order to overcome the inherent simple recognition mechanism of single pore systems. Within this system, the large pores facilitate the initial preferential adsorption of C2H6, which gives sufficient gas uptakes at low pressure region, while the small pores highly match the cross-section of C2H6, allowing their precise capture via the gating effect (Fig. 1), providing extra recognition selectivity. Despite the great potential of such flexible framework systems, their effective implementation requires a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic adsorption behavior of C2H6 in binary pore system MOFs, particularly their sequential filling and reversible release mechanisms39–42. For a long time, chemists have used X-ray diffraction and other techniques in order to identify the adsorption sites of C2H6 within MOFs, while the specific pore-filling process remains poorly understood41,43–50. Notably, Yaghi et al. combined in situ gas-loading single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) with density functional theory (DFT) calculations to systematically elucidate the filling mechanism of water molecules in MOF-30351, establishing a research paradigm for probing filling pathways of guest molecules in robust porous materials at the molecular scale52. Subsequently, through in situ gas-loading SCXRD analysis and DFT calculations at different temperatures, Kitagawa et al. systematically elucidated the sequential filling mechanism of C2 gas in flexible–robust MOFs and its correlation with framework flexibility, greatly advancing the understanding of the sequential adsorption behavior of guest molecules in flexible-robust MOFs38. Based on this, we further expanded our research perspective to pressure-mediated effects to reveal the sequential adsorption behavior of C2H6 molecules in flexible–robust MOFs under different pressures and their corresponding structural responses.

Step-shaped adsorption and dynamic pore-filling behavior of C2H6 in TYUT-18.

The C2H6 adsorption isotherm of TYUT-18 shows a typical step-shaped adsorption feature (solid: adsorption, hollow: desorption): In the low-pressure region, C2H6 molecules preferentially enter into large pore cavity A of the β phase; as the pressure increases, the C2H6 molecules fill into small pore cavity B, accompanied by a transition of the β phase into the α phase; during the desorption process, the C2H6 molecules are first removed from pore cavity A and remain in the α phase. The C2H6 molecules in small pore cavity B are then gradually removed and finally return to the β phase.

Herein, we have synthesized a flexible–robust MOF, Zn(L1)(L2), referred to as TYUT-18 (TYUT: Taiyuan University of Technology, L1: 1,3-di(4-carboxyphenyl)benzene, L2: 1,4-bis(4-pyridyl)benzene), which features a binary pore system that combines the structural advantages of large pore cavities for rapid adsorption and small pore cavities for selective gate-opening responses. TYUT-18 exhibits a pronounced difference in gate-opening pressures for C2H6 (0.18 bar) and C2H4 (0.48 bar), and enables the purification of C2H4 to an ultra-high purity level (99.995%). Furthermore, we have clearly discovered the dynamic pore-filling behavior of C2H6 molecules in flexible, robust MOF combining SCXRD and DFT calculations, which followed the principle of first occupying the large pores and then filling the small pores. This study provides a theoretical basis for the rational design of next-generation materials for efficient C2H6-selective materials.

Results

Synthesis and structural determination

Single crystals of TYUT-18 were successfully synthesized via the self-assembly of zinc nitrate with two organic ligands, 1,3-di(4-carboxyphenyl)benzene (L1) and 1,4-bis(4-pyridyl)benzene (L2) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1). In situ SCXRD studies revealed the single-crystal to single-crystal (sc-sc) transformation of guest-loaded TYUT-18 (α) (CCDC 2407210, Supplementary Data 1 and 9) to guest-free TYUT-18 (β) (CCDC 2412958, Supplementary Data 2 and 10) after the activation process. SCXRD analysis revealed that the structure of TYUT-18 (α) consisted of a three-dimensional (3D) framework composed of Zn paddle-wheel secondary building units (SBUs) and organic ligands, which formed a porous architecture with a binary pore system. The L1 ligand resided in the equatorial plane, forming pore cavity A (6.5 Å), whereas the L2 ligand occupied the axial coordination site and formed pore cavity B (4.8 Å) with L1 ligand. The pore size and adsorption properties of TYUT-18 were evaluated using N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms at 77 K (Supplementary Fig. 2). The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) specific surface area of TYUT-18 was calculated to be 636.3 m2 g–1 and the pore size distribution exhibited binary pores of 6.5 Å and 4.8 Å. The crystal belonged to the monoclinic C12/c1 space group. Guest-free TYUT-18 (β) could be obtained by heating the guest-loaded TYUT-18 (α) crystal at 423 K for 12 h. Although its space group remained unchanged, the cell parameters changed significantly, which indicated that TYUT-18 underwent a structural transformation after removing the guest molecules (Supplementary Table 2). We can also observe the phase transition from TYUT-18 (β) to TYUT-18 (α), powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) results showed a clearly diffraction peak position shifted after the activation process (Supplementary Fig. 3). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) confirmed that the 21.5% weight loss observed below 573 K, which could be attributed to the complete removal of the N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent molecules (Supplementary Fig. 4). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed that the material retained its intact lamellar morphology throughout the activation and adsorption processes (Supplementary Fig. 5), thereby further confirming its structural stability.

It is noteworthy that TYUT-18 displayed multidimensional structural flexibility, encompassing ligand rotation and dynamic modulation of the bond angle between the ligand and metal node (Fig. 2b). Rotation of the benzene ring in the L1 ligand during the α to β phase transition (Supplementary Fig. 6), which was accompanied by changes in the bond angles, altered the dimensions of pore cavity A from 5.3 Å × 6.4 Å to 4.8 Å × 7.9 Å (Fig. 2c). Simultaneously, rotation of the benzene ring and pyridine ring in the L2 ligand shifted its corresponding bond angles from 120.2° and 121.0° to 123.5° and 119.1°, respectively (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, ligand rotation modulated the bond angle between the ligand and metal node: In TYUT-18 (α), ∠L1-SBU-L2 ranged from 69.98° to 110.02°, whereas in TYUT-18 (β) it ranged from 63.84° to 116.16° (Supplementary Fig. 7). The neatly arrayed benzene rings and exposed oxygen atoms in pore cavity A created an aromatic-rich environment with a pore diameter of 6.4 Å, which permitted C2H6 to preferentially occupy it at low pressure, thereby imparting a higher initial adsorption capacity for C2H6. In addition, the 4.8 Å pore diameter of cavity B is highly matched with the cross-section of a C2H6 molecule, further triggering a preferential gate-opening effect for C2H6. The synergistic effect of the binary pore system and dynamic structural responsiveness ultimately led to typical step-shaped adsorption behavior during the C2H6 adsorption process.

Gas sorption analysis

Subsequently, we systematically studied the gas adsorption behavior of TYUT-18. The adsorption isotherms of C2H6 and C2H4 on TYUT-18 were measured at 273, 298 K and 313 K (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 8). The isotherm obtained for C2H6 exhibited a significant step-shaped feature with an adsorption capacity of 21.3 cm3 g−1 at 0.1 bar and 298 K, which could be attributed to the preferential occupation of large pore cavity A. As the pressure increased to 0.18 bar, the adsorption capacity rapidly increased to 35.6 cm3 g–1, accompanied by a phase transition from the β to α phase. When the pressure was further increased to 0.5 bar, the flexible pores were fully opened, and the adsorption capacity of TYUT-18 for C2H6 reached 59.7 cm3 g–1, which was significantly better than most reported flexible C2H6-selective MOFs, such as NKPOC-DS (43.7 cm3 g–1)44, ZIF-7 (35.4 cm3 g–1)28, FJI-W-66 (23.9 cm3 g–1)53 and IITKGP-39 (11.3 cm3 g–1)32, and second only to TYUT-17 (66.9 cm3 g–1)19 (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, compared to most reported robust C2H6-selective MOFs, TYUT-18 exhibits a moderately high C2H6 adsorption capacity (Supplementary Fig. 9). Subsequently, the saturated adsorption capacity at 1 bar reached 67.9 cm3 g–1. In contrast, the adsorption isotherm obtained for C2H4 did not show an obvious step-shaped feature; its structural phase transition occurred and was delayed until 0.48 bar, corresponding to an uptake of 43.5 cm3 g–1. The saturated uptake at 1 bar reached 60.0 cm3 g–1. At the same time, at 298 K and 1 bar, the C2H6/C2H4 uptake ratio of TYUT-18 reached 1.13 (Fig. 2g), which was better than most reported flexible C2H6-selective MOFs, such as ZIF-7 (1.04)28, FJI-W-66 (1.02)53, NTU-101-NH2 (1.03)31 and TYUT-17 (1.09)19, and second only to IITKGP-39 (1.47)32. When comparing with the robust C2H6-selective MOFs, although the uptake ratio of TYUT-18 is not outstanding, TYUT-18 still exhibits excellent separation performance, which may be closely related to its flexible mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 9). Upon heating to 313 K, both gases exhibited delayed phase transition pressures with C2H4 showing a more sluggish response compared to C2H6. The uptake at 1 bar decreased to 57.9 cm3 g–1 for C2H6 and 50.2 cm3 g–1 for C2H4 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Our results indicated that TYUT-18 was highly sensitive to C2H6 due to its binary pore system and reversible structural transition, thereby enabling the highly selective capture of C2H6.

We conducted in situ gas-loaded variable pressure PXRD experiments to systematically study the structural transformation mechanism of TYUT-18 induced by C2H6 and C2H4 under different pressure conditions. For C2H6 adsorption, during the large-pore filling stage, the PXRD pattern of TYUT-18 at a pressure of 0.13 bar retained the characteristic features of the β phase, as shown in Fig. 3a, b. Upon increasing the pressure to 0.18 bar, the framework underwent a phase transition and entered the small-pore filling stage, as evidenced by the gradual disappearance of the β phase diffraction peaks at 2θ = 8.2°, 14.1° and 22.7°, and the emergence of new peaks corresponding to the α phase at 2θ = 9.1° and 14.8°. Concurrently, the diffraction intensities at 2θ = 21.2°, 23.6° and 25.7° were markedly enhanced, further confirming the structural transformation from the β to α phase. During C2H6 desorption, the PXRD pattern reverted to the β phase when the pressure decreased to 0.13 bar, thereby confirming the reversible nature of the phase transformation in TYUT-18 under a C2H6 atmosphere. In contrast, in situ variable-pressure PXRD analysis of C2H4 at 298 K (Fig. 3d, e) revealed that a significantly higher pressure threshold was required to induce a phase transition compared to C2H6. A phase transition from the β to α phase was observed in the PXRD pattern when the pressure reached 0.48 bar. During desorption, the structure reverted to the β phase as the pressure decreased to 0.05 bar. These results well demonstrate that C2H6 could more effectively trigger the reversible phase transition in TYUT-18 at lower pressure, which was consistent with the distinct adsorption behaviors of C2H6 and C2H4 observed in our isothermal adsorption studies. Furthermore, the contour plot of in situ PXRD patterns (Fig. 3c, f) clearly depicts the dynamic structural evolution of TYUT-18 in C2H6 and C2H4 atmospheres that occurs with changes in pressure, demonstrating its preferential recognition ability for C2H6 and reversible structural phase transition behavior during the adsorption-desorption process.

The isosteric heat of adsorption (Qst) serves as a key indicator of the interaction strength between the adsorbent and adsorbate. Accordingly, we calculated the Qst values for C2H6 and C2H4 in TYUT-18 using the Clausius–Clapeyron equation (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). At zero coverage, the Qst values for C2H6 and C2H4 were 28.6 and 24.1 kJ mol–1, respectively, which confirmed the stronger binding affinity of TYUT-18 toward C2H6. Moreover, the moderate Qst value not only favoured the selective adsorption of C2H6, but also enabled efficient material regeneration under ambient conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12). In addition, kinetic measurements demonstrated that TYUT-18 exhibited excellent mass transfer characteristics (Supplementary Figs. 13–15 and Supplementary Table 6). At 298 K, with a pressurization rate of 100 mbar min–1, both C2H6 and C2H4 reached adsorption equilibrium in TYUT-18 within approximately 10 min. This adsorption equilibrium time is shorter than that of Cu(Qc)2 (13 min), MAF-7 (15 min), TYUT-12 (18 min) and ZIF-7 (40 min) (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). And due to the preferential flexible response of TYUT-18 to C2H6, its diffusion time constant (1.77 × 10–2 min–1) is greater than that of C2H4 (1.27 × 10–2 min–1). Desorption experiments further showed that both C2H6 and C2H4 could be completely desorbed from TYUT-18 within 10 min. This excellent kinetic behavior could be attributed to the synergistic effect of a binary pore system and dynamic structural response mechanism in TYUT-18; the binary pore system constructs multi-level precise recognition sites, and the reversible configuration regulation in the framework adsorption process reduces the diffusion resistance and accelerates the diffusion of the guest molecules. In summary, TYUT-18 combined high C2H6 selectivity adsorption, moderate adsorption heat, and fast adsorption kinetics, offering advantages for energy-efficient separation processes and demonstrating strong potential for industrial applications.

Gas-loaded SCXRD and computational simulation studies

These excellent thermodynamic and kinetic adsorption properties prompted an in-depth investigation into the adsorption mechanism. The dynamic pore filling mechanism of hydrocarbon molecules in the binary pore system was clearly elucidated using SCXRD analysis on TYUT-18 (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 3). The results revealed that C2H6 molecules followed the dynamic adsorption-desorption order of preferentially occupying the larger pore cavities then filling the small ones in TYUT-18. During the initial stage of adsorption, the C2H6 molecules preferentially and symmetrically occupied large pore cavity A, maintaining the β phase and forming TYUT-18(β)•0.25C2H6 (CCDC 2449695, Supplementary Data 3 and 11). When the adsorbed C2H6 in cavity A reached saturation, it triggered a phase transition from the β to α phase. Subsequently, the remaining C2H6 molecules sequentially filled small cavity B and reached thermodynamic saturation, thereby forming TYUT-18(α)•3C2H6 (CCDC 2473904, Supplementary Data 5 and 13). The desorption process followed the same sequence as the adsorption process. Initially, the C2H6 molecules in pore cavity A were first desorbed, while the framework remained in the α phase, forming TYUT-18(α)•1C2H6 (CCDC 2449696, Supplementary Data 4 and 12). Subsequently, the C2H6 molecules in pore cavity B were removed and the framework gradually returned to the TYUT-18 (β) phase. This process was accompanied by significant structural dynamics: the unit cell parameters along the a-axes and b-axes underwent significant changes and the L1 and L2 ligands underwent rotations, resulting in fine-tuning of the overall framework geometry; the L1 ligand angle change was particularly pronounced. Similarly, the dynamic filling mechanism of C2H4 in TYUT-18 was elucidated using a combination of in situ PXRD, in situ SCXRD, and Rietveld structure refinement (Supplementary Figs. 18–20 and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). The adsorption–desorption sequence of C2H4 was similar to that of C2H6. The C2H4 molecules initially preferentially occupied pore cavity A (β phase). When the adsorbed C2H4 in cavity A reached saturation, the framework underwent a phase transition to the α phase. Subsequently, the C2H4 molecules filled pore cavity B until equilibrium was established. During desorption, the C2H4 in pore cavity A was first removed (maintaining the α phase), followed by gradual desorption of the C2H4 in pore cavity B, causing the framework to return to the β phase. A systematic elucidation of the dynamic adsorption sequence of C2H6 and C2H4 not only deepens our understanding of the adsorption mechanisms governing flexible MOFs but also provides an important theoretical basis for their structural design and functional optimization.

We systematically analysed the adsorption interactions of the three gas-loaded single crystal structures in order to further elucidate how the TYUT-18 pore chemical environment regulated the C2H6 adsorption process: TYUT-18(β)•0.25C2H6, TYUT-18(α)•3C2H6, and TYUT-18(α)•1C2H6 (Fig. 5). In TYUT-18(β)•0.25C2H6, each C2H6 molecule resided within pore cavity A, establishing four C–H···π interactions (3.33–3.70 Å) and seven C–H···H van der Waals interactions (2.88–3.97 Å) with the benzene ring of ligand L1, along with two C–H···O interactions (3.11 Å and 2.97 Å) with the exposed O atoms (Fig. 5a). In TYUT-18(α)•3C2H6, the C2H6 molecules were distributed across both pore cavities A and B. Within pore cavity A, each C2H6 molecule formed five C–H···π interactions (3.27–3.95 Å) and nine C–H···H van der Waals interactions (2.70–3.98 Å) with ligand L1, together with one C–H···O interaction (3.31 Å) with the exposed O atom (Fig. 5c). Within pore cavity B, each C2H6 molecule formed four C–H···π interactions (3.33–3.93 Å) and fifteen C–H···H van der Waals interactions (2.33–3.86 Å) with the benzene rings in ligands L1 and L2, and one C–H···N interaction (3.93 Å) with the N atom of ligand L2 (Fig. 5b). In TYUT-18(α)•1C2H6, each C2H6 molecule was located within pore cavity B, establishing three C–H···π interactions (3.31–3.70 Å) and sixteen C–H···H van der Waals contacts (2.90–3.60 Å) with the benzene rings in ligands L1 and L2, together with one C–H···N interaction (3.82 Å) involving the nitrogen atom of ligand L2 (Fig. 5d). These observations demonstrated that the C2H6 molecules were bound to the framework through multiple weak interactions across various C2H6-loaded states. Similarly, our structural analysis of the C2H4-loaded single crystals [TYUT-18(β)•1C2H4 (Supplementary Data 7), TYUT-18(α)•2C2H4 (Supplementary Data 8), and TYUT-18(α)•0.25C2H4 (CCDC 2449694, Supplementary Data 6 and 14)] revealed that the C2H4 molecules in pore cavities A and B engaged in fewer C–H···π, C–H···N, and C–H···O interactions when compared to C2H6 with longer interaction distances and correspondingly weaker binding strengths (Supplementary Fig. 21). This disparity clearly demonstrated that TYUT-18 exhibited a higher binding affinity toward C2H6, thereby clarifying the potential reasons for preferential adsorption of C2H6 in C2H6/C2H4 separation.

The in situ gas-loading SCXRD analysis presented above indicates that TYUT-18 exhibits sequential adsorption behavior, preferentially occupying the large pore cavities before filling the smaller ones. Subsequently, we performed DFT calculations and Monte Carlo simulations in order to elucidate this behavior. In our model, the deformation energy (Edef) is defined as the energy difference between the closed-pore (β) and open-pore (α) states in the absence of guest molecules, whereas the interaction energy (Eint) represents the energy reduction resulting from guest-host interactions due to the intrinsic flexibility of TYUT-1840,54. Supplementary Fig. 22 shows the Eint of C2H6 adsorption within the small pores is comparatively higher (–25.84 kJ mol–1). However, the Edef required for transition from the β phase to the α phase is ~18.79 kJ mol–1. Under low-pressure conditions, C2H6 is preferentially adsorbed in the deformation-free large pores of the β phase (–17.90 kJ mol–1). As the large pores gradually reach saturation, the cumulative energy released from subsequent adsorption into the small pores surpasses Edef, thereby inducing a structural transition from the β to α phase, which initiates filling of the small pores. This energy balance explains the order of large pore adsorption followed by small pore filling observed in our experimental isotherms. In contrast, the Eint of C2H4 adsorption during this process is weaker (–11.96 kJ mol–1 within the large pores and –19.28 kJ mol–1 within the small pores), rendering framework deformation more challenging under identical conditions and necessitating, which necessitates higher pressures to trigger the α to β phase transition (Supplementary Fig. 23). When considering that TYUT-18 reverts to the α phase upon saturation, DFT calculations were performed on the geometrically optimized TYUT-18 (α) framework in order to identify the adsorption sites. Supplementary Fig. 24 shows the C2H6 molecules form more and stronger van der Waals forces and hydrogen bond interactions with TYUT-18 than C2H4, which is consistent with our in situ gas-loading SCXRD analysis results.

Dynamic breakthrough experiments

Given the preferential recognition and high absorption of C2H6 by TYUT-18, we performed further fixed-bed breakthrough experiments in order to assess its performance under realistic dynamic separation conditions. Breakthrough tests were conducted at 298 K and 313 K using a gas flow rate of 2 mL min–1 with 50/50 and 10/90 (v/v) C2H6/C2H4 mixtures, respectively (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 26). The breakthrough curve showed that C2H4 passed through the column first and C2H6 was retained in the bed due to its preferential adsorption by TYUT-18. In the C2H6/C2H4 (v/v = 50/50) system, TYUT-18 achieved a maximum separation time of 18 min g–1 at 298 K and high purity C2H4 ( > 99.99%) could be collected within this time interval, which demonstrated its excellent selective separation ability for C2H6 (Fig. 6a). In addition, compared to the previously reported single pore flexible-robust TYUT-17 (Supplementary Fig. 25), TYUT-18 exhibits a significantly shorter co-adsorption time under the same dynamic breakthrough conditions, indicating that the synergistic effect of its binary porous structure and flexibility effectively weakens the competitive adsorption behavior of C2H4 under dynamic conditions. This shortens the overall separation cycle, thereby improving its overall separation efficiency. Furthermore, their desorption curves (Supplementary Fig. 27) show that both C2H6 and C2H4 can achieve complete desorption and regeneration within 8 min g–1 at 298 K. When the temperature increased to 313 K, TYUT-18 could still maintain its good separation performance for the C2H6/C2H4 mixture (v/v = 50/50), indicating the effectiveness of its flexible adsorption mechanism over a wide temperature range. Based on the above results, we further investigated the separation ability of TYUT-18 in a C2H6/C2H4 (v/v = 10/90) mixture. Figure 6b and Supplementary Fig. 26 show TYUT-18 achieved separation times of 28 min g–1 and 22 min g–1 at 298 K and 313 K, respectively, and high purity C2H4 ( > 99.99%) could be collected within these time intervals, which demonstrated its excellent separation ability. To verify the separation capability of TYUT-18 for low-concentration C2H6 impurities, we conducted separation experiments using C2H6/C2H4 mixtures with volume ratios of 5/95 and 1/99 (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29). The experimental results confirm that TYUT-18 can effectively remove low-concentration C2H6 impurities, demonstrating excellent separation performance. In addition, five consecutive breakthrough cycles were performed for both gas mixtures (Fig. 6, d and Supplementary Figs. 30–33) and the results demonstrated that TYUT-18 retained excellent cyclic stability at both 298 K and 313 K.

Based on the excellent separation performance of TYUT-18 for C2H6/C2H4 gas mixtures, we further conducted the quantitative analysis of the product yield and purity using breakthrough curves to intuitively evaluate the C2H4 separation effect of TYUT-18. At 298 K, TYUT-18 enabled the recovery of C2H4 from a C2H6/C2H4 (v/v = 10/90) mixture, affording a highly pure product (99.99%) with a yield reaching 50.4 L kg–1 (Supplementary Figs. 34-36). This performance markedly surpassed that of several representative C2H6-selective adsorbents, including TYUT-12 (24.5 L kg–1)19, Zn(ad)(int) (36.2 L kg–1)55, MUF-15 (20.3 L kg–1)22, JNU-2 (27.5 L kg–1)56, ZJU-120a (10.8 L kg–1)57, and ZIF-7 (7.2 L kg−1)28. Although its performance is slightly lower than that of Fe2(O2)(dobdc) (72.3 L kg–1) and TYUT-17 (77.4 L kg–1), it fully demonstrated the application potential of TYUT-18 in C2H6/C2H4 separation (Supplementary Table 7). In order to further verify the feasibility of deep purification, a ternary C2H2/C2H4/C2H6 (1/98/1, v/v/v) gas mixture was designed to mimic a complex industrial feedstock with a total flow rate of 2 mL min–1 and employed to evaluate the capacity for producing ultra-high purity C2H4 ( > 99.995%). Supplementary Fig. 37 illustrates that C2H4 was the first component to elute from the packed column at 21 min with its purity rapidly increasing to 99.999% by 24 min. The C2H6 and C2H2 were subsequently eluted and detected at about 29 min and 30 min, respectively. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis (detection limits: 0.001% for C2H6 and 0.1 ppm for C2H2) confirmed that the concentrations of C2H6 and C2H2 in the ultra-pure C2H4 product were below 0.005% and 0.1 ppm, respectively. Ultimately, TYUT-18 enabled the separation of ultra-high purity C2H4 ( > 99.995%) with a yield of up to 15.7 L kg–1. In three breakthrough cycle tests of C2H2/C2H4/C2H6 (1/98/1, v/v/v), the separation performance remained stable, indicating that there is no irreversible retention of C2H2 in the pores of TYUT-18 (Supplementary Fig. 37b). Moreover, regeneration experiments of C2H2 showed that C2H2 can be completely and reversibly desorbed from TYUT-18 (Supplementary Fig. 38). In summary, these results fully demonstrate the excellent cycling stability and regeneration performance of TYUT-18, providing a reliable and efficient strategy for the deep purification of C2H4, and showing promising prospects for industrial applications.

In actual industrial separation processes, a certain amount of moisture is inevitably encountered, typically originating from high-temperature processes such as steam cracking and ethane dehydrogenation. The presence of water can lead to competitive adsorption between the gas and water, thereby reducing the hydrothermal stability of the adsorbent and weakening its separation performance. To evaluate the reliability of TYUT-18 in humid environments, we first conducted room temperature water vapor adsorption tests. The results showed that under a relative humidity of 40%, TYUT-18 absorbed only 0.008 g g−1 of water, exhibiting excellent hydrophobic properties. The water contact angle test further confirmed its excellent hydrophobicity (Supplementary Fig. 39a). This is mainly attributed to the fact that TYUT-18 is composed of dense aromatic rings, forming a highly hydrophobic microenvironment. Simultaneously, the absence of open metal sites or strongly polar functional groups in the framework results in weak interactions between the adsorbent and water molecules, thus ensuring the TYUT-18 exhibits excellent resistance to water competitive adsorption under humid conditions. Furthermore, the positions and intensities of the characteristic peaks in the PXRD pattern after water absorption (Supplementary Fig. 39b) did not change, indicating that the crystal structure of the material remains stable under humid conditions. Based on this, we further conducted dynamic breakthrough experiments at 298 K and 1 bar using C2H4/C2H6 mixtures (v/v = 50/50) with different relative humidities (RH = 40% and 70%) (Supplementary Figs. 39c and 38d). Notably, even under high humidity (70% RH) conditions, the breakthrough time of TYUT-18 remained almost identical to that under dry conditions, demonstrating its excellent stability in high-humidity environments and showcasing highly competitive industrial application prospects and environmental adaptability.

Stability study

Adsorbents must exhibit both excellent structural stability and efficient regenerability for practical application. Therefore, a systematic investigation was carried out from three perspectives in order to comprehensively assess the application viability of TYUT-18: Long-term structural stability, solvent resistance, and acid-base tolerance. After five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles, the PXRD pattern of TYUT-18 remained unchanged when compared to the original sample and no loss in its C2H6 adsorption capacity was observed (Supplementary Fig. 40). Furthermore, after storage in air for six months, the PXRD pattern of TYUT-18 remained identical to that of the fresh sample. In addition, its crystalline structure was preserved even after multiple adsorption and breakthrough experiments (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 41). In the chemical stability test, the intensity of the characteristic diffraction peaks observed in the PXRD pattern did not change after TYUT-18 was soaked in solvents of different polarities such as dichloromethane, methanol and ethanol for 7 d or soaked in acidic and alkaline solutions with pH in the range of 1–13 for 48 h, which indicated that it could maintain high crystallinity in polar/non-polar solvents over a wide pH range. At the same time, its C2H6 adsorption performance was almost unaffected and the adsorption capacity remained stable even after being soaked in acidic (pH = 1) or alkaline (pH = 13) solutions (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Figs. 42–45). A photograph of TYUT-18 single crystals obtained using microscopy revealed that the TYUT-18 crystals retained their intact particle structure after solvent exchange, activation, adsorption testing, and soaking in solutions at pH = 3 and 13, exhibiting a smooth surface without cracks (Fig. 6f). In summary, TYUT-18 exhibited excellent structural stability and regenerability. It not only withstood harsh chemical environments, but also demonstrated strong reusability, laying a robust foundation for its application in industrial-scale continuous separation processes.

Discussion

In summary, we have constructed a flexible, robust MOF, designated TYUT-18. Its binary pore system, combined with the flexible deformation of its framework, provided excellent C2H6 separation performance. Among them, the large pore cavities provided preferential adsorption sites for C2H6 molecules, while the small pore cavities achieved precise molecular recognition due to the high matching of its pore size with the cross-sectional size of C2H4. At 298 K, TYUT-18 exhibited distinct gate-opening pressures for C2H6 and C2H4, enabling the preferential recognition of C2H6 and demonstrating excellent C2H6/C2H4 separation performance. Using a combination of SCXRD analyses and DFT calculations, the pore filling behavior in the binary pore system around the flexible porous materials was clearly discovered, providing an in-depth understanding of the guest adsorption-desorption process and a pathway for structural design and functional optimization of high-performance adsorbents.

Methods

Synthesis of TYUT-18

A mixture Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (29.7 mg, 0.1 mmol), 1,3-di(4 carboxyphenyl)benzene (33 mg, 0.1 mmol) and 1,4-di(pyridin-4-yl)benzene (23.2 mg, 0.1 mmol) were dissolved in 8 mL DMF of 20 mL vial. After placed the vial in 373 K oven for 2 days, colourless plate-type single crystals obtained. The contents were cooled to room temperature, filtered, and washed with fresh DMF a couple of times.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction

The SCXRD data of TYUT-18 (α) and TYUT-18 (β) were collected on the Bruker D8 VENTURE TXS PHOTON II diffractometer. For in situ SCXRD measurements, the TYUT-18 sample was first purged with N2 at 423 K for 12 h to completely remove solvent molecules from the pores. Then, the target feed gas was introduced to the set pressure and equilibrated for 6 h. The specific conditions are as follows: for TYUT-18(β)•0.25C2H6 and TYUT-18(α)•3C2H6, the C2H6 gas was pressurized to 0.13 bar and 1 bar respectively using the Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyzer; for TYUT-18(α)•1C2H6 and TYUT-18(α)•0.25C2H4, the C2H6 and C2H4 gases were first pressurized to 1 bar, and then desorbed to 0.4 bar and 0.6 bar respectively. The SCXRD data of TYUT-18(β)•0.25C2H6, TYUT-18(α)•1C2H6 and TYUT-18(α)•0.25C2H4 were collected on the Bruker D8 VENTURE TXS PHOTON II diffractometer. The SCXRD data of TYUT-18(α)•3C2H6 was collected Single crystal X-ray diffraction data were collected at the Shanghai Synchrotron Light Source Line BL10U2 station, and station equipped with a Dectris Eiger 16 M, S/N E-32-0111 surface detector, respectively. The structure was solved by direct methods using the SHELXTL software package and further refined with different Fourier syntheses. All non-hydrogen atoms on the framework were refined anisotropically; all hydrogen atoms were generated geometrically and refined in the riding mode. All the phenyl rings were treated with rigid constraints.

In situ PXRD measurements

A customized gas partial pressure control system was developed (Supplementary Fig. 52). To precisely regulate gas composition, two mass flow controllers (MFCs) with different flow ranges (100 sccm and 5 sccm for low partial pressures below 5%) were connected to either the C2H6 or C2H4 gas cylinders. An additional pair of MFCs was linked to a nitrogen (N2) cylinder, serving as the purge gas for sample activation. The gas mixture was then introduced into an in situ PXRD chamber (Bruker D8 Advance, Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5418 Å). Diffraction patterns were collected over the 2θ range of 4–40° with a total acquisition time of 5 min at room temperature about 298 K. All stainless-steel valves and fittings were sourced from Shanghai X-tec Fluid Technology Co., Ltd., and the MFCs were supplied by Alicat Scientific (a Halma company).

PXRD refinement

The refined package is TOPAS V5.0, which describes the background using 24th-order Chebyshev polynomials and the peak shapes using pseudo-Voigt peak shapes. Preferred orientation is described using the spherical cofunction of order 4. The refinement was performed sequentially on the scale factor, atomic coordinates, isotropic displacement parameters, site occupancies, and preferred orientation. Guest molecules were introduced into the host framework model as rigid bodies, and their possible positions were determined through a simulated annealing algorithm.

Density functional theory calculations

The structures of TYUT-18 as well as their complexes with C2 molecules were optimized at GFN2-xTB level by using a tight-binding quantum chemical xTB method performed with the ASE software suite. The energy difference of the TYUT-18 framework from closed phase to open phase was calculated by DFT method. To gain deeper insights into the energy change in the adsorption process, we evaluated the energy landscape in the C2H6 and C2H4 adsorption processes on TYUT-18. The adsorption position of the guest molecules was estimated using classical Monte-Carlo simulations, then optimized using DFT calculations with the dispersion-corrected Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional (PBE-D3), and the interaction energy of the gas (Eint) at each adsorption step was then calculated.

Adsorption

Adsorption isotherms for C2H6 and C2H4 at 298 K and 313 K were measured using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyzer. Prior to measurement, the sample was activated under high vacuum at 423 K for 10 h until no further mass loss was detected. High-purity C2H6 and C2H4 gases (both 99.995%) were employed for the adsorption studies.

Breakthrough experiments

The breakthrough experiments are performed on a homemade set-up involving two gas columns; one is linked to a stainless-steel column loaded with activated TYUT-18, while the other served as a blank control to stabilize the gas flow (Supplementary Fig. 53). Both columns had an inner diameter of 6 mm. The temperature of the adsorption bed is maintained at 298 K or 313 K using a thermostat. The outlet and inlet flow rates and pressures of the C2H6/C2H4 mixtures are regulated by a mass flow meter and a pressure gauge, respectively. Gas from the outlet of the adsorption column is monitored by gas chromatography (Agilent 490) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). Before the experiment, the adsorption column is heated at 423 K whilst purging with Ar, and then, the inlet gas is switched to the mixture to be tested at a total gas flow of 2 or 8 mL min–1. For the cycling experiments, the sample is regenerated by in situ purging with Ar at a flow rate of 50 mL min–1 at 298 K for 1 h. The sample mass of TYUT-18 was 0.6 g.
