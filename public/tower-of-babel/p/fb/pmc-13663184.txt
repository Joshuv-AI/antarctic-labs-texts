The role of the dentist in promoting HPV vaccination in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ana Rita Fernandes Teixeira, José Frias-Bulhosa, Juan Antonio Ruiz Roca, Otília Pereira-Lopes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral health professionals may play a key role in promoting HPV vaccination among adults through counseling and health education. Understanding their impact and the effectiveness of educational interventions is crucial for public health strategies.ObjectivesTo assess the influence of oral health professionals on adherence to HPV vaccination in adults and to identify the effectiveness of educational strategies aimed at improving their counseling practices.Eligibility criteriaThis review included cross-sectional and interventional studies evaluating adult-focused HPV vaccination counseling provided by oral health professionals.Information sourcesSearches were conducted in PubMed, EBSCO, the Cochrane Library, Science Direct, Wiley, and TRIP Database from inception to April 21, 2025.Methods of synthesisData were analyzed using a random-effects meta-analysis model in RStudio (version 4.2.2) to estimate pooled proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsA total of 10 studies were included, involving 9,575 participants, of which 3 studies implemented educational interventions. Prior to educational interventions, many dentists demonstrated limited knowledge about HPV and its link to oropharyngeal cancer, and only a few routinely discussed HPV vaccination with patients. Post-intervention, there was a notable improvement in professionals' knowledge, confidence, and frequency of patient counseling.DiscussionEducational interventions were effective in enhancing dentist's preparedness to address HPV vaccination. However, barriers such as lack of privacy, discomfort discussing the topic, and limited prior training were also reported.LimitationsHeterogeneity in study design, limited geographic diversity, and variability in intervention approaches were noted.ConclusionsOral health professionals can significantly contribute to adult HPV vaccination efforts through targeted counseling, especially when supported by ongoing education and institutional strategies that address systemic barriers.RegistrationPROSPERO ID: CRD42024617902.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08517-x.

Introduction

The global rise in HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancers, particularly in high-income countries where traditional risk factors such as tobacco use have declined, marks a significant shift in the epidemiology of these malignancies, with HPV now identified as a leading cause [1, 2]. This trend highlights that traditional cancer prevention strategies, such as tobacco control, are insufficient for this specific malignancy, underscoring a critical public health challenge that necessitates urgent preventive measures. Expanding vaccination efforts through the integration of non-traditional settings, such as dental clinics, is essential to enhance vaccine uptake and reduce the incidence of HPV-related diseases, including cervical, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers [3, 4].

Despite substantial global efforts to implement vaccination programs, progress remains uneven. Seventeen years after initial approval of the HPV vaccine, only 55% of the World Health Organization (WHO) Member States have incorporated it as a primary prophylaxis measure, and only 33 out of those 107 countries offer gender-neutral programs that include all adolescents, regardless of sex or gender identity [5, 6]. In Europe, 77% of countries have introduced the HPV vaccination, typically through national immunization programs. However, implementation timelines vary significantly across nations, reflecting different public health priorities and resource allocations [7].

Oral health professionals (OHPs), including dentists and hygienists, occupy a unique position in promoting public health due to their regular access to the oral cavity and consistent interactions with patients during routine care [8]. In recent years, the scope of dental practice has increasingly incorporated preventive health discussions, providing opportunities to address topics such as HPV vaccination, particularly in relation to oropharyngeal cancer, a condition whose prevention is often undervalued [9]. In 2019, 65.5% of adults aged 18–64 in the United States reported a dental visit in the previous 12 months, which may have been with a dentist, a hygienist, or another OHP [10].

Notably, in 2018 approximately 28.2 million Americans (8.3% of the population) visited a dental professional without attending a medical visit, representing a unique opportunity to reach individuals who may have limited interaction with primary care [11]. Despite the strategic access OHPs have to patients, this potential remains largely untapped in the face of suboptimal adult vaccination rates, which are driven by misinformation, myths about vaccine safety, sociocultural barriers, and inconsistent provider recommendations [12]. For example, data from the 2018 United States National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), a nationally representative study of adults aged 19–45, showed that the estimated proportion receiving at least one dose of HPV vaccine was 36.3% (females: 51.5%, males: 21.2%, p < 0.001), with even lower rates among mid-adults [(defined as individuals aged 27 to 45 years, aligning with the NHIS age categorization and current FDA/CDC catch-up vaccination guidelines) (females: 15.8%, males: 3.2%, p < 0.001)] [13]. The persistence of sex-based disparities reflects the historical prioritization of female vaccination and highlights the need for targeted strategies addressing all genders.

Given their strategic position, OHPs can play a critical role in bridging gaps in HPV vaccination coverage, especially for populations at higher risk for HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancers. Evidence indicates that many unvaccinated individuals visit dental practices annually, creating opportunities for OHPs to provide educational recommendations on vaccination [14]. Notably, data from 2018 show that approximately 28.2 million Americans (8.3% of the population) visited a dental professional without attending a medical visit, representing a substantial subset of the population that could be reached for vaccination counseling through the dental setting ecosystem.

Although several individual studies have examined the role of OHPs in promoting HPV-related health, few comprehensive and up-to-date reviews have synthesized their effectiveness in increasing vaccination rates among adults. Previous reviews have often focused on adolescent vaccination [15] or on health care providers in general, with limited emphasis on dental professional [16, 17]. Furthermore, earlier syntheses, such as Walker et al. [18], predate the COVID-19 pandemic, a global health crisis that substantially altered public perceptions of vaccination. Despite the widespread acceptance of COVID-19 vaccines, HPV vaccination rates among adults remain disproportionately low, suggesting that hesitancy is influenced by distinct factors that require updated analysis in the post-pandemic context. More recently, Chan et al. [19] explored the perspectives of OHPs and students regarding HPV-related knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors, their review primarily focused on provider perspectives, with limited attention to how adult patients perceive and respond to HPV vaccination counseling in dental settings.

To address these gaps, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the role of OHPs in promoting HPV vaccination in adults, identify barriers to their involvement, assess the impact of education interventions, and highlight their potential to enhance vaccine uptake through oral healthcare services.

Materials and methods

Protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [20].

The research protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Review (PROSPERO), under ID: CRD42024618902, to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Eligibility criteria

This review included studies that examined the role of OHPs in promoting HPV vaccination among adults. Eligible participants comprised dentists, dental hygienists, dentistry and dental hygiene students and adult patients aged 18 years or older. The inclusion of students aimed to capture the impact of early education and training on preparedness to counsel patients, while adult patients were included to assess their receptiveness to HPV vaccination advice delivered in dental settings.

Given the exploratory and public health-oriented nature of this review, the PICO framework was intentionally expanded to reflect the dental setting ecosystem, ensuring a comprehensive overview of vaccination-related perspectives within this unique environment. Studies were included if they involved adult participants (vaccinated or unvaccinated), were available in full text, and published in English, Portuguese, or Spanish. Studies focusing on other health professionals (e.g., physicians, nurses), child or adolescent populations, or publication types such as letters to the editor, reviews, meta-analyses, and opinion pieces were excluded.

Studies exclusively addressing ‘‘intention to recommend’’ HPV vaccination without evaluating actual counseling practices or educational interventions were also excluded to maintain focus on tangible behaviors and outcomes.

After selecting and evaluating the 10 included studies, a thematic analysis was performed with the aim of identifying recursive patterns related to the role of the dentist in recommending HPV vaccination. The results and discussion sections of each study were read in full by two reviewers independently. Relevant information was highlighted, coded, and organized into a thematic matrix. The analysis followed an inductive approach, allowing themes to emerge from the study data, without pre-established categories.

Eighteen articles were excluded because they focused primarily on populations under 18 years of age, such as adolescents and children, or on caregivers of these groups. Although caregivers are adults, these studies did not address the adult populations relevant to this review, making them incompatible with its objectives.

Nineteen studies presented a broad focus on vaccination, without specific reference to the HPV vaccine, making it impossible to extract relevant data for this analysis.

Thirteen studies did not specifically focus on the role of OHPs in HPV vaccination, generically addressing other health professionals or contexts unrelated to dental practice.

Eight articles did not include outcomes related to vaccine recommendation, such as intention to recommend, attitudes, or behaviors of OHPs, and were therefore excluded as they did not directly contribute to the objective of this review.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using RStudio (version 4.2.2). A random-effects model was applied to calculate the pooled proportion and 95% confidence interval (CI) for all analyzed outcomes. The meta package in R was used to apply the metaprop function.

Heterogeneity among included studies was assessed using the I2 statistic, τ2, and Cochran’s Q test. The I2 statistic quantifies the percentage of total variation between studies due to heterogeneity rather than chance. Values of I2 below 40% were considered low, 40–75% moderate, and above 75% substantial heterogeneity. These thresholds guided interpretation and model selection but were considered in the context of clinical and methodological diversity across studies.

In our analysis, the pooled estimates showed considerable heterogeneity, with I2 values ranging from 0 to 97%. For example, in the analysis of barriers to HPV vaccination, subgroups such as “Lack of knowledge” (I2 = 97%, p < 0.001), “Privacy concerns” (I2 = 95%, p < 0.001), and “Not comfortable” (I2 = 96%, p < 0.001) showed particularly high heterogeneity. Conversely, the subgroup “Religious reasons” showed no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p = 0.82). Overall heterogeneity across studies was also high (I2 = 98%, p < 0.001). Similar variability was observed in the knowledge and attitudes section, with I2 values ranging from 61 to 95%.

These findings justified the use of a random-effects model with a random-intercept logistic regression approach and maximum-likelihood estimation for τ2, which appropriately accounts for between-study variability. Despite heterogeneity, meta-analytic pooling of proportions has been widely used in public health research to summarize patterns across diverse observational studies. In this review, the meta-analysis is intended to provide a pooled estimate of general trends rather than a precise effect size. Consequently, the narrative synthesis is employed as the primary tool for interpretation, ensuring that the nuances of different study contexts and qualitative patterns are fully captured alongside the statistical estimates.

Assessment of publication bias

Potential publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of a funnel plot generated from the proportion estimates and standard errors of the studies included in the meta-analysis. Egger’s linear regression test for funnel plot asymmetry was also performed to evaluate the presence of small-study effects.

Given the variation in study outcomes and participant perspectives, the funnel plot should be interpreted with caution, as outcome heterogeneity may confound the visual symmetry typically used to assess publication bias. Specifically, when statistical heterogeneity is high, the included studies likely represent distinct populations or methodological frameworks rather than a single ‘‘true’’ effect. In such instances, an asymmetrical distribution in the funnel plot may arise from these inherent clinical and methodological differences between studies, rather than indicating the presence of publication bias alone.

Results

Searches of databases and other sources identified 2926 records. After removing 2787 irrelevant articles, 139 articles remained for further review. Twenty-three duplicates were removed, leaving 116 records for eligibility assessment. Of these, 31 were excluded for not meeting the general inclusion criteria, 48 due to lack of access or review format, and 27 not addressing the association of interest. All included studies were conducted in the United States, covering diverse geographic regions such as New England, Arizona, Michigan, Iowa and Pennsylvania, as well as specific populations including American Indian and Alaska Native communities.

Most studies recruited OHPs, including dentists, hygienists, and dental students. Only three studies included other groups: adults aged ≥ 18 years from the general population or university students enrolled in health-related courses [21–23]. In these cases, inclusion served to assess the adult public’s knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to receive HPV vaccination recommendations from OHPs, as well as the future intentions of students who may later enter clinical practice. Specifically, Steinbaum et al. [21] examined adult patient’s attitudes toward HPV vaccination and their perceptions of the dentist’s role in vaccine advocacy. Naavaal et al. [22] assessed knowledge and perceptions about HPV and preventive counseling among adults aged ≥ 18 years. Merrell et al. [23] focused on the comfort levels of university students enrolled in a health-related course future OHPs when discussing HPV vaccination. In these three studies, a purely observational cross-sectional study was conducted, without any educational intervention or training component.

Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 75 years, though most fell between 30 and 60 years. Studies involving dental students typically included younger participants (18–19 years) [23, 26]. Across studies, the majority of participants were female. Notably, all dental hygienists in the included samples were women [24, 27]. This reflects current workforce demographics and may be relevant given that women are more likely to have been vaccinated and to possess higher awareness of HPV-related risks, partly due to historical public health campaigns focused on cervical cancer prevention.

Ethnicity data, when reported, indicated that most participants were Caucasian, followed by African American, Asian and Hispanic individuals. Some studies targeted specific populations, such as Alaska Natives and American Indians [28, 29]. For example, Salous et al. [29] examined OHPs from Native American and Alaska Native communities, assessing their knowledge and comfort levels in discussing HPV vaccination, while Pampena et al. [28] evaluated the impact of educational interventions in these populations. These studies provide valuable insights into cultural and regional differences in vaccination-related counseling.

Across the ten studies, several key themes emerged regarding the role of OHPs in HPV vaccination counseling. Awareness of the link between HPV, oral health, and oropharyngeal cancer risk was limited among both patients and professionals. In North America, only 32.8% of adults considered the HPV vaccine ‘‘very important’’ for disease prevention a proportion higher among those aware of its link to oral health. In Steinbaum et al. [21], 93% of respondents reported being informed about COVID-19 vaccination, compared to 50% for HPV vaccination. Just half of participants knew that HPV vaccination could prevent certain head and neck cancers. Barriers to counseling

Data on barriers to discussing HPV vaccination were extracted from all studies reporting them, then grouped by conceptual similarity. Pooled meta-analytic estimates were calculated when outcome definitions and response formats were comparable. The most common barrier was lack of privacy in the clinical setting (44%, 95% CI [37%—71%]), followed closely by limited HPV-related knowledge (43%, 95% CI [25%—63%]). Other barriers included time constraints (38%, 95% CI [22%—55%]), discomfort discussing the topic (11%, 95% CI [7%—16%]), safety concerns (3%, 95% CI [1%—6%]), and religious objections (2%, 95% CI [1%—4%]) (Fig. 2).

Educational interventions

Of the ten studies included in this review, only three specifically evaluated educational interventions targeting OHPs [28–30]. These interventions, delivered through lectures, workshops or digital modules, aimed to improve knowledge about HPV, its transmission, and the role of vaccination in cancer prevention. Pre and post-intervention assessments consistently demonstrated improvements in participants’ knowledge, comfort, and willingness to discuss HPV vaccination with patients.

In these studies, OHPs answered a questionnaire before the intervention and a post-lecture questionnaire to assess whether the training improved their knowledge. For example, regarding the first question, “Do you think HPV infection is rare?”, the percentage of those who answered ‘‘I don’t know’’ decreased from 9% prior to the intervention to 1% after the intervention. For the question “Do you think HPV is a sexually transmitted infection?”, those who answered ‘‘I don’t know’’ decreased from 5 to 1%. Similarly, for the question “Do you think that HPV would go away on its own without treatment?”, the percentage of those who answered ‘‘I don’t know’’ decreased from 12 to 2%.

For the question “Do you think that an HPV infection can be prevented?”, the percentage of those who answered "I don’t know" decreased from 6 to 1%. Additionally, for the question “Do you think that an HPV infection can cause genital warts?”, the percentage of those who answered "I don’t know" decreased significantly from 21 to 1%.

Moreover, for the question “How comfortable do you feel talking to patients about the HPV vaccine?”, the percentage of those who answered ‘‘not comfortable at all’’ decreased from 9 to 4%.

Finally, for the question “HPV vaccine can reduce or prevent genital warts?”, the percentage of those who answered ‘‘Yes’’ increased from 58 to 85%.

The meta-analysis of the pre- and post-intervention data regarding professional knowledge is summarized in Fig. 3, illustrating the shift in the prevalence of correct responses across the evaluated studies (Fig. 3).

Potential publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of a funnel plot generated from the proportion estimates and standard errors of the nine studies included in the final analysis (Fig. 4). One study (Steinbaum et al. [21]) was excluded from the funnel plot due to insufficient data to calculate a valid event proportion. Egger’s linear regression test for funnel plot asymmetry produced a t-statistic of 0.50 with 7 degrees of freedom and a p-value of 0.6299. Discussion

Risk of bias evaluation

The methodological quality of the included studies varied, with five out of ten articles assessed as having a moderate risk of bias, mainly due to the absence of standardized measurement criteria, insufficient description of participants and settings, and lack of control for confounding factors [21–23, 27, 29]. For example, Kline et al. [27] did not use objective criteria to measure outcomes or address confounders clearly. In contrast, the other five studies showed low risk of bias, indicating higher reliability and relevance [24–26, 28, 30].

All studies were cross-sectional, which limits causal inference. The absence of control groups and inconsistent management of confounding variables further restricts internal validity. Additionally, the predominance of studies conducted in the United States limits the generalizability of findings to other geographic or cultural contexts.

Regarding publication bias, the visual inspection of the funnel plot and Egger’s test (p = 0.6299) indicated no significant evidence of publication bias, suggesting that the results are unlikely to be influenced by small-study effects. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The substantial heterogeneity observed across study outcomes and participant perspectives may confound the visual symmetry typically used to assess publication bias.

Furthermore, the high statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 98%) represents an important limitation. Methodological variations – such as differences in study design, target populations, and data collection tools – limit the generalizability of the findings. Consequently, the meta-analytic estimates should be regarded as indicative of overall trends rather than precise effect sizes, reinforcing the importance of the narrative synthesis for a comprehensive interpretation of the role of OHP in HPV vaccination.

Barriers to HPV vaccination counseling among oral health professionals

This review reveals several significant barriers faced by OHPs when counseling adults on HPV vaccination. The most frequently cited obstacles include lack of privacy in dental offices (reported by 44% of participants) and limited knowledge regarding HPV and its vaccination (67%). These findings underscore the need for multifaceted interventions addressing both the physical environment and the educational preparation of OHPs.

Educational interventions have demonstrated positive effects, with training programs increasing dentist’s confidence to discuss HPV vaccination from 26.2% feeling prepared before intervention to 67.5% after [30]. This suggests that targeted training can effectively reduce knowledge gaps and discomfort when approaching this sensitive topic. Together, these findings indicate significant knowledge deficits among OHPs regarding HPV prior to training. However, the results also demonstrate the effectiveness of targeted educational interventions in addressing these deficits, as evidenced by the consistent improvement in knowledge scores and counseling confidence.

Limited knowledge and communication difficulties likely stem from insufficient coverage of HPV-related content during dental education and continuing professional development. Integration of digital tools such as interactive modules, explanatory videos, and simulated counseling scenarios has shown promise to enhance both knowledge and communication skills in an accessible, scalable manner [27, 28, 30]. The success of similar digital training in other health fields (e.g., cardiovascular emergency training for nurses) supports the potential of this approach [33–35].

Influence of practice setting and sociocultural factors

The type of dental practice influences counseling practices. Dentists working in private settings report greater discomfort discussing HPV vaccination compared to those in public clinics, where institutional support and public health campaigns facilitate communication [24, 27, 30, 36]. In countries like Portugal, where HPV vaccination for adults is not subsidized, dentist’s advisory role becomes even more crucial, given their frequent contact with adult patients.

Cutural and regional differences also affect the implementation and effectiveness of vaccination counseling. Most studies originate from the USA, limiting applicability to other contexts with different sociocultural dynamics and health systems. Future research should explore these factors to tailor interventions accordingly.

Translating findings into practical strategies

Addressing the identified barriers requires comprehensive, multifaceted strategies. Creating private spaces within dental offices could enhance patient comfort and confidentiality, fostering more open discussions about HPV vaccination [37]. Integrating dentists into national vaccination campaigns would provide institutional backing and increase public awareness, legitimizing their role as vaccination advocates [38]. Expanding continuing education through accessible digital platforms would allow OHPs to acquire up-to-date knowledge and develop effective communication skills flexibly and efficiently [38].

International examples illustrate the benefits of such approaches. Countries like Australia and the United Kingdom have successfully involved dentists in vaccination policies and campaigns, leading to improved vaccine uptake and increased public awareness [37]. In these countries, dentists are encouraged to participate in HPV vaccination awareness campaigns. While vaccine administration by dentists is not yet widespread, they play a critical role in patient education and vaccination recommendation [39]. These models demonstrate the potential gains from policy support, educational investment, and system-level integration of OHPs into broader public health initiatives.

Expanding the role of dental professionals in HPV vaccination promotion

The role of OHP, particularly dentists, in promoting HPV vaccination represents an important frontier in integrating dentistry with broader public health strategies. Traditionally, dentists have focused on clinical and curative aspects of oral health, but the increasing recognition of the link between HPV and oropharyngeal cancers is expanding their scope to include primary prevention, especially through vaccination counseling. This expanded role requires not only changes in initial academic training but also robust investment in continuing education to ensure professionals are equipped with up-to-date knowledge and confidence. While approximately 28.2 million Americans visit a dental setting without a concurrent medical visit – creating a ‘‘unique opportunity’’ for public health outreach – current practice patterns reveal significant gaps. Evidence suggests that OHPs do not always provide recommendations based on risk profiles; for instance, adults without traditional oral cancer risk factors are sometimes more likely to receive counseling or screenings than high-risk individuals [11]. This inconsistency highlights that merely having access to patients is insufficient. OHPs require systematic training and institutional support to transition from curative care to proactive HPV vaccination advocacy.

Continuing education is key to empowering dentists to take on additional responsibilities in HPV vaccination counseling and promotion. Multiple studies highlight significant knowledge gaps regarding HPV and vaccination among OHPs, which directly affect their confidence and willingness to discuss the topic with patients [27, 30, 35].

From an ethical perspective, expanding the dentist’s role in HPV vaccination promotion aligns with fundamental bioethical principles, particularly beneficence and justice. By providing accurate information and appropriate recommendations, dentists contribute to protecting individual and public health by preventing serious diseases such as oropharyngeal cancer, which significantly impact morbidity and mortality [27, 33]. Additionally, dentists’ involvement may help reduce health disparities, especially in vulnerable populations with limited access to immunization information and services [30, 35]. As active public health agents, OHPs can identify at-risk patients, offer personalized counseling, and refer them for vaccination, thereby increasing vaccine coverage.

However, practical implementation faces challenges, including structural limitations such as lack of adequate private space for confidential vaccination discussions, as well as cultural barriers and varying perceptions among patients and professionals [39]. These issues highlight the need for integrated strategies involving professionals, institutions, public policies, and health campaigns to create an environment conducive to vaccination promotion within dentistry [30, 38]. Policies encouraging and regulating dentist’s roles in vaccination campaigns, including potential vaccine administration in dental offices, could strengthen this practice and enhance its impact [37].

It is also important to consider regional and sociocultural differences in HPV vaccination approaches. Most studies in this review originate from the United States and reflect specific contexts that may not be directly transferable to other settings, such as Portugal and European countries. Interventions should be adapted to reflect local cultural, economic, and healthcare system particularities to ensure effectiveness and acceptance [40, 41]. This adaptation should also consider patient age, socioeconomic status, gender, and personal beliefs, which significantly influence vaccine acceptance.

The dentist’s role in HPV promotion also aligns with a growing trend toward integrated, multidisciplinary healthcare that values collaboration among different professionals to improve health outcomes. Dentists can partner with physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and others to form support networks facilitating patient access to information, vaccination, and follow-up care. This collaborative approach is especially relevant for diseases like oropharyngeal cancer, which require multifaceted preventive strategies.

Strengths, limitations and future research directions

A key strength of this review is the implementation of a meta-analysis as an exploratory synthesis tool. Rather than seeking a single, definitive universal effect size, the meta-analysis provides a quantitative map of the existing evidence and illustrates the extent of its variability within the dental setting ecosystem. The use of forest plots serves as a powerful visual aid to highlight the divergence in outcomes among different populations, which is, in itself, a significant finding of this research.

This review is subject to several limitations. The predominance of cross-sectional studies limits the ability to draw causal conclusions and hinders to assessment of long-term intervention effects. The heterogeneity of study populations including practicing dentists, other OHPs, and students as well as the variety of intervention types (face-to-face, digital, or blended) complicate result synthesis and identification of best practices.

These limitations, including the heterogeneity of study populations and interventions, the predominance of cross-sectional designs, and methodological variability, were explicitly acknowledged and carefully considered in our interpretation of results. Consequently, our conclusions should be regarded as indicative of overall trends and gaps in the evidence rather than as precise effect estimates.

The high heterogeneity observed in the analyses, particularly in the pooled data on barriers faced by OHPs, represents an important limitation of this review. Methodological variations among the included studies – such as differences in study design, target populations, data collection tools, and how barriers were categorized—may have contributed to this heterogeneity. For example, although lack of privacy was identified as the most common barrier (44%, 95% CI [37% – 71%]), the wide confidence interval indicates substantial variability across studies. This heterogeneity limits the generalizability of the findings and underscores the need for standardization in future studies evaluating the role of OHPs recommending HPV vaccination. Therefore, results should be interpreted with caution, taking these contextual and methodological differences into account.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs to evaluate lasting impacts, focus on clinical realities with attention to differences between public and private settings, investigate policy measures to more actively involve dentists in vaccination efforts, and assess the feasibility and impact of vaccine administration within dental offices. It is also important to evaluate both digital and in-person training interventions using standardized and validated outcome measures [35]. Such research will strengthen the evidence supporting the role of dentists as public health agents in promoting HPV vaccination.

The high statistical heterogeneity observed in our analyses (with I2 values reaching 98%) underscores the diverse nature of the evidence currently available on this topic. As a result, the findings from our meta-analysis should be interpreted as indicative trends within the dental setting, rather than absolute values. To address this, we have reinforced the narrative synthesis as our primary interpretative framework, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of how barriers and educational interventions vary across different populations and geographic regions, which a single pooled estimate might otherwise obscure.

Moreover, systematic evaluation of both digital and traditional educational interventions using standardized and validated outcome measures is essential. Such research will generate robust evidence to support the integration of dentists as active agents in public health efforts to increase HPV vaccination coverage.

Conclusion

The results of this study highlight the strategic role of dentists in promoting HPV vaccination, especially in a context of increasing rates of oropharyngeal cancer associated with the virus. Despite the great potential of these professionals in raising patient awareness, barriers such as lack of privacy, specific training and discomfort in approaching sensitive topics make effective counseling difficult. To overcome these challenges, it is crucial to implement targeted educational interventions and promote environments that encourage open discussions about HPV. By addressing these barriers, dentists can play a key role in promoting vaccination and ultimately reducing the burden of HPV-related cancers. As previously noted, the predominance of studies from the United States and the heterogeneity of the analyzed populations may limit the generalizability and certainty of some findings. Future research should prioritize context-specific interventions and explore the direct integration of OHPs into vaccination strategies, particularly across diverse public and private healthcare settings.
