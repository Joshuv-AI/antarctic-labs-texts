Atrial fibrillation detection using an implantable loop recorder after cryptogenic stroke
Authors: Evan C. Adelstein, Deborah F. Steinberg, David A. Steckman, Alfred M. Loka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImplantable loop recorders (ILRs) assist in detecting occult atrial fibrillation (AF) in patients with cryptogenic stroke (CS), yet many patients do not manifest subsequent AF.ObjectiveTo identify factors associated with ILR-detected AF, thereby improving device specificity.MethodsAll 744 patients implanted with ILRs at our Stroke Center from 2014 to 2025 after ischemic CS were followed for AF episodes of ≥6 minutes and recurrent stroke.ResultsDuring 657 ± 450 (median 591) days, 138 (18.5%) patients manifested AF lasting ≥6 minutes, with the highest risk within the first 6 months. Older age (hazard ratio [HR], 1.05 per year; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.03–1.07; P < .001), tricuspid regurgitation (HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.10–2.35; P = .015), and middle cerebral artery stroke (HR, 1.79; 95% CI, 1.18–2.71; P = .006) were independently associated with shorter time to initial AF. Multivessel strokes were not associated with AF detection. In the oldest age quartile (≥76), 28% manifested AF. Mitral regurgitation, tricuspid regurgitation, and β-blocker use were associated with shorter time to initial AF. In the middle 2 age quartiles (57–75), 18% had detected AF. Middle cerebral artery stroke was independently associated with AF risk. Only 6% in the youngest age quartile (≤56) manifested AF. Of 44 patients with recurrent stroke, 6 had detected AF. Stroke was temporally related to AF in 2 patients.ConclusionOlder age is highly associated with subsequent AF detection in CS. Mitral and tricuspid regurgitation and middle cerebral artery strokes are associated with AF in older and middle-aged patients, respectively. Recurrent stroke was rarely associated with temporally related AF.

Key Findings ▪In this single-center retrospective study at an academic stroke center, 18.5% of patients who received an implantable loop recorder (ILR) after cryptogenic stroke manifested at least 6 minutes of atrial fibrillation during a median 19-month follow-up.▪Atrial fibrillation incidence was highest during the first 6 months after ILR implant and increased linearly thereafter at a slower rate.▪Atrial fibrillation incidence was highly age-dependent, with very low rates seen in patients under 60 years of age and increasing stepwise with older age.▪Atrial fibrillation detection was independently associated with mitral regurgitation, tricuspid regurgitation, and β-blocker use on stroke discharge in the oldest patients.▪Atrial fibrillation incidence was independently associated with middle cerebral artery strokes in middle-aged patients.▪Patients who experienced recurrent stroke rarely had temporally related atrial fibrillation episodes.

Introduction

Stroke is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. Its global annual incidence has approached 12 million cases, and it is the second leading cause of mortality.1 Determining the cause of ischemic stroke guides secondary preventative strategies, which include antiplatelet agents, statins, and revascularization for large artery atherosclerosis, anticoagulation for cardioembolism or hypercoagulable states, antihypertensive and antiplatelet therapy for small vessel cerebrovascular disease, and percutaneous closure of patent foramen ovale.2 At least one-quarter of strokes are cryptogenic, in that there is no clear etiology determined after systematic investigation.3

Identifying occult paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF) has become integral to the evaluation of cryptogenic stroke (CS). AF is a well-established cause of cardioembolic strokes, and AF-attributable strokes are associated with marked morbidity and mortality.4 Several trials have demonstrated that implantable loop recorders (ILRs) provide superior yield for diagnosing occult AF in patients with CS compared with shorter-term, wearable cardiac monitors.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 However, no definitive studies to date have proven that identification of paroxysmal AF and subsequent treatment with anticoagulation in patients with CS reduces risk of recurrent stroke, and the benefit of anticoagulation in patients with device-detected, subclinical, low-burden AF has been questioned.6,7,13 Recent American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines give prolonged ILR monitoring a class IIa indication after CS.8

Previous studies have examined demographic factors, medical comorbidities, and cardiac findings associated with risk of subsequent AF detection. We performed an observational study examining the ILR experience at a large, academic stroke center, using these characteristics as well as stroke-related data to elucidate which patients are at highest risk for occult AF after CS.

Methods

We included all patients implanted with an ILR at Albany Medical Center from 2014 to 2025 for the indication of ischemic CS. Patients were managed at our Comprehensive Stroke Center and underwent 12-lead electrocardiography, transthoracic echocardiography, routine laboratory testing, screening head computed tomography, and head magnetic resonance imaging. Large vessel atherosclerosis was evaluated using computed tomography angiography or invasive angiography, the latter performed during thrombectomy. Transthoracic echocardiography including agitated saline injection to evaluate for patent foramen ovale, quantification of valvular stenosis and regurgitation using Doppler measurements, 2-dimensional measurements for hypertrophy, and LA volumetric analysis when image quality allowed. Demographic and medical history details were identified in the electronic medical record. Patients with prior diagnosis of AF or already implanted with an ILR, pacemaker, or defibrillator were excluded. The Institutional Review Board of Albany Medical College approved this study. No informed consent was required for this exempt registry study. This study adhered to ethical standards put forth by the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients were referred for ILR by stroke neurologists. Most ILR implants were performed immediately before discharge from the index stroke hospitalization when no AF was identified during at least 48 hours of continuous inpatient telemetry, no obvious cardioembolic source was identified on echocardiography, and no flow-limiting large vessel atherosclerotic disease was seen. ILRs were implanted by cardiac electrophysiologists and included the Reveal LINQ™, Reveal LINQ II™ (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN), Lux-Dx™, and Lux-Dx II™ (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA). Devices were followed monthly via remote transmission by a dedicated nurse-run and physician-supervised device clinic. Devices were programmed at implant for the indication of CS, in which any rhythm consistent with AF lasting ≥2 minutes is recorded. All recorded arrhythmias and monthly ILR checks were overread by a cardiac electrophysiologist.

The primary clinical endpoint was first identification of AF lasting ≥6 minutes based upon the recent ARTESIA and NOAH AF-NET 6 trials.6,7 We also catalogued the number of AF episodes, AF burden, and subsequent new radiographically confirmed ischemic stroke. Patients were followed until ILR removal or battery depletion, death, or study completion at the end of 2025.

Statistical methods

This was a retrospective and prospective registry study; existing patients receiving ILR were initially identified in device clinic follow-up in 2020, patients who received ILR earlier were added retrospectively, and subsequent patients receiving ILRs were prospectively included in the registry. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are listed as means ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables and as medians with interquartile range (IQR) for skewed distributions. Normally distributed variables were compared using Student’s t-test. Skewed variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U testing. Time-dependent outcomes were compared between groups using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. Hazard ratios were generated using the Cox proportional hazards method. Multivariate time-dependent analyses used Cox proportional models, in which independent factors that differed between comparison groups with a P-value of <.10 on univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Logistic regression was used to determine factors associated with discrete, time-independent endpoints. All P-values are 2-sided, and P-values of ≤.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 31.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) was used for statistical analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics

We included 744 patients who were followed for 657 ± 460 (median 591; IQR, 279–1050) days. The median time from CS to ILR implant was 8 (IQR, 4–73) days. Presently, 320 (43%) patients are actively monitored, 39 (5%) patients have died, 214 (29%) patients have undergone ILR removal, and 171 (23%) patients have been lost to follow-up.

Incidence and burden of detected AF

Overall, 138 (18.5%) patients manifested AF lasting ≥6 minutes during follow-up. New AF was most frequently detected in the first 6 months, and its incidence increased linearly thereafter (Figure 1). Median time to first detected AF episode was 111 days (IQR, 24–314), and 45 (6%) patients had their first AF episode within 30 days of ILR implant.Figure 1Time to first atrial fibrillation episode ≥6 minutes in overall study population.

Among patients with detected AF, median AF duration was 24 hours (IQR, 5–263; Figure 2A). The median number of AF episodes was 4 (IQR, 2–33; Figure 2B). Of the 138 patients with detected AF, 119 (86%) had at least 30 minutes of AF, 54 (39%) had at least 24 hours of detected AF, and 25 (18%) ultimately developed persistent AF.Figure 2Duration (A) and number (B) of detected atrial fibrillation episodes in overall study population.

Recurrent ischemic stroke

There were 46 (6%) patients with recurrent ischemic stroke after ILR implant. 6 patients with recurrent stroke had detected AF, 2 of whom had temporally related AF. 1 patient had recurrent stroke 2 months after first detection of AF, which was >24 hours in duration. The patient was not compliant with apixaban, which had been prescribed when AF was initially detected. The second patient had recurrent stroke after a 10-hour AF episode that occurred 10 days before the stroke, and anticoagulation had not yet been started. In the other 4 patients, AF was not detected until 7 months to 3 years after the recurrent stroke.

Time to first detected episode of AF

Older age, non-Black identity, hypertension, increased CHA2DS2-VASc score, longer PR interval, echocardiographic evidence of left atrial enlargement, mitral and tricuspid regurgitation of at least mild severity, premature atrial complexes captured on admission 12-lead electrocardiogram, cortical stroke, middle cerebral artery stroke, anterior circulation stroke, lower low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol concentration, discharge on β-blockers, and discharge on oral anticoagulation were individually associated with shorter time to first detected AF (Table 2). Notably, strokes in multiple vascular distributions and prior stroke were not associated with detected AF. For multivariable analysis, age and hypertension were used instead of CHA2DS2-VASc score because both are components of the CHA2DS2-VASc score that were significantly associated with time to first AF. Middle cerebral artery stroke was included in the multivariate model, as it is more specific than anterior circulation or cortical stroke. When age, Black identity, hypertension, PR interval, left atrial enlargement, mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, premature atrial complexes, low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol concentration, discharge on β-blockers or anticoagulation, and middle cerebral artery stroke were included in a Cox model, age (hazard ratio [HR], 1.05 per year; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.03–1.07; P < .001), tricuspid regurgitation (HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.10–2.35; P = .015), and middle cerebral artery stroke (HR, 1.79; 95% CI, 1.18–2.71; P = .006) were independently associated with shorter time to first AF episode (Table 2). Two sensitivity analyses were performed using AF duration cutoffs of 2 minutes and 30 minutes, respectively. A 2-minute cutoff had identical univariate and multivariate findings for those factors associated with shorter time to first AF detection (Supplemental Table 1). A 30-minute cutoff differed from the 6-minute cutoff, in that hypertension and PR interval were no longer associated with shorter time to first AF detection in univariate analyses, although multivariable findings were also identical to those observed using the 6-minute cutoff (Supplemental Table 2).

The cohort was analyzed according to age quartile, combining the middle 2 quartiles. There was a stepwise shorter time to first detection with increasing age quartile (Figure 3). In the oldest quartile (≥76 years old), 59 of 185 patients (32%) had detected AF, and mitral regurgitation (HR, 1.88; 95% CI, 1.09–3.22; P = .02), tricuspid regurgitation (HR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.06–3.10; P = .03), and β-blocker on discharge (HR, 1.77; 95% CI, 1.06–2.95; P = .03) were independently associated with shorter time to first AF (Figure 4A–C). In the middle quartiles (57–75 years old), 68 of 371 patients (18%) had AF detections. Middle cerebral artery stroke was independently associated with shorter time to first AF (HR, 2.50; 95% CI, 1.31–4.80; P = .006; Figure 5). In the youngest quartile (≤56 years old), only 11 of 185 patients (6%) manifested AF. The number of patients with AF detected within 30 days of ILR implant was 21 (11%) in the oldest age quartile, 22 (6%) in the middle age quartiles, and 2 (1%) in the youngest age quartile. Increasing age (odds ratio, 1.05 per year; 95% CI, 1.02–1.09; P = .001) and middle cerebral artery stroke (odds ratio, 2.64; 95% CI, 1.14–6.09; P = .02) were independently associated with AF detection within 30 days of ILR implant. In the oldest age quartile, 13 (19%) patients with at least mild tricuspid regurgitation had AF detected within 30 days, compared with 8 (7%) patients without tricuspid regurgitation (P = .015). Similarly, AF was detected within 30 days of ILR implant in 14 (21%) patients with at least mild mitral regurgitation, compared with 7 (6%) patients without mitral regurgitation (P = .003).

Discussion

In this single-center observational study performed at an academic stroke center, we found that detection of new subclinical AF ≥6 minutes in patients implanted with an ILR after CS was most likely in the first 6 months and increased linearly thereafter. Increasing age and middle cerebral artery (MCA) stroke were independently associated with AF detection overall and within the first 30 days. Notably, strokes involving multiple vascular distributions, usually considered pathognomonic for thromboembolism, were not associated with subsequent AF detection. In the oldest age quartile, mitral regurgitation, tricuspid regurgitation, and β-blocker use were associated with occult AF detection, whereas middle cerebral artery distribution stroke was associated with AF detection among patients in the middle 2 age quartiles. The temporal relation between recurrent stroke and detected AF was low. Median cumulative AF duration was relatively short—24 hours during over 19 months of follow-up.

Multiple studies have shown that prolonged monitoring provides greater opportunity for finding occult AF in patients with CS, and ILRs facilitate monitoring compared with serial wearable monitors.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12,14 CRYSTAL-AF was a seminal ILR study in which occult AF was seen in approximately 10% of patients with CS per year, rising linearly during 3 years of continuous monitoring.11 Large, single-center observational studies have shown lower diagnostic yield for AF detection than CRYSTAL-AF,15 suggesting that randomized trials may include higher risk patients than those seen in clinical practice. In our study, AF detection was also less frequent than observed in CRYSTAL-AF, and incidence of new AF rose more slowly after 6–12 months.

Factors associated with AF detection among patients with CS in prior studies include age, left atrial enlargement, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, frequent atrial premature beats, P-wave duration, P-wave morphology, abnormal P-wave axis, and left atrial end-systolic volume index.16, 17, 18, 19 A novel finding of our study is that it included stroke characteristics in addition to demographic data, comorbid medical conditions, and cardiac findings. Mitral and tricuspid regurgitation were independently associated with AF in our study among older patients, which is not a surprising finding despite its novelty in the literature, given the association between atrioventricular valvular regurgitation and AF. β-blocker use was also associated with AF in older patients, possibly because these agents were used in patients with more recalcitrant hypertension, another known risk factor for AF. In middle-aged patients, MCA stroke was associated with subsequent AF, perhaps because MCA strokes tend to be larger and more indicative of a macro cardioembolic etiology, and the MCA receives the most blood flow and tends to be straighter than the other cerebral vessels.20 The lack of association between acute stroke involving multiple vascular territories or prior stroke and subsequent AF detection is also an unexpected finding. The CRYSTAL-AF study did not find an association between acute infarct pattern and subsequent AF detection, although chronic cerebral infarcts were associated with subsequent AF.21

Pacemakers and defibrillators with atrial leads also provide long-term AF surveillance. Detection of subclinical atrial tachyarrhythmias in patients with recently implanted cardiac devices was associated with a 2.5-fold increased risk of first stroke in the ASSERT trial.22 Singer et al found that AF within the 5 previous days was associated with increased risk of ischemic stroke among patients with implanted cardiac devices.23 Correlation between individual AF episodes and stroke was poor in IMPACT, TRENDS, and the Reveal Linq registry. In IMPACT, initiation of oral anticoagulation based upon detected AF using implanted defibrillators did not reduce stroke risk, and stroke was not temporally related to paroxysms of AF.24 Three-quarters of patients with stroke or systemic embolism in TRENDS had no AF detection in the previous 30 days.25 Only 1 of 11 patients with recurrent stroke in the Reveal Linq registry had detected AF.19 Our study results also suggest poor temporal relation between AF and recurrent strokes as described in these prior publications.

More recently, there are conflicting data regarding the risk of stroke among patients with high atrial rate episodes detected by implantable devices. In the ARTESIA study, apixaban decreased stroke among patients with subclinical AF episodes between 6 minutes and 24 hours duration, although the increased risk of bleeding offset this benefit in patients with CHA2DS2-VASc under 4.6 In the NOAH AF-NET6 study, edoxaban increased bleeding risk but did not decrease stroke risk in a similar patient population.7 The observed lack of correlation between recurrent stroke and detected AF may be related to non-cardiac thromboembolism, such as aortic atheroma. In our study, 94% of patients were on a statin after discharge, and plaque stabilization could reduce risk of subsequent atheromatous stroke.

Despite overwhelming evidence that occult AF is common among patients with CS, few data demonstrate that routine ILR implantation with subsequent anticoagulation if AF is detected reduces recurrent stroke. Although some studies indicate that routine use of ILRs in patients with CS leads to a reduction in subsequent strokes,12 other studies suggest this is not the case despite increased AF detection with an ILR.10,14 In CRYSTAL-AF, there were only 4 strokes in each arm of the study, so no conclusions could be drawn regarding prevention of recurrent stroke.11 A meta-analysis of 5 randomized controlled trials and 3 observational studies demonstrated no benefit to prolonged cardiac monitoring for prevention of recurrent stroke in the randomized studies despite reduced recurrent stroke in observational studies, even with higher rates of AF detection in the randomized studies.13

Several explanations have been offered to explain why the greater AF detection rate with ILRs and subsequent anticoagulation has not translated into reduced recurrent stroke risk. ILRs may detect AF in patients who are inherently at lower risk of recurrent ischemic events, such that subsequent anticoagulation does not reduce event rates.26 Subclinical AF detected after stroke also seems to be less associated with thromboembolism than clinical AF detected before stroke.27 The ASSERT trial suggested that AF before stroke is a high-burden arrhythmia, whereas AF after stroke is a low-burden arrhythmia.28 AF detected using event monitors or ILRs is also associated with lower risk of recurrent stroke than AF detected on a 12-lead ECG, suggesting that the former represents a lower risk group.29 Indeed, our data do suggest that AF after stroke is a relatively low-burden arrhythmia, with a median burden of 0.17%, which was 24 hours during 19 months of follow-up. We hypothesize that the high sensitivity of ILRs in detecting AF likely allows for detection in a large number of patients in whom AF was not causative of but associative with prior stroke. This would explain the low AF burden in those patients with AF, the lack of association with multiple vascular territories, the poor correlation between AF episodes and recurrent stroke, and the lack of uniformity in studies demonstrating reduced subsequent stroke risk with routine ILR use. AF detection may be a proxy for the true cause of cardioembolism, such as atrial cardiomyopathy.30

The labor-intensive follow-up and monetary cost of routine ILR use in patients with CS are not inconsequential. ILR monitoring requires significant time on the part of clinic technicians, nurses, and physicians, and false positive findings are commonplace despite updated detection algorithms.11,12 Patient financial liability in some instances is significant and reduces compliance with remote follow-up; in some cases, ILRs are subsequently infrequently monitored in real time, if at all. Ability to interface with technology also presents a barrier to ILR monitoring, particularly among older patients.31

Some experts advocate implanting an ILR immediately after CS, whereas others recommend event monitoring for 30 days prior to ILR consideration. Occult AF was detected in 5% of patients using 30-day ambulatory ECG monitoring in 1 study,32 which was similar to the 6% incidence of AF within 30 days of ILR implant in our study. Although a relatively small percentage of patients do have detected AF within 30 days, it is not a trivial finding; detecting early AF with a wearable monitor obviates the need for ILR implant. Another study evaluating ILR implant before hospital discharge from CS identified AF within the first month in 41% of patients, a much higher incidence than other reports.33 Remarkably, the authors concluded that this supported “ultra-early” implant rather than 30-day ambulatory event monitoring. Our study suggests that older patients with tricuspid and/or mitral regurgitation should wear a 30-day monitor before referral for ILR, as AF is detected in nearly 1 in 5 patients, and this would potentially obviate the need for an ILR for diagnostic purposes.

This present study has several limitations. It is a single-center, observational study, so there is no comparison group without ILRs. Although ILR implants were performed at the behest of neurologists, there were no codified inclusion criteria, which may have introduced bias in patient selection. We do not know how many patients with CS were not referred for ILR nor what factors may have informed these decisions. The minimum duration of AF necessary to increase risk of stroke is also controversial. We chose an AF detection window of 6 minutes based upon NOAH AFNET 6,6 although sensitivity analyses using 2-minute and 30-minute cutoffs showed similar findings. Some patients were lost to follow-up, limiting long-term data collection. This is a limitation of ILRs in general, particularly when implanted as inpatients while hospitalized for debilitating stroke at geographically distant stroke centers. It is possible that patients who were lost to follow-up were more likely to manifest AF or recurrent stroke. Finally, we do not know how many strokes may have occurred in patients who were found to have AF and were subsequently anticoagulated had they not been monitored with an ILR.

Conclusion

In this large, single-center study of patients implanted with an ILR after CS, detection of new occult AF ≥6 minutes is most frequent in the first 6 months. AF detection is highly correlated with increasing age, with very low incidence in young patients. Mitral and tricuspid regurgitation are associated with detected AF in older patients, whereas middle cerebral artery strokes are associated with AF in middle-aged patients. These findings have implications in patient selection for ILRs after CS.
