Exploring the relationship between oral healthcare service choice, treatment needs and oral health-related quality of life
Authors: Otso Tirkkonen, Ville Vuollo, Jussi Miikkael Leppilahti, Marja-Liisa Laitala, Saujanya Karki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIrregular use of oral healthcare is associated with oral disease burden, leading to poorer oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL). There is evidence suggesting an association between dental care visits or access and OHRQoL. However, no studies have assessed the relationship of use of oral healthcare services on OHRQoL in countries where universal health coverage, including oral health, has been prioritized. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether choosing either public or private oral healthcare service mediates the association between oral health treatment need and OHRQoL. Another aim was to compare the difference in treatment need between two cohorts born 20 years apart.MethodsThis study utilized the data of two birth cohorts from the two northernmost provinces of Finland, the Northern Finland Birth Cohort 1966 (NFBC1966) and the Northern Finland Birth Cohort 1986 (NFBC1986). A clinical oral examination was conducted when NFBC1966 were 45–46-year-olds (n = 1964), and NFBC1986 were 33–35-year-olds (n = 1807). Participants in both cohorts were asked to complete an oral health-related questionnaire before their clinical examination. A Structural Equation Model was employed to examine the impact of choosing either public or private oral healthcare services.ResultsAlmost half of the study participants in the older cohort and almost two-thirds of the participants in the younger cohort used private oral healthcare services. The younger cohort had a greater need for restorative treatment than the older cohort. In contrast, the need for extensive periodontal treatment was higher in the older cohort than in the younger cohort. Oral health treatment needs had a direct association with OHRQoL (β = 0.432, p < 0.001). Additionally, choosing public oral healthcare services also had a direct association with OHRQoL (β = 0.057, p = 0.014). There was no indirect association between choice of oral healthcare service and oral health treatment need and OHRQoL (β = 0.008, 95% CI: -0.022 to 0.029).ConclusionThe association between oral health treatment needs and OHRQoL persisted, independent of the relationship with Oral Healthcare Service choice. Background

Oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) is a person-reported measure that evaluates the impact of oral disease on social, psychological, and physical wellbeing [1]. It is well known that untreated dental caries and severe periodontal disease have a considerable impact on OHRQoL [2, 3]. For example, pain or discomfort related to untreated dental caries and periodontitis may impair function.

Previously, Sischo and Broder [1] presented a theoretical model where access to oral healthcare is linked to OHRQoL. This model for OHRQoL includes biological, social, psychological, and cultural factors. It connects health status, functional status, appearance, psychological state, OHRQoL, and overall Quality of Life. It also considers environmental factors like sociocultural influences and access to care or utilization of health services. Additionally, the World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQOL) refers to quality of life as a subjective evaluation that is embedded in a cultural, social and environmental context. Environmental factors such as Health and social care: accessibility and quality (Item 24), and Financial resources (Item 23) in Domain V were also considered to be important aspects of life when assessing an individual’s quality of life [4]. Utilization of oral healthcare services can be partly described by the materialist explanations that suggest that the inability to afford preventive and regular dental care due to low income may cause poor oral health [5], subsequently leading to poor OHRQoL [1]. There has been a shift in OHRQoL measures, from solely focusing on the individuals’ perspectives of their oral status to considering the determinants of care and satisfaction with care received from health care providers [6, 7].

Numerous studies have shown a significant relationship between oral health treatment needs and poor OHRQoL [2, 3]. The need for dental care was higher among symptom-oriented users and non-users of oral healthcare services [8]. Unmet need for oral health care is mainly due to waiting times and the cost of treatment [9]. For example, individuals who can afford private oral healthcare services can choose to avoid longer waiting times, subsequently receiving primary preventive care. In contrast, there is a higher risk of the progression of oral diseases into advanced stages that may need curative care, if early prevention is not prioritized. Furthermore, it is crucial to recognize that individuals with dental fear and anxiety often avoid utilization of oral healthcare services, thereby complicating this relationship between unmet care needs and utilization of oral healthcare services [10].

The existing body of literature lacks evidence on the impact of choosing either public or private oral healthcare services for oral treatment need as well as OHRQoL. There is some evidence suggesting an association between dental care visits or access and OHRQoL. Åstrøm and colleagues found that routine dental care is a significant predictor of good OHRQoL [11]. Likewise, a previous study from Finland that utilized data from two national surveys concluded that those with good OHRQoL tend to use oral healthcare services regularly, rather than the regular service use leading to better OHRQoL [12]. However, during the baseline of that Finnish national survey, participants neither had access to subsidized oral healthcare nor a choice between private or public oral healthcare services. Likewise, Crocombe and Mahoney in their study conducted in the Australian Defence Force, concluded that despite having universal and optimal access to dental care there is an OHRQoL social gradient [13]. However, these studies have used self-reported measures for assessing oral health treatment needs.

Individuals needing oral treatment can either choose a private or public oral healthcare services. In the first scenario, those in need of oral treatment and able to afford the cost will choose the private sector for faster access. In the second scenario, an individual is forced to choose the public service due to its affordability despite having a treatment need. In this case, however, due to long waiting times, treatment is postponed, which can lead to a progression of diseases, thereby impacting OHRQoL. However, optimally timed visits to dental care could promote appropriate self-care and non-invasive or minimally invasive treatments instead of procedures requiring time and resources. Considering the scenarios and the context of Finland, where universal health coverage, including oral health, has been prioritized, a directed acyclic graph is constructed (Fig. 1). Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether choosing either public or private oral healthcare service mediates the association between oral health treatment need and OHRQoL. Another aim was to compare the difference in treatment need between two cohorts born 20 years apart.

Methods

Study participants

This study is based on two birth cohorts from the two northernmost provinces (Northern Ostrobothnia and Lapland) of Finland, the Northern Finland Birth Cohort 1966 (NFBC1966) and the Northern Finland Birth Cohort 1986 (NFBC1986) [14–17].

The NFBC1966 cohort includes offsprings of pregnant mothers from Northern Ostrobothnia and Lapland whose expected delivery dates were between January 1 st, 1966 and December 31 st, 1966 (NFBC1966, n = 12,527), which was 96.3% of all births in 1966. Later in 2012, a follow-up study was arranged for NFBC1966 when the cohort members were 45–46 years old. All the NFBC1966 participants alive and with a known address in Finland (n = 10,331) who lived within a 100 km radius of the centre of Oulu (n = 3,150) received questionnaires regarding health, background, and lifestyle. This group was later invited for a clinical examination, and a total of 1,964 cohort members participated in the clinical oral examination. Likewise, the NFBC1986 also includes offsprings of pregnant mothers from Northern Ostrobothnia and Lapland whose expected delivery dates were between July 1 st,1985 and June 30th, 1986 (NFBC1986, n = 9,575), which was 99% of all births in July 1985 and June 1986. A follow-up study was arranged from May 2019 to December 2020 for NFBC1986 when the cohort members were 33–35 years old. All the NFBC1986 participants alive and with a known address in Finland living within a 250 km radius of the centre of Oulu (n = 5,743) received questionnaires regarding their health, background, and lifestyle. Later, participants were invited for a clinical oral examination, and a total of 1,807 cohort members participated in the clinical oral examination. Because 37 (NFBC1966 members) and 118 (NFBC1986 members) withdrew their participation, the full clinical oral health records of n = 1,927 and n = 1,689 from the NFBC1966 and the NFBC1986, respectively, were received from the NFBC centre. Therefore, the final sample in this study was n = 3,616. All the participants in both cohorts were asked to complete an oral health-related questionnaire before the clinical examination.

Oral health-related quality of life

Oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) was used as the outcome variable in this study. A previously validated Finnish version of the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-14) was used to measure OHRQoL [18].

The OHIP-14 consists of 14 questions, two from each of the seven domains (physical pain, psychological discomfort, physical disability, psychological disability, functional limitation, social disability, and handicap). Participants were given a reference period of one month to answer the questions and they had the opportunity to respond on a Likert scale as follows: never, hardly ever, occasionally, fairly often, very often and don’t know [18]. The score for each domain and the overall OHIP-14 score were calculated using the additive method, as suggested in the original instrument. The score for each domain ranged from 0 to 8 and the overall OHIP-14 score from 0 to 56. During the analyses, the ‘don’t know’ response was treated as ‘missing’.

Oral health status

All the clinical examinations were conducted in a modern dental chair facility with optimal lighting at the Research Unit of Oral Health Sciences, Faculty of Medicine, University of Oulu. Oral examinations were performed using a mouth mirror, periodontal probe and fibre-optic transillumination light. Before the clinical examination, teeth were blow-dried using a three-in-one syringe; no professional cleaning was done. A total of seven trained and calibrated dentists performed the clinical oral examination in the NFBC1966 while nine trained and calibrated dentists performed the clinical oral examination in the NFBC1986. In both cohorts, the dentists followed the same examination protocol. The protocol has been described in detail in previous papers [19, 20]. The intra and inter-examiner agreements are presented in Supplement file 1 .

The clinical examiners were asked to record the presence or absence of coronal caries lesion on all the surfaces. Caries lesions were registered using the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS). The ICDAS system for caries lesion detection is based on the visual appearance and histologic depth of the lesion with scores ranging from 0 to 6 depending on the severity of the lesion [21]. The ICDAS scoring criteria is presented in Supplement file 1. Any caries lesions with a score of 4 or more were considered to have an absolute need for restorative treatment in both cohorts. However, for the NFBC1966, in borderlines cases, especially concerning scores 3 and 4, the clinical examiners were asked to record the most severe option. Additionally, for NFBC1986, the activity of caries lesions (active and inactive) was also recorded. Therefore, any caries lesions with a score of 3 (with active) or more were also considered as needing restorative treatment in the NFBC1986. Later, tooth-level restorative treatment needs were calculated (except for third molars).

Periodontal status was examined by probing all teeth except for the third molars. Periodontal pocket depth was measured with a standard ball-ended periodontal probe (LM 8-520B, Lääkintämuovi) from 4 sites (mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal and midoral) of the teeth in the NFBC1966 and from 6 sites (mesiobuccal, buccal, distobuccal, mesiooral, midoral and distooral) of the teeth in the NFBC1986. In the NFBC1966 and NFBC1986, periodontal pocket depth was measured in millimetres. Additionally, any bleeding on probing (BOP) was measured after probing pocket depth measurements and recorded as presence or absence of bleeding on the same occasion. Later, BOP percentage was computed as the number of sites with bleeding divided by the number of all sites examined, except for the third molar.

The need for periodontal treatments were categorized into three categories based on BOP percentage and periodontal pocket depth [22]. The periodontal treatment needs groups were: no periodontal treatment need (mainly preventive care, participants with BOP percentage ≤ 15% and no teeth with periodontal pockets ≥ 4 mm), preventive care and/or early periodontal treatment need (participant with BOP percentage >15% and/or less than eight natural teeth with periodontal pockets ≥ 4 mm and no teeth with periodontal pockets ≥ 6 mm); and extensive periodontal treatment need (participants with at least 8 teeth with periodontal pockets ≥ 4 mm or a tooth with periodontal pocket ≥ 6 mm).

Choice of oral healthcare services

The inquiry on choice of oral healthcare services (public or private) was made when the participants were asked to fill in the oral health-related questionnaire. The question asked during dental visits was as follows: “Where do you primarily go for your dental care?”, with the option private dentists, public dentists, other dentists (Finnish Student Health Services, Finnish Defence Forces, and University Hospital), private dental hygienist, and public dental hygienist. Participants were categorized as public oral healthcare services users (public dentists, public dental hygienist and other dentists) and private oral healthcare services users (private dentists and private dental hygienist). The validity of the question and its responses concerning the utilization of oral healthcare services was assessed based on constructs from two national health surveys, Health 2000 and Health 2011, conducted in Finland [23, 24].

Additionally, data on participants’ gender, education, age, household income and oral health-related behaviours (tobacco use and toothbrushing frequency) were requested from the NFBC database and these variables were considered confounders. The NFBC collected this information in 2012 and 2019 for NFBC1966 and NFBC1986, respectively.

Participants who reported completing higher education (bachelor’s degree or more) were categorized as the higher education group, and the rest were as the lower education group. Likewise, household incomes were categorized into quartiles as defined by Statistics Finland [25]. For the NFBC1966, household income quartiles from 2012 were considered and categorized as the first quartile (less than 31,252 euros per year), second quartile (31,252–49,921 euros per year), third quartile (49,922–71,693 euros per year) and fourth quartile (more than 71,693 euros per year). Likewise, household income quartiles from 2019 were considered for the categorization of the NFBC1986 participants. Participants whose yearly household income was less than 31,843 euros were categorized as the first quartile group, those with 31,843–51,455 euros as the second quartile group, those with 51,456–75,417 euros as the third quartile group, and those with more than 75,417 euros as the fourth quartile group. Participants were also categorized based on their habit of using tobacco products (snus and/or cigarette) as non-user (never, few times a month) and regular user (weekly, daily), as well as their toothbrushing frequency twice daily and once daily or less frequently.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.2 [26]. The Structural Equation Model (SEM) analyses were done using the R package Lavaan (v4.2.2) [27]. In the SEM analyses, participants with any missing values were not considered. The results are presented as means (standard deviation) and proportions. An Independent Student’s t-test and analysis of variance test (ANOVA) were performed to compare means between groups. Likewise, a chi-square test was performed to compare differences in proportions between groups. Direct and indirect associations between exogenous and endogenous variables were computed using SEM. Oral health treatment need and Socioeconomic Status (SES) was considered a latent endogenous variable in this study. The latent endogenous variable, Oral health treatment need was estimated by the number of teeth requiring restorative treatment (NFBC1966: ICDAS 4−6 and NFBC1986: ICDAS 3 & active−6) and the need for periodontal treatment (preventive care and/or early periodontal treatment need and extensive periodontal treatment need). SES was estimated by categorized household income and educational background . Cohort was used as exogenous variable in the model. Additionally, Gender, Smoking, Toothbrushing, Choice of oral healthcare and OHRQoL were used as observable endogenous variables in the model. Cohort and oral health-related behaviours were also considered as potential confounders and were included in the SEM. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to investigate the measurement quality of the latent variables. The goodness of fit indices included the comparative fit index (CFI = 0.924), the incremental fit index (IFI = 0.925), the root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA = 0.052) and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR = 0.060). The goodness of fit indices were acceptable [28]. Additionally, bootstrapping was done to evaluate the indirect path coefficients in the SEM. The SEM model fit was tested using goodness of fit indices. Participants with missing values were not considered in the analyses.

To further, investigate the relationships of oral health, choice of oral healthcare services and OHRQoL a negative binomial regression model was constructed utilizing all study variables. Adjusted prevalence rate ratios (PRR) were derived for analyses. The level of significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 for all the statistical tests.

Results

Almost half of the study participants in the older cohort and almost two-thirds of the participants in the younger cohort used private oral healthcare services. The proportion of those with restorative treatment need was higher in the younger cohort (61.5%) than in the older cohort (39.8%). The same was true for periodontal treatment need. Only 6% of the participants in the younger cohort had no need for any form of periodontal treatment. However, the proportion of those in need of extensive periodontal treatments was lower among the participants of the younger cohort (Table 1). The majority of the participants with a need for restorative treatment (56.0%) as well as those in need of periodontal treatment (89.5%) choose public sector services(Supplement file 1).

Discussion

In the present study, oral health treatment needs and choice of oral healthcare services had a direct association with OHRQoL. However, the association between oral health treatment needs and OHRQoL persisted, independent of choosing either public or private oral healthcare service. The younger cohort had a greater need for restorative treatment than the older cohort. In contrast, the need for extensive periodontal treatment was higher in the older cohort.

Despite national efforts to minimize the prevalence of oral diseases, surprising results were revealed in this study with respect to the need for restorative and periodontal treatments. The proportion of caries-free individuals was higher among older cohorts (NFBC1966) than among younger cohorts (NFBC1986). Variations in dietary habits among younger cohorts may partially account this observed difference. Another explanation may be due to the presence of fewer teeth requiring restoration in the NFBC1966 participants compared to the NFBC1986 (mean number of filled teeth 12.8 vs. 6.51), meaning that the older cohort had already received most of the operative care [29]. However, it is also important to consider radiological findings and individual-based dental caries risk evaluation when planning operative care, though this was not studied here. In addition to restorative treatment needs, the younger cohort in the present study also exhibited a higher frequency of early periodontal treatment needs, including cases of gingivitis and early periodontal disease. In contrast, the older cohort showed a greater need for more extensive periodontal treatment.

Periodontal measurements are always susceptible to subjective assessment, and researcher-dependent factors can partly explain small differences between studies, in spite of careful validation and calibration processes. Furthermore, some methodological differences existed between the NFBC1966 and NFBC1986 periodontal examination protocols. In the NFBC1966, periodontal probing was conducted at four sites per tooth, with bleeding on probing (BOP) recorded accordingly, whereas in the NFBC1986, measurements were taken at six sites per tooth. These differences may have contributed to discrepancies in the results when comparing the cohorts.

Nevertheless, a similarly low frequency of periodontally healthy individuals has been reported when applying the relatively strict definitions of periodontal health outlined in the EFP/AAP classification and the ACES framework [30]. Our findings are also consistent with preliminary results from the most recent national survey, as reported in the national clinical practice guideline and web-page report [31], which found gingival bleeding (BOP ≥ 10%) in 91% and periodontal pocketing (at least one tooth with PD ≥ 4 mm) in 74% of the adult population aged 20 years or older. This result is particularly concerning when compared to a previous national study conducted two decades ago (2000–2001) [32]. Having said that, participants with gingivitis were included in the early periodontal treatment need group (need for preventive care). It can be presumed that gingivitis is common in the younger cohort as the number of participants requiring preventive care and early intervention was higher in the younger cohort. Oral health behaviours such as toothbrushing play a significant role here, which can also be observed in the SEM model. Therefore, any future study should evaluate oral hygiene patterns and oral health behaviours, as well as radiological findings among these cohorts born 20 years apart.

Although some of the Cohen’s kappa values in the present study appeared to be low, this outcome is expected and methodologically explainable due to the high number of healthy teeth (negative case) and the rarity of teeth affected by dental disease (positive case) in the calibration data. In such imbalanced scenarios, kappa tends to underestimate agreement because it adjusts for chance agreement, which becomes disproportionately large when one category dominates [33]. Therefore, the low kappa values reported here do not necessarily reflect poor reliability but rather the statistical sensitivity of kappa to skewed marginal distributions. Moreover, the percent agreement was reported alongside kappa to provide a more complete picture of inter-examiner reliability.

It is evident from previous studies that the presence of oral diseases has a significant impact on OHRQoL [2, 3]. These findings partially corroborate with the result in the present study. Although, SEM model demonstrates a relationship between oral health treatment needs and OHRQoL, the regression analysis provides more insight, indicating that this link is more explained by restorative treatment needs rather than periodontal treatment needs. Physical pain and psychological discomfort were the most impacted domains of OHIP-14. However, since confounding factors were not accounted for when comparing the OHIP-14 domains, these results should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, higher OHIP scores, indicating poorer OHRQoL, were observed among individuals in the older cohort. This finding is similar to a previous Finnish national longitudinal study [34]. Furthermore, individuals choosing public care demonstrated a worse OHRQoL, as indicated with OHIP-14 scores of 2.72 compared to 3.28 for those choosing private care. However, in the SEM model, association is observable only with a negligible coefficient value. This can also be observed also in the regression analyses. It can be presumed that the individuals requiring oral health treatments are utilizing public care mainly because of the cost. This was also evident in a recent study which clearly showed that Finns are shifting from the private to the public sector after a reform in 2015–2016. The reform reduced reimbursements for utilizing private services, thus increasing out-of-pocket payments [35]. This might also be the reason for individuals requiring oral treatment chose the public sector in this study.

Notably, the role of choosing either private or public oral healthcare service was not evident in the relationship between oral health treatment needs and OHRQoL. This might be due to the profound association between oral treatment need and OHRQoL rather than the use itself, as is evident in the present study as well. This could also explain the weak link between choice of oral healthcare services and OHRQoL in the present study. During non-emergency cases, such as need for restorative treatment (ICDAS 4), or early periodontitis, individuals familiar with public care tend to utilize them despite the waiting time. For example, almost two-thirds of younger cohorts chose public oral healthcare rather than private because they have received free oral healthcare from the public sector since their childhood. However, according to the Finnish legislation, the maximum waiting time should not be more than six months [36]. Furthermore, a recent study from the US [37] concluded that delays in dental care utilization impact daily activities. Indeed, longer waiting times can cause early lesions to progress into advanced stages that may require curative treatment. However, waiting times were not studied here. Therefore, future research should examine the association between waiting time and the choice of oral healthcare services and OHRQoL. Additionally, the reform in 2015–2016 may have also played a role in choosing suitable oral healthcare services [38]. It should be noted that the NFBC1966 were questioned and examined in 2012 and the NFBC1986 in 2019–2020. Another reason might be the nature of the observable endogenous variable (choice of oral healthcare services). Participants were asked to answer only the service they utilize most often, even though they may have utilized both private and public simultaneously. This causes random misclassification to our study, shifting the results more towards the null hypothesis.

Direct association between SES and choice of oral healthcare services was found in the current study. It can be presumed that the oral treatment need burden has cumulated among the lower SES, compelling them to opt for public oral healthcare services primarily due to economic constraints rather than genuine choice. Despite the longer waiting times, the lower cost of public services makes them a necessity for individuals with limited financial options. Previous studies from Australia [39] and Finland [40] have also shown that the financial burden incurred by utilizing either public or private oral healthcare services is relatively high among low-income individuals. Likewise in this study financial limitations have been also recognized to limit the use of oral health services in USA [41] and Europe [42]. This might also explain why low-income individuals avoid regular dental visits [37], thereby leading to poor oral health status, such as a high occurrence of dental caries among non-regular visitors [39], which subsequently has an impact on OHRQoL. A recent cross-national study using a vignette approach identified a socioeconomic gradient in OHRQoL, regardless of a country’s economic classification [42]. Additionally, it should be noted that the participants in the younger cohort in the present study were entitled to free oral healthcare from childhood until 18 years of age and the participants in the older cohort received free oral healthcare services from the age of 6 years until they turned 18 [43]. Therefore, it can be speculated that inequalities in the choice of (public vs. private) oral healthcare and oral health treatment needs in relation to SES are more noticeable in later life. For example, a negative change in SES was found to be significantly associated with poor oral health outcome in the Dunedin cohort study [44].

The main strength of the present study is that the clinical examinations were completed by dentists who underwent a systematic training and calibration process. This study includes clinical oral data following the same protocol in both NFBCs and self-reported information that complement each other. Despite modest participation rates in both NFBCs, the study sample provided a good representation of all SES classes and both genders when compared to national Figures [45]. Therefore, findings from this study can be generalized to the young adult Finnish population. There are several limitations in this study. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of this study prevents the establishment of causality, limiting our ability to determine whether the choice of oral healthcare services directly influences OHRQoL. Secondly, due to geographical limitations for the clinical examination, which were 100 km for (NFBC1966) and 250 km for (NFBC1986), the study may be subject to selection bias. One-month reference period used for the OHIP-14 may be subject to recall bias. The large dropout rate in the NFBCs can also be considered as limitation in this study. Additionally, the household income was not adjusted for household size. Dental fear and anxiety could act as potential confounder between choice of oral healthcare service and OHRQoL, as these psychosocial factors may have related to both the utilization of services and the perceived quality of life. However, this aspect was not considered in this study. Another limitation of the current study could be due to the estimates of oral health status based on clinical examination findings without radiographic assessment. While this approach is justified due to the large sample size, it may underestimate the prevalence of interproximal caries and periodontal bone loss, leading to missing diagnostic information. Not including place of residence of participants can also be considered one of the limitations. One more notable limitation of this study is the low inter-rater reliability observed in caries lesions and periodontal pocket depth measurements. These results may reflect genuine measurement error, raising concerns about the generalizability of the findings among broader populations. Finally, not considering all potential confounders such as dietary factors and fluoride exposure in the SEM may be another limitation here. Future studies should incorporate dietary and fluoride exposure data to better understand variations in caries and periodontal disease prevalence.

It was evident from the present study that oral treatment need has a link on OHRQoL and most individuals seeking oral treatment chose the public sector. It is therefore recommended to conduct longitudinal studies that can support or refute the findings of this study. Future longitudinal studies can consider factors such as socioeconomic trajectories, access to care, and oral health-related behaviors at multiple time points, as these factors may influence the choice and consequently impact oral health outcomes and OHRQoL.

Conclusions

In the cohorts studied, the association between oral health treatment needs and OHRQoL persisted, independent of choosing either public or private oral healthcare service. Additionally, participants in this study chose public oral healthcare services mainly when oral health treatment needs were present. Furthermore, the results of this study also highlight a greater need for restorative treatment in younger cohorts compared to older cohorts.
