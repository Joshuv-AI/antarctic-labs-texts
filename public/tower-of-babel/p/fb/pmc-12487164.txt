The relationship between pregnancy complaints and obsessive-compulsive behaviors during the postpartum period regarding baby care: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Gamze Ceylan, Ayla Kanbur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is known that complaints (nausea, vomiting, frequent urination, breast tenderness, etc.) that occur during pregnancy affect anxiety and mood changes related to childbirth. Complaints may impact women’s adaptation to their new situations and roles in the postpartum period. The inability to cope with the postpartum period may cause increased complaints of obsessive-compulsive disorder in women. The present study investigated the relationship between pregnancy complaints and obsessive-compulsive behaviors during the postpartum period regarding baby care.MethodsThis descriptive, cross-sectional and relational study was conducted with the participation of 265 women receiving prenatal care in an urban hospital in eastern Türkiye. At the first stage of this two-stage study, pregnant women in the last trimester were recruited, and their pregnancy complaints were examined. At the second stage, women were contacted between 2 and 8 weeks postpartum, and obsessive-compulsive behaviors related to baby care were examined. Data were analyzed with the mean, Pearson’s correlation, and linear regression analyses.ResultsThe mean score for the frequency of women’s experiencing complaints during pregnancy was 37.71 ± 12.68. The mean score for women’s postpartum obsessive and compulsive behaviors was 14.06 ± 5.60. It was found that the presence of complaints during pregnancy was a positive predictor of postpartum obsessive-compulsive behaviors and explained 15% of the variance in obsessive-compulsive behaviors (adjusted R2 = 0.015, F = 5.011, p < 0.05).ConclusionWith the increased frequency of experiencing complaints during pregnancy, the rate of obsessive and compulsive behaviors of mothers toward baby care increases during the postpartum period. It is important to screen women for obsessive-compulsive disorder in the postpartum period.

Background

A number of physiological changes occur in the woman’s body during pregnancy [1]. These changes that occur during pregnancy may cause pregnancy complaints [2]. Aforementioned physiological changes specific to pregnancy could be the source of psychological changes. Whereas some women easily adapt to these physiological changes during pregnancy, some women may suffer from mood disorders due to these physiological changes [3, 4]. While complaints such as breast tenderness, vomiting, frequency urination, ptyalism, leukorrhea are experienced in the early period of pregnancy; heartburn, varicose veins, back pain, hemorrhoids, constipation, cramps are experienced more frequently in the last trimester of pregnancy [5]. Furthermore, if women cannot cope with pregnancy complaints effectively, ambivalent feelings can be observed in pregnant women, and the pregnancy period may turn into a crisis for the mother, possibly leading to the deterioration of mental health and postpartum psychiatric disorders [3, 4]. After the physiological complaints they experience during pregnancy, women try to adapt to their new situations and roles after childbirth. The inability to cope with this process in the postpartum period may cause increased complaints of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) in women [6].

It has been reported that life events such as pregnancy and the postpartum period are effective in the development of OCD [7]. Furthermore, it has been that the perinatal period is a risk factor for the onset or exacerbation of OCD and women during pregnancy and the postpartum period are 1.5-2 times more likely to experience OCD than women in the general population [7–9]. OCD during the postpartum period is an anxiety disorder that begins or tends to worsen after childbirth [10, 11]. The prevalence of OCD in the postpartum period is reported to be between 0.7 and 9% [7, 10, 12]. Studies show that 2.2–16.9% of women experience OCD after childbirth [9, 13–15]. There are many biological, psychological and environmental risk factors that play a role in the emergence or exacerbation of OCD during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Rapid changes in hormones (estrogen, progesterone, prolactin, oxytocin) and fluctuations in serotonin levels can trigger OCD symptoms. A family history of OCD or anxiety also increases genetic predisposition. Psychologically, increased anxiety, a sense of loss of control, responsibilities and fear of harming the baby are at the forefront. In particular, involuntary obsessions and compulsions related to them can disrupt the mother’s functionality. A previous history of OCD also increases the risk. Socially, factors such as inadequate support, relationship problems, economic difficulties, traumatic birth and unplanned pregnancy can increase stress and pave the way for the development of OCD [16–18].

Pregnancy and the postpartum period are high-risk periods for the emergence or exacerbation of obsessive-compulsive disorder symptoms in women [10, 12, 19]. Postpartum OCD negatively affects the health of the baby [19, 20]. In the postpartum period, aggressive attitudes and infection obsessions are common in women with OCD, and the desire to clean and check are among the most common compulsions [11, 21]. OCD symptoms seen during this period usually occur in the form of fear of harming the baby, contamination obsessions, anxiety about making mistakes in care, and involuntary thoughts of aggressive or sexual content. In response to these obsessions, mothers may develop compulsions such as frequent cleaning, constant checking, avoidance behaviors, and seeking reassurance. While loss of control and increased sense of responsibility trigger these symptoms during pregnancy, excessive concerns about the baby’s safety are especially prominent in the postpartum period. These symptoms can disrupt the mother’s functionality, negatively affect the motherhood experience, and weaken the mother-baby bond [8, 12]. Abramowitz et al. [22] observed that 69% of new mothers experienced unwanted intrusive thoughts about their babies. One study showed at women had thoughts that the baby would die or have an accident during the postpartum period and had a frequent urge to check the baby’s breathing [12]. Therefore, early recognition and intervention of OCD symptoms during pregnancy and the postpartum period is critical for the mental health of both the mother and the baby [8, 12].

The term postpartum psychiatric disorders usually refers to cases of maternity blues, postpartum depression, or psychosis. This narrow conceptualization of postpartum disorders generally excludes other psychiatric disorders that occur after birth, which causes the necessary screening for OCD not to be performed and to be overlooked [23]. Moreover, another gap in the field is that risk factors for postpartum baby care-related OCD are rarely addressed. It is a well-known fact that complaints during pregnancy are associated with psychiatric diseases. Hence, it is very important in terms of the literature to reveal the relationship between the complaints experienced during pregnancy and OCD, which occurs at an undeniable frequency in the postpartum period.

By following the pregnant woman in the antenatal period, questioning the pregnancy complaints and putting the women’s coping mechanisms into action will contribute to reducing postpartum psychiatric diseases and, therefore, to the health of the mother and infant. The present study aimed to reveal the effects of pregnancy complaints on obsessive-compulsive behaviors related to postpartum baby care.

Methods

Study design

The current research is a descriptive, cross-sectional, and relational study. The study was conducted between January 2022 and May 2022 in the NST (please use long version first) unit of a hospital in eastern Türkiye. This hospital is a regional hospital and serves people from all socio-economic levels.

Population and sample group

The study population consisted of all pregnant women who were admitted to the NST (non-stress test) unit, and the study sample comprised pregnant women who were admitted to the relevant unit and met the inclusion criteria. A priori power analysis was performed to determine the sample size of the study. As a result of the power analysis performed according to Cohen’s standard effect size reference method [24], it was calculated that data should be collected from approximately 260 pregnant women to reach a significance level of 0.05, an effect size of 0.2, a confidence interval of 95%, and a power of 90%.

The study was carried out in two stages. At the first stage, 331 pregnant women between 28 and 40 weeks were interviewed. 31 pregnant women were excluded from the study because they did not meet the study criteria (illiterate − 8 women, high-risk pregnancy − 17 women, being under 28 weeks of gestation − 6 women). At this stage, data were collected from 300 pregnant women. At the second stage of the study, data collection continued by reaching the same pregnant women between 2 and 8 weeks postpartum. At this stage, 35 data were lost due to various reasons (incorrect contact information-18 women, the inability to be reached by phone-11 women, withdrawal from the study-6 women). The study was completed with a total of 265 women. In the post hoc power analysis, it was determined that the power of the study was 0.95 at a significance level of 0.05 and a confidence interval of 95%. With these values, it was concluded that the study’s sample was sufficient [24].

Criteria for inclusion in the study for the pregnancy period

Being literate, being over the age of 18, being within 28–40 weeks of gestation (It was preferred due to the intensification of pregnancy-related complaints and the increased prominence of OCD symptoms), being willing to participate in the study.

Criteria for inclusion in the study for the postpartum period

Being within 2–8 weeks postpartum, breastfeeding.

Criteria for exclusion from the study

Being illiterate, having a high-risk pregnancy, being under 28 weeks of gestation, being over 8 weeks postpartum, having a psychiatric disease diagnosed during pregnancy and postpartum period (Psychiatric diseases are based on clinical history) (High-risk pregnancies and psychiatric disorders were excluded as they could introduce ambiguity in the results).

Measures

Personal information form

It is a form consisting of questions containing women’s descriptive characteristics. The form consists of questions regarding age, pregnancy or postpartum period information, educational status, employment status, family type, number of pregnancies, pregnancy planning status, prenatal care status, social support status for pregnancy and baby care, and breastfeeding status.

Pregnancy symptoms inventory (PSI)

It was developed by Foxcroft et al. [25] to evaluate the frequency of complaints experienced during pregnancy and the effect of these complaints on daily activities. Can Gürkan and Ekşi Güloğlu [26] performed a validity and reliability study for the Turkish version of the scale. The scale can be applied to pregnant women between 12 and 40 weeks. The 42-item scale consists of two sections. In the first section of the PSI, the frequency of complaints experienced during pregnancy (fatigue/tiredness, insomnia, breast tenderness, vomiting, frequency urination, leukorrhea, varicose veins, edema, back pain, low back pain, leg pain and cramps, insomnia, hemorrhoids, constipation etc.) is scored between 0 and 3 on a 4-point Likert scale. An increase in the score in this section, where a score between 0 and 126 is obtained, indicates that the frequency of experiencing complaints increases. In the second section of the PSI, the limitation of daily activities by complaints is scored between 1 and 3 on a 3-point Likert scale. An increase in the score in this section, where a score between 42 and 126 is obtained, means that the complaints experienced limit daily activities [22]. In the validity and reliability study of the Turkish version of the scale, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.82 [26], and 0.86 in this study.

Obsessive and compulsive behaviors scale of mothers in postpartum period regarding baby care

It was developed by Özdemir et al. [21] to determine the obsessive and compulsive behaviors of mothers regarding baby care in the postpartum period. The scale can be applied to mothers between 2 and 8 weeks postpartum. The 9-item scale has one subscale and is in a five-point Likert type. A score between 9 and 45 can be obtained from the scale, and a high score indicates that mothers in the postpartum period have more obsessive and compulsive behaviors regarding baby care. It is recommended that mothers with a score above 9 on the scale undergo a detailed evaluation. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the original scale was 0.75 [21], and 0.84 in this study.

Data collection

The study was conducted in two stages. At the first stage, the personal information form and the Pregnancy Symptoms Inventory were applied to pregnant women by interviewing them face-to-face between 28 and 40 weeks in the NST unit. At this stage, the contact information of pregnant women was also recorded. At the second stage, the pregnant women, whose contact information was obtained, were contacted by phone between 2 and 8 weeks postpartum, and the Obsessive and Compulsive Behaviors Scale of Mothers in Postpartum Period Regarding Baby Care was applied. Although telephone interviews increase participant accessibility, they may increase the risk of social desirability bias, particularly in maternal health research where participants may be more likely to present themselves in a way that conforms to social norms. To minimize the limitations inherent in telephone interviews, all interviews were conducted using a standard script that included neutral, non-directive language, and researchers were trained to maintain a professional and non-judgmental tone. While practical constraints did not permit the use of blinded, independent interviewers, safeguards were implemented, including the use of structured interview protocols and consistent administration procedures across participants. Interviews were scheduled at a time convenient for the participants. All stages adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Figure 1 shows the flowchart of the research.

Research variables

Independent variable

Pregnancy symptoms inventory mean score.

Dependent variable

Obsessive and compulsive behaviors scale mean score.

Data analysis

The data were evaluated using the SPSS 20 (Statistical Package for Social Science) software. For the normality values of the data, skewness – kurtosis values were examined. For the normal distribution of the data, the skewness – kurtosis values should be between − 2 and + 2 [27]. The skewness value for the complaints section of the Pregnancy Symptoms Inventory was 0.382, and the kurtosis value was − 0.244; the skewness value for the limitation of daily activities section was 1.069, and the kurtosis value was 1.045; the skewness value for the Obsessive and Compulsive Behaviors Scale was 1.368, and the kurtosis value was 1.960.

Percentage distribution, mean and standard deviation were used to determine the sample characteristics and the scores obtained from the scales. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between the Pregnancy Symptoms Inventory and obsessive and compulsive behaviors. The level of significance was accepted as p < 0.05.

As a result of the analysis performed, a significant relationship was determined between pregnancy complaints and obsessive and compulsive behaviors (p < 0.05). This significant finding was evaluated by simple linear regression analysis.

Results

The mean age of women was 28.34 ± 4.69 years. At the first stage of the study, the mean gestational week of women was 35.25 ± 2.57, and the mean postpartum days in the postpartum period were 37.99 ± 7.26. Of women, 35.2% were university graduates, and 84.2% had a nuclear family. It was the second pregnancy for 38.9% of women, 72.5% stated that they planned their pregnancy, and 90.9% indicated that they received regular prenatal care. Of women, 86.8% stated that they received social support during pregnancy, and 95.8% indicated that they received social support during baby care (Table 1). There is no difference between descriptive characteristics with OCD and pregnancy complaints.

It was observed that a significant proportion of the women participating in the study experienced various complaints during pregnancy. Of the participants, 64.5% reported fatigue/tiredness, 50.2% reflux, 62.3% insomnia, 84.9% frequent urination, 66% increased vaginal discharge, 30.6% back pain, and 37.4% low back pain. Discussion

Physiological, psychological, and anatomical changes caused by pregnancy in the female organism cause various maternal complaints in pregnant women, called pregnancy complaints. Considering the studies on the complaints experienced during pregnancy, it is seen that there are various complaints in pregnant women, including fatigue in 40.4–87%, sleep problems in 51.7%, frequent urination in 75-93.9%, vaginal discharge in 45.3–86.4%, heartburn in 58.4–79.1%, leg cramps in 31.5–72.8%, and nausea-vomiting in 39.4–66.1% [25, 26]. In the present study, it can be said that the frequency of experiencing complaints during pregnancy was low in women. Furthermore, it is seen that complaints experienced during pregnancy do not cause many limitations on daily activities. It is thought that complaints such as nausea and vomiting, heartburn, and vaginal discharge in women decreased during this period since the mean gestational week of the women enrolled in the study was 35.25 ± 2.57. Particularly in the third trimester, expectant mothers both want to hold their infants in their arms as soon as possible and also experience fear and anxiety about the labor, feeding of the infant, basic care, and communication with the infant. This may change the way women perceive their pregnancy complaints.

The pregnancy and postpartum period is a natural life crisis in which psychiatric disorders are commonly observed. The complex interaction of past experiences and current biological, psychological, and social factors may leave women vulnerable to the onset or exacerbation of mental disorders during the perinatal period. OCD, an anxiety disorder characterized by obsessions and compulsions, is among the psychiatric disorders frequently observed during pregnancy and the postpartum period [10, 13–15]. It has been reported that the perinatal period is a risk factor for the onset or exacerbation of OCD, and women during pregnancy and the postpartum period are 1.5-2 times more likely to experience OCD compared to the general population [7–9]. Unlike OCD during pregnancy, OCD in the postpartum period has been the focus of researchers [8].

The women in the postpartum period enrolled in the study were reached within 2–8 weeks, and the mean postpartum days were 37.99 ± 2.57. In a study examining OCD symptoms in the postpartum period, Sichel et al. [28] found the mean onset time of OCD to be 2.2 weeks postpartum. Wenzel et al. [29] found the rate of subsyndromal OCD at 8 weeks postpartum to be 5.4%.

Considering the study results, it can be said that the women within the scope of the study demonstrate obsessive and compulsive behaviors regarding baby care in the postpartum period. The score of 181 women was found to be above 9, and a detailed evaluation is needed. According to a recent study, the prevalence of OCD in the postpartum period was found to be 16.9% [13]. Uğuz et al. [30] reported that OCD symptoms started in 4% of the women they reached within the first 6 weeks postpartum. Zambaldi et al. [12] determined the prevalence of postpartum OCD to be 9% in a group of 400 women followed up to 26 weeks postpartum.

The main source of obsessions observed in mothers in the postpartum period consists of concerns about the infant. This causes urges and thoughts to be experienced, such as dropping the infant, the infant dying or having an accident, and checking the infant’s breathing [12]. Sichel et al. [28] reported that postpartum women had aggressive-obsessive thoughts about any harm to the newborn. A retrospective study demonstrated that contamination obsession was associated with the onset of perinatal OCD [31]. Postpartum psychiatric studies generally focus on anxiety and depression. However, postpartum psychiatric conditions are not limited to these, and particularly OCD has a prevalence that should not be ignored. Considering that studies on postpartum obsessive and compulsive behaviors are limited in the literature, the present study takes an important place in terms of presenting new information to the literature since it was conducted on obsessive and compulsive behaviors of mothers related to postpartum baby care.

As a result of the study, it was concluded that the frequency of experiencing complaints during pregnancy increases the tendency to exhibit obsessive and compulsive behaviors. According to the results of the linear regression analysis, pregnancy complaints explain 15% of the total variance in obsessive-compulsive behaviors. Women who have to cope with maternal complaints during pregnancy may be prone to psychiatric problems in the postpartum period with the addition of anxiety about issues such as feeding and caring for the infant. It is known that complaints that occur during pregnancy affect anxiety and mood changes related to childbirth [4]. If the woman cannot cope with pregnancy complaints effectively, events such as childbirth and becoming a parent can turn into a crisis for the mother [3, 4].

After the physiological changes they experience during pregnancy, women try to adapt to their new situations, roles, and responsibilities in the postpartum period [6]. The inability to cope with this process in the postpartum period may cause an increase in OCD symptoms. Obsessive mothers may exhibit obsessive-compulsive behaviors such as insomnia, frequent hand washing, not being able to spare time for themselves, and not being able to touch their infants because they are worried about any harm to their infants [10].

Aggressive obsessions (such as dropping the infant, throwing, and imaginary images that harm the infant) and compulsions such as avoiding kitchen knives, not washing the infant, and controlling the infant’s breathing and body, which are frequently encountered in the postpartum period [7, 12, 28], can cause mothers to move away from their infants, adversely impacting mother-infant health and disrupting the mother-infant bond [32]. In the literature review, it is seen that postpartum OCD studies are limited, and there is no study examining the relationship between pregnancy complaints and postpartum OCD. Therefore, the study results are very meaningful and valuable in terms of shedding light on the literature and providing new information.

Limitations

Since the Pregnancy Symptom Inventory is a scale that can only be applied after the 12th week of pregnancy, the fact that pregnant women in the first trimester, when pregnancy complaints are experienced most, were not included in the study is a limitation of the study. Also limitation is filling the data by the telephone interview method at the second stage of the study. In addition, the fact that a longer-term follow-up could not be carried out in relation to the duration of the study constitutes another limitation of the study.

Conclusion

In the postpartum period, mothers’ scores for obsessive and compulsive behaviors regarding baby care are high. With the increased frequency of pregnancy complaints, the rate of obsessive and compulsive behaviors of mothers toward baby care increases in the postpartum period. Pregnancy complaints explain 15% of the total variance in obsessive and compulsive behaviors regarding postpartum infant care. It is important to evaluate pregnant women psychologically during antenatal follow-ups, identify pregnant women at risk for psychiatric disorders, provide counseling services for those at risk, and screen women for OCD in the postpartum period. It is recommended that postpartum OCD be studied in larger populations in terms of different factors and dimensions. In addition, examining the effects of each symptom experienced during pregnancy on obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCD) separately could provide significant contributions to both clinical practice and mental health research. Such an analysis would allow for a more detailed understanding of the specific factors that affect psychological well-being during pregnancy. In this context, revealing the possible relationships of each symptom with OCD symptoms could contribute to the development of more targeted approaches to both diagnostic processes and intervention strategies. Therefore, this topic is suggested as an important area of ​​research to be considered for future studies.
