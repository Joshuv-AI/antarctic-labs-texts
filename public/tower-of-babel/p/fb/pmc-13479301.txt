Effects of a school-based water and hygiene intervention on the health and attendance of preschool pupils in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a cluster-randomised controlled trial
Authors: Sarah Bick, Alem Ezezew, Charles Opondo, Baptiste Leurent, Wossen Argaw, Oliver Cumming, Elizabeth Allen, Robert Dreibelbis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren under the age of 5 bear the highest burden of disease related to inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH). However, evidence of the effectiveness of WASH interventions in preschool settings is limited. Nested within a cluster-randomised controlled trial in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, this substudy evaluated the impact of a water and hygiene intervention on health and school attendance among preschool children.MethodsSchools were randomly assigned 1:1 to receive the intervention during the 2021/2022 academic year or the following year (waitlist control). We included schools enrolled in the main trial with preschool classes and randomly selected and allocated additional schools with preschool classes to reach 20 schools in each arm. The intervention comprised school-wide infrastructure improvements (water storage and filtration systems, drinking water and handwashing stations) and hygiene behaviour change promotion. Planned sanitation improvements were not delivered within the trial duration. Within each school, we randomly selected 20 preschool pupils (ages 3–6), recording caregiver-reported diarrhoea, respiratory illness and absence over four consecutive weekly telephone interviews with caregivers of the selected pupils from April to June 2022, following intervention delivery.ResultsWe found no evidence of effects on caregiver-reported diarrhoea (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 0.85; 95% CI 0.46 to 1.59; p=0.61), respiratory illness (aOR 0.84; 95% CI 0.60 to 1.17; p=0.30) or absence (aOR 0.75; 95% CI 0.51 to 1.08; p=0.13).ConclusionsDropout of three schools and challenges in telephone-based data collection resulted in large loss to follow-up, reducing statistical power. Further research should examine improving telephone-based methods to obtain sufficient follow-up data for school-based evaluations.Keywords: DIARRHEA, CHILD HEALTH, ENVIRONMENTAL HEALTH, EDUCATION, RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIAL

Background

Children under 5 years continue to bear the highest burden of disease due to inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH), accounting for almost 400 000 deaths and 36 million disability-adjusted life-years annually.1 WASH interventions in both domestic and public settings have been associated with reductions in acute respiratory infection, diarrhoeal disease and mortality.2–4 Evaluations of the impact of WASH interventions in educational settings have typically focused on older children in primary and secondary schools.5 However, children attending preprimary education programmes spend larger portions of the day outside of the domestic environment and in close contact with other children,6 which may be implicated in the spread of infectious illness among the preschool children and their wider communities.7

Children in preschools may be exposed to greater disease risks than those at home,8 as numerous young children prone to repeated infections interact closely throughout the day, often in overcrowded spaces.9–11 Young children frequently ingest pathogenic material by touching their mouths with hands or objects in environments with widespread faecal contamination.8 12 Activities such as sorting and arranging toys, and the duties of preschool educators, such as assisting with toilet routines, meals and respiratory hygiene, may create opportunities for transmission.8 In many high burden settings, lack of basic handwashing facilities with soap and water and age-appropriate toilet facilities, noted in multiple settings,7 10 13 contributes to inadequate hygiene practices among children and staff. The lack of adequate WASH services presents a neglected opportunity to create safe learning environments that are a key target of the sustainable development goals.6 14

These burdens extend beyond just the direct health impact. Illness is assumed to be a significant driver of missing preprimary school and improving attendance at preschool through reducing pupil illness could help secure some of the benefits of early childhood development programmes: increased literacy and development outcomes, improved school enrolment and achievement, and enhanced productivity in adult life.15 16 Gaps in preschool education are associated with later learning difficulties, preventing children from attaining their full learning potential.8 15 Consequently, inadequate provision of WASH in preschools may impact young children’s health, well-being and life trajectory, and also present a missed opportunity to establish positive WASH-related habits at a critical moment for behavioural establishment prior to school-entry age.6 9

Evidence suggests handwashing interventions in daycares and schools can reduce the burden of WASH-related morbidity in children, but available data are primarily from low-burden settings with low environmental contamination and robust access to WASH infrastructure.17 Recent decades have seen significant growth in the number of children enrolled in preprimary education programmes, with a 2.4-fold increase since 2000 in low-income countries where infrastructure access is lower and disease burden is higher. There is limited data, however, on effective WASH programmes among preschool children in high-burden settings,9 and the few published impact evaluations focus primarily on hygiene,9 11 18–20 with two promoting hand sanitiser exclusively.11 19 In this substudy of a cluster-randomised controlled trial (cRCT),21 we present evidence of the impact of a large-scale WASH infrastructure and behaviour change project in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, on the health and attendance of preschool pupils (aged 3–6 years). The primary outcomes of the main trial were measured on primary school pupils (aged 7–16 years) and were reported previously.21

‘Project WISE (WASH in Schools for Everyone)’, implemented by US-based NGO Splash in Addis Ababa public schools, was delivered to predefined groups of schools on an annual basis. We hypothesised that the intervention, which included water and sanitation infrastructure and behaviour change promotion, would improve the health and attendance of preschool pupils.

Methods

Study design

Within the context of a cluster-randomised trial assessing intervention impacts on primary school-aged children (WISE evaluation21), we conducted a substudy to specifically assess impacts on preschool pupils. This substudy was a parallel, two-arm school-based cRCT, with 40 public preschools in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia constituting the study clusters—23 schools were primary schools with a preschool recruited as part of the larger trial, and 17 were additional preschools recruited specifically for this study. We used a cluster-randomised design because the intervention evaluated was delivered at the school level and comprised changes to the whole school environment. Methods for the main trial have been described previously.21

The trial was conducted over the course of one Ethiopian academic year (November 2021 to July 2022; schools were open from September to July), and during the active phase of the COVID-19 pandemic following school re-opening. In each school, 20 preschool pupils were randomly selected and enrolled into a cohort, and data were collected from caregivers, identified in school records or reported by household contacts as the person with primary responsibility for the selected child, over the course of four consecutive weekly telephone interviews post-intervention (April to June 2022), concurrently in intervention and control arms. We used remote computer-assisted telephone interviews (data collectors completed surveys on tablets during the interview) to collect data from caregivers of preschool pupils, who cannot report on their own health, and to minimise COVID-19 risk.

Participants, randomisation and masking

The study population comprised preschool pupils aged 3–6 years (and their caregivers) attending schools due to receive the WISE intervention in either the 2021/2022 or 2022/2023 academic years. Selection of schools and randomisation was conducted in July 2021, using a random number generator in Stata V.18.0 (StataCorp), and followed a two-stage approach, because the number of schools eligible for the main trial that had preschool classes was not sufficient to attain the prespecified sample size for this substudy.

From the list of 143 schools due to receive the intervention, we first excluded schools without pupils in grades 2–8, schools that received a WASH intervention in the 3 years prior to study activities and schools that provided education to vulnerable populations only. We then randomly selected 60 schools of 83 eligible schools for enrolment in the main trial. Randomisation was stratified by school size (< or ≥1200 pupils across primary grades) and presence of a preschool (to facilitate this substudy), creating four strata. Within strata, the schools were randomly ranked, and the first half of the schools was assigned to the intervention (implementation during the 2021/2022 academic year). The remaining schools were assigned to the waitlist control arm, to receive the intervention in the 2022/2023 academic year, after study completion. Among the 60 schools in the main trial, we enrolled the 23 schools in the two strata with preschool classes in this substudy. Second, we randomly selected 17 additional schools with preschool classes, also excluding schools with a recent WASH intervention or serving vulnerable populations only, from the original list (44 of the remaining 83 schools were eligible) to meet the sample size of 20 schools in each arm. These schools were randomly ranked, and the first nine schools were assigned to the intervention arm.

All eligible schools had consented to the receipt of the Project WISE intervention. Once the random allocation had been performed, formal consent for participation in the substudy was sought from school principals in loco parentis, on behalf of all pupils in the school. Schools were enrolled in the substudy in March 2022, while follow-up was ongoing for the main trial. School principals received guidance for communicating to parents, and parental information sheets and opt-out forms were distributed to preschool classes. Pupils were excluded from data collection if their parent or guardian returned the opt-out form at any point and oral consent was obtained from the selected caregivers during telephone interviews.

Within each participating school, we obtained class rosters and caregiver contact information for preschool classes and randomly selected 20 preschool pupils for enrolment through simple random sampling across preschool classes, with a backup list of 10 pupils in case of refusals. Pupils were included in data collection regardless of age as age was not typically listed in rosters, but only pupils aged 3–6 at enrolment were included in analysis.

Investigators performing the randomisation had no prior knowledge of any of the study schools. Masking of school administrators or those delivering the intervention was not possible. Caregivers (outcome reporters) were not informed of treatment assignment, but might have inferred it, for example, from the distinctive WASH infrastructure components installed in intervention schools.21

Intervention components

As previously described,21 the Project WISE intervention combined infrastructure and behaviour change promotion activities, so that handwashing and drinking exclusively from filtered water became normative behaviours. The intervention was delivered at the school level, so all children attending school, including preschool pupils, were exposed to the intervention regardless of trial participation.

Infrastructure components included correcting water storage capacity deficits through water storage tanks; water filtration systems for drinking water; and durable plastic drinking water and handwashing stations with specific features differentiating their use and installed with taps to meet sufficient tap-to-pupil ratios. The intervention also includes provision of new or rehabilitated toilet facilities to meet standards; however, this component is managed by the Addis Ababa Education Bureau on a separate timeline and was not delivered to intervention schools until after the evaluation period.

Intervention staff conducted a site engagement meeting with school administration and worked with the school to organise a family ‘soap drive’, whereby families of pupils are encouraged to donate hygiene products to the school to ensure availability of products throughout the year. School janitors, maintenance staff and food handlers also received training on hygiene and operation and maintenance of infrastructure. Behaviour change activities with older pupils included formation of ‘hygiene clubs’ and promotion of menstrual hygiene management.

School engagement and training began in November 2021, and all infrastructure components were installed (excluding sanitation infrastructure) and core training modules delivered by January 2022 in all 20 intervention schools, 3 months prior to outcome data collection. Further details of intervention components and programme standards can be found alongside the main trial publication.21

Data collection

Between May and July 2021, data collection tools and methods for school and pupil enrolment and routine follow-up surveys were piloted in three randomly selected schools not included in the primary impact evaluation. During piloting, in-country data collection partners followed full study procedures outlined below, with one minor variation: only two telephone interviews were conducted 1 week apart. The study pilot was used to assess the logistics of data collection and verify assumptions made in sample size calculations.

Caregivers were contacted by telephone once per week for four consecutive weeks between April and June 2022 and information on the selected child collected on tablets. During the first interview, consent was collected using a script, and caregivers were enrolled in the study. These surveys were used to create a digital roster of enrolled caregivers, which was automatically updated with data on number and timing of completed calls. A pupil identification number was assigned internally to all pupils on the roster to anonymously link their data across surveys. Several repeat telephone calls (median 4 attempts recorded among caregivers not reached due to non-response) and evening calls were made if caregivers could not be reached during the day. Additional data collection days were organised and extended over several weeks beyond the original data collection period in order to obtain complete data.

The first interview included demographic information and household WASH access, and caregiver-reported illness and absence data. The three follow-up interviews collected illness and absence data only. The selected child’s age or date of birth (if known) and gender were reported by caregivers. Age at enrolment was calculated, and analysis was restricted to pupils aged 3–6.

Outcomes

All outcomes were measured at the individual level. Outcomes were caregiver-reported diarrhoea (defined as the occurrence of at least three loose stools in a 24-hour period), respiratory illness (defined as the occurrence of cough, sneezing or rhinorrhoea) and absence from preschool (full days absent). During each weekly telephone interview, caregivers were asked for the number of days with illness/absence over the past week, and the number of days reporting each outcome over the 4 weeks was summed to a total number of events over the 28-day period. All outcomes were therefore reported as number of days with illness/absence over the reporting period, which was a maximum of 28 days for illness outcomes and approximately 20 days for absence (depending on maximum days in preschool per week). We excluded calls that were conducted earlier than planned as recall periods were overlapping. We included calls conducted later than planned and assumed that the reporting period was continuous in analysis, although there were gaps between weekly recalls for many participants. To assess balance between intervention and control schools, gender parity in preschool enrolment was assessed using the adjusted gender parity index.22

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculations were based on estimating the mean difference between arms in pupil-level proportions of days with illness or absence across the reporting period. Assuming a mean pupil-level proportion of days with diarrhoea in the control group of 0.08 (SD 0.05), a two-sided type I error (α) of 0.05, and an intracluster correlation coefficient of 0.15, we estimated 40 schools (20 per arm) with 20 children per school was sufficient to detect a reduction in the mean proportion of days with diarrhoea of 0.019 (standardised effect size 0.38).

The statistical analysis plan was preregistered on 1 March 202323 before allocation was revealed. Analyses were done by intention-to-treat, analysing schools according to their randomisation. Characteristics of the children and schools at enrolment were summarised by treatment arm. Statistical analyses of the outcomes were conducted at the individual level with mixed effects regression modelling using a binomial model, that is, number of days with illness/absent with offset of number of days of reporting. We additionally carried out a confirmatory analysis comparing the mean proportion of days with illness/absent per pupil in the intervention and control arms. Analyses included a random effect for school. Primary estimates of effectiveness were calculated using a model adjusting for stratification factors alone: school size and whether the school was selected as part of the main study. Further adjustments in secondary analyses were made for gender and child age in years. The prespecified analysis plan23 included further adjustment for school location by subcity; however, the low numbers of schools in some subcities following withdrawal of three schools did not permit this analysis. Additional adjustments for caregiver gender and education level were performed in exploratory analysis. Interaction tests were used to examine the differential effect of the intervention by gender. We examined factors associated with missing outcome data after the second telephone interview. We performed sensitivity analysis excluding all data from the final telephone interview (call 4), in order to examine the impact of large losses to follow-up observed after this point. Sensitivity analysis to include all pupils regardless of age, was also performed. We used Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp) for all analyses.

The trial protocol (Additional file 1) is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, number NCT05024890. Deidentified participant data and code to reproduce analyses using Stata are available in a public, open access repository.24

Results

Of the 143 schools due to receive the intervention in 2021/2022 or 2022/2023, 83 were primary schools eligible for the main trial (with grades 2–8), and 60 of them were enrolled and randomised in the main trial, including 23 schools (11 intervention and 12 control) that also had preschool classes (included in this substudy; online supplemental figure S1). We selected 17 additional schools from among 44 schools from the original list that were eligible for the substudy (with a preschool, regardless of primary grades 2–8), and randomised 9 to the intervention and 8 to the control arm. Following randomisation, three schools withdrew: one intervention school did not have preschool classes at the time of enrolment, and two control schools were unwilling or unable to provide contact details for caregivers of preschool pupils. The remaining 37 schools provided contact details and 30 preschool pupils per school were randomly selected. Data collection began several weeks late for four schools (three intervention, one control) that delayed in providing caregiver contacts.

Incomplete or invalid contact numbers meant that only some contacts could be attempted (median 21 per school; figure 1). Data collectors made 1124 initial call attempts, of which 616 resulted in successful enrolment of a caregiver (median 17 per school), and 525 of these enrolments were later determined to be eligible by age of the child (median 15 per school). Wrong numbers and non-response despite repeated attempts were the most common reasons for the unexpectedly low enrolment (figure 1). There was also considerable loss to follow-up of 124 (45%) control and 119 (47%) intervention participants over the four telephone interviews, partially due to delays to the start of data collection leaving no time for subsequent interviews, despite additional data collection days. For the final interview, errors in fetching updating server datasets led to 33 interviews being conducted too early and data being discarded. However, the analysis included all eligible participants regardless of number of calls completed. The number of pupils contributing data to outcome assessments is shown for each time point in figure 1 and in analysis tables.

Trial profile–pupils.

There were more boys than girls enrolled in the study (56.2% male), and the mean school-level adjusted ratio of female to male pupils enrolled across the whole preschool was 0.94 (6% fewer female preschool pupils than male; table 1). Caregivers reporting on child outcomes were almost always mothers or fathers, with a roughly even split between the two. Most households had a basic sanitation service (75%) and a basic water supply (56%), but few households had a handwashing facility with access to soap and water (18%). There was a difference in preschool size across arms, as well as slight differences in caregiver gender and education; other characteristics were balanced (table 1). Characteristics of participants missing after the second interview were similar to those present (data not shown).

Discussion

In this substudy of a cluster-randomised trial, we report the impact of a large school-based WASH infrastructure and behaviour change programme on the health and attendance of preschool pupils. We found no evidence of an effect of this intervention on caregiver-reported diarrhoea, respiratory illness and absence among preschool pupils. Evidence that effects may differ by child gender mirrors the main study21 and remains unexplained. This finding warrants further investigation into whether differences by gender in young children may be related to the functioning of the intervention or differential reporting by caregivers.

Findings from this evaluation reflect the mixed impacts observed in other preschool WASH evaluations in low and middle-income countries,9 11 18–20 as well as evaluations among older children.5 Among preschool studies, randomised trials evaluating multiple hand hygiene and surface cleaning interventions in China,18 and hand sanitiser provision as a complement to handwashing with soap (HWWS) in Colombia19 both found significant reductions in diarrhoea and respiratory illness. However, the provision of supplies and promotion of HWWS in Israel did not significantly impact illness-related absence.20 This substudy contrasts with the main trial among older children, in which we observed evidence of a significant reduction in pupil-reported respiratory illness, and no effects on diarrhoea or absence.21 In both studies, impacts on diarrhoea may be precluded by the delayed delivery of the sanitation component until after the evaluation, as sanitation facilities are necessary to adequately sequester faecal waste and prevent contamination.4

Limitations of this study include the short follow-up period post-intervention and reliance on caregiver-reported outcome measures, which may present higher risk of bias than more labour-intensive pathogen detection methods.25 Outcome prevalence also differed by gender of the caregiver reporting on child outcomes (online supplemental table S4); future studies collecting remote caregiver-reported data might consider the role of the reporter in direct childcare giving. The effect of the sanitation components, not delivered until after study completion, is also unknown. There are scarce data on daily absence rates in LMIC preschools, to which our estimates can contribute. It is possible that the observed absence rate of approximately 20% may have also limited intervention exposure and therefore effectiveness among pupils frequently absent. Given resource limitations, we were not able to measure the behaviour of preschool pupils or educators or assess environmental conditions. Full engagement of preschool staff who are responsible for encouraging habit formation7 as well as sustained supply of materials7 8 may be critical to intervention success.

One factor potentially affecting our ability to detect effects on study outcomes was loss of statistical power due to the very low prevalence of diarrhoea in this population (mean proportion of days with diarrhoea was 0.033 in the control group compared with 0.08 estimated in sample size calculations) and the challenges of obtaining both the initial sample size and complete follow-up data over 4 weeks using telephone interviews. An additional complication was that the three schools in our limited pilot study provided much more timely and complete contact lists than those found in the full trial. Resources were only available for data collection staff for a finite period, so the unanticipated low completion rate resulted in many participants being unreachable for follow-up interviews in the study period. For many enrolled participants, reporting periods were therefore constrained to 7 or 14 days.

Remote data collection methods are widely used to collect health data in LMICs, where access to mobile phones is widespread,26 and became essential during the COVID-19 era.27 These methods offer advantages including acceptability, privacy, convenience and low costs, and inclusion of participants who might otherwise face challenges accessing in-person visits.27–29 School rosters can provide a list of potential participants that is representative of the population of interest.29 Given the distribution of schools over a large area and difficulties in accessing households in this setting, we therefore initially believed computer-assisted telephone interviews to be a feasible approach. However, phones were frequently switched off or unanswered or had wrong numbers. Similar challenges have been noted in previous studies.30 31 Previous impact evaluations of WASH interventions in preschools collected data through varied approaches: telephone interviews with teachers and parents (once-off),20 existing school absence recording systems,11 calendars to record symptoms delivered to parents18 or telephone interviews with teachers confirmed with class visits to verify symptoms.19 A practical alternative in future studies may be SMS messaging, which has been shown to improve response rates26 and allow flexibility in response times and greater privacy,27–29 32 but can place additional costs on participants or require high literacy rates.27 29 Further research should examine how the use of remote methods can be improved to obtain sufficient follow-up data for evaluating school-based interventions. Our results call into question whether reported high coverage of mobile phone networks and phone ownership is sufficient to attain adequate reach of remote telephone surveys.

Conclusions

Nested within a larger trial, the results of this cluster-randomised study evaluating a large-scale WASH programme in Ethiopian preschools did not find evidence of impacts on preschool pupil absence and illness. The study lost statistical power due to a high rate of attrition and a lower than anticipated baseline prevalence of certain outcomes. Our findings highlight some of the challenges in measuring health impacts among young children, and the need for refined telephone-based measurement techniques to obtain adequate follow-up data among caregivers.
