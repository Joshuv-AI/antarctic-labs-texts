Sialic acid cis-ligand dynamics modulate Siglec-7 and -9 function and affect Siglec-7/9 co-blockade to potentiate natural killer cell anti-tumor activity
Authors: Jesper van Eck van der Sluijs, Anne H. C. Valk, Eline J. H. van Houtum, Esther D. Kers-Rebel, Maaike Looman, Willemijn Hobo, Anna E. Marneth, Harry Dolstra, Lune van den Boogaard, Christian Büll, Lenneke A. M. Cornelissen, Gosse J. Adema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe glyco-immune checkpoints Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 have received considerable interest as targets for cancer immunotherapy. How Siglec-7/9-sialic acid cis-interactions on immune cells influence trans-signaling induced by tumor cells and whether Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade can enhance immune effector cell function are key questions for clinical translation. We developed and applied single and dual Jurkat/MA NFAT-luciferase reporter cells expressing wild-type or mutant chimeric Siglec-7 and/or -9. Cis-interactions on these Jurkat/MA reporter cells prevented Siglec-7 and -9 signaling induced by trans-ligands, i.e. on tumor cells. In Jurkat/MA cells expressing both receptors, Siglec-7/9 co-inhibition was essential to fully block receptor signaling. Extrapolating our findings to human primary cells, NK cell-mediated killing of melanoma and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell lines and patient-derived AML cells was increased upon Siglec-7 and/or -9 blockade. Importantly, co-blockade was superior to single blocking strategies and the effects were most pronounced when cis-ligands were removed from the NK cell’ surface using sialidase. Further diving into cis-ligand dynamics on primary human NK cells, physiological NK cell activation with IL-2 or IFN-α or IL-15/IL-2-induced proliferation was shown to significantly downregulate Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligand expression. Moreover, Siglec-7 and -9 ligands were progressively downregulated with each round of NK cell division. Taken together, our findings highlight the important role of cis-interactions in regulating trans-interactions and emphasize the potential of simultaneously blocking Siglec-7 and -9 for clinical applications. These insights may guide the design of next-generation Siglec-targeted immunotherapies.

Introduction

The development of effective cancer immunotherapies has received significant attention in recent years. However, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) hampers the efficacy of cancer immunotherapies in clinical settings. The hypersialylation of tumor cells, driven by dysregulated glycosylation pathways, contributes to the immunosuppressive TME.1-3 Hypersialylation is associated with aggressive cancer phenotypes, reflected by enhanced tumor progression, metastasis and immune escape4,5, and has been correlated with poor prognosis in cancer patients6,7. Hence, there is an upcoming interest in glyco-immune checkpoints for the treatment of cancer patients.1-3

Glycosylation involves the modification of proteins and lipids with carbohydrate molecules, modulating their structure, function, and stability. When sialic acids are attached to the terminal ends of glycan structures, they form sialoglycans. Sialoglycans are recognized by the sialic-acid binding immunoglobulin-like lectin (Siglec) receptor family. Similar to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), the majority of these receptors contain an intracellular immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory (ITIM) domain, as well as an ITIM-like domain. Upon Siglec ligation by sialoglycans, intracellular inhibitory signaling dampens immune activity and contributes to cancer immune evasion.1-3

Amongst the Siglec-receptor family, Siglec-7 and Siglec-9 have attracted considerable interest, as a variety of human cancers express sialoglycan ligands for both receptors8-11. Furthermore, these receptors exhibit close homology12 and co-expression patterns on both innate and adaptive immune cells13, including myeloid cells, natural killer (NK) cells and T cells. Siglec-7 and -9 signaling in myeloid cells has been shown to drive polarization toward a tumor-associated phenotype9,14,15 and to inhibit effective T-cell priming16,17. In NK cells, degranulation capacity and cytotoxic activity against hypersialylated tumor cells are inhibited upon Siglec-7 and -9 activation.8,18 Additionally, CD8+ T cells were shown to upregulate Siglec-7 and -9 upon co-culture with cancer-associated fibroblasts19, and Siglec-9 upon tumor infiltration in, amongst others, melanoma and lung cancer patients.20,21 Whether co-inhibition of Siglec-7 and -9 is superior to single blocking strategies remains a key question for advancing toward clinical application.

An interesting but understudied aspect of Siglec function is the role of cis-interactions, where Siglecs on a cell bind sialic acid motifs present on the same cell surface, and their impact on trans-interactions, when Siglecs engage ligands on other cells (e.g. cancer cells). Interestingly, global sialic acid removal using a sialic acid mimetic significantly reduced tumor growth in multiple murine tumor models, highlighting the role of cis/trans sialic acids in anti-tumor immunity.22 Specific removal of cis-sialoglycans from murine dendritic cells led to a more robust T cell response23 and, in human dendritic cells, increased responsiveness to toll-like receptor (TLR) ligands.24 How cis-interactions affect Siglec-specific signaling is best explored for Siglec-2 (CD22), which has been shown to be masked by cis-ligands25, affecting its function in B cells.26 However, the role of cis-ligand interactions for other Siglecs, including Siglec-7 and -9, remains poorly understood. On the one hand, the presence of cis-ligands may prevent trans-interactions and thus Siglec-mediated immune cell inhibition. For example, Siglec-7 could only inhibit NK cell-mediated killing of ganglioside-expressing target cells after unmasking of the receptor using sialidase.27,28 On the other hand, cis-ligand interactions may already by themselves induce some level of immune inhibitory signaling. The insertion of Siglec-9 cis-ligands into the cell membrane of Siglec-9+ HEKBlue reporter cells inhibited lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced NF-κB signaling, as well as MAPK signaling and phagocytosis in macrophages.29 Interestingly, the latter was also observed when Siglec-7 cis-ligands were inserted, albeit the difference was not statistically significant.29 Taken together, these studies highlight the potential role of cis-ligands in modulating Siglec-7 and -9 signaling.

Here, we show that cis-ligand masking prevents Siglec-7 and -9 receptors from signaling in trans, both in Jurkat/MA (J/MA) reporter cell lines and in primary human NK cells. We identified two distinct mechanisms – NK cell proliferation and cellular activation with IL-2 or IFN-α – that mediate the downregulation of Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands on NK cells. In addition, co-blockade was shown to be essential for significantly downregulating inhibitory Siglec-7 and -9 signaling in J/MA cells expressing both receptors. The superiority of co-blockade over single blocking strategies was also observed in NK cell cytotoxicity assays, which use melanoma and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell lines as well as patient-derived AML cells as target cells. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that cis-ligands are important regulators of Siglec-7 and -9 signaling and offer translational support for the co-targeting of both receptors in cancer immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Study period

This study was conducted between October 2020 and September 2025.

Informed consent

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell culture

J/MA NFAT-luciferase cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM, Gibco™, 21980065) supplemented with 8% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (H.I. FBS, Sigma-Aldrich, F7524), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin (1x Pen/Strep, Gibro™, 15140122) and 500 µg/mL hygromycin B (Invitrogen, 10687010). Upon introduction of the respective chimeric Siglec-receptors, 1 mg/mL geneticin/G418 (Invitrogen, 10131027) was added. K562 (RRID:CVCL_0004) and MV4-11 (RRID:CVCL_0064) cells were cultured in IMDM/10% H.I. FBS/1x PenStrep. MEL-624 (RRID:CVCL_8054), THP-1 (RRID:CVCL_0006), U937 (RRID:CVCL_0007) and NB-4 (RRID:CVCL_0005) cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco™, 42401042)/10% H.I. FBS/1x PenStrep/1x GlutaMAX™ (Gibco™, 35050061). MDA-MB-231 (RRID:CVCL_0062), T98G (RRID:CVCL_0556), SK-N-AS (RRID:CVCL_1700), MEL-603 (RRID:CVCL_RA52) and MEL-57 (RRID:CVCL_4454) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco™, 32430100)/10% H.I. FBS/1x PenStrep/1x GlutaMAX™. T98G and SK-N-AS cells were additionally supplemented with 1x sodium pyruvate (ThermoScientific™, 11360039) and 1x non-essential amino acids (Gibco™, 11140035). All the cell lines were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cells were confirmed to be mycoplasma negative (Lonza, LT07-518).

Generation of chimeric Siglec-7/9 and corresponding RMUTANT J/MA NFAT-luciferase reporter cells

Chimeric Siglec-7 (ChS7) and ChS7R124A J/MA NFAT-luciferase reporter cells were previously generated.30 Using a similar approach, chimeric Siglec-9 (ChS9) and ChS9R120A J/MA NFAT-luciferase reporter cells were generated using the following primers: forward primer 5'-ACGACGCTCGAGACCGGTATGTTGTTGCTGCTGCTGCCC-3', reverse primer 5'-GTAGCAAAGCTTGGGCTGTGTCACGCCGCTGGTGG-3'.30

Generation of ST6GALNAC1+ chimeric Siglec-7 J/MA NFAT-luciferase reporter cells

The ST6GALNAC1_OHu31015C_pcDNA3.1/Zeo (+) plasmid (Genscript, NM_018414.5) was linearized by restriction with Pcil and purified using phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol. The electroporation of ChS7 J/MA cells was performed as described previously.30 After 24 hours of incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2, the medium was changed to IMDM/8% H.I. FBS/1x PenStrep/500 µg/mL hygromycin B/1 mg/mL geneticin/G418/15 µg/mL zeocin (InvivoGen, ant-zn-05). Sialyl-Tnnegative and Sialyl-Tnhigh cells were sorted using FACS Aria II Sorp Flow Cytometer Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences).

Flow cytometry

Viability staining was performed using eFluor450 or eFluor780 (eBioscience, 65-0863-18/65-0865-18) in PBS (20 min, 4 °C). Surface stainings were performed in PBS supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.05% sodium azide (PBA) (20 min, 4 °C). Sialyl-Tn was detected by staining with AF488 goat-anti-mouse IgG (30 min, 4 °C). Siglec-7 and -9 ligand stainings were performed in Hank’s buffered salt solution (HBSS, Gibco™, 14-025-092) with 0.4 µg/mL (for cell lines) or 4 µg/mL (for primary cells) recombinant human (rh) Siglec-7/9-Fc chimera proteins or IgG-Fc, which were pre-complexed with 4 µg/mL (for cell lines) or 10 µg/mL (for primary cells) AF488 goat-anti-human IgG or AF647 donkey-anti-human IgG in HBSS for 20 minutes at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were stained in HBSS with respective pre-complexed Siglec-Fc-chimeras or IgG-Fc (45 min, 4 °C). Lectin stainings were performed in PBA with 5 µg/mL MAL-II, 1 µg/mL SNA-I, 5 µg/mL PNA, or 10 µg/mL HPA (45 min, 4 °C). Subsequently, cells were stained with 0.8 µg/mL Streptavidin PE (10 min, 4 °C). The cells were acquired on a Canto II (BD biosciences), followed by data analyses in FlowJo (version 10.7.0, Tree Star Inc., RRID:SCR_008520). The flow cytometry reagents used are outlined in Supplementary Table 2.

qPCR

RNA was isolated using quick-RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, ZY-R1055) with DNase I treatment according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed by incubating 1 µg of RNA with random p(dN)6 hexamers (Roche, 11034731001) and dNTPs (Eurogentec, NU-0020-50) (5 min, 65 °C), cooling it on ice, and incubating it with first-strand buffer, DTT, M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, 28025-021) and RNAs in ribonuclease inhibitor (Promega, N2515) (10 min, 25 °C; 50 min, 37 °C; 15 min, 70 °C). qPCR reactions were performed with 25x diluted cDNA, FastStart SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, 4673484001), 300 nM ST6GALNAC1 forward primer (CCTGGTCTTCTTTCTCTT), and 300 nM ST6GALNAC1 reverse primer (GACCTTTCTTTAATGTTCTC) using the cfx96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Relative gene expression was shown with PBGD as reference gene.

J/MA NFAT-luciferase reporter assays

Flat-bottom 96-wells MaxiSorp plates (Greiner Bio One, 655092) were coated with 5 µg/ml anti-Siglec-9 clone 191240 (R&D systems, MAB1139-500, RRID:AB_2270258), 5 µg/ml anti-Siglec-7 clone S7.7 (BioLegend, Ultra-LEAF purified, Custom made), 27.5 µM Neu5Acα-sp3-PAA-biotin (Neu5Ac, GlycoNZ, 0035-PB), 27.5 µM Neu5Gcα-sp3-PAA-biotin (Neu5Gc, GlycoNZ, 0018-BP), and/or with 10 µM Neu5Acα2-3Galβ1-4(Fucα1-3)GlcNAcβ-sp3-PAA-biotin (Sialyl Lewisx, GlycoNZ, 0062-BP) for 2 h at 37 °C, followed by blocking using carbo-free™ blocking solution (Vector Technologies, SP-5040-125). When indicated, ChS9, ChS7, and ChS7/9 J/MA reporter cells were pre-treated 3 d with 31.25 µM sialyltransferase inhibitor SiaFEt (GlycoTherapeutics), or DMSO as a vehicle control. Next, 0.1 × 106 J/MA cells were seeded in IMDM phenol red-free medium (Gibco™, 21056023)/2.5% H.I. FBS. When J/MA cells were co-cultured with tumor cells, 0.1 × 106 tumor cells were added to the same medium. When monoclonal antibody (mAb) blockade was performed, J/MA cells were pre-incubated for 45 min at 4 °C with anti-Siglec-7 clone S7.7, anti-Siglec-9 clone 191240, a combination of the aforementioned, or corresponding isotype controls (mIgG1, Bio X Cell, BE0083, RRID:AB_1107784; mIgG2a, Bio X Cell, BE0085, RRID:AB_1107771). Antibody end concentrations were 5 µg/mL. When indicated, the cells were stimulated with 5 ng/mL PMA and 1 µM ionomycin. Subsequently, cells were incubated for 16h at 37 °C and 5% CO2, followed by luminescence measurement using Bright-Glo™ Luciferase Assay System (Promega, E2620) according to the manufacturer’s protocol on a Victor3 Multilabel Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer).

Western blot

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed in buffer (1% SDS, 62.5 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 1% phenyl methyl sulfonyl fluoride (v/v), 1x protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 4693132001) and 100 U benzonase nuclease (Novagen, 70746-3)). Next, 4x loading dye was added, and the lysates were boiled for 5 min at 95 °C. Lysates were resolved on a 7% (ST6GALNAC1) or 10% (Sialyl-Tn) SDS‒PAGE gel and blotted on a nitrocellulose membrane. Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with 3% BSA (Roche, 10735094001) and 1% non-fat dry milk (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-2325) in PBS 0.1% Tween (PBS-T, blocking solution). After being washed with PBS-T, the membrane was stained with 1 µg/mL anti-ST6GALNAC1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-113365, RRID:AB_2868099) and 0.4 µg/mL mouse-anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A4700-2ML, RRID:AB_476730) or 2 µg/mL anti-Sialyl-Tn (Abcam, Ab115957, RRID: AB_10901541) and 1:1000 rabbit-anti-β-actin (Cell Signaling, 4970S, RRID:AB_2223172) in 1:4 blocking solution in PBS-T overnight at 4 °C, respectively. After being washed with PBS-T, the membrane was incubated with 0.2 µg/mL IRDye 800CW goat-anti-mouse IgG (LICORbio, 926-32210, RRID:AB_621842) and 0.2 µg/mL IRDye 680RD goat-anti-rabbit (LICORbio, 926-68071, RRID:AB_10956166) for 1 hour at room temperature. After a final wash in PBS-T, the blot was imaged using the Odyssey CLx infrared imaging system (LICORbio).

Primary human NK cell isolation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from healthy donor buffy coats (Sanquin Blood Bank, The Netherlands, Nijmegen) using Lymphoprep™ (Serumwerk Bernburg, 1858) density separation. NK cells were negatively selected using the EasySep™ human NK cell isolation kit (Stem Cell Technologies, 17955).

Primary human NK cell assays

NK cells were cultured overnight in IMDM/10% H.I. supplemented with FBS/1x PenStrep and 80 IU/ml rhIL-15 (ImmunoTools, 11340155). Subsequently, some of the cells were treated with 200 mU/mL clostridium perfringens sialidase (Roche, 11585886001) for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2, followed by extensive washing. NK cells were then pre-incubated for 10 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with the anti-Siglec-7 clone S7.7, the anti-Siglec-9 clone 191240, a combination of the aforementioned, or corresponding isotype controls. Antibody end concentrations were 5 µg/mL, and the assay was performed in IMDM/2.5% H.I. FBS. Without washing off the antibodies, 0.5 × 105 NK cells were seeded in round-bottom ultra-low adhesion 96-well plates (Corning®, CLS7007-24EA), which were previously blocked with carbo-free™ blocking solution. Subsequently, tumor cells labeled with 1.25 µM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) were added at the indicated E:T ratios. The cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 4 h (NK cells + tumor cell lines) or 16 and 24 h (NK cells + primary AML cells). Next, the frequency of dead tumor cells was assessed by flow cytometry (Canto II, BD Bioscience) by detecting 7-AAD+CFSE+ cells. NK cell degranulation was determined by CD56 and CD107a staining. When indicated, the supernatants were used for ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (IFN-γ: Invitrogen 88-7346, TNF-α: Invitrogen 88-7316).

Siglec-7 and -9 ligand modulation on primary human NK cells

NK cells were labeled with 1.25 µM CFSE and resuspended in NK MACS medium (Miltenyi, 130-114-429)/5% platelet-poor human AB serum (Sigma Aldrich, P2918). NK cells were either rested with 2.4 IU/ml rhIL-15, or expanded by 140 IU/ml rhIL-15 and 500 IU/ml rhIL-2 (Immunotools, 11340027). The medium supplemented with cytokines was added again at day 5, according to Miltenyi’s NK cell expansion protocol. NK cells were harvested at day 4 or day 7 and resuspended in IMDM/2.5% H.I. FBS/1x PenStrep. 1.2 × 105 NK cells were seeded in round-bottom tissue culture-treated 96-well plates. The cells were either stimulated with 4500 U/ml rhIFN-α (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 300-02AA-250UG), 9000 IU/ml rhIL-2 or left unstimulated, and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Next, rhSiglec-7-Fc, rhSiglec-9-Fc and IgG1-Fc stainings were performed as described under “Flow cytometry”.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2, RRID:SCR_002798) or RStudio (version 4.4.1, RRID:SCR_000432) when appropriate, as indicated in the figure legends.

Results

Siglec-9 cis-ligands impede trans-ligand-mediated signaling of chimeric Siglec-9 J/MA NFAT-luciferase reporter cells

Previously, we established a ChS7 J/MA reporter system to study sialic acid-mediated Siglec-7 signaling.30 To this end, reporter cells were developed expressing ChS9 and a mutant variant ChS9R120A, lacking the essential arginine for sialic acid binding (Figure 1A).

ChS9 signaling was first assessed using PMA/Ionomycin, an anti-Siglec-9 mAb, synthetic sialic acid (SSA) polymers, or co-culture with tumor cell lines. PMA/ionomycin and the anti-Siglec-9 mAb robustly induced ChS9 signaling, validating the system’s functionality (Figure 1B). Polymers decorated with the known Siglec-9 ligand Sialyl Lewisx31 initiated moderate ChS9 signaling, unlike the lower-affinity Neu5Ac and Neu5Gc polymers that did not induce signaling (Figure 1B). Next, we performed a screening to check whether tumor cell lines could induce ChS9 activation. Despite high expression of Siglec-9 ligands on tumor cells (Figure S1a), hardly any ChS9 signaling was detected in these tumor cell co-cultures. Notably, the tumor cells initiated ChS7 signaling (Figure 1B). These data suggest that the presence of Siglec-9 ligands on tumor cells alone may be insufficient to induce receptor signaling. We hypothesized that ChS9 masking by cis-sialic acids may impede receptor activation. Indeed, ChS9 J/MA cells expressed high levels of Siglec-9 cis-ligands, which could be effectively reduced by treatment with the sialyltransferase inhibitor SiaFEt (Figure 1C, Figure S1b), generating ChS9-cislow cells. Despite slightly lower cell surface ChS9 expression on cislow J/MA cells (Figure 1D, Figure S1c), significant and robust signaling was readily induced by lower-affinity Neu5Ac and Neu5Gc SSA polymers, with even stronger activation observed using the high-affinity Sialyl Lewisx polymer (Figure 1E). More importantly, the melanoma cell line MEL-624 robustly activated the ChS9-cislow J/MA cells in a sialic acid-dependent manner (Figure 1F). Notably, no signaling was detected in our (cishigh and cislow) mutant ChS9R120A J/MA reporter cells (Figure S1d-e). Altogether, a functional ChS9 J/MA reporter system was developed in which Siglec-9 cis-ligands were found to impede Siglec-9 trans-ligand-mediated ChS9 signaling.

Siglec-7 cis-ligands on ST6GALNAC1high chimeric Siglec-7 J/MA reporter cells also impede trans-signaling

Prompted by our findings regarding cis-ligands in the ChS9 J/MA reporter system, we investigated the impact of Siglec-7 cis-ligand expression on Siglec-7 signaling. Notably, ChS7 J/MA cells presented low levels of Siglec-7 cis-ligands (Figure 2A, Figure S2a), which may explain their greater sensitivity to trans-ligands on tumor cells compared to ChS9 J/MA cells (Figure 1B). RNA expression of key genes involved in Siglec-7 ligand generation32,33 was compared between Siglec-7 ligandlow Jurkat cells and Siglec-7 ligandhigh K562 cells,30,34 using the Human Protein Atlas. Notably, ST6GALNAC1 expression was absent in Jurkat cells but present in K562 cells (Figure 2B), which was confirmed in our ChS7 J/MA cells using qPCR (Figure 2C). ST6GALNAC1 forms the Sialyl-Tn antigen, which has been shown to be a ligand for Siglec-7.32

ST6GALNAC1 expression was introduced into ChS7 J/MA cells, as confirmed by Western blot analyses (Figure S2b), leading to increased rhSiglec-7-Fc staining (Figure 2D, Figure S2c). Importantly, ChS7 receptor expression remained unchanged (Figure 2E, Figure S2d). Lectin staining revealed that ST6GALNAC1 expression resulted in comparable SNA-I (a2-6-linked sialic acids), reduced HPA (T/Tn antigens) and abrogated MAL-II (a2-3-linked sialic acids) and PNA (terminal galactose) binding (Figure S2e-f). Loss of MAL-II and PNA binding aligns with ST6GALNAC1’s dominant role in overriding biosynthesis of core 1 and core 2 GalNAc-type O-glycans, resulting predominantly in Sialyl-Tn expression35 (Figure S2g), which was confirmed by flow cytometry (Figure 2F, Figure S2h) and Western blot analyses (Figure S2i).

ST6GALNAC1low (cislow) and ST6GALNAC1high (cishigh) ChS7 J/MA cells were then analyzed for receptor signaling following stimulation with SSA polymers and K562 cells. In ChS7-cislow cells, receptor signaling was effectively initiated, while in ChS7-cishigh cells, signaling was minimal (Figure 2G, H). SiaFEt-mediated removal of Siglec-7 ligands from ST6GALNAC1high ChS7 cells (Figure S2j) restored their responsiveness to K562 cells (Figure 2I). No signaling was detected in ChS7R124A J/MA mutant reporter cells (Figure S2k). In summary, ST6GALNAC1 overexpression in ChS7 J/MA cells induced the expression of the Siglec-7 ligand Sialyl-Tn. High Sialyl-Tn cis-ligand expression inhibited trans-mediated induction of ChS7 receptor signaling, which was reversed upon SiaFEt treatment.

Effective inhibition of sialic acid-mediated receptor signaling requires co-blockade of both Siglec-7 and -9

As Siglec-7 and -9 are often co-expressed on immune cells,13 we next studied the impact of single versus co-blockade of these receptors on signaling induced by the complex sialoglycan landscape on tumor cells. J/MA reporter cells were pre-treated with SiaFEt to initiate the strongest trans-sialic acid-mediated receptor signaling.

ChS9 and ChS7 J/MA cells were stimulated with SSA polymers (Neu5Ac + Sialyl Lewisx and Neu5Gc + Sialyl Lewisx) or melanoma tumor cell lines (MEL-624, MEL-603 and MEL-57) in the presence of isotype antibodies or anti-Siglec-9 and/or anti-Siglec-7 mAbs. ChS9 and ChS7 signaling were effectively induced and could be prevented upon incubation with their specific anti-Siglec mAb, but not with isotype nor the opposed mAb (Figure 3A–D). When ChS9 and ChS7 J/MA cells were co-cultured one-to-one and stimulated with MEL-624 cells, targeting of Siglec-7 and -9 was key to fully prevent receptor co-signaling (Figure S3a).

To more closely resemble the natural situation, new J/MA reporter cell lines were engineered which co-expressed ChS9 and ChS7 (Figure S3b, c). SSA polymer and melanoma cell line-induced co-signaling of Siglec-7 and -9 was fully inhibited upon mAb co-blockade (Figure 3E, F). For example, MEL-624 cells induced a 4.1-fold increase in ChS7/9 receptor signaling in clone 3E9, which was reduced to 3.3-fold by anti-Siglec-7 blockade and 2.7-fold by anti-Siglec-9 single blockade, whereas co-blockade fully prevented signaling (0.8-fold) (Figure 3F). In summary, the co-targeting of ChS7 and ChS9 is required to competently inhibit trans-signaling by SSA polymers and melanoma cell lines.

Cis-ligands and Siglec-7 and -9 co-signaling modulate NK cell cytotoxicity against melanoma cells

To extrapolate our findings, primary human NK cell assays were carried out. NK cells exhibit potent antitumor activity, which is governed by the balance between activating and inhibitory receptor signaling. The inhibitory receptor Siglec-7 is broadly expressed on CD56dim and CD56bright NK cells, whereas Siglec-9 is present on only a fraction of CD56dim NK cells13, which was confirmed by flow cytometry (Figure 4A, Figure S4a). NK cells also expressed Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands. Cis-ligand expression could be reduced upon sialidase treatment while maintaining Siglec-7 and -9 receptor expression (Figure 4B, Figure S4b), thus potentially opening a larger window to interfere with Siglec-7 and -9 blocking mAbs.

Siglec-7 and/or -9 mAb blockade improved NK cell killing of MEL-624 cells, which was further substantiated by upregulation of the degranulation marker CD107a. For untreated NK cells, Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade significantly improved MEL-624 killing compared with single Siglec-7 blockade (from 4.2% to 11.5%) and degranulation compared with single Siglec-9 blockade. After removing sialic acid cis-ligands from the NK cell surface, co-blockade was significantly more efficient than single Siglec-7 (increase in killing from 14.0% to 31.3%) and single Siglec-9 blockade (increase in killing from 12.0% to 31.3%) (Figure 4C). The sialic acid dependence of the mAb blocking effects was verified by performing killing assays with sialidase-treated MEL-624 cells (Figure S5a). Finally, the production of IFN-γ and TNF-α was elevated following blockade of Siglec-7 or -9, with the most pronounced increase observed upon co-targeting of both receptors (Figure 4D, E).

Taken together, NK cell killing, degranulation and cytokine production are augmented upon Siglec-7 and/or -9 blockade, with these effects being particularly pronounced when cis-ligands are low. Moreover, co-blockade proves more effective than single mAb blocking strategies.

NK cell degranulation and killing of acute myeloid leukemia cells is modulated by sialic acid cis-ligand expression and improved upon Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade

As Krishnamoorthy et al. recently reported that AML cells abundantly express Siglec-9 ligands,36 and NK cell therapies are being clinically explored in the treatment of AML,37 we explored the role of cis-ligands and Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade in the context of AML. The expression of Siglec receptor and ligand expression were characterized in four AML cell lines (THP-1, U937, MV4-11, and NB-4) (Figure S5b, c). AML cells were indeed found to highly express Siglec-9 ligands, and a varying degree of Siglec-7 ligands.

Particularly using cislow NK cells, Siglec-7 and/or -9 mAb (co-)blockade significantly enhanced the killing of all 4 AML cell lines, often accompanied by increased degranulation (Figure 5A–D). Baseline killing and degranulation differed for cislow and cishigh NK cells in a tumor cell line–dependent manner (Figure S5d). Importantly, no antibody-mediated enhanced killing was observed when trans-ligands were removed from the target cell surface using sialidase (Figure S5a), demonstrating that the observed effects were dependent on the sialic acid-Siglec axis.

To evaluate the relevance of Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade in a more clinically relevant setting, a proof-of-concept study using primary AML (pAML) patient samples was carried out. All pAML samples showed Siglec-7 and -9 ligand staining, and 2 out of 4 primary samples substantially expressed the Siglec-7 and -9 receptors (Figure 5E). In cislow NK cells, Siglec-7 and/or -9 (co)-blockade improved NK cell cytolytic activity against pAML cells at 16 hours, which was most effective upon co-blockade. Similar trends were present for NK cell degranulation, although they were less pronounced (Figure 5F). Importantly, upon co-culture with sialidase-treated pAML cells, Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade did not improve NK cell-mediated killing, confirming that the mAb effects were sialic acid-dependent (Figure S6a). Similar results were obtained at a later timepoint (24h), strengthening our findings (Figure S6b, c).

To conclude, NK cell degranulation and killing of AML cell lines and pAML patient cells are modulated by sialic acid cis-ligand expression and significantly improved upon Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade.

Siglec-7/9 cis-ligands are downregulated upon NK cell expansion and IFN-α/IL-2 stimulation

Our findings demonstrating a key role for cis-ligands in regulating NK cell function prompted us to assess which factors co-determine Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligand expression dynamics on primary human NK cells. Interestingly, high-dose IL-15- and IL-2-expanded NK cells showed significantly downregulated Siglec-7 and -9 ligand expression at day 5 as compared to non-proliferating NK cells cultured with low-dose IL-15 for the same time period (57.3% reduction in Siglec-7 ligands; 27.9% reduction in Siglec-9 ligands) (Figure 6A–B). Moreover, within the expanded NK cells, the expression of Siglec-7 and -9 ligands was progressively downregulated with each round of NK cell division (i.e. CFSE dilutions) (Figure 6C–D). On day 8, almost all the NK cells had proliferated (Figure 6E), essentially lost their Siglec-7 cis-ligands and exhibited reduced levels of Siglec-9 cis-ligands (Figure 6F). Similarly, overnight IFN-α and IL-2 stimulation of rested NK cells reduced the expression of Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands on the NK cells’ surface (IFN-α: 38.7% and 19.5% reduction; IL-2: 27.9% and 32.9% reduction, for Siglec-7 and -9 ligands, respectively) (Figure 6G, H). In summary, primary human NK cells downregulated Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands upon cellular proliferation and IFN-α or IL-2 stimulation.

Discussion

Despite the increasing recognition of Siglec-7 and -9 as glyco-immune checkpoint receptors, the functional consequences of their sialic acid cis- versus trans-ligands engagement remain poorly understood. In both J/MA reporter cell lines and primary human NK cells, cis-ligand binding was shown to prevent the Siglec-7 and -9 receptors from interacting with trans-ligands and subsequent signaling events. To provide further mechanistic insight, we demonstrated that NK cells downregulated Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands in response to proliferation stimuli and activation with IFN-α or IL-2. In addition, NK cell cytotoxicity against tumor cell lines and patient-derived AML blasts was most effectively improved upon Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade compared with single blockade strategies. Co-blockade effects were most pronounced in cislow NK cells, highlighting the importance of cis-ligands in controlling Siglec-7 and -9 receptor signaling. These findings support a key role for cis-interactions regulating trans-interactions and underscore the potential of Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade for clinical translation.

The J/MA reporter system offers a clean platform to study Siglec-7 and -9 signaling in the context of cis and trans interactions. First, the direct effects of cis-ligands on trans-induced Siglec signaling, rather than on downstream pathways, could be studied and quantified for the Siglec-7 and -9 receptor separately. The sialic acid dependence of the observed effects was confirmed by performing experiments with the corresponding RMUTANT clones, strengthening our conclusions. Moreover, J/MA reporter cells co-expressing the Siglec-7 and -9 receptors allowed us to investigate the potential of Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade in a highly controlled setting. While offering clear benefits, chimeric Siglec receptor reporter cells are less suited for exploring the pathways activated by Siglec-7 and -9 cis-signaling. Delaveris et al. demonstrated that the presence of Siglec-9 cis-ligands inhibited TLR4 signaling in HEKBlue cells, and LPS-mediated MAPK signaling and cytokine production in Siglec-9+ THP-1 cells,29 as well as TLR7/8-induced NETosis in primary human neutrophils.38 Future studies are warranted to identify alternative cellular pathways modulated by Siglec-7 and -9 signaling in cis.

NK cell cytotoxicity was augmented upon Siglec-7 and -9 mAb blockade, especially for sialidase-treated cislow NK cells, supporting the idea that cis-masking may prevent trans Siglec-7 and -9 signaling in NK cells. Masking of the Siglec-7 receptor on NK cells by α2,8-linked disialic acid cis-ligands has previously been described.39 Moreover, sialidase treatment revealed Siglec-7-dependent effects on NK cell-mediated tumor cell killing in two other studies.27,28 Notably, sialidase treatment not only reduces Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligand expression, but may also affect the stability and ligand-affinity of sialylated NK cell receptors, the charge of the NK cells, and thereby binding avidity to their target cells.23,40 Indeed, we observed tumor cell line-dependent changes in baseline killing after sialidase treatment, with some cell lines showing increased and others showing decreased cytotoxicity, likely resulting from a combination of these factors. Delineating the isolated effects of removing Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands on NK cell cytotoxicity thus remains challenging. Targeted removal or insertion of Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands in primary cells may partially circumvent this issue and is recommended for further investigation in future studies.

IL-2/IL-15-induced NK cell proliferation and activation with IL-2 or IFN-α were identified as mechanisms that downregulated Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands on NK cells. A decrease in cis-ligands upon cellular activation has been described by others for multiple immune cells, including B cells,25 neutrophils41 and monocytes.42 In addition, IL-2 stimulation has previously been linked to the unmasking of Siglec-7 on NK cells, as demonstrated by reduced ST8SIA1 gene expression and surface expression of GD3 in NK-92 cells.43 Besides cellular activation, sialic acid expression can be altered upon proliferation and differentiation. For example, proliferated T cells expressed lower levels of Siglec-7 ligands, but increased levels of Siglec-9 ligands. Moreover, naive CD4+ T cells displayed higher Siglec-7-Fc staining than naive CD8+ and memory CD4+/CD8+ T cells.16 Collectively, these data point to a regulatory function of cis-ligands in immune cell activity; upon immune cell activation or proliferation, cis-ligand downregulation may unmask Siglec-7 and -9 receptors, which in turn could bind in trans, dampening the immune response and preventing an overreaction of the immune system. Likewise, PD-1, which shares inhibitory motifs with Siglecs, is upregulated following T-cell activation, establishing a similar negative feedback loop that limits immune responses.44

In the present study, we specifically focused on the role of Siglec-7 and -9 cis-ligands in the cytotoxic activity of NK cells against sialylated tumor cells. It is, however, important to realize that many other cell types in the TME express cis-ligands, such as myeloid cells,45,46 or trans-ligands, i.e. stromal cells and more specifically cancer-associated fibroblasts.47,48 The total of all these cis- and trans-expressed Siglec ligands likely contribute to immunosuppression in the TME. Several approaches have been developed to target the Siglec-sialoglycan axis, such as anti-Siglec antibodies,49 sialic acid mimetics,22 bi-sialidase50 and antibody-lectin chimeras.51 Collectively, these advances underscore the rapid development of therapeutic strategies targeting the Siglec–sialoglycan axis. Our study results imply that not only trans- but also cis-ligands should be taken into account in the design of novel targeting approaches.

As previously mentioned, Siglec-7 and -9 are co-expressed on multiple innate and adaptive immune cells. Interestingly, these proteins share 98% amino acid and 84% sequence homology13 and recognize overlapping sialoglycans.52 Due to this redundancy, loss or blockade of one receptor allows compensation by the other receptor. Thus, co-inhibition is likely necessary to fully overcome compensatory mechanisms and to maximally increase immune cell activity. Many studies have focused on Siglec-7 or -9 targeting in isolation,16,18,53,54 but we studied them in a redundant context. Other studies have demonstrated the value of Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade in murine models in vivo. For instance, Wen et al. found inhibited tumor growth and increased immune cell infiltration in a humanized prostate cancer xenograft mouse model,11 and Ibarlucea-Benitez et al. observed reduced numbers of metastatic lung foci in a B16-FUT3 melanoma model upon Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade.55 Our study significantly contributes to this body of literature by improving understanding of the dynamic role of sialic acid cis-ligands in regulating Siglec-7 and -9 signaling.

Our results further emphasize the important role of the sialic acid-Siglec axis in AML and melanoma. Siglec-7 and -9 co-blockade improved the NK cell mediated-killing of patient-derived AML blasts, indicating the activity of this axis in a clinically relevant disease context. Additionally, adoptive transfer of NK cells for AML treatment is actively being pursued in clinical settings.37 Interestingly, these NK cells used for adoptive transfer are usually ex vivo expanded or cytokine-induced,37 which according to our data suggests a downregulation of cis-ligands. Given these observations, there is a strong rationale for combining Siglec-7 and -9 blocking strategies with adoptive NK cell therapy in AML. Moreover, other malignancies also express high levels of Siglec-7 and -9 ligands, which have been established not only in tumor cell lines but also in patient-derived tumor tissues, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma,9,47 glioblastoma,45 triple-negative breast cancer10 and prostate cancer,11 underscoring their physiological and clinical relevance. Several studies have reported clinical correlations with Siglec-7/9 expression. In metastatic colorectal cancer, high Siglec-7 in macrophage-rich areas was linked to poor survival in patients receiving peptide vaccines.56 Similarly, high Siglec-7 and -9 expression in prostate cancer correlated with poor prognosis.11 Collectively, these findings point to a broad and clinically meaningful role for Siglec-7 and -9 receptor/ligand expression across diverse cancer types and TMEs, highlighting their potential as immunotherapeutic targets.
