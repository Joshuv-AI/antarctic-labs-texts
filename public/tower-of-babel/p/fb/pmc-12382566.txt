Post-COVID-19 depression prevalence in Iranian nurses: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hamid Sharif-Nia, Mohammad Heidari, Mozhgan Moshtagh, Mahdi Nabi Foodani, Amir Hossein Goudarzian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the pooled prevalence of post-COVID-19 depression among Iranian nurses, identify at-risk groups and provide practical recommendations for intervention.DesignIn adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, encompassing studies published from 2019 to 2024. Comprehensive searches were performed across international and Iranian databases.Data sourcesPubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar and Scientific Information Database.Eligibility criteria for selecting studiesStudies meeting the following criteria were included in the analysis: (1) conducted on the population of Iranian nurses, (2) keywords explicitly included in the title or abstract, (3) studies published between 2019 and 2024, (4) published in Persian or English and (5) reported the prevalence of depression either in the entire population or differentiated by gender.Data extraction and synthesisData extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers, and quality assessment was performed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Statistical analyses were executed using random effects models to estimate pooled prevalence rates, with subgroup analyses and sensitivity tests conducted to explore sources of heterogeneity and confirm result robustness.ResultsA total of 22 studies met the inclusion criteria, capturing data from various provinces across Iran. The pooled prevalence of depression among Iranian nurses post-COVID-19 was estimated at 23% (95% CI 19% to 30%), indicating a substantial mental health burden within this population. Subgroup analyses revealed notable disparities in depression rates across demographic and professional characteristics. Nurses holding advanced degrees exhibited a higher mean depression score (13.33, 95% CI 9.48 to 16.74) compared with those with bachelor’s degrees. Male nurses also reported slightly higher depression scores (12.04, 95% CI 7.58 to 16.50) than their female counterparts. Furthermore, moderate depression emerged as the most common severity level, affecting 24% of nurses. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimates, reinforcing the reliability of the findings.ConclusionsThis review and meta-analysis illuminate the mental health challenges faced by Iranian nurses in the wake of COVID-19. With a significant proportion of nurses experiencing depression, addressing their psychological needs is imperative. Tailored interventions, such as stress management workshops, access to professional counselling and workplace policies that prioritise mental health, are essential to enhance resilience and sustain healthcare quality during future public health crises. Efforts must also focus on structural changes to create a supportive environment that fosters well-being and professional satisfaction among nurses, ultimately improving patient outcomes and overall healthcare system performance.Keywords: Public health, COVID-19, MENTAL HEALTH

Introduction

COVID-19 has spread globally since December 2019, causing thousands of deaths. According to statistics published by the WHO, by the end of 2023, the number of cases and deaths caused by this disease exceeded 772 million and 6 million people, respectively.1 The highest infection rates were reported in European countries, while the highest mortality rates were reported in the USA.1 In Iran, there have been 7 627 863 confirmed cases and 146 837 deaths as of the latest data. This pandemic brought significant changes to the world and posed challenges to the daily activities of many workers. Healthcare personnel worldwide faced greater exposure to the disease compared with others, with nurses being at higher risk due to their direct contact with COVID-19 patients.2

One of the major results of this crisis is mental health problems, especially depression 3 . Depression was a common issue among nurses during the pandemic, with the WHO reporting it as a key symptom of post-COVID-19 syndrome.4 To date, numerous review studies have been conducted worldwide on depression among nurses and healthcare workers. Cumulative reports from around the globe have indicated depression rates ranging from 22% to 36%.5,9 Similarly, in Iran, a review study on the mental health disorders of healthcare workers revealed an increase in depression among them since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic.8 Another study also identified depression as one of the most prevalent mental health disorders among nurses.10

In Iran, nurses represent the backbone of the healthcare system, constituting a significant proportion of the healthcare workforce and directly managing patient care, including during the COVID-19 crisis.11 The pandemic imposed additional stressors on Iranian nurses due to existing systemic challenges such as shortages of personal protective equipment, high patient-to-nurse ratios and limited mental health support resources within Iranian hospitals.8

Studies conducted in Iran have indicated a notable increase in depression rates among nurses following the COVID-19 outbreak, with some reports suggesting rates rising from prepandemic levels of approximately 10%–15% to over 20%–35% during the pandemic.12,14 This increase can lead to decreased job performance, reduced quality of patient care and potential burnout among Iranian nurses, further straining the healthcare system during a critical period.14 However, despite the considerable time that has passed since the beginning of the pandemic, a detailed meta-analytical study on changes in the prevalence of depression among Iranian nurses has not yet been conducted.

It is worth noting, however, that the risk of depression did not differ significantly between nurses working in COVID-19 wards and those in non-COVID-19 wards. This suggests that nearly all nurses faced similar psychological pressures resulting from the pandemic.15 This may be due to fear of infection, high workloads and redeployment across wards, creating widespread stress regardless of direct COVID-19 patient care. Similar patterns have been reported in other countries, indicating that this lack of difference is not unique to Iran but reflects the global impact of the pandemic on nurses’ mental health.12 16

Depression among nurses can lead to irreparable physical and psychological health consequences for the nurse, as well as pose health risks for patients.17 Physical complications include cardiovascular diseases and sleep disorders, which also contribute to a decline in nurses’ work-life quality.18 The mental and emotional well-being of a nurse can affect the quality of care they provide to patients. If a nurse is anxious or depressed, it may lead to delays in decision-making and actions that could negatively impact patient health outcomes.19

Given the importance of the issue and the aforementioned consequences, examining depression among nurses, who are the beating heart of Iran’s healthcare system, is both crucial and essential. Understanding the current state and its changes may broaden the perspective of healthcare policymakers in developing medium- and long-term plans. Additionally, it can provide practical guidance and valuable lessons to better prepare for future pandemics worldwide. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to determine the pooled prevalence of depression among Iranian nurses following the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

This study is a systematic review conducted in 2024 in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. Studies meeting the following criteria were included in the analysis: (1) conducted on the population of Iranian nurses, (2) keywords explicitly included in the title or abstract, (3) studies published between 2019 and 2024, (4) published in Persian or English and (5) reported the prevalence of depression either in the entire population or differentiated by gender. Exclusion criteria were (1) studies published as theses, books, or abstracts from congresses and conferences and (2) studies with a sample size of fewer than 50 nurses (except for interventional studies).

Although prevalence meta-analyses typically exclude randomised controlled trials (RCTs), one RCT20 was included because it reported baseline depression prevalence data collected prior to any intervention. These data were cross-sectional in nature and aligned with our research focus on estimating post-COVID-19 depression prevalence among nurses. Including this baseline information increased the precision and generalisability of our pooled estimate. To ensure this inclusion did not bias the findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that demonstrated the removal of this RCT did not significantly alter the overall prevalence estimate, confirming the robustness of our results.

Searching databases

The search was independently conducted by AHG and MNF. A systematic review of relevant studies was conducted across international databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar, and Iranian database, including the Scientific Information Database. The search was performed in December 2024, covering articles published from 2019 up to this date.

The keywords used included ‘healthcare provider’, ‘healthcare professional’, ‘Iran’, ‘healthcare worker’, ‘nurse’, ‘depression’, ‘depress’, ‘Coronavirus Infections’, ‘COVID-19’ and ‘COVID’. These terms were combined using OR/AND operators for searching in international databases. All original articles were collected and screened for inclusion in the study. The search was limited to studies conducted after 2019 and to original research articles. If full-text access was unavailable, the corresponding author was contacted via email. The search strategy in detail has been shown in online supplemental Appendix 1.

The main question addressed in this systematic review is the prevalence of depression among Iranian nurses after the COVID-19 pandemic. The target population for this study is Iranian nurses, their exposure to the COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent outcomes related to depression. These three factors were taken into account in the search strategy and inclusion criteria for the study.

Data extraction

The following information was extracted from each of the included articles by AHG and MNF: first author, year of publication, number and average age of participants, location of the study, education level of participants, scales used for assessment and rates of depression in the entire population.

Risk of bias assessment

Two researchers (AHG and HSN) independently assessed the risk of bias for each of the included studies and discussed their evaluations to reach a consensus. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the risk of bias or methodological quality of the studies.21 This tool was applied to evaluate the adequacy of study design, sample collection strategy, response rate, sample representativeness, objectivity/reliability of results, study power and statistical analyses. Any discrepancies in scoring were resolved by consensus, with the involvement of a third colleague to assign a final score to the respective study. Studies that received a score lower than 7 in the quality assessment were excluded.

Meta-analysis

To synthesise the prevalence rates of post-COVID-19 depression among nursing professionals, we used STATA V.17 for our analysis. The METAPREG package was used to pool the prevalence estimates. In addition to calculating the observed prevalence rates, smoothed prevalence estimates were produced through the application of logistic regression. Moreover, we used a random effects model to accommodate the heterogeneity existing between the studies. The extent of heterogeneity was evaluated by the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test.

For the pooling of the mean scores of depression, the METAN package was employed. Potential confounding variables, such as gender, educational attainment and age, were meticulously controlled through subgroup analyses to reinforce the robustness of the findings. To assess the stability of the results and the impact of individual studies on the overall pooled prevalence, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using the METANINF package. The findings were disseminated in graphical representations to enhance interpretation and visualisation.

Registration

The protocol of this study was registered (code=4021134) in the National Institute for Medical Research Development.

Using a random-effects model, the forest plot (figure 2) shows the pooled prevalence of depression among nurses, stratified by severity level. The total prevalence of depression, according to the results, is 0.23 (95% CI 0.19 to 0.30). With significant variation among studies, as demonstrated by prevalences of 0.11 in Kakemam et al28 and 0.81 in Karimi et al,39 subgroup analysis showed a mild depression prevalence of 0.22 (95% CI 0.17 to 0.30). With a pooled prevalence of 0.24 (95% CI 0.19 to 0.33) for moderate depression, there was some variation, as seen by prevalences of 0.11 in Bakhtiari et al37 and 0.42 in Hassannia et al.24 The pooled prevalence for severe depression, after combining studies, was 0.22 (95% CI 0.17 to 0.31), comparable to that for mild depression. Compared with the other two severity levels, moderate depression was more common overall. The model-derived, smoothed prevalence estimates (darker points) produced by logistic regression analysis are displayed in the forest plot with observed study-specific prevalence rates (lighter points). Interestingly, the approach reduced the impact of high prevalence numbers.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to determine the prevalence of depression among Iranian nurses after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. This study is the first systematic review to specifically evaluate depression among Iranian nurses following the pandemic. Previous studies have focused on broader populations, such as healthcare providers, and have not concentrated on a specific psychological outcome like depression.40 However, nurses spend more time interacting with patients compared with other healthcare providers, so in critical situations such as the COVID-19 outbreak, they are at a higher risk not only for physical health threats but also for psychological harm, including anxiety and depression.5

In the initial studies, the diversity of research locations and participant selection from various cities in Iran is clearly evident. However, in some studies where online sampling was conducted, the exact location was not reported. The overall prevalence of depression in this study was determined to be 0.23, with the highest rate associated with moderate levels, indicating a high rate of this issue among Iranian nurses. The heterogeneity in depression prevalence between studies could be due to factors such as cultural-social characteristics, the unequal gender ratio of men and women, differing work environments (eg, type of ward), variations in the tools used for depression screening and the different timing of the studies (various stages of the pandemic). However, the most commonly used tool in the initial studies was the DASS-21, a validated tool for clinical research widely used internationally.41 In the study by Sahebi et al, which was a review of previous meta-analyses, the prevalence of depression was reported as 24.83%. Additionally, findings from another study assessing mental health in developing countries during the COVID-19 pandemic also reported depression as the most common mental disorder during this period.42

Among the initial studies, only two focused specifically on assessing particular wards, evaluating nurses who had direct exposure to COVID-19 patients.12 33 In the 2022 study by Sharifi et al, the overall prevalence of depression was found to be 74.1%, with the prevalence of moderate to severe levels reported as 24%. This finding aligns with the cumulative results of the study by Sahebi et al.5

A higher depression score among men compared with women is another finding of this study, which does not align with the results of other studies. Previous research has reported that women experienced higher levels of post-traumatic stress, anxiety and depression during the pandemic.1643,45 The differing nature of depression from other psychological disorders, as well as the timing of the study, may be factors contributing to this finding. In terms of the timeline, depression typically arises in later stages, especially if post-traumatic stress and anxiety are prolonged or untreated. Previous findings have confirmed that depression could emerge up to 3 years after an epidemic (such as SARS) in hospital staff.16

Factors such as being single, weak social support, direct exposure to COVID-19 patients and long working hours have been identified as contributing to the development of mental health disorders.16 46 47 Since male nurses typically have longer working hours, the higher depression scores among them may be attributed to spending more time in the hospital during the COVID-19 outbreak or having greater exposure to high-risk patients.46 47

Another finding of this study was the higher average depression score among nurses with a master’s or doctoral degree compared with those with a bachelor’s degree. Work conditions or characteristics (such as the type and environment of the ward), as well as age and work experience, have been identified as factors related to psychological disorders in nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic.44 46 48 49 Nurses with higher education or male nurses are often assigned to more demanding roles within both COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 wards, such as managing critically ill patients, leading teams or performing procedures that require close patient contact. These responsibilities increase psychological stress, workload and fear of making critical errors, thereby raising the risk of depression irrespective of whether they are in COVID-19-designated units.46 49 Thus, while ward type alone did not determine depression risk, the intensity of exposure to critically ill patients and higher responsibility roles within any ward likely contributed to the observed higher depression rates in these subgroups.

The present study shows that the COVID-19 pandemic has had significant effects on the mental health of Iranian nurses. Prepandemic studies indicated that the prevalence of depression among Iranian nurses, depending on factors such as workload, work environment and social support systems, was approximately between 10% and 15%.50 These rates were relatively stable and reflected the challenges of usual nursing practice before the pandemic.

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, the prevalence of depression among Iranian nurses was estimated to be around 10%–15%, influenced by routine occupational stressors such as heavy workloads and staffing shortages.50 Following the onset of the pandemic, our findings indicate a notable increase in depression prevalence to approximately 23%, reflecting the compounded pressures during this period. While fear of virus transmission was a major contributor, extended working hours during the pandemic also played a significant role.

Reports indicate that Iranian nurses experienced an increase in working hours from an average of 44–48 hours per week prepandemic to over 60 hours per week in many hospitals during peak COVID-19 waves, primarily due to staff shortages and increased patient loads.30 50 Additionally, social support systems weakened during the pandemic as nurses isolated themselves from family to prevent transmission and experienced reduced peer support due to high turnover and absenteeism among colleagues, further exacerbating feelings of loneliness and psychological distress. These factors collectively contributed to the significant rise in depression prevalence among Iranian nurses during the pandemic.

Limitations

In most of the initial studies, sampling was conducted using convenience and online methods, which may reduce the generalisability of the study findings. In many studies, the prevalence percentage was not reported, or the average depression score based on individual characteristics such as marital status, type of ward or the type of care provided by the participant was not available. As a result, subgroup analysis and comparison of the relationship between these characteristics and depression scores were not possible. Nurses working in specialised and infectious disease wards were rarely studied. Additionally, the workplaces of the participating nurses were limited to hospitals and other healthcare and caregiving settings, such as health centres, nursing homes and care homes, were not assessed.

Implication of the results

The results of this study have practical implications for nurses and healthcare systems. First, they highlight the urgent need for mental health support programmes specifically designed for nurses, especially during public health crises. Interventions such as counselling services, stress management workshops and peer support groups can help reduce psychological burden, particularly among high-risk groups identified in this study, including male nurses and those with higher educational qualifications, who may face increased responsibilities and exposure to critically ill patients during crises.

Our findings align with prior research indicating that extended working hours, high patient loads and reduced social support contribute significantly to depression among nurses during pandemics.16 44 In Iran, working hours during COVID-19 peaks often increased from an average of 44–48 hours to over 60 hours per week,11 intensifying psychological distress. Therefore, addressing systemic issues, such as ensuring adequate staffing, reducing overtime and providing sufficient personal protective equipment, is crucial to mitigate stressors contributing to depression.

Additionally, creating a supportive work environment where nurses feel valued and supported can improve both psychological well-being and the quality of patient care. Organisational measures, including routine mental health screenings, flexibility in work schedules and resilience-building programmes, can further aid in reducing depression risk among nurses, especially those in high-responsibility roles. By implementing these measures, healthcare systems can enhance the mental health of nurses, sustain workforce resilience and improve patient care quality during pandemics and beyond.

Conclusions

The findings of this systematic review indicate a high prevalence of depression among nurses after the outbreak of COVID-19. According to the results of this study, the likelihood of depression was higher in male nurses and those with higher educational qualifications, compared with other nurses, while age did not show a significant relationship with depression. Despite certain limitations, the sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis of this study have confirmed the validity of the results and the absence of bias in the findings. Therefore, the findings of this study can be useful in decision-making and the implementation of health policies aimed at improving the working conditions and promoting the mental health of nurses, particularly in special and critical situations such as epidemics. It is recommended that future studies focus more on nurses in specialised wards, preventive care centres such as health centres, and long-term care facilities such as nursing homes. Additionally, the mental health of nurses should be examined and compared based on the type of services provided, the timing and the quality of the care delivered.
