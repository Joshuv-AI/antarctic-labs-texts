Trends in First-Time Symptomatic Antidementia Drug Dispensations to Home-Dwelling People in Norway: A Nationwide Register-Based Study
Authors: Torunn Gudmestad Øvrebø, Anita L Sunde, Arvid Rongve, Svein Reidar Kjosavik, Ingvild Dalen, Ragnhild Oesterhus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSymptomatic antidementia drugs can influence cognitive symptoms. According to Norwegian dementia guidelines, general practitioners (GPs) are responsible for assessing, treating, and following up on dementia, with extended assessments provided by specialist healthcare in more complicated cases and for patients under 65 years of age.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate trends in first-time antidementia drug dispensations to home-dwelling people aged 60 years and older in Norway (2006–2020), and to analyse whether a GP issued the first prescription.MethodsThis repeated annual cross-sectional study utilized data from the Norwegian Prescribed Drug Registry (NorPD). Age- and sex-stratified incidence rates of antidementia drugs (ATC N06D) dispensed by Norwegian pharmacies between January 1, 2006, and December 31, 2020, were analysed. The Norwegian GP Registry was linked with NorPD to identify if the prescriber worked as a GP at the time of the first antidementia prescription.ResultsIn this study, 66,886 individuals received a novel antidementia drug dispensation. The incidence rates of first-time antidementia drug dispensations showed an overall declining trend during the study period. Females accounted for 61.1% of first-time dispensations, with the highest incidence rates observed in the 80–84-years age group across both sexes. Across all age groups, female patients were more likely than male patients to receive their first antidementia prescription from a GP. Patient age had a notable influence on prescriber type, with older people more often receiving their first prescription from a GP compared with younger people.ConclusionsThere was a declining trend in the annual incidence rate of antidementia drug dispensations to home-dwelling people. Older age and female sex were associated with an increased likelihood of receiving a first-time symptomatic antidementia drug prescription from a GP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40801-026-00547-9.

Key Points

The annual incidence rate of antidementia drug dispensations declined throughout the study period.Older individuals and females were more likely than younger individuals and males to receive their first antidementia drug dispensation from a general practitioner (GP).The proportion of first-time antidementia drugs prescribed by GPs versus non-GPs declined over time in both females and males. This raises questions about potential barriers to GP involvement. There is a need for further research into factors influencing prescribing practice in dementia care.

Introduction

Dementia is a chronic and progressive syndrome affecting the brain, leading to a decline in cognitive functions that interferes with daily life activities and independence. Although dementia primarily affects older people, it is not a regular part of aging [1, 2]. Globally, the prevalence of dementia is rising due to aging populations, contributing to increased healthcare expenditures and placing growing demands on health and social care systems [3–5]. In Norway, approximately 101,000 people were living with dementia in 2020, with around 70% residing in their own homes [6].

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60–70% of cases, followed by dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), vascular dementia, Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD), and frontotemporal dementia [3, 7]. Symptomatic pharmacological treatments are available for AD, DLB, and PDD [8–11]. Disease-modifying anti-amyloid therapies for AD have recently been introduced [12, 13].

Four symptomatic antidementia drugs are currently licensed for use in Norway. Donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine are acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs) recommended as first-line pharmacological treatments for managing symptoms of mild to moderate AD [14–16]. Rivastigmine is also approved for the treatment of PDD [17]. Memantine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is indicated for symptomatic treatment of moderate to severe AD [18]. AChEIs and memantine are not recommended for people with mild cognitive impairment [19, 20].

People with dementia represent a vulnerable and growing patient population, with the majority living at home. The national guidelines recommend that dementia assessment should be conducted by general practitioners (GP). Referral to specialist healthcare is recommended in complicated cases when the diagnosis is uncertain or if the patient is under 65 years of age [21]. It is of both clinical and organizational interest to analyse to what extent antidementia medications are initiated by GPs, and how their share of treatment initiation has changed over time. Population-based studies on prescription patterns of antidementia medication exist for some countries [22–25], but there is limited knowledge about the prescription pattern of antidementia medications among home-dwelling people, as well as the involvement of GPs in the prescription of antidementia medication for both men and women. Understanding current treatment practices in a real-world setting is essential for forecasting and planning future healthcare services in the context of an aging population and increasing dementia prevalence. Primary care is increasingly being acknowledged as an important arena for both dementia diagnosis and treatment [26]. While dementia assessment and treatment in general practice are well established in Norway, this is lacking in many other countries. Data on Norwegian prescription patterns can thus be of interest for researchers and policymakers in other countries as well. Therefore, we aimed to investigate trends in incidence rates of symptomatic antidementia drug dispensations among home-dwelling people aged 60 years and older in Norway from 2006 to 2020. We also aimed to examine whether a GP prescribed the first prescription or not.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a repeated annual cross-sectional study utilizing data from the Norwegian Prescribed Drug Registry (NorPD) to investigate the incidence rates of symptomatic antidementia drug (ATC N06D) dispensations from Norwegian pharmacies between January 1, 2006, and December 31, 2020.

Data

In Norway, all residents are assigned a unique personal identification number at birth or upon immigration, which enables the linkage of individual-level data across nationwide registers. This study has linked data from NorPD and the Norwegian General Practitioner (GP) Registry. NorPD covers the entire Norwegian population [27] and contains individual information on all medications dispensed (reimbursed or not) from all Norwegian pharmacies to home-dwelling people by any prescriber since January 1, 2004. NorPD classifies drugs according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system [28]. This study included all symptomatic antidementia drugs licensed in Norway and recommended in the Norwegian dementia guidelines from 2017: donepezil (ATC code N06DA02), rivastigmine (N06DA03), galantamine (N06DA04) and memantine (N06DX01) [21].

Dispensations of Ginkgo biloba (ATC N06DX02) were excluded from the analysis, as this product is not recommended in the Norwegian dementia guidelines [21]. Additional variables extracted from NorPD included the month and year of dispensations, prescriber information, and patient age and sex. The database does not contain information on prescriptions for individuals residing in nursing homes, nor does it include information about over-the-counter drugs [29, 30]. Antidementia drugs are not approved nor, to our knowledge, prescribed for any indication other than dementia in Norway and are not available over the counter [31].

The Norwegian GP Registry is updated monthly and provides an overview of all GPs in Norway and the patients on their lists [32]. We used information from the GP Registry to clarify whether a GP prescribed the first prescription for an antidementia drug or not. Prescriptions were classified as written by a GP if the prescribing doctor had worked as a GP up to 90 days before the prescription was dispensed. This definition reflects the one-year prescription validity and the unknown time between prescribing and dispensing.

The annual Norwegian general population number (60 years and above) per year was provided by Statistics Norway (SSB) [27] and was used as the denominator when presenting trends in incidence rates over time. To ensure a more representative population, the general population was defined as the mean of the general population from January 1 of the current year to January 1 of the following year.

The incidence rates were calculated annually. Incident users were defined as individuals who received their first antidementia drug dispensation between January 1, 2006, and December 31, 2020, with no antidementia drug dispensations in 2004 or 2005. Each person was counted only once; for example, if a person had a novel dispensation for donepezil in 2006 and then later a novel dispensation for memantine in 2009, only the first of the two dispensed drugs was counted.

Study Sample

The study sample included all residents in Norway aged 60 years or older with a first antidementia drug dispensation registered in NorPD in the study period (n = 66,886). Prescriber information was available for 66,789 (99.9%) of the study population but was missing for 97 individuals.

Ethical Considerations

The Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics of Western Norway (REK ID 184568) and the Norwegian Centre for Research Data (SIKT ID 815152) have approved this study. Additionally, this study has approval from the Directorate for e-health regarding exceptions to confidentiality (H-280, Helsedata ID: S-78B978DD).

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA/SE version 18.0 for Windows. Demographic variables were presented as counts and percentages for categorical variables and mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Simple comparisons of proportions were performed with Chi-square tests.

Incidence rates were estimated using Poisson regression with aggregated dispensation counts within strata of sex and 5-year age categories: 60–64.99 years (denoted 60–65 for simplicity), 65–69.99, and so on up to 90+ (age 90 years and over) for each calendar year. Annual national population data from Statistics Norway [27] were converted into mid-year population counts within the same strata and used as exposure in the regression models. Age adjustment was performed by including age group as a categorical variable, both as a main effect and in interaction with calendar year. Marginalization over age then yielded incidence rates that mimic direct standardization to a hypothetical population with an age distribution averaged across the study period (see Supplementary Table S1 in the electronic supplementary material [ESM]). Marginal predictions were weighted by the mid-year population counts. Observed/unadjusted and adjusted incidence rates were estimated per 1000 people in the source population (60 years or older) and presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI) based on the delta method. Linear trends were assessed by modelling calendar year as a continuous independent variable, giving the average change per year. Average incidence rates over the study period for sex and age groups, and incidence rate ratios comparing sexes within age groups, were estimated using the same setup while withholding calendar year from the models. In addition to overall incidence rates for first dispensation of antidementia drugs, we estimated incidence rates for each of four types of antidementia drugs.

The distribution of features within the registered dispensations and associations between them were assessed using regression models for individual (non-aggregated) data. Specifically, factors associated with having had antidementia drugs first prescribed by a general practitioner (as opposed to a specialist) were explored using binary logistic regression models. Calendar year, patient sex, and age group were added as predictors in the full model. Subsequently, all interactions were included and removed in a backwards stepwise manner, keeping and presenting those that were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (OR) were presented with Wald-type 95% CI. Prescription patterns of different types of drugs were compared between GPs and specialists using multinomial logistic regression and presented as differences in marginal proportions with 95% CI derived using the delta method. Adjusted differences were obtained by marginalizing over the distribution of patient sex and age group, based on a model including all two-way interactions (three-way interaction tested and found not statistically significant).

Data Availability

The health registry data used in this study is accessible upon application via Helsedata, a national portal for locating and applying for access to health-related data from various Norwegian sources. The datasets used include data from the Norwegian Prescribed Drug Registry (NorPD). Access to data from the Norwegian General Practitioner Registry requires a separate application to the Norwegian Directorate of Health.

Results

During the study period, 66,886 people received an incident dispensation of an antidementia drug. The mean age at first dispensation was 80.1 years (SD 7.1), ranging from 60 to 105 years. More females (n = 40,760; 61.1% of dispensations) were dispensed an antidementia drug than males (n = 26,126; see Supplementary Table S2, ESM), with a mean age of 80.6 years (SD 7.1) for females and 79.2 years (SD 7.1) for males.

The average incidence rates in different age groups over the study period, separately for males and females, are provided in Table 1. Females had higher incidences in all age groups except among the eldest (90 years or older). The absolute difference between the sexes became more pronounced with increasing age and reached a maximum for those aged 80–85 years, for whom the incidences were highest for both sexes. The relative difference in incidence rates was greatest for age group 70–75 years, where females were dispensed 20% more than males (IRR 1.20, 95% CI 1.15–1.24).

Annual Incidence Rate of Antidementia Drugs

The annual incidence rate of first-time antidementia drug dispensations showed an overall declining trend during the study period, with the highest incidence rate in 2006 and the lowest in 2020 (see Fig. 1A). In 2006, 5.0 per 1000 people aged 60 years and older were dispensed their first antidementia drug, decreasing to 3.0 per 1000 people by 2020. The average decline was −0.11 prescriptions per 1000 people per year (95% CI − 0.12 to − 0.10; p < 0.001). Annual incidence rates for males and females showed similar patterns, but with stronger decline among females: −0.13 (95% CI − 0.14 to − 0.12) for females versus −0.08 (95% CI − 0.09 to − 0.07) for males. Age adjustment modified the temporal trends somewhat, while maintaining the overall pattern (Fig. 1B), giving an estimated decline of −0.08 per 1000 people per year (95% CI − 0.09 to − 0.07) for the total population, −0.09 (95% CI − 0.10 to − 0.07) for females, and − 0.07 (95% CI − 0.08 to − 0.06) for males. Details including confidence intervals for annual incidence rates, as well as age- and sex-adjusted incidence rates, are provided in Supplementary Table S3 (ESM). Supplementary Fig. S1 displays annual incidence rates for each age group for each sex (see ESM).

Types of Antidementia Drug

Donepezil was the most frequently initiated drug, with 38,552 of the 66,886 dispensations (57.6%) being a solo dispensation of donepezil. Rivastigmine was the second most commonly dispensed drug (20,210 dispensations; 30.2%), then memantine (7308 dispensations; 10.9%), and finally galantamine (714 dispensations; 1.2%). One hundred and two dispensations (0.2%) were combinations of two drugs (these are not included in further analyses of types of drugs).

Males were more likely to receive memantine than females (11.9 vs 10.3%; p < 0.001). Males also received more rivastigmine (32.4 vs 29.0%; p < 0.001), whereas females received more donepezil (59.8 vs 54.5%, p < 0.001). The prescription of memantine also increased with higher patient age, 1.4 (95% CI 1.2–1.6) percentage points on average per 5 years, see Supplementary Fig. S2 (ESM).

Temporal trends in incidences of each of the drugs are displayed in Fig. 2. The use of donepezil as first dementia medication declined over the study period, from 3.9 per 1000 in 2006 to 1.5 per 1000 people in 2020 (Fig. 2A). The incidence rate of rivastigmine increased from 0.6 per 1000 people in 2006 to 1.6 per 1000 people in 2012, before declining to 1.2 per 1000 people in 2020. Galantamine dispensation remained consistently low throughout the study period. Memantine incidence rate increased from 0.2 per 1000 people in 2006 to 0.7 per 1000 in 2012, before declining to 0.3 per 1000 people in 2020. Adjusting for age and sex did not change these patterns substantially (Fig. 2B). Further details and confidence intervals are provided in Supplementary Table S4 (ESM).

Prescriber of the First Antidementia Prescription

Among the 66,789 dispensations with known prescriber, a GP was the prescriber in 35,245 cases (52.8%). Females were more likely than males to have the first prescription by a GP (55.1 vs 49.1%; p < 0.001). The probability of a GP being the prescriber increased with age for both males and females, although with a slightly different pattern (p = 0.015), see Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S5 (ESM).

Predicted probability of receiving the first antidementia drug prescription from a general practitioner (GP) in Norway by age

The annual predicted probability of first prescription by GP showed a slight decrease over the study period, with an average reduction of −0.40 percentage points (95% CI −0.49 to −0.31) per year (Fig. 4). After adjustment for age and sex, the marginal reduction was by −0.26 (95% CI −0.35 to −0.17) percentage points per year. The age-adjusted proportions for males and females showed no overall diverging patterns (Supplementary Fig. S3, ESM).

Prescription Patterns for GPs versus Specialists

The GPs prescribed donepezil to a larger extent than specialists (63.6 vs 51.1%, p < 0.001), whereas specialists prescribed more rivastigmine (36.9 vs 24.3%; p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in prescription of memantine, with 11.1 versus 10.8% (p = 0.207) for GPs versus specialists. Allowing for differences in patient age and sex, the adjusted differences in prescription were essentially unchanged (Supplementary Table S6, ESM). Seen over the study period, these patterns were relatively stable, see Figure 5, although with statistically significant year-to-year variation (p < 0.001).

Discussion

There was a declining trend in the annual incidence rate of antidementia drug dispensations to home-dwelling people aged 60 years and older. In addition, there was a decreasing trend in the proportion of people who received their first antidementia drug prescription from a GP during the entire study period. Females consistently had a higher predicted probability of receiving their first antidementia prescription from a GP compared with males, and this sex difference remained stable throughout the study period.

Incidence of Antidementia Drug Dispensations

Our finding of a declining trend in the incidence rate of antidementia drug dispensations aligns with previous studies reporting similar patterns in the use of antidementia drugs over time [22, 24]. This trend may reflect the reported decline in dementia incidence observed in recent decades [33, 34]. However, other factors may also contribute to the observed declining antidementia drug dispensation incidence rates. A Norwegian study analysing age-specific dementia incidence from 2000 to 2019 found a significant decline among participants aged 60–99 years, with similar patterns observed across sexes. The authors conclude that the observed increase in dementia prevalence is primarily driven by an aging population rather than a rise in dementia incidence [33]. The time frame and age group in that study closely match our study, supporting the assumption that the observed declining trend in first-time antidementia drug dispensations may be related to a decline in dementia incidence rates. Following the marked introduction of symptomatic antidementia drugs, relatively more patients, who previously had no option of medical treatment, became eligible for pharmacological therapy. Over time, this may have led to a “saturation point”, where most eligible individuals had already initiated treatment, thereby contributing to the declining trend observed in our study. Additional contributing factors may be prescriber-related, such as increased awareness among prescribers of the modest and short-term efficacy and side effects associated with these medications, as well as their lack of disease-modifying effects [14, 35–37].

Changes in clinical practice, guideline development, and service organization may also have influenced prescribing trends during the study period. The first Norwegian National guidelines for dementia assessment in primary care were established in 2011 [38]. Recommendations prior to 2011 were less formal. Since 2017, a national Norwegian guideline for dementia assessment, including both primary and special healthcare, exists, which is regularly updated [21].

Reimbursement rules during the study period have been independent of the prescriber being a GP or a specialist doctor. Thus, we have no reason to believe that this has influenced the observed incidence of antidementia drug dispensations by GP/non-GP. Further, Norwegian reimbursement rules state that pharmacies need to hand out the cheapest available medication, which often is a generic when marketed, unless the prescription contains a medical statement on why a generic should not be dispensed. We therefore do not believe that generic entries influenced our findings.

Finally, the marked decline in 2020 should be interpreted in light of the COVID-19 pandemic. The nationwide lockdown introduced in March 2020, followed by strict restrictions throughout the year, influenced healthcare utilization during the last months of the study period [39]. Previous studies have reported reductions in face-to-face general practitioner consultations and increased use of virtual consultations, which may have influenced referral patterns and contributed to the observed decline in incidence rates in the last year of the study [40, 41].

Donepezil was the most frequently initiated antidementia drug throughout the study period, consistent with previous studies, which report donepezil as the most common first-line therapy [42, 43]. Its early marked availability and relatively favourable tolerability profile, particularly regarding gastrointestinal adverse effects, compared with galantamine and rivastigmine, may explain its widespread use [44–46]. Rivastigmine, licensed shortly after donepezil in 1998 for mild to moderate AD, was the second most frequently initiated antidementia drug. Its extended indication for symptomatic treatment in PDD in 2006 [10, 47], along with the introduction of the transdermal patch in 2007, may explain the observed increase in use from 2008 onward. Memantine, approved in 2002 for moderate to severe AD and severe mixed dementia [18], showed consistently low incidence rates in this study, possibly reflecting its marginal clinical effects [14, 18] and narrower indication profile. Moreover, people with more severe dementia are often institutionalized and therefore not captured in NorPD, which may further contribute to the low initiation rates observed for memantine [48]. Galantamine was the least dispensed acetylcholinesterase inhibitor in our study. However, a metanalysis concluded that galantamine is effective across all aspects of AD and may be the first choice for the treatment of AD [35]. This may indicate a gap between clinical evidence and prescribing practice or may be related to suitability of available galantamine formulations that may affect adherence negatively [21, 44].

Patient Characteristics and Prescribing Physicians of First Antidementia Drug Prescriptions

Although other studies have smaller cohorts and there may be differences in healthcare systems, the mean age of first antidementia drug dispensation from our study (80.1 years) corresponds well with previous European studies [25, 49]. In our study, the incidence rates increased with age, but declined from age 90 years and older. This may be related to the higher rate of nursing home admissions in the oldest age groups, where medication use is not captured in our database. Overall, females constituted the majority of those who were dispensed a first-time antidementia drug (61.1%) compared with males (38.9%); a trend observed across all age groups, except in the oldest age group (90 years and older). Similar patterns have been reported in other studies [24, 25, 49] and may be related to differences in life expectancy and healthcare-seeking behaviour [50].

To ensure effective and efficient use of healthcare resources, treatment should be provided at the lowest appropriate level of care. According to Norwegian guidelines, dementia assessment, treatment, and follow-ups should mainly be conducted by a GP. Younger patients (<65 years), patients with a high degree of neuropsychiatric symptoms and uncertain or complicated cases are recommended for assessment in specialized care [21]. Our findings show that the likelihood of receiving the first antidementia prescription from a GP increases with age, consistent with previous studies indicating that patients diagnosed in primary healthcare tend to be older than those assessed in specialist healthcare settings [51, 52]. This trend also reflects guideline recommendations that individuals under 65 years of age should be assessed in specialist healthcare [21].

Across all age groups, females were more likely than males to receive their first prescription from a GP, a trend that remained stable throughout the study period. In contrast, men more often received their first prescription from a specialist healthcare professional, consistent with another Norwegian study reporting higher referral rates among men [50]. This could be related to GPs being more confident in diagnosing and treating dementia in females than males, or sex differences in dementia symptom presentation, which may complicate diagnosis in primary healthcare and increase the likelihood of referral to specialist healthcare services. Over time, first prescription by GPs has declined. This trend may reflect evolving practice patterns and increased reliance on specialist memory clinics, alongside changes in clinical guidelines and service organization. Although GPs retained full prescribing rights under the same reimbursement rules as specialists, differences in patient selection and clinical experience partly explain this trend. These changes have implications for service planning, including equitable access to diagnostic services and continuity of care, and raise questions about potential barriers to GP involvement (such as diagnostic uncertainty or reliance on specialist confirmation) and underscore the need for further research into factors influencing prescribing practice in dementia care.

Real-world evidence from other European countries supports the patterns observed in our study. A large multinational study from the UK, Spain and the Netherlands found declining or stabilizing initiation rates of antidementia drugs and substantial heterogeneity in utilization, and age- and sex-related prescribing differences similar to ours [22]. German data further demonstrate regional disparities in access to specialist care and antidementia prescribing, highlighting the influence of system-level factors [53]. To our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically examined GP involvement in initiating antidementia drug treatment, and our findings therefore provide new comparative insight into this aspect of dementia care. European comparisons have shown considerable heterogeneity in how dementia care is organized across countries, including differences in access to memory clinics and pathways to specialist assessment, which may influence the degree of GP involvement in early diagnostic and treatment processes [54, 55]. Nonetheless, cross-country differences in reimbursement policies and healthcare organization likely contribute to variation in prescribing patterns and access to care. Together, these findings indicate that the Norwegian trends align with broader European developments in dementia diagnosis and treatment.

Prescription Patterns for GPs versus Specialists

GPs and specialists showed distinct but overall stable prescribing patterns across the study period. GPs more frequently initiated donepezil, even after adjusting for age and sex, and this aligns with previous studies which reported donepezil as the most common first-line therapy [42, 43]. Specialists, on the other hand, prescribed rivastigmine more often, possibly reflecting its broader indication profile. No statistical difference was observed between GPs and specialists in the prescribing of memantine. A German study reported that specialists prescribed more antidementia drugs than GPs. The majority of AD patients with antidementia drug treatment received AChEIs, and both specialists and GPs prescribed more AChEIs than memantine [56]. This aligns with our findings showing that AChEIs are more often prescribed than memantine, both by specialists and GPs. A UK study reported that memantine is rarely initiated in general practice, due to uncertainty about dementia staging, limited awareness of national guidelines, conflicting local formularies, and broader system-level barriers [57]. Although these factors were not reflected in differing memantine prescribing rates in our study, they highlight the potential influence of system-level structures and prescriber confidence in prescribing roles in dementia care. Galantamine use remained uniformly low for both prescriber groups, suggesting that national prescribing norms and tolerability considerations outweigh differences in diagnostic settings. The largely parallel trends seen in Fig. 5 indicate that although specialists adopt certain treatments earlier, overall prescribing patterns in primary and specialist care are shaped by shared guidelines and clinical practice norms.

Strengths and Limitations

The inclusion of all antidementia drug dispensations in Norway at the individual level enhances the comprehensiveness of the dataset. Another strength is the extended observation period, which reduces the influence of short-term fluctuations in drug dispensation patterns. The use of a national central health registry ensures high quality and completeness of the data. This minimizes selection bias and provides high generalizability to the population receiving these medications. Access to individual-level data enables stratification by sex and age, as well as potential linkage to other national health registries. However, this study has some limitations. First, we did not have information about the dementia diagnosis. Therefore, we used the first antidementia drug dispensation as a proxy for dementia diagnosis. As a result, this study cannot inform on the proportion of those with a dementia diagnosis receiving symptomatic antidementia drugs. Secondly, clinical information such as dementia severity was not available, nor was data on medication use in nursing homes or other institutional settings. Moreover, the denominator included all individuals aged ≥ 60 years, including nursing home residents who are not captured in NorPD, potentially leading to some underestimation of incidence rates. Data on changes in institutionalization during the study period were unavailable. The multivariable logistic regression models included age, sex, and year only, as data on other potential confounders such as geographic region, socioeconomic status, comorbidity burden, and proximity to specialist services were not available.

Conclusion

There was a declining trend in the annual incidence rate of antidementia drug dispensations to home-dwelling people from 2012 until the end of the study period in 2020, with donepezil remaining the most commonly dispensed antidementia drug. Older age and female sex were associated with a higher likelihood of receiving a first-time symptomatic antidementia drug prescription from a GP. However, the proportion of such prescriptions prescribed by GPs to both females and males declined over time. This raises questions about potential barriers to GP involvement, and there is a need for further research into factors influencing prescribing practice in dementia care.

The datasets used include data from the Norwegian Prescribed Drug Registry (NorPD). Access to data from the Norwegian General Practitioner Registry requires a separate application to the Norwegian Directorate of Health.
