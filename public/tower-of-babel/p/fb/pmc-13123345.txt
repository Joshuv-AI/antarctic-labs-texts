Pilot Study of Diagnostic Performance of Serum Creatinine/Cystatin C Ratio for Pseudo-AKI
Authors: Ran Jiang, Yafeng Huang, Lingling Deng, Lu Yang, Huijuan Mao, Buyun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe conducted a proof-of-concept study to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the serum creatinine/cystatin C ratio (CrCyR) in distinguishing pseudo-acute kidney injury (AKI) from true AKI.MethodsThis study included patients with confirmed AKI (excluding rhabdomyolysis) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (May 2023–August 2024) and pseudo-AKI cases identified through a literature review (1994–2025). CrCyR values (unit: l/dl) measured before (CrCyRpre) and during AKI onset (CrCyRpost) were collected. Diagnostic performance of CrCyRpost was assessed using the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsOf the 239 patients, 197 had true AKI and 42 had pseudo-AKI (mechanisms: reduced creatinine excretion [n = 18], assay interference [n = 14], urine leakage [n = 7], and increased creatinine production [n = 3]). Median CrCyRpost was significantly higher in the pseudo-AKI group than in the true AKI group (1.89 [Inter Quartile Range {IQR}: 1.32–3.28] vs. 0.78 [0.64–0.94] l/dl; P < 0.001). CrCyRpost showed a preliminary AUC of 0.97 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.95–1.00); an exploratory threshold of > 1.11 l/dl yielded 95% sensitivity and 91% specificity for diagnosing pseudo-AKI. After adjustment for age and sex, the covariate-adjusted AUC remained high at 0.98 (0.96–1.00). Diagnostic utility was consistently high across pseudo-AKI subtypes (AUCs: 1.00 for assay interference, urine leakage, and increased creatinine production; 0.94 for reduced creatinine excretion). A cross-center robustness assessment using patients with true AKI from the Salzburg Intensive Care database suggested consistent diagnostic utility (AUC: 0.94 [0.90–0.98]) in Europeans.ConclusionCrCyRpost preliminarily demonstrates promising diagnostic accuracy for distinguishing pseudo-AKI from true AKI. Further validation in prospective studies is warranted.

AKI affects approximately 20% of hospitalized patients and is associated with a mortality rate of 20% to 30%.1 Current diagnosis relies primarily on serum creatinine (SCr)–based criteria as defined by the 2012 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines.2 However, SCr is an indirect surrogate of glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and may increase in the absence of true impairment in kidney function. Consequently, some patients meet diagnostic criteria for AKI despite preserved GFR, a condition known as pseudo-acute kidney injury (pseudo-AKI). Pseudo-AKI may arise from increased creatinine (Cr) production,3 reduced tubular Cr secretion,4 sampling or assay interference,5 or urine leakage (including urinary ascites and urinomas).6 Misclassification of pseudo-AKI as true AKI can lead to inappropriate diagnostic labeling and unnecessary interventions, including invasive procedures, dialysis, or unwarranted discontinuation of essential anticancer or anti-infective treatments.7,8

Conventional approaches to identifying pseudo-AKI, such as formal GFR assessment, exclusion of alternative causes of AKI, or repeated biochemical testing after elimination of interfering factors, are often time-consuming, costly, and disruptive to ongoing treatment. There is therefore an urgent need for rapid and reliable methods to distinguish pseudo-AKI from true AKI at the time of presentation. Cystatin C (CysC), a low–molecular-weight protein produced at a relatively constant rate and freely filtered by the glomerulus, provides a creatinine-independent marker of renal filtration.9 It can be increased during AKI episodes after cardiac surgery10 or sepsis.11 We previously observed that an elevated serum CrCyR was associated with pseudo-AKI in a patient with urinary ascites.12 Building on this observation, we conducted a pilot study to evaluate the diagnostic performance of CrCyR for differentiating pseudo-AKI from true AKI, hypothesizing that CrCyR could serve as a promising diagnostic marker.

Methods

Study Design

This was a proof-of-concept diagnostic study, which was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Jiangsu Province Hospital, Ethics No.: 2025-SR-106) and conducted in adherence to the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies standards.

Study Population

Pseudo-AKI

We searched the PubMed, Embase, and CNKI databases for studies reporting pseudo-AKI cases published between January 1994 and December 2025. The core search strategy, adapted to the specific syntax of each database, was based on the following terms: (('‘Pseudo’' OR ‘Spurious’ OR ‘False’') AND (‘Creatinine’ OR ‘Acute Kidney Injury’ OR ‘AKI’) AND ‘Cystatin C’). The inclusion criteria encompassed urine leakage, drug-related pseudo-AKI, assay interference, or elevated Cr, in patients of all ages, with CysC values measured concurrently during elevated Cr episodes. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) not fulfilling AKI criteria; (ii) end-stage kidney disease (ESKD); (iii) pregnancy or postpartum period. True AKI as controls

We retrospectively included consecutive patients diagnosed with AKI according to KDIGO-AKI guidelines from May 2023 to August 2024, identified through the AKI alert system at Jiangsu Province Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) diagnosis of AKI per KDIGO-AKI guidelines; (ii) traceable baseline SCr and CysC values, with simultaneous testing for SCr (using enzymatic method, μmol/l) and CysC (using immunoturbidimetry method, mg/l) at the time of AKI diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) ESKD; (ii) pregnancy or postpartum period; (iii) receiving targeted anticancer therapy; (iv) baseline SCr < 0.5 mg/dl or > 3 mg/dl; (v) possible rhabdomyolysis defined as creatine kinase > 1000 U/l; (vi) diagnosis of pseudo-AKI.

True AKI in cross-center assessment

We screened all patients having AKI with CrCyR in the Salzburg intensive care database (SIC-db). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) availability of simultaneous SCr (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes code 2160-0) and CysC (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes code 33863-2) at the time of AKI diagnosis; and (ii) diagnosis of AKI per KDIGO-AKI guidelines. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) ESKD or baseline SCr > 3 mg/dl; (ii) pregnancy or postpartum period; (iii) possible rhabdomyolysis defined as creatine kinase >1000 U/l; (iv) recent urinary tract surgery potentially complicated by urine leakage; (v) use of anticancer agents.

Data Collection

Baseline characteristics were collected for both groups, including sex, age, cause of AKI, cause of pseudo-AKI, baseline SCr, CysC and calculated CrCyR (CrCyRpre, units: l/dl), and SCr, CysC and calculated CrCyR (CrCyRpost, units: l/dl) at the time of AKI or pseudo-AKI. “Sex” referred to phenotypical characteristics and was extracted from electronic health records or case reports.

Definitions

AKI was defined in line with the 2012 KDIGO AKI Clinical Practice Guidelines as follows: (i) an increase in SCr of ≥ 0.3 mg/dl (> 26.5 μmol/l) within 48 hours; (ii) an increase in SCr to ≥ 1.5 times baseline, occurring within 7 days; or (3) urine output of < 0.5 ml/kg/hour for 6 hours.2 Baseline SCr was defined as the lowest SCr value before AKI alert in our hospital or that during hospitalization in SIC-db. Baseline CysC in pseudo-AKI was defined as the earliest available value before pseudo-AKI onset; if unavailable, the lowest value among ≥ 2 measurements was used, otherwise the baseline value was considered missing.

Laboratory Methods

Information regarding assay methods for SCr and CysC was unavailable for most patients with pseudo-AKI. For patients with true AKI at JSPH, SCr and CysC concentrations were measured using the Beckman Coulter AU5800 system. SCr levels were determined via an enzymatic method (reagents from Shanghai Kehua Bio-Engineering Co., Ltd), whereas CysC levels were assessed using immunoturbidimetry (reagents from Beijing Leadman Biochemistry Co., Ltd).

Assay imprecision was evaluated by analyzing 2 control specimens provided by Lyphochek Assayed Chemistry Control. For SCr, the within-run and between-run coefficients of variation were 1.74% and 1.80%, respectively, when at a low concentration of 155.92 μmol/l, and 2.07% and 2.32% when at a high concentration of 445.56 μmol/l. For CysC, the within-run and between-run coefficients of variation were 2.65% and 3.97%, respectively, when at a low concentration of 0.38 mg/l, and 3.94% and 4.06% when at a high concentration of 0.66 mg/l.

Statistical Methods

Sample size considerations were guided by the anticipated diagnostic performance of the index test, quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Assuming an expected AUC of 0.85, a 2-sided α of 0.05, 90% power, and a 1:1 case-control ratio, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 12 participants per group.

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as medians (interquartile range [IQR]: Q1, Q3) or means (standard deviation [SD]) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Paired comparisons (e.g., CrCyRpost vs. CrCyRpre) were performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The diagnostic performance of the CrCyR was evaluated using ROC analyses. Unadjusted ROC curves were constructed, and AUCs with 95% CIs were estimated using DeLong’s method. To account for potential confounding by age and sex, covariate-adjusted ROC analyses were conducted using the ROCnReg package (function AROC.sp),47,48 with bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) used to estimate 95% CIs for the adjusted AUC. Exploratory optimal cut-off values were determined by maximizing Youden’s index. In addition, prespecified alternative thresholds were evaluated to support rule-in (high specificity) and rule-out (high sensitivity) strategies. Diagnostic performance at each cut-off was summarized using sensitivity, specificity, Youden’s index, and positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR−).

Primary analyses were conducted using the first CrCyRpost measurement for each patient. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the results by the following: (i) excluding pediatric cases to account for physiological differences; and (ii) using repeated CrCyRpost measurements as independent observations to assess stability against measurement variability. Subgroup analyses were then performed to assess diagnostic consistency across sex, age categories, and pseudo-AKI subtypes. Furthermore, exploratory analyses assessed the diagnostic performance of the relative change (CrCyRpost / CrCyRpre) and absolute change (CrCyRpost - CrCyRpre) among patients with available baseline values. Finally, to assess potential robustness and applicability across centers, the distribution of CrCyRpost in pseudo-AKI cases from the derivation cohort was compared with that observed in an independent true-AKI cohort from SIC-db.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2., The R foundation). All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The study included 239 patients (Figure 1), comprising 197 with true AKI and 42 with pseudo-AKI (including 3 pediatric cases). Among patients with true AKI, the most common etiologies were surgery-associated (n = 64, 32.5%) and septic AKI (n = 64, 32.5%), followed by nephrotoxic (n = 39, 19.8%) and obstructive AKI (n = 30, 15.2%). Among pseudo-AKI cases, increased Cr production, urine leakage, reduced Cr excretion, and Cr interference accounted for 3 (7.1%), 7 (16.7%), 18 (42.9%), and 14 (33.3%) cases. Stratified by sex (Figure 2b), CrCyRpost levels remained significantly higher in the pseudo-AKI group compared with the true AKI group for both females (1.36 [1.21–2.44] vs. 0.68[0.55–0.83] l/dl, P < 0.001) and males (2.38 [1.75–5.56] vs. 0.81 [0.68–0.95] l/dl, P < 0.001). Additionally, we observed a sex difference within both groups, with males exhibiting significantly higher CrCyRpost values than females (Pseudo-AKI: P = 0.008; True AKI: P = 0.001).

Among true AKI subtypes (Figure 2c), patients with obstructive AKI exhibited the highest median CrCyRpost (0.90 [0.79–1.04] l/dl), which was significantly higher than those with septic (0.76 [0.64–0.96] l/dl; P = 0.006) , nephrotoxic (0.79 [0.62–0.86] l/dl; P = 0.003) and surgical AKI (0.70 [0.61–0.88] l/dl; P < 0.001). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the surgical and septic groups (P = 0.227).

Among the 4 pseudo-AKI subtypes (Figure 2d), the interference group exhibited the highest first CrCyRpost (3.50 [2.62–9.04] l/dl). This was significantly higher than both the decreased Cr excretion group (1.29 [1.16–1.56], P < 0.001) and the increased Cr production group (1.75 [1.68–1.90], P = 0.006). The urine leakage group also showed elevated levels (2.38 [1.94–5.84] l/dl), which were significantly higher than the decreased Cr excretion group (P = 0.002) but statistically comparable to the interference group (P = 0.254).

Diagnostic Efficacy of CrCyR for Pseudo-AKI

Forty-two patients with pseudo-AKI exhibited substantially higher CrCyRpost than the 197 patients with true AKI (1.89 [1.32–3.28] vs. 0.78 [0.64–0.94] l/dl; P < 0.001), with clear separation between groups on the log-transformed scale (Figure 3a). CrCyRpost preliminarily demonstrated promising diagnostic performance for pseudo-AKI, with an AUC of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–1.00); the exploratory optimal cutoff (>1.11 l/dl) yielded a Youden’s index of 0.87, with 95% sensitivity and 91% specificity (Figure 3b). To enhance potential clinical utility, we suggested a high-specificity cutoff (>1.67 l/dl, specificity 100%) to rule in pseudo-AKI, and a high-sensitivity cutoff (<0.76 l/dl, sensitivity 100%) to rule it out (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis using repeated CrCyRpost measurements suggested the robustness of the findings, yielding an AUC of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–0.99) with a cutoff of >1.05 l/dl.

CrCyRpost preliminarily demonstrated near-perfect diagnostic accuracy (AUC 1.00) for assay interferences, increased Cr production, and urine leakage, with exploratory thresholds of >1.75, >1.49, and >1.21 l/dl, respectively. For reduced Cr excretion, performance remained acceptable (AUC 0.94) with a lower cutoff of >1.05 l/dl. All subtypes demonstrated favorable sensitivity (94%–100%) and specificity (87%–100%).

Exploratory change-based analyses demonstrated similar diagnostic performance with that of CrCyRpost. The relative change of CrCyR (CrCyRpost / CrCyRpre) yielded an AUC of 0.91 with a cutoff of > 1.22, whereas the absolute change of CrCyR (CrCyRpost − CrCyRpre) showed an AUC of 0.95 with a cutoff of > 0.20 l/dl. Both models achieved a sensitivity of 94%, with specificities of 78% and 84%, respectively.

Cross-Center Assessment

To assess the robustness and transportability of the diagnostic approach, additional analyses were conducted using true AKI controls from the SIC-db, whereas the pseudo-AKI cases remained fixed. CrCyRpost levels were markedly higher in patients with pseudo-AKI than in those with true AKI from the SIC-db (1.89 [1.32–3.28] vs. 0.91 [0.71–1.06] l/dl; P < 0.001) (Figure 3c). CrCyRpost demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.90–0.98) and a covariate-adjusted AUC of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.87–0.97) (Figure 3d). Sex-stratified analyses showed consistent results, with AUC values of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.87–0.98) for males and 0.96 (95% CI: 0.89–1.00) for females, further supporting diagnostic performance across sexes in Europeans.

Discussion

The lack of rapid and effective diagnostic tools for pseudo-AKI often delays recognition and leads to inappropriate treatment modification or unnecessary interventions. In this proof-of-concept study, we demonstrate that CrCyR exhibits promising diagnostic performance for pseudo-AKI and its 4 subtypes, providing preliminary evidence of robustness and generalizability in Asian and European populations. To our knowledge, this is the first biomarker-based diagnostic approach specifically developed to address this clinically critical condition. Accordingly, individual centers could consider establishing a diagnostic approach for pseudo-AKI by referencing our summarized pseudo-AKI data with local CrCyR distributions and applying center-specific calibration to account for population characteristics.

The diagnostic separation observed is biologically plausible and supported by historical evidence. CysC, originally identified as 'γ-trace' in 196149 and proposed as a biomarker for GFR in 1985,50 differs fundamentally from SCr in its non-GFR determinants, being largely independent of muscle mass and tubular handling. These properties provide a clear biological rationale for combining the 2 markers into a ratio that captures discordant changes beyond GFR alone. On this basis, the CrCyR was first introduced as a diagnostic marker for postrenal AKI in 2014,51 and has since been extensively studied as a surrogate for muscle mass and sarcopenia. It has shown consistent associations with mortality across diverse populations, including community-dwelling adults,52,53 patients with cancer,54 chronic kidney disease (CKD),55,56 and dialysis-requiring AKI.57

Across these studies, CrCyR values vary systematically by population and sex. Higher ratios have been reported in US adults (median 1.13 [0.97–1.30])53 than in East Asian populations, including Chinese community-residents aged ≥ 45 years (0.77 [0.67–0.89]),58 Korean patients with cancer (0.82 [0.63–1.05]),54 or patients with dialysis-requiring AKI (0.91 [0.64–1.27]).57 Moreover, CrCyR is consistently higher in males than in females by approximately 0.15 l/dl across multiple populations (0.85 ± 0.20 vs. 0.74 ± 0.19 in Chinese community-residents58; 1.09 ± 0.19 vs. 0.91 ± 0.18 in Korean patients with non-dialysis CKD55; 1.05 ± 0.17 vs. 0.89 ± 0.18 in middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults59), reflecting sex-related differences in muscle mass and Cr generation. In our cohort, baseline CrCyR levels in both true AKI and pseudo-AKI were comparable to those reported in East Asian populations but lower than those observed in US adults, likely reflecting older age and higher burden of comorbidities in the study population. Importantly, true AKI was characterized by a slight increase in CrCyR (95% CI of mean difference: 0.02–0.07 l/dl), consistent with concurrent impairment of GFR affecting both Cr and CysC. In contrast, pseudo-AKI was associated with disproportionate SCr elevations because of increased production, reduced excretion, urinary leakage, or assay interference, without a parallel rise in CysC, resulting in a marked increase in the CrCyR. This divergence may provide a clear pathophysiological basis for its diagnostic utility.

CrCyR preliminarily demonstrated promising diagnostic accuracy across multiple pseudo-AKI etiologies, with near-perfect discrimination for urine leakage and assay interference. Diagnostic performance remained favorable in drug-related pseudo-AKI, despite relatively modest Cr elevations, as an increase of approximately 0.20 l/dl in CrCyR exceeded the intrinsic change observed in true AKI (95% CI of mean difference: 0.02–0.07 l/dl). Of note, CysC rises earlier and more rapidly than SCr in true AKI, leading to a decline in CrCyR during the early phase of AKI, which may enhance discrimination between early true AKI and pseudo-AKI.60 These findings suggest CrCyR as a promising alternative to complex exogenous GFR measurements,61 enabling reliable identification of pseudo-AKI even when Cr elevation is not extreme.

Urine leakage represents a clinically critical and high-risk subtype of pseudo-AKI, often mimicking true AKI with azotemia, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis, and is frequently associated with diagnostic delay.12 A systematic review reported a misdiagnosis rate of 64% and a mortality rate of 15% among 351 patients with pseudo-AKI because of spontaneous bladder rupture.62 The majority of such cases are diagnosed during exploratory laparotomy (51.9%), and diagnostic delay was identified as the primary contributor to mortality.7 In this context, the near-perfect diagnostic performance of CrCyR observed in our analysis preliminarily supports its role as a rapid, cost-effective, and noninvasive tool for identifying pseudo-AKI in patients with suspected urinary ascites.12

In our study, CrCyR changes from baseline did not improve discrimination over CrCyRpost. Despite accounting for low baseline CrCyR in conditions such as severe sarcopenia or liver cirrhosis, both relative and absolute changes may be susceptible to post-AKI muscle catabolism, which may cause nonspecific increases in CrCyR and attenuate diagnostic specificity. Moreover, baseline SCr and CysC measurements are often unavailable in routine practice, limiting the feasibility of baseline-dependent approaches. These considerations favor the use of CrCyRpost as a more practical diagnostic measure for pseudo-AKI.

The exploratory optimal CrCyR cutoff identified in this study was lower than values typically observed in healthy individuals, thereby limiting its application to distinguishing pseudo-AKI from true AKI rather than screening the general population. To enhance diagnostic certainty, we propose an exploratory tiered threshold strategy as follows: a cutoff of > 1.67 l/dl to confirm pseudo-AKI (100% specificity in our cohort) and < 0.76 l/dl to rule it out (100% sensitivity). A cutoff of > 1.11 l/dl may serve as an alert threshold to prompt suspicion of pseudo-AKI, achieving a sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 91%. These thresholds may help diagnostic, confirmatory, and exclusionary decisions but need further validation before clinical use. However, caution is warranted in hypercatabolic states. We observed significantly elevated CrCyR in rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI, indicating a risk of false-positive classification; accordingly, patients with creatine kinase levels >1000 U/l were excluded. Similarly, patients with markedly elevated baseline SCr and ESKD were excluded. Awareness of these exclusions is essential for future applications.

This pilot study has several limitations. First, SCr and CysC assays were not fully standardized across the literature and our center, potentially introducing methodological heterogeneity. Systematic assay-related bias may shift the CrCyR distribution and influence the exploratory optimal thresholds without fundamentally altering discriminatory capacity. Nevertheless, the widespread use of automated assays, comparable baseline distributions between true AKI and pseudo-AKI, and consistent findings from cross-center and European population validations suggest that substantial bias is unlikely. Second, our pseudo-AKI cases derived from previously reported cases requiring concurrent SCr and CysC measurements, were inherently limited in size. This necessitates robust external validation in larger, prospective cohorts before these preliminary thresholds can be considered for potential clinical application. Third, although the 3 included pediatric cases preliminarily suggest CrCyR values consistently above the proposed 1.11 l/dl, targeted validation in larger pediatric cohorts is essential given their distinct physiological characteristics. Finally, true AKI cases were predominantly drawn from surgery-associated, obstruction-related, and septic AKI identified through our electronic alert system, limiting the evaluation of anticancer drug-related AKI. Further studies are therefore needed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of CrCyR in this population.

In conclusion, our proof-of-concept study indicates the feasibility and preliminary potential of the serum creatinine/cystatin C ratio for differentiating pseudo-AKI from true AKI, with consistent diagnostic performance across sexes, multiple etiologies, and populations. The exploratory cutoffs identified in this study require further rigorous validation in larger, independent cohorts to support potential future clinical application.
