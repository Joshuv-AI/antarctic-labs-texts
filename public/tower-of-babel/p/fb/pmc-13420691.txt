This ECG Does Not Fit

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnderson-Fabry disease (AFD) is a rare cause of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) that can be challenging to diagnose, particularly when comorbidities are present.Case SummaryWe report a 70-year-old man with controlled hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, prior percutaneous coronary intervention on the left anterior descending artery, evaluated for progressive LVH and electrocardiogram (ECG) changes during follow-up. ECG showed an atypical pattern with right bundle branch block. Cardiac magnetic resonance then demonstrated reduced native T1 values. Careful evaluation of ECG abnormalities may be sufficient to trigger targeted, second-level, diagnostic tests.

Progressive Left Ventricular Hypertrophy With an Atypical Electrocardiogram in a 70-Year-Old Patient With Several Comorbidities

A 70-year-old man with hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic kidney disease (CKD), prior percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) on the left anterior descending artery (LAD), with progressive left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) during follow-up (FU). Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed an atypical pattern with right bundle branch block (RBBB). Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated reduced native T1 values. Genetic testing identified the p.N215S (p.Asn215Ser) mutation, confirming late-onset Anderson-Fabry disease (AFD).

History of Presentation

A 70-year-old man with chronic ischemic heart disease and hypertrophic left ventricle (LV) experienced his first heart failure (HF) decompensation requiring hospitalization. He presented clinically with exertional dyspnea with mild to moderate physical activity consistent with NYHA functional class II-III. He denied typical angina, syncope, or palpitations. The electrocardiogram (ECG) (Figure 1) revealed atrial fibrillation with complete right bundle branch block (RBBB) with a prolonged QRS duration of 136 ms, with triphasic rSR’ pattern in the right precordial leads, and slurred S waves in the lateral leads, in addition to electrocardiographic signs of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH). Transthoracic echocardiography showed concentric LVH with an interventricular septal thickness of 19 mm and a posterior wall thickness of 20 mm, preserved LV systolic function, and a restrictive diastolic filling pattern, consistent with HF with preserved ejection fraction. The ECG findings, together with the progressive worsening of LVH at echocardiography despite stable or improving blood pressure control over the years, prompted further diagnostic evaluation.

Past Medical History

The patient underwent percutaneous coronary intervention with stent placement in the circumflex artery and mid-distal segments of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) at age 57. Relevant medical history also included long-standing arterial hypertension, atrial fibrillation, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage IV, and peripheral artery disease treated with percutaneous angioplasty. During cardiology follow-up, routine echocardiographic assessment demonstrated progressive LVH worsening (interventricular septal thickness increasing from 13 mm to 16 mm and subsequently to 19 mm over 10 years). This finding was ultimately interpreted as a hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenotype, in the context of multiple comorbidities including hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and CKD.

Differential Diagnosis

Review of the patient's longitudinal clinical records revealed generally acceptable blood pressure control over time, with office measurements largely within the normotensive range or consistent with mild (grade 1) hypertension. LVH appeared disproportionate to the degree of hypertension documented during follow-up and therefore not fully explained by hypertensive heart disease alone.

Moreover, review of the patient's historical ECGs revealed progressive changes over the years preceding the present encounter (Figure 2). Approximately 14 years earlier, ECG demonstrated high QRS voltages, a borderline-short PR interval and deep inverted T waves, interpreted in the context of ischemic heart disease and hypertrophic phenotype (Figure 2A). ECG then showed progressive reduction of QRS voltages, reversion of T-wave morphology to upright/biphasic T waves, PR prolongation, and nonspecific ischemic changes (Figure 2B), following hypertension control. Months before HF decompensation, complete RBBB appeared, with progressive slurring and widening of the QRS (Figure 2C). Notably, ECGs B and C in Figure 2 both showed only mild ECG changes in terms of LVH parameters; the main finding was the eventual development of complete RBBB with QRS slurring, which in the absence of significant proximal LAD disease, ongoing ischemia, or chronic cor pulmonale, and with a progressive increase in LVH, was considered worthy of further investigation. The late development of complete RBBB is unusual in sarcomeric HCM and hypertensive heart disease, prompting reconsideration of the underlying diagnosis. The differential diagnosis was therefore expanded to include infiltrative and storage cardiomyopathies as alternative etiologies of conduction abnormalities and myocardial hypertrophy.

Investigations

Coronary angiography confirmed patency of the stents in the circumflex artery and mid-distal LAD, with noncritical distal stenoses beyond the LAD stent and no hemodynamically relevant disease involving the left main coronary artery or proximal LAD. Cardiac amyloidosis was ruled out with complete hematological tests (that excluded the presence of a monoclonal component) and a negative bone scintigraphy.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (Figure 3) demonstrated asymmetric LVH, with maximal wall thickness of 23 mm at the basal-to–mid-anterior interventricular septum. Normal LV cavity size, systolic function and regional wall kinetics were confirmed. Contrast medium was not administered due to stage IV CKD with estimated glomerular filtration rate of 29 mL/min/1.73 m2. Next-generation sequencing panels for genetically determined LVH identified a pathogenic variant in the GLA gene (c.644A>G, p.N215S) encoding for alpha-galactosidase A, confirming the diagnosis of late-onset Anderson-Fabry disease (AFD).

Management

The patient was then referred to our multidisciplinary AFD team and disease-specific enzyme replacement therapy was initiated. Moreover, a transvenous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was inserted for primary prevention following episodes of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia recorded at telemetry monitoring during a subsequent hospitalization for HF, also considering the progressive phenotype of LVH.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient tolerated the enzyme replacement therapy well. He was followed up at the local cardiology clinic and at our AFD clinic with clinical stability for 2 years. He then died due to HF progression and concomitant severe comorbidities. Among the patient's pedigree, 2 female patients were identified and are followed in our outpatient AFD clinic.

Discussion

AFD is a rare, X-linked, metabolic disorder determined by pathogenic variants in the GLA gene, leading to reduced or absent enzymatic activity of alpha-galactosidase and lysosomal accumulation of globotriaosylceramide in multiple organs and tissues.1 The clinical spectrum ranges from the pediatric-onset classic multisystem involvement to late-onset disease that manifests clinically in adulthood. The latter is characterized by cardiac manifestations as predominant—but not unique—features, including LVH, conduction disturbances, and, lately, HF.2

Late-onset AFD represents a particular diagnostic challenge for cardiologists, as cardiac involvement can overlap phenotypically with other etiologies. The prevalence of AFD in screening studies of patients with LVH is variable,3 but approximately 1% in a cohort of unselected, primarily hypertensive, patients with LVH.4 Late-onset disease can be recognized in patients with long-standing LVH that may have been attributed to systemic arterial hypertension or other more common etiological causes (like HCM).

The present case exemplifies this clinical scenario: a patient followed for more than a decade with progressive LVH, initially attributed to hypertensive heart disease and multiple comorbidities, including CKD initially ascribed to diabetic nephropathy, without suspicion of a multisystemic disease. Importantly, it was the progression of ECG abnormalities on routine ECG, associated with increasing LVH, that prompted suspicion of an alternative diagnosis. Retrospective review of serial ECGs revealed progressive electrocardiographic evolution over more than a decade, with initial repolarization abnormalities and high voltages followed by voltage reduction, PR prolongation, and eventual development of complete RBBB with initial QRS slurring. This progression paralleled the increase in LVH and restrictive diastolic pattern at echocardiography. Such dynamic ECG changes reflect the evolving myocardial substrate of AFD, with storage-related and inflammatory changes followed by progressive fibrosis and conduction system involvement.5 The first ECGs showing relatively short PR and marked T-wave inversion further support the importance of a cardiomyopathy-oriented mindset when evaluating unexplained LVH and considering alternative etiologies, including AFD. However, this remains a rare and challenging diagnosis, and in this patient the coexistence of significant coronary artery disease at that time may have further complicated the interpretation of electrocardiographic findings.

In this regard, suspicion of an alternative etiology does not necessarily require advanced or resource-intensive diagnostic modalities. Recent studies have investigated distinctive ECG features associated with AFD that differ from sarcomeric HCM and hypertensive heart disease. In a comparative analysis between AFD and HCM, short PR interval, prolonged QRS duration, RBBB, prominent R wave in aVL (>1.1 mV) and inferior ST-segment depression were independently correlated with AFD.6 Notably, a predilection for RBBB emerged in patients with AFD, which has been corroborated by further studies. In a study analyzing ECG features as markers of progressive phenotypic expression in AFD, a significant association between AFD and RBBB was confirmed, with increasing prevalence in proportion to the degree of LVH.7 Complete RBBB was present in more than half of patients with LV wall thickness >19 mm. Although the precise mechanisms underlying the selective vulnerability of the RBB in AFD are not completely clear, it could be related to the thinner anatomical structure of the right bundle branch compared with its left counterpart, its superficial course, and a potentially more prevalent early globotriaosylceramide accumulation at the basal interventricular septum level, a region critical for right-sided conduction tissue integrity.7

This case underscores some critical take-home messages for clinical practice. Late-onset AFD should be considered in the differential diagnosis of LVH even in older adults and those with apparent alternative etiologies or multiple cardiovascular risk factors. Importantly, sophisticated diagnostic tools are not always required to raise initial suspicion. Careful ECG analysis can provide critical diagnostic clues and may be sufficient to prompt further evaluation. When LVH and RBBB coexist, echocardiographic reassessment is reasonable; here it demonstrated marked septal hypertrophy inconsistent with hypertensive disease alone, broadening the differential diagnosis toward infiltrative/storage cardiomyopathies. This case serves to highlight the value of ECG as a powerful and accessible tool in the identification of rare cardiomyopathies.
