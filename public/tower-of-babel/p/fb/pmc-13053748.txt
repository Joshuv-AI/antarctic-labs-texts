Parabacteroides distasonis ameliorates ovariectomy-induced bone loss by regulating the gut microbiota and Th17/Treg balance
Authors: Wenyuan Hou, Fangjun Xiao, Jiaru Liao, Junjiao Zhang, Longfei Han, MinCong He, Qiushi Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPostmenopausal osteoporosis is linked to estrogen deficiency, immune imbalance, and gut microbiota alterations that can promote bone loss. Using an ovariectomized (OVX) rat model, we found that Parabacteroides distasonis abundance decreased after OVX. Oral supplementation with live P. distasonis improved distal femoral trabecular microarchitecture and increased femoral bending strength, alongside shifts in bone turnover markers consistent with reduced resorption. Treatment was associated with improved colonic histology, higher expression of tight junction markers (ZO-1 and occludin), and lower circulating LPS. In mesenteric lymph nodes, P. distasonis shifted Th17/Treg-related readouts and cytokine profiles toward a less inflammatory state, with parallel changes in marrow-region markers. 16S rRNA profiling and targeted SCFA analysis showed microbiota remodeling and increased butyrate after treatment. These results support microbiota-based approaches targeting the gut-immune-bone axis to mitigate estrogen deficiency-associated bone loss.Subject areas: health sciences, microbiology, microbial metabolism, molecular microbiology, clinical microbiology

Introduction

Postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP) is a metabolic bone disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and disrupted bone microarchitecture, leading to increased fragility and fracture risk.1 These changes are primarily driven by an imbalance between bone formation and resorption due to estrogen deficiency.2 With rising incidence and associated disability and mortality, PMOP has become a significant global public health concern.3 Recent studies highlight the pivotal role of gut microbiota disturbances and Th17/Treg immune imbalance in promoting osteoclast overactivity, thereby accelerating bone loss.4 The gut microbiota and intestinal barrier form a critical ecosystem that maintains immune homeostasis. Disruptions in this ecosystem can skew the Th17/Treg balance, intensifying intestinal inflammation and systemic immune dysregulation in PMOP.5 These observations have led to growing interest in the gut-immune-bone axis as a therapeutic target for PMOP.

Several studies have reported that probiotic supplementation or microbiota modulation can attenuate bone loss in estrogen-deficient models.6,7 However, many of these studies focus on mixed probiotic formulations or global alterations in microbial communities, providing limited insight into the contribution of specific bacterial species or immune pathways. Therefore, identifying defined gut commensals and clarifying their mechanistic links to osteoimmune regulation remain important challenges.

Parabacteroides distasonis (P. distasonis), a commensal bacterium in the human and animal gastrointestinal tract, has shown promise in modulating immune responses and improving inflammatory diseases.8 It has been reported to alleviate rheumatoid arthritis by restoring Th17/Treg balance,9 and its supplementation has improved liver fibrosis in animal models.10 Additionally, P. distasonis enhances intestinal barrier integrity and improves insulin resistance by activating intestinal G protein-coupled receptor 109a.11 These findings suggest that P. distasonis may regulate the gut-immune-bone axis, offering therapeutic benefits in PMOP.

In this study, we examined the potential role of P. distasonis in PMOP using an ovariectomized (OVX) rat model. We observed a reduced relative abundance of P. distasonis-assigned sequences in OVX rats, and supplementation was associated with remodeling of the gut microbiota toward a sham-like profile. These changes coincided with Th17/Treg-related immune shifts and lower inflammatory readouts, along with improved colonic histology and increased expression of barrier-associated markers. Collectively, our findings support an association between P. distasonis supplementation and improved gut-immune-bone features in OVX rats, suggesting P. distasonis as a candidate for microbiota-targeted strategies against estrogen deficiency-related bone loss.

Results

Probiotic properties of P. distasonis

P. distasonis was successfully activated and cultured on tryptic soy agar/broth solid medium, where it exhibited round, smooth, and off-white raised colonies with relatively neat edges. After 16S rRNA sequencing, the sequence homology was 99%, confirming the successful activation of P. distasonis. When cultured for 0–2 h, bacterial growth was slow; from 2 to 14 h, bacteria entered the logarithmic growth phase, showing rapid growth, which stabilized around 12–14 h. At 14 h, the growth rate peaked, and the bacteria then entered the stationary phase, as shown in Figure 1A. In the gastrointestinal tract, gastric acid and bile salts have a potential inactivating effect on probiotics. Resistance to these external factors is crucial for ensuring bacterial survival and effective colonization. The growth of P. distasonis was inhibited to varying degrees under acidic conditions and different bile salt concentrations, as shown in Figures 1B and 1C.

P. distasonis improves femoral microstructure and bone strength in OVX rats

To evaluate the impact of P. distasonis on OVX-induced OP, P. distasonis transplantation was performed (Figure 2A). The uterine index (uterine weight/body weight) and serum estrogen (E2) levels were significantly decreased in OVX rats, confirming the successful establishment of the model. Administration of P. distasonis increased serum E2 levels (Figure 2C), but the effect was not significant, and it had no impact on estrogen deficiency-induced uterine atrophy (Figure 2D). Compared to the sham group, the OVX and P. distasonis groups showed a significant increase in body weight (Figure 2B). Therefore, P. distasonis treatment had no significant effect on weight gain or uterine atrophy induced by estrogen deficiency.

To investigate the effect of P. distasonis on OVX-induced bone loss, Micro-CT was used to assess the bone microstructure in the distal femur. In the OVX group, BMD, BV/TV, Tb.Th, and Tb.N were reduced, while Tb.Sp was increased (Figures 3D–3H), indicating significant loss of bone microstructure. Oral administration of live P. distasonis improved distal femur bone microstructural integrity, while oral administration of killed P. distasonis had no effect on the bone microstructure of OVX rats (Figure 3A). These results suggest that oral P. distasonis has a therapeutic effect in alleviating OP.

Biomechanical properties of the femur were measured using a three-point bending test. The maximum bending stress represents the highest stress a material can withstand before permanent deformation, reflecting the strength of the material under bending force. The elastic modulus measures a material’s ability to return to its original shape after external force is applied. A larger elastic modulus indicates greater rigidity and resistance to deformation. The OVX group showed decreased elastic modulus and maximum bending stress, while LPD treatment significantly improved these parameters, indicating that LPD enhances femoral bone strength in OVX rats (Figures 3B and 3C).

P. distasonis regulates OVX-induced bone turnover

OP is typically accompanied by changes in bone metabolism. To evaluate the regulatory effect of LPD on bone metabolism, we assessed changes in bone pathology and serum levels of bone turnover biomarkers. HE staining results were consistent with those obtained by Micro-CT (Figure 4A). In the OVX group, serum calcium, CTX-I, and CTX-I/PINP levels were significantly increased, while PINP expression was decreased. LPD treatment significantly reduced these changes, indicating a marked increase in bone resorption activity, which was reversed by LPD (Figures 4C–4F).

TRAP staining was performed to further evaluate the expression of bone turnover markers in bone tissue. Osteoclasts, identified as multinucleated positive cells with red peripheral staining, were quantified for osteoclast number per unit bone surface area (OC.N/BS). OVX led to a significant increase in osteoclasts (Figure 4G). LPD treatment effectively countered this increase, as shown in the representative images (Figure 4B). These results suggest that LPD may regulate bone metabolism by modulating the activity of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, thereby counteracting OVX-induced bone loss.

P. distasonis enhances intestinal barrier-marker expression

Given the close relationship between intestinal barrier function, systemic inflammation, and bone metabolism, we next investigated whether the bone-protective effects of P. distasonis were associated with improvements in intestinal barrier integrity. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is present in the outer membrane of most Gram-negative bacteria and is considered a potential marker for increased intestinal permeability, specifically indicating bacterial translocation.12 After LPD treatment, serum LPS levels were significantly reduced (Figure 5A). To evaluate the effect of LPD on estrogen deficiency-induced intestinal barrier damage, we analyzed colonic structure and barrier integrity using HE staining and Alcian Blue-Periodic Acid-Schiff (Ab-PAS) staining. In the sham group, the mucosa, muscularis mucosa, submucosa, and muscular layers were clearly defined, with well-arranged crypts and no signs of inflammation, necrosis, edema, or goblet cell atrophy. The morphology was normal. OVX caused localized damage to the tissue and mucosa, including atrophy and deformation of the intestinal glands, with some replacement by proliferating fibrous tissue and inflammatory cell infiltration. LPD treatment reduced mucosal damage and inflammatory cell infiltration, improving crypt structure (Figures 5B–5D).

ZO-1 and Occludin are essential tight junction proteins that maintain epithelial barrier integrity.13 Immunohistochemistry and RT-qPCR results showed increased expression levels of ZO-1 and Occludin after LPD intervention (Figures 6A–6F). These findings suggest that LPD may protect or repair the intestinal mucosal barrier by influencing the expression of tight junction proteins.

P. distasonis modulates Th17/Treg-associated marker and cytokines in mesenteric lymph nodes

The thymus and spleen are essential immune organs in animals. When the body encounters external stimuli, immune cells such as T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and natural killer cells are activated and proliferate, leading to thymic and splenic enlargement.14 As shown in Figures 7A and 7B, compared to the sham group, the thymus index (thymus/body weight) was significantly increased in the OVX group, suggesting an inflammatory response. LPD treatment slightly reduced this increase. However, there was no significant difference in the spleen index (spleen/body weight) between the groups.

To assess intestinal inflammation, we compared the populations of CD4 + IL-17A + Th17 cells and CD4+CD25 + FOXP3 + Treg cells in the mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) (Figure 7C). Due to variations in the size of MLN tissue samples, absolute cell counts from flow cytometry might lack precision. Therefore, we calculated the proportions: the percentage of IL-17A/CD4 represented the Th17 cells, the percentage of FOXP3/CD25 represented Treg cells, and the IL-17A/FOXP3 ratio was used to evaluate the inflammatory status of the MLN. Compared to the sham group, the OVX group showed an increase in Th17 cells (Figure 7D), which was reduced after LPD treatment, while Treg cells exhibited the opposite trend (Figure 7E). Furthermore, IL-17A/FOXP3 ratio analysis revealed that OVX-induced osteoporosis (OP) was accompanied by an imbalance in the Th17/Treg ratio in the gut, and LPD treatment significantly reduced this ratio (Figure 7F), alleviating intestinal inflammation.

During intestinal inflammation, the body releases various pro-inflammatory factors, such as IL-17 and TNF-α. As shown in Figures 7G–7J, compared to the sham group, the OVX group exhibited significantly higher levels of IL-17 and TNF-α in the colon, and significantly lower levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β, indicating that OVX exacerbated colonic inflammation. After LPD intervention, IL-17 and TNF-α levels in the colon significantly decreased, while IL-10 and TGF-β levels increased. These results suggest that the impact of LPD treatment on OVX-induced intestinal inflammation may be associated with mechanisms that suppress Th17 cell production and related cytokines IL-17 and TNF-α, promote Treg cell proliferation, and enhance the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β. Given the limited biological n for MLN flow cytometry; these immune findings are interpreted as supportive mechanistic evidence rather than definitive quantification of the immune phenotype in OVX animals.

P. distasonis regulates femoral Th17/Treg-associated marker in relation to colonic inflammation

To explore the connection between the gut-bone axis and inflammatory responses, we assessed the expression levels of the specific transcription factors RORγt (a marker for Th17 cells) and FOXP3 (a marker for Treg cells) in bone marrow (BM) to evaluate the changes in the Th17/Treg cell ratio. Immunohistochemistry and quantitative analysis revealed that, compared to the sham group, the OVX group exhibited significantly higher expression of RORγt and lower expression of FOXP3 in BM (Figures 8A–8C). LPD treatment downregulated RORγt and restored FOXP3 expression. Further analysis showed that the RORγt/FOXP3 ratio was significantly increased in the BM of the OVX group, indicating an imbalance in Th17/Treg immune homeostasis. LPD intervention reduced this ratio to near-normal levels. Cytokine assays in BM further supported these findings. In the OVX group, pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-17 and TNF-α were elevated, while anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β were reduced. LPD treatment reversed this trend (Figures 8D–8G), suggesting that LPD alleviates systemic inflammation by modulating the Th17/Treg-associated marker.

P. distasonis modifies gut microbial diversity in OVX rats

By sequencing the 16S rRNA gene amplicons from the feces of each group of rats, we analyzed the diversity, compositional changes, and differential probiotic bacteria of the gut microbiota. The Chao1 and Shannon indices reflect species richness and diversity. Compared to the OVX group, the Chao1 indices in the LPD and sham groups were significantly reduced, indicating a decrease in species richness. However, no significant differences were observed in the Shannon index across the three groups, suggesting that the overall diversity (including both richness and evenness) of the microbial communities remained relatively stable (Figures 9A and 9B). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was used to assess the similarity of microbial communities between the groups. The results showed a significant overlap between the sham and LPD groups, indicating that their microbial community structures were quite similar (Figure 9C).

(D) Relative abundance of gut microbiota at the phylum level across groups.

(E) LDA identifying differentially abundant taxa among the three groups.

To further investigate the specific changes in bacterial communities, we generated a clustering histogram to display the variations at the phylum level in the gut microbiota of each sample. The results revealed that Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Verrucomicrobiota were the predominant phylum, with Bacteroidetes increasing in both the sham and LPD groups, while it decreased in the OVX group (Figure 9D). This suggests that Bacteroidetes may contain key beneficial bacteria. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) is a powerful statistical tool used for biomarker discovery in high-dimensional data, and is widely applied in microbiome studies involving 16S rRNA sequencing. In this context, the primary function of LDA is to identify microbial taxa that are significantly associated with different biological conditions or phenotypes. To identify probiotics significantly expressed in the sham group, LDA analysis revealed that P. distasonis was elevated in both the sham and LPD groups (Figure 9E).

P. distasonis promotes production of short-chain fatty acids

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), key metabolic products of gut microbiota, have a wide range of biological functions. They play crucial roles in immune modulation, promoting the differentiation and maturation of immune cells, maintaining microbial balance, inhibiting the growth and proliferation of harmful bacteria, stimulating the growth of beneficial bacteria, enhancing intestinal digestion and absorption, accelerating gastrointestinal motility, and regulating glucose and lipid metabolism. As shown in Figure 10A, compared to the sham group, SCFA levels were significantly reduced in the OVX group. However, after LPD intervention, butyrate levels were significantly elevated.

To further visualize the relationship between the inflammation generated in the gut and bone tissue and the association between OP and Th17/Treg in OVX, we performed a correlation analysis using integrated data and heatmaps. The results aligned with our initial hypothesis, showing significant correlations between osteogenic factors and anti-inflammatory factors, as well as between osteoclast factors and pro-inflammatory factors. Moreover, trends in the sham and LPD treatment groups were more similar (Figure 10B). The specific correlation of SCFAs is shown in Figure 10C, where butyrate was significantly positively correlated with bone density.

Discussion

In this study, we observed that the abundance of P. distasonis in the feces of OVX rats was significantly reduced compared to healthy controls, accompanied by a marked decrease in the gut microbiota-derived metabolite butyrate. Further analyses showed that P. distasonis supplementation was associated with increased fecal P. distasonis abundance, elevated butyrate levels, shifts in gut microbiota composition, improved barrier-related readouts, and Th17/Treg-associated immune changes, alongside attenuation of OVX-induced femur bone loss in rats.

Bone is a dynamic tissue that undergoes continuous remodeling, involving bone resorption and formation.15 Serum C-terminal telopeptide of CTX-I and PINP are commonly used biomarkers to evaluate bone turnover, representing bone resorption and formation, respectively.16 In our study, P. distasonis administration not only reduced CTX-I levels but also elevated PINP levels in OVX rats, indicating an improvement in bone turnover. Prior to menopause, stable estrogen levels help maintain the balance between bone formation and resorption. However, during menopause, the decline in estrogen accelerates bone remodeling, where resorption exceeds formation. This increased resorptive activity leads to excessive calcium loss from the skeleton into circulation.17 Consistent with this mechanism, our results showed a marked increase in serum calcium in OVX rats, which was significantly reduced following P. distasonis supplementation.

The intestinal barrier is a complex, multilayered system that prevents harmful substances from entering bodily tissues while facilitating nutrient absorption.18 It is composed of the gut microbiota, an external mucus layer, the intestinal epithelial cell layer, and the lamina propria.19 However, in postmenopausal women, declining estrogen levels increase intestinal permeability.20 This may facilitate translocation of microbial products across the epithelium and trigger immune responses. This may promote the upregulation of osteoclast-related factors such as TNF-α and RANKL, which can enter systemic circulation through the compromised barrier and subsequently affect the skeletal system, contributing to bone loss.21 Our data are consistent with this model: P. distasonis administration was associated with improved colonic histology and increased expression of tight junction-associated markers (ZO-1 and Occludin). However, functional permeability was not directly measured in this study.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating immune responses and bone mass.22 Around menopause, significant alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota can influence both local and systemic immunity, disrupting bone homeostasis and potentially leading to the development of OP.23 Previous studies have reported that estrogen deficiency induces gut dysbiosis, which abnormally activates the immune system. This activation promotes the upregulation of RANKL on Th17 cells and increases the proliferation of Th17 cells and their pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-17. These factors collectively enhance osteoclast activation, resulting in bone resorption and OP.24 Conversely, regulatory T cells (Treg), another major T cell subset, suppress osteoclast differentiation by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and IL-35, thereby contributing to bone preservation.25 Hence, maintaining the Th17/Treg balance is critical for skeletal homeostasis. In our study, we examined Th17/Treg-related changes in gut-associated immune tissues and bone-associated readouts. We observed an increased proportion of Th17 cells and a marked decrease in Treg cells in the MLNs, along with consistent changes in RORγt and FOXP3 expression in the femur. P. distasonis treatment was associated with a shift toward a less inflammatory Th17/Treg profile and reduced inflammatory readouts.

Probiotic bacteria are live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host, primarily by competing with pathogenic bacteria and maintaining overall microbial balance.26 Under normal conditions, the gut microbiota and the host engage in mutualistic interactions that sustain a relatively stable microbial composition and function, a state referred to as gut microbiota homeostasis.27 Modulating gut microbiota composition is one plausible mechanism by which probiotics may influence OP-related phenotypes. Our study demonstrated that OVX alters the diversity and richness of the gut microbiota, with Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Verrucomicrobia identified as the dominant phylum. Notably, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio was increased in the OVX group, consistent with previous findings and other studies using OVX models.28,29 Recent research has highlighted the protective role of P. distasonis in preserving the intestinal barrier and mucus layer, as well as its ability to attenuate intestinal inflammation.30 In line with these findings, we observed a significant reduction in the abundance of P. distasonis in OVX rats. Supplementation with P. distasonis not only modulated the alterations in various genera induced by OVX but also markedly increased its own abundance. These results suggest that P. distasonis may exert its beneficial effects by enhancing its own colonization and thereby reshaping the gut microbiota composition to restore functional balance.

SCFAs, key metabolic byproducts of gut bacteria, are primarily derived from the fermentation of undigested carbohydrates.31 These metabolites possess diverse biological functions, including lowering intestinal pH, promoting mucosal growth, altering intestinal villus architecture, thereby increasing calcium absorption in the gut.32 In addition, SCFAs can promote the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Tregs.33 The expansion of Treg populations downregulates AP-1 activity, facilitating NFAT and SMAD transcriptional signaling, which in turn activates Wnt10b and stimulates osteoblast differentiation via the Wnt signaling pathway.34 Targeted fecal metabolomic analysis revealed that P. distasonis treatment significantly enhanced SCFA metabolism in OVX rats, with a marked increase in butyrate levels. Although our data are consistent with an SCFA-associated immunomodulatory framework linking P. distasonis to bone-related outcomes, definitive causal relationships-such as the necessity or sufficiency of butyrate or specific immune subsets-will require targeted interventions (e.g., metabolite supplementation, receptor perturbation, or immune-specific manipulation) in future studies.

In summary, live P. distasonis supplementation was associated with improved distal femur trabecular microarchitecture and femoral mechanical properties in OVX rats, alongside coordinated changes in gut microbiota composition, barrier-related markers, immune-associated readouts, and SCFAs.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Barrier-related conclusions are based on histology, tight-junction marker expression, and serum LPS, rather than direct functional permeability assays. Microbiome and SCFA profiling was performed only at the endpoint, and SCFAs were quantified in serum only, limiting longitudinal inference and a direct readout of microbial production in the gut. Immune evidence is supported by MLN flow cytometry with a limited biological n and by marker-based readouts in femoral marrow regions rather than lineage-resolved BM flow cytometry. In addition, skeletal outcomes were assessed primarily in the femur, and thus conclusions should be interpreted as femur-focused. Future studies incorporating functional permeability assays, longitudinal sampling, tissue-resolved immune quantification, and targeted causal interventions will be important to test mediation and strengthen translational relevance.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals and OVX procedure

Female specific pathogen-free Sprague-Dawley rats (10 weeks old, 200-240g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (GZUCM), Certificate No. SYXK(GD)2023-0347. Rats were housed under controlled temperature and humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to sterilized food and water. After a 7-day acclimatization period, experiments were conducted in an SPF barrier facility, and all materials were sterilized using ultraviolet light.

The OP model was established by bilateral OVX. Rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg, 0.1 mL/100 g), and the ovaries were surgically removed at the intersection of the femur and spine. Sham-operated rats underwent removal of equal amounts of adipose tissue. To prevent infection, penicillin was administered intramuscularly for three consecutive days postoperatively. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of GZUCM (Approval No. 20240130017).

Treatments and sample collection

P. distasonis (BNCC 354946, Henan Industrial Microbial Strain Engineering and Technology Research Center, China) was cultured in Brain Heart Infusion medium (Huankai, China) and tryptic soy agar/broth containing 5% sheep blood (Solarbio, China).

To assess the therapeutic effects of P. distasonis, rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 8 per group): Sham, OVX, OVX + live P. distasonis (OVX+LPD), OVX + heat-killed P. distasonis (OVX+KPD), and OVX + alendronate sodium (OVX+ALN). OVX or sham surgery was performed on Day 0, followed by postoperative penicillin sodium (200,000 U/kg, intramuscular, once daily) for 3 days (Days 0–3) and a 1-week recovery including an antibiotic-free washout (Days 4–7). Oral gavage started on Day 7 and continued for 12 weeks: OVX+LPD received live P. distasonis (2 × 108 CFU/day in sterile PBS, 1 mL), OVX+KPD received heat-killed P. distasonis at an amount equivalent to 2 × 108 CFU prior to inactivation (in sterile PBS, 1 mL), and OVX+ALN received alendronate sodium (1 mg/kg, 1 mL); all gavage interventions were administered once daily. Fecal, serum, and tissue samples were collected only at the endpoint (at sacrifice after 12 weeks of gavage). Because baseline/pre-gavage fecal and serum samples were not collected, microbiome and SCFA analyses represent endpoint cross-sectional comparisons rather than within-animal longitudinal changes. Live P. distasonis was prepared freshly under anaerobic conditions and administered at 2 × 108 CFU/day by oral gavage. Heat-killed P. distasonis was generated by autoclaving at 121°C for 15–20 min, and loss of viability was confirmed by absence of colony formation on anaerobic blood agar plates after incubation in an anaerobic jar.

Body weights were monitored weekly. At the end of the intervention, rats were euthanized, and fecal samples were collected aseptically and stored at −80°C. Serum was obtained by centrifuging whole blood at 3000 rpm for 1 min at 4°C. Uterus, spleen, and thymus were excised and weighed. Uterine, spleen, and thymus indices (g/kg) were calculated as organ weight (g) divided by body weight (kg). Colonic tissues were rinsed in PBS and fixed in 10% formalin. Mesenteric lymph nodes (100 mg) were preserved in precooled PBS. Femoral bones were fixed in 10% formalin.

Method details

Preparation and activity assessment of P. distasonis

Lyophilized P. distasonis powder was reconstituted in sterile conditions and cultured at 37°C for 24h. Bacterial cultures were transferred to Brain Heart Infusion medium, and counted by plate counting to form up to 108 CFU and stored at 4°C.

Strain identification was confirmed by 16S rRNA sequencing. Bacterial growth kinetics were determined by measuring OD600 at various time points over 24h. Acid and bile salt tolerance were assessed by culturing in media at pH 2–6 and bile salt concentrations of 0–0.3%, followed by OD600 measurement.

Micro-CT analysis

Distal femurs were scanned using a NEMO micro-computed tomography system (NMC-200, Pingseng Scientific, Kunshan, China) at 50 kV and 300 μA with an isotropic voxel size of 9 μm. Scans were acquired using a 0.5-mm aluminum filter, an exposure time of 800 ms, over a 360° rotation with a 0.3° rotation step and frame averaging of 4. For trabecular bone analysis, the region of interest (ROI) was defined as 70 consecutive cross-sectional slices (total length 0.63 mm) starting 0.05 mm distal to the growth plate. BMD was calibrated using hydroxyapatite phantoms (0.25 and 0.75 gHA/cm3) scanned under identical conditions. Trabecular bone was segmented using a uniform global fixed threshold (80–255 grayscale units) applied to all samples. Quantitative parameters within the ROI included bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Bone biomechanics

Biomechanical properties of the femurs were assessed using a three-point bending test. After blood collection, femurs were rapidly dissected and stored at −80°C until testing. Prior to testing, right femurs were thawed overnight at room temperature. Each femur was placed horizontally on a biomechanical testing machine (Labsans, Shenzhen, China) with a support span of 12 mm. Loading was applied at the midshaft at a constant displacement rate of 0.01 mm/s until fracture occurred. Load-displacement curves were recorded to obtain the maximum bending load. To account for the presence of the medullary cavity, the femoral midshaft was modeled as a hollow ellipse. Cross-sectional geometric parameters were derived from micro-CT-based three-dimensional reconstructions. Elastic modulus and maximum bending stress were calculated using standard beam-bending equations.

Biochemical analysis

Serum calcium was measured using a Mindray calcium assay kit. ELISA kits were used to quantify PINP, CTX-I, TNF-α, IL-17, TGF-β, IL-10, and LPS levels in serum, colon, and bone marrow samples. Absorbance was measured with a microplate reader, and standard curves were used for quantification.

Histological assessment of the bone

Distal femurs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 week, decalcified in EDTA for 4 weeks, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate trabecular bone morphology and overall histological features. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was performed to visualize osteoclasts, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Osteoclasts were defined as TRAP-positive multinucleated cells located on the trabecular bone surface. Quantification was performed using ImageJ within a standardized trabecular region of interest (ROI) in the distal femur metaphysis (measurement area: 0.2 mm2, 0.4 mm × 0.5 mm), with the nearest and farthest borders positioned 0.2 mm and 0.7 mm distal to the growth plate, respectively. Osteoclast number was expressed as osteoclast number per bone surface (Oc.N/BS). For histological analyses, two non-adjacent sections were examined per animal, and three non-overlapping fields within the ROI were quantified per section. Measurements were averaged to yield a single value per animal prior to statistical analysis.

Histological evaluation of the colon

Paraffin-embedded proximal colon tissues were sectioned at 4 μm and stained with H&E and Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schiff (Ab-PAS). Ab-PAS positive goblet cells within colonic crypts were quantified using ImageJ. For each animal, two sections were analyzed and three non-overlapping fields were randomly selected per section within the same anatomical region; values were averaged to generate a single value per animal for statistical analysis (animal as the experimental unit; n = 3 per group). Samples were coded prior to quantification, and investigators were blinded to group allocation.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections of femur and colon were immunohistochemically stained with FOXP3, RORγt, ZO-1, and Occludin antibodies. Integrated optical density (IOD) values were calculated using ImageJ.

RNA Extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAeasy Animal RNA Extraction Kit (Beyotime, R0026, China). cDNA was synthesized using M-MLV reverse transcriptase and oligo-dT primers (Promega, USA). Gene expression was analyzed by qRT-PCR and normalized to GAPDH using the 2ˆ-ΔΔCT method. Primers used: ZO-1: F: 5′-GGGGAAACCCGAAACTGATGC-3′, R: 5′-GTGGAGAGAAGAGAGTTGGACAGAGGC-3′; Occludin: F: 5′-GGGACAGAGAGCCTATGGAACG-3′, R: 5′-CCAAGGAAGCGATGAAGCAA-3′; GAPDH: F: 5′-TATCCGACCCCTCGTTAC-3′, R: 5′-CTGTGCCGTTGAACTTGC-3′.

Flow cytometry analysis

Mesenteric lymph nodes were mechanically dissociated into single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed using ammonium chloride-based lysis buffer at room temperature, followed by washing with PBS. For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 50 ng/mL) and ionomycin (1 μg/mL) at 37°C for 2 h. Brefeldin A (10 μg/mL) was then added, and cells were incubated for an additional 4-6 h to block cytokine secretion. After stimulation, cells were washed and stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 to exclude dead cells. Cells were then stained with antibodies against CD45 (APC-Cy7), CD3 (FITC), CD4 (PE-Cy7), and CD25 (BV421) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Following surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using a transcription factor staining buffer set according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular staining was performed with anti-FOXP3 (APC) and anti-IL-17A (PE) antibodies for 40–50 min at 4°C in the dark. Cells were washed and resuspended in staining buffer for acquisition. Data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star). Single-stained controls were used for compensation, and fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls were applied to define gating boundaries. The gating strategy was as follows: lymphocytes were identified by forward- and side-scatter characteristics, followed by singlet discrimination. Live CD45+CD3+CD4+ T cells were selected for downstream analysis. Th17 cells were defined as CD4+IL-17A + cells. Treg cells were defined as CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ cells.

16S rRNA sequencing

Fecal pellets were collected at the endpoint, snap-frozen, and stored at −80°C until processing. Microbial DNA was extracted using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with ultrapure water processed in parallel as a negative control. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). PCR cycling conditions were: 98°C for 30 s; 32 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 54°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Amplicons were purified and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 2 × 250 bp). Demultiplexed reads were processed using a standardized pipeline: primer trimming (cutadapt), paired-end merging (FLASH), quality filtering (fqtrim; Q < 20; removal of reads <100 bp or containing >5% ambiguous bases), and chimera removal (VSEARCH). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred using DADA2 within QIIME2 (denoise-paired), and singleton ASVs were removed. Taxonomy was assigned using the QIIME2 feature-classifier against the SILVA database (release 138; min_confidence = 0.7). Sequencing depth was summarized as ∼74,000 reads per sample (range: 56,139-81,258). For α- and β-diversity analyses, samples were rarefied to 56,000 sequences per sample, corresponding to the minimum sequencing depth. β-diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis distances and visualized by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Group differences in β-diversity were tested by PERMANOVA (Adonis; 999 permutations) with effect size reported (F(2,21) = 2.95, R2 = 0.33, p < 0.001). Differentially abundant taxa were evaluated using LEfSe with parameters p < 0.05 and LDA score ≥3.0. Where multiple comparisons were performed, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied as indicated.

Targeted metabolomics

Standard curves were established for seven SCFAs (acetic, propionic, butyric, isobutyric, valeric, isovaleric, hexanoic acids). Samples were homogenized with phosphoric acid, internal standard (isocaproic acid), and ether, then centrifuged. Supernatants were analyzed via GC-MS under optimized chromatographic and mass spectrometry conditions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (unless otherwise indicated), The value of n represents the number of animals per group. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Brown-Forsythe test. For comparisons among the multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was applied when assumptions were met, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. When assumptions were violated, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used with Dunn’s multiple-comparisons correction. Weekly body weight was analyzed using a mixed-effects model (REML) with group, time, and their interaction as fixed effects and animal as a random effect, followed by multiplicity-adjusted post hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001). Exact n values and statistical tests used are specified in the corresponding figure legends.
