Long-term Outcomes (10 years) of Hepatic Transplantation in a Patient with Hepatic Adenomatosis: A Case Report
Authors: Abdullah Almunifi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHepatocellular adenoma is a rare benign liver neoplasm, and hepatic adenomatosis is associated with risks such as intertumoral hemorrhage or necrosis. The imaging features of adenomatosis closely resemble those of solitary adenomas, differing only in the number of lesions. This study reports a case of an 18-year-old woman with perturbed liver function enzymes and multiple hepatic nodules. She had no significant medical history, chronic liver disease, or diabetes and was not taking any regular medications or anabolic steroids. Abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly and multiple hepatic nodules. Subsequently, the patient underwent hepatic orthotropic transplantation without any complications and was followed-up for 10 years. Multiple lesions with suspicious or confirmed malignant transformation and unwillingness to undergo surgical resection are considered leading indications for hepatic transplantation. Despite efforts to manage the condition conservatively, including hepatic transplantation, the patient experienced complications related to immunosuppressant therapy and ultimately developed chronic rejection. Hepatic transplantation is rarely indicated for benign liver tumors, especially when life-threatening complications or hepatic tumor malignant transformation cannot be ruled out, and tumors are unrespectable. This case highlights the complexity of managing hepatic adenomatosis and underscores the importance of individualized treatment strategies and long-term follow-up.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular adenoma is a rare benign liver neoplasm characterized by its hyper vascular nature, devoid of bile ducts, and marked by multiple dilated capillaries.[1] The risk factors for this condition include obesity, familial polyposis, and glycogen storage disease.[2] Hepatic adenomatosis is a benign lesion typically occurring in young women of childbearing age.[3]

Hepatocellular adenoma is a single tumor that may appear as two or three nodules. In 1985, Flejou et al.[4] originally described hepatic adenomatosis as numerous hepatocellular adenomas in the normal liver parenchyma. At that time, the minimum number of adenomas necessary for the diagnosis of hepatic adenomatosis diagnosis was 10.[56] Although individuals with simple hepatocellular adenomas seldom have more than four lesions, several studies have selected four or five nodules as the threshold.[78] Although its origin and natural history are unknown, hepatic adenomatosis is currently regarded as a clinical-pathological entity separate from hepatocellular adenoma.

Hepatic adenomatosis is associated with risks such as intertumoral hemorrhage or necrosis.[91011] The imaging pattern of hepatocellular adenoma is nonspecific, given its location in the adenomatous tumor and varied vascularization. In addition to the number of lesions, the imaging characteristics of adenomatoses and solitary adenomas are identical, therefore, the differential diagnosis remains a challenge for medical teams.[12]

Herein, we report a case of a young female patient with hepatic adenomatosis and describe the clinical presentation, diagnostic challenges, management strategies, and long-term outcomes, aiming to contribute to the understanding and management of this rare condition. This case was reported in accordance with the Surgical CAse REport (SCARE) criteria.

CASE PRESENTATION

An 18-year-old woman presented with no significant medical history, chronic liver disease, or diabetes. She was not taking any regular medications or anabolic steroids. She visited a gynecologist for screening and check-ups before using oral contraception. Her gynecologist found perturbed liver function enzymes, with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level 120, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level 88, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) level 590, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) level 505, cholesterol level >11, and creatinine level 49, and her serology revealed negative result for viral hepatitis and cytomegalovirus (CMV).

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed hepatomegaly and multiple hepatic nodules, prompting referral to a hepatologist. Laboratory findings included a-fetoprotein level of 2.4 mg/mL, albumin level of 43.4, alpha-1 globulin level of 2.9, alpha-2 globulin level of 11.8, beta globulin level of 11.7, gamma globulin level of 14.2, total protein (TP) level of 82%, fibrinogen level of 5.6, and platelet count of 54,000. Axial T2 and T1 magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with perfusion (TESLASCAN) showed multi-nodular intrahepatic lesions, with the most significant lesion of approximately 13 cm in the right liver and additional lesions spanning all liver segments except for segments VII and IV. These lesions were presumed to be adenomas based on imaging characteristics. Hepatic percutaneous biopsy confirmed adenomas in all lobes without fibrosis, ruling out any underlying hepatic disease.

A multidisciplinary team discussed the case. Given the number and size of the adenomas, risk of hemorrhage, and unresectable nature of lesions, the patient was assessed for hepatic transplantation. Two years later, she underwent orthotopic hepatic transplantation from a deceased 22-year-old donor without complications. The donor has died due to cranial trauma. The operation was performed without intraoperative complications, and the ischemia duration was 9 h and 30 min. She was discharged on day 10 after transplantation, with immunosuppression prescribed using cortancyl 5 mg daily and prograf 2 mg bid.

Macroscopic examination of the explanted liver revealed a weight of 2090 g and dimensions of 28 × 20 × 12 cm, with more than 30 adenomas, including multiple intrahepatic masses and a large lesion measuring 17 × 11.5 × 8 cm in the right lobe. Seven years post-transplantation, she was readmitted due to elevated liver enzymes attributed to immunosuppressant drug rupture. Upon discharge, her laboratory results showed a creatinine level of 93 μmol/l, TP level of 100%, bilirubin level of 48 μmol/l, AST i.u/l level of 132 i.u/l, ALT level of 388 i.u/l, GGT level of 1048 i.u/l, and ALP level of 280 i.u/l. The immunosuppressive regimen was adjusted, consisting of prograf 4 mg twice daily, cellcept 1.5 g twice daily, and cortancyl 40 mg/day.

Her final admission occurred 10 years post-transplantation due to discontinuation of immunosuppressant medication, resulting in chronic rejection. Laboratory findings upon admission included a creatinine level of 92, TP level of 67%, total bilirubin level of 196, AST level of 340, ALT level of 584, GGT level of 356, and ALP level of 351. Liver biopsy confirmed chronic rejection. Upon discharge, her laboratory results revealed a TP level of 79%, AST level of 283 i.u/l, ALT level of 537 i.u/l, GGT level of 370 i.u/l, and ALP level of 321 i.u/l. The immunosuppressive regimen was modified to prograf 5 mg twice daily, cellcept 1.5 g twice daily, and cortancyl 60 mg/day. She is currently awaiting re-transplantation.

DISCUSSION

The definition of adenomatosis, elucidated in 1985,[4] distinguishes between single or numerous adenomatoses and hepatic adenomatosis. Unlike hepatocellular adenomas, which are not associated with oral contraceptive use, hepatic adenoma is linked to this etiology, characterized by an expected increase in serum ALP and GGT levels in adenomatosis but uncommon in hepatocellular adenoma. Despite this, the definition of hepatic adenomatosis has occasionally been overlooked, with some publications applying it when there are more than three or four nodules,[8] leading to a shifting connotation, especially in women exposed to oral contraceptives.

Hepatic transplantation for hepatic adenomatosis is warranted only in exceptional situations.[13] The primary rationale for liver transplantation in patients with hepatocellular adenomas involves multiple lesions with suspected or proven malignant transformation, reluctance toward surgical excision, and other factors. In a case series, Chiche et al.[12] proposed a guideline encompassing one significant criterion—histologic proof of malignant transformation—and five minor criteria, including previous life-threatening hemorrhages, hepatectomies, β-catenin mutations or inflammatory adenomas, liver disease such as significant steatosis or vascular abnormalities, and age >30 years, serving as a valuable tool for guiding hepatic transplantation decisions in patients with hepatocellular adenomas.

Hepatic adenomatoses present as benign hepatic lesions with clinical features and the potential for serious consequences such as abrupt bleeding and malignant transformation. Recent advancements in genomic profiling have bolstered our comprehension of the genetic underpinnings of hepatocellular adenomas and its potential long-term effects.[14]

Hepatocellular adenoma, primarily associated with prolonged oral contraceptive use, is a benign condition. Surgical intervention becomes imperative in cases where a nodule biopsy or resection of nodules larger than 5 cm is feasible[15] to minimize complications like intraperitoneal hemorrhage or to address malignant transformation.[16] Hepatic transplantation may be necessary for patients ineligible for resection surgery due to the complexity of the disease and various considerations, necessitating an individualized treatment approach.[17]

Recently, changes in the diagnosis and treatment of hepatocellular adenoma have emerged, driven by novel insights and evolving experiences in managing this condition.

The diagnosis relies on MRI findings, which can distinguish between different subtypes, particularly steatosis and inflammatory HA. Biopsy should be reserved for patients undergoing non-resection therapy in cases of diagnostic uncertainty or to exclude a beta-catenin-mutated lesion. Adenomas in men and non-steatosis adenomas larger than 5 cm after 6–12 months of oral contraceptive withdrawal should be considered for excision. As our understanding of the molecular biology of these tumors advances, many contemporary approaches are likely to undergo significant changes.

CONCLUSIONS

This case highlights that hepatic transplantation is seldom recommended for benign liver tumors, given the risks of life-threatening complications and the potential for malignant transformation that cannot be ruled out in unresectable tumors. While various treatments such as radiological interventions and liver resection are available for benign hepatic tumors, hepatic transplantation emerges as a viable and safe treatment option, particularly considering the favorable long-term outcomes associated with it in cases of hepatic adenomatosis.
