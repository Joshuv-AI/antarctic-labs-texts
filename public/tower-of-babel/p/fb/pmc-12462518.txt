Bird mortality on high-speed railways: lessons from two large contrasting species
Authors: Cristina Mata, Juan E. Malo, Eladio L. García de la Morena, Israel Hervás, Ana E. Santamaría, Jesús Herranz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Collisions are the chief effect of transport infrastructures on vertebrate populations, but their relevance in high-speed railways (HSR) is largely unknown. We analyzed Great Bustard (Otis tarda) and Eurasian Eagle-owl (Bubo bubo) mortality along two 5-km stretches of a Spanish HSR. Five fresh bustards and 10 owl carcasses were collected during 40 weeks of monitoring over two years, plus older remains of 14 bustards and 17 owls more. Relevant mortalities of both bird species thus occur, and according to complementary results differences in local use of the infrastructure explain their spatial patterns. Bustards die at points where they fly across optimizing their local movements, while owls probably die while hunting the abundant rabbits present on railway verges. The case alerts about potential bird mortality due to HSR and exemplifies how impact prediction, evaluation, and mitigation must accommodate species’ characteristics to be successful.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-12012-8.

Introduction

European accords in line with the objectives of the ‘European Green Deal’, as well as others of a global character aimed at reducing CO2 emissions, have promoted railways as the most sustainable means of transport. This is based, in part, on calculations of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions per passenger and per kilometer travelled1–4. Hence, for example, the European Union intends to triple relative to 2015 its high-speed railway (HSR) network by 20501 and the United States is investing heavily in railway connections between cities less than 1,000 km apart as well as improving existing railway corridors5,6. There are likewise several projects of this nature in Asia and Africa linked to the Belt and Road Initiative promoted by China7,8. In fact, there are plans for 50,000 km of new HSR, 2.3 times their current extent4.

The drive for this form of apparently most sustainable transport has positive aspects that are fundamentally socioeconomic2,9. There are nonetheless negative consequences in the form of impacts on the landscape, in reducing ecosystem resilience through effects on bird populations affected by collisions with trains10 and from loss of ecological connectivity11.

Numerous bird species are subject to this additional mortality, reflecting their local abundance as well as behavioral aspects and the uses they make of habitats neighbouring the HSR12,13, as happens with other man-made structures like wind farms14,15, power lines16 or buildings17. This is worrying in the cases of small birds, often synanthropic species like starlings (Sturnus unicolor), magpies (Pica pica) or pigeons (Columba livia), that occupy the vicinity of the lines as well as for species of open agrarian landscapes. However, it is especially alarming when the species involved are of conservation interest and also of large size, which implies additional risk to the security and normal operation of the infrastructure in the event of collisions18. Such characteristics are shared by the two emblematic species considered here: the Great Bustard (Otis tarda) and the Eurasian Eagle-owl (Bubo bubo), very large birds known to suffer mortality by collisions with high-speed trains19.

The extensive distribution of both species in Spain20,21 includes large tracts crossed by both high-speed and conventional railways. Great Bustard presence is essentially determined by the extent of steppic or pseudosteppic habitats, such as herbaceous croplands or pastures, while the Eagle-owl is more generalist in habitat selection being dependent above all on prey availability.

The Great Bustard, the heaviest flying bird in Europe, weighing up to 18 kg and with a 2.6 m wingspan, is listed as ‘Vulnerable’ by the International Union for Conservation of Nature. It was originally a species of Eurasian steppes and has adapted to agricultural landscapes. It occurs in open, flat or somewhat rolling terrain, usually offering a mixture of crops (cereals, vineyards, fodder plants) and, in some areas, steppic grassland22. It has suffered an alarming decline globally over the past two decades23, chiefly due to agricultural intensification and collisions with powerlines24. The current Spanish population, the most important in the world, is estimated at 22,000–24,000 individuals, 15% less than previously estimated in 201020.

The Eurasian Eagle-owl is the largest nocturnal raptor in Europe, weighing up to 1.7 kg and with a 1.6 m wingspan. Its extremely large range includes a broad diversity of habitats, from montane and forest areas to arid and unvegetated zones, always provided sufficient prey is available. It is classified as of ‘Least Concern’ and although its current global population trend is decline, moderate increases are occurring in some areas of Europe including Spain. Most declines are due to human activity25. According to Pérez-García and Real21, the minimum Spanish population exceeds 3,000 pairs, with marked variation in occurrence and population density between different areas. The Spanish population is increasing, a trend associated with current increases in European Rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus) populations. In fact, where conditions are optimal in terms of trophic and nest-site availability, Spanish population densities are the highest so far recorded in Europe (≥ 35–40 pairs / 100 km2)25,26.

Our principal objective was to assess the extent and causes of the mortality that these two ecologically very distinct species suffer at HSRs. They were chosen because both are of conservation interest and, due to their size and weight, collisions with trains offer a potential risk to the operation of the infrastructure. The information obtained is indispensable for focusing measures to prevent or mitigate such impacts, thus reducing both species mortality and its potential cascading effects.

Results

Great Bustard mortality

Two HSR stretches, 5.59 km and 5.01 km long, were monitored intensively for 48 weeks over two years. In total, 19 bustards killed by trains were found, five of them (‘recent’) during sampling, along the study period (Figures S1 and S2). The others were killed either between survey periods or before the study began. It is significant that all mortality occurred along only one of the two stretches (Χ2 17.99; d.f. 1; p < 0.001). Within that stretch, collisions were concentrated at the western end, aggregated at scales between 170 and 200 m (Ripley’s L p < 0.05; Figs. 1 and 2 and S1). These locations were associated to fixed feeding and flight patterns, as reflected by models of habitat suitability and analysis of movement costs incurred by the species based on systematic sampling of the surrounding areas (Fig. 2). Collisions were actually at points where displacement costs were significantly lower (Student’s t-test − 2.94, d.f. 59, p < 0.005) than available elsewhere on the study stretches. It is worth pointing out that collisions occurred at all the embankment types present in that zone (Fig. 3A), although weakly, and not significantly, concentrated at the lowest ones (Χ2 4.24; 2 d.f.; p = 0.120). Recently killed bustards were found always in single events along both sampling periods (three and two carcasses respectively), and mainly in autumn-winter (four) rather than in spring-summer (one), but features are too low to draw robust conclusions.

Eurasian eagle-owl mortality

In total 27 owl casualties were recorded during the course of the study, at different locations across the study stretches (Figure S2). Ten of these were classed as ‘recent’ and the remainder were previous to the beginning of the study periods. For this species there were no significant mortality differences between the two stretches studied (Χ2 < 0.01 d.f.: 1; p = 0.960) nor was there any consistent pattern in the spatial distribution of casualties. No concentration of casualties was apparent along one of the stretches while along the other an aggregation at scales 280–360 m was detected due to four occasions where pairs of carcases were found close together. Owl kills were randomly distributed between all terrain types along the two stretches (Fig. 3B; Χ2 2.78; 6 d.f.; p = 0.835), although they were proportionally more frequent on the taller embankments. Of the four cases mentioned above, one was in level terrain, two on 5–10 m embankments and one on an embankment over 10 m high. Recent collisions were all encountered isolated, and they were chiefly recorded in winter (40%) and autumn (30%), and 80% of them occurred in 2018-19. However, since actual features are low, these (non-significant) tendencies should be treated with caution. Trail cameras located on railway verges indicated the widespread presence of rabbits, the potential prey species, which may explain why collision sites were not apparently clumped. In particular, 88% of 25 trail cameras installed in spring 2019 along the line (mean inter-camera distance being 163.0 m ± S.D.113.8 m) recorded rabbits during their first 24 h of operation and all of them did so within 48 h, showing the ubiquity of rabbits within the railway fencing throughout the study area.

The possible pervasiveness of the problem

Bearing in mind that the above results are from an intensive survey of 10.3 Km of HSR, our findings depict that this is potentially a widespread problem. To showcase its pervasiveness, we superimposed the current Spanish HSR network onto the two species’ current distributions. We found that habitats where Great Bustards were recorded are transversed by 434.4 km of HSR, which is a figure that increases to 1,202.5 km of HSR for Eagle-owls. Although these figures are not precise and it is not advisable to extrapolate from a 10-km scale to the railway network, their relevance lies in revealing the potential magnitude of this problem. The picture further broadens considering that these species, and others of very similar characteristics, occur worldwide: the bustard family Otididae has some ten species close in similar size to the Great Bustard in Africa, Asia, and Australia, whereas Bubo owls comprise 19 species distributed between the Americas, Africa, Europe, and Asia27. In the case of other raptors potentially attracted to railway verges in search of prey, the picture broadens attending to the detection of carcasses from Buteo buteo, Circus pygargus and Falco naumanni, even if in small numbers, during the same project19. Among Otididae, old remains from two Tetrax tetrax were also found at the site.

Discussion

Here we show that a HSR installation results in relevant mortalities of two large species of conservation interest. Moreover, the potential collision risk posed to trains by the Great Bustard because of its weight and flocking tendencies is high. And both cases highlight the wider problem of the casualties caused by high-capacity railway lines to birds overall, even if our study embraced only 10.3 km of line. Irrespective of the exact magnitude of the problem for the whole railway network, whose precise evaluation would require population studies on a local scale in several areas, the results obtained emphasize the need to develop preventive and mitigation approaches sensitive to the potentially affected avifauna, which may demand site-specific solutions in HSRs. In fact, our findings may exemplify the case for related bird species of similar size distributed across the world affected (now or in the future) by high-speed railways.

The collision problem arises from two well-differentiated situations. With respect to the bustards, the HSR line crossed an area of optimal habitat28, implying an increased collision risk and resultant inexorable increase in mortality. There was no Before-After Control-Impact (BACI) study that would have enabled the effects of installing the infrastructure on bustard behavior and population to be measured. Very probably, and as reported by Torres and her co-workers in the case of a motorway29, the noise and visual intrusion of the railway have induced bustards to move further away. This may have resulted in a negative trend for the local population, although general distributional and movement patterns, being strongly linked to habitat availability on a landscape scale, have persisted despite the new infrastructure. In contrast, collisions with owls seem linked to the increased prey availability, i.e. rabbits, associated with the line30. This has probably resulted in an increased use of the adjacent terrain by hunting owls, exposing them to colliding with trains and the catenaries. Collisions of owls with trains may be of greater concern in autumn and winter due to a higher proportion of circulations occurring between dusk and dawn.

With a view to reconciling the development of the transport infrastructure with conservation interests1, it would be necessary to eliminate or reduce these negative impacts as far as possible; taking steps that address the differences between the two cases.

Regarding the bustard, a species clearly suffering population decline, collision risk maps should be produced based on modelling habitat suitability and the costs of displacements between areas bisected by future railway lines. The present approximation has proved useful for predicting the most likely crossing, and hence collision, points. It may thus serve as an indispensable protocol for avoiding or reducing the negative impact of such infrastructures. In fact, a similar procedure focused on non-flying vertebrates has been proposed in a Spanish context for road and railway developments31 and this should be broadened to include birds in the light of the present findings. By this means, and using species data obtained locally32, the possibility of predicting spatial risk patterns would allow railway lines that are still in the design phase to be routed to avoid areas of great habitat suitability as well as those that link them. Planning new HSR lines could thus be improved, and the emergence of the collision problem avoided. Where a line crossing sensitive areas for a species already exists, it would be best to carry out targeted research similar to the one presented here, and to adopt mitigation measures to reduce the collision risk in case mortality is found. Such mitigation has been trialled in an EU project that employed an anti bird-strike barrier comprising individual poles that compel birds to fly higher33, whose effectiveness has been demonstrated on a stretch of line34,35.

On the contrary, owl collisions represent a spatially widespread problem, their use of the railway and its surroundings possibly being linked mainly to prey availability. This situation reflects a cascade effect: the infrastructure supplies new habitat that proves ideal for the prey species, whose population increases, attracting predators within their (usually large) home range areas36. Change in predator foraging habits as a functional response to prey increase is a classical observation in animal ecology37 and in the case of roads it has been given as a reason explaining carnivore roadkill locations38. This being so, any possible remedy is more complex and would involve managing the surroundings of the infrastructure. It is known that rabbit increases are associated on a regional scale with (i) the presence of major transport infrastructures, such as railway lines, and (ii) the coincidence of these with agricultural regions having little shrub or tree cover30. To address this question steps should be taken to reduce the abundance of prey species, such as rabbits, on verges by managing embankment vegetation and terrain, or sealing of burrows. Complementary nature-based solutions, involving favouring the populations of predatory mammals capable of regulating prey populations, may be employed, alongside taking steps to encourage the development of dense prey populations away from the infrastructure in these areas. This approach employs the well-established community ecology concept of achieving top-down population control in which predators limit prey species populations at levels much lower than their potential size, provided that conditions for the persistence of predator populations are met39. In this situation it would be important to evaluate to what extent mesopredators can withstand some mortality on railways, where the low frequency of trains and the strong vibration that they generate may facilitate evasive behaviors by mammals. These measures would achieve better environmental integration of projects, as well as significant savings in infrastructure maintenance costs currently dedicated to rabbit control.

Finally, the present results may be extrapolated to other species with extensive home ranges and low-altitude flight like the Great Bustard, even if they do not show any attraction to the railway infrastructure. In the same vein, other predatory species may be attracted the HSRs by dense prey populations in the vicinity of the lines. Thus, there is evidently a need during new railway planification and evaluation to (i) be aware of the presence, abundance and daily movement patterns of potentially vulnerable species; (ii) increase the ability to predict how local species will respond to the new infrastructure; and (iii) evaluate the actual effectiveness of proposed corrective measures, such as the installation of anti bird-strike barriers or the management of surrounding habitats. It is essentially a matter of improving the information supporting all environmental impact assessments from baseline data collection to impact prediction and mitigation design. The final aim should be to implement proposals that improve the compatibility between the operation of these infrastructures and conservation objectives40.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted along a railway line in an area of level or gently undulating paramo in northeast Toledo province at a mean altitude of 770 m. The climate is continental mediterranean, with a mean estimated temperature of 13.9 °C (mean of monthly minima: 7.4 °C; mean of monthly maxima: 20.5 °C) and mean annual precipitation of 437.8 mm41. The area traversed by the study stretches is open terrain where non-irrigated herbaceous crops (mainly cereals), olive groves and vineyards predominate (Figure S3). To the south of the route lies one of 10 tracts that comprises the ‘Steppe areas of northern La Mancha’ Special Protection Area (ZEPA ES0000170), which is especially important for the Great Bustard in Castilla-La Mancha since it holds 60% of the Regional population42.

All experimental protocols were done in two sections, 7.8 km apart, with a total of 10.6 km of the Madrid-Comunidad Valencia HSR (Fig. 2). The Western section is close to Villarrubia de Santiago (39°57′1,122′′ N–3°23′25,444′′ W–39°57′45,187′′ N − 3°19′37,749′′ W) and the Eastern one near Santa Cruz de la Zarza (39°57′11,275′′ N − 3°14′16,353′′W– 39°58′56,09′′ N − 3°10′57,842 ′′W). The line crosses the study area at different heights relative to the neighboring terrain, hence potentially having different effects on bird behavior and mortality. Thus, the railway sections were categorized into seven classes of rail level relative to the adjacent area: Deep Cutting (5–10 m below surroundings), Cutting (1–5 m below surroundings), Level (between − 1 and + 1 m), Embankment (1–5 m above surroundings), High Embankment (5–10 m above surroundings), Very High Embankment (> 10 m above surroundings), Bridge (an 110 m-long bridge over a regional railway), and Viaduct (a 296 m structure over a regional road not accessible for carcass removal, see below). The railway line was inaugurated in 2010 and during the study period was used daily between 06 and 23 h by approximately 45 trains, at speeds in the range 250–300 km/hour.

Sampling period

Sampling occurred each season across two annual cycles, summer 2014 to spring 2015 and summer 2018 to spring 2019.

Bird monitoring

Bustards in the HSR surroundings were surveyed twice during each of the 8 sampling seasons. Counting and geolocation of individuals while travelling by car along the tracks of the study area is the recommended method for far-ranging species such as this28. Sampling was conducted at low speed (10–15 km/h) along all drivable tracks within a 1 km-strip to either side of the railway line, stopping regularly for at least 2 min every 500 m approximately. The terrain was scanned at each stop by telescope (20x-60x) and binoculars (10 × 40). For each observation, the date, location and time, the number of individuals, and the direction, if any, of movement or flight were recorded to avoid duplications.

Censuses took place during the four hours after dawn and the two hours before sunset, with variation related to the current weather conditions, on dry days with good visibility. Days with hunting activity were also avoided.

Bird collision monitoring

Bird deaths from collisions with trains or railway infrastructure were monitored along the two survey stretches weekly for six weeks per season over the two annual cycles, 48 visits per stretch in total. Thus, for each sampling season we had five consecutive full weeks between the first and last visit that allowed the detection of recently killed animals, referred to as ‘recent’ along the paper in contraposition to carcasses coming from inter-season intervals or older remains. Surveying was conducted on foot along the path of the railway by a minimum of three observers accompanied by a safety inspector (Figure S3). Both sides of the line were checked, first in one direction and then the other, covering the whole area between the line and the enclosing fences.

Each carcass found was initially analyzed in situ, noting the species, the site characteristics, geographical coordinates and railway kilometer post. Most carcasses were left where they were found after being marked with fluorescent dye to avoid duplicate counting later. Whenever identification was in doubt the remains were taken to the laboratory for checking against reference collections. It was impossible to ascertain precise causes of death given that carcasses were found at different stages of decomposition and the fact that birds may be killed not just by collisions with trains but also by barotrauma or turbulence that causes them to hit the cables43. Nevertheless, all fresh carcasses showed evidence of collision (e.g. body fragmentation) or of having been flung aside, resulting in fractures or dislocated wings. None showed signs of electrocution.

Evaluation of potential bias in carcass counts

A parallel study was run between spring 2014 and winter 2015 to determine the persistence and detectability of bird carcasses on the railway, in order to correct general mortality44. Trail cameras were set up uniformly along both stretches, always having five in operation along each stretch, at a minimum separation of 500 m. The cameras were staked 20–30 cm above the ground and focused on a fresh bird carcass (commercial chick ≈ 35 g, quail ≈ 150 g, or pigeon ≈ 350 g) from about 1.5 m away, and they were removed when the carcass disappeared. Browning DarkOPS HD Pro BTC-6HD (18 Mp resolution) digital infrared cameras, activated by passive movement or PIR (Pyroelectric InfraRed) detectors, were used. A total of 141 time-to-disappear data were obtained (36 chickens, 56 quails, 49 pigeons) to build survival functions with the Kaplan-Meier approximation for each size class of birds45. These persistence functions were used to compute a multiplier factor (persistence correction factor) to be applied to the number of retrieved carcasses in weekly visits for extrapolation of the actual number of bird deaths of all bird species along the year46. As expected, the greater the carcass, the smaller persistence correction factor found: 1.998 (chick), 1.313 (quail) and 1.129 (pigeon). To analyze errors in carcass detection by researchers, carcasses of the same three species were laid on the railway platform and verges by an independent person in advance to each monitoring, and the encounter success evaluated after the monitoring session. Such carcasses (a total of 65 pigeons, 102 quails and 114 chickens throughout the year) were left on different types of verges and distances from the railway at positions unknown by searchers, and they were used to produce a second multiplier factor (detectability correction factor). Detectability correction factors again decreased with carcass size, being 2.000 for chicken, 1.594 for quail and 1.083 for pigeon. Of interest for the present study, the size of Great Bustard and Eurasian Eagle-owl (over 10 kg and 1.5 kg respectively) in comparison with carcasses used in the experiments supports the confidence on mortality estimations for both species based directly on carcass counts. Thus, detection errors by researchers are most probably negligible for bustards and owls (see Figure S2) accounting for the computed 8.3% underestimation of pigeons due to the carcasses passing unnoticed to researchers, and the probability of scavengers removing large-sized birds without leaving abundant fleece or feathers on the ground should be almost nil in face of the computed 12.9% underestimation of pigeons (see Figure S4).

Data analysis

Analysis of the spatial distribution of carcasses of the two species involved two consecutive approximations. To compare mortality between the two stretches Chi-squared tests were carried out of the hypothesis that deaths would occur as a function of stretch length, which would imply an almost equal distribution between both (c. 51.4% v. 48.6%). Smaller scale analysis of carcass spacing was performed separately for each species and stretch in which mortality occurred: both stretches for the owl and only the western stretch for the bustard. For this purpose, the observed values of Ripley’s L function for 10 m distance ranges were computed up to a maximum of 20% of the total length of each stretch and compared with the 0.025–0.975 probability envelope obtained by randomizations with 999 replicates. These second-order analyses were carried out with PASSAGE 2.047.

Model building

Only diurnal censuses were conducted and so the predictive model of collision risk was only constructed for the bustard. The model used distributional data from all the census samples, the habitats of the study area (Supplementary Table S1), the terrain topography and the particular characteristics of the railway line and its adjacent infrastructures.

A habitat suitability model (HSM) was first obtained using MaxEnt versión 3.4.148–50 in order to determine the zones of greatest occurrence probability (see more detail in supplementary material). The Corridor Designer extension in ArcGIS 1051 was then used to construct a model of the displacement cost to bustards across the line, displacement being defined as movement between two areas located 1 km to either side of the railway (cross-hatched areas in Fig. 2). In this model, the value of each pixel represents the lowest possible cumulative resistance of a path including that pixel and having one endpoint in each area. The starting hypothesis was that this map reflects priority zones for flights across the HSR and hence the locations of greatest collision probability would be those with the lowest cumulative resistance values.

In order to analyze whether actual collision locations corresponded with those predicted by the risk map, the median value of the model for an area of 25 m radius around such locations was calculated and compared with the median value of the model for controls spread along the entire stretch. For this purpose, the same procedure was used to extract model values around points 50 m apart along the railway line (control points). The averages of the median values of the model around collision locations and control points were compared by Student’s t test using Statistica software45.

Upscaling the problem

The kilometers of high-speed railway line that traverse 10-km squares in which the species have been recorded in the Spanish breeding bird atlas20,21, which shows the distribution range of the bird species in a Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) grid system of 10 × 10 km, were determined by overlapping the atlas maps with that of the actual HSR network, using ArcGIS Pro (Fig. 4).
