Applying the CHEST Guidelines for Management of Central Airway Obstruction
Authors: Udit Chaddha, Robert J Lentz, Peter H Lenz, Van K Holden, Ashutosh Sachdeva, Samira Shojaee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApplication of guidelines to the day-to-day clinical practice of clinicians is not always straightforward. Guidelines often provide a structure to the thought process demanded during clinical decision-making, which requires the application of evidence as best suited for each individual patient. These guidelines provide recommendations and suggestions, and the weight of these recommendations relies heavily on the quality of evidence and the vote of experts in the field, when evidence is lacking or insufficient. The following document elaborates on our approach to benign and malignant central airway obstruction, applying the principles of recently published central airway obstruction American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST) guidelines.Key Words: benign airway obstruction, central airway obstruction, malignant airway obstruction, tracheal obstruction

Clinical Vignette

A 32-year-old woman with a 5-year history of progressive dyspnea, previously diagnosed and managed as asthma, developed stridor. CT scan of the neck noted subglottic stenosis, which was diagnosed as idiopathic. She presented to the laryngology clinic at our center for a second opinion and due to the severity of her presenting symptoms was admitted for expedited direct laryngoscopy, endoscopic stenosis excision, and steroid injection. Her subglottic stenosis was found to be only moderate in severity, out of proportion to her symptom burden.

Postoperatively, she was transferred to the medical ICU for persistent severe dyspnea and tachypnea. Chest CT scan was performed, noting thin opacities in the bilateral mainstem bronchi. Given the clinical context, interventional pulmonology was consulted to evaluate for additional foci of central airway stenosis (Fig 1).

Multiplanar reconstruction of chest CT scan, coronal view, demonstrating 4 foci of airway stenosis or webs (red arrows).

During bronchoscopy, spiral-shaped webs were noted in the left and right mainstem bronchi, with the latter also extending into the right upper lobe bronchus. There was a slit-like orifice in the right main bronchus (Fig 2B) and only a pinpoint orifice (Fig 2D) in the left main bronchus. Mucosa overlying the distal trachea, main carina, and proximal mainstem bronchi was discolored and inflamed.

A-F, Composite of preintervention and postintervention bronchoscopic images. A, Extensive fibroinflammatory change over main carina and both proximal mainstem bronchi. B-C, Right main bronchus before and after intervention, respectively. D-F, Left main bronchus with pinpoint orifice before intervention (white arrow), during balloon dilation, and after intervention, respectively.

Question: Based on the aforementioned findings, which of the following would be the most appropriate first intervention at this time during bronchoscopy?

A.Tissue excision alone

Tissue excision alone

B.Airway dilation alone

Airway dilation alone

C.Airway stenting alone

Airway stenting alone

D.Tissue excision and airway dilation together

Tissue excision and airway dilation together

E.Tissue excision and airway dilation along with airway stenting

Tissue excision and airway dilation along with airway stenting

Answer: D. Tissue excision and airway dilation together

The left and right mainstem bronchi exhibited only pinpoint or slit-like residual lumens to introduce interventions. Recommendation 6 from the recently published American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST) guidelines mentions that tissue excision should be used for symptomatic airway obstruction in benign and malignant disease, and recommendation 7 says dilation can be performed alone or in combination with other therapeutic modalities. In this case, both excision and dilation need to be applied due to the anatomy; therefore, options A and B are incorrect. Excision of the right main bronchus web was successfully accomplished using an electrocautery knife, but dilation was also needed to maximally reestablish luminal patency. Airway stenting is not correct because the guidelines mention airway stenting should be considered when other modalities fail, especially for benign disease. Thus, options C and E are incorrect. The left main bronchus was recanalized using an electrocautery knife to make a small incision at the expected midpoint of the lumen to allow passage of a balloon across the stenosis, followed by balloon dilation. The right upper lobe was highly stenosed, and web excision was limited by the smaller bronchial lumen; thus, only 1 segment was recovered. The patient reported resolution of her dyspnea on being awakened from general anesthesia.

Subsequent evaluation revealed a positive antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody titer, and she was diagnosed with airway-limited granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA). Initially wary of recommended systemic therapies, she returned for bronchoscopy 6 months later after recrudescence of her dyspnea. The proximal left main bronchus was restenosed to a nadir of 4 mm, and inflammatory mucosal discoloration similar to the index procedure remained; however, the right main bronchus and subglottic areas were widely patent with no significant restenosis. She has since been initiated on biological systemic therapy.

Discussion

Benign central airway obstruction (BCAO) encompasses etiologies such as tracheal or mainstem (or bronchus intermedius) bronchial strictures from postintubation cuff-related stenosis, posttracheostomy stoma, suprastomal or substomal cuff-related stenosis, stenosis secondary to infectious or inflammatory etiologies including TB or GPA, post-lung transplant or other surgical anastomotic stenosis, and stenosis secondary to radiation therapy or chemical injury. BCAO can also occur from endoluminal growth or extrinsic compression by a benign tumor or adenopathy.

In patients with BCAO, although subjective evaluation of the airway lumen diameter is often inaccurate,1,2 objective evaluation with the use of dyspnea scales3 to determine the severity of narrowing may be performed. In general, symptoms do not occur until the airway stenotic index is > 70%, as extrapolated from pressure drops across a narrowing in computed fluid dynamic studies.4 Comprehensive physical examination must include evaluation of the upper airway anatomy to determine the feasibility for a rigid vs flexible bronchoscopic approach. Chest CT scan can help identify airway stenosis and its potential etiologies, and to aid in developing a plan for subsequent therapeutic bronchoscopy,5 but should not be a substitute for bronchoscopy due to its low sensitivity to identify endobronchial diseases.6

A key initial step during a diagnostic (or therapeutic) bronchoscopy for BCAO is to determine whether the stenosis is simple or complex. Stricture complexity is defined by the presence of airway cartilaginous involvement (ie, malacia) or long-segment involvement (> 1 cm) and is a predictor of recurrence or failure of endoscopic interventions.7,8 Simple stenoses often manifest as band- or web-like, short strictures without cartilaginous involvement, as is often seen in association with GPA. In such scenarios, an endoscopic intervention-first approach should be pursued to restore luminal patency. Endoscopic interventions usually encompass the use of radial cuts with an electrocautery knife, lasers, or endoscopic scissors, followed by balloon dilation using controlled radial-expansion balloons or a rigid bronchoscope. Local steroid injections and mitomycin C application have not shown a clear benefit in reducing recurrence frequency.9, 10, 11, 12 The patient in the vignette presented with multilevel simple stenoses from GPA; therefore, a cut and dilate endoscopic approach was pursued. Radial cuts are cautiously made to avoid collateral thermal injury to the adjacent airway wall or normal mucosa, which can cause scarring and potentially accelerate the recurrence. Patients should be followed closely to see whether the stenoses recur.

When possible, treatment and control of the underlying disease etiology is of utmost importance. However, if endoscopic interventions fail or the stenosis recurs soon, a definitive surgery or silicone stent placement must be considered. The threshold to operate or stent is best determined as a shared decision between the patient and the surgical team, including a multidisciplinary discussion involving interventional pulmonology, otolaryngology, thoracic surgery, and other specialties, such as rheumatology in the case of this patient. The treatment plan should factor not only the patient’s preferences, but also the feasibility of surgery and response to medical therapy, when one is available. Only when surgery is not feasible (surgically unresectable or medically inoperable) or when the risks of surgery are not acceptable to the patient should silicone stenting be considered. Bronchial strictures, seen after postinflammatory or infectious etiologies or with lung transplant anastomotic stenoses, are often inoperable. In these scenarios of recurrent simple or complex stenoses that have failed endoscopic intervention or when an endoscopic intervention is deemed unlikely to succeed, airway stenting is usually pursued. Occasionally, airway stenting may be cautiously considered as a bridge to surgery for patients who may not immediately be considered operable, but require a stent to reestablish airway patency to relieve symptoms.

Surgical resection of benign airway stenosis has been associated with an approximately 10% failure rate and up to 5% mortality13,14; however, existing evidence is dated and scant and may not represent the performance of modern surgical techniques. Anastomotic complications such as dehiscence, restenosis, granulation, or nonanastomotic complications (eg, bleeding, infection, glottic dysfunction) are not infrequently encountered. Airway stenting is also associated with a variably reported but high complication rate.15, 16, 17 Mucostasis (leading to stent mucus impaction and occlusion) and stent migration are the most reported complications associated with airway stents.16 In general, after stenting is pursued, the benefit of routine surveillance over symptom-driven bronchoscopies is yet to be determined.18,19 At the very least, patients should be closely followed up in an outpatient setting.16

The aforementioned management principles can be largely extrapolated to BCAO in patients with GPA. However, based on small studies, the postsurgical recurrence rates requiring an endoscopic intervention may be higher in this population.20,21 In inflammatory disorders, poor wound healing, the need for immunosuppressive medications, and disease-related inflammation usually cause more anastomotic complications. This should translate into a higher threshold to operate in BCAO with ongoing, active inflammation as opposed to mechanical or postinflammatory BCAO such as posttracheostomy or postintubation stenoses. In general, any definitive intervention must be pursued during the fibrotic phase of the disease and not when active inflammation is present, barring critical stenoses in symptomatic patients.

Clinical Vignette

A 58-year-old woman with a 19 pack-year smoking history presented with 1 week of productive cough and fatigue. Symptoms were associated with night sweats, progressively worsening orthopnea, and wheezing over the past few months. She also noted a raised bruise on her anterior chest. Her mother had passed from lung cancer at 66 years of age. Physical examination was notable for audible expiratory wheezing. Chest CT scan showed a 2-cm spiculated right middle lobe nodule, bulky subcarinal lymphadenopathy with critical airway narrowing, anterior chest wall heterogeneous soft tissue lesion, and a radiolucent lesion in the right anterior fourth rib (Fig 3).

A, B, CT chest scan. A, Coronal image demonstrates narrowing of the bilateral mainstem bronchi and bronchus intermedius (red arrows). B, Axial images at the level of the main carina, mainstem bronchi, and bronchus intermedius with narrowing.

Question: Based on these findings, bronchoscopy is pursued. Which of the following sets of interventions would give this patient the highest chance of avoiding complications?

A.Flexible bronchoscopy with moderate sedation

Flexible bronchoscopy with moderate sedation

B.Flexible bronchoscopy with general anesthesia

Flexible bronchoscopy with general anesthesia

C.Rigid bronchoscopy with moderate sedation

Rigid bronchoscopy with moderate sedation

D.Rigid bronchoscopy with general anesthesia

Rigid bronchoscopy with general anesthesia

Answer: D. Rigid bronchoscopy with general anesthesia

When expertise is available, rigid bronchoscopy is usually preferred for central airway obstruction management because it allows better control of the airway, enabling the introduction of multiple instruments through the rigid barrel which allows safer and quicker debulking of endobronchial lesions and management of any consequent bleeding. Moreover, silicone stenting, which has the advantages of on-site customization of length and cutting holes to avoid jailed airways, Y stenting with on-site limb length customization to minimize plastic in the airway, and easier removability (after the patient receives disease-directed therapies), can only be performed via rigid bronchoscopy. Thus, when expertise in rigid bronchoscopy is available, in this clinical context, options A and B are incorrect. General anesthesia establishes a quieter operative field, and for therapeutic bronchoscopies, its use has been associated with fewer complications than moderate sedation. Thus, option C is incorrect and option D is correct.

The patient underwent a rigid bronchoscopy under general anesthesia for therapeutic interventions. Bronchoscopic examination identified extrinsic compression of the left mainstem bronchus with approximately 75% narrowing and bronchus intermedius with approximately 90% narrowing. Balloon dilation to 10 mm was performed. Thereafter, a 10- × 20-mm fully covered self-expanding metallic stent was deployed in the bronchus intermedius under direct bronchoscopic visualization. A second 10- × 40-mm fully covered self-expanding metallic stent was similarly deployed in the left mainstem bronchus with success (Fig 4).

A-F, Composite of preintervention and postintervention bronchoscopic images. A, Narrowing of the bronchus intermedius. B, Closer look of the obstruction revealing approximately 90% obstruction. C, A fully covered self-expanding metal stent (SEMS) in the bronchus intermedius (BI), without obstruction of the right upper lobe (RUL) ostium. D, Left mainstem obstruction, intrinsic and extrinsic, requiring balloon dilation (E) and subsequent placement of SEMS resulting in improved luminal patency (F).

The procedure successfully alleviated her dyspnea symptoms; however, the cough persisted. She was started on a mucociliary clearance regimen.

Ultrasound-guided core biopsy of the anterior chest mass revealed metastatic lung adenocarcinoma with a KRAS mutation. Due to the airway compromise, she received 20 Gy over 5 fractions of radiation to the left and right mainstem bronchi tumor and was started on pembrolizumab, pemetrexed, and carboplatin. Unfortunately, she missed her scheduled appointment for surveillance bronchoscopy 4 weeks later and came in at 2 months after stent placement. There was improvement in the airway caliber, and the bronchus intermedius stent was removed. The left mainstem stent was revised to a 12- × 40-mm fully covered self-expanding metallic stent. She had rapid disease progression 2.5 months later and elected to pursue only comfort care.

Discussion

Malignant central airway obstruction (MCAO) is defined as narrowing of the central airways (trachea, mainstem bronchi, or bronchus intermedius) from a neoplastic process. Although MCAO can develop in 13% of patients with primary lung cancers,22 the exact incidence of MCAO from other cancers metastatic to the central airways remain unknown.

MCAO can result from an endoluminal (ie, intrinsic), extraluminal (ie, extrinsic), or mixed-morphology etiology. The type of obstruction dictates the therapeutic approach to restore airway patency. For endoluminal or mixed-morphology MCAO with a predominantly intrinsic component, tumor debulking will usually suffice. Tumor debulking can be performed using thermal (laser or contact or noncontact electrocautery) or cold (ie, cryorecanalization) ablative modalities or using a coring action with the beveled tip of the rigid bronchoscope and rigid graspers. There are no studies comparing different debulking modalities, and providers usually resort to a multimodal approach.23 In mixed-morphology tumors wherein after debulking there remains significant (> 50%)4,16,17 residual stenosis from extrinsic compression, airway stenting can be pursued. In addition, when endoluminal tumor is frequently recurrent, or the tumor is expected to have slow or no response to medical and radiation therapy, airway stenting may be considered. For MCAO of extrinsic morphology, airway stenting remains the only feasible bronchoscopic therapeutic intervention. However, despite > 3 decades of experience with airway stenting, there are currently no published adequately powered, comparative randomized trials addressing this topic. The only randomized trial that compared silicone stenting with no stenting was prematurely terminated after enrolling 78 patients, but demonstrated greater dyspnea improvement in the stenting group.17 Consequently, practices vary,24 and management is based largely on expert consensus.15,16 When feasible, rigid bronchoscopy under general anesthesia is preferred over flexible bronchoscopy, as previously detailed.25

When palliating a patient with MCAO, it is vital to suspect that the patient has attributable symptoms (eg, dyspnea, hemoptysis) or signs (eg, postobstructive pneumonia) from the MCAO before pursuing a therapeutic intervention. The overall assessment must involve a chest CT scan to not only evaluate the anatomy of the stenosis, but also the viability of the downstream lung parenchyma. It is important not to mistake technical success (ie, successfully recanalizing of the obstructed airway, as assessed during bronchoscopy or with cross-sectional imaging) for clinical success (ie, improving the patient’s symptoms, survival, or quality of life).23 In general, patients who are more dyspneic and have a poorer functional status may benefit more from a therapeutic intervention, likely as a larger demonstrable change can be achieved.23 Moreover, in patients with respiratory failure from MCAO necessitating positive pressure ventilation, airway recanalization and/or stenting can lead to prompt liberation from ventilatory support.26 MCAO is typically associated with a higher hazard ratio of death,22 and with good patient selection, survival is likely better when the MCAO is successfully intervened on,27 with survival curves matching those in patients without MCAO.28

In the aforementioned reference case, severe, flow-limiting4 narrowing of the bronchus intermedius and the left main bronchus was noted from extrinsic MCAO. This necessitated airway stenting to restore airway luminal patency. The choice of a covered metallic stent was operator and airway dependent. Stent selection is usually based on airway-related factors and the feasibility and availability of expertise in rigid bronchoscopy. In general, either covered metallic or silicone stents can be used for MCAO because there is no conclusive evidence that complications are different with either stent material.16 With advancements in antineoplastic therapies and expected disease (and consequently, MCAO) response, silicone stents when feasible, may be preferred due to their greater ease of removability.29,30 Tracheal stenting was not necessary in this case, and when feasible Y stents should be avoided; although they expectedly migrate less, they may be associated with higher risk of mucus plugging.
