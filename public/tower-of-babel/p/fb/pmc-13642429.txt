Clinical management of vestibular schwannoma – what’s the evidence?
Authors: Egil Brudvik, Morten Lund-Johansen, Øystein Vesterli Tveiten, Terje Sundstrøm, Frederik Goplen, Dhanushan Dhayalan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical management of vestibular schwannomas (VS) encompasses three main strategies: observation (“wait-and-scan”), stereotactic radiosurgery, and microsurgery. Despite extensive literature, a consensus on the optimal modality is lacking due to the scarcity of high-level evidence.MethodsThis narrative review explores the current understanding of the most significant clinical and radiographic outcomes following wait-and-scan, radiosurgery, and microsurgery. This is not a systematic review of the literature.ResultsObservational studies indicate that approximately one-third of tumors demonstrate growth within 3 years, and approximately 40% of patients eventually require active treatment. Radiosurgery achieves high long-term control, with radiological control exceeding 90% and clinical control above 95% at 10 years. However, hearing preservation is variable and time-dependent. Microsurgery can achieve gross total tumor removal and high long-term tumor control, with reported recurrence rates below 2% following gross total resection. However, recurrence estimates vary according to follow-up methodology and definitions of recurrence. Risks of hearing deterioration and facial nerve impairment increase with tumor size and extent of resection. Comparative studies, largely nonrandomized, suggest that radiosurgery offers better hearing and facial nerve outcomes than microsurgery in small- to medium-sized tumors. Quality-of-life assessments show little difference among treatment modalities. The V-REX trial remains the only randomized study to date, showing that upfront radiosurgery reduces tumor volume but does not yield superior clinical outcomes compared with observation.ConclusionThe current evidence is dominated by retrospective case series with heterogeneous methodologies. Prospective, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are critically needed to guide superior treatment strategies and enhance patient care.Keywords: Vestibular schwannoma, Review, Evidence, Tumor control, Growth, Hearing

Introduction

Contemporary treatment strategies for vestibular schwannomas (VS) include an observational wait-and-scan approach, stereotactic radiosurgery, and microsurgery. No consensus exists regarding the superiority of any of these treatment modalities. Although the outcomes of each of the three strategies are extensively reported in the literature, the vast body is case series receiving single treatment presented by the caring treatment center. Comparative studies are few and mainly retrospective and non-randomized. As of now, the V-REX trial remains the sole randomized trial comparing treatment modalities in vestibular schwannomas [30].

This narrative review explores the existing knowledge concerning the most important clinical and radiographic outcomes following wait-and-scan, radiosurgery, and microsurgery. This is not a systematic review of the literature. Outcomes of particular interest include tumor growth rates (radiological control), risk of active treatment (clinical control), and hearing preservation. Facial nerve function and patient quality of life (QoL) are also commonly reported in the literature and reviewed herein.

Observation (“WAIT-AND-SCAN”)

Historically, vestibular schwannomas were recognized as tumors that would inevitably grow, and surgical removal was considered mandatory irrespective of tumor size. Conservative treatment gained popularity during the 1980 s in cases where surgery was considered too risky. The first report on the conservative management of VS was published in 1985 in a cohort of elderly patients [117]. In the following decades, numerous studies have documented that a substantial portion of vestibular schwannomas does not grow. Consequently, a “wait-and-scan” approach has evolved as an alternative to active treatment. The Mayo Clinic has estimated that by 2026, half of all VS patients in the US will be managed initially with wait-and-scan [14]. The issue of scan intervals was addressed in a 2022 Delphi Consensus. The suggested scanning protocol begins 6 months after diagnosis, transitioning to annual scans for 3 years, followed by two biennial scans, and concluding with assessments at 3- and 5-year intervals [115].

Radiological tumor control

The future growth rate of a VS is unpredictable. The tumor may grow, stay stable, or even demonstrate shrinkage. By all accounts, no one is born with a VS; thus, the tumor has grown at some point. The evidence for the natural history of VS growth is comprehensive. Unfortunately, there is an inconsistency in the reported growth rates, with growth risk ranging from 12 to 69% [74, 102].

Table 1 lists selected single-center reports with large sample sizes, extended follow-up, and, preferably, volumetric measurements [5, 56, 74, 85, 102, 111, 133]. Two historically significant, highly cited papers are also included [105, 126]. The 2021 study from Copenhagen and the 2022 study from the Mayo Clinic, with 2312 and 952 patients, respectively, differ significantly in growth rate, most likely because volumetric measurements are more sensitive. In a literature review by Caye-Thomassen et al. with 53 studies and 6000 patients, 33% of conservatively managed VS demonstrated growth within 3.3 years [21]. This is similar to a systematic review by Paldor et al. in which 33% showed growth within three years and 50% within five years [91]. However, these data should be interpreted with caution, as there is a discrepancy in the definition of growth, and most reports used linear measurements rather than volumetric measurements. Furthermore, follow-up is limited, and many series rely on observational data from available imaging rather than systematic MRI of all patients. In addition, definitions of recurrence vary with extent of resection, as residual tumor growth is often analyzed separately or excluded.

If tumor growth occurs, it usually occurs within the first five years of observation following diagnosis [119]. Thus, many centers, including ours, prolong the interval between scans after five years of observation without growth. However, a recent long-term study by Macielak et al. showed that 8% (14 of 172) of tumors that underwent growth experienced growth after five years of observation [78]. The authors advocate lifelong surveillance to prevent uncontrolled growth.

Spontaneous shrinkage has been considered rare. In the Danish database, only 4% of 1261 cases showed spontaneous shrinkage as measured by linear measurements [54]. However, in a recent volumetric multi-center study, 12% of 952 patients experienced a minimum 20% decrease in tumor volume during observation [81]. The potential regressive capacity remains uncertain, further complicating the understanding of vestibular schwannoma growth dynamics.

To date, no baseline parameters have consistently predicted future growth. Generally, symptom progression is not strongly correlated with tumor growth. Recently published data from the Danish National Database, the Mayo Clinic, and a 2022 multicenter report from the U.S. suggest that the most consistent predictor of future growth is tumor size at diagnosis [74, 82, 102]. However, a systematic review by paldor et al. did not find tumor size to predict growth [91]. According to the authors, only proven tumor growth in the first follow−up year could predict future tumor growth. A volumetric study by van de langenberg et al. supports this [130]. Emerging evidence also suggests that the inflammatory microenvironment plays a crucial role in growth. recently, an in vivo biomarker of inflammation, the neutrophil−to−lymphocyte blood ratio, was proposed as a predictor of tumor growth [47, 70]. Furthermore, two investigations have hinted at a potential association between patient−reported unsteadiness and linear tumor growth [59, 124]. Our group demonstrated that objective unsteadiness on posturography at the time of diagnosis was associated with volumetric tumor growth in conservatively managed patients [90].

There is an increased interest in biomarkers that reflect the underlying biology of vestibular schwannoma growth. Advanced MRI approaches, such as radiomics and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging, together with blood-based markers, may offer added value for risk stratification beyond conventional parameters [75]. Recent work highlights the potential of combining artificial intelligence with dynamic MRI to predict vestibular schwannoma growth. Parameters from DCE imaging show strong predictive value and may reflect underlying tumor biology. Moreover, radiomic features outperform traditional first-order statistics when it comes to tumor growth prediction, while maintaining interpretability [26].

Clinical tumor control

The risk of active treatment (= “treatment failure”/“lost clinical tumor control”) is well studied in conservatively managed patients. The percentage of cases requiring active treatment ranges from 6 to 74% (Table 2) [11, 28, 46, 56, 74, 100, 102, 105, 122]. The variation is most likely explained by the substantial differences in indication for treatment among the centers. Two extensive systematic reviews by Yamakami et al. (n = 903) and Yoshimoto et al. (n = 1340) indicate a risk of active treatment of 20% (mean follow-up 3.1 years), and 18% (mean follow-up, 3.2 years), respectively [139, 142].

If tumor growth is definitively confirmed, guidelines recommend treatment with either radiosurgery or microsurgery [41]. For volumetric studies, > 20% volume expansion is widely accepted as the threshold for significant growth [73, 74, 82, 111, 125, 130]. However, in everyday clinical practice, volume acquisition is time-consuming, and linear measurement is still the standard method.

A 2021 report from Marinelli et al. challenges the dogma that detected tumor growth is equivalent to an indication for active treatment [80]. They studied a selected population of 592 patients from the US and Denmark who denied active treatment despite documentation of significant tumor growth. Half of the tumors that had initially demonstrated growth had stopped growing on subsequent MRI at three years.

Hearing outcomes

The natural course of hearing acuity is well-studied at several centers. Generally, the long-term hearing prognosis is disappointing. Our group found that only 23% of conservatively managed patients had serviceable hearing (AAO-HNS Class A or B) following 7.7 years of observation, regardless of baseline hearing acuity [128]. In patients with serviceable hearing (SH) at diagnosis, 40% retained SH. In Table 3, our data is compared to other selected reports [55, 66, 92, 120, 121, 128]. A recent systematic review from 26 studies and more than 3600 conservatively managed patients with SH at baseline shows a consistent pattern of hearing loss: 75% retain hearing at 3 years, 60% at 5 years, and 40% at 10 years [65].

Several studies have attempted to predict the risk of hearing loss. In a study by van Linge et al. intracanalicular growth was associated with hearing decline, while extracanalicular growth was not [131]. Sughrue et al. found that patients with slow-growing tumors had superior hearing preservation rates than those with faster-growing tumors [121]. Paradoxically, they also found that patients with preserved hearing had larger initial tumor size, indicating that hearing loss is more closely associated with tumor growth rate than tumor size. In recent years, several reports have indicated a relationship between labyrinthine and cochlear hypointensity on cisternographic T2-weighted MRI and hearing loss in patients with vestibular schwannoma during observation [86, 130]. This association is still debated and has recently been refuted in a 2023 report [67].

Radiosurgery

Radiation therapy emerged as an alternative to surgery for vestibular schwannoma in the 1980 s and 1990s. Radiosurgery aims to halt tumor growth and is typically reserved for tumors with an extracanalicular diameter of less than 3 cm, as larger tumors increase the risk of radiation-induced complications [32]. Long-term tumor control rates exceed 95%, with slightly lower rates for larger tumors [3, 33, 68, 77, 97, 140].

Radiological tumor control

The Pittsburgh group has granted the literature with several comprehensive reports on the long-term clinical and radiological outcomes of radiosurgery since the early 1990 s [23, 33–37, 61, 68, 69, 77]. In a recent retrospective review of 871 patients who underwent Gamma Knife radiosurgery as their initial treatment, radiographic tumor control was 97% at 3 years, 95% at 5 years, and 94% at 10 years [61]. Probably, the second most comprehensive data on radiosurgical outcomes comes from Hasegawa et al. [49–53]. They found similar compelling long-term radiological tumor control among their 440 VS patients (including NF2), with 93% after 5 years and 92% after 10 years [51]. There is also evidence of a tumor volume-reducing effect of radiosurgery. While only 4 to 12% of tumors demonstrate shrinkage during observation, a prospective volumetric study from our group found 71% of patients to experience tumor shrinkage following radiosurgery [54, 81, 134].

Clinical tumor control

In the Pittsburgh report on 871 patients who underwent radiosurgery, clinical tumor control was achieved in 98.7% (median follow-up 5.2 years, range 1–25 years) [61]. Hasegawa et al. found similar clinical tumor control rates, and no patients experienced treatment failure more than 10 years after radiosurgery [51]. A prospective study from our group found a 5-year clinical tumor control of 94% [134]. There are no reliable predictors for the risk of salvage treatment following radiosurgery. Tumor growth prior to radiosurgery has been proposed as a predictor, but a recent paper from Chang et al. refuted this potential association [22].

Hearing loss

Whether radiosurgery protects or aggravates hearing loss remains uncertain. A systematic review by Yang et al. (74 articles, 5825 patients) found an overall hearing preservation rate of 57% in patients with serviceable hearing prior to radiosurgery (mean 3.4 years following radiosurgery) [141]. Another systematic review by Coughlin et al. (47 articles, 2195 patients) found a hearing preservation rate of 58% (mean 3.9 years following radiosurgery) [27]. The Congress of Neurological Surgeons Systematic Review and Evidence-Based Guidelines estimates the probability of preserving serviceable hearing to > 75% after 2 years, 50–75% after 5 years, and 25–50% after 10 years [20]. A Delphi study among a multidisciplinary panel of experts codified 88 to 94% consensus on these estimates for hearing preservation [16]. The level of hearing at the time of treatment is the best predictor of hearing preservation rates following radiosurgery [61]. The Pittsburgh Hearing Prediction Score (PHPS), which assigns a total of 5 points based on patient age, tumor volume and hearing class, found a hearing preservation rate at 92% at 10 years in patients whose score total was 1, and 0% in patients whose PHPS was 5 [61]. The maximum dose at the modiolus of the cochlea has also been suggested as a negative predictor for hearing preservation with a threshold around 4 Gy. However, definite evidence is lacking [58, 62]. The Mayo Clinic recommends that routine tumor dose planning should not be modified to limit cochlear dose at the expense of tumor control [58].

Microsurgery

Surgery remains the preferred modality for patients with large tumors or those with symptoms attributable to brain stem compression, hydrocephalus, or other mass effect [20, 41]. The early attempts at surgical resection of vestibular schwannoma were associated with high perioperative mortality and poor cranial nerve outcomes. Along with improvements in surgical techniques and anesthetics, surgical management of vestibular schwannoma has shifted from focusing on peri- and postoperative mortality to preserving facial nerve function and, potentially, hearing.

Radiological tumor control

The risk of postoperative growth of the residual tumor is chiefly determined by the volume left behind (extent of resection), while the surgical approach (retrosigmoid, middle fossa, or translabyrinthine) does not affect radiological tumor control [1, 41]. Recurrence following gross total resection (GTR) is considered rare. In the 1997 series by Samii and Matthies, recurrence occurred in 0.7% (6 out of 880) of non-NF2 patients following GTR [109]. A more recent paper by Ahmad and Sanna found the recurrence rate in 2400 surgically treated cases to be 0.05% for the translabyrinthine approach, 0.7% for the retrosigmoid approach, and 1.8% for the middle cranial fossa approach [1]. In a 2017 retrospective multicenter study conducted at Tokyo Hospital and the Mayo Clinic, the estimated recurrence-free survival after GTR was 96%, 82%, 73%, and 56% at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years, respectively [89]. In a review of more than 5000 surgically managed patients where 96% underwent GTR, the recurrence rate was 1.8% [139]. Overall, radiological tumor control following GTR exceeds 95%. However, reported recurrence rates should be interpreted with caution because long-term MRI surveillance is often incomplete, patients may be lost to follow-up, and definitions of recurrence and extent of resection vary across studies.

Progression of residuals of near-total (NTR) and subtotal resection (STR) is considerably more frequent. This was best demonstrated by Seol et al. in 2006. In a series of 116 patients, they found GTR, NTR, and STR to yield recurrence rates of 3.8, 9.4, and 27.6%, respectively [114]. The mean time to recurrence was 1.8 years (range 0.5 to 11.9 years). In a 2016 Mayo Clinic report on 103 patients treated with STR and NTR, 14% recurred at a median of 3.4 years. Patients who underwent STR were over 13 times more likely to recur compared with those treated with NTR [57].

For large vestibular schwannomas, a novel multimodality approach with initial microsurgical subtotal resection and subsequent adjuvant radiosurgery is gaining popularity. This approach achieves high rates of tumor control while optimizing preservation of facial and cochlear function. Notably, the timing of SRS does not appear to influence these outcomes [29, 127].

Clinical tumor growth

The risk of salvage treatment following surgery is strongly correlated with the extent of resection; achieving gross total resection is the single most important parameter for predicting the likelihood of secondary treatment [1, 109]. In the case of recurrence or residual growth following microsurgical resection, stereotactic radiosurgery is recommended as salvage treatment [41].

Hearing outcomes

Hearing outcomes following surgery are directly proportional to preoperative tumor size, the single most important criterion for assessing the likelihood of hearing preservation. While 40 to 70% with small tumors (< 1.5 cm) retain serviceable hearing, only 5% do in tumors that are more than 2.5 cm [4, 15, 20, 98, 108, 138]. In the 1997 series by Samii and Matthies, functional cochlear nerve preservation was achieved in 39.5% of patients with preoperative hearing [108], while a 2006 update from the group reported an improvement to 51% hearing preservation [107]. In a recent systematic review of 2034 patients treated with retrosigmoid resection, hearing preservation rates were approximately 33% [98].

In recent years, some centers have opted for surgical resection of small tumors with a hearing-preserving intention, preferably with the middle fossa approach [38, 99, 104]. Advances in cranial nerve and auditory brainstem neuromonitoring facilitate this strategy [118]. A 2025 retrospective report on 230 consecutive patients with small vestibular schwannomas treated with microsurgery demonstrated that hearing was preserved in 61% of patients [60].

Facial nerve outcomes

Profound facial nerve paresis is highly unusual following both radiosurgery and a wait-and-scan strategy. Hasegawa et al. found the actuarial 10-year facial nerve preservation rate to be 100% in patients treated with a marginal dose < 13 Gy [51, 52]. However, in surgical management, facial nerve function and tumor control are the two primary benchmarks. To some degree, the two goals are competing, as total tumor removal places the facial nerve at greater risk. Risk of postoperative facial nerve paralysis following GTR ranges from 20 to 50% [45]. In comparison, NTR and STR ± adjuvant/salvage radiosurgery offers less than 10% risk of facial nerve injury [48]. Preoperative tumor size remains the most consistent baseline predictor of facial nerve function [31, 45]. Furthermore, ventral growth in the internal auditory canal increases the risk of facial nerve injury, even after controlling for tumor size [44, 137].

The influence of surgical approach on facial nerve function is controversial. A systematic review by Gurgel et al. found no significant differences between approaches. In contrast, another systematic review by Ansari et al. found the retrosigmoid approach superior to the translabyrinthine approach for tumors > 3.0 cm in diameter [4, 45].

Quality of life

Tumor control, hearing acuity, and facial nerve function have traditionally been the benchmark parameters in evaluating outcomes of vestibular schwannoma treatment. However, during the 2010 s, patient-reported outcomes gained increasing focus, and the escalation in the number of comparative studies on QoL is a testimony to this shift. Vertigo and dizziness appear to be the primary drivers of long-term reductions in QoL among patients with VS, irrespective of treatment status [18, 76, 88]. Several nonrandomized, nonblinded studies, including one from our group, have evaluated the three treatment modalities concerning QoL [12, 13, 18, 19, 83, 103, 110]. Altogether, they have shown surprisingly little difference among treatment modalities. The initial 6 months after diagnosis is probably the worst period in terms of QoL; a new diagnosis of brain tumor is an anxiety-provoking situation [17]. Microsurgery may be slightly advantageous with regard to patient anxiety. This is presumably due to the underappreciated psychological benefit of having removed the “brain tumor,” whereas radiosurgery is non-curative [13]. However, based on the overall data from these studies, treatment modality, in large, does not seem to confer improved QoL.

Comparative Studies

The heterogeneity in tumor and patient characteristics complicates the execution of well-designed trials on vestibular schwannoma management. Prior to the V-REX trial, there were no Class I Evidence (randomized trials). All other studies are purely observational, in most cases retrospective, and are therefore subject to selection bias. Single-center studies with small sample sizes and short follow-up predominate, and there is an unfortunate inconsistency in endpoint definitions; some studies evaluate radiographic outcomes, while others prioritize clinical outcomes such as risk of salvage treatment, hearing acuity, facial nerve function, and QoL. Moreover, most comparative studies have evaluated only two of the three modalities. This section reviews previously conducted comparative studies on treatment modalities for vestibular schwannoma, with Evidence Class II (prospective non-randomized) [41].

Radiosurgery versus Wait-and-Scan

Two prospective, nonrandomized, nonblinded studies have compared radiosurgery with wait-and-scan as the initial treatment strategy: one from Marseille and one from our group [10, 100]. Regis et al. compared wait-and-scan (n = 47) and upfront radiosurgery (n = 34) in small intracanalicular vestibular schwannomas [100]. The primary endpoint was treatment failure, defined as either significant growth or hearing deterioration requiring treatment. Treatment failure was evident in 74% of the wait-and-scan group and 3% in the radiosurgery group. The authors found that radiosurgery reduced the risk of both tumor growth and hearing deterioration and recommended proactive radiosurgery for all patients with a newly diagnosed VS and useful hearing. Our prospective study from 2013 compared wait-and-scan (n = 124) and upfront radiosurgery (n = 113) in VS with extracanalicular growth [10, 100]. Radiosurgery significantly reduced the risk of tumor growth and new treatment. However, there were no differences in symptoms or quality-of-life outcomes, including hearing loss.

The V-REX study is, to date, the only randomized trial on the management of vestibular schwannoma. Among 98 patients with newly diagnosed small- and medium-sized vestibular schwannoma, upfront radiosurgery demonstrated a significantly greater tumor volume reduction at 4 years than a wait-and-scan approach. However, there were no differences between the groups in clinical outcomes. Hearing acuity declined in both groups, regardless of treatment strategy. In the upfront radiosurgery group, 18 (53%) of 34 participants with serviceable hearing at baseline had non-serviceable hearing at the 4-year follow-up. In the wait-and-scan group, the corresponding numbers were 22 (54%) of 41 participants. The T-REX trial, a 10-year follow-up study, is ongoing.

Another recent, unique study is the VISAS trial, a retrospective, international, multicenter investigation comparing radiosurgery with wait-and-scan [7–9]. They found no significant difference between the groups in terms of preservation of serviceable hearing. However, radiosurgery was associated with a lower risk of tinnitus, vestibular dysfunction, and cranial nerve palsy.

Despite concerns raised by case reports of malignant transformation following radiosurgery for vestibular schwannoma, robust evidence does not support a clinically meaningful risk [112, 116]. Large cohort studies demonstrate an exceedingly low incidence, comparable to that of primary CNS tumors in the general population, with no increased risk observed in long-term prospective data [106, 136]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the risk of radiation-induced malignancy is negligible and likely comparable to, or lower than, the risk of serious complications associated with microsurgical resection [95].

Microsurgery versus radiosurgery

Six nonrandomized, nonblinded prospective studies, including one from our group, have evaluated radiosurgery to microsurgery in small and medium-sized vestibular schwannomas (Table 4) [43, 63, 87, 94, 96, 101, 123, 132]. The collected evidence is somewhat in favor of radiosurgery above microsurgery in terms of preserving hearing and facial nerve function, while there were mostly no differences in tumor control rates. A 2012 meta-analysis and a 2016 matched cohort comparison support these findings [42, 79].

Single-session versus fractionated/hypofractionated radiotherapy

Several non-randomized studies have compared single-session stereotactic radiosurgery with mainly LINAC-based fractionated/hypofractionated radiotherapy [2, 3, 24, 25, 64, 71, 84, 93]. Altogether, they show similar tumor control and hearing preservation rates. If any difference, fractionated radiotherapy seems to be associated with increased risk of facial palsy and trigeminal nerve dysfunction, most likely explained by the larger fall-off doses in LINAC-based systems compared to the Gamma Knife.

Discussion

The management of vestibular schwannoma presents a complex landscape with innumerable treatment pathways. The fundamental debate centers on the choice between wait-and-scan, radiosurgery, and microsurgery.

To justify invasive tumor-targeted treatment, the outcomes should be superior to those resulting from the natural history of the disease. Thus, a profound understanding of the natural course is paramount in managing vestibular schwannomas. The adoption of a wait-and-scan strategy in recent decades has enabled us to study the natural course of VS. However, there is an issue concerning selection bias. Conservatively managed patients harbor small and usually slow-growing tumors with mild symptoms, and elderly patients with significant comorbidities are overrepresented. Large tumors in young patients are often treated upfront. Therefore, knowledge gained entirely from studies of conservatively managed patients must be interpreted with caution and cannot be regarded as a direct reflection of the natural course of VS.

Randomized trials are the gold standard for determining whether an intervention is superior, equivalent, or inferior to an alternative [39]. Unfortunately, RCTs are rare in the field of neurosurgery [6]. In a 2005 paper, Gnanalingham et al. found that fewer than 1% of published papers in the leading neurosurgical journals are randomized trials [40]. In the European Association of Neuro-Oncology 2020 guidelines for the management of vestibular schwannoma, there were no Class I Evidence or Level A Recommendations, meaning no randomized controlled trials were available [41]. The vast majority of the existing literature on the clinical management of vestibular schwannomas is purely observational and, as a result, is at high risk of selection, provider, and publication bias. Because of inherent biases, caution is required when comparing results from different treatment modalities across publications. This limitation is particularly relevant when interpreting recurrence rates after microsurgery, as many surgical series have incomplete long-term MRI surveillance, loss to follow-up, and heterogeneous definitions of recurrence, residual tumor, and extent of resection.

In clinical practice, linear measurement is the current standard for measuring tumor size and determining growth. However, as the proportion of patients managed conservatively with wait-and-scan increases, there is a need for accurate measurements of tumor size to guide and predict future active tumor-targeted treatment [113, 129]. Beyond that, there is an even greater demand for accurate tumor size measurements in research regarding VS treatment outcomes. A recurring frustration when reviewing VS literature is the variability in methods for measuring tumor size and in definitions of growth. The adoption of precise volumetric analysis in VS research in recent years is therefore welcome [24, 72, 74, 111, 113, 125, 129, 135].

Conclusion

Treatment strategies for vestibular schwannomas remain a controversial topic. The existing literature provides mostly low-level evidence and few comparative studies. In this narrative review, we have provided an overview of the existing evidence and call for more high-level evidence. We encourage more prospective, randomized controlled trials.
