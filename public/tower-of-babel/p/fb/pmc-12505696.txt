Epidemiological trend in diabetic kidney disease (DKD) burden in China from 1990 to 2021, and projections for 2041: an analysis of the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Hui Li, Jing Wang, Tingting Zhang, Pengwei Lou, Lijuan Xu, Li Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a leading cause of renal failure and mortality in diabetic patients. This study aims to describe and assess the burden of diabetic kidney disease in China, based on the global burden of disease (GBD) 2021 data and to provide a crucial scientific foundation for the prevention and control of this life-threatening disease.Methods This study utilizes data from the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study database. During the study, we systematically assessed the disease burden characteristics of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in the Chinese population through comprehensive analysis of key epidemiological indicators including prevalence, incidence, mortality, years of life lost (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). We also used Joinpoint decomposition analysis and age-period-cohort (APC) analysis methods to interpret DKD epidemiological characteristics. Furthermore, we extended the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model to predict the disease burden of DKD in 2041.ResultsAge-standardized rates in terms of prevalence (ASPR), incidence (ASIR), mortality (ASMR), DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs of DKD-T1DM in 2021 were 28.32 cases (95% CI:23.95,33.23) per 100,000, 0.7 new cases (95% CI:0.38,1.14) per 100,000, 1.06 deaths (95% CI:0.79,1.38) per 100,000, 47.95 DALYs (95% CI: 36.9,60.73) per 100,000, 4.55 YLDs (95% CI:3,6.25) per 100,000, and 43.4 YLLs (95% CI:33.05,55.97) per 100,000. Age-standardized rates in terms of prevalence (ASPR), incidence (ASIR), mortality (ASMR), DALYs, YLDs, and YLLs of DKD-T2DM in 2021 were 1053.92 cases (95% CI: 971.11, 1139.64) per 100,000, 16.29 new cases (95% CI: 14.92,17.53) per 100,000, 5.64 deaths (95% CI: 4.46,7) per 100,000, 122.15 DALYs (95% CI: 99.62,146.99) per 100,000, 23.3 YLDs (95% CI: 16.29,30.54) per 100,000, and 98.85 YLLs (95% CI:78.16,123.45) per 100,000. The average annual percentage change (AAPC) in age-standardized prevalence, incidence and mortality rates for T1DM-DKD in China were 0.79 (95% CI:0.75,0.84), -0.62 (95% CI:-0.66,-0.59), -1.72 (95% CI:-1.91,-1.53) and for T2DM-DKD they were − 0.46 (95% CI:-0.49,-0.42), 0.22 (95% CI:0.20,0.26),-0.6417 (95% CI:-0.82,-0.47). The effects of age, period, and cohort on incidence and mortality rates differed.The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of DKD-T1DM is projected to decline from 1.06(95% CI: 0.79–1.38) per 100,000 in 2021 to 0.60(95% CI: 0.32–0.88) per 100,000 in 2041. In contrast, while the ASMR of DKD-T2DM is expected to decrease from 5.64(95%CI: 4.46-7.00) in 2021 to 4.84(95% CI: 3.66–6.02) per 100,000 in 2041.The trend of ASIR in DKD-T1DM is predicted to decrease slightly, but in DKD-T2DM will show an obvious upward trend, it will increase from 16.29(95% CI: 14.92–17.53) per 100,000 in 2021 to 24.14(95% CI: 14.78–33.50) per 100,000 in 2041.ConclusionsThe burden of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in China exhibits a dual trajectory: declining mortality in type 1 diabetes-related DKD (DKD-T1DM), alongside rising incidence of type 2 diabetes-related DKD (DKD-T2DM). This epidemiological divergence is associated with China’s vast population base, demographic aging, and evolving risk profiles, though projections require cautious interpretation given ARIMA model limitations in accounting for unforeseen healthcare disruptions or policy shifts. Sustainable mitigation necessitates precision prevention strategies, enhanced primary healthcare services, and targeted interventions to address urban-rural diagnostic disparities, particularly underestimated burdens in resource-limited rural settings, alongside multidisciplinary coordination to address this growing public health challenge.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24674-0.

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is one of the most severe chronic microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus (DM). Currently, DKD has surpassed glomerulonephritis to become the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD), accounting for 27.0% of all CKD etiologies, and is the primary driver of renal failure and mortality in diabetic patients [1].According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), approximately 537 million people worldwide had diabetes in 2021, with 30%−40% progressing to DKD, which constitutes over 50% of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) cases [2]. Diabetes-related chronic kidney disease has become a leading cause of end-stage renal disease in China [3].The rising prevalence of DKD, due to its significant negative impact on quality of life and the high costs associated with its management, will lead to a substantial increase in disease burden, posing a critical public health issue requiring immediate attention.

In the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study of 2021 (GBD 2021), researchers present the most recent data and research techniques, with estimates updated throughout the time series [3]. The GBD 2021 results presently supersede those of prior GBD rounds. Based on the most recent GBD 2021 data, we present long-term trends in the burden of DKD in China over a period of more than 30 years, including prevalence, incidence, mortality, years of life lived with disability (YLDs), years of life lost (YLLs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). While DKD prevalence in China and prediction models are well-documented [4], few studies have systematically decomposed drivers (e.g., age structure vs. epidemiological changes) or projected future burdens—limitations our study addresses using Joinpoint regression, decomposition analysis, and ARIMA modeling.By leveraging GBD 2021 data (the most recent global dataset) and advanced methodologies, we offer updated, actionable evidence to inform DKD prevention strategies in China, where the disease burden is increasingly shaped by unique demographic and epidemiological transitions.

The prevalence of DKD is influenced not only by the degree of glycemic control [5], but also by demographic factors such as age, population aging, and gender [6–8]. In the future, with the growing number of diabetic patients and extended life expectancy, the prevalence of DKD is likely to rise further. Additionally, advancements in medical technology have impacted the quality and accessibility of DKD healthcare. These time-related factors often interact synergistically, making it challenging to quantify the independent contribution of individual factors in epidemiological analyses.The Age-Period-Cohort model can monitor for interactions between age, period, and cohort, allowing the trend of prevalence and mortality to be more clearly reflected [9]. The Joinpoint regression model detects trend inflection points (joinpoints) and quantifies the Annual Percent Change (APC) across distinct time periods [10].Decomposition analysis enables the disentanglement of multidimensional drivers within health data from the GBD database. The ARIMA model has shown unique predictive power in the GBD database, especially for long-term trend modeling and dynamic prediction of disease burden indicators [10]. In this study, we used the most recent Global Burden of Disease (GBD 2021) data to characterize the temporal trends of DKD prevalence and mortality in China between 1990 and 2021, to evaluate their age, period and cohort effects.Using the ARIMA model, projections were developed to forecast DKD prevalence, incidence and mortality trends through 2041, providing evidence-based insights for sustainable disease control strategies.

Materials and methods

Overview

The GBD 2021, which provides the most up-to-date estimates for 369 diseases, injuries, and impairments, as well as 88 risk factors, across 204 countries and territories grouped into 21 regions [11, 12], which are further aggregated into 7 super regions based on epidemiological similarity and geographic proximity. These super regions provide a standardized framework for comparing disease burden patterns across macro-geographic scales. Which includes estimations of numerous different models for disease and injury outcomes [13].

In this study, the data on diabetic kidney disease (DKD) burden in China were extracted from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021) database. According to the GBD 2021 classification system, China is categorized within the “East Asia” super region. The East Asia super region, as defined by GBD 2021, includes several countries and territories, with China being a key component. This regional classification helps in comparing the epidemiological trends of DKD in China with other economies and populations in the same geographic and socio-economic context, providing a reference for understanding the uniqueness and commonality of China’s DKD burden. By specifying that our analysis focuses on China within the East Asia super region, we aim to better situate our findings in the global landscape of disease burden. This allows readers to recognize both the specific characteristics of DKD in China and its position relative to other regions with similar developmental and demographic patterns.

The global burden of disease website (https://www.healthdata.org/) was used to collect data on DKD burdens in China between 1990 and 2021.We analyzed the trends in incidence, prevalence, mortality rate, and Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) rates of DKD in China.To ensure methodological transparency, our analysis strictly adhered to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) [14]. All utilized data represent de-identified, population-level aggregates from the GBD repository, thus exempting this study from institutional ethics review per the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki principles on secondary data analysis.The GBD 2021 standard population was used consistently throughout our analysis to calculate age-standardized prevalence (ASPR), incidence (ASIR), mortality (ASMR), and disability - adjusted life years (DALYs) rates. However, the use of the GBD 2021 standard population in our study ensures consistency with the broader GBD framework, facilitating international and temporal comparisons.

The 95% CIs and the uncertainty intervals for all epidemiological indicators (prevalence, incidence, mortality, DALYs, YLDs, YLLs) in this study are derived from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021) modeling framework. GBD 2021 calculates CIs using a Bayesian meta-regression framework, which integrates data from multiple sources (including cohort studies, cross-sectional surveys, and disease registries) and accounts for various sources of uncertainty, such as sampling error, measurement error, and model uncertainty. This approach generates 95% uncertainty intervals that represent the range within which the true value is Likely to fall with 95% probability.The GBD uses a Bayesian meta-regression approach (DisMod-MR 2.1) to calculate these uncertainty intervals, which accounts for multiple sources of uncertainty inherent in disease burden estimation.Including data variability, model uncertainty and parameter uncertainty.For the ARIMA model projections (2022–2041), uncertainty intervals were calculated based on the residual variance of the model, capturing the potential variability in future trends due to unforeseen changes in demographic, epidemiological, or healthcare factors. This ensures that the projected estimates account for both the model’s predictive power and the inherent uncertainty of long-term forecasting.

GBD modeling framework

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 modeling framework, which underpins our analysis, integrates multiple data sources and statistical methods to estimate disease burden metrics (prevalence, incidence, mortality, DALYs) across geographies and time. GBD integrates all available data (surveys, registries, administrative records, published studies, cohort data) across countries/years. Sources include WHO, UNICEF, academic literature, and government reports.Estimates are aggregated at the national level for China.A Bayesian meta-regression tool (DisMod-MR 2.1) is used to synthesize data, addressing gaps through statistical extrapolation and adjusting for confounding factors Like age, sex, and time trends. This model quantifies uncertainty, generating 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported in our study.This framework ensures transparency and reproducibility, as all methods and data sources are publicly documented by the GBD Collaborators. By relying on this established approach, our study benefits from the rigor of global standardized methodologies, enhancing the reliability of our conclusions on DKD burden trends in China.

The GBD 2021 data integrate multi-source information via a Bayesian meta-regression model (DisMod-MR 2.1), which involves statistical extrapolation and assumptions (e.g., data distribution, missing value imputation) that may introduce uncertainties. Although the DKD data for China used in this study underwent standardized processing, sparse surveillance data in remote regions could lead to estimation biases. Thus, national-level trends should be interpreted with caution.

Disease definition

In the GBD 2021 classification system, diabetic kidney disease is categorized at the fourth level, falling under chronic kidney disease (level 3), diabetes mellitus and kidney disease (level 2), and non-communicable diseases (level 1). Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) can be diagnosed clinically by either persistent albuminuria or sustained reduction in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) for at least three months in patients with diabetes [15].

Join-point regression model

The Joinpoint regression model is a collection of linear statistical models that were used to evaluate the trends in disease burdens attributable to DKD across time [16, 17]. Based on the temporal characteristics of the disease distribution, this model builds a piecewise regression and performs trend fitting and optimization on the data points in each segment.Joinpoint (version 5.3.0.0; National Cancer Institute, Rockville, MD, USA) was used to create this model. Model results can be summarized using the metrics of annual percentage change (APC) and average annual % change (AAPC).

Age-period-cohort model

The age-period-cohort (APC) model [18], a statistical method widely used in recent decades in economics, sociology, and epidemiology, can quantitatively estimate the efficacy of different age, period, and birth cohort groups for DKD by separating the effects of these three dimensions and by analyzing confounding factors among the time variables. Age effect refers to the risk of outcomes at different ages, period effect refers to the effect of temporal changes on outcomes across all age groups, and cohort effect refers to the changes in outcomes among participants with the same birth cohort.The APC model may enhance the understanding of DKD epidemiology, aiding in identifying potential gaps in various aspects of disease prevention, control, and treatment strategies [19].The APC model is a linear model, represented as follows: In (Refg)=α + Ae + Pf + Cg, where Refg denotes the incidence or mortality rate of PF in g birth cohorts, e refers to age groups, and f represents periods, where Ae, Pf, and Cg represent the effects of age, period, and cohort, respectively [20].

Decomposition analysis

Decomposition analysis is a robust epidemiological methodology that systematically partitions temporal changes in population health metrics – such as disease incidence, mortality, or disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) – into distinct contributions from age structure shifts, population dynamics, and epidemiological transitions to disease burden [21, 22]. By isolating the proportional impacts of these drivers, it enables precise attribution of disease burden trends to underlying structural determinants. Methodologically aligned with causal inference frameworks, decomposition analysis provides actionable insights for prioritizing interventions that address the most consequential drivers of disease burden, thereby bridging empirical epidemiology and evidence-based health governance.

Autoregressive integrated moving average model

The autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model consists of the autoregressive (AR) model and moving average (MA) model, which are combined to form the ARIMA model. The underlying assumption is that data series are time-dependent random variables, whose autocorrelation can be characterized by the ARIMA model, and future values can be predicted based on past values [23, 24]. The ARIMA model necessitates that the time series be a stochastic sequence with a mean of zero and must demonstrate stationarity. We employed R version 4.4.2 for all statistical analyses and graphical representations.

ARIMA projection methodology and uncertainty quantification

Projections for 2022–2041 were generated using ARIMA models optimized through automated parameter selection via the Hyndman-Khandakar algorithm in auto.arima(), minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Model validation included Ljung-Box testing for white noise residuals (all p > 0.05) supplemented by Q-Q plots and autocorrelation function (ACF)/partial autocorrelation function (PACF) analysis. The ARIMA model assumes stationarity of the time series and the continuity of historical trends (e.g., no major policy reforms or unforeseen public health events). However, real-world factors such as abrupt demographic shifts or breakthroughs in medical technology may disrupt these trends.

Between 1990 and 2021, age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) for DKD-T1DM and DKD-T2DM in China declined: DKD-T1DM ASMR decreased from 1.8 (95% CI: 1.42, 2.25) to 1.06 (95% CI: 0.79, 1.38) per 100,000, while DKD-T2DM ASMR fell from 6.83 (95% CI: 5.74, 8.34) to 5.64 (95% CI: 4.46, 7) per 100,000. The age-standardized DALY of DKD-T1DM in China decreased from 80.92 (95% CI: (65.12,98.39) per 100,000 people in 1990 to 47.95 (95% CI: 36.9,60.73) per 100,000 people in 2021. The age-standardized DALY of DKD-T2DM in China decreased from 155.94(95% CI: (131.16,188.26) per 100,000 people in 1990 to 122.15 (95% CI: 99.62,146.99) per 100,000 people in 2021 (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Table 2). When compared to global data, China’s DKD burden has changed significantly.

Conclusion

This study examines the trends in the burden of DKD, which include T1DM-DKD and T2DM-DKD, in China over the last 30 years. It also predicts the prevalence, incidence and mortality trend for the next 20 years. To our knowledge, this is the first analysis of the epidemiological trends of DKD in China using Joinpoint analysis combined with the APC model and decomposition analysis, also using the ARIMA model for prediction of DKD epidemiological trends in the next twenty years. Through systematic analysis, we have arrived at the following conclusions.

Overall trends and differences in disease burden

In 2021, the number of new cases of DKD-T1DM in China (6,321 cases) was significantly lower than that of DKD-T2DM (354,157 cases). However, the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of DKD-T1DM (1.06 per 100,000) was notably higher than that of DKD-T2DM (5.64 per 100,000). This paradox May stem from the longer disease duration and faster progression of complications in type 1 diabetes (T1DM) patients, while the high incidence of DKD-T2DM is directly Linked to the widespread prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and population aging [25, 26]. Notably, the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) of DKD-T2DM (1,053.92 per 100,000) far exceeded that of DKD-T1DM (28.32 per 100,000), underscoring its substantial public health burden. Between 1990 and 2021, there was a decline in age-standardized mortality rates (DKD-T1DM: 40.9% decline; DKD-T2DM: 17.4% decline). The significant decline in DALYs of both DKD-T1DM and DKD-T2DM is associated with concurrent trends in optimized patient management, early intervention, pharmacological advancements [27–35], and expanding healthcare access [36].Future efforts should prioritize targeted interventions for high-risk populations (such as elderly and rural communities) to achieve sustained reductions in disease burden.

Sex and age heterogeneity

In 2021, males with DKD-T1DM exhibited significantly higher incidence, mortality, and DALYs compared to females. This is potentially due to genetic factors, hormone levels, Male smoking, Lifestyle, immune response differences and other factors.Although genetic factors play a role in the development of type 1 diabetes, specific to diabetic nephropathy, there may be sex-related genetic differences [37]. Studies have shown that at some gene loci associated with diabetic nephropathy, there are differences in gene expression or polymorphisms between males and females, which may affect the kidney’s susceptibility to diabetic damage [38]. Estrogen takes part in kidney repair and regeneration via its receptors. Estrogen also participates in the regulation of phosphorus homeostasis via its receptors in the proximal tubule [39].Differences in lifestyle between men and women may also affect the mortality rate of diabetic kidney disease. For example, men are more inclined to have unhealthy lifestyle habits such as smoking, drinking alcohol, consuming a high-salt and high-fat diet, and lacking physical exercise [40]. Finally, there is innate immunity in diabetic kidney disease [41] and there are certain differences in the immune system between men and women, which may affect the development of diabetic nephropathy.In contrast, no significant sex differences were observed in DKD-T2DM. This May be related to the complex pathogenic factors, similar disease courses, as well as to the active treatment and comprehensive Management for type 2 diabetes [42].

In 2021,DKD-T1DM prevalence peaked in the 45–59 age group, rising rapidly among individuals aged 20–54 (Fig. 1A). DKD-T2DM was most prevalent in the 45–79 age group, with a sharp increase between the ages of 60–79.The prevalence peak of DKD-T2DM is later than DKD-T1DM, which may be related to the differences in diagnosis age and disease course and differences in pathogenesis.T1DM typically manifests with acute onset during adolescence or young adulthood. At diagnosis, beta-cell function is already severely impaired, and the earlier, more concentrated exposure to hyperglycemia accelerates the progression of microvascular complications, such as nephropathy [43, 44]. In contrast, T2DM predominantly affects middle-aged and older populations [45]. By the time of diagnosis, patients may have had undetected insulin resistance and chronic hyperglycemia for years. During this period, the body develops adaptive and compensatory mechanisms, resulting in relatively insidious renal injury and slower progression of kidney disease. Notably, the incidence of DKD-T2DM surged anomalously among Males aged 85–94. The elevated incidence of type 2 diabetic nephropathy in elderly men is likely attributable to a confluence of factors encompassing physiological attributes, lifestyle patterns [46], and disease management practices. Androgen may affect glycometabolism through multiple pathways [47]. Meanwhile, with the increase of age, there is a progressive deterioration in the structure and function of the kidneys [48]. Dietary habits are associated with the risk of developing diabetic nephropathy; many elderly men tend to have dietary patterns characterized by high salt, fat, and sugar intake. Such eating habits not only exacerbate insulin resistance but also significantly raise blood glucose levels [49]. Additionally, elderly men generally have insufficient physical activity. The lack of physical movement reduces energy expenditure, severely impairing effective blood glucose control and thus substantially increasing the risk of developing diabetic kidney disease [50, 51]. Moreover, poor treatment adherence and the presence of multiple comorbidities also contribute to the rise in the incidence of diabetic kidney disease [52].

Temporal dynamics: key inflection points and driving factors

Joinpoint regression analysis revealed

DKD-T1DM

There was a significant rise in prevalence from 2000 to 2005 (females: APC = 4.19, males: APC = 4.34), likely linked to expanded diabetes screening and some cognitive and practical development.Conversely, there was a Marked decline in incidence from 2015 to 2018 (females: APC=−3.49, males: APC=−2.20), which may reflect the success of early intervention strategies.For example, with the widespread application of the Continuous Glucose Monitoring System (CGMS) [53–55], the continuous emergence of new hypoglycemic drugs [56–59], and the more refined insulin treatment regimens [60, 61], blood glucose control level of patients has been improved. In addition, the blood pressure management has become more effective. The medical system has been continuously improved, enabling patients to undergo regular screening for diabetic nephropathy. Meanwhile, the standardized follow-up system can ensure that doctors can promptly understand the changes in patients’ conditions and adjust treatment plans.

DKD-T2DM

Prevalence surged from 2010 to 2015 (APC = 2.18), and it is considered that this is related to changes in population factors and other factors during the same period.The rise in DKD-T2DM incidence coincides with the growing prevalence of type 2 diabetes in China, suggesting a potential association between these trends [62]. Additionally, the escalating rates of overweight/obesity among Chinese adults, causing intensified insulin resistance and lipid metabolism disorders [63]., promoted renal hemodynamic abnormalities [64, 65]. These factors collectively accelerated the prevalence of T2DM. A sharp increase in incidence in Males from 2019 to 2021 (APC = 1.33) may be attributed to disrupted chronic disease management owing to epidemiological factors [66, 67].

Decomposition analysis further quantified driving factors

The decline in DKD-T1DM incidence was primarily driven by aging (89.50% contribution) and epidemiological changes (57.57%), while population growth had a negative effect (−47.07%), indicating that prevention measures offset demographic expansion. The rise in DKD-T2DM incidence was predominantly fueled by aging (75.04%) and population growth (19.22%), with minimal contribution from epidemiological changes (5.74%), suggesting inadequate control of risk factors under current strategies.

Deep impact of age-period-cohort effects

Age effects analysis of the effects of period and cohort showed that DKD-T1DM mortality peaked at 40–55 years, while DKD-T2DM mortality rose continuously with age. This is similar to the trend in chronic kidney disease(CKD) mortality in China, which increases with age [23].This trend may be related to the increase in the course of diabetes and the occurrence of complications with age.

Period effects Analysis of the effects of age and cohort showed that mortality for both DKD-T1DM and DKD-T2DM declined steadily, reflecting medical advancements. However, rising DKD-T1DM incidence may relate to improved diagnostic sensitivity.

Cohort effects Analysis of the effects of age and period showed that DKD-T1DM incidence peaked in the 1997–2002 birth cohort, possibly Linked to childhood environmental factors, such as viral infection, which is considered an important environmental triggering factor for type 1 diabetes, especially enteroviruses [68].DKD-T2DM incidence was highest in the 1962–1977 cohort, correlating with lifestyle shifts during China’s economic reforms.

Epidemiological trends in DKD in China over the next 20 years

Based on ARIMA model predictions, the epidemiological trends in DKD-T1DM and DKD-T2DM in China over the next 20 years exhibit significant differences.The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of DKD-T1DM is projected to decline from 1.06 per 100,000 in 2021 to 0.60 per 100,000 in 2041, with minimal fluctuations in age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) and a gradual decrease in age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), reflecting improvements in glycemic control and early interventions.However, the global DKD-T1DM data projections suggest that the prevalence will showing a smooth increase from 2020 to 2030 and surpass 7.5 million cases worldwide by 2030 [69]. In contrast, while the ASMR of DKD-T2DM is expected to decrease (from 5.64 in 2021 to 4.84 per 100,000 in 2041), its incidence will rise sharply (from 16.29 in 2021 to 24.14 per 100,000 in 2041), highlighting the dual pressures of population aging. Gender disparities reveal a more pronounced increase in ASIR among males with DKD-T2DM, potentially linked to metabolic risks and lower healthcare adherence.

Although model robustness was validated through the Ljung-Box test, confirming that the residuals of the model were white noise, its predictions still have some limitations. Limitations include the exclusion of socioeconomic factors and unexpected public health events. Future efforts should integrate multi-source data and machine learning to refine predictive frameworks and support precision prevention strategies.ARIMA projections do not account for unforeseen factors (e.g., long-term impacts of pandemics on chronic disease management). Therefore, they should be dynamically adjusted using real-time surveillance data and not relied upon as the sole basis for decision-making.

The significance to public health of this study

Addressing the dual trajectory of DKD-T1DM and DKD-T2DM

Our findings reveal a divergent trend: declining mortality in DKD-T1DM and rising incidence in DKD-T2DM. This necessitates differentiated strategies: For DKD-T1DM: Sustain advancements in glycemic control and early nephropathy screening, as these have likely contributed to reduced mortality. Policy support for affordable access to these technologies, particularly in rural areas, will help maintain this trend. For DKD-T2DM: Prioritize primary prevention, given its strong association with population aging and lifestyle factors. This includes scaling up community-based interventions to reduce obesity, promote physical activity, and improve dietary habits—key drivers of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) progression to DKD.

Targeting high-risk populations

Our analysis identifies specific high-risk groups that require focused intervention, namely the elderly populations and males with T2DM.DKD-T2DM prevalence peaks in the 45–79 age group, with a sharp increase between 60 and 79 years. We recommend integrating DKD screening into routine geriatric care and simplifying medication regimens to improve adherence in this group.Males exhibit a steeper rise in DKD-T2DM incidence and higher mortality in DKD-T1DM. Tailored health education (e.g.,addressing gender-specific barriers to care, such as lower healthcare-seeking behavior) and targeted lifestyle counseling (e.g., reducing smoking and alcohol consumption) could mitigate these disparities.

Strengthening healthcare systems and policy support

To translate emerging trends into tangible impact, systemic reforms are essential. First, Primary care empowerment.Given the role of early intervention in reducing DKD burden (e.g., declining DKD-T1DM incidence post-2015 likely linked to improved screening), we advocate for training primary care providers in DKD risk assessment and management.Second, Policy incentives.Expanding health insurance coverage for DKD-related tests (e.g., albuminuria screening) and medications (e.g., renoprotective agents) can reduce financial barriers. Additionally, recent years have seen rapid technological advancements, with the Internet of Things (IoT) integration in healthcare revolutionizing chronic disease management [70]. IoT devices, such as wearables and smart glucometers, enable real-time tracking of glycemic levels, blood pressure, and renal biomarkers in diabetic patients, enhancing early detection of DKD progression and personalized interventions. For example, they alert clinicians to abnormal albuminuria or eGFR trends for timely treatment adjustments. These innovations help bridge urban-rural healthcare disparities, though data security, interoperability, and equitable implementation challenges need to be addressed to maximize their potential in mitigating the DKD burden.

Anticipating future burdens

ARIMA projections indicate that without intervention, DKD-T2DM incidence will continue to rise through 2041. This underscores the urgency of long-term planning. such as, investing in research on DKD pathogenesis in aging populations to develop targeted therapies.Integrating DKD metrics into non-communicable disease (NCD) surveillance systems to monitor progress and adjust strategies dynamically.

These actionable insights, grounded in our epidemiological findings, aim to guide policymakers and healthcare providers in prioritizing resources and designing contextually relevant strategies to reduce DKD burden in China. Therefore, a comprehensive strategy that includes risk factor prevention at the primary care level, DKD screening among the elderly and high-risk people, and access to high-quality medical services, is necessary to reduce the DKD burden and achieve better health outcomes for DKD patients. In the future, precise prevention and control, optimization of medical system and collaborative response of multidisciplinary research should be adopted to achieve sustainable control of disease burden.

Limitations of the study

Uncertainties in modeled GBD data

The GBD 2021 data used rely on statistical modeling to address gaps in primary data, incorporating assumptions about distribution, missing values, and geographic/temporal extrapolation. While standardized to enhance comparability, this modeling introduces inherent uncertainty [71], where estimates represent statistical inferences from limited primary data rather than direct observations. For instance, urban-rural disparities in healthcare access may result in underreporting of DKD cases in rural areas, potentially leading the model to underestimate the actual burden. Consequently, population-level findings cannot be directly equated to individual-level risks.

This study uses aggregate data, which is susceptible to ecological fallacy. For example, the finding that ‘males have higher mortality rates in DKD-T1DM’ cannot be directly extrapolated to conclude ‘individual males face higher risk,’ as individual-level variables (e.g., smoking status, glycemic control) are not controlled for. Future cohort studies are needed to validate the drivers of gender disparities.

Lack of China-specific primary survey data

Notably, the GBD 2021 data used in this study are aggregate and lack granular information on individual-level clinical factors and behavioral determinants specific to the Chinese population. This limits our ability to definitively attribute trends to particular drivers. Such as, distinguishing whether declining mortality reflects improved glucose management, wider use of renoprotective drugs, or other unmeasured factors. Additionally, while we note associations with demographic shifts or healthcare system changes, we cannot disentangle the independent effects of these factors without data on policy implementation details, regional healthcare disparities, or individual patient behaviors. Future studies integrating GBD data with national clinical registries or population-based surveys would help clarify these mechanisms.

Urban-rural bias in burden estimates

Stark urban-rural disparities in healthcare access and reporting may skew GBD-derived estimates. Urban areas benefit from better diagnostic capacity and comprehensive records, ensuring more complete case identification. In contrast, rural regions face shortages of specialists and limited diagnostics, likely leading to underreporting, especially for early-stage DKD. While GBD models adjust for such disparities using global patterns, these may not fully account for China’s unique urban-rural divide, potentially underestimating rural burden and overrepresenting urban accuracy in national trends.

These limitations warrant caution in interpreting findings, particularly for subpopulations or regions with sparse data. Future studies combining GBD estimates with China-specific primary data could mitigate these constraints.

Future research directions

Geographic and ethnic disparities in DKD burden

Our analysis focused on national-level trends, but China’s vast regional and ethnic diversity warrants more granular investigations. Future studies could utilize provincial or prefectural data to examine variations in DKD incidence, mortality, and risk factors across different regions and ethnic groups, which may inform targeted regional policies.

Mechanisms underlying cohort-specific trends

Our APC analysis identified distinct cohort effects. Future research could explore the role of historical lifestyle shifts and early-life exposures in shaping these cohort-specific risks, potentially revealing modifiable targets for prevention.

Evaluation of intervention efficacy

Given the projected rise in DKD-T2DM incidence, research on the effectiveness of targeted interventions—such as community-based diabetes management programs, DKD screening initiatives for high-risk groups, and the real-world impact of kidney protective medications is urgently needed to validate strategies for mitigating the growing burden.

Integration of socioeconomic and genetic factors

Our study, like many GBD-based analyses, lacked data on socioeconomic status (SES) and genetic determinants. Future work integrating SES indicators and genetic markers could clarify how these factors interact with demographic trends to influence DKD outcomes, enabling more precise prevention.

Conclusion

Over the past three decades (1990–2021), China has witnessed a significant decline in age-standardized mortality and DALYs for both DKD-T1DM and DKD-T2DM, which can be attributed to advancements in diabetes management and healthcare access. However, the burden of DKD-T2DM remains disproportionately high, driven by population aging, westernized lifestyles, and rising incidence rates, particularly among males and older adults. Joinpoint regression identified critical inflection points, such as a surge in DKD-T2DM prevalence post-2010 and declining DKD-T1DM incidence post-2015, reflecting evolving risk factors and intervention efficacy. Decomposition analysis revealed aging as the dominant driver of DKD-T2DM burden, while epidemiological improvements offset population growth effects in DKD-T1DM. APC effects highlighted distinct mortality peaks and cohort-specific risks tied to historical lifestyle shifts. ARIMA projections forecast a continued decline in DKD-T1DM age-standardized mortality rate (0.60/100,000 by 2041), but a sharp rise in DKD-T2DM age-standardized incidence rate (24.14/100,000 by 2041), underscoring the urgency of targeted strategies: prioritizing metabolic screening for aging populations, enhancing primary care for early diagnosis, and addressing gender-specific risks. In the future, precise prevention and control, optimization of medical systems and collaborative response of multidisciplinary research should be adopted to achieve sustainable control of disease burden.
