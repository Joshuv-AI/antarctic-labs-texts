Association of oral frailty, social support, and technophobia with depressive symptoms in Chinese older adults with chronic diseases: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wenwen Xu, Xiangdong Li, Yuanhao Sun, Yangyang Pan, Lin Zhang, Hao Wen, Ting Yuan, Hairong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to examine the associations of oral frailty, social support, and technophobia with depressive symptoms in Chinese older adults with chronic diseases. A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February to April 2025 in selected departments of a tertiary hospital in Anhui Province, China. Participants were adults aged 60 years or older with at least one physician-diagnosed chronic disease. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), with a score of ≥ 10 indicating depressive symptoms. Oral frailty, social support, and technophobia were measured using the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8), Oslo Social Support Scale-3 (OSSS-3), and Technophobia Scale, respectively. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine correlations between PHQ-9 total scores and the main study variables. Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to examine factors associated with depressive symptoms, adjusting for demographic, social, health-related variables, and the number of chronic diseases. A total of 525 participants were included, and 108 participants (20.6%) had depressive symptoms. PHQ-9 total scores were positively correlated with OFI-8 total scores (r = 0.616) and technophobia total scores (r = 0.635), and negatively correlated with OSSS-3 total scores (r = − 0.576). In the fully adjusted logistic regression model, oral frailty was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms (adjusted OR = 7.148, 95% CI: 2.740–18.653), higher social support was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms (adjusted OR = 0.264, 95% CI: 0.177–0.396), and higher technophobia was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms (adjusted OR = 1.246, 95% CI: 1.158–1.340). In the technophobia subdomain model, techno-anxiety and privacy concerns were significantly associated with depressive symptoms, whereas techno-paranoia was not independently associated with depressive symptoms. Oral frailty, social support, and technophobia remained associated with depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults with chronic diseases after adjustment for potential confounders. These findings suggest that oral functional status, perceived social support, and technology-related concerns may be considered in multidimensional assessment of depressive symptoms in this population. Background

Depressive symptoms are a common mental health concern in later life and are characterized by persistent low mood, diminished interest, sleep or appetite disturbance, fatigue, and impaired daily functioning1,2. Their clinical relevance is particularly evident among older adults with chronic diseases, because long-term disease management, functional decline, repeated healthcare use, and economic burden may increase psychological vulnerability3,4. China is experiencing rapid population aging, and the health burden associated with chronic diseases and mental health problems among older adults is increasing5,6. Therefore, identifying factors associated with depressive symptoms among older adults with chronic diseases is important for early recognition and multidimensional assessment.

Oral frailty has increasingly been recognized as a clinically relevant indicator of age-related decline in oral function. It refers not merely to oral disease, but to a broader functional condition involving tooth loss, impaired chewing ability, swallowing difficulty, reduced oral health-related behaviors, and social participation problems related to oral function7–9. Previous studies have reported associations between oral frailty, oral functional decline, and depressive symptoms in older adults10–12. Possible explanations include difficulty eating, reduced enjoyment of meals, restricted food choices, impaired communication, and avoidance of social dining. These mechanisms may be especially relevant for older adults with chronic diseases, who often experience multiple health burdens. However, evidence on the association between oral frailty and depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults with chronic diseases remains limited13.

Social support represents the social dimension of late-life mental health. For older adults with chronic diseases, support from family members, friends, and community resources may provide emotional reassurance, practical assistance, and help with disease management. Insufficient social support may increase psychological distress by reducing social participation, weakening perceived belonging, and limiting access to help during illness14–17. Prior studies have shown that lower social support is associated with a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms among older adults18,19. In the present study, social support was considered an independent social factor rather than a consequence of oral frailty.

The increasing use of digital and smart technologies in healthcare has created additional challenges for older adults with chronic diseases. Appointment registration, health monitoring, telehealth services, electronic payment, and online health information increasingly require older adults to interact with smartphones, online platforms, and intelligent medical devices20. Technophobia refers to fear, anxiety, avoidance, or distrust related to the use of modern technologies21. In older adults, age-related changes in cognition, memory, vision, and learning capacity may make technology use more difficult and may intensify technology-related anxiety22,23. Complex interfaces, multi-step operations, and privacy concerns may further discourage older adults from using digital health services24. Recent studies have also suggested that technophobia is associated with social support, eHealth literacy, healthcare technology self-efficacy, anxiety, and discontinued technology use among older adults25–27. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of Chinese older adults also reported moderate technophobia in digital health contexts, with privacy concerns being particularly prominent28. However, the association between technophobia and depressive symptoms has rarely been examined among Chinese older adults with chronic diseases.

The biopsychosocial model views health and illness as the result of interactions among biological, psychological, and social factors29. Within this framework, oral frailty may reflect biological and functional vulnerability, technophobia may represent a psychological and behavioral barrier in the context of digital healthcare, and social support may capture the social resources available to older adults. Although these factors have been studied separately, few studies have examined them simultaneously among Chinese older adults with chronic diseases. In particular, technophobia has rarely been incorporated into studies of depressive symptoms among older adults with chronic diseases, despite the increasing reliance on digital health services in chronic disease management. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the associations of oral frailty, social support, and technophobia with depressive symptoms in this population.

Materials and methods

Study participants and procedure

This hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February to April 2025 in the departments of endocrinology, cardiology, and geriatrics of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical University, Wuhu, Anhui Province, China. These departments were selected because they routinely provide care for older adults with common chronic diseases and were suitable for recruiting the target population. Eligible participants were recruited from survey stations established in the selected departments.

For all the participants, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 60 years; (2) diagnosis of at least one chronic disease; (3) able to understand the questionnaire and provide written informed consent. Chronic disease was defined as a physician-diagnosed long-term medical condition requiring ongoing monitoring or treatment. Information on chronic diseases was obtained through participant self-report and cross-checked against available medical records. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete or invalid questionnaire responses; and (2) severe cognitive impairment, intellectual disability, or severe mental disorder that prevented valid questionnaire completion.

A total of four investigators with medical, nursing, and public health backgrounds participated in data collection. Before data collection, all investigators received standardized training on the study protocol, informed consent procedure, questionnaire administration, neutral communication skills, scoring rules, and data checking. For participants with elementary school education or below, investigators administered the questionnaire face-to-face by reading each item and response option verbatim. Investigators were instructed not to explain item meanings in a leading manner or suggest any answer, in order to minimize interviewer bias.

All participants were informed of the purpose of the study, their right to withdraw at any time, and the confidentiality of their information. Questionnaires were completed after written informed consent was obtained. Of 559 individuals initially recruited, 525 provided valid questionnaires and were included in the final analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 93.92%.

Assessment instruments

Demographic questionnaire

Demographic and health-related information was collected using a self-reported questionnaire. The variables included sex, age, residence, educational level, alcohol drinking, smoking status, marital status, pension insurance, living arrangement, surgery in the past two years, regular physical examination, and chronic disease information. Marital status was categorized as married and not married; the latter included unmarried, divorced, and widowed participants. Living arrangement was assessed according to whether participants lived with a spouse, children, both spouse and children, alone, or in an institution, and was further categorized as living alone or not living alone. Surgery in the past two years was defined as any self-reported surgical experience during the previous two years, regardless of whether it was a major or minor procedure. The number of chronic diseases was calculated by summing the reported physician-diagnosed somatic chronic disease categories. Conditions conceptually overlapping with the outcome or main exposure, such as emotional or psychiatric problems and oral diseases, were not included in the chronic disease count used for regression adjustment.

PHQ-9 (patient health questionnaire-9)

The PHQ-9 was developed based on the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV)30. It contains 9 items, which include anhedonia, sleep disturbance, depressed mood, fatigue, appetite changes, concentration problems, low self-esteem, suicidal ideation, and psychomotor disturbances. A four-point Likert scale was used from 0 “not at all” to 3 “nearly every day” with a total score ranging from 0 to 27: 0 point means non-depressive symptoms, 1–4 points mean minimal depressive symptoms, 5–9 points indicate mild depressive symptoms, 10–14 points indicate moderate depressive symptoms, 15–19 points indicate moderately severe depressive symptoms, and 20 points or above mean severe depressive symptoms. As recommended by Levis, harmonized criterion cutoff values were used to determine the total scores ≥ 1031. In this study, a score of ≥ 10 was considered depressive symptoms. Both the structural and content validity of this scale are good32,33.

OFI-8 (oral frailty index-8)

The OFI-8 was developed by Tanaka et al. in 2021 to assess oral frailty34. The scale was composed of 8 items and divided into 5 dimensions, which included tooth loss, chewing ability, subjective swallowing difficulty, decreased social participation, and oral health-related behaviors. The total scores on the scale range from 0 to 11. A score below 2 means low risk, 3 points means moderate risk, and 4 points or above means high risk. As recommended by Tanaka, using harmonized criterion cutoff values, a total score ≥ 4 was established as oral frailty. The OFI-8 has indicated good validity and reliability for older adults in China35,36.

OSSS-3 (Oslo social support scale)

The OSSS-3 was standardized by Kocalevent et al. in 2018 and is used to evaluate social support37. The scale comprises 3 items, and the total score ranges from 3 to 14. A higher score means a stronger level of social support, with 3–8 points indicating low, 9–11 points indicating moderate, and 12–14 points indicating high. The OSSS-3 has been validated and used in Chinese populations38,39.

Technophobia scale

The Technophobia Scale was developed by Khasawneh in 2018 to measure technophobia and was translated into Chinese by Sun et al. in 202240,41. It consists of 13 items, which are divided into 3 dimensions, including techno-anxiety for 5 items, techno-paranoia for 5 items, and privacy concerns for 3 items. Techno-anxiety reflects emotional tension and avoidance when using new technologies; techno-paranoia reflects fear or distrust regarding the broader consequences of technology; and privacy concerns reflect worries about surveillance, tracking, or personal information exposure when using digital technologies. The total score is 13 ~ 65, with higher scores meaning higher levels of technophobia. A five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) was used. The Chinese version has shown good reliability and validity among older adults40.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Measurement data were described by means and standard deviations, while count data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. This study utilized chi-square tests for categorical variables and an independent-sample t test for continuous variables. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine correlations between PHQ-9 total scores and the total scores of oral frailty, social support, and technophobia. Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to examine factors associated with depressive symptoms. Depressive symptoms were treated as the dependent variable, coded as 0 = no and 1 = yes. In the main model, oral frailty, social support, and technophobia were entered as the primary independent variables. The model was adjusted for age, sex, residence, educational level, marital status, pension insurance, living alone, smoking status, alcohol drinking, surgery in the past two years, regular physical examination, and number of chronic diseases. To further examine technophobia subdomains, a second logistic regression model was constructed by replacing the technophobia total score with techno-anxiety, techno-paranoia, and privacy concerns, which were entered simultaneously. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Model performance was evaluated using the omnibus likelihood-ratio test, Nagelkerke R2, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

This study included 525 older adults with chronic diseases, with a mean age of 72.88 years (SD = 7.92; range: 60–97 years). Among them, 417 participants (79.4%) were classified as not having depressive symptoms, whereas 108 participants (20.6%) were classified as having depressive symptoms. Of all participants, 269 (51.2%) were male, 243 (46.3%) were aged 70–79 years, 275 (52.4%) lived in rural areas, and 328 (62.5%) had an educational level of elementary school or below. In addition, 417 participants (79.4%) were married, 353 (67.2%) had pension insurance, 72 (13.7%) lived alone, 164 (31.2%) had undergone surgery in the past two years, and 276 (52.6%) reported regular physical examinations. Oral frailty was identified in 236 participants (45.0%) (Table 1).

Cross-sectional associations of oral frailty, social support, and technophobia with depressive symptoms

In the fully adjusted binary logistic regression model, oral frailty, social support, and technophobia were significantly associated with depressive symptoms. Participants with oral frailty had higher odds of depressive symptoms than those without oral frailty (adjusted OR = 7.148, 95% CI: 2.740–18.653, P < 0.001). Higher social support was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms (adjusted OR = 0.264, 95% CI: 0.177–0.396, P < 0.001), whereas higher technophobia was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms (adjusted OR = 1.246, 95% CI: 1.158–1.340, P < 0.001). The number of chronic diseases and surgery in the past two years were not significantly associated with depressive symptoms after adjustment (Table 4).

Discussion

Principal findings

This study examined the associations of oral frailty, social support, and technophobia with depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults with chronic diseases. Depressive symptoms were identified in 20.6% of participants. In the fully adjusted logistic regression model, oral frailty and higher technophobia were positively associated with depressive symptoms, whereas higher social support was negatively associated with depressive symptoms. In the model including technophobia subdomains, techno-anxiety and privacy concerns remained significantly associated with depressive symptoms, whereas techno-paranoia was not independently associated with depressive symptoms. These findings support the relevance of considering oral functional status, perceived social support, and technology-related concerns within a biopsychosocial framework. However, given the cross-sectional design, these results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships.

Oral frailty and depressive symptoms

The adjusted OR remained above 6 in both the total technophobia model and the subdomain model, suggesting that the association between oral frailty and depressive symptoms was not fully explained by measured demographic, social, or health-related covariates. Oral frailty showed a substantial association with depressive symptoms in this sample. Nevertheless, the magnitude of this estimate should be interpreted cautiously. As shown in Table 1, oral frailty was present in 98 of 108 participants with depressive symptoms and in 138 of 417 participants without depressive symptoms. Although complete separation was not present because all four cells contained observations, this sparse-cell pattern, particularly the small number of participants with depressive symptoms but without oral frailty, may have contributed to the large adjusted OR and the wide confidence interval. Therefore, the adjusted OR should be interpreted as evidence of a strong cross-sectional association in this sample rather than as a precise causal effect size. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that oral functional decline and poor oral health are related to depressive symptoms among older adults10,12,42,43. Oral frailty may be linked to depressive symptoms through several pathways. Impaired chewing and swallowing may restrict food choices and reduce enjoyment of eating, while tooth loss, dry mouth, or oral discomfort may limit communication and participation in shared meals. In the Chinese cultural context, eating with family members and participating in festival meals are important forms of social connection; therefore, oral functional problems may contribute to embarrassment, social withdrawal, and psychological distress44.

The interpretation of this association requires caution. Although oral frailty may contribute to depressive symptoms through reduced nutrition, impaired social participation, and perceived loss of dignity, reverse causality is also plausible. Older adults with depressive symptoms may have lower motivation for oral self-care, poorer appetite, less social interaction, and more negative perceptions of their oral function. In addition, medication use, polypharmacy, xerostomia, inflammatory status, and nutritional status may partly explain the observed association, but these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study45–47. Therefore, oral frailty should be interpreted as a clinically relevant correlate of depressive symptoms rather than as an established cause.

Social support and depressive symptoms

The inverse association between social support and depressive symptoms also remained stable across both adjusted models, supporting the robustness of this association in the present dataset. Higher social support was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms. This finding is consistent with previous evidence that social support and social relationships are closely related to mental health in later life14,15,48,49. For older adults with chronic diseases, social support may be particularly important because disease management often requires help with medical visits, medication routines, lifestyle adjustments, and emotional coping. Supportive family and community relationships may reduce perceived burden, provide practical assistance, and maintain a sense of belonging and meaning.

This finding should also be interpreted in light of possible bidirectionality. Low social support may increase depressive symptoms, but depressive symptoms may also reduce social engagement, weaken perceived support, and lead to withdrawal from family or community interactions. The present study measured perceived social support using a brief scale, and did not collect detailed information on frequency of social activities, social network size, or time spent outside the home. Therefore, the observed association reflects perceived support rather than the full structure and quality of participants’ social relationships.

Technophobia and depressive symptoms

The subdomain analysis suggested that the association between technophobia and depressive symptoms was mainly driven by techno-anxiety and privacy concerns rather than techno-paranoia. Technophobia was positively associated with depressive symptoms after adjustment for demographic, social, and health-related variables. This result is clinically relevant because older adults with chronic diseases increasingly encounter digital health technologies, including online appointment systems, electronic health records, health-monitoring devices, and telehealth services20,24. For individuals who feel anxious, distrustful, or overwhelmed by technology, these digital demands may increase helplessness and avoidance, limit access to health information, and reinforce feelings of exclusion. Existing studies have linked technophobia with lower eHealth literacy, lower healthcare technology self-efficacy, and social support deficits among older adults23,25,26.

The subdomain analysis further clarified this association. Techno-anxiety and privacy concerns remained significantly associated with depressive symptoms, whereas techno-paranoia was not independently associated with depressive symptoms after the three subdomains were entered simultaneously. This suggests that emotional tension during technology use and concerns about personal information exposure may be more directly related to depressive symptoms than broader distrust of technology. For older adults with chronic diseases, privacy concerns may be particularly salient because digital health services often involve sensitive medical information. Nevertheless, because this study was cross-sectional, it remains possible that depressive symptoms increase technology-related anxiety and privacy concerns rather than the reverse.

Integrated interpretation and implications

Taken together, these findings suggest that depressive symptoms among older adults with chronic diseases may be related to multiple domains of vulnerability. Oral frailty reflects biological and functional decline, social support reflects social resources, and technophobia reflects psychological and behavioral barriers in a digital healthcare environment. The simultaneous inclusion of these variables extends previous studies that examined oral health, social support, or technology-related anxiety separately. The high Nagelkerke R² value should also be interpreted cautiously. Nagelkerke R² is a pseudo-R² statistic for logistic regression and does not have the same interpretation as the proportion of variance explained in ordinary least-squares regression. In the present study, the high value may reflect the strong separation between depressive symptom groups by oral frailty, social support, and technophobia. In addition, all major constructs were assessed using self-reported instruments, and overlap among perceived oral functional limitation, perceived social support, technology-related anxiety, and depressive symptoms may have contributed to the model’s explanatory power. Therefore, the model fit should be interpreted as sample-specific explanatory performance rather than evidence of externally validated or generalizable prediction accuracy. The findings do not justify causal or intervention claims, but they indicate that assessment of depressive symptoms in older adults with chronic diseases may benefit from attention to oral functional status, perceived social support, and technology-related concerns.

From a clinical and public health perspective, the results support a multidimensional assessment approach rather than a single-factor interpretation. Screening for depressive symptoms in older adults with chronic diseases may be strengthened by identifying oral functional problems, low perceived social support, and technology-related anxiety or privacy concerns. However, whether modifying oral frailty, enhancing social support, or reducing technophobia can reduce depressive symptoms requires confirmation in longitudinal and intervention studies.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths. First, it examined depressive symptoms among older adults with chronic diseases by integrating oral frailty, social support, and technophobia within a biopsychosocial framework. Second, validated instruments were used to assess depressive symptoms, oral frailty, social support, and technophobia. Third, the analysis adjusted for multiple demographic, social, and health-related variables, including the number of chronic diseases.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a hospital-based cross-sectional study conducted in one province of China, and participants were recruited from selected departments of a tertiary hospital. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to community-dwelling older adults, older adults without chronic diseases, or older adults in other regions. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The observed associations may be bidirectional; for example, depressive symptoms may reduce perceived social support, worsen self-perceived oral function, or increase technology-related anxiety. Third, although information on the type and number of chronic diseases was included, data on disease duration, severity, medication use, and polypharmacy were not collected. Therefore, residual confounding related to disease burden and medication-related oral symptoms, such as xerostomia, cannot be excluded. In addition, the regression models adjusted for the number of chronic diseases but did not include specific chronic disease categories simultaneously. Different chronic conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease, may have different relationships with depressive symptoms, oral frailty, and technology-related barriers. Because chronic disease categories were not mutually exclusive and some categories had relatively small numbers, entering all disease categories simultaneously could have introduced model instability. Therefore, disease count was used as a parsimonious adjustment variable. However, this approach may not fully capture disease-specific pathways, and residual confounding by chronic disease type cannot be excluded. Fourth, activities of daily living, fall history, physical frailty, frequency of going out, and detailed hospital visit frequency were not assessed, although these factors may influence social engagement and depressive symptoms. Fifth, some questionnaires were completed with interviewer assistance among participants with lower educational levels, which may have introduced interviewer bias despite standardized training. Finally, oral frailty, social support, technophobia, and depressive symptoms were assessed using self-reported instruments, and measurement bias may exist.

Conclusions

This study identified several factors associated with depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults with chronic diseases. Oral frailty and higher technophobia were positively associated with depressive symptoms, whereas higher social support was negatively associated with depressive symptoms. Among technophobia subdomains, techno-anxiety and privacy concerns were independently associated with depressive symptoms after adjustment. These findings suggest that oral functional status, perceived social support, and technology-related concerns may be considered in multidimensional assessment of depressive symptoms in older adults with chronic diseases. Given the cross-sectional design, longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether modifying these factors can reduce depressive symptoms.
