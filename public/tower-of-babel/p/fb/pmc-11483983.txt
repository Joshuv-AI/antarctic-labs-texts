Trends in viral load suppression among HIV patients on antiretroviral therapy (ART) at Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital, Ghana: 2019–2023
Authors: Gideon Amankwah Kyere, Godwin Adjei Vechey, Veronica Okwuchi Charles-Unadike, Elvis Enowbeyang Tarkang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Joint United Nations Programme on Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)/Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) (UNAIDS) aims to eradicate AIDS by 2030 through 95:95:95 targets: identifying 95% of persons living with HIV (PLHIV), initiating 95% of those identified on antiretroviral therapy (ART), and ensuring that 95% of those initiated on ART are virally suppressed. Virally suppressed patients pose minimal risk of HIV transmission. ART aims to suppress the HIV-viral load (VL) and increase immunity, reducing morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to determine the trends in VL suppression among HIV patients on ART from 2019 to 2023 at Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital.MethodsThis study adopted a retrospective Hospital-based design in which secondary data from 842 patients on ART from 2019 to 2023 were used. The study design specifically involved conducting serial cross-sectional studies to measure the prevalence of VL suppression each year from 2019 to 2023. This approach allowed the researchers to analyse the annual prevalence of VL suppression among study participants without following individual participants longitudinally throughout the entire period. The data were analysed via STATA version 17.0. Chi-square and logistic regressions were used to determine the associations between VL suppression and the independent variables at p < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsIn 2019, VL suppression was 79.6%, decreasing to 40.0% in 2020 and then rising to 82.7% in 2021, dropping to 67.8% in 2022 and 66.7% in the first quarter of 2023. Clients aged 40–49, 50–59, and 60–69 years were more likely to have VL suppression [aOR = 4.4 (1.36–14.25), p = 0.013], [aOR = 5.5 (1.65–18.39), p = 0.006] and [aOR = 5.0 (1.42–17.46), p = 0.012], respectively. Clients who were consistently on the same type of ART for more than a year were more likely to have VL suppression [aOR = 10.6 (4.18–26.76), p < 0.001].ConclusionVL suppression was low among patients. Advanced age and being on the same ART for more than 12 months were significantly associated with VL suppression. Health promotion activities are needed for people who have been suppressed to maintain and achieve a lifetime undetectable VL, targeting the younger age group.Keywords: Viral load suppression, Antiretroviral therapy, Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital, Ghana

Introduction

The global response to end Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)/Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) by 2030 is critically dependent on effective viral load (VL) monitoring and management [1, 2]. Early initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) is essential for suppressing viral replication, enhancing immune function, and reducing morbidity and mortality rates [3]. Viral suppression is defined as having a VL of fewer than 1000 copies/mL after a minimum of six months on ART [4]. The Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) 95:95:95 target aims to identify 95% of persons living with HIV (PLHIV), initiate 95% of those identified on ART, and ensure that 95% of those initiated on ART are virally suppressed by 2030 [5, 6]. This approach not only benefits individual patients but also reduces HIV transmission at the population level [4].

Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has made significant progress by shifting from cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) cell count monitoring to VL monitoring, reflecting a more effective strategy for managing HIV [2]. The availability of potent ART has revolutionised HIV care, leading to improved patient outcomes and sustained viral suppression [7]. However, challenges such as viral rebound and suboptimal suppression rates among specific demographic groups, particularly adolescents, continue to hinder effective HIV management [8, 9]. The epidemic is driven primarily by factors such as unprotected sex, multiple sexual partnerships, and low condom use [10].

In Ghana, as of 2022, 354,927 people were living with HIV (PLHIV), with females accounting for 66% and children accounting for 7% of the population. The national HIV prevalence is 1.7% (ranging from 0.45% in the Northeast Region to 2.29% in the Eastern Region), with 16,574 new infections, predominantly among adults (83%) [11]. ART coverage for adults aged 15 and older was 62.78%, and prevention of mother-to‒child transmission (PMTCT) for HIV coverage was 71.59%. Despite these advancements, regional disparities persist, with Ghana reporting a VL suppression rate of 64.3% [8].

In the Asante Mampong municipality in the Ashanti region of Ghana, where this study was conducted, the prevalence of HIV is 1.89% [11]. This prevalence is higher than the national prevalence (1.7%), indicating a pressing issue that warrants investigation. The high HIV prevalence in Asante Mampong suggests a significant burden on the local healthcare system and underscores the necessity of effective VL suppression strategies.

Understanding VL suppression among PLHIV in this municipality is important, as it can provide insights into the effectiveness of ART programmes in high-prevalence areas, highlighting potential gaps in treatment adherence, healthcare delivery, and patient support services. By identifying successful strategies and common challenges, this study offers valuable lessons for other high-prevalence regions. This knowledge can guide national policies and programs to optimise HIV care and meet the UNAIDS 95:95:95 target. This study aims to analyse VL suppression trends among HIV patients on ART at Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital from 2019 to 2023.

Methods

Study site description

Asante Mampong, situated in the Mampong Municipality of the Ashanti region, serves as an administrative hub with a population of 116,632 spread across 670.7 square kilometres, experiencing an annual population growth of 2.6% [12]. It shares borders with Ejura, Sekyere Kumawu, Sekyere Central Nsuta, and Sekyere South. The town encompasses approximately 48 subcommunities, extending from Mampong to Nsuta.

Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital is the only facility in the municipality that provides VL testing services. The Hospital serves not only its patients but also those referred from other facilities. As of December 2023, approximately 1,200 patients were receiving ART at this Hospital [12]. The socioeconomic context of the population in the municipality includes a predominantly rural community with a moderate level of education and income. These factors are critical, as they could influence adherence to ART and retention in care, which ultimately affect VL suppression rates.

Population and eligibility criteria

The study population included all PLHIV receiving ART at Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital, including those referred from other Hospitals for VL testing. The inclusion criteria included reviewed records of HIV patients who had been on ART at the Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital for at least six months and had undergone at least one VL test from January 2019 to December 2023. All complete records within this timeframe were included, whereas those with incomplete data were excluded.

Study design

This study adopted a retrospective Hospital-based design using secondary data from the ART clinic at the Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital. Given that this was a retrospective study, the most suitable method for data collection involves the use of secondary records. The study design specifically involved conducting serial cross-sectional studies to measure the prevalence of VL suppression each year from 2019 to 2023. This approach allowed the researchers to analyse the annual prevalence of VL suppression among study participants without following individual participants longitudinally throughout the entire period. Instead, each year’s data provided a snapshot of VL suppression rates, enabling a comprehensive assessment of trends over the specified timeframe.

Sample size determination

All the data captured on HIV patients on ART testing for VL within the five years from January 2019 to December 2023 were 842, representing the total sample size.

Sampling method

The Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital was purposively selected for the study because it is the main health establishment that monitors patients on ART and tests for VL in the entire Mampong Municipality. Therefore, the data needed for the current study could only be found in the ART clinic records of the Hospital.

Data collection procedure

A data extraction sheet was used to collect the results of VL tests among HIV patients on ART within five years, their ART status, and their sociodemographic characteristics (January 2019-December 2023). The data were extracted from the HIV patients’ records on the ART register from the Hospital ART clinic because the ART register contains all the necessary information relevant to the study. The data were captured via the Kobocollect tool.

Study variables

The dependent variable in the current study was VL suppression among HIV-positive patients. Patients with a plasma viral load of 1000 copies/mL or higher after 12 months on ART, despite effective adherence support, were classified as having viral non-suppression. Those with a plasma VL of less than 1000 copies/mL were classified as having viral suppression [4]. At present, the VL testing programme for ART delivery in Ghana is being administered through the use of 9 Roche CobasAmpliPrep/TaqMan 48 PCR (TaqMan 48) machines [13], which detect plasma VL of less than 50 copies/mL [14].

The independent variables included age, marital status, religion, education level, ethnicity, occupation, and ART status. These variables were analysed to predict VL suppression. Early or late ART initiation was defined on the basis of the duration between HIV diagnosis and the commencement of ART. Participants who began ART within 7–30 days of their HIV diagnosis were categorized as having initiated ART early. Conversely, those who started ART more than 30 days after their HIV diagnosis were categorised as having initiated ART late. This classification was used to evaluate the impact of the timing of ART initiation on VL suppression outcomes [15].

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed via STATA version 17. VL suppression status was categorised according to the WHO’s guidelines for low- and middle-income countries. Patients with a plasma VL of 1000 copies/mL or higher after 12 months on ART, despite effective adherence support, were classified as having viral non-suppression. Those with a plasma VL of less than 1000 copies/mL were classified as having viral suppression. Additionally, patients with a plasma VL of less than 50 copies/mL were considered to have undetectable VLs [14]. The dependent variable was binary: 1 for suppressed VL and 0 for unsuppressed VL. To determine the associations between the dependent variables and VL suppression, the chi-square test and bivariable and multivariable logistic regressions were used, with statistical significance determined at a p-value of 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval (CI). Variables with a p -value < 0.05 in the chi-square test were candidates for the bivariate and multivariate logistic regressions.

Trends in viral load suppression and undetected viral load (2019–2023)

Figure 1 shows the trends of VL suppression and undetected VL from 2019 to 2023, highlighting critical changes in HIV treatment outcomes. In 2019, the VL suppression rate was 79.6%, whereas the undetected VL rate was 48.8%. Both metrics experienced a sharp decline in 2020, with VL suppression dropping to 40% and undetected VL falling to 13.3%, likely due to disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic.

Trends in viral load suppression and undetected viral load (2019–2023)

By 2021, there was a marked increase in VL suppression, which peaked at 82.7%, indicating a significant improvement in HIV treatment outcomes. In contrast, undetected VL improved, rising to 41.5%. However, the following years presented a divergence in these trends. VL suppression decreased to 67.8% in 2022 and further to 66.7% in 2023, suggesting some challenges in maintaining earlier gains. The undetected VL, on the other hand, showed a more gradual recovery. It increased to 43.7% in 2022 and decreased to 36% in 2023.

Statistically significant associations between the year of registration and both viral load suppression and undetected viral load

The chi-square test for the association between the year of registration and VL suppression was statistically significant (χ2 = 27.00, p < 0.001). This result indicates that there is a significant change in the proportion of VL suppression across the years. In particular, the percentage of VL suppression dropped sharply in 2020 (40%) possibly due to COVID-19-related disruptions but recovered in subsequent years, as shown by the row percentages (Table 2). Similarly, the chi-square test for the association between the year of registration and undetected VL was also statistically significant (χ2 = 10.30, p = 0.036). This result suggests a significant change in the proportion of individuals with undetected VL across the years. There was a marked decrease in undetected VL in 2020 (13.3%), with gradual improvement afterwards, yet challenges remain in maintaining consistent outcomes in recent years (Table 3).

Discussion

This study revealed that the VL suppression rate was 78.6% over five years, with 43.3% of patients achieving undetectable VLs. Clients aged 40–49, 50–59, and 60–69 years were more likely to achieve VL suppression than those aged less than 20 years. Additionally, clients on the same ART regimen for more than a year were significantly more likely to achieve VL suppression than those who started ART late.

The findings revealed a VL suppression rate of 78.6% among HIV patients receiving ART at Asante Mampong Municipal Hospital over the five years from 2019 to 2023. This rate is lower than the global target of 95% VL suppression among PLHIV on ART [16]. However, the VL suppression in the current study is greater than that in studies conducted in Ghana and Ethiopia (69% [1] and 73% [17], respectively). The VL suppression rate in the current study was lower than those reported in studies conducted in Uganda (95.0%) [18] and Nigeria (90.4%) [19]. Several factors could contribute to these discrepancies. Differences in healthcare infrastructure, access to medication, and adherence support programmes may play a role. Additionally, variations in the definition of VL suppression cut-off values and the implementation of adherence strategies could impact outcomes. Our findings underscore the need for advocacy for the continuation of current HIV management approaches, emphasising the importance of sustained adherence counselling and comprehensive healthcare delivery systems in optimising treatment outcomes. Moving forward, efforts should focus on maintaining and further improving VL suppression rates to effectively combat the HIV/AIDS epidemic.

Within five years, the percentage of patients with undetectable VL was 43.3%, indicating that there is a high chance of HIV transmission. This finding is lower than that of a study conducted in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (51.8%) [20]. According to the WHO [21], PLHIV with an undetectable VL cannot transmit HIV to their partners during sexual intercourse. Additionally, pregnant women with undetectable VL are at low risk of transmitting HIV to their children during childbirth or breastfeeding. The results from the trends of undetected VL across the years are all low. In 2023, the rates of VL suppression and undetected VL were 84.6% and 36.0%, respectively. The decline in undetectable VL between 2022 and 2023 (43.7% vs. 36.0%) may be a result of viral rebound as a result of non-adherence to ART. Thus, there is a need for interventions to increase the prevalence of undetected VL among PLHIV to prevent HIV transmission. The results of the current study revealed that in 2019, before COVID-19, VL suppression was 79.6% and decreased to 40.0% in the COVID-19 era. The significant drop in VL suppression in 2020 likely resulted from the COVID-19 pandemic and its associated disruptions. Various studies have shown that during this period, access to healthcare, including ART services, was severely impacted by movement restrictions, fear of infection, and reduced availability of healthcare staff [22, 23]. In northern Nigeria, despite community-based ART distribution models, VL testing has decreased, and missed appointments have increased during the pandemic [24]. Similar findings were reported in Venezuela and Kenya, where the pandemic interrupted routine HIV care, resulting in a reduction in ART adherence and viral suppression [23, 25]. Therefore, the sharp decline in viral suppression observed in 2020 in this study can be attributed to these global trends, where disruptions in health services impacted adherence to ART and follow-up care. The decrease in VL suppression during the COVID-19 pandemic highlights the vulnerability of HIV treatment programs to external disruptions, such as public health emergencies. This emphasises the need for resilient healthcare systems that can maintain continuity of care during crises.

In the present study, demographic factors (age and occupation) and the ART status of clients significantly influenced VL suppression. Compared with those younger than 40 years, those aged 40–69 years presented greater odds of VL suppression. These findings align with those of previous research conducted in Ugandan health facilities, indicating that older age groups are more likely to achieve VL suppression, possibly because of their extensive experience with ART services and better adherence [26]. The observed higher odds of VL suppression among older age groups could be attributed to several factors. First, older individuals may have been on ART for a longer duration, allowing for better adherence to treatment regimens and routine medical appointments. Additionally, older age may correlate with increased health literacy and awareness of the importance of adherence to ART, leading to improved treatment outcomes. To address the observed age-related disparities in VL suppression, targeted interventions tailored to younger age groups may be necessary. These interventions could include enhanced adherence support programmes, such as mobile health initiatives and peer support groups, to promote medication adherence and regular clinic attendance.

The study revealed that clients who were on the same ART for more than 12 months were more likely to achieve VL suppression. This underscores the importance of consistent ART adherence over time. Similar findings in Trinidad revealed a greater likelihood of VL suppression at 12 months among individuals [27]. Possible reasons for these findings include the time needed for ART to effectively suppress VL, as well as the importance of regular medication intake and clinic attendance. Healthcare providers should strengthen ongoing adherence counselling, provide support to clients to increase appointment attendance and ensure consistent access to ART medications. Additionally, community-based interventions and peer support groups can help reinforce adherence behaviours and promote sustained viral suppression among PLHIV on ART.

Study limitations

The major limitation of the study is that the study methodology involved a review of records; therefore, the analysis and interpretation of the data are limited to only those variables that are routinely collected from patients and captured in patient records. Some variables, such as patients’ clinical condition, which could have played a major role in initial VL testing and VL suppression, were not available. A baseline VL count was not available for some of the clients; hence, analysis of the baseline VL could not be performed for those patients. Since the study did not measure these other variables, the study was unable to account for the influence of these factors in the analysis.

Conclusion

VL suppression was low among the patients. Advanced age and being on the same ART for more than 12 months were significantly associated with VL suppression. Health promotion activities are needed for people who have been suppressed to maintain and achieve a lifetime undetectable VL, targeting the younger age group.
