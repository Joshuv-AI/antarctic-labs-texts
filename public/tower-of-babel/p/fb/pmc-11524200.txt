Tofersen for SOD1 ALS
Authors: William H Everett, Robert C Bucelli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a neurodegenerative condition affecting the motor system. The heterogenous nature of ALS complicates trial design. Genetic forms of ALS present an opportunity to intervene in a less heterogeneous population. ALS associated with gain of function mutations in SOD1 make ‘knock-down’ strategies an attractive therapeutic approach. Tofersen, an antisense oligonucleotide that reduces expression of SOD1 via RNAase mediated degradation of SOD1 mRNA, has shown robust effects on ALS biomarkers. While a Phase III trial of tofersen failed to meet its primary end point, open label extension data suggests that tofersen slows progression of SOD1 ALS.Keywords: : amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, antisense oligonucleotide, edaravone, MRNA degradation, riluzole, sodium phenylbutyrate-taurursodiol, SOD1, tofersen

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative condition primarily affecting the motor system. First described by Charcot [1], the clinical presentation of ALS is quite variable with a spectrum of upper versus lower motor neuron involvement. Most commonly, symptoms indicative of upper motor neuron (e.g., stiffness) or lower motor neuron pathology (e.g., weakness, atrophy, fasciculations) begin in one limb or body region with subsequent progression to involve other body regions. Impaired bulbar and respiratory function leads to death, typically within 2–5 years of symptoms onset. Rarely, bulbar or respiratory dysfunction can be the initial presentation [2].

The incidence of ALS varies depending on the population studied with values ranging from 0.6 to 3.8 per 100,000 person-years [3]. Of note, most incidence studies have looked at European populations with few studies from outside Europe [4,5]. Additionally, some studies suggest that the incidence of ALS is increasing, though it is not clear whether this is due to improved diagnosis, population dynamics or other causes [6,7]. The prevalence of ALS ranges from 4.1 to 8.4 per 100,000 people [3]. There is some variation in the prevalence of ALS in different ethnic groups with Americans of European ancestry having approximately double the rate of African Americans in the US National ALS Registry [8].

The clinical heterogeneity of ALS presentation and progression has encouraged the development of biomarkers to assist in diagnosis, prognosis and monitoring response to treatment. Such biomarkers are also critical for clinical trials, both for initial balancing of groups and for assessing the target engagement of an intervention [9]. While a variety of biomarkers including imaging and electrophysiologic measures have been developed, fluid-based biomarkers, particularly blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) neurofilaments, have garnered a great deal of attention in recent years. Neurofilaments are intermediate filaments that are highly abundant in myelinated axons. There are three types of neurofilaments – light (NfL), medium (NfM) and heavy (NfH) – named on the basis of their molecular weights. The precise function of neurofilaments is not entirely known but they are critical for radial growth of axons [10]. Neurofilaments have been found to be elevated in the CSF and blood of patients with a broad spectrum of neurological disorders, including multiple sclerosis, neurodegenerative dementias, traumatic brain injury (TBI), cerebrovascular disease and ALS. The development of single molecule array (SiMoA) technology has enabled the reliable detection of very small concentrations of NfL in blood samples, facilitating repeat measurement and longitudinal monitoring [10,11]. With this improved feasibility, neurofilament biomarkers have become important secondary end points in ALS clinical trials.

While most cases of ALS are sporadic, about 10% of cases are familial and associated with a family history of ALS or other neurodegenerative disorders, such as frontotemporal dementia [12–14]. This classification of ALS into sporadic or familial however is clearly incomplete with emerging evidence of genetic variants with incomplete penetrance as well as private mutations in sporadic cases [12,15]. Overall, about 12% of all ALS cases have a mutation in a known gene [13]. For the remainder of cases, the concept of polygenic risk is an emerging area of research [16].

Mutations in more than 40 genes have been associated with ALS. The most common ones (C9orf72, SOD1, FUS and TARDBP) account for 48% of familial cases and 5% of sporadic cases. Notably, the frequency of these mutations varies based on the population studied [17]. These familial forms of ALS have offered many insights into the pathophysiology of ALS and unique opportunities for intervention.

Mutations in SOD1 were the first genetic form of familial ALS to be identified and are found in 15–25% of familial cases and 1-2% of ALS cases overall [18]. SOD1 is a highly abundant cytosolic protein that exists as a homodimer and catalyzes detoxification of superoxide to hydrogen peroxide. While initially thought to be related to loss of function and subsequent ROS generation, further studies have demonstrated that the pathogenesis of SOD1 related ALS is most likely mediated through a toxic gain of function [19–21]. Additionally, SOD1 knockout models and patients with biallelic SOD1 loss of function mutations have been found to have a phenotype different from ALS [22–24]. Pathologically, SOD1 ALS has hyaline conglomerate inclusions containing SOD1 rather than the TDP43 inclusions seen in the majority of ALS [25–29]. One proposed mechanism of evolution in SOD1 ALS is prion-like spread of misfolded SOD1 [30,31] There is a wide clinical spectrum of disease in SOD1 ALS with marked variation between mutations. In general, SOD1 ALS tends to have onset 10 years earlier than sporadic ALS and has predominantly lower motor neuron signs [32]. Frontotemporal dementia is less commonly seen than in other forms of ALS [33].

More than 200 pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in SOD1 have been identified. Most mutations are dominant with variable penetrance, though some (e.g., p.D90A/D91A) are associated with a recessive pattern [34,35]. Some variants such as p. A4V/A5V (among the most common in North America) are associated with rapid progression while others are associated with slower (“non-fast”) progression [32,36]. The variation in nomenclature in SOD1 mutations is related to the initial description of SOD1 as a 153-amino-acid protein. Later work recognized that it is a 154-amino-acid protein prior to post-translational modification. This has lead to descriptions of mutations differing by a single number (eg. p.A4V vs A5V) [37]. The unified mechanism of SOD1 ALS has made it an attractive candidate for targeted therapy, though the heterogeneity of the disease adds many challenges to clinical trial design and execution.

Current standard of care guidelines & therapeutics for ALS

Management of ALS is divided into pharmaceuticals aimed at slowing disease progression and supportive care, given the unrelenting course of the disease. At the time of this writing, four disease modifying medications have been approved by the FDA for use in ALS.

Riluzole was the first medication approved based on evidence it prolongs survival in ALS. The mechanism of action is presumed to be via modulation of synaptic glutamergic transmission. High synaptic levels of glutamate are known to occur in ALS and possibly contribute to pathogenesis via excitotoxicity [38]. An initial clinical trial showed a modest increase in survival [39]. This study and others (some of which were negative) were analyzed in a Cochrane review in 2012 [40] and other meta-analyses of real-world data [41], which cumulatively support a 2-3 month survival benefit in ALS with riluzole 100 mg daily. Riluzole is not associated with a significant increase in adverse events though liver function can be altered and should be monitored. The most common side effects are nausea and fatigue [42].

Edaravone (Radicava) was the second disease modifying medication approved for ALS. The mechanism of action in ALS is unknown though it is suspected to act as an antioxidant, reducing oxidative stress and thereby improving neuron survival [43,44] l. Initially, only an intravenous infusion of edaravone was approved but more recently an oral formulation has been approved by the FDA [45]. Several randomized clinical trials have evaluated edaravone in ALS patients [46,47]. Recently, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 studies of edaravone in ALS showed improved survival at 18, 24 and 30 months without improvement in functional measures [48,49]. There were no significant adverse effects [48,49].

Sodium phenylbutyrate-taurursodiol (Relyvrio) was the third medication approved for ALS. Its mechanism of action is proposed to be via amelioration of endoplasmic reticulum stress related to protein misfolding and aggregation. In particular, sodium phenylbutyrate acts as a histone deacetylase inhibitor upregulating heat shock proteins [50]. Taurursodiol reduces mitochondrial permeability by preventing Bax protein translocation [51]. In the CENTAUR trial, a Phase II randomized placebo-controlled trial, participants receiving a fixed dose of oral sodium phenylbutyrate–taurursodiol showed slowing in the mean rate of change in ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSRFR-R) of -1.24 per month with active drug and -1.66 with placebo. Secondary outcomes did not differ significantly and the most common adverse effect in the treatment groups was gastrointestinal disorders [52]. Due to failure to meet prespecified primary or secondary end points in the follow-up Phase III PHOENIX trial, sodium phenylbutyrate-taurursodiol was recently pulled from the market and its approval status for ALS treatment is tenuous [53].

Another important aspect of ALS is monitoring for respiratory failure and initiation of non-invasive ventilation (NIV). While the ideal threshold for initiation of NIV has not been identified, NIV has been shown to improve survival and quality of life [54–56]. Percutaneous gastrostomy tubes (“g-tubes”) are also frequently used in the care of advanced or bulbar-onset ALS patients. G-tubes allow for easier administration of fluids and medications as well as providing additional nutritional support. The effect of g-tube placement on survival however is unclear [57,58]. Finally, palliative care has key roles in ALS not only in symptom management and advanced care planning, but also in caregiver support [59].

Introduction to the compound

Antisense oligonucleotides

Tofersen is an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO). Molecules of this class are DNA oligomers of 10–50 base pairs that binds to target RNA via base pairing and alter protein expression. ASOs are attractive therapeutics given their potential for high specificity as well as their lack of interaction with other medications. The specificity of an ASO is driven by the DNA sequence which allows the ASO to engage target mRNAs in the nucleus [60]. Following target engagement, ASOs can act through a number of different mechanisms [60,61]. In one, RNase H binds the mRNA-DNA (ASO) heteroduplex, cleaves the mRNA, and releases the ASO which can go on to bind another mRNA [62,63]. Over time, this reduces the pool of mRNA available for translation resulting in decreased target protein. ASOs (inotersen and eplontersen) utilizing the RNase H degradation mechanism have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of transthyretin amyloidosis and act to mediate degradation of TTR mRNA [64]. Whether an ASO acts via degradation of the target mRNA or not depends on several features of the ASO backbone and sugar moieties [60].

ASO backbone modifications also play a critical role in circumventing two of the major obstacles facing nucleic acid-based therapeutics—nuclease degradation and immune stimulation via Toll-like receptors (TLRs). Substitution of sulfur for phosphorus in the non-bridging phosphate oxygen atom position increases resistance to nucleases and improves recruitment of RNase H. Modification of the 2’-position on the ribose moiety with 2’-O-methyl or 2’-O-methoxyl groups increase binding affinity and nuclease resistance while importantly reducing TLR stimulation [65–67]. If all of the 2’ ribose positions are modified in this manner, the ASO is unable to recruit RNase H limiting the ASO to altering post-transcriptional changes without degrading the transcript. Thus, many ASOs are designed with gaps of unmodified bases to allow for target degradation while still improving the stability and reducing stimulation of the immune system [68].

Tofersen

Tofersen is a 20-base ASO designed with such a gap. The five sugar residues at the 5’ and 3’ ends are 2′-O-(2-methoxyethyl)-D-ribose while the inner ten are 2-deoxy-D-ribose [69]. As SOD1 in ALS likely acts through a toxic gain of function mechanism, the design of tofersen allows for recruitment of RNase H and degradation of the SOD1mRNA leading to a decrease in SOD1 protein concentration.

Pharmacodynamics

As ASOs such as tofersen are unable to cross the blood brain barrier, tofersen is administered as an intrathecal infusion with the proof of concept of intrathecal delivery of ASO established in a Phase I trial (the first such use of an ASO in humans) [70]. In this trial, an SOD1-targeted ASO (ISIS 333611, a precursor to tofersen), was administered intrathecally to 24 patients with SOD1 ALS at escalating doses. Of note, the delivery was given by an external pump over 11.5 h. The ASO was well tolerated with most adverse events (back pain and post-lumbar puncture syndrome) related to the administration.

In preclinical studies, treatment with tofersen was found to significantly reduce SOD1 mRNA and protein levels for up to 10 weeks following a single intrathecal dose. Further, in SOD1G93A rodent models, administration of tofersen increased time to disease onset, preserved motor function and increased survival [71]. Other biomarkers including compound motor action potentials (CMAP) and serum pNFH were also markedly improved compared with wild-type control. The effects on biomarker and survival were seen even when tofersen was given once the models were symptomatic. Finally, testing in a non-human primate model showed decreases in SOD1 mRNA and protein levels [71].

Since tofersen acts upstream of protein synthesis, it was also key to examine the kinetics of SOD1 to determine its half-life in the central nervous system (CNS) and CSF. Using a stable isotope-labeling kinetics (SILK) model, SOD1 was found to undergo slow turnover in the CSF and spinal cord of rats. Follow-up study in humans using this technique calculated an average SOD1 half-life of 25+/- 7.4 days [72]. Of note, mutant forms of SOD1 may have different kinetics [72].

Pharmacokinetics

CSF steady state concentration of tofersen in the Phase I–II clinical trial was achieved following the three initial loading doses [73]. While the elimination of tofersen and other ASOs has not been fully characterized, it is expected to undergo degradation via exonuclease (3’- and 5’)-mediated hydrolysis with an estimated CSF half-life of 4 weeks [74]. Tofersen is not known to interact with CYP enzymes, major transporters, or other drugs. Further, there are no recommended dosing changes for patients with hepatic or renal dysfunction [69].

Clinical efficacy

Phase I–II

Tofersen in SOD1 ALS has been evaluated in two clinical trials, a Phase I–II study [73] and the Phase III VALOR study [75]. The Phase I–II trial was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial that evaluated the safety of tofersen in SOD1 ALS. Trial participants were adults with ALS and a documented SOD1 mutation and forced vital capacity (FVC) of at least 50% the predicted value. Participants on riluzole had to remain on a stable dose and those receiving edaravone were excluded. Participants receiving edaravone were excluded as preclinical models had shown reduction in SOD1 levels in rodent spinal cords [43]. This effect could have potentially interfered with interpretation of changes in SOD1 due to tofersen.

Participants were randomized to either placebo or one of four doses of tofersen (20, 40, 60 or 100 mg) administered by intrathecal bolus injection. Doses were given on days 1, 15, 29, 57 and 85 of the trial. The primary outcome was the incidence of adverse events and the secondary outcome was change in CSF SOD1 concentration. Clinical measures, including change in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R), percentage change of predicted slow vital capacity, handheld dynamometry megascore, as well as CSF neurofilament light and heavy chain concentrations were exploratory outcomes. Analysis was performed on the modified intention-to-treat population (i.e., all participants who were randomized and received at least one dose of tofersen or placebo). Further post-hoc analysis was performed on a subgroup of participants with faster progressing disease (defined by having one of ten prespecified mutations known to associate with faster disease progression and a pre-randomization decline in ALSFRS-R of at least 0.2 points per month).

Participants receiving tofersen showed reduction in CSF SOD1 protein concentration of 1% in the 20-mg dose group, 27% in the 40-mg dose group, 21% in the 60-mg dose group and 36% in the 100-mg dose group. Participants receiving placebo had a 3% decrease in the same timeframe. CSF NfH and NfL were also decreased in the 100 mg dose group and stayed approximately the same in the placebo group. Overall, the Phase I–II trial showed that tofersen was well-tolerated (see safety section for further discussion). CSF biomarkers of pharmacodynamic efficacy (SOD1 concentration) and neurodegeneration (NfH and NfL) as well as several clinical markers showed trends toward improvement, particularly in the 100 mg dose group. Also important was the identification of the rapidly progressing ALS subgroup as a key part of clinical trial analysis for the drug. As alluded to above, ALS, including ALS associated with SOD1 mutations, is heterogeneous with marked variation in rates of progression [76]. Focusing on the participants with fast-progressing disease could allow for more predictable disease courses and further permit the detection of a clinical effect within the limited time frame of the clinical trial [77]. These lessons informed the design of the VALOR Phase III clinical trial.

Phase III

In the follow-up VALOR Phase III trial [75], the 100 mg dose of tofersen was tested in a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial. The primary analysis population was adults with SOD1 ALS with a pre-defined balance of faster-progressing and slower-progressing participants. Participants in the faster-progressing group were required to have slow vital capacity >65% of predicted and either an SOD1 mutation typically associated with fast progressing disease and a pre-study ALSFRS-R slope decline of >=0.2 per month or other SOD1 mutation with pre-study ALSFRS-R slope decline of >=0.9 per month. In contrast to the Phase I–II study, participants receiving edaravone were not excluded, but edaravone could not be infused (only the IV formulation was available during VALOR) on days of tofersen/placebo administration. Further, there was subgroup analysis based on the baseline concentrations of plasma NfL (above vs. below median).

The screening phase of the study lasted up to 4 weeks for each participant. Following this, participants received three doses of tofersen or placebo at 2-week intervals followed by five doses at 4-week intervals for an overall treatment time of 24 weeks. Sixty participants were calculated to be necessary for 84% power to detect a difference assuming ALSFRS-R difference from baseline of -4.8 in the tofersen group and -24.7 in the placebo group. Joint rank test was used to compare difference in the ALSFRS-R score.

The change in the ALSFRS-R total score in the faster-progressing group from baseline to week 28 was –6.98 points in the tofersen group and –8.14 points in the placebo group (difference, 1.2 points; 95% confidence interval [CI], –3.2 to 5.5; p = 0.97). In the slower-progressing group the changes in the ALSFRS-R total score were -1.33 in the tofersen group and -2.73 in the placebo group (difference, 1.4; 95% confidence interval [CI], -1.1 to 3.9). Given the lack of difference in the primary outcome variable, differences in secondary outcomes were considered nonsignificant per study protocol (Table 2). SOD1 CSF concentrations were reduced by 29% from baseline in the faster progressing group and by 40% in the slower progressing group. In the faster-progressing groups, plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL) decreased by 60% in the tofersen group compared with a 20% increase in the placebo group.

While the primary outcome measure in the initial trial was not met, the robust pharmacodynamic effect on SOD1 concentration as well as on plasma NfL led to further analysis of the data. A statistical model assessing the relationship of tofersen-mediated reduction in plasma NfL and disease progression was developed. In this model, a 10 pg/ml reduction in plasma NfL at day 114 of the VALOR trial was associated with a reduction of ALSFRS-R worsening at day 197 of 0.772 points (p = 0.00381). Interestingly, the model also showed that if participants in the tofersen group had been assigned to placebo, they likely would have experienced faster disease progression with an average of 3.83 points of further decline on ALSFRS-R over the 28 weeks of the VALOR study [80]. Subsequent case series and clinical cohorts have also shown decreases in plasma NfL in patients treated with tofersen with stabilization or improvement in ALSFRS-R or Medical Research Council strength scale (MRC) in in many cases [81–85].

That significant differences in outcome measures observed in the 12 month OLE population in the absence of observed differences in the 6 month Phase III VALOR trial suggests that a longer-duration trial may have shown significant differences between tofersen and placebo. This is in keeping with the half-life of SOD1 protein (about 25 days) in humans and the four week CSF half-life of tofersen, as it would take several months for reduced expression via tofersen to reduce mutant SOD1 concentrations. Balancing longer duration trials with the rapid disease progression associated with some variants of SOD1 ALS (e.g., ∼12–15 months from onset to death/ventilator dependence for A4V/A5V variant) is an ongoing area of debate in clinical trial design. Biomarkers such as NfL are being used to predict effects in shorter duration trials before clinical effects are apparent.

Future directions

There are several ongoing studies evaluating tofersen. One area of inquiry is the correlation between CSF and CNS tissue concentrations of SOD1 in patients treated with tofersen. Ongoing work with post-mortem samples is investigating changes in various areas of the CNS. As for clinical studies, NCT03070119 is an open-label extension study evaluating the long-term safety and tolerability of tofersen (estimated completion date of third quarter of 2024). NCT05725759 is a trial evaluating personalized rehabilitation programs for patients with SOD1 ALS treated with tofersen with functional assessments every 3 months.

NCT04856982, the ATLAS study [86], is enrolling carriers of SOD1 variants with high or complete penetrance and rapid progression who are clinically presymptomatic for ALS. These participants are monitored monthly for elevation in plasma NfL or emergence of clinical ALS. If a participant's plasma NfL exceeds the threshold of 44 pg/ml (and is up 10 pg/ml from baseline), the participant is screened and randomized to tofersen vs. placebo if they remain asymptomatic and without signs of clinically manifest ALS. Randomization is dynamic according to SOD1 variant, last plasma NfL, and age. Participants who develop clinical ALS are offered enrollment in an open-label study of tofersen. The primary outcomes are whether presymptomatic initiation of tofersen slows development of clinically overt ALS and whether presymptomatic initiation slows progression over time as compared with later start.

Other strategies are being explored to reduce expression of mutant SOD1. One such strategy is using a viral vector to express microRNA to suppress SOD1 [87]. Such a strategy would potentially allow for a treatment with one-time administration. This was tested in two patients by using intrathecal administration of an AAV vector expressing an anti-SOD1 microRNA. One patient had some improvement in strength and reduction in lumbosacral spine tissue SOD1 (but not CSF SOD1). The other patient had no change in clinical measures. Adverse events in this small proof of concept study included one patient with meningoradiculitis [87]. CRISPR gene editing offers another targeted approach to produce sustained reduction in SOD1 expression [88].

Enhancing delivery of ASOs to the CNS across the blood brain barrier is another area of active research and could allow for intravenous administration rather than repeat lumbar punctures. Strategies to accomplish this include nanoparticles [89], peptide conjugates [90], and DNA/RNA heteroduplexes [91]. Repeat lumbar punctures could also be reduced by utilization of an intrathecal pump (such as that used for baclofen) for delivery of ASOs. Finally, the specificity of ASOs could be improved by the use of allele-specific ASOs that would target only mutant mRNA transcripts [92].

The genetics of ALS continue to grow in clinical importance, a leading reason being the development of a targeted treatment for SOD1 ALS and similar therapies for other genetic forms of ALS (e.g., FUS) in various stages of clinical development [93,94]. With these rapid changes, genetic testing has become standard of care. Recent consensus guidelines have been published to help guide clinicians in this evolving area. These guidelines recommend that all ALS patients be tested at minimum for mutations in SOD1, C9orf72, FUS and TARDBP with additional gene testing to be added if specific treatments are developed. Accordingly, genetic counseling will remain a critical part of ALS care going forward [95].

Safety & tolerability

Overall, tofersen has been well tolerated in clinical studies and in early clinical use. In the Phase I–II clinical trial of tofersen, most adverse advents in the tofersen group were related to lumbar puncture (headache 42%, procedural pain 42%, post-lumbar puncture syndrome 34%) and occurred at similar rates to the placebo group. Five patients (out of 38) in the tofersen group had serious adverse events and two died (one from pulmonary embolism and the other from ALS-related respiratory failure). CSF pleocytosis was noted in 42% participants in the tofersen group compared with 8% in the placebo group [73]. It is important to note that CSF abnormalities, including elevations in protein and pleocytosis, in the absence of clinical signs or symptoms were not consistently considered an adverse event by site investigators.

Adverse events in the Phase III VALOR trial were similar, with most events related to ALS disease progression or side effects of lumbar puncture. The most common adverse events in the Phase III VALOR trial were headache, procedural pain and falls. In the tofersen group, 58% of participants had at least one instance of CSF pleocytosis (nucleated cell count >10) compared with 6% in the placebo group. The average CSF protein increased by 110 mg/l in the tofersen group and decreased by 15 mg/l in the placebo group [75]. There were 3 participants in the placebo group and 4 in the tofersen group that had clinically significant neurological adverse events (myelitis, aseptic meningitis, lumbar radiculopathy, increased intracranial pressure and papilledema). The participant with myelitis underwent treatment with glucocorticoids and plasma exchange with resolution of symptoms and imaging findings at 3 months. Regulatory affairs

Tofersen (brand name Qalsody) was approved by the FDA under the accelerated approval pathway on April 25, 2023. This pathway may be used by the FDA for drugs for serious conditions with unmet medical needs which have shown efficacy via a surrogate end point. Further randomized clinical trial data will be considered to confirm the clinical benefit of tofersen. Tofersen was also recently approved by the European Medicines Agency on 30 May 2024. Applications for approval are currently being considered in the United Kingdom and Japan.

Conclusion

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a fatal neurodegenerative disease of upper and lower motor neurons. Though several medications have been approved for clinical use by the FDA, the disease modifying effect for most of these agents remains modest. ALS attributed to mutations in specific genes offers therapeutic targets and an opportunity to study a less heterogeneous population. Tofersen, an ASO leading to RNAse H mediated degradation of SOD1 mRNA, has shown robust pharmacodynamic effects in reduction of SOD1 CSF protein concentration and plasma and CSF neurofilament levels. While the primary clinical outcome measure in the Phase III clinical trial (VALOR) was negative, further data from its associated open label extension as well as analysis of the original trial data shows slowing of functional decline and even stabilization in SOD1 ALS patients receiving tofersen. Further clinical trial data from the open label extension and the presymptomatic ATLAS study will be important in further characterizing the clinical efficacy of tofersen.
