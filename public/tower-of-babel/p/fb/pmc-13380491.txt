Multidrug-resistant Shewanella putrefaciens isolated from ascitic fluid in a patient with decompensated cirrhosis: A case report
Authors: Ziheng Wang, Jiaxu Qin, Rui Kong, Peng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShewanella putrefaciens is a rare opportunistic pathogen found in marine, freshwater, and soil environments. Although it is increasingly recognized as a cause of human infection, reports of NDM- and OXA-48-like carbapenemase-producing S. putrefaciens isolated from ascitic fluid in patients with decompensated cirrhosis remain exceedingly rare.Case presentationA 68-year-old man with chronic hepatitis B-related decompensated cirrhosis and diabetes mellitus was readmitted with progressive abdominal distension and oliguria. He had no history of seawater or freshwater exposure or raw seafood consumption. Ascitic fluid analysis showed markedly elevated nucleated cell counts, with calculated polymorphonuclear neutrophil counts consistently exceeding 250 cells/µL, supporting bacterial peritonitis. Ascitic fluid culture yielded S. putrefaciens, which was identified by MALDI-TOF MS with 99.9% confidence. Immunochromatographic testing and PCR confirmed production of NDM and OXA-48-like carbapenemases. The isolate was multidrug resistant, with resistance to most β-lactams, carbapenems, aztreonam, quinolones, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, but it remained susceptible to amikacin, tobramycin, and minocycline. Treatment was adjusted to colistin methanesulfonate combined with amikacin-based therapy, along with ascitic drainage and supportive care. Abdominal symptoms partially improved, but the patient requested discharge before completing antimicrobial treatment.ConclusionS. putrefaciens can cause clinically significant ascitic fluid infection in patients with decompensated cirrhosis, even without aquatic exposure. Accurate species identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and carbapenemase detection are essential for guiding targeted therapy.

Background

Shewanella putrefaciens is a Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic bacillus widely distributed in marine environments, freshwater, soil, and fish-associated habitats [1]. Although it was traditionally regarded as an environmental microorganism, it is increasingly recognized as an opportunistic human pathogen [2], [3], [4]. Reported infections most commonly involve the skin and soft tissues, ear, biliary tract, and abdominal cavity and may occasionally progress to invasive disease such as bacteremia [2], [3], [4], [5]. However, earlier clinical reports of S. putrefaciens should be interpreted cautiously, because some isolates may have been misidentified and may actually represent S. algae. This limitation highlights the importance of accurate species identification [2], [4].

Human infection caused by S. putrefaciens remains uncommon and is mainly described in sporadic case reports or small case series. Patients with hepatobiliary disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, malignancy, or immunocompromised status appear to be particularly susceptible [2], [3], [4], [5]. Previous reports indicate that this organism can cause a broad range of clinical manifestations, including bacteremia associated with terminal ileitis, splenic abscess, peritonitis, and bloodstream infection caused by multidrug-resistant strains [6], [7], [8], [9]. However, isolation of S. putrefaciens from ascitic fluid in patients with decompensated cirrhosis has rarely been reported. Ascitic fluid infection is one of the most serious complications of decompensated cirrhosis, so the pathogenic significance of any uncommon Gram-negative bacillus isolated from ascitic fluid should be evaluated in relation to host condition, clinical presentation, and laboratory findings [10]. Here, we report a case of bacterial peritonitis caused by PCR-confirmed NDM- and OXA−48-like carbapenemase-producing S. putrefaciens isolated from ascitic fluid in a patient with decompensated cirrhosis and diabetes mellitus, without a clear history of aquatic exposure.

Case presentation

The patient was a 68-year-old man who was admitted on March 11, 2026, with abdominal distension and fatigue. His medical history included chronic hepatitis B, decompensated cirrhosis, and diabetes mellitus. On admission, physical examination revealed positive signs of ascites and marked redness and swelling of the right lower limb. Lower-extremity infection was initially suspected, and linezolid therapy was started. Subsequent imaging demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm of the right anterior tibial artery with thrombus formation and possible compartment syndrome. Percutaneous coil embolization of the lower-limb artery was therefore performed on March 15. After postoperative improvement, he was discharged on March 25, 2026.

Five days after discharge, the patient was readmitted on March 30, 2026, with progressive abdominal distension and oliguria. Physical examination revealed abdominal distension, positive shifting dullness, and bilateral lower-limb pitting edema. He reported no seawater or freshwater exposure and no raw seafood consumption. Decompensated cirrhosis with ascites was diagnosed, and bacterial peritonitis was suspected. Abdominal paracentesis and drainage were performed, and empirical anti-infective therapy, diuretic treatment, and supportive care were initiated. Routine ascitic fluid analysis showed markedly elevated nucleated cell counts, with calculated polymorphonuclear neutrophil counts of 1880, 7650, and 2120 cells/µL on hospital days 2, 8, and 13 after readmission, respectively. On April 3, peripheral blood testing showed a white blood cell count of 13.3 × 10⁹/L, a neutrophil percentage of 92.7%, and a high-sensitivity C-reactive protein level of 125.75 mg/L. An ascitic fluid sample collected on April 2 yielded positive results in both aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles on April 6. The isolate was identified as S. putrefaciens by MALDI-TOF MS (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) with 99.9% confidence. The isolate exhibited a pronounced multidrug-resistant phenotype. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed resistance to ticarcillin, ticarcillin/clavulanic acid, piperacillin, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefepime, cefoperazone/sulbactam, aztreonam, imipenem, meropenem, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, norfloxacin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. It showed intermediate susceptibility to tetracycline, tigecycline, doxycycline, and colistin, but remained susceptible to amikacin, tobramycin, and minocycline (Table 1). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Because species-specific breakpoints for Shewanella spp. are not available, MIC interpretive categories were assigned by reference to CLSI M100 breakpoints for Enterobacterales where applicable. Tigecycline, colistin and cefoperazone/sulbactam were interpreted according to the automated system-derived criteria. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality-control strain.

Empirical imipenem/cilastatin therapy was initiated on April 1 at 1.0 g/day and continued until April 7 because bacterial peritonitis was clinically suspected. On April 6, the hs-CRP level remained elevated at 91.01 mg/L, and ascitic fluid analysis showed a positive Rivalta test with a total protein level of 22.2 g/L. After multidrug-resistant S. putrefaciens was identified from ascitic fluid, treatment was changed on April 7 to polymyxin E plus amikacin according to the susceptibility results. Polymyxin E was administered at 100 mg/day from April 7 to April 12, while amikacin was given at 0.4 g/day on April 7 and 0.6 g/day from April 8 to April 12 (Fig. 2). Discussion

Bacteria of the genus Shewanella have long been regarded as environmental microorganisms. However, increasing evidence shown that they can act as opportunistic pathogens and cause diverse clinical manifestations, including skin and soft tissue infections, biliary tract infections, intra-abdominal infections, bone and joint infections, and bacteremia [3], [4], [5].

In the present case, recovery of S. putrefaciens from both aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles inoculated with ascitic fluid, together with serial ascitic fluid polymorphonuclear neutrophil counts above 250 cells/µL and elevated systemic inflammatory markers, supported its role as the causative pathogen rather than an incidental contaminant. Patients with cirrhosis are at particularly high risk for intra-abdominal infection because of immune dysfunction, impaired intestinal barrier integrity, and increased bacterial translocation [11], [12]. Although Shewanella infections are frequently associated with seawater or freshwater exposure, raw seafood consumption, or disruption of the skin barrier, none of these exposures was identified in this patient [13]. Given his recent hospitalization and invasive procedures, healthcare-associated acquisition or intestinal translocation may be considered. Therefore, uncommon Gram-negative bacilli recovered from inflammatory ascitic fluid in high-risk hosts should be interpreted in conjunction with culture results, ascitic fluid cell counts, and clinical findings rather than dismissed as contaminants.

The isolate recovered in this case was resistant to commonly used β-lactams, carbapenems, and quinolones. Immunochromatographic testing demonstrated NDM and OXA−48-like carbapenemase production, while PCR detected the corresponding blaNDM and blaOXA−48-like gene families. A previous genomic study reported a hospital-environment isolate classified as S. putrefaciens SA70 that harbored chromosomal blaOXA−436 together with plasmid-borne blaNDM−1, demonstrating the coexistence of NDM and an OXA−48-like determinant in an isolate assigned to this species [14]. More broadly, Shewanella spp. have been proposed as progenitors and environmental reservoirs of blaOXA−48-like genes, raising concern about the potential mobilization of these determinants to other Gram-negative bacteria [15]. The dual carbapenemase findings were compatible with the carbapenem-resistant phenotype, whereas the broader multidrug-resistant pattern suggests additional resistance mechanisms that were not evaluated in this study. Treatment was guided by the limited susceptibility profile. Colistin methanesulfonate plus amikacin, ascitic drainage, and supportive care were associated with partial symptomatic improvement. In such patients, early and accurate species identification together with antimicrobial susceptibility testing is particularly critical for timely optimization of anti-infective therapy [13], [16].

This case report has several limitations. First, it describes a single patient, and therefore the findings cannot be generalized to all patients with cirrhosis or intra-abdominal infection. Second, species identification was based on MALDI-TOF MS without sequence-based confirmation. Although the confidence value was high, closely related Shewanella species can be difficult to distinguish. Third, although PCR detected blaNDM and blaOXA−48-like, their exact variants, genomic locations, and transferability remain unknown because sequencing and genomic analyses were not performed. Fourth, no environmental sampling or molecular epidemiological investigation was performed. Therefore, the source of infection and any possible healthcare-associated transmission could not be determined.

Conclusion

Shewanella putrefaciens can cause clinically significant bacterial peritonitis in patients with decompensated cirrhosis, even without a history of aquatic exposure. The emergence of multidrug-resistant isolates carrying blaNDM and blaOXA−48-like highlights the importance of accurate species identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and molecular detection of carbapenemase genes for guiding appropriate therapy and infection-control measures.
