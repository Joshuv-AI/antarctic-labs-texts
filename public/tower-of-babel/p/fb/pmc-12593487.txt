Deliberate use of mycophenolate mofetil in pregnant patients with lupus after the first trimester
Authors: Houssem Abida, Farah Tamirou, Frédéric Debiève, Frédéric A Houssiau
Published: 2025

Pregnancy increases the risk of flare in patients with SLE and is associated with excess fetal morbidity and mortality.1 The management of pregnant patients with lupus is difficult, partly because some medications used to control maternal disease are teratogenic. Thus, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), one of the most prescribed immunosuppressive agents in SLE, has been strongly associated with an increased risk of first trimester fetal loss and congenital malformations, especially external ear and other facial abnormalities including cleft lip with or without cleft palate, external auditory canal atresia, microtia, hypertelorism and micrognathia. Anomalies of the distal limbs, heart, oesophagus, kidney and nervous system have also been described.24 This MMF embryopathy, affecting up to 26% of exposed babies, has been observed even with low daily doses and after a short exposure period.

The mechanisms underlying MMF teratogenicity remain uncertain. Inhibition of purine synthesis (through blockade of inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, the MMF target) is invoked. More recent data also suggest that MMF inhibits the proliferation of human embryonic palatal mesenchymal cells by inducing expression of two microRNAs (let-7c-5p and miR-4680–3 p) and, thereof, by downregulating downstream genes.5

For these very reasons, MMF is always discontinued at least 6 weeks prior to conception, except in the case of unplanned pregnancy. That said, once organogenesis is completed, that is, after the first trimester of pregnancy, the use of MMF is theoretically safe. Consistently, the recent update of the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) points to consider for the use of antirheumatic drugs in reproduction and pregnancy concedes that ‘in severe, refractory maternal disease during pregnancy, …, in the second and third trimesters, …, MMF can be considered’.6 Yet, to the best of our knowledge, deliberate MMF use beyond the first trimester of pregnancy in patients with SLE has never been reported. Here, we retrospectively review the outcome of newborns who were exposed to MMF from the second trimester of pregnancy onwards to control maternal disease.

As described in figure 1, MMF was started between week 16 and 22 during eight pregnancies in six patients (mean age at the start of pregnancy: 31.9 years). All mothers suffered from severe refractory SLE and had been treated before pregnancy with several immunosuppressants, including MMF, either for lupus nephritis (five cases) or recurrent pericarditis (one case). MMF had been discontinued at least 8 weeks before pregnancy. The reason for restarting MMF after the first trimester was a severe flare during pregnancy (n=4) or a history of severe flare when MMF had been switched to azathioprine due to pregnancy planning (n=4). One fetal loss occurred at week 25 due to severe uncontrolled lupus nephritis requiring cyclophosphamide. The fetus did not suffer from MMF-induced embryopathy but from anasarca, and pathological examination of the placenta revealed signs of chronic ischaemia. The seven other pregnancies were successfully delivered (between week 34 and 39) and no MMF-induced embryopathy was observed at delivery. After a mean follow-up of 6.5 years, all living children had normal physical and neurological development.

In conclusion, although MMF is a potent teratogen strictly contraindicated in the first trimester of pregnancy, these few cases suggest that, in highly selected patients, MMF can be safely resumed after completion of organogenesis. Targeted time use of critical medications during pregnancy is standard for other conditions than SLE. Thus, pregnant women with mechanical heart valves are treated with heparin for the first trimester (as warfarin also has an associated embryopathy risk) and transitioned back to warfarin during the second and third trimesters until near term.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported series of patients with SLE who deliberately received MMF during pregnancy beyond the first trimester. Additional cases should be reported to support EULAR guidance on the use of MMF during the second and third trimester of pregnancy, if critically needed.6
