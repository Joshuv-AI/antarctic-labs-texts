Chicken viperin inhibits fowl adenovirus serotype 4 replication and affects related innate immune signaling pathways
Authors: Lijun Wan, Sheng Wang, Tingting Zeng, Can Wang, Houxun Ya, Hongyu Ren, Zuoxin Chen, Qing Fan, Meng Li, Zhixun Xie, Sisi Luo, Ming Yan, Liji Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFowl adenovirus serotype 4 (FAdV-4) is the main pathogen causing hydropericardium‑hepatitis syndrome (HHS), which seriously endangers the poultry industry. Viperin, an interferon‑stimulated gene (ISG), possesses broad‑spectrum antiviral activity, but its role during FAdV‑4 infection remains unclear. In this study, the chicken viperin gene was cloned and expressed, and its sequence characteristics, protein structure and tissue distribution were systematically analyzed. By overexpressing or silencing viperin in chicken hepatocellular carcinoma (LMH) cells, the effect of viperin on FAdV‑4 replication was investigated; meanwhile, this study examined the expression levels of multiple factors associated with innate immune signaling pathways under different treatment conditions, to investigate the regulatory role of viperin in the innate immune response induced by FAdV‑4. The main results showed that chicken viperin is a ∼41 kDa protein predominantly composed of α‑helices and random coils, and is highly expressed in immune organs. Overexpression of viperin significantly reduced FAdV‑4 viral load and titer, whereas knockdown of viperin promoted viral replication. The expression levels of several innate immune factors, including LGP2, MDA5 and MAVS, varied under different conditions. This study preliminarily demonstrates that chicken viperin inhibits FAdV‑4 replication. By measuring the expression changes of multiple innate immune factors under different conditions, we analyzed and conjectured the innate immune regulatory mechanism of viperin against FAdV‑4. This research provides an important theoretical basis and potential molecular targets for the design of antiviral prevention and control strategies in poultry as well as for the development of next‑generation vaccine adjuvants. Moreover, it offers research directions and experimental ideas for further in‑depth dissection of the molecular mechanism by which viperin inhibits FAdV‑4.Keywords: Fowl adenovirus serotype 4, Viperin, Antiviral, Innate immune signaling pathways

Introduction

When pathogens invade the body, innate immune cells can be rapidly recruited as the first line of defense against infection. Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the surface of these cells can specifically recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) carried by pathogens, thereby initiating a series of intracellular signal transduction events and triggering innate immune responses and inflammatory reactions to eliminate pathogens and maintain homeostasis in the body (Li et al., 2020). However, while the innate immune system is functioning, pathogens have evolved a variety of mechanisms to antagonize this process and achieve effective infection (Chen et al., 2024).

Interferons (IFNs) serve as critical bridges in innate immunity. Upon the recognition of invading viruses by PRRs, intracellular signaling cascades are triggered to induce the production of IFNs (Kawai and Akira, 2010; Ivashkiv and Donlin, 2014). IFNs exert antiviral effects primarily through the upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) (Borden et al., 2007). ISGs exhibit a diverse array of immunomodulatory functions. A subset of ISGs acts directly on critical steps of the viral replication cycle to suppress viral infection, thereby executing direct antiviral activity. Other ISGs contribute to indirect antiviral effects by potentiating viral recognition and immune responses in the host. Furthermore, numerous ISGs function as negative regulators that are induced following viral infection to preserve immune homeostasis and prevent tissue injury elicited by exacerbated immune reactions (Schneider et al., 2014). Therefore, ISGs play a central role in host antiviral immunity by targeting multiple stages of the virus life cycle to effectively restrict its replication and spread.

Viperin is an ISG that can be induced by IFN-I and IFN-III. It is also known as Cig5 and RSAD2 (Chin and Cresswell, 2001; Rivera-Serrano et al., 2020). Studies have shown that viperin is a virus-inhibiting protein closely associated with the endoplasmic reticulum. It has an interferon-inducing effect and can influence the pathogenic mechanism of viruses in various forms (Ghosh and Marsh, 2020). For example, the C-terminal domain of the human viperin protein exerts an anti-hepatitis C virus (HCV) effect. Specifically, the C-terminal domain of viperin interferes with the interaction between the C-terminal domain of the host protein hVAP-33 and the NS5A protein of HCV by interacting with it, thereby exerting its antiviral effect on HCV (Wang et al., 2012). Chicken viperin can inhibit the replication of Newcastle disease virus (NDV) by interacting with the matrix protein NDV (Shah et al., 2019). IBV infection induces upregulation of viperin expression in host cells, and viperin in turn inhibits viral replication by restricting cholesterol synthesis (Zhang et al., 2024). Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) viperin is the main HCMV effector that regulates lipid metabolism in human cells. HCMV can induce viperin to enter the mitochondria, where it interacts with the trifunctional protein and blocks its function, thereby interfering with the normal metabolism of cells and promoting the formation of the viral envelope (Seo and Cresswell, 2013). These findings indicate that viperin from different sources plays different roles in the antiviral responses caused by different viruses. These findings also highlight the complexity of the regulation of host antiviral responses by ISGs.

Fowl adenovirus serotype 4 (FAdV-4) is a double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the family Adenoviridae and the genus Aviadenovirus that can widely infect a variety of poultry, such as chickens, ducks and geese (Hess, 2000). Research indicates that FAdV-4 exhibits high pathogenicity in broilers between 3 and 5 weeks of age and serves as the primary etiological agent responsible for hydropericardium–hepatitis syndrome (HHS) (Rashid et al., 2020, 2024). HHS core features include the presence of clear, pale yellow fluid within the pericardial cavity, along with pathological changes such as hepatomegaly and jaundice, often leading to high mortality (Changjing et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2017). At present, this disease is highly prevalent worldwide, inflicting substantial economic losses on the poultry sector and severely hindering its healthy development.

The occurrence and development of HHS is closely related to the host immune response, which is essentially an imbalance of the severe inflammatory response in the host induced by FAdV-4 (Schachner et al., 2014). Existing studies have confirmed that although the heart is the primary site for disease-related lesions caused by FAdV-4, the virus does not directly act on cardiac tissue, nor does it necessarily induce myocardial apoptosis or local inflammatory responses; in contrast, the virus indirectly causes liver damage by inducing host cell apoptosis, an autophagic response and a strong systemic inflammatory response, thereby triggering a series of subsequent pathological symptoms (Niu et al., 2018, 2019a, 2019b; Yuan et al., 2021). In FAdV-4-infected host tissues, the expression levels of various inflammatory factors and interferon family members (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-α, IFN-β, and IFN-λ) are significantly upregulated, and their expression levels are positively correlated with tissue viral loads, indicating that inflammatory and interferon responses exert critical regulatory functions during FAdV-4 infection (Wu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2020). In vitro cell-based experiments have further verified the antiviral function of interferons: IFN-α and IFN-λ can effectively suppress FAdV-4 replication in chicken hepatocellular carcinoma (LMH) cells and significantly upregulate the expression of various ISGs, including MX1, PKR, IFIT5, viperin, et al (Lai et al., 2024). These results indicate that IFN-α, IFN-λ and their regulated ISGs play key roles in host immunity against FAdV-4 infection, providing an important theoretical basis for the subsequent development of FAdV-4 prevention and control strategies and related mechanistic research.

In preliminary experiments using the FAdV-4-GX2019-010 strain isolated in our laboratory, we observed the significant upregulation of viperin expression in LMH cells, suggesting that viperin may contribute to the host response to FAdV-4. However, the precise antiviral effect of viperin on FAdV-4 infection and its potential regulatory functions in the context of DNA virus sensing pathways remain unclear.

In the present study, we aimed to clone and express chicken viperin; systematically analyze its sequence characteristics, protein structure and tissue expression profile; and explore its functional properties. The effect of viperin on FAdV-4 replication was determined by overexpressing or silencing viperin in LMH cells followed by FAdV-4 infection. Moreover, the expression levels of multiple key innate immune molecules, including IFN-α, MAVS, IFN-β, IRF7, STING, IFN-γ, NF-κB, MDA5, IRF10 and LGP2 were detected in cells subjected to different treatments, including FAdV-4 infection alone, viperin overexpression or silencing alone, and FAdV-4 infection after viperin overexpression or silencing, to define the function of viperin within the innate immune response to FAdV-4 infection. This study will further improve our understanding of the structure and function of chicken viperin, expand its antiviral spectrum, and initially reveal its molecular mechanism against FAdV-4, thereby offering a rationale for creating new agents and vaccine adjuvants targeting avian viral infections.

Materials and methods

Viruses, cells and animals

The FAdV-4 strain used in this study (FAdV-4-GX2019-010, GenBank:MW439040.1) was previously isolated from a diseased chicken in Guangxi and is maintained in our laboratory. LMH cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin (Beyotime, China). Specific-pathogen-free White Leghorn chickens were hatched from embryos purchased from Merial Vital Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). At 14 days of age, tissue samples (including blood, spleen, intestine, skin, heart, liver, lung, bursa of Fabricius, thymus, glandular stomach, gizzard, muscle, weasand, brain, kidney, and joint) were collected from three healthy chickens for subsequent analysis.

Bioinformatics analysis and plasmid construction

The chicken viperin gene sequence was retrieved from the NCBI database. Secondary and tertiary structures were predicted using NPS@: GOR4 and SWISS-MODEL, respectively. The primers (viperin-U and viperin-D, Table 1) for cloning the full‑length viperin CDS were designed based on the sequence under accession number EU427332.1, covering the complete open reading frame (1065 bp). The viperin was cloned into the pEF1α-Myc vector. The recombinant plasmid was confirmed by sequencing (Ruibo Xingke, Guangzhou) and transfected into LMH cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Overexpression of viperin was verified by Western blotting 24 h post-transfection.

RNA interference and gene expression analysis

Three siRNAs targeting chicken viperin (si418, si902, si1148) and a non-targeting control siRNA (siNC) were designed and synthesized (Table 2) (Suzhou GenePharma). Transfection into LMH cells was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen). The most efficient siRNA was selected by real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR 48 h post-transfection. Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissue samples using a GeneJET RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA), and cDNA was synthesized with a Maxima H Minus cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). The specific primers (Table 3) used for qPCR of hexon, viperin, LGP2, MDA5, IFN‑α etc. were designed to span exon–exon junctions to avoid genomic DNA amplification, and their specificity was confirmed by BLAST analysis against the chicken genome, showing no significant homology to other genes. Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR was conducted using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Scientific) with specific primers. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with GAPDH as the reference.

DNA extraction (for detection of FAdV‑4 hexon gene)

Viral DNA was extracted from the supernatant of infected cells and cell lysates using the Thermo Scientific GeneJET Viral DNA and RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific) with the DNA extraction protocol provided by the manufacturer.

RNA extraction and RNA reverse transcription (for gene expression analysis of viperin, LGP2, IFN‑β, etc.)

RNA was extracted from cells, blood, intestine, skin, heart, liver, lung, bursa of Fabricius, thymus, spleen, glandular stomach, gizzard, muscle, weasand, brain, kidney and joint samples using the Thermo Scientific GeneJET RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a Thermo Scientific MaximaTMH Minus cDNA Synthesis Master Mix with a dsDNase Kit (Thermo Scientific).

Western blotting

Protein samples were collected from transfected LMH cells 24 h post-transfection, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with anti-Myc antibody (Beyotime) followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime). Protein bands were visualized using a DAB substrate kit (Beyotime).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted at least three biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test or multiple t tests with Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli false discovery rate (FDR) correction via GraphPad Prism 8.0. Statistical significance was denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Differences with P > 0.05 were regarded as non-significant and left unlabeled.

Results

Cloning and bioinformatics analysis of viperin

Using cDNA reverse transcribed from LMH cell mRNA as a template, the viperin gene fragment was amplified with the primers viperin-U and viperin-D. An approximately 1065 bp fragment was obtained (Fig. 1). NCBI-Blast analysis confirmed that this sequence represents the full-length coding sequence of the chicken viperin gene, located on chromosome 3, with a CDS of 1065 bp encoding 354 amino acids.

Cloning of the viperin gene and verification of the recombinant plasmid‌. 1: DL5000 DNA marker; 2: PCR amplification product of chicken viperin. The band indicated by the red arrow (1065 bp) corresponds to the amplified viperin CDS.

Primary structure analysis indicated that the viperin protein comprises 354 amino acids with a predicted molecular weight of approximately 40.598 kDa (Fig. 2A). Secondary structure prediction using GOR4 revealed that alpha helices accounted for 40.96%, extended strands for 16.38%, and random coils for 42.66% (Fig. 2B, C). A three-dimensional structural model generated via SWISS-MODEL showed a GMQE score of 0.80 and 85.71% coverage compared with the crystal structure of mouse viperin bound to CTP-SAH (PDB: 6Q2P) (Fig. 2D–F).

Tissue distribution of viperin

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR was used to assess viperin expression across various chicken tissues, with muscle as the reference. The expression levels of viperin in 16 tissues or organs from low to high were as follows: muscle, joint, gizzard, skin, weasand, glandular stomach, intestine, kidney, heart, brain, spleen, blood, bursa of Fabricius, liver, lung, and thymus. Among these tissues, the expression level in the thymus tissue, which was the highest, was more than 8000 times higher than that in the muscle tissue, which was the lowest, indicating that its expression exhibits significant tissue specificity (Fig. 3).

Viperin reduced FAdV-4 replication

To evaluate the effect of viperin on FAdV-4 replication, LMH cells were transfected with pEF1α-Myc-Viperin and subsequently infected with FAdV-4. Western blotting using an anti-Myc antibody confirmed viperin protein expression (approximately 41 kDa) in transfected cells, whereas no signal was detected in empty vector controls (Fig. 4A). Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR revealed a significant increase in viperin expression—approximately 300,000-fold higher than in controls (P < 0.001)—following transfection (Fig. 4B). Viral load, assessed via hexon gene expression, was markedly reduced in viperin-overexpressing cells (P < 0.05), and viral titers in the supernatant were also significantly lower (P < 0.01), confirming that viperin overexpression inhibits FAdV-4 replication (Fig. 4C, D).

Among three siRNAs designed to knock down viperin, si418 exhibited the highest silencing efficiency (Fig. 4E). LMH cells transfected with si418 showed increased viral RNA levels and titers upon FAdV-4 infection (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01), further supporting the inhibitory role of viperin in viral replication (Fig. 4F, G).

Impact of viperin on innate immune signaling during FAdV-4 infection

These results indicate that chicken viperin can inhibit the replication of FAdV-4, but the specific mechanism underlying this inhibition remains unknown. LMH cells were used as the model, and the following treatment groups were established to further explore the mechanism by which viperin inhibits FAdV-4 replication: 1) infection with FAdV-4; 2) overexpression (transfection of pEF1α-Myc-Viperin) or knockdown (transfection of si418) of viperin, along with the corresponding controls (transfection of the empty pEF1α-Myc plasmid or siNC); and 3) infection with FAdV-4 after the overexpression or knockdown of viperin. Changes in the expression of genes such as LGP2, MDA5, MAVS, IFN-α, STING, IRF7, IFN-β, NF-κB, IRF10 and IFN-γ were detected. The results showed that FAdV-4 infection led to varying degrees of downregulation of all the aforementioned genes in LMH cells (Fig. 5). In LMH cells, following either overexpression or knockdown of viperin alone, the mRNA levels of ten innate immune genes (LGP2, MDA5, MAVS, STING, IFN-α, IFN-β, IRF7, NF-κB, IRF10, and IFN-γ) were measured. Compared with their corresponding controls (empty vector or siNC), none of these genes showed statistically significant changes in expression (P > 0.05 after FDR correction) (Fig. 6). In the context of FAdV-4 infection, viperin overexpression substantially upregulated the expression of all the genes detected (up to approximately 3 × 10⁶ times), whereas viperin knockdown significantly weakened the upregulation of these genes (up to only approximately 2 times) (Fig. 7). In summary, viperin can significantly affect the expression of key molecules in the innate immune signaling pathway and has a strong immune-enhancing effect in cells infected with FAdV-4, suggesting that it may regulate the innate immune response to inhibit viral replication.

Due to the supraphysiological expression level (≈300,000-fold vs. endogenous), the upregulation of immune genes shown in Fig. 7A should be interpreted as a phenotype observed under artificial, supraphysiological overexpression conditions, not necessarily reflecting genuine gain-of-function at physiological levels. Discussion

In the present study, the chicken viperin gene was cloned, and the recombinant protein was successfully expressed. This finding is consistent with the identification results of chicken viperin reported by Goossens et al. (2015). Meanwhile, we have further conducted additional exploratory research in this study. The secondary and tertiary structures of viperin were predicted. The secondary structure of the chicken viperin protein is composed mainly of the following three elements: random coil (42.66%), which accounts for the highest proportion; α-helix (40.96%), which is the second most abundant; and extended strand (16.38%), whose content is the lowest. Chicken viperin is predicted to be a typical protein that is composed mainly of α-helices and random coils. Such structural features generally indicate that it may contain an enzymatically active center or specific binding sites, which are often located in random coil regions, providing important structural biological clues for further elucidating the molecular mechanism underlying the ability of chicken viperin to restrict viral infection in vivo (Daggett and Fersht, 2003; Tome-Stangl et al., 2017). For tertiary structure homology modeling, the crystal structure of the mouse Viperin–CTP–SAH complex (PDB: 6Q2P) was used as a template, yielding a high-confidence model with a GMQE value of 0.80 and coverage of 85.71%. Viperin is an evolutionarily ancient and conserved immune protein belonging to the radical S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) enzyme superfamily (Gizzi et al., 2018; Wein and Sorek, 2022; Culbertson and Levin, 2023). Understanding the relevant structural and functional characteristics of the viperin protein from other species is highly important for the study of viperin. Research has shown that the active sites of viperin in rats and mice are consistent and can convert cytidine triphosphate (CTP) into the RNA chain terminator 3′-deoxy-3′,4′-dideoxy-CTP (ddhCTP), thereby inhibiting the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase of dengue fever, West Nile virus, Zika virus and hepatitis C virus (Fenwick et al., 2017; Fenwick et al., 2020). These findings provide insights for research on the structure and function of viperin in poultry. The model developed in this study shows that the complete viperin protein of chickens retains the abovementioned core structural features, suggesting that it may possess CTP-to-ddhCTP catalytic activity similar to that of homologous proteins in mammals. However, FAdV-4 is a DNA virus, and its replication does not rely on the virus-encoded RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp). Therefore, the direct chain termination mechanism of ddhCTP does not fully explain the antiviral effect observed by our research group, leading to the exploration of the nonenzymatic regulatory function of viperin.

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR revealed that the expression of viperin differed significantly across 16 chicken tissues: viperin expression in the thymus was more than 8000-fold higher than that in muscle tissue, and immune organs such as the bursa of Fabricius, blood and spleen also presented high expression levels. Goossens et al. also reported high viperin expression in the thymus, bursa of Fabricius and spleen (Goossens et al., 2015).A recent study by Lai et al. confirmed that recombinant chicken IFN-α and IFN-λ could dose-dependently induce the expression of viperin in LMH cells and significantly inhibit FAdV-4 replication. Moreover, IFN-α exhibited a stronger ability to induce ISG expression than IFN-λ (Lai et al., 2024). These findings are highly consistent with the tissue expression profile identified in the present study: immune organs, including the thymus, bursa of Fabricius, blood and spleen are not only regions with active IFN signaling but also sites with high basal viperin expression. Collectively, the aforementioned evidence suggests that the differential tissue-specific expression of viperin may be maintained by the local IFN tone and directly determines the inherent resistance of different organs to FAdV-4 infection. Given that IFN-α/λ can induce the expression of viperin and other ISGs and suppress FAdV-4 replication, exogenous IFN preparations may represent a potential intervention for HHS. This study provides a molecular explanation for the mechanism underlying the effect of IFN therapy.

Viperin has an extremely broad antiviral spectrum, covering DNA viruses (e.g., HCMV and HSV-1) and RNA viruses (e.g., influenza virus, HCV, and HIV) (Shaveta et al., 2010; Chaumont et al., 2024). Studies in fish have also confirmed that viperin inhibits the replication of viruses such as VHSV and SVCV (Chaumont et al., 2024). In this study, viperin expression was bidirectionally regulated by overexpression (pEF1α-Myc-Viperin) and RNA interference (si418), providing definitive evidence for its antiviral function: viperin overexpression markedly reduced the viral load (hexon gene level) and decreased the viral titer in the supernatant, whereas the knockdown of endogenous viperin promoted viral RNA expression and viral amplification. The present study extends the antiviral spectrum of viperin to the avian adenovirus FAdV-4, further supporting the conclusion that viperin is an evolutionarily conserved broad-spectrum antiviral effector molecule.

At present, how chicken viperin exerts its antiviral effect after FAdV-4 infection is still unknown. This study preliminarily explored the regulatory function of viperin in the innate immune signaling pathway and analyzed its potential molecular mechanism underlying the inhibition of FAdV-4 replication.

LGP2, MDA5, and RIG-I are key components of the retinoic acid-induced gene I-like receptor (RLR) family. LGP2 has a high degree of homology with MDA5 and RIG-I. Chickens lack RIG-I. LGP2 does not have independent signal transduction activity but can promote the activation of the MDA5 signal by binding to viral RNA (Uchikawa et al., 2016). Further studies have shown that LGP2 can recognize infectious bronchitis virus (IBV) and inhibit its replication (Wang et al., 2021). MAVS and STING are key proteins for the transduction of signals downstream of the recognition receptor. IRF3/7 and NF-κB are transcription factors that are activated during signal transduction and transcribe interferons. Chickens lack IRF3. Studies have shown that IRF7 is the most important interferon-β regulatory factor in chickens and that IRF7 is involved in the regulation of IFN-β mediated by chicken STING and chicken MAVS (Huang et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2019). IRF10 was discovered in birds and fish but is absent in mammals. Studies have shown that in chickens, IRF10 plays a role in the later stage of antiviral defense by regulating the expression of IFN-γ target genes (Nehyba et al., 2002; Santhakumar et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2024).

We investigated the effects of isolated viperin modulation on the mRNA expression of multiple innate immune genes in resting LMH cells. As shown in Fig. 6, compared with control groups, overexpression or knockdown of viperin induced no statistically significant alterations in the mRNA levels of the detected genes, namely LGP2, MDA5, MAVS, STING, IFN-α, IFN-β, IRF7, NF-κB, IRF10 and IFN-γ (P > 0.05). This result indicates that under our experimental conditions (LMH cells without exogenous stimulation), independent regulation of viperin alone cannot trigger remarkable responses of the tested innate immune genes. Previous studies have reported that viperin-driven induction of type I interferons and interferon-stimulated genes displays cell-type specificity (Kim et al., 2019), and this induction usually requires activation of upstream signaling pathways such as TLR7/9 or DNA-sensing cascades (Saitoh et al., 2011; Crosse et al., 2021). Accordingly, we speculate that in LMH cells, viperin may need to cooperate with other signaling molecules or exert immune regulatory functions only at specific infection stages.

Upon FAdV-4 infection, viperin overexpression markedly upregulated all tested innate immune-related genes (LGP2, MDA5, IFN-α, MAVS, STING, IFN-β, IRF7, NF-κB, IRF10 and IFN-γ), with the expression of some genes elevated up to approximately 3 × 10⁶-fold (Fig. 7A). Knockdown of viperin resulted in a modest (<3-fold) increase in the expression of these immune genes compared to the siNC control, which was far weaker than the tens of thousands of fold upregulation triggered by viperin overexpression during FAdV-4 infection (Fig. 7B). Collectively, these data suggest that viperin expression level may serve as a vital determinant for sustaining robust interferon and innate immune responses during FAdV-4 infection.

Hou et al. found that FAdV‑4 replicates more efficiently in LMH cells, yet it induces only limited interferon‑stimulated gene (ISG) expression. In contrast, FAdV‑4 infection in CEF cells triggers a robust antiviral response, including the upregulation of cytosolic DNA sensing pathways and ISGs. Knockdown of key cytosolic DNA sensing molecules enhanced FAdV‑4 replication in LMH cells while simultaneously reducing ISG expression (Hou et al., 2024). Gao et al. recently uncovered the molecular mechanism underlying FAdV-4 immune evasion: FAdV-4 infection activates the chicken cGAS-STING signaling pathway, while the viral non-structural protein ORF1B directly binds IRF7 and blocks its nuclear translocation, thereby suppressing type I interferon responses mediated by the cGAS-STING axis. Functional assays further verified that IRF7 overexpression potently inhibits FAdV-4 replication, whereas IRF7 silencing facilitates viral proliferation (Gao et al., 2025). These findings provide direct mechanistic references to interpret our observations: the downregulation of STING, IRF7 and downstream interferon genes post FAdV-4 infection (Fig. 5); the recovery and upregulation of STING, IRF7 and downstream interferon genes when viperin is overexpressed alongside FAdV-4 challenge; and the lack of gene changes when viperin is modulated without viral infection (Fig. 7). We speculate that viperin regulates the STING-IRF7 signaling pathway in the innate immune response triggered by FAdV-4 infection. Whether viperin exerts functional antagonism against ORF1B sheds new light on exploring the pathological mechanism of viperin-mediated anti-FAdV-4 activity.

Studies conducted by Chaumont et al. in cyprinid fish also overturned the traditional view that viperin merely acts as a positive immune regulator. Instead, viperin functions as a fine-tuning negative modulator that regulates the expression of inflammation-related genes (including NF-κB pathway inhibitors) and participates extensively in cellular metabolic processes (Chaumont et al., 2024). Our observation that viperin modulates the innate immune signaling network shares common ground with these results, demonstrating that viperin possesses far more complex and diverse functions than initially assumed, and may exert distinct regulatory roles across different species and cellular microenvironments.

In this study, we preliminarily validated the inhibitory effect of viperin on FAdV-4 replication via viperin overexpression and silencing strategies. By measuring expression variations of multiple innate immune factors under different treatments, we analyzed the antiviral mechanism of viperin against FAdV-4 and its potential roles in innate immunity. This research has several limitations. First, commercial chicken-specific monoclonal antibodies targeting hexon and viperin are unavailable, precluding protein-level validation of transcriptional data. Second, no positive control for type I IFN induction (e.g., poly(I:C)) was included, so insufficient detection sensitivity cannot be fully excluded. Third, to reliably capture antiviral phenotypes in the transient expression system, a relatively high plasmid transfection dosage was applied. Transient overexpression raised viperin mRNA abundance to roughly 300,000 times the endogenous level, greatly exceeding physiological ranges, which may cause off-target effects or non-specific immune activation. Accurate control of physiological dosage is hard to achieve with plasmid transfection, as plasmids enter cells in thousands of copies. The current work represents a phenotype observed under supraphysiological overexpression conditions. In subsequent studies, we plan to employ a tetracycline-inducible system to precisely control the expression level of viperin within the range of its endogenous induction fold change post-infection, in order to fully validate its antiviral function. Meanwhile, a few key genes (such as hexon, viperin, IRF7, or STING) will be selected, and we will attempt to verify the mRNA-level results using custom antibodies, mass spectrometry, or by switching to other model systems for which antibodies are available. Meanwhile, positive controls such as poly(I:C) will be added in future experiments to evaluate the sensitivity of the detection system. In summary, this study provides solid theoretical foundations and candidate molecular targets for designing poultry antiviral prevention strategies and developing next-generation vaccine adjuvants, and offers novel ideas and directions for further investigation into the mechanism by which viperin combats FAdV-4.
