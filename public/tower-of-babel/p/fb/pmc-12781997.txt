Evaluating markers for methamphetamine use in electronic health records
Authors: Emily Bacon, Ryan Loh, Jenai DeNardo, Deborah Rinehart, Scott A Simpson, Alia Al-Tayyib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Methamphetamine is an addictive stimulant with serious health effects and its use has been increasing in the United States. Electronic health records (EHRs) capture timely information on substance use and are increasingly harnessed for public health surveillance and research. Identifying methamphetamine use in EHRs is challenging because existing metrics, such as diagnosis codes, are non-specific to methamphetamine, and clinicians record methamphetamine use in different ways. Little guidance exists about capturing methamphetamine use in EHRs.Methods:We identified eight markers of methamphetamine use in the EHR of an integrated, safety-net hospital system. We evaluated markers based on individual or combined likelihood of accurately capturing methamphetamine use and computational lift to extract from the EHR. We conducted chart reviews to calculate the predicted probability of identifying true methamphetamine use for each group of markers.Results:We reviewed 814 charts across eight groups of methamphetamine markers. Positive predictive values (PPVs) of electronic definitions ranged from 0.21 to 1.00, with four groups obtaining PPVs > 0.70 compared to chart review. Groups of methamphetamine markers varied substantially in lift; the easiest was extracting non-specific diagnosis codes and the most challenging was capturing mentions of methamphetamine use in free-text clinical notes.Conclusions:Results provide multiple options to consider when extracting data on methamphetamine use from EHRs with guidance on computational lift and complexity of different markers. Users can select specific definitions of methamphetamine use depending on the project, technical capacity, time, and EHR system. More accurate definitions of methamphetamine use can inform interventions to curb its use.Keywords: Methamphetamine use, Electronic health records (EHRs)/ electronic medical records (EMRs), Substance use disorders

Introduction

Methamphetamine is a stimulant associated with serious health effects, including psychosis, mood disorders, severe impacts to cardio-vascular and pulmonary systems, overdose, and death (Rusyniak, 2011; Courtney and Ray, 2014). Methamphetamine use increased by approximately 40 % in the United States between 2015 and 2019 with concomitant increases in related overdoses and deaths (Han et al., 2021; Palamar et al., 2020). These concerning trends are exacerbated in healthcare settings by challenges identifying at-risk patients as well as substantial variation in use across clinical service areas. For example, estimated population prevalence for methamphetamine use is around 0.7 %, but in settings such as emergency departments (EDs) or among outpatient mental health clients, prevalence can be as high as 13–34 % of patients (Jones et al., 2020a, 2020b; Sibanda Bn et al., 2019; Xing et al., 2024; Pocuca et al., 2025; Olatunji et al., 2025). Even if the challenges of identifying methamphetamine use are overcome, additional treatment barriers exist, including a lack of pharmacological treatment and limited access to evidence-based treatments such as contingency management (Moszczynska, 2021; Brown and DeFulio, 2020).

Developing and delivering interventions for methamphetamine use disorder require accurate and timely estimates of methamphetamine use. Electronic health records (EHRs) can provide such data and are increasingly used for public health surveillance as well as identifying patient characteristics, potential interventions, and barriers for substance use disorders (Simpson et al., 2023a; Ghitza et al., 2013; Tai et al., 2012). Despite the power and prevalence of EHRs, specifically identifying methamphetamine use is difficult, particularly across multiple clinical service areas. In the ED, patients presenting with methamphetamine-induced psychosis may not be captured as having used methamphetamine as its symptoms often overlap with those of other psychiatric and substance use disorders (Glasner-Edwards and Mooney, 2014; Camfield et al., 2024). Additionally, the most common urine drug immunoassay screens nonspecifically indicate the presence of any amphetamine-type substance and may be positive for commonly prescribed medications such as bupropion or dextroamphetamine. Furthermore, the International Classification of Disease, Tenth Edition, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis codes for methamphetamine use disorder are the same as the diagnosis codes for a caffeine use disorder: “F15: Mental and behavioral disorders due to use of other stimulants, including caffeine.” (Bloor and Sgouros, 2019) Diagnosis codes for “mental and behavioral disorders due to use of other stimulants, including caffeine” have an estimated positive predictive value (PPV) between 65 % and 85 % for identifying methamphetamine use (Bertholf et al., 2016; Shearer et al., 2021). However, for situations requiring high sensitivity (e.g., clinical trials, implementation of novel treatments) or contexts where stimulant diagnosis codes are inconsistently applied (e.g., acute care settings), (Camfield et al., 2024; Simpson et al., 2023b) diagnosis codes alone may not be sufficient.

There is a dearth of evidence demonstrating whether and how multiple methamphetamine-related markers within an EHR can be used to obtain a more accurate estimate of methamphetamine use in a patient population across multiple clinical service areas. Development, implementation, and dissemination of effective clinical treatments for methamphetamine addiction and related disorders requires greater understanding of the epidemiology of methamphetamine use. In this study, we used qualitative and quantitative methods to identify markers of methamphetamine use in the EHR of a large, urban, integrated safety-net healthcare system. We then evaluated several of these promising case definitions for accuracy and computational lift compared to a manual chart review. Findings from this study can provide quality improvement, research, and evaluation efforts around methamphetamine use with better tools and multiple options to identify patients using methamphetamine in EHRs.

Methods

Setting

Denver Health and Hospital Authority (DH) is an integrated urban safety net healthcare system that provides preventive, primary, and acute care services for the City and County of Denver, Colorado. DH serves one-third of Denverites annually including over 14,000 unhoused patients, 10,000 patients with food insecurity and 75,000 patients without insurance in 2023. The system includes a 525-bed medical center; an Adult Level One Trauma Center; paramedic and public health divisions; and correctional care health services. Addiction services include opioid use disorder (OUD) treatment and contingency management programs; an American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) level 3.2 withdrawal management program for adults; and a level 3 withdrawal management program for adolescents. The system has a substance use treatment clinic with an Opioid Treatment Program. All patients entering outpatient behavioral health services (OBHS) are screened for SUDs.

Population

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients aged 18 or older who had at least one completed encounter at DH with any clinical service area between January 1st and December 31st, 2021. Any patient with an index encounter in 2021 was then evaluated retrospectively for a methamphetamine marker between 2018 and 2021.

Identification of methamphetamine use markers

To identify markers in the EHR that might indicate a patient was using methamphetamine, we used multiple approaches. We conducted a literature review of methamphetamine markers extracted from EHRs in other health systems, convened a local working group of data scientist and clinician subject matter experts (SMEs) with extensive experience in addiction medicine, and conducted qualitative interviews with clinicians from across the health system to understand their processes of identifying methamphetamine use and documentation processes within the EHR. Supplementary Table 1 has a breakdown of interview participants. We conducted thirteen semi-structured interviews with clinicians from these service areas: medical inpatient, inpatient addiction consult liaison service, inpatient psychiatry, OBHS services, primary care, infectious disease clinic, sexual health clinic, and withdrawal management. Respondents included eight physicians, three licensed social workers, and two nurse practitioners. The interview guide is included in Supplementary Figure 1.

Clinicians reported diverse practices for documenting methamphetamine use based on their clinical setting and specialty. Thus, we were concerned that relying solely on the F15 ICD-10 diagnosis code for “mental and behavioral disorders due to use of other stimulants, including caffeine,” would both undercount patients using methamphetamine and also imprecisely include patients using other types of stimulants.

After engaging SMEs and conducting semi-structured interviews, we identified eight markers of methamphetamine use available in the EHR (Table 1). We categorized the eight methamphetamine markers into four categories: diagnoses, positive lab results, positive assessments, and clinical notes. A diagnosis included either any F15 ICD-10 code or a clinical impression, which was a subset of text fields available for clinicians to select that included diagnostic criteria from sources such as ICD-9, ICD-10, and Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine (SNOMED) codes. A positive lab result included either a rapid, hospital-based urine drug screen (UDS) or a more specific, outsourced UDS conducted primarily for patients seen in OBHS. The outsourced UDS returns a methamphetamine-specific result. Positive assessments reflect multiple ways in which a patient might self-report methamphetamine use. We identified three high-value targets for analysis: a positive result for methamphetamine in the Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) conducted in the ED; methamphetamine as one of the top two substances reported on the OBHS intake form; and methamphetamine as one of the top three substances reported during the intake process for withdrawal management or residential treatment. The final marker category was a mention of methamphetamine use in clinical notes.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from the EHR and stored on a secure computer in the facility. Data cleaning and analyses were performed using Python (Goerzen, 2004). The algorithm to determine if free-text notes contained relevant mentions of methamphetamine was as follows: For each patient in the dataset, all clinician-written notes were extracted from the EHR. The notes were then queried for the presence of “meth” or “methamphetamine”, while excluding the match if phrases were present like “method,” “denies”, “no”, or “negative” that were frequently associated with false positives. Any note templates or lab order results associated with negative screens for methamphetamine were also removed. This process ensured that data elements identified through structured means (urine drug screens) are not double counted as a marker for also being included in the notes section. Finally, a random sample of notes were quality checked by researchers to identify any additional patterns that could be systematically removed. The presence of “methamphetamine” in the note was then converted to a binary variable for use as a marker. All markers were coded as binary for an encounter (e.g., Yes/No the patient encounter had any F15 diagnosis). Lack of a methamphetamine marker did not necessarily indicate that the marker did not exist or the patient was not using methamphetamine but instead that this information could not be systematically extracted from the EHR. Demographic data were extracted for patients who were chart reviewed, including patient age, sex, gender identity, race, and ethnicity.

Chart review

To understand how well markers correctly identified patients using methamphetamine, we conducted chart reviews of 100 randomly selected patients with a specific marker or certain combinations of markers across 8 groups, for a total of 814 charts for 713 unique patients.1 Patients could be included in more than one review group if they had the relevant markers and were randomly selected more than once. Reviewers double reviewed approximately 20 % of all charts for each group. After reviews concluded for each group, we discussed any disagreements in the 20 % of charts that were double reviewed. This reconciliation process aligned reviewers on the chart review criteria and disagreements were rare.

For each chart, reviewers completed an extraction form that included the following information: patient medical record number, date of most recent methamphetamine marker, result of review, and any other notes or comments about the patient. Reviewers could select from five results of the review: 1) patient using methamphetamine between 2018 and 2021; 2) patient denies methamphetamine use or no methamphetamine use found; 3) Patient used methamphetamine in the past, but no marker was identified in the 2018–2021 period; 4) Patient has family history of methamphetamine use, but is not currently using methamphetamine in the 2018–2021 period; 5) Other (please specify). After completing the forms for each patient, reviewers recorded the result of the review in a spreadsheet for analysis.

Due to resource constraints, we did not conduct chart reviews for each methamphetamine marker independently and in combination. Based on conversations with the team of SMEs, we identified eight groups to chart review a priori. The groups reflect a range of considerations, including ease of extraction, likelihood of accurately capturing methamphetamine use based on feedback from SMEs and interview respondents, and how often the marker was used. Table 2 describes the eight chart review groups. If a marker category was used in a group, any marker in that category could be present. For example, Group 6 could include a patient with any positive assessment (e.g., ASSIST score >0) or a positive lab result from the outsourced UDS. The number of patients varied across groups from 755 with only a positive assessment or outsourced UDS to 12,399 with only a mention of methamphetamine use in clinical notes. Based on qualitative feedback from SMEs and clinicians, two groups were hypothesized to have low or low/moderate accuracy in identifying methamphetamine use, with three markers hypothesized to have moderate accuracy in identifying methamphetamine use and two groups hypothesized to be highly accurate. Two groups were a low lift to calculate from a data extraction and computation perspective, with two groups considered a moderate lift and four groups considered a high lift (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to understand characteristics of the patient population randomly selected for review. We calculated the PPV as a metric for comparing accuracy of identifying methamphetamine use across the 8 review groups. We calculated the PPV by dividing the patients determined to be using methamphetamine between 2018 and 2021 (numerator) by the total number of charts reviewed for that marker (denominator). Less than 5 % of our demographic data was considered missing or otherwise uncountable (Table 3). As mentioned above, a lack of clear methamphetamine use in chart reviews does not necessarily indicate that the patient was not using methamphetamine, only that we did not have enough information to conclude their use. This is reflected in the PPV calculations as a person not using methamphetamine. This study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB).

Results

In 2021, a total of 510,885 unique patients were seen at DH. If any encounter for a patient seen in 2021 had one of the eight identified methamphetamine markers between 2018 and 2021, that encounter was extracted from the EHR and included in the analytic dataset. Of all patients seen at DH in 2021, 29,602 unique patients (5.8 %) had at least one methamphetamine marker between 2018 and 2021.

Based on the groups, 814 charts for 713 unique patients were randomly sampled for chart review. Table 3 shows demographic characteristics for the randomly selected patient population compared to those with methamphetamine markers not selected for chart review and the total patient population with an encounter at Denver Health in 2021. Chart reviewed patients were more likely to be younger (average age 38.2) compared to those not reviewed (36.6) and the total patient population (37.9), with a higher percentage of males (62 %) compared to those not reviewed (59 %) and the total patient population (50 %), with non-Hispanic White race/ethnicity (57 %) compared to those not reviewed (50 %) and the total patient population (38 %). Chart reviewed patients were less likely to be Hispanic (28 %) compared to those not reviewed (33 %) and the total patient population (35 %).

The eight marker combination groups demonstrated a wide range of accuracy in identifying patients using methamphetamine between 2018 and 2021 (Table 2). The groups ranged in accuracy from a PPV of 0.21 (hospital UDS alone) to a PPV of 1.00 (clinical impression and positive assessment/OBHS UDS). We hypothesized that hospital UDS alone and clinical notes alone would have the lowest accuracy of identifying patients using methamphetamine. This was supported by chart review results. We hypothesized that a clinical impression alone or any stimulant-related diagnosis paired with either the less-precise hospital UDS or clinical notes would be moderately accurate in identifying patients using methamphetamine. While this was confirmed by chart review for a clinical impression alone and any stimulant-related diagnosis paired with a hospital UDS, the combination of any stimulant-related diagnosis and clinical notes had the second highest PPV of 0.85. We hypothesized that the combination of a clinical impression and a positive assessment or methamphetamine-specific OBHS UDS would have the highest accuracy in capturing patients using methamphetamine. We also hypothesized that any positive assessment or OBHS UDS would be highly accurate, although the overall number of patients with these markers was relatively low (N = 755, 2.6 %). Chart review confirmed that both of these groups of markers were highly accurate in identifying patients using methamphetamine (PPV=1.00, 0.79, respectively). Calculating each marker category required different amounts of time and computational resources. Searching for methamphetamine-related terms in unstructured data points such as clinical notes, along with the additional data cleaning associated with removing the false positives associated with unstructured data took hours of computational time. Alternatively, pulling structured data like diagnosis codes can occur in seconds. Adding the rapid hospital-based UDS to any stimulant-related diagnosis was only moderately more complex and produced fairly accurate results (PPV=0.74). The clinical impression, which first used a search term to identify diagnosis most likely related to methamphetamine, was only slightly more accurate than using any stimulant-related diagnosis (PPV=0.57 vs. 0.61, respectively) but took substantially more effort to identify and extract from the EHR. Positive assessments reflect patient-reported methamphetamine use. While there were relatively few encounters with positive assessments and the more accurate OBHS UDS’ and these data were more time consuming to extract, most of the patients with any of these markers were using methamphetamine between 2018 and 2021 (PPV=0.79).

Discussion

Accurately understanding the public health impact of methamphetamine use and its effect on individual patients’ care is of urgent importance for developing effective intervention strategies, yet there is little understanding of how well existing tools identify patients with methamphetamine use disorder across diverse clinical settings. Our results demonstrate that reliance on a diagnose code alone has limited accuracy in identifying the prevalence of methamphetamine use in a large urban health system.

More accurate measures of methamphetamine use can be captured by combining diagnosis codes, patient screeners, and laboratory results. Our findings advance efforts to select a “gold standard” for identifying methamphetamine-related healthcare encounters. Each of these markers can be used in concert to realize a better understanding of which patients are at risk for methamphetamine-related outcomes, direct treatment interventions, and track progress in addressing the methamphetamine epidemic. While this gold standard may not be accessible to each individual clinician, a primary goal of the method described is to outline how including additional markers in identifying this population can have massive increases in the accuracy of that identification. Clinicians and health system leaders can leverage these findings to assist work with their local analysts or EHR delivery services identifying the best approach.

We developed a probabilistic model recognizing that different applications may require different diagnostic performance. For example, for projects involving clinical trials or specific treatment protocols, it may be important to have a conservative definition of methamphetamine use with few false positive cases. For other projects, a broader definition that captures all patients who may be using methamphetamine is acceptable and definitions of methamphetamine use may include patients with multiple markers. The false positive rate declined when multiple markers were combined—but fewer patients with possible methamphetamine use were identified. Similarly, some clinical contexts may be better suited for specific definitions of methamphetamine use. Shearer and colleagues used similar chart review methods and found that an F15 billing diagnosis code on an inpatient admission was associated with a PPV of 0.78–0.86 (Shearer et al., 2021). In outpatient settings, it is common for health conditions such as diabetes or hypertension to require two diagnoses to confirm the condition (Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services, 2025). In our study, requiring 2 or more outpatient F15 diagnoses would have resulted in a 33 % decrease in the number of patients identified (n = 9,404 vs. n = 14,082). Because of the stigma, lack of treatment, and inadequate and inconsistent coding and screening tools, using more stringent diagnostic criteria, especially in outpatient settings, is likely to increase the false negative rate in methamphetamine use identification. Interviews with clinicians demonstrated the resulting variety of strategies for identifying and documenting methamphetamine use across the health system (Supplementary Table 1).

In addition to combining multiple methamphetamine markers, we also considered the data extraction process and computational resources required to extract each marker from the EHR. Some markers, such as a mention of methamphetamine use in clinical notes, require substantial effort to devise an appropriate algorithm and considerable computational resources to generate each time the marker is needed. Moreover, extracting from free-text codes requires continual code maintenance as documentation habits evolve. However, clinical notes were the most common and least accurate marker of methamphetamine use. Other markers—such as assessments – require front-end work to construct a reliable definition but are thereafter enduring and quickly extracted. These considerations will reflect the available resources, timelines, and usage of specific projects.

These results also speak to the complexity of diagnosing methamphetamine use in clinical practice. Single markers, and especially non-specific urine drug screens, are of limited utility in identifying patients with problematic methamphetamine use. For example, we found that only 21 % of patients who only had a positive hospital UDS between 2018 and 2021 were using methamphetamine. Many of these patients were taking amphetamine/dextroamphetamine for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or a similar condition, which resulted in the positive amphetamine UDS result. Furthermore, the stigma experienced by patients with SUDs may result in underreporting of use during a single episode of care (Simon et al., 2020). The more complex models validated here can obviate the limitations of any one test, patient report, or care encounter.

Our approach to classification merits replication in other health systems. Additionally, future efforts could combine emerging artificial intelligence (AI) tools, similar to those used to identify OUD, (Garbin et al., 2023; Soley et al., 2025) to improve efficiency and accuracy of identifying methamphetamine use in clinical notes through more complex and comprehensive linguistic analyses. Concomitant quality improvement efforts could target clinician education to steer clinicians towards markers such as diagnosis codes that are more accurate and extractable, and improve access to patient screening tools to better capture self-reported use.

Limitations

This analysis has several limitations. There is no gold standard for determining the presence of methamphetamine use in a retrospective chart review. We used a time-consuming standardized chart abstraction and standardized data extraction to improve interrater reliability, and the uncertain validity of this evaluation led us to build a probabilistic rather than deterministic model. Moreover, we could not easily discern the intensity of methamphetamine use. Some clinicians reported that patients would deny using methamphetamine even when clinicians suspected use; in these instances, we deferred to the patient’s report and indicated that the patient was not using methamphetamine. Our methodology was limited to a single point in time, and we do not have insight into how markers or use may change over time. The result of these limitations is that our estimates likely undercount the prevalence of methamphetamine use in this population and decreased the accuracy of some groups. Our study also used a more time-intensive process for generating methamphetamine related terms in clinical notes. Advancements in natural language processing (NLP) algorithms have made much of this manual work faster and potentially more exhaustive (Garbin et al., 2023; Soley et al., 2025). Efforts are ongoing to build infrastructure within the hospital system that is better able to automatically identify these key markers. Finally, our results may not be generalizable to dissimilar health systems with variations in frequency of methamphetamine use or documentation and diagnostic practices. However, other health systems can use this information as a launching point for deciding the best way to capture methamphetamine use for specific projects.

Conclusion

Defining the extent of methamphetamine use, particularly for healthcare systems and across diverse communities, is challenging. Understanding the scope of the methamphetamine epidemic and identifying which patients are at greatest risk are critical for disseminating evidence-based interventions, designing new treatments, and realizing success in reducing the morbidity and mortality associated with this epidemic. Accurate identification of methamphetamine use has direct implications for resource planning for hospital systems so they are better equipped to provide integrated care services, including addiction consults and peer navigation.

Our work suggests promising approaches to better identifying methamphetamine use in EHRs. Diagnosis codes hold promise for accurately identifying patients but are imprecise for methamphetamine. While the newest ICD-11 classification does not explicitly separate amphetamines from methamphetamine, (World Health Organization, 2024). future classification system (e.g., SNOMED) updates should allow greater precision in identifying methamphetamine use. Diagnosis codes, laboratory testing, and standardized screening instruments should be utilized to discriminate among different stimulants, and clinicians should consider the prevalence of methamphetamine use in their communities when consider PPVs of relevant tests. Ultimately, widespread adoption of more specific codes and screeners would improve accuracy for detecting methamphetamine addiction.
