Exploring shared biomarkers and their mechanisms in thyroid cancer and systemic lupus erythematosus via bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Ruijiao Liu, Xiangying Dong, Yinan Lu, Tongtong Wang, Licui Zhang, Chen Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), an autoimmune disorder, is linked to a heightened risk of multiple malignancies, including thyroid cancer. Thyroid cancer is the most prevalent malignancy of the endocrine system, and its autoimmune-related pathological features render it an optimal subject for investigating the mechanisms of their comorbidity. The molecular mechanisms underlying this comorbidity are still ambiguous. The accurate diagnosis and treatment of thyroid cancer urgently necessitate innovative molecular targets that extend beyond conventional pathological characteristics. This study seeks to employ integrated bioinformatics approaches to elucidate potential shared molecular mechanisms and immunological features between thyroid cancer and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), aiming to enhance understanding of their comorbidity and identify novel intervention targets.MethodsThis study initially acquired gene expression data for TC and SLE from the GEO database and subsequently screened and identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) shared by both diseases. Subsequently, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome functional enrichment analyses on these 46 shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and further assessed the activation status of pertinent pathways using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Subsequently, we employed CIBERSORTx to examine immune infiltration patterns and developed protein-protein interaction networks utilising the STRING database. We identified hub genes utilising the MCODE and cytoHubba plugins and visualised the findings with Cytoscape software. We additionally assessed the diagnostic efficacy of these core hub genes in an independent dataset utilising ROC curves and investigated their prognostic relevance in thyroid cancer through Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression. Ultimately, we employed the Network Analyst platform to forecast transcription factor-gene and miRNA-gene regulatory networks and identified potential targeted therapeutic compounds utilising the DSigDB database.ResultsThis study identified 46 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) commonly linked to thyroid cancer and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which were significantly enriched in signalling pathways associated with immune-inflammatory activation, type I interferon responses, and complement pathway activation. Moreover, GSEA findings validated that immune-inflammatory and autoimmune-related pathways are markedly activated in both conditions. Twelve hub genes were discerned through protein-protein interaction networks. Analysis of immune infiltration indicated that thyroid cancer and systemic lupus erythematosus exhibit a shared characteristic of innate immune dysregulation, marked by the infiltration of myeloid cells (neutrophils, M0/M2 macrophages). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified six significant core hub genes with substantial diagnostic value: C1QB, LCN2, C1QC, LTF, VSIG4, and C3AR1. Univariate survival analysis indicated that elevated expression of C1QC and C3AR1 significantly enhances overall survival in thyroid cancer patients; however, multivariate COX regression analysis revealed that their independent prognostic significance necessitates further validation. This study predicted the interaction networks of transcription factors and miRNAs regulating key genes, with LCN2 demonstrating the highest connectivity to miRNAs, and identified candidate therapeutic compounds linked to it.ConclusionThis study employed bioinformatics analysis to identify critical shared hub genes and molecular pathways connecting thyroid cancer and systemic lupus erythematosus, offering novel insights into their shared pathogenesis and the advancement of targeted biomarkers and therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Thyroid cancer, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Core hub genes, Tumor immune microenvironment, Bioinformatics analysis

Introduction

A prevalent systemic autoimmune illness, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is defined by aberrant immune system activation that results in a persistent inflammatory state that affects several organs and systems [1]. There are between 13 to 7,713.5 instances of SLE per 100,000 individuals. The disease’s frequency has been steadily rising over time, and it mainly affects young women [2, 3].Over the years, a great deal of research has been done on its disease mechanisms, but the precise etiology is still unknown. It is now thought to be caused by a confluence of factors acting on a background of genetic predisposition, including immunological dysregulation, hormonal imbalances, and environmental impacts [4].Additionally, epidemiological research has demonstrated that SLE is closely linked to a number of systemic problems in addition to being an autoimmune disease [5]. SLE has a wide range of complicated clinical symptoms. A butterfly rash on the face, joint discomfort and swelling, serositis, kidney injury, and hematological abnormalities are typical symptoms. Patients’ quality of life and long-term prognosis can be greatly impacted by the disease’s course, which is frequently marked by alternating periods of remission and recurrence [6]. Corticosteroids and immunosuppressants continue to be the mainstays of treatment for SLE; no curative, disease-specific therapies are currently available, and long-term medication side effects and organ damage from the disease itself continue to be significant obstacles in clinical management [7].Clinical observations show that people with SLE have a markedly higher risk of acquiring lymphoma, lung cancer, thyroid cancer, and other types of cancer in addition to organ damage brought on by the disease itself [8, 9].

It is important to note that thyroid cancer (TC) is one of the cancer forms linked to a markedly elevated risk among autoimmune illnesses [10, 11]. According to cohort studies, SLE patients had a 52% greater chance of dying from cancer and a 62% higher overall cancer incidence. Cancer is a prominent co-morbidity that affects the long-term prognosis of SLE patients due to their markedly increased risk of acquiring malignancies in specific areas, such as the thyroid [12]. Following tumor removal surgery, some SLE patients with concomitant tumors obtained a clinical remission of their lupus symptoms, according to clinical case reports [13]. This study’s focus on thyroid cancer offers a distinct research advantage, as the thyroid is a target organ commonly impacted by autoimmune damage, in contrast to the prevalent association between SLE and various cancer types. TC is a prototypical inflammation-associated neoplasm, and its pathological feature of significant immune cell infiltration within the tumor lesions corresponds closely with the systemic chronic autoimmune inflammatory microenvironment of SLE. Moreover, clinical epidemiological data demonstrate that the incidence of thyroid cancer in SLE patients is increasing annually, implying a possible causal relationship between the two conditions. Therefore, there is considerable research merit in exploring the shared connection between them. A meta-analysis found that TC is more common in patients with SLE, suggesting that the two conditions may interact biologically or share some pathogenic pathways [10, 14]. The underlying mechanism of this connection may be closely related to the shared traits of immunological dysregulation and chronic inflammation in both illnesses. According to recent research, immune cell infiltration, dysregulation of the complement system, and abnormal activation of the type I interferon signaling pathway are important factors in the mechanism of disease of both solid tumors and SLE, hinting at a possible shared molecular pathway basis [15–18].

The molecular processes connecting TC and SLE are still unknown despite the previously cited clinical evidence of a relationship, especially in light of the dearth of systematic, integrated analyses carried out at the genome-wide level. An efficient strategy to methodically find shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and signaling pathways is through bioinformatics techniques [19]. Nevertheless, the majority of current research has concentrated on the examination of specific illnesses, and there are still few studies that combine multi-omics data to methodically look at the genetic mechanisms that these two illnesses share. In order to determine the biological pathways and molecular roles of the DEGs that TC and SLE patients shared, this study employed enrichment analysis. To find core hub genes, we built networks of protein-protein interactions (PPI). We examined immune infiltration patterns at the cellular level to better clarify the immunological implications of shared genetic characteristics. The diagnostic and prognostic worth of the core genes were thoroughly examined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. We explored their regulatory networks upstream as well. Our research will offer novel insights into the biological connection between malignant tumors and autoimmune diseases, and it may serve as a theoretical foundation for the development of treatment approaches that target similar targets.

Materials and methods

Sources of public data

The study’s workflow is shown in Fig. 1. To find gene expression profiles in patients with TC and SLE, we searched the Gene Expression Online Resource (GEO) using terms like “thyroid cancer” and “systemic lupus erythematosus” (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [20]. Only gene expression profiles from case and control groups make up the chosen analytical datasets. The entries are arranged in series and subjected to array-based expression analysis; they are exclusively derived from human samples. Specifically, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, which more accurately reflect the state of the systemic immune system, are included in all SLE datasets. The training datasets that were ultimately chosen were GSE50772 (GPL570; consisting of 61 SLE samples and 20 healthy control samples) and GSE33630 (GPL570; consisting of 60 THCA samples and 45 normal samples). Additionally, because of their moderate sample sizes, balanced disease-to-control ratios, and consistency with the tissue origins of the discovery dataset, we chose GS81622 (GPL10558; comprising 30 SLE samples and 25 healthy control samples) and GSE29265 (GPL570; comprising 29 THCA samples and 20 normal samples) as validation datasets.

Flowchart for research design

Identification of differences in gene expression

GEO2R(http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/) is an R-based web application, enables to use the “limma” package to search for DEGs in every GEO dataset. We divided GSE33630 and GSE50772 into the disease group and the control group, respectively, based on the samples’ disease status. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR), and an adjusted P-value (adj.P.Val) < 0.05 together with |log₂FC| ≥ 1 were used as the thresholds for identifying significantly differentially expressed genes. Furthermore, probe IDs without matching gene symbols and genes with multiple probe sets will be eliminated independently.

Weshengxin(http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn), an online platform for data analysis and visualization, produced the volcanic map visualization of DEGs [21] .Next, we created a Venn diagram to show the DEGs shared among the different GSE datasets using the Venn online platform༈http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/༉. This study analyzed each dataset independently to prevent non-biological variation from disparate tissue sources and chip platforms, thus eliminating the need for batch correction methods like ComBat and SVA. Quantile normalization and background correction were executed for each dataset utilizing the limma package, and a consistent screening threshold (P < 0.05, |log₂FC| ≥ 1) was implemented. Ultimately, only genes exhibiting significant differences in both datasets were preserved, thus reducing false positives attributable to platform and batch variations.

DEG functional enrichment analysis

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis and DAVID(https: //david.ncifcrf.gov/) for Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation were used to examine the chosen DEGs, A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was established. Go offers three levels of analysis: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Pathway enrichment analysis is used to find pathways that exhibit adequate DEG enrichment in comparison to the whole-genome background. KEGG is the main public pathway information repository. The Reactome database offers extensive and detailed pathway coverage along with hierarchical annotation, facilitating a systematic comprehension of the biological regulatory processes involving genes. Enrichment analysis was conducted using the ReactomePA R package on the GSE33630 and GSE50772 datasets, based on 46 differentially expressed genes, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05, to identify significantly enriched Reactome pathways. The R package “ggplot2” is used to visualize the results.

GSEA pathway gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) is extensively employed to investigate the molecular mechanisms of diseases and the activation patterns of signaling pathways alongside their biological significance. This study conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on the thyroid cancer dataset (GSE33630) and the systemic lupus erythematosus dataset (GSE50772). We generated a ranked list of genes according to the log₂(fold change) values derived from comparisons between each disease group and the normal control group. The “clusterProfiler” R package was employed to conduct GSEA analysis for the systematic assessment of pathway activation discrepancies between the two groups. KEGG pathway gene sets from the MSigDB database served as reference gene sets. The iterations were configured to 1,000, while all other parameters remained at their default settings. A false discovery rate (FDR) q-value of less than 0.05 was employed as the threshold for significant enrichment.

PPI network creation and key gene identification

The Strength of Interaction Gene Retrieval (STRING)(http://string-db.org/) tool was used to create a PPI network of DEGs. Cytoscape༈https://www.cytoscape.org/༉ was used to visualize the PPI network. After choosing core genes with the highest scores (MCODE > 4) using Cytoscape’s Molecular Complex Identification (MCODE) method༈http://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/mcode༉,we utilized the CytoHubba plugin’s MCC algorithm to determine the top 15 genes. In the end, the genes found by both algorithms were verified as trustworthy hub genes and demonstrated with Venn diagrams.

Examination of immune cell infiltration and its association with important genes

CIBERSORT, a traditional deconvolution algorithm, uses gene expression profiles to quantify the different immune cell types that infiltrate the tumor microenvironment or other pathological tissues [22] .The CIBERSORT algorithm is used to examine the ratios of 22 immune cell types in peripheral blood samples from the GSE33630 and GSE50772 datasets. In CIBERSORT, P-values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant and incorporated into the analysis. To prevent bias arising from inherent disparities in immune components between thyroid tissue and peripheral blood, we refrained from directly comparing absolute immune cell fractions across tissues. We examined immune modifications in the disease group and the control group independently within each dataset and compared the uniform immune patterns exhibited by TC and SLE. The relationship between immune cell infiltration and core hub genes was evaluated using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. The R package’s heatmaps, butterfly plots, and box plots were used to visualize the data.

Verification of survival analysis and core shared genes

The GSE81622 and GSE29265 datasets were used to validate the hub genes’ diagnostic potential. To verify the sensitivity and specificity of the key genes found in the aforementioned analysis for the diagnosis of TC and SLE, plot ROC curves, compute the area under curve (AUC) values for the key genes, and compare them with data from TC and SLE. Create ROC curves using the R package “pROC,” and compute and compare sensitivity and specificity quantitatively. The Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/) server then provided us with important details regarding important hub genes. A well-liked online tool for evaluating the relationship between genes of interest and survival rates in patients with different cancer types is the Kaplan–Meier Plotter. (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) [23] With the exception of biased arrays, patient samples were split into two groups according to the median expression of the important genes found using the screening procedure described above; a p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. To assess the independence of C1QC and C3AR1 as prognostic factors for thyroid cancer while accounting for potential confounding variables, gene expression profiles and associated clinical follow-up data from the TCGA-THCA cohort were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were developed utilising the “survival” package in R. The model utilised the expression levels of C1QC and C3AR1 as primary independent variables, while integrating clinical and pathological variables, including age, sex, and TNM staging, as covariates. The independent prognostic influence of each factor was evaluated by computing the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI), with P < 0.05 denoting statistical significance.

Analysis of Hub gene networks

GeneMANIA (http://www.genemania.org/) was used to create a co-expression network of six core hub genes. This technique is frequently used to uncover intricate relationships and interactions between gene sets, which advances our knowledge of the functional connections between key genes [24].

Construction of transcription factor–miRNA gene networks

We created networks of transcription factor-gene and miRNA-gene interactions using the Network Analyst website (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/). Using the Network Analyst platform, transcription factor-gene interaction networks were constructed using the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net). Additionally, we created visual interaction networks for transcription factor( TF)-gene and miRNA-gene pairs using Cytoscope after identifying important miRNAs that affect protein expression by interacting with hub gene transcripts using the TarBase database.

Gene-drug interaction analysis

We used the Enrichr platform (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) to analyze potential therapeutic drugs that interact with important genes. We then used the Drug Signature Database (DSigDB) to screen for relevant drugs, ultimately choosing the top ten drugs.

Results

Screening for shared variant genes

Using a threshold of adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction) and |log₂FC| > 1, we used GEO2R to filter out significant DEGs between the control and disease groups in each dataset that we collected from patients with TC and SLE. To confirm that the identified shared degree is not attributable to organizational or platform heterogeneity, as outlined in the Methods section, all analyses were performed independently on each dataset before cross-validation. The GSE33630 dataset contained 494 DEGs, of which 252 were downregulated and 242 were upregulated (Fig. 2a). 570 DEGs, comprising 279 downregulated and 291 upregulated genes, were found in the GSE50772 dataset (Fig. 2b). We discovered 46 common DEGs shared by patients with SLE and TC using a Venn diagram to cluster DEGs across the two datasets (Fig. 2c).

DEGs’ functional enrichment

While KEGG enrichment identifies the link between genes and pathways, GO enrichment analysis usually clarifies the relationship between genes and their functions. We grouped the top 10 KEGG pathways and important GO terms into three subcategories (Cell Constituents [CC], Molecular Functions [MFs], and Biological Processes [BP]) in order to gain a better understanding of the biological functions of the identified DEGs. The findings indicated that DEGs were markedly enriched in BP including innate immune responses, negative regulation of viral genome replication, positive regulation of angiogenesis, extracellular matrix organization, apoptotic process, and antibacterial humoral responses (Fig. 3a). The pronounced activation of the “innate immune response” pathway, alongside the “downregulation of viral genome replication” pathway, indicates that the type I interferon response may be a crucial factor connecting SLE and TC. Concerning CC, DEGs are chiefly linked to specific granule membrane, the complement component C1q complex, extrinsic component of presynaptic membranes, and specific granule lumen (Fig. 3b). Enriched MFs among DEGs encompass binding to RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription factor binding, serine-type endopeptidase activity, and identical protein binding (Fig. 3c). Moreover, KEGG pathway analysis underscored the enrichment of immune signaling pathways, notably the IL-17 signaling pathway, the complement and coagulation cascades, the relaxin signaling pathway, and pathways in cancer (Fig. 3d), suggesting their pivotal role in the cellular signaling mechanisms that govern chronic inflammation and immune responses. We conducted a Reactome pathway enrichment analysis to achieve pathway annotations with extensive coverage and detailed hierarchical structure, facilitating a multidimensional examination of the biological processes associated with core differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The findings indicated that the common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the TC dataset were markedly enriched in pathways including Neutrophil degranulation, Class I MHC-mediated antigen processing and presentation, and Transcriptional Regulation by TP53 (Fig. 3e). Reactome enrichment analysis of the SLE dataset revealed that the shared DEGs were significantly enriched in Neutrophil degranulation, MAPK family signaling pathways, Interleukin-4 signaling, Interleukin-13 signaling, among others (Fig. 3f). The differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in both disease datasets within essential pathways, including Neutrophil degranulation, Transcriptional Regulation by TP53, MAPK family signaling cascades, Class I MHC-mediated antigen processing and presentation, and PIP3 activation of AKT signaling. The enhancement of shared pathways indicates that SLE and TC exhibit analogous immune-inflammatory dysregulation and abnormalities in associated signaling pathways, suggesting a potential connection between the two in terms of pathogenesis.

GSEA enrichment analysis and pathway activation

This study conducted GSEA on the thyroid cancer and systemic lupus erythematosus datasets to examine the activation status of pertinent pathways at the genome-wide level. In TC, the GSEA results indicated significant enrichment for systemic lupus erythematosus, complement and coagulation cascades, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and autoimmune thyroid disease, all demonstrating pronounced positive activation. This indicates that autoimmune responses, complement system activation, and the JAK-STAT inflammatory signaling pathway are markedly upregulated in thyroid cancer (Fig. 4a). In SLE, GSEA demonstrated significant enrichment in systemic lupus erythematosus, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, indicated by positive enrichment scores. This suggests the activation of innate immune responses, cytokine signaling, and pathways associated with autoimmunity in SLE (Fig. 4b). The results indicate that thyroid cancer and systemic lupus erythematosus exhibit similarities in the activation of associated immune and inflammatory pathways. This indicates that the two conditions may possess analogous immune irregularities and inflammatory mechanisms, offering a novel perspective for comprehending the intrinsic pathway linkages between the two diseases.

Construction of PPI networks and identification of Hub genes

We conducted a PPI-based network analysis of the DEGs utilizing the STRING database. Figure 5a illustrates that the PPI network comprises 43 nodes interconnected by 52 edges, exhibiting a confidence score of 0.4, thereby demonstrating the intricate interrelationships among the DEGs. The STRING file was imported into Cytoscape to create a protein interaction network; a higher score corresponds to a darker circle and a larger area (Fig. 5b). The MCODE plugin identified 14 potential hub genes (score: 4.154), which include C1QB, MMP, MMP1, ISG15, LCN2, LY6E, C1QC, LTF, SLPI, VSIG4, USP18, IFI27, C3AR1, and PTX3 (Fig. 5c). Additionally, utilizing the Cytoscape cytoHubba plugin, we identified 15 prospective hub genes (Fig. 5d) through the Maximum Cluster Centrality (MCC) algorithm (C1QC, LCN2, ISG15, FOS, MMP9, MMP1, C1QB, PTX3, CDC20, C3AR1, IFI27, VSIG4, CKS2, SLPI, LTF). Subsequently, genes common to both the cytoHubba and MCODE methodologies were identified. The Venn diagram illustrates that the two algorithms collectively identified 12 hub genes: C1QB, MMP9, MMP1, ISG15, LCN2, C1QC, LTF, SLPI, VSIG4, IFI27, C3AR1, and PTX3 (Fig. 5e). IFI27, as a sensitive signal amplifier in the type I interferon pathway, directly affects the expression level of interferon-mediated inflammation in the disease. LCN2 precisely indicates the infiltration and activity of innate immune cells within the lesion and is integral to tumorigenesis and immune regulation, whereas VSIG4, an immune checkpoint molecule found on macrophages, adeptly connects inflammatory activation to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Immune cell infiltration and its correlation with principal genes

The examination of the functions and pathways of pivotal genes related to TC and SLE demonstrated a strong association with inflammatory and immune mechanisms. We employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to evaluate the infiltration of 22 immune cell types in both diseases and analyzed the correlations between these pivotal genes and immune cells in TC and SLE.

Spearman’s correlation coefficient was employed to evaluate the relationship between pivotal genes and immune cells in TC and SLE. A heatmap visually represents the correlation analysis between the 12 principal genes and immune cell infiltration. In TCs, MMP9, C1QB, C1QC, C3AR1, VSIG4, MMP1, IFI27, and ISG15 exhibited robust positive correlations in resting natural killer cells, monocytes, resting memory CD4 T cells, M0 macrophages, activated memory CD4 T cells, neutrophils, M1 macrophages, memory B cells, and naive B cells (Fig. 6b). In SLE, IFI27, ISG15, PTX3, MMP9, SLPI, VSIG4, C3AR1, LCN2, LTF, C1QB, C1QC, among others, exhibit robust positive correlations with diverse immune cell types, notably neutrophils, activated dendritic cells, plasma cells, resting CD4 memory T cells, activated mast cells, naive CD4 T cells, M0-type macrophages, and monocytes (Fig. 7b). The butterfly plot offers a more thorough, intuitive, and multidimensional examination of the correlations between genes and immune infiltrating cells, as well as the relationships among the 22 types of immune cells. In TC, naive B cells, activated CD4 + memory T cells, γδ T cells, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, resting dendritic cells, and neutrophils exhibited positive correlations with various genes; in contrast, memory B cells, plasma cells, resting CD4 + memory T cells, resting NK cells, and monocytes demonstrated negative correlations with multiple genes. M1 macrophages demonstrated a robust positive correlation with naive B cells, whereas memory B cells displayed a significant negative correlation with naive B cells (Fig. 6a). In SLE, plasma cells, monocytes, M0 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, activated mast cells, and neutrophils exhibit a positive correlation with numerous genes, whereas naive B cells, naive CD4 T cells, and resting CD4 memory T cells demonstrate a negative correlation with several genes. Conversely, activated dendritic cells exhibited a robust positive correlation with plasma cells, neutrophils, and activated dendritic cells; CD8 T cells and plasma cells; as well as resting CD4 memory T cells and naive CD4 T cells, while activated mast cells demonstrated a significant negative correlation with resting mast cells, plasma cells, and naive B cells (Fig. 7a).

Assessing the diagnostic significance of essential genes and analyzing core shared gene expression alongside clinical outcomes

We conducted ROC curve analyses on the GSE81622 and GSE29265 datasets to assess the diagnostic potential of 12 hub genes in TC and SLE. The diagnostic significance of these pivotal genes was evaluated through the AUC for the test subjects (Fig. 8a, b). Genes exhibiting an AUC value exceeding 0.70 were deemed to possess significant diagnostic potential. In the two datasets, six pivotal genes exhibited AUC values exceeding 0.70: C1QB, LCN2, C1QC, LTF, VSIG4, and C3AR1. Consequently, we suggest the six hub genes identified previously as biomarkers for TC and SLE. We explored comprehensive descriptions of these six diagnostically relevant hub genes in the Human Protein Atlas (Table 1). We subsequently examine the prognostic relevance of these six hub genes in TC patients through Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. High expression levels of C1QC (P = 0.024) and C3AR1 (P = 0.033) were significantly correlated with enhanced overall survival in patients with TC (Fig. 8c). Considering that SLE primarily impacts women, we conducted a further examination of gender-specific disparities in TC. The findings indicated that increased expression of C1QC and C3AR1 was significantly correlated with prognosis in female patients; in a sex-stratified analysis, elevated expression levels of both genes were significantly linked to survival outcomes (Fig. 8d, e). This study performed a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis utilising the TCGA database (TCGA-THCA cohort) to ascertain the independence of C1QC and C3AR1 as prognostic factors for thyroid cancer, while controlling for confounding variables including age, sex, and tumour stage. The forest plot illustrates that C1QC has an adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of 1.007, suggesting a tenuous correlation with adverse prognosis (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.001–1.013, P = 0.022). In contrast, C3AR1 demonstrated an HR of 0.864, indicating a potential protective effect, yet it did not achieve statistical significance (95% CI: 0.742–1.006, P = 0.059). It is important to highlight that while C1QC was statistically significant, its hazard ratio was nearly 1, indicating a minimal effect size and restricted clinical relevance (Fig. 8f). Consequently, according to the existing data, the independent prognostic significance of C1QC and C3AR1 in thyroid cancer is ambiguous and necessitates additional elucidation.

Interactions among TF, MiRNAs, and Genes

The Network Analyst website was utilized to forecast interactions between TF- genes and miRNA-genes, resulting in the identification of 35 transcription factors as depicted in Fig. 10b. Both GATA2 and FOXC1 interacted with three principal genes. This discovery underscores possible regulatory mechanisms that could facilitate disease progression and assists in investigating upstream regulatory processes of genes with shared attributes. Following this, an analysis of the miRNA-gene regulatory network (Fig. 10a) identified hsa-miR-34a-5p, hsa-let-7d-5p, hsa-miR-182-5p, hsa-miR-18a-5p, hsa-miR-103a-3p, hsa-miR-195-5p, and hsa-miR-92b-3p. These miRNAs demonstrate robust interactions with various core genes within the regulatory network. LCN2 exhibits the greatest connectivity with miRNAs, including the tumor suppressor miR-34a-5p, miR-195-5p, and the oncogenic miR-182-5p. These miRNAs have been thoroughly investigated, and their functions validated in cancer and autoimmune diseases, indicating their significant roles in upstream regulatory pathways.

Discussion

In recent years, the mechanisms linking chronic inflammation to cancer development have become increasingly clear. Chronic immune activation and the inflammatory microenvironment are considered critical connections between autoimmune disorders and malignant neoplasms [25–27]. Moreover, an elevated risk of malignant tumors is not exclusive to systemic lupus erythematosus; instead, it is a prevalent clinical characteristic among numerous autoimmune disorders. Individuals with diverse autoimmune disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren’s syndrome, and systemic sclerosis, demonstrate a heightened susceptibility to malignant neoplasms to differing extents [28]. Numerous autoimmune diseases exhibit shared pathological alterations, such as enduring systemic chronic inflammation, deficiencies in immune surveillance, and the ongoing aberrant secretion of inflammatory mediators. The prolonged disturbance of the immune microenvironment disrupts the equilibrium among cellular proliferation, apoptosis, and immune defense, consequently elevating the risk of malignant tumors in the general populace [29]. The elevated prevalence of malignancies in SLE patients exemplifies the overarching immunological principle that autoimmune dysregulation facilitates tumor susceptibility. The cancer profiles linked to various autoimmune diseases display distinct differences, potentially indicating disease-specific activation of immune pathways and organ-specific vulnerability. This study examines the particular relationship between systemic lupus erythematosus and thyroid cancer. By investigating the shared background of immune dysregulation, we seek to identify potential common molecular targets and signaling pathways between the two conditions, thus offering new perspectives and insights into their specific characteristics.

In SLE, continuous activation of the type I interferon signaling pathway, aberrant activation of the complement system, and immune cell dysfunction collectively induce a systemic pro-inflammatory condition [30]. This condition may facilitate the malignant transformation of thyroid epithelial cells via several mechanisms, including the induction of aberrant tissue repair, the enhancement of genomic instability, and the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [31] .Mendelian randomization studies have offered genetic evidence for the correlation between the two, indicating that specific immune-related genetic variants may heighten susceptibility to both SLE and TC [10, 16] .Currently, there is an absence of systematic clarification concerning the specific molecular pathways and cellular mechanisms common to these two diseases. This study intends to employ bioinformatics techniques to examine the pathways, interactions, and alterations in the immune microenvironment between these two diseases, thus offering a theoretical foundation for the creation of biomarkers for early detection, the identification of high-risk patients, and the investigation of therapeutic strategies targeting common pathways.

This study identified 46 DEGs common to patients with these two diseases by analyzing data from the GEO database. GO enrichment analysis of the overlapping DEGs identified BP including innate immune responses, negative regulation of viral genome replication, and extracellular matrix organization. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs were significantly enriched in the IL-17 signaling pathway, the complement and coagulation cascade, and cancer-related pathways. This enrichment pattern is closely linked to type I interferon-mediated immune responses [32]. This indicates that the IFN-I signaling pathway may connect the autoimmune condition of SLE with the tumor microenvironment of TC. This indicates that the IFN-I signaling pathway may connect the autoimmune condition of systemic lupus erythematosus with the tumor microenvironment of thyroid cancer. This study did not directly measure its activity, but multidimensional bioinformatics evidence may support its essential role in linking the two diseases. Subsequent analysis utilizing Reactome pathway analysis indicated that the differentially expressed genes common to both diseases were significantly enriched in Neutrophil degranulation, Transcriptional Regulation by TP53, MAPK family signaling pathways, Class I MHC-mediated antigen processing and presentation, and PIP3-activated AKT signaling. This indicates that irregularities in immune inflammation, complement-associated antigen presentation, and associated tumor signaling pathways are fundamentally involved in initiating and advancing both diseases. Moreover, the outcomes of GSEA substantiated that immune-inflammatory and autoimmune-related signaling pathways were markedly activated in both conditions, affirming the credibility of the enrichment of these pivotal pathways at the genome-wide expression level. The collective results of this multidimensional enrichment analysis suggest that disruptions in immune-inflammatory networks and signaling pathways are significant molecular characteristics of the comorbidity between the two diseases, offering pathway-level evidence and insights for a more thorough understanding of the mechanisms driving their comorbidity.

Utilizing the identified DEGs, we developed a PPI network via the STRING database and imported it into Cytoscape software for visualization and analysis. Through the amalgamation of MCODE module analysis with diverse topological algorithms from the cytoHubba plugin (notably Degree and MCC), we initially identified 12 hub genes (C1QB, MMP9, MMP1, ISG15, LCN2, C1QC, LTF, SLPI, VSIG4, IFI27, C3AR1, PTX3). Among the 12 aforementioned hub genes are various complement components (including C1QB, C1QC, and C3AR1) and interferon-induced genes (such as ISG15 and IFI27), indicating their potential significance in immune regulation. To further examine the correlation between pivotal genes common to both diseases and the immune microenvironment, we subsequently conducted an analysis of immune cell infiltration. Complement genes (C1QB/C1QC) and interferon-stimulated genes (IFI27/ISG15) exhibited a significant positive correlation with the infiltration of myeloid immune cells, specifically M0/M1 macrophages, monocytes, and neutrophils. The findings collectively illustrate a common immune environment marked by the activation and infiltration of innate immune cells, which may facilitate tumor-associated inflammation and immunosuppression, as well as induce systemic autoimmune responses [33, 34]. Notably, our analysis emphasizes the comparison of association patterns between core genes and immune cells across each disease, rather than the direct comparison of absolute cell proportions. However, we cannot completely dismiss the possibility that certain observed similarities indicate nonspecific inflammation instead of disease-specific mechanisms. Subsequent research must corroborate our findings utilizing paired samples from patients diagnosed with both TC and SLE. Notably, these hub genes are not merely nonspecific inflammatory markers. Their specific enrichment in complement and type I interferon pathways, consistent dysregulation across datasets, and unique correlation with disease-related myeloid immune cells support their specific roles in the shared pathogenesis of TC and SLE. Consequently, focusing on this common myeloid inflammatory pathway may provide novel perspectives for formulating immunotherapeutic approaches that concurrently tackle both conditions.

To enhance the validation of these genes and their correlation with the disease, we assessed their diagnostic efficacy through receiver operating characteristic curve analysis in independent GEO validation datasets (GSE81622 and GSE29265). We identified six principal hub genes with significant discriminatory capability: C1QB, LCN2, C1QC, LTF, VSIG4, and C3AR1. Moreover, survival analysis revealed that elevated expression of the core complement pathway genes C1QC and C3AR1 was significantly correlated with improved overall survival in TC patients, with this survival benefit being especially notable in female patients. This gender-specific survival advantage corresponds with the epidemiological trait of markedly elevated SLE prevalence in women. We propose that a female-specific immunogenetic framework or hormonal regulatory context may influence the risk factors for the onset of autoimmune diseases such as SLE [35] .This may also create a C1QC/C3AR1-mediated immune-activating microenvironment in TC, potentially linked to a favorable prognosis through enhanced complement-mediated tumor clearance or the reconfiguration of the antitumor immune response, thus offering new perspectives on the intricate relationship between gender, autoimmunity, and cancer [36, 37]. A multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to assess the independent prognostic significance of C1QC and C3AR1. Upon adjusting for clinical and pathological variables, including age, sex, and tumour stage from the TCGA database, the findings indicated that C1QC demonstrated a statistically significant, albeit weak, risk trend, while C3AR1 did not achieve statistical significance. The independent prognostic significance of C1QC and C3AR1 in thyroid cancer is indeterminate based on the results of univariate and multivariate analyses, necessitating further functional validation to establish a causal relationship. This discovery underscores the intricate roles of these two genes in thyroid cancer prognosis and indirectly illustrates the functional complexity of complement activation; their distinct effects may necessitate a particular tumour microenvironment or synergistic interactions with additional factors to become evident. While their independent prognostic significance is uncertain, the aforementioned findings imply a possible biological correlation between these two conditions. This offers initial insights into the possible involvement of complement molecules in the comorbidity of TC and SLE, necessitating additional functional research.

To clarify the mechanisms by which C1QC and C3AR1 connect autoimmunity to cancer prognosis, we offer a comprehensive overview of these two fundamental complement genes and perform a thorough examination of their biological roles and pathological implications in both conditions. C1QC is one of the three subunits of the C1 complex in the classical pathway of the complement system, tasked with recognizing antigen-antibody complexes and initiating the complement cascade. The activation of this complex results in the cleavage of C4 and C2, ultimately generating C3 convertase, which produces essential molecules that regulate phagocytosis and enhance inflammation [38, 39]. This pathway is crucial for the removal of immune complexes and apoptotic cells, serving as a fundamental mechanism for sustaining immune homeostasis [40]. Abnormalities in C1QC expression have been extensively associated with numerous autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, including SLE [41] .In accordance with our findings, C1q levels are markedly increased in patients with SLE, and C1q deposition in affected tissues, such as the glomeruli, constitutes a pathological hallmark of lupus nephritis [42]. The disease activity of SLE correlates with complement consumption and C1q levels, as the activation and deposition of complement components directly contribute to tissue inflammation and damage [43]. The function of C1QC within the tumor microenvironment is garnering heightened interest. Research indicates that elevated C1QC expression correlates with immune infiltration in diverse solid tumors, notably linked to the M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages [44]. C1QC may enhance tumor-associated inflammation or influence antitumor immune responses by detecting “eat-me” signals on tumor cell surfaces or by directly engaging with specific receptors on the tumor cell membrane [45, 46]. The upregulation of this factor in TC indicates that complement activation may affect tumor progression and patient prognosis by creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

C3AR1 is a designated receptor for the complement activation product C3a. It is predominantly expressed on the surface of myeloid cells (such as macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells) and is tasked with conveying complement signals into the interior of immune cells [47] .The interaction of C3a with C3AR1 activates G protein-coupled signaling pathways, resulting in chemotaxis, the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and increased phagocytic activity; it functions as a crucial nexus connecting complement activation to adaptive immune responses. C3AR1, while serving a protective function in antimicrobial defense, has been implicated in the pathological mechanisms of several autoimmune diseases due to its aberrant activation [48]. Research indicates that increased serum C3a levels in patients with SLE are positively associated with disease activity and the degree of renal involvement [49].In a murine model of lupus, inhibition of C3AR1 signaling markedly diminished proteinuria and glomerulonephritis, alongside a reduction in macrophage infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine production [50].The dual function of C3AR1 in tumor biology is garnering heightened interest. C3AR1 signaling facilitates the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and the modulation of macrophage activity [18]. Elevated C3AR1 expression is correlated with unfavorable prognosis and resistance to immune checkpoint therapy across multiple cancer types [51]. While direct evidence linking C3AR1 to tumor progression in TC through M2 macrophage polarization is currently scarce, its involvement in regulating M2 macrophage polarization and angiogenesis is well-documented in other solid tumors [52, 53]. The persistent upregulation of C3AR1 noted in both TC and SLE in this study indicates that the effector component of complement activation may function as a crucial inflammatory amplifier connecting the two conditions. This suggests that targeting C3AR1 may serve as a novel therapeutic approach for concurrently addressing autoimmune processes and tumor progression [54, 55].

To enhance our comprehension of the transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of shared genes, we developed distinct networks of TF-gene and miRNA-gene interactions. We conducted a systematic analysis of their regulatory features at both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels, identifying critical TFs and miRNAs potentially implicated in the shared pathogenic mechanisms of TC and SLE. GATA2 and FOXC1 were recognized as potential pivotal regulatory factors. GATA2 is a crucial transcription factor that governs the differentiation and functionality of myeloid cells [56]. Research indicates that GATA2 can directly modulate the expression of genes linked to myeloid cells. Huang et al. demonstrated via chromatin immunoprecipitation and gene expression profiling that C1QB is a direct transcriptional target of GATA2, and that GATA2 knockdown results in a substantial downregulation of C1QB expression [57]. The significant function of myeloid cells in the tumor immune microenvironment has led to heightened interest in the role of GATA2 in solid tumors. In lung adenocarcinoma, GATA2 facilitates M1-type macrophage polarization through the transcriptional activation of KANK3, consequently hindering tumor progression [58]. Additionally, in the pro-tumor neutrophil subpopulation of non-small cell lung cancer, GATA regulates HMGB1 expression, facilitating immune evasion [59].The findings indicate that GATA2 has a multifaceted and essential role in the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating the functions of various myeloid cell subsets. Concurrently, the correlation between GATA2 and SLE has progressively surfaced in recent research. Emmanouilidou et al. identified a deleterious variant in the GATA2 gene in a patient with SLE, indicating that GATA2 may play a role in the immune dysregulation linked to SLE by affecting T-cell and B-cell development [60].Conversely, FOXC1 is intricately linked to tissue invasion and angiogenesis. It has been demonstrated that FOXC1 promotes invasion and metastasis in various malignant tumors by regulating matrix metalloproteinases and genes associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition [61, 62]. In gastric cancer, elevated FOXC1 expression is significantly correlated with lymph node metastasis and unfavorable patient prognosis; it facilitates the degradation of the tumor extracellular matrix and increases tumor cell invasiveness by activating MMP9 expression [63]. Currently, there is insufficient research on the expression patterns of FOXC1 in SLE and its possible role in immune dysregulation. In rheumatoid arthritis, an autoimmune disorder, FOXC1 has been demonstrated to enhance inflammatory responses and excessive proliferation in fibroblast-like synoviocytes by modulating the TRIM22/NF-κB signaling pathway [64].

Among these, LCN2—a pivotal gene in neutrophil function—demonstrates the greatest number of miRNA interactions, indicating that its expression may be influenced by intricate post-transcriptional regulation. Subsequently, we performed a comprehensive analysis of pivotal miRNAs within the network that exhibit significant connectivity and distinct correlations with disease. miR-34a-5p is a direct transcriptional target of the p53 signaling pathway. As a quintessential tumor suppressor, its expression is diminished in numerous malignant neoplasms, including TC, and it impedes tumor proliferation by targeting cell cycle-associated genes such as CCND1 and CDK6 [65–67]. Recently, the significance of miR-34a-5p in autoimmune diseases has increasingly garnered attention. Bolouri et al. discovered that the expression of miR-34a-5p was markedly increased in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from SLE patients, with levels approximately 1.5 times greater than those in healthy controls [68]. Functional studies indicate that miR-34a-5p sustains immune tolerance by modulating the differentiation of Foxp3 + regulatory T cells, and its diminished expression may concurrently facilitate tumorigenesis and autoimmune inflammation [69]. miR-195-5p, a constituent of the miR-15/107 family [70], In TC, miR-195-5p has been demonstrated to function as a tumor suppressor. Liu et al. discovered that miR-195-5p suppresses the proliferation and invasion of TC cells by targeting TERT. A study by Qin et al. demonstrated that miR-195-5p modulates TC progression by targeting FGF2. Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-195-5p plays a role in the development and progression of TC by modulating various downstream target genes [71, 72]. Altered expression of miR-195-5p has been noted in SLE ; it may function as a non-invasive biomarker for lupus nephritis and could play a role in the pathogenesis of SLE by modulating target genes [73].Research indicates that miR-182-5p is a crucial regulator in tumor progression and autoimmune inflammation; it facilitates tumor advancement by inducing M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages and creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment [74]. by modulating T-cell differentiation to engage in immune inflammatory responses [75], This indicates that they may function as a potential shared regulatory center for both carcinogenesis and inflammation. According to the findings documented in the literature and the results of this study, these miRNAs may be potentially linked to the comorbidity mechanisms of these two diseases. Predictive analysis indicates that they may affect the NF-κB-mediated inflammatory pathway and the T-cell differentiation regulatory network by synergistically modulating several pivotal hub genes. Nonetheless, these regulatory associations are computational forecasts that necessitate experimental confirmation.

Through drug-gene interaction analysis, we identified the ten most promising therapeutic drugs. Nimesulide is a selective COX-2 inhibitor that demonstrates anti-inflammatory properties and mitigates tumor-associated inflammation [76]. COX-2 is significantly upregulated in numerous cancers, facilitating tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune evasion; thus, COX-2 inhibitors present substantial potential for anticancer treatment [77]. Furthermore, deferoxamine hydrochloride serves as an iron chelator that diminishes the activity of hyperactive immune cells by restricting iron availability, and it may also impede tumor proliferation [78, 79]. The findings indicate that the identified compounds merit further examination as potential candidates for future research in the co-regulation of SLE immune responses and TC tumor progression. Nevertheless, these are solely bioinformatics predictions and necessitate experimental validation. The correlations between essential genes and pharmaceuticals offer initial insights for subsequent pharmacological investigation.

This study offers new insights into the molecular mechanisms connecting TC and SLE. Through the integration of diverse bioinformatics methodologies—such as differential expression analysis, immune infiltration assessment, network pharmacology, and multi-omics validation—these results enhance our comprehension of the mechanisms governing autoimmune-oncological comorbidity and underscore the distinctive advantages of bioinformatics in clarifying shared pathways in intricate diseases. Prior to clinical application, it is imperative to conduct future experiments utilizing animal models, followed by prospective clinical studies to further assess these targets. Only then can these prospective targets attain enhanced value.

Limitations

This study identified common molecular and immunological features between TC and SLE through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis. Nonetheless, several limitations persist. This study exclusively utilized publicly accessible transcriptomic data from the GEO database. The sample sizes of the TC and SLE datasets were modest, varying from 20 to 150 cases per dataset, potentially impacting statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, permutation-based or random overlap analyses were not conducted to statistically validate the significance of the 46 shared DEGs, potentially diminishing the statistical robustness of the overlapping gene set. Current datasets are derived exclusively from a singular tissue type, and there is an absence of multi-omics paired data from the same patient cohort exhibiting two concurrent diseases, direct evaluation of molecular associations in patients with comorbidities is unfeasible. Our cross-tissue design, encompassing both thyroid tissue and PBMCs, may serve as a potential source of confounding variables. Despite performing independent DEG analyses and validations across various datasets to address this issue, we cannot entirely eliminate the potential for residual tissue-specific effects to impact the overlapping genes. Subsequent research must employ paired samples from patients with simultaneous TC and SLE or conduct cross-tissue single-cell sequencing to further corroborate these common molecular signatures. The identified regulatory hub genes may partially reflect nonspecific immune activation rather than disease-specific pathogenic signatures. Further mechanistic validation using conditional knockout mice or in vitro co-culture models is required to confirm their exact roles in the shared pathogenesis of TC and SLE. We employed CIBERSORT to conduct an immune infiltration analysis on various sample types. To mitigate cross-tissue bias, we analyzed variations within the same dataset instead of absolute cell proportions; nonetheless, these common immune traits may partially indicate a generalized inflammatory condition. The study lacked a direct quantitative evaluation of type I interferon pathway activity, thus, the assertion that the IFN-I signaling pathway functions as a fundamental shared mechanism remains conjectural, grounded in current multidimensional bioinformatics enrichment analyses. The exact biological function necessitates additional confirmation in independent cohorts utilizing pathway activity scores.

Secondly, the study failed to perform systematic quantitative statistical analyses regarding the overlap rate and the consistency of expression directions between genes identified in the discovery set and those in the validation set, leading to an absence of intuitive quantitative data to substantiate the cross-cohort reproducibility of gene expression patterns. Future research should encompass cohort studies with larger sample sizes to enhance the validation of the reproducibility of these shared genes and to refine the consistency validation across multiple datasets. This will more effectively mitigate result biases stemming from single-cohort samples and further augment the generalizability and practical significance of conclusions concerning core hub genes and associated mechanisms. The regulatory functions of the TF-gene and miRNA-gene interaction networks identified in this study remain experimentally unvalidated. Consequently, they ought to be considered as prospective hypotheses and predictions rather than confirmed mechanisms. The biological validity of these regulatory axes necessitates additional verification via appropriate experimental techniques, such as luciferase reporter assays. This research exclusively utilizes enrichment analysis from the DSigDB database for pharmacological prediction. The candidate compounds should be considered merely as initial bioinformatics predictions that may provide research insights; however, further assessment is necessary through molecular docking, in vitro cellular assays, pharmacodynamic validation in animal models, and drug sensitivity analysis to evaluate the actual effects and clinical viability of these compounds for the co-morbidity of TC and SLE.

This study’s findings offer preliminary insights into the potential mechanisms of comorbidity between TC and SLE. However, the present findings are confined to initial bioinformatics analyses and lack direct clinical relevance or applicability. Therefore, they require validation through prospective cohort studies, multi-omics integrated analyses, and functional in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Conclusion

This research constitutes a bioinformatics analysis utilizing the GEO dataset, wherein we identified six pivotal hub genes (C1QB, LCN2, C1QC, LTF, VSIG4, and C3AR1) linked to TC and SLE. Chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation may constitute the shared pathological foundation for both TC and SLE; the key genes and upstream regulatory networks identified herein establish an important foundation for the creation of biomarkers and targeted therapeutic approaches for disease diagnosis, prognosis evaluation, and potential cross-disease treatment. The precise clinical translation potential of these shared mechanisms requires further elucidation and validation through advanced omics technologies and prospective in vivo and in vitro studies. Only then can the shared pathogenic mechanisms of these two diseases be systematically elucidated, thus offering more effective treatment strategies for patients with comorbidities.
