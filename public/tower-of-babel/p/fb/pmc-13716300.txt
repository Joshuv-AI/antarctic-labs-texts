Adult Misuse of ADHD Stimulant Medication in the United States
Authors: Margaret A. Maglione, Jaskanwar Batra, Bradley S. Peterson, Joey Trampush, Sheila Pakdaman, Kimny Sysawang, Sachi Yagyu, Erin Foster, Diana Zhang, Aneesa Motala, Danica Tolentino, Susanne Hempel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To assess the prevalence, patterns, and consequences of ADHD stimulant medication misuse among adults in the United States.Methods:PubMed and PsycINFO were searched for US-based studies from 2004 to 2024. Cross-sectional and longitudinal studies on stimulant medication misuse among adults were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for prevalence studies and the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool for longitudinal designs. Data were summarized narratively.Findings:Sixty-four studies met inclusion criteria, including several high-quality federally funded national surveys. Many other incidence and prevalence studies were conducted at universities; these had inconsistent quality due to low response, non-representative samples, or unclear methodology. National surveys show that past-year misuse among adults is declining, with 2023 estimates ranging from 1.4% to 3.7% among young adults and 1.9% among adults of all ages. Misuse is most prevalent among younger, White, metropolitan-dwelling individuals, and among college students. Misuse is primarily oral and infrequent, although a subset of high-frequency users obtain stimulants from physicians or dealers and exhibit higher rates of polysubstance use. Consequences of misuse may include psychiatric admission, emergency department visits, and illicit drug use. Long-term studies do not support a link between prescribed stimulant treatment in adolescence and later substance use disorder; unfortunately, no studies collected data on long-term physical health consequences.Conclusions:ADHD stimulant medication misuse among adults, although declining, remains a public health concern, particularly among high-frequency users. Research gaps remain, especially regarding long-term health outcomes.

The National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) defines prescription drug misuse as: “taking a medication in a manner or dose other than prescribed; taking someone else’s prescription, even if for a legitimate medical complaint such as pain; or taking a medication to feel euphoria (ie, to get high).”1 In 2022, over 4.3 million Americans over age 12 reported misusing stimulants FDA-approved to treat attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).2 The number of prescriptions for stimulants like products containing mixed amphetamine salts or methylphenidate increased significantly among adults from 2016 to 2021, contributing to greater availability and potential for misuse. 3 Misuse of ADHD stimulants may be associated with cardiovascular problems, mental health disorders (such as anxiety and depression), cognitive impairment, and substance use disorders.4

To inform strategies to prevent misuse and diversion, in 2024, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requested a Rapid Review on adult misuse of ADHD stimulant medications, focused on the prevalence and consequences of misuse and the pathways, if any, from the misuse of prescription stimulants to use and misuse of illicit drugs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This project followed a detailed protocol, which is registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024627066).

The FDA provided the following Key Questions about the patterns, trends, and consequences of ADHD stimulant medication misuse among adults in the United States:

KQ1: What are the patterns and trends in prescription stimulant misuse among adults?

KQ1a: How does this vary by patient characteristics, such as age, sex, race, and comorbidities? This includes:Who misuses prescription stimulants, and what are the reasons for misuse?Where and how do people acquire the prescription stimulants that they are misusing?What is the frequency of misuse and how do they differ by type of stimulant, method of ingestion (orally, nasally, injected), and source of diversion?

KQ2: What are the shorter-term (eg, overdose) and longer-term (eg, cardiovascular) health consequences of prescription stimulant misuse among adults?

KQ2a: How do these consequences vary based on type of stimulant and patient characteristics?

KQ3: What is the role of appropriate use or misuse of prescription stimulants on the use of illicit stimulants and other substances?

The term “prescription stimulant misuse” in the Key Questions was intended to differentiate misuse of FDA-approved medications from illicit stimulants such as cocaine and methamphetamine; the project did not focus only on misusers who had a prescription. The electronic databases PubMed and PsycINFO were searched systematically for studies published from 2004 through September 2024 to identify recent data and extend back to a period of increasing emergency department visits related to ADHD stimulants among adults (from 5212 in 2005 to 15,585 in 2010, per the Drug Abuse Warning Network).5 Systematic reviews were screened for relevance and their references were mined. Because of the expedited timeline, studies that included only undergraduate students were excluded, unless they were published after a search previously conducted for a high-quality 2020 systematic review.6 Studies reporting only academic, occupational, or physical task performance measures were excluded, as these outcomes were beyond the scope of the project.

After review of titles and abstracts resulting from the electronic searches, each full-text article/report retrieved was independently reviewed for eligibility by a literature reviewer and checked by the project leader. One researcher abstracted data from each publication that met inclusion criteria, and the project leader checked the data for accuracy and completeness.

For cross-sectional studies, we assessed Risk of Bias using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Checklist for Prevalence Studies,7 which covers sampling, data collection, reporting, and statistical analysis. For longitudinal studies, we used the Quality In Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool,8 which covers study participation, attrition, prognostic factor measurement, outcome measurement, confounding, and statistical analysis.

Abstracted data and Risk of Bias items for each study are displayed in Evidence Tables A and B, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/JCP/B37. Pertinent data are displayed in the supplemental tables, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/JCP/B37 and summarized below in the main text and figures.

RESULTS

After full-text review of 375 publicly available documents, 64 studies published in 71 documents met eligibility criteria. Figure 1 displays the literature flow diagram. The primary reasons for exclusion were not a US study (N=33), not adults (N=104), and no data on ADHD stimulant medications (N=60; these often pertained to non-prescription stimulants such as cocaine or methamphetamine).

Literature flow diagram.

Ten longitudinal9–18 and 54 cross-sectional19–72 studies were included. Despite excluding studies of undergraduates published before a recent systematic review on the topic, almost half of the included studies focused on university students. Our review focuses on the broader adult population and features highlights of the university studies most relevant to the Key Questions.

Prevalence

Figure 2 displays past-year misuse of amphetamine mixed salts (specifically Adderall) and methylphenidate (Ritalin) among US adults aged 19 to 30 years, as estimated by Monitoring the Future, a large annual survey funded by National Institute on Drug Abuse.52 In 2023, 3.7% misused Adderall, reflecting a large and statistically significant decrease from 7.8% in 2022 (P<0.001) despite an increase in prescribing of amphetamine mixed salts over the last decade;32 this may reflect a shortage of the immediate release formulation in the United States that began in 2022.73,74 Misuse of Ritalin remained low at 1.2% in 2023, with no significant change in the past 10 years.32

Past year misuse of Adderall and Ritalin among adults aged 19 to 30 years, US, 2023. Source: Monitoring the Future study, National Institute on Drug Abuse.52

The estimated prevalence of misuse from studies that include adults over age 30 is lower. The Researched Abuse, Diversion and Addiction-Related Surveillance (RADARS) System’s Survey of the Nonmedical Use of Prescription Drugs (NMURx) found national prevalence of nonmedical use of prescription stimulants among adults of all ages was 1.9% (95% CI: 1.8%-2.0%), including amphetamine-based medications (1.6%, 95% CI: 1.5%-1.6%), methylphenidate (0.3%, 95% CI: 0.3%-0.4%), and modafinil (0.2%, 95% CI: 0.2%-0.3%) in 2020.55

The National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), a nationally representative annual survey funded by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), also found ADHD stimulant medication misuse decreased. In 2023, an estimated 1.4% of Americans aged 12 and older misused these drugs in the prior year, for a total of 3.9 million people; prevalence of past month misuse was 0.4%.60 Past year misuse among young adults aged 18 through 25 has been steadily declining, from a high of 7.5% in 2016 to 5.8% in 2019, then from 3.7% in 2021 to 3.1% in 2023. (Because of changes in methodology, including roll-out of web-based interviews, estimates for 2021 and later are not comparable with estimates from 2019 or earlier.) Past year misuse was highest among Whites (1.8%), adults with at least some college education, and those living in metropolitan areas (significance tests not reported).60

Several researchers conducted multivariate regression analyses to determine associations with demographic characteristics. An analysis of the 2015 to 2019 National Surveys on Drug Use and Health42 found that men had a 1.37 times higher odds of misuse in the past year (P<0.001), whereas no sex difference was observed in the prevalence of prescription stimulant-use disorder (~0.2% for both). The authors found no significant interaction of misuse with sexual orientation; however, another analysis of NSDUH data54 found that lesbian, gay, and bisexual individuals had uniformly higher stimulant use than their heterosexual peers (P<0.001). An in-depth 2018 analysis of NSDUH data27 categorized high-frequency misuse as at least 7 days per month; those with high-frequency misuse were less likely than those with lower frequency misuse to be under age 30, non-White, or have a college degree (all P<0.05), and they were more likely to be separated/divorced or on work disability (all P <0.05).

As mentioned, the prevalence of ADHD stimulant medication misuse is higher among college-age individuals than in the general adult population. Figure 3 compares self-reported past year misuse in 6 studies of university undergraduates,19,23,26,33,46,72 with prevalence ranging from 8.0% at East Carolina University in academic year 2019 to 2020 to 19.9% at an unnamed university in the Southeast reported in a study published in 2020 (year of data collection not reported).26 The risk of bias of these studies varied; some conducted at single universities had high risk due to convenience sampling, resulting in participant demographics that were not representative of that university’s population. A more robust study, the National College Health Assessment, reported that 8% of US undergraduates misused ADHD stimulant medications at least once in 2017.72

Five university studies found ADHD stimulant misuse significantly associated with white race/ethnicity9,26,33,66,72 in multivariate analyses; no university studies found a significant association of any other race/ethnicity with misuse. Results for sex were mixed, with 5 studies reporting that misuse was significantly associated with male sex,11,19,26,36,72 whereas 2 studies found that misuse was significantly associated with being female.9,33 The NCHA also found that prescription stimulant misuse was significantly associated with past-year diagnosis or treatment of depression [adjusted odds ratio (AOR): 1.16, CI: 1.01-1.33], anorexia (AOR: 1.44, CI: 1.02-2.03), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (AOR: 1.66; CI: 1.41-1.95), and substance use disorder/other addiction (AOR: 1.79, CI: 1.30-2.46).72

Three university studies collected data on specific name-brand stimulants. A study of seven universities in Georgia found Adderall the most commonly misused stimulant medication, accounting for 42.9% of past year misuse, followed by lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse, 27.4%) and then methylphenidate (Ritalin, 13.2%).11 Studies at Texas State University, San Marcos16 and a dental school in Texas43 also found that Adderall was much more commonly misused than Ritalin or other brands.

Reasons for Misuse

The following reasons for “most recent misuse” among all adults who misused at least once in the prior year were identified by the 2023 NSDUH: to help concentrate (36.1%); to be alert or stay awake (29.3%); to help study (11.0%); to get high, counter the effects of other drugs, or to experiment (16.1%); and for weight loss (3.9%).60 In contrast, the following reasons were reported by those with prescription stimulant use disorder in the 2022 NSDUH:32 to help concentrate (34.0%); to be alert or stay awake (33.6%); to help study (5.5%); to get high, counter the effects of other drugs, or to experiment (16.0%); for weight loss (3.7%) and for other reasons (7.2%).

Frequency of Misuse

Adults who misused in the past month did so on an average of 4.5 days; 4.3% misused 20 or more days (2023 NSDUH).60 A recent publication on the 2022 NSDUH32 provided more nuance on frequency of use, as follows: misused past year but not past month 68.2%; 1 to 2 days past month 14.3%; 3 to 5 days past month 8.2%; and 6 or more days past month 9.3%.

Misuse was occasional for most college students; for example, among psychology students at Syracuse University who misused, 76% did so three or fewer times per year.19 A survey conducted at 15 universities found that of those who misused to study, 70% did so during finals or exams, 51% before a test or project, 47% when they felt behind, 20% when they wanted to get ahead, 5% when they needed a competitive edge, and 10% used continuously throughout the term.20 Two university studies asked about frequency by specific brand-name;33,45 both found Adderall was misused at a higher frequency than Ritalin. Thirty-seven students who misused prescription stimulants at Texas State University San Marcos tracked their misuse for a month; on days that stimulant medication misuse occurred, 88.8% of those days had one episode of misuse, 10.8% two episodes, and 0.4% three episodes.16

Means of Acquisition

The ways by which prescription stimulants are acquired differ by frequency of misuse. An analysis of 2016 NSDUH data27 found adults who misuse with high frequency (7+ days in the past month) were less likely than those who misuse with low frequency (less than monthly) to obtain stimulants from their friends/relatives for free (44.9% vs. 58.8%) and more likely to obtain them from one or more doctors (25.2% vs. 10.1%) or from drug dealers/strangers (9.2% vs. 4.0%). According to 2022 NSDUH data, those with prescription stimulant use disorder (SUD) were less likely to obtain stimulants from friends/relatives for free (23.4%) and more likely to obtain from doctors (38.5%) than those without the SUD diagnosis.32

The only nationally representative source of data on internet acquistion, a 2005 Harris Poll of Americans aged 18 to 49, found adults who misused ADHD stimulant medications in the past year acquired them in the following ways (not mutually exclusive): given by friend/ family member (65.8%), purchased from friend/family (13.0%), stolen (including stolen from friend/family member, 34.5%), obtained fraudulently from a doctor (19.8%), or from an internet pharmacy (5.3%).49

Mode of Administration

ADHD stimulant medications are primarily misused orally. According to a 2010 national YouGov internet survey,24 route of administration (not mutually exclusive) was primarily oral (94.7% for Concerta, 80.7% for Ritalin), followed by snorting (Ritalin, 39.6%), and injecting or smoking (6% or less). More recently, researchers conducted outreach specifically to adults who misuse these medications intranasally (N=225) through Reddit.68 A large proportion of the adults who misuse intranasally also misused the stimulants orally (85%), and some injected (6%) or smoked (4%) prescription stimulants. This study asked participants to indicate all their sources of medication: 72.1% purchased the medication from a family member or friend, 64.5% were given by a family member or a friend, 10.5% stole it from a family member or friend, 39.6% bought it from dealers, 24.4% had a prescription from a physician, and 5.3% bought it online without having seen a doctor. This study has unclear risk of bias, as the sample size was small, only 13.8% of respondents were female, and only 10.9% were age 35 or older. Thus, responses may not reflect national trends.

Consequences of Misuse

Analysis of data from the US National Poison Data System from January 2012 to December 2016 described the medical and health consequences of exposures to ADHD medications containing amphetamines;29 these were substantial across all modes of administration. During those five years, 33,239 exposures involving prescription amphetamines among persons aged 12 or older were reported to US poison control centers; 15,876 had data available for analyses (9713 with available data were adults). The mean age of adults was 31.5 years (SD=11.03). The prevalence of another substance was 46% for oral intentional misuse, 65% for nasal, 50% for intravenous (IV) use, and 22% for to controls defined as unintentional oral exposures (P<0.000). Compared with these controls (7.9%), IV (36.6%), nasal (21.2%), and oral administration (24.6%) were significantly associated with higher rates of admission to critical care units (P<0.0001); this may reflect the significantly lower prevalence of other substances among control exposures. Oral administration had the highest likelihood (OR=7.48) of admission to psychiatric care facilities, followed by nasal (OR=3.22) and IV (OR=3.11), compared with controls (P<0.0001). The high rate of psychiatric admission among oral exposures is likely because suspected suicide attempts were significantly more common among oral (74.1%) compared with IV (20.7%), and nasal (15.6%) routes, with a modest but significant increase in risk overtime (P<0.0001). All groups exhibited a higher prevalence of adverse clinical effects compared with controls, with IV exposure having the most severe outcomes, including a 21.9-fold increased risk of mortality compared with controls. Males were more likely to engage in nasal and IV administration (P<0.0001), whereas oral use was similar among both sexes.

According to the 2022 Treatment Episode Data Set (TEDS), only 0.2% (N=2543) of about 1.5 million substance use treatment admissions documented (including both outpatient and residential modalities) were for primary drug “other stimulants,” defined as “includes methylphenidate and any other prescription stimulants.“61 (The TEDS category “amphetamines” includes MDMA and other drugs which are beyond the scope of this project.) The annual number has remained relatively steady since 2018. TEDS provides data specific to Ritalin; there were 78 drug treatment admission for this medication in 2022, representing the primary drug for 31, secondary drug for 31, and tertiary drug for 16 admissions. Admissions specific to Adderall or other brand names for prescription stimulants were not reported.

We found no long-term studies reporting the physical health consequences of ADHD stimulant medication misuse.

According to an analysis of longitudinal data from Monitoring the Future, individuals who at age 18 simultaneously used prescription stimulants for both nonmedical and medical purposes (P=0.01), and those who used them for only nonmedical use (P=0.001) were more likely to have at least 2 Substance Use Disorder (SUD) symptoms at age 35, compared with those with no use of stimulant medications or only medical use at age 18.14 Multivariate regression analysis by the same researchers15 yielded no significant association of prescribed stimulant therapy for ADHD at age 18 with past year cocaine use during young adulthood; moreover, adolescents who reported prescription stimulant misuse (and who had no history of stimulant therapy for ADHD) had the highest adjusted odds of later cocaine (OR: 2.59, CI: 1.51-4.46) and methamphetamine (OR: 4.66, CI: 0.91-23.71) use at 19 to 24 years of age compared with population controls who reported no lifetime use of stimulants at age 18.13 Another analysis of 8362 Monitoring the Future participants followed for 15 years found no evidence for an association of childhood treatment with stimulant medication with adolescent or young adulthood substance use disorder (OR: 1.18, CI: 0.93-1.50).14 These findings together suggest stimulant treatment for ADHD before or at age 18 does not increase the risk of ADHD stimulant medication misuse, use of illicit stimulants, or substance use disorders in young adulthood. Conversely, the studies support an association between misuse of stimulants at age 18 with future use of illicit stimulants and SUD.

National cross-sectional studies reported that many adults who misuse ADHD stimulant medication engage in polysubstance use. A 2005 Harris Poll found that adults who misuse stimulant medication used the following substances “at the same time or within a couple hours” in the past year: alcohol 52.8%, marijuana 26.2%, cocaine 19.8%, heroin 6.4%, other prescription medications 19.5%, hallucinogens 3.6%, or any substance 67.9%.49 A 2018 analysis of NSDUH data found adults who misuse ADHD stimulant medication at high frequency (7+ days past-month) had high rates of past-year cocaine use disorder (10.9%), past year heroin use or heroin use disorder (14.3%), past-year inhalant use or inhalant use disorder (11.4%), prescription tranquilizer/sedative disorders (9.8%), and prescription opioid misuse disorders (10.1%).27 The 2023 NSDUH survey estimated that 786,000 Americans aged 12 and over misused both cocaine and prescription stimulants, 190,000 misused both methamphetamine and prescription stimulants, and another 182,000 misused all three drugs.60 It is important to note that these cross-sectional studies did not report the age at which each substance was first used, so it is not possible to determine whether use of one substance pre-dated another.

The misuse of ADHD stimulant medication among university students is associated with alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana use. Reported prevalence levels and measurement varied by university, and most studies had high or unknown risk of bias due to convenience sampling or low response rate. The most recent nationally representative study of college students72 reported that the odds of ADHD stimulant medication misuse were 5.5 times higher (CI: 4.54-6.71) for students reporting a past-month use of “legal drugs” (cigarettes, alcohol, marijuana) and 8.0 times higher (CI 6.96–9.15) for students reporting a past month use of “illegal drugs” (cocaine, sedatives, hallucinogens, opiates, methamphetamine). The odds ratios for specific substances were not reported.

As noted above, polysubstance use has also been documented in overdose data. Of all Rhode Island accidental fatal overdoses between May 1, 2017, and May 31, 2020 in which prescription amphetamines or methylphenidate were deemed to contribute to the cause of death (N=39), 90% involved opioids, 33% cocaine, and 18% benzodiazepines.41

DISCUSSION

Adult ADHD stimulant medication misuse declined significantly in recent years, driven by a reduction in misuse of amphetamine-based medications among young adults. This dramatic and rapid decline likely reflects the worldwide shortage,73,74 suggesting that policies that aim to limit supplies to those who misuse or divert prescription stimulants may be effective in reducing misuse rates. The Drug Enforcement Agency’s 2022 decision to impose tighter quotas on ADHD medications stemmed from concerns about diversion and misuse. The resulting shortage seems to have reduced the supply available for diversion,75 as the average monthly prescription fill rate for amphetamine salts declined 11% from 2022 to 2023. Unfortunately, this had a substantial detrimental effect on patients diagnosed with ADHD who need these medications. The CDC estimated that 71.5% of ADHD patients had difficulty filling their prescription because the medication was unavailable.76 Using such policies to reduce misuse must be weighed against these and other adverse consequences.

Despite the decline, misuse remains a significant public health concern in the United States, as millions of American adults continue this practice. Misuse is most prevalent among young adults and college students, with a smaller but clinically important subgroup engaging in high-frequency use. This subgroup demonstrates markedly higher rates of substance use disorders, psychiatric comorbidity, and non-oral routes of administration, suggesting that overall prevalence trends may obscure concentrated risk among vulnerable individuals.

ADHD stimulant medication misuse is closely intertwined with broader substance use patterns and overdose risk. High rates of concurrent use with alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, opioids, and methamphetamine underscore its role within polysubstance use networks. National survey data indicate that hundreds of thousands of Americans misuse ADHD stimulant medication in combination with cocaine or methamphetamine each year, and fatal overdose cases frequently involve opioids. Poison center data further document substantial morbidity associated with misuse, including critical care admissions and elevated mortality risk with intravenous exposure. Importantly, oral misuse is often associated with suicide attempts, highlighting the intersection between stimulant misuse and psychiatric vulnerability. During the final drafting of this manuscript, we identified a new study of McLean Hospital patients,77 which found patients admitted for psychosis or mania were more likely than patients admitted for other psychiatric symptoms/ disorders to report misuse of nonprescribed stimulant medications (P=0.007). Further analysis of existing federal datasets could provide additional insights into these and other consequences of ADHD stimulant medication misuse among adults. For instance, the Drug Abuse Warning Network (DAWN), a national database tracking drug-related emergency department visits, includes data on these medications. However, this information was not presented in recent DAWN reports, because they only include the ten most common substances involved in ED visits.78

This review’s findings underscore the need to distinguish clearly between therapeutic use and misuse in clinical and policy discussions. Continued surveillance, careful prescribing practices, screening for comorbid substance use and mental health conditions, and targeted interventions for high-frequency users are essential. Moreover, the absence of long-term data on cardiovascular and neurological outcomes represents a critical research gap, particularly given increasing stimulant prescribing over the past decade.

The studies included in this review have important methodological limitations; future research should strengthen the evidence base by addressing them. Nearly half the studies focused on college or university samples, many of which relied on convenience sampling, low response rates, or single-institution recruitment. These designs introduce substantial risk of selection bias and limit generalizability to the broader adult population. Even when multivariate analyses of such data are conducted, residual confounding is likely.

A significant limitation across the reviewed literature is the exclusive reliance on self-reported data, which is inherently susceptible to recall and social desirability biases,79 as well as participant misclassification, such as the misidentification of specific medication formulations. Although toxicology screenings of blood, urine, and saliva are established methods for validating self-reported drug use,80 our search strategy yielded a notable absence of such objective verification for ADHD medication misuse. Furthermore, data regarding the frequency, dosage, and route of administration were inconsistently reported across the included studies.

Finally, relatively few longitudinal studies were identified, and those did not assess the potential long-term health effects of prescription stimulant misuse such as neurological or cardiovascular problems. This Rapid Review was limited to studies conducted in the United States; studies conducted in other countries might contain important information that this review missed. There was minimal route-specific outcome data, limited information on high-frequency misuse trajectories, and insufficient detail on timing of initiation across substances to establish progression patterns. Together, these limitations highlight the need for more rigorous, prospective, and nationally representative research to clarify long-term outcomes and high-risk pathways.
