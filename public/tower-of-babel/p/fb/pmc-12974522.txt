Dynamic reprogramming of the tumor immune network via multicycle checkpoint degradation for cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Tao Shi, Yuanyuan Wu, Yiran Cai, Yuting Luo, Shiji Ren, Yaning Cao, Yin Tang, Zizheng Jiang, Shiyao Hu, Wenxue Xie, Yuanyuan Chen, Lin Shen, Hang Xing, Jia Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceImmune checkpoint inhibitors often fail because suppressive checkpoint-linked networks span tumor and myeloid compartments in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). We developed Receptormediated Endolysosomal recYcling Chimera (RECYC), a recyclable extracellular degrader that pairs a pH-stable CI-M6PR aptamer with a pH-responsive, moderate-affinity programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) binder to drive multicycle checkpoint clearance. RECYC depleted PD-L1 on both tumor cells and tumor-associated myeloid cells in the TIME. By redirecting an immunosuppressive TIME to an immunostimulatory state, RECYC surpassed antibody blockade in tumor control. This TIME-wide, recycling degradation offers a practical blueprint, which converts transient epitope blocking into durable immune network remodeling for improved cancer immunotherapy.

Cancer immunotherapy, most notably immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), has transformed the treatment of many tumors by reactivating antitumor immunity (1, 2). Despite its clinical success, a significant proportion of patients fail to benefit from ICB therapies (3, 4), highlighting the need to elucidate resistance mechanisms and develop more effective strategies. Increasing evidence suggests that the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) critically limits ICB efficacy, as complex immune-checkpoint regulatory networks span across various cellular compartments and engage in multicellular interactions that actively suppress antitumor immunity (5, 6). In particular, key checkpoints such as PD-L1 are not limited to tumor cells but are dynamically expressed and regulated across diverse TIME populations, including tumor cells and myeloid subsets, including macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells (DCs) (7, 8). These PD-L1 myeloid populations mediate immunosuppressive effects that actively compromise T cell activation and function (9, 10). A full understanding of these multicellular regulatory networks and their distinct contributions to therapeutic resistance is therefore essential for the design of next-generation immunotherapies.

Clinically approved PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies, including atezolizumab, durvalumab, and avelumab, have demonstrated significant clinical efficacy across multiple cancer types by neutralizing PD-L1 signaling (11–15). However, current inhibitors typically operate in a stoichiometric mode, blocking extracellular epitopes without removing the full receptor scaffold (16–18). As a result, the residual receptor scaffold can still engage in off-target interactions and contribute to potential resistance. Moreover, antibody-mediated blockade provides only transient inhibition of PD-L1, allowing the underlying immunosuppressive networks in the TIME to persist and potentially limiting the observed clinical outcomes (19–21).

Recently, extracellular targeted protein degradation (eTPD) has emerged as a promising complementary strategy that can address this limitation by eliminating both membrane-bound and soluble tumor-associated proteins (22–25). Representative eTPD platforms such as lysosome-targeting chimera (LYTAC) (26, 27), molecular degrader of extracellular proteins (28), cytokine receptor-targeting chimera (29), and covalently engineered nanobody chimera (30), are bispecific molecules that are designed to simultaneously bind a target protein (e.g., PD-L1, EGFR, IL-4R) and a lysosomal trafficking receptor, thereby recruiting the endolysosomal pathway for target degradation (23, 31–34). In a typical eTPD system, such as the LYTAC platform, a bifunctional degrader simultaneously engages a cell-surface or soluble target protein and a lysosomal trafficking receptor. A commonly employed receptor is the cation-independent mannose-6-phosphate receptor (CI-M6PR), which is ubiquitously expressed across tissues and exhibits robust endolysosomal trafficking activity. Upon binding, the receptor facilitates the internalization of the target complex into endosomes, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes, leading to the complete elimination of the target (27). This “outside-in” degradation mechanism removes the entire protein scaffold rather than transiently blocking an extracellular epitope, thereby offering sustained modulation of signaling networks and expanding the scope of targeted protein degradation beyond intracellular targets. Notably, several PD-L1-targeting degraders have been reported to effectively remove surface PD-L1 on cancer cells and enhanced antitumor responses in preclinical models (35–37). Therefore, by enabling irreversible clearance of target proteins, eTPD platforms offer distinct mechanistic advantages and therapeutic potential over conventional ICB modalities.

Despite the promises, two key challenges remain (22). First, most reported degraders have primarily focused on tumor cells, overlooking PD-L1 expression on tumor-associated myeloid populations. Consequently, the multicellular communication network that sustains the immunosuppression within the TIME remains unaddressed. Second, many reported degraders are consumed after a single round of PD-L1 removal, with few demonstrating multicycle degradation capability, limiting sustained PD-L1 depletion and long-term tumor suppression (22). Therefore, expanding PD-L1 clearance across the full cellular landscape of the TIME and engineering degraders that can recycle for repeated usage are essential to achieve durable remodeling of the immunosuppressive network, and to sustain therapeutic responses.

To address these challenges, we reported a multicycle degrader, termed as RECYC (Receptor-mediated Endolysosomal recYcling Chimera), engineered for sustained and recyclable PD-L1 removal to reprogram the tumor immune network.

Results

Broad Immune Checkpoint Distribution in the TIME Links to Immunotherapy Responses.

To explore the potential impact of the checkpoint-modulated tumor-immune network, we first analyzed the expression patterns of multiple immune checkpoints within the TIME. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1A, in the mouse 4T1 tumor model, single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis of tumor tissue and uniform manifold approximation and projection clustering revealed distinct tumor and immune cell populations. We observed broad expressions of various immune checkpoints (Lag3 encoding LAG3, Havcr2 encoding TIM3, Pvr encoding PVR, Vsir encoding VISTA, and Cd274 encoding PD-L1) across tumor cells, monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, DCs, and T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B–F). High PD-L1 (encoded by Cd274) protein expression in the 4T1 TIME was further confirmed by flow cytometry (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). To validate these findings in human tumors, we analyzed the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from gastric cancer patients treated with anti-PD-1 antibodies (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). Consistent with the mouse tumor data, we observed broad expression of immune checkpoints in both tumor and myeloid cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 I and J). Notably, compared with patients who failed to respond to ICB therapy (non-responders), treatment-responsive patients exhibited significantly reduced expressions of all examined immune checkpoints (CD274, LAG3, HAVCR2, PVR, and VSIR) across all major TIME cell populations (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 I and J). Together, these analyses indicate that both tumor cells and myeloid cells broadly express immune checkpoints, and that their expression levels are associated with clinical patient responses to immunotherapy.

Design and Characterization of a PH-Responsive RECYC Degrader for PD-L1.

Building on our observation that both tumor cells and myeloid cells broadly express immune checkpoints and that those expression levels correlate with clinical responses to immunotherapy, we selected PD-L1 as our target owing to its established role in clinical practice and designed a RECYC eTPD degrader capable of multicycle, sustained PD-L1 removal and consequent remodeling of the TIME (Fig. 1A). RECYC is an asymmetric two-module chimera: one module binds PD-L1, and the other binds a lysosomal-shuttle receptor to drive target endocytosis and lysosomal degradation. Based on the scRNA-seq analyses, the CI-M6PR was found to be broadly expressed across diverse cell types, especially myeloid cells in the TIME of both mouse and human tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Given that, we selected CI-M6PR as the endolysosomal shuttle receptor for the RECYC system. To achieve recyclable degradation, our design was inspired by the catalytic principles of proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and molecular glues: rather than requiring ultra-tight, irreversible target binding, effective catalytic degraders typically employ moderate-affinity (μM-range) target binders to enable transient target engagement and ternary complex formation coupled with high-affinity E3 ligase binders that drive efficient target ubiquitination, while allowing turnover of the degrader (38, 39). Following this principle, RECYC shall incorporate a PD-L1-binding module with micromolar, pH-sensitive affinity together with a pH-stable high-affinity lysosomal receptor ligand.

For the PD-L1–binding module, we surveyed reported peptides and nanobodies and selected a PD-L1-binding peptide (PBinder) with a reported Kd of approximately 0.51 µM (SI Appendix, Table S1) (40). Additionally, the selected sequence (NYSKPTDRQYHF) contains histidine and aspartate residues, which are known to become protonated under acidic conditions to weaken binding in endosomes. For the lysosomal shuttle module, we selected a DNA aptamer with high-nanomolar affinity for CI-M6PR, a receptor broadly expressed across TIME cell types (41–43). Reported examples show that certain aptamer–protein interactions remain stable under acidic conditions (44, 45), suggesting that this type of ligand has potential for co-recycling with CI-M6PR.

We next quantitatively assessed the apparent binding of each RECYC module on two key TIME populations, 4T1 tumor cells and RAW264.7 myeloid cells, by flow cytometry at physiological (pH 7.4) and endosomal (pH 5.3) pH (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The PD-L1 binding peptide (PBinder) displayed apparent Kd values of 0.75 µM (4T1) and 1.4 µM (RAW264.7) at pH 7.4, weakening to 1.2 µM and 2.0 µM at pH 5.3, respectively, showing a ~40 to 60% decrease. (Fig. 1C). In contrast, the CI-M6PR aptamer bound with apparent Kd values of 30.4 to 33.3 nM (4T1) and 33.4 to 26.9 nM (RAW264.7) with negligible pH dependence (Fig. 1D). These data confirm the intended pH-switchable behavior of the PD-L1 module and the pH-stable, high-affinity CI-M6PR engagement required for recycling.

To assemble RECYC, we genetically fused the PD-L1 peptide to the duck circovirus HUH endonuclease domain (dRep), expressed the PBinder-duck circovirus–derived HUH endonuclease domain (dRep) fusion in E. coli, and then performed site-specific ligation to a CI-M6PR aptamer bearing the cognate dRep recognition sequence, producing a covalent bispecific peptide–aptamer conjugate (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3) (46–48). SDS-PAGE showed a clear mobility shift after conjugation with >90% conversion to the degrader, confirming the efficient, stoichiometric (1:1) assembly of RECYC construct (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Table S2).

We further performed live-cell confocal microscopy to validate the capture–release mechanism of RECYC. Co-incubation of Alexa488-labeled PBinder-dRep and Cy3-labeled CI-M6PR aptamer with 4T1 and RAW264.7 cells resulted in strong colocalized membrane fluorescence at both pH 6.8 and 7.4 (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Upon shifting to more acidic conditions (pH 5.3 or 6.0), the Alexa488 signal decreased remarkably, indicating the dissociation of the PBinder from PD-L1. In contrast, the Cy3 signal remained membrane-associated, demonstrating pH-stable engagement of CI-M6PR by the aptamer (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Quantitative fluorescence analysis further confirmed this pH-responsive capture-release pattern. These findings support the RECYC design, wherein CI-M6PR binding is maintained across endosomal acidification while PD-L1 is selectively released in late endosomes. By using CI-M6PR’s broad tissue distribution, RECYC is expected to achieve sustained membrane PD-L1 removal across TIME populations while avoiding the one-time consumption common to many degraders.

RECYC Mediates PD-L1 Internalization and Multicycle Degradation Across Tumor and Myeloid Cells.

We next examined whether the asymmetric RECYC degrader could mediate CI-M6PR-dependent PD-L1 internalization and degradation. PBinder-dRep was conjugated to either the CI-M6PR aptamer (RECYC for PD-L1) or to a scrambled DNA control sequence (PBinder-Rnd). In both 4T1 and RAW 264.7 cells, confocal microscopy revealed substantial intracellular accumulation of RECYC within 0.5 h, whereas PBinder-Rnd remained largely extracellular, indicating that CI-M6PR targeting is essential for efficient internalization (Fig. 2 A and B). Flow cytometry revealed a strong RECYC signal, indicating efficient intracellular accumulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Time-dependent confocal microscopy imaging and subsequent quantitative analysis further showed the intracellular trafficking itinerary of RECYC. Within 0.5 h, RECYC fluorescence mostly colocalized with clathrin, consistent with clathrin-mediated endocytosis as the primary entry route. By 1.5 h, RECYC signal shifted to overlap with early endosome antigen 1, confirming its progression to early endosomes (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). These observations indicate that RECYC undergoes standard CI-M6PR-mediated endocytic pathways, similar to those of the native ligand pathway.

To evaluate RECYC-enabled PD-L1 degradation, 4T1 and RAW 264.7 cells were treated with RECYC and co-stained for PD-L1 and lysosomal markers. We observed time-dependent loss of PD-L1-associated green fluorescence (primarily on cell surface) accompanied by increased lysosomal colocalization, while RECYC signal intensity remained stable (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Flow cytometry results revealed a concentration-dependent reduction of surface PD-L1 after 18 h RECYC treatment (Fig. 2E), and Western blotting further confirmed a time-dependent decrease in total PD-L1 levels (Fig. 2 F and G). Additionally, lysosomal inhibition using chloroquine or bafilomycin A1 restored PD-L1 expression levels, whereas proteasomal inhibition by MG132 had no effect (Fig. 2 H and I). These findings establish that RECYC effectively targets PD-L1 for degradation via endolysosomal pathways.

Next, to investigate RECYC’s multicycle degradation capacity, we conducted a multicycle uptake assay. RECYC’s PD-L1 binding domain was first blocked by pre-incubation with unlabeled recombinant PD-L1 extracellular domain (ECD) to form RECYC-PD-L1ECD complexes. These blocked complexes were then incubated with cells, followed by removal of unbound complexes. Subsequently, Alexa488-labeled PD-L1ECD was added to quantify repeated rounds of PD-L1ECD uptake by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2J). Both 4T1 and RAW 264.7 cells treated with RECYC-PD-L1ECD complexes showed substantial uptake of subsequently added Alexa488-PD-L1ECD, suggesting preserved PD-L1-binding capacity after RECYC recycling (Fig. 2K). To validate CI-M6PR’s role in multicycle degradation, we established two single-cycle degradation control groups: 1) a RECYC variant containing intracellularly degradable aptamers with triple disulfide bonds (SS-RECYC), and 2) monensin treatment to inhibit CI-M6PR recycling from trans-Golgi network (Fig. 2L) (49). Both single-cycle degradation controls impaired PD-L1 degradation compared to RECYC, demonstrating CI-M6PR-mediated recycling is essential for RECYC’s multi-round degradation (Fig. 2M). Furthermore, to investigate the recycling potential of RECYC directly, we performed time-course confocal imaging experiments in 4T1 and RAW264.7 cells. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S8, the Cy3-labeled aptamer predominantly co-localized with lysosomes at 2 h. By 4 h, the degree of lysosome-aptamer colocalization decreased, and a portion of the aptamer signal reappeared at the cell surface. This pattern is consistent with the recycling of the CI-M6PR-aptamer complex back to the plasma membrane. Collectively, these results indicate that RECYC mediates efficient, multicycle PD-L1 degradation in both tumor and myeloid cells.

PD-L1 Degradation in Tumor Cells and Myeloid Cells Enhances CD8+ T Cell Responses.

Having shown the multicycle degradation PD-L1 ability of RECYC in cells, we next evaluated whether RECYC-mediated PD-L1 degradation enhances CD8+ T cell antitumor activity ex vivo. Primary naïve CD8+ T cells were isolated from spleens of tumor-free mice. 4T1 tumor cells were pretreated for 48 h with one of the different groups: PD-L1 mAb, PBinder-Rnd, RECYC, or RECYC with monensin. Treated tumor cells were then co-cultured with CD8+ T cells for 48 h, and T cell activation and function were analyzed (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C–E, flow cytometry results showed that CD8+ T cells co-cultured with RECYC-pretreated tumor cells exhibited significantly higher expression of activation markers CD25 and CD69, and the lysosomal degranulation marker CD107a compared to all controls, including PD-L1 blockade, non-targeting DNA, and single-cycle degradation pretreatments. These CD8+ T cells co-cultured with RECYC-treated tumors also displayed the highest tumor-killing ability (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 F and G) and IFN-γ secretion (SI Appendix, Fig. S9H), correlating well with their enhanced activation profiles.

In addition to tumor cell, we performed an analogous co-culture experiment using tumor-associated myeloid cells (CD11b+) to evaluate how RECYC-treated myeloid cells modulate CD8+ T cell responses (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 I and J). Consistent with tumor cell results, RECYC-pretreated myeloid cells induced the strongest CD8+ T cell activation and cytotoxicity (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 K and L), as well as highest IFN-γ secretion (SI Appendix, Fig. S9M) compared to all control groups (PD-L1 mAb, PBinder-Rnd, and RECYC+monensin). Collectively, these ex vivo assays demonstrate that multicycle PD-L1 removal by RECYC on TIME-resident PD-L1+ cells (tumor and myeloid cells) significantly enhanced CD8+ T cell activation and effector functions, supporting RECYC’s potential to remodel the TIME and improve antitumor immunity.

RECYC Induces Tumor Regression in Immune-Cold and Immune-Hot Murine Cancer Models.

We next evaluated RECYC therapeutic efficacy in vivo using both immunologically cold and hot murine tumor models. Biodistribution studies showed that RECYC remains detectable in the tumor microenvironment for at least 24 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). In the immune-cold 4T1 model (low T cell infiltration, exhausted phenotype) (Fig. 3 A and B), RECYC treatment produced substantial tumor regression, with treated mice exhibiting significantly smaller tumor volumes and lower tumor weights than antibody blockade or non-targeting scramble groups (PBinder-Rnd), and outperformed the single-cycle degradation (RECYC + monensin) (Fig. 3 C and D). No obvious toxicity or pathological changes in the major organs were detected for RECYC-treated mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). To better assess the clinical translational potential of RECYC, we employed two robust models: the orthotopic 4T1 mammary tumor model and the spontaneous MMTV-PyMT mammary tumor model. Consistent with our hypothesis, we found RECYC enabled superior antitumor activity compared with an anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (Fig. 3 E–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). We also observed a similar therapeutic efficacy pattern in the immune-hot CT26 model (high T cell infiltration, less-exhausted) (Fig. 3 H–K). RECYC treatment showed significantly inhibited tumor growth relative to antibody blockade and RECYC + monensin groups in the CT26 model, further confirming RECYC’s superior efficacy. Hematoxylin and Eosin and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining revealed larger necrotic regions and reduced Ki67 expression in RECYC-treated tumors than control-treated ones (Fig. 3L and SI Appendix, Fig. S11), consistent with effective antitumor activity and reduced proliferation. Together, these in vivo results demonstrate that RECYC realizes robust tumor control in both immune-cold and -hot tumors, supporting its potential to mediate durable antitumor responses across diverse TIME landscapes.

RECYC Treatment Reduces PD-L1 Levels Within the TIME.

Given the significant tumor regression observed after RECYC treatment, we next examined its correlation with PD-L1 degradation across both tumor cells and tumor-associated myeloid cells (DCs, TAMs, and TANs) (Fig. 4A). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that while PD-L1 blockade and single-cycle degradation (RECYC + monensin) reduced PD-L1 levels on tumor cells (CD45-) and myeloid cells (CD11b+), RECYC treatment produced the most pronounced PD-L1 depletion (Fig. 4 B and C). More specifically, RECYC treatment generated the lowest PD-L1 levels across different myeloid populations, including DCs (CD11c+), TAMs (F4/80+), and TANs (Ly6G+) (Fig. 4D). Consistently, PD-L1 IHC staining of tumor tissues confirmed RECYC achieved more effective PD-L1 degradation within the TIME compared to blockade or one-cycle degradation controls (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, correlation analyses revealed that PD-L1 levels in tumor tissue, tumor cell, and myeloid cell were negatively associated with tumor volume, suggesting the immunosuppressive role of PD-L1 in TIME (Fig. 4F and SI Appendix, Figs. S13 and S14). We further extended this analysis to the CT26, 4T1 orthotopic, and spontaneous MMTV-PyMT mammary tumor models, and recorded similar observations across all three models (SI Appendix, Figs. S12, S13, and S15). As illustrated by flow cytometry, RECYC greatly reduced PD-L1 levels within the CT26 TIME, outperforming PD-L1 blockade and one-cycle PD-L1 degradation groups (SI Appendix, Figs. S12, S13, and S15 A–D). The PD-L1 expression also positively correlated with CT26 tumor volume (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 E and F). These findings demonstrate that RECYC induces potent PD-L1 degradation across both tumor and myeloid populations within the TIME, establishing a mechanistic basis for its robust antitumor efficacy in both immune-cold and hot tumor models.

PD-L1 Removal Enhances Adaptive and Innate Immunity Within the TIME.

Given that RECYC induced effective TIME PD-L1 removal and tumor regression, we next sought to examine the underlying immune-modulatory mechanism in the TIME. We first evaluated the T cell-mediated adaptive antitumor immunity after RECYC treatment (Fig. 5A). Flow cytometry results of 4T1 tumors demonstrated that RECYC treatment produced greater intratumoral T cell infiltration than PD-L1 blockade, scramble control, and one-cycle degradation group (Fig. 5B). RECYC further showed the highest expression levels of CD25, CD69, and CD107a on CD8+ T cells, indicating robust T cell activation, enhanced effector function, and increased degranulation (Fig. 5C). Similar results regarding the improvements in T cell infiltration and activation by RECYC treatment were also observed in the CT26, 4T1 orthotopic, and spontaneous MMTV-PyMT mammary tumor models (SI Appendix, Figs. S12, S13, and S16 A–C), indicating that RECYC enhances adaptive immunity across diverse TIMEs.

We then evaluated the phenotype and functional changes of myeloid populations after RECYC treatment (Fig. 5D). As shown in Fig. 5E, RECYC reduced overall myeloid infiltration, producing significantly downregulated TAMs and TANs compared to other control groups. In addition to cell numbers, RECYC effectively reprogrammed tumor-associated myeloid functional phenotypes toward a more pro-inflammatory, antigen-presenting state. Specifically, DCs in RECYC-treated TIME showed the highest proportions of MHC-II+ and CD83+ populations, indicating the improved antigen presentation (Fig. 5F). Macrophages and neutrophils displayed significantly increased activation and inflammation markers with higher proportions of CD86+ and iNOS+ TAMs and TANs, and concurrent reduced expression in immunosuppressive markers CD163 and ARG1 (Fig. 5 G–K). Consistent reprogramming of myeloid cell immunity in TIME was also observed with RECYC treatment in the CT26, 4T1 orthotopic, and spontaneous MMTV-PyMT mammary tumor models (SI Appendix, Figs. S12, S13, and S16 D–G). IHC analysis of the 4T1 and CT26 models further confirmed these immunological changes, demonstrating increased T cell infiltration and a reduced myeloid cell population in the TIME (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). These results indicate that multicycle PD-L1 removal remodels the TIME by simultaneously enhancing adaptive CD8+ T cell responses and reprogramming innate myeloid populations to immunogenic active phenotypes.

PD-L1 Removal by RECYC Reprograms the Tumor-Immune Cell Network.

Crosstalk between tumor and immune cells forms a complex regulatory network in the TIME that can strongly influence the therapeutic responses (50). To understand how RECYC-mediated PD-L1 degradation remodels the immune-modulatory network, we integrated phenotypic and functional readouts across tumor, myeloid, and T cell populations. Correlation analyses illustrate that RECYC-induced PD-L1 depletion on myeloid populations is closely associated with reprogramming of myeloid functional phenotypes (CD83, CD86, CD163, iNOS, and ARG1) in TAMs, TANs, and DCs (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S18). In parallel, reduced PD-L1 levels on tumor and myeloid cells also correlates with enhanced CD8+ T cells activation, as measured by CD25, CD69, and CD107a expression. These coordinated changes in adaptive and innate immunity mechanistically underpin the improved immunotherapy response and antitumor efficacy. To sum up, these data indicate that recyclable checkpoint degradation converts an immunosuppressive TIME into an immunostimulatory state (Fig. 6C). By multicycle degrading PD-L1 across diverse TIME compartments, RECYC disrupts myeloid cell-mediated immunosuppression while enhancing antigen presentation and effector T cell responses, thereby remodeling the tumor-immune network for enhanced immunotherapy.

Discussion

Two features identify RECYC from existing eTPD approaches for checkpoint degradation. First, many reported eTPD modalities operate stoichiometrically through a one-time consumption (27, 36, 51–53), which limits durable efficacy when targets are abundant or dynamically replenished, such as immune checkpoints like PD-L1 (54, 55). In contrast, the RECYC’s moderate-binding, pH-responsive PD-L1 binder that dissociates under endosomal acidity, while the CI-M6PR aptamer remains engaged, enabling carrier co-recycling and repeated target capture. This modality converted transient PD-L1 blockade into sustained, multicycle clearance in cellular and in vivo assays, substantially improving PD-L1 removal and downstream immune activation. Second, RECYC targets TIME as an integrated system for checkpoint elimination. Most reported eTPD platforms target tumor-associated markers (e.g., EGFR, BRD4, CD24) (27, 56, 57) or TAM-associated proteins (e.g., IL-4R, HDAC3) (53, 58). PD-L1 degraders reported to date primarily focus on tumor cell PD-L1 (34–36), failing to address checkpoint biology across TIME populations (59–61). In our study, RECYC was shown to mediate efficient PD-L1 degradation across tumor cells and myeloid subsets to disrupt immunosuppressive networks, broadening the therapeutic scope of checkpoint clearance. Furthermore, in contrast to previously reported PD-L1 degraders (e.g., glycan- or antibody-based strategies like LYTAC and CYpHER) (62–64), the RECYC design demonstrates superior recycling efficiency and sustained efficacy without the engagement of the FcRn pathway. The synergistic combination of a pH-stable CI-M6PR-targeting aptamer and a pH-responsive PD-L1-binding peptide facilitates sustained, multicycle PD-L1 degradation. Meanwhile, although RECYC is designed to recycle, the apparent effective dose is still influenced by the intrinsic affinity and epitope properties of the PD-L1–binding peptide. Importantly, the recycling-enabled multicycle degradation mechanism reflects capture–release turnover rather than a fixed concentration requirement, and thus can operate across dosing conditions to ensure sufficient target engagement. Future studies are still required to optimize the PD-L1-binding module toward higher binding affinity to reducing the degrader concentration.

Placing these findings in a clinical context reveals the potential impact. PD-1/PD-L1 blockade has revolutionized cancer treatment for many patients, but responses remain variable across cancer types (65, 66). Approved agents such as atezolizumab are widely used for cancers, but several large-scale multicenter trials (e.g., IMpassion130, IMvigor130) have reported that atezolizumab plus chemotherapy fails to improve survival (67–69). The suboptimal response to atezolizumab may result from limited epitope masking, insufficient target engagement due to transient binding, and long exposure-induced adaptive resistance. In our head-to-head comparisons, RECYC outperformed atezolizumab in both tumor regression and immune activation, likely due to its multicycle PD-L1 elimination across both tumor and immune cells rather than only transient inhibition. Our findings support the clinical translational potential of RECYC as a next-generation immunotherapy platform and motivate the development of combination strategies to maximize therapeutic benefit and overcome resistance across patient populations. However, a potential limitation of the current RECYC design is that endosomal release may allow a fraction of PD-L1 to recycle back to the plasma membrane during endosomal sorting, thereby partially reducing overall degradation efficiency. Future work should therefore focus on optimizing the system’s pH-dependent catch-release profile to further improve both degradation efficacy and recycling efficiency.

Safety profile is a central consideration for clinical translation. Immunotherapies are reported to cause immune-related adverse events, including cytokine release syndrome, autoimmune manifestations, and off-target tissue inflammation (70). Importantly, in our mouse models, RECYC was well tolerated with no observed organ toxicity or weight loss. The RECYC construct utilizes exclusively biological components (recombinant fusion protein and unmodified ssDNA), potentially minimizing systemic immune activation and antigenicity. Also, the recyclable mechanism could allow lower dosing frequency relative to stoichiometric blockades. Nevertheless, rigorous PD/PD, biodistribution, and potential long-term immune-remodeling studies in larger and more clinically relevant models are required.

Mechanistically, RECYC’s antitumor activity reflects coordinated remodeling of the tumor-immune network following PD-L1 removal. RECYC treatment increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation, and concurrently reprogrammed myeloid populations, decreasing immunosuppressive TAM and TAN infiltration while tuning M1/M2 and N1/N2 balance toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes. It is well-reported that high PD-L1 expression contributes to a suppressive intercellular network through multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of T cell cytotoxicity via PD-1-PD-L1 interactions and immunosuppressive cytokine secretion (71), promotion of tumor progression via EMT and enhanced angiogenesis (72), as well as resistance against immunotherapies (73). Our correlation analyses reveal that RECYC-mediated TIME-wide PD-L1 clearance correlates with enhanced adaptive and innate immunity as well as improved tumor control. These findings support a model wherein systemic checkpoint clearance reprograms the tumor-immune network to establish an immunostimulatory microenvironment. However, the patient scRNA-seq dataset analyzed in this study was derived from gastric cancer, which may not fully represent all tumor types in clinical settings, and further validation across multiple patient tumor types remains to be explored.

In summary, RECYC represents a next-generation eTPD design that enables recycling-based, multicycle degradation of PD-L1 on tumor and immune cells, thereby remodeling the TIME to promote robust and durable antitumor immunity. By addressing both the limited scope to only tumor cells and inefficient turnover of current eTPDs, RECYC establishes a transformative platform for extending recyclable degrader design to enable TIME-wide protein clearance.

Materials and Methods

Mice.

As previously described by us and others (74, 75), Balb/c mice were purchased from Institute of Hematology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Tianjin, China). FVB/NJGpt-Tg(MMTV-PyMT)/Gpt mice were provided by GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Drum Tower Hospital (approval number: 2020AE01064).

Cell Lines.

As previously described by us and others (74, 75), Mouse breast cancer cell line 4T1, colon cancer cell line CT26, and mouse macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 were purchased from the Cell Bank of Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Shanghai, China).

Statistical Analysis.

As previously described by us and others (74, 75), R software (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software were used for data analysis and graphic representation. Comparison of two groups was performed by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, or Mann-Whitney test. Multiple groups were compared by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by multiple comparison test.
