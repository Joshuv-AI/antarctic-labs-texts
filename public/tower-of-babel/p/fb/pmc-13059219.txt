Determinants of repeat miscarriage among adult women of reproductive age in Guanggu, Wuhan, China
Authors: Jing-Quan Lu, Jingzhan Lu, Li-Na Hu, Yu Song, Yu Huang, He-Yu Liu, Jing-Qiu Guo, Song-Yan Chen, Zheng-Yan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveUnintended pregnancy leading to induced abortion is a significant public health concern affecting women’s reproductive health globally. In China, a high rate of repeat induced abortion persists, yet the determinants among specific populations like those in Wuhan’s Guanggu area are not fully elucidated. Identifying high-risk groups is crucial for developing targeted interventions to reduce repeat procedures. The study aimed to investigate the factors influencing repeat miscarriage among adult women of reproductive age in Guanggu, Wuhan, China.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the case data of 2,428 women aged 18–49 years who underwent early induced abortion due to unintended pregnancy at the Guanggu Hospital Area of Wuhan Third Hospital from January to December 2022. Data were analyzed using Chi-squared tests and logistic regression model (α = 0.05).ResultsAmong the 2,428 women with unintended pregnancy who underwent early induced abortion, the average number of induced abortions was 1.77 ± 0.57, and the average number of children was 0.76 ± 0.79. Women aged 20–29 years accounted for the highest proportion of cases (50.95%), while 55.68% of the women had a marriage history (including 53.67% married and 2.02% divorced) and 69.32% had non-Wuhan household registration. The overall rate of repeat induced abortion (defined as having ≥ 2 times of induced abortion) was 49.84%, with 54.41% of women who had children experiencing repeat induced abortion and 45.59% of childless women having repeat induced abortion. Logistic regression analysis showed that married women had a higher rate of repeat induced abortion than unmarried women (OR = 1.80, 95%CI:1.61–2.02, p < 0.001); women who had children had a higher rate of repeat induced abortion than those without children (OR = 1.87, 95%CI:1.67–2.10, p < 0.001); and women with non-Wuhan household registration had a higher rate of repeat induced abortion than those with Wuhan household registration (OR = 1.23, 95%CI:1.07–1.42, p = 0.0005). Additionally, women who had given birth to two boys had a higher risk of early induced abortion due to unintended pregnancy compared with those who had one boy (OR = 11.82, 95%CI:1.11–2.99, p = 0.02).ConclusionInduced abortion is still very prevalent in the Guanggu area, Wuhan. However, married women of childbearing age without Wuhan household registration and those who have given birth to two boys are high-risk groups for repeat induced abortion. Therefore, repeat induced abortion’s eradication strategies should include patient-centered contraceptive guidance targeting these high-risk groups.Keywords: Early induced abortion, Reproductive health, Unintended pregnancy, Wuhan

Introduction

Globally, 38% of pregnancies are unplanned, and 22% result in induced abortions [1]. There are 13 million induced abortions performed in China every year, which is approximately 3.5–7 million more than the data published in the Health Statistics Yearbook [2]. Induced abortion is a temporary solution to contraceptive failure, which has serious consequences for women’s fertility, reproductive health, and mental health [3]. As per the Healthy China Action (2019–2030) formulated by the National Health Commission and the Healthy China Action Promotion Committee before the end of June 2019, it is imperative to protect the reproductive health of women [4].

Induced abortion, especially repeat induced abortion, imposes far-reaching and multi-dimensional harms on women’s fertility, reproductive health, and mental health that extend beyond immediate surgical risks. For fertility, even a single induced abortion can disrupt the physiological integrity of the endometrium. This is because mechanical curettage damages the basal layer of the endometrium, reducing its ability to support embryo implantation and increasing the risk of subsequent infertility, recurrent spontaneous abortion, or ectopic pregnancy. In terms of reproductive health, induced abortion disrupts the normal vaginal microecological balance and uterine immune environment, elevating the risk of short-term complications such as intrauterine adhesions, pelvic inflammatory disease, and endometritis, as well as long-term sequelae including premature ovarian failure and an increased lifetime risk of gynecological malignancies such as endometrial cancer [5]. Mentally, the experience of induced abortion—often accompanied by feelings of guilt, anxiety, or grief over the loss of a pregnancy—can trigger or exacerbate mental health issues: clinical studies have linked induced abortion to a 30% higher risk of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms compared to women with planned pregnancies [6]. However, the repeat abortions further amplify this psychological burden by reinforcing feelings of loss of control over reproductive health, leading to chronic depression or anxiety that impairs daily functioning and interpersonal relationships. These cumulative physical and mental harms not only diminish women’s quality of life but also place additional burdens on public health systems. Avoiding induced abortion, particularly repeat cases, is therefore critical not only to protecting individual women’s reproductive autonomy and long-term health but also to advancing broader public health goals of reducing preventable reproductive morbidity and supporting women’s lifelong reproductive well-being.

However, the prior studies on induced abortion in China have largely focused on migrant women in major municipalities or adolescents [2], with limited attention to the interplay of marital status, household registration, and parity in driving repeat induced abortion in Guanggu’s distinct demographic context. This knowledge gap underscores the need for a retrospective analysis of early induced abortion cases at the Guanggu Hospital Area of Wuhan Third Hospital to clarify the status quo, identify high-risk groups, and fill regional data gaps—ultimately laying the groundwork for evidence-based strategies to reduce unintended pregnancies and protect the reproductive health of childbearing-age women in this area.

To gain a better understanding of the population characteristics of women with unintended pregnancies and undergoing early induced abortion in Wuhan in 2022, a retrospective analysis of such early induced abortions in the outpatient clinic of the Guanggu Hospital Area of Wuhan Third Hospital was conducted from January to December 2022. The aim of this is study is to provide a scientific basis for reducing unintended pregnancies, lowering the induced abortion rate, practicing the Healthy China Initiative, and improving the reproductive health of women of childbearing age in the Guanggu area of Wuhan.

Participants and methods

Information collection

This study was conducted from January 2022 to December 2022 in the outpatient clinic of the Guanggu Hospital Area of the Wuhan Third Hospital, where approximately 200 early induced abortions were performed monthly. Using both collective and one-on-one education modes, post-abortion care (PAC) health education was provided before and after surgery, as well as patient-centered contraceptive counseling. A total of 2,428 cases of unintended pregnancy abortions were recorded in 2022.

Information collection

Data extraction was conducted by two independent trained researchers to ensure accuracy, with discrepancies resolved via consultation with a third senior researcher. The extraction procedure followed a standardized, pre-tested data collection form, which included fields for: sociodemographic information (age, household registration status, marital status, education level), reproductive history (number of previous pregnancies, live births, induced abortions, natural miscarriages), current pregnancy details (gestational age at abortion), and clinical records (type of abortion procedure, post-abortion care participation). All data were extracted from electronic medical records of the Guanggu Hospital Area of Wuhan Third Hospital, with cross-verification against paper-based surgical consent forms and follow-up notes to confirm missing or ambiguous entries. For inclusion in the study, participants met the following criteria: (1) aged 18–49 years; (2) underwent early induced abortion (gestational age ≤ 12 weeks) due to unintended pregnancy at the study site between January 1 and December 31, 2022; (3) had complete electronic medical records to ensure data integrity. Exclusion criteria were: (1) induced abortion for medical reasons; (2) spontaneous abortion or ectopic pregnancy; (3) incomplete medical records that could not be supplemented via cross-verification; (4) participants who declined implicit consent.

Data analysis

Python was used for raw data exploration and processing, while STATA SE17 was used for statistical and regression analysis. The number of induced abortions was defined as the number of times they had previously undergone intentional surgical or medical termination of pregnancy (excluding natural miscarriages, ectopic pregnancies, or molar pregnancies). Repeat induced abortions were defined as patients with two or more induced abortions. For uncertainties in pregnancy and birth records: (1) For women who registered pregnancies at the health facility but delivered elsewhere, we supplemented data by reviewing their prior prenatal care records and contacting the participant via phone to confirm delivery outcomes, ensuring accurate recording of “actual number of births”; (2) For participants lost to follow-up, we excluded them from the analysis to avoid data bias, as their pregnancy/birth status could not be verified; (3) All ambiguous entries were resolved by two independent researchers consulting with the participant’s former attending physicians or excluding the case if verification was impossible. A total of 1,210 participants (49.84%) were eligible based on this definition.

Using STATA, the chi-squared test was used to detect the relationship between potential risk variables and repeat induced abortions. After using the chi-squared test to screen for the variables (e.g., marital status, household registration, parity) that were bivariately associated with having a history of repeat induced abortions, logistic regression analysis was further applied to identify independent factors for this main outcome variable. The reference P value < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

Basic information

This study included a total of 2,428 participants who had early induced abortions, with the average number of induced abortions being 1.77 ± 0.57, the highest number of abortions being 7, and the average number of children being 0.76 ± 0.79. The oldest participant was 49 years old, while the youngest was 18 years old, the average age was 28, and women aged 20–29 who had induced abortions made up half of the total participants. Most women who had an early induced abortion due to an unintended pregnancy were married, accounting for 53.67%, while 44.32% of unintended pregnant women were unmarried and 2.02% were divorced. Only 30.68% of them had Wuhan household registration (hukou) (Table 1).

The results of chi-squared verification of factors influencing induced abortion are shown in Table 2. The chi-squared test revealed that marriage history, age, fertility history, household registration, and number of children were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Repeat induced abortions were more common in married women than in unmarried women [Odds Ratio (OR) = 1.80, 95% Confidence Interval (CI):1.61–2.02, p < 0.001]).Women who had children had a higher rate of repeat miscarriage than women who had not had children (OR = 1.87, 95% CI:1.67–2.10, p < 0.001). However, there was no correlation between educational level and the number of repeat induced abortions in this sample (p = 0.08). Therefore, the educational level was not considered when developing the final model.

The results of the logistic regression analysis model of the factors influencing induced abortion are reported in Table 3. The number of children, the number of girls, and the number of boys were modeled and compared separately, and the R2 value of the number of children was 0.135, indicating that the regression model of the number of children was the best fit. Age, fertility history, household registration in Wuhan, and number of children were all statistically significant independent variables (p < 0.05).

Associations between variables and repeat induced abortions in unintended pregnancy

Older age, a history of induced abortion, non-Wuhan household registration, and having more children were associated with a higher incidence of repeat induced abortions. Women with children had a higher rate of repeat miscarriage than women without children (OR = 4.07, 95% CI:3.43–4.82, p 0.001). Women with non-Wuhan household registration experienced more miscarriages than women with Wuhan household registration (OR = 0.66, 95% CI:0.53–0.81, p 0.001). As the number of children increased, the incidence of repeat induced abortions became more prevalent. The OR value increased from 3.88 to 29.20 as the number of children increased from 1 to 3 (Table 3).

Discussion

Induced abortion mainly performed in young women aged 20–29

Age is considered a risk factor for induced abortion in unintended pregnancies, and as women get older, repeat induced abortions become more common [7]. In this study, the proportion of unintended pregnancies among young women aged 20–29 years accounted for roughly half of the total number of participants, with the reasons possibly related to population distribution in Wuhan City’s Guanggu area. It is also suggested that such young women were unaware of highly effective contraception. Many studies have suggested that induced abortion can result in intrauterine adhesions, infection, irreversible damage to the endometrium, and other complications, all of which have a negative impact on women’s fertility [8]. Induced abortion, especially repeat cases, leads to fertility impairment via complications like intrauterine adhesions, infection, and irreversible endometrial damage—risks that are clinically significant and dose-dependent. Intrauterine adhesions form when curettage damages the non-regenerable endometrial basal layer, blocking fallopian tube ostia or fragmenting the uterine cavity. Previous studies note women with adhesions have 40%–60% lower clinical pregnancy rates and 2–3 times higher miscarriage rates than those with intact endometria [8, 9]. Infection from inadequate aseptic technique or poor post-operative hygiene causes pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), which scars fallopian tubes, raising ectopic pregnancy risk and accounting for female infertility [3]. Irreversible endometrial thinning occurs with repeated curettage, and only 20%–30% of affected women achieve endometrial regeneration even with treatment [9]. These issues not only delay conception but also increase preterm birth or placental abnormalities in subsequent pregnancies, while each additional abortion raises complication odds. This underscores why reducing induced abortion—particularly repeats—is key to protecting women’s reproductive potential and advancing the Healthy China Action’s reproductive health goals. Under China’s current three-child policy, it is critical to protect the fertility of young women of childbearing age and reduce unnecessary abortions, implying the need to focus on reproductive health and effective contraceptive knowledge education for female adolescents. Among women who had miscarriages, 86.9% were due to improper use of condoms, either not using them every time or not using them throughout the entire process. 31.9% of women use safe period contraception. 34.4% use in vitro ejaculation for contraception, and 25.9% use emergency contraceptives for contraception. From these results, it can be seen that the pregnant women included in this study lack correct contraceptive knowledge. For unmarried women after puberty (including college students), in collaboration with the education department and university medical institutions, “Scientific contraception + reproductive health risks” should be incorporated into the freshmen orientation and general education course modules. Case teaching and interactive lectures should be adopted to avoid merely theoretical indoctrination. For married young women, reproductive health consultation clinics are set up in community health service centers to provide personalized guidance based on their fertility needs. For instance, for those who do not plan their families for the time being, a detailed comparison of the effectiveness, safety and applicable scenarios of long-acting reversible contraceptive measures and short-acting contraceptive measures should be made to correct the cognitive misunderstanding that “long-acting contraception = affecting fertility”.(Table 4).

Focus on abortions among college students

In this study, women with non-Wuhan household registration who had induced abortions were more likely to have unintended pregnancies than those with Wuhan household registration who had induced abortions. According to population distribution characteristics, there are more than 800,000 college students in the Guanggu area, accounting for a higher proportion of women with non-Wuhan household registration who have had an induced abortion. College students are nearing the end of puberty, and induced abortions, particularly recurrent abortions, cause reproductive health issues and have a negative impact on future generations. The negative impact primarily refers to the potential sequelae of induced abortion that can affect the outcomes of a woman’s future, desired pregnancies. This includes risks such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and placental complications in subsequent pregnancies, which directly impact the health of the next generation. Therefore, it is critical to ensure that college students receive appropriate sexual and reproductive health education, improve their reproductive health status, and have access to quality health care services.

Rates of repeat induced abortions are high, and patient-centered contraceptive guidance needs to be promoted

Based on studies, it is evident that the likelihood of thin endometrium after one induced abortion is 20%, and the incidence in those with two or more induced abortions rises to 53.33% [9]. The risk accumulates because each instrumental uterine evacuation increases the chance of injuring the regenerative basal layer of the endometrium. This repetitive trauma leads to progressive scarring, fibrosis, and diminished blood flow, explaining why the incidence of thin endometrium rises dramatically with the number of prior procedures. In this study, the rate of repeat induced abortions was as high as 49.84%. Following an induced abortion, it is imperative to assist patients in implementing effective contraception measures in a timely manner, and to avoid unintended pregnancies to reduce the rate of repeat induced abortions, the incidence of thin endometrium, and protect fertility. PAC services have been shown to be effective in lowering the rate of repeat induced abortions [10, 11]. However, the implementation of contraceptive methods is frequently influenced by subjective factors such as the personal bias of PAC counselors, friends, relatives, and so on [12, 13]. Women and clinicians must mutually decide on the best contraceptive method, after discussing the personal values and preferences of the women with respect to the risk of pregnancy, changes in menstrual bleeding, and potential risks of contraceptive methods.

At the hospital level, PAC services have been expanded from a single postoperative consultation to full-process management. Before the operation, the patients’ awareness level and needs regarding contraception were evaluated through questionnaires. During the operation, the key time points for postoperative contraception were briefly restated. One month and three months after the operation, follow-up visits were conducted by phone or online to verify the implementation of contraceptive measures and promptly address any issues arising during the execution. At the family level, encourage spouses to participate in contraceptive decision-making. Invite spouses to attend PAC consultations together to explain the protective effect of mutual participation in contraception on women’s health, correct the traditional notion that contraception is the sole responsibility of women, and enhance men’s awareness of their partners’ reproductive health.

Postpartum contraception

In this study, women who had given birth to two boys were at a higher risk of having an early induced abortion due to an unintended pregnancy. Therefore, the contraceptive education window should be pushed forward in clinical practice, and the use of long-acting contraceptive methods should be increased by providing postpartum contraceptive services and long-acting contraceptive health education to women who have given birth to two boys in order to reduce unintended pregnancies [14–16]. Before a woman is discharged from the hospital after giving birth, obstetricians, in collaboration with gynecologists, provide specialized guidance on postpartum contraception. Based on their reproductive history, long-acting contraceptive measures are recommended as a priority. At the same time, the golden period for postpartum contraception is informed to avoid delaying the contraceptive opportunity due to waiting for menstruation to resume. In addition, women who have given birth to two boys will be included in the community’s key follow-up list. They will be followed up at home or online three and six months after giving birth to verify the use of contraceptive measures. Women who experience side effects should be promptly referred to the hospital to adjust their treatment plans to prevent them from discontinuing contraceptive measures on their own due to poor tolerance to side effects.

Notably, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study to contextualize its findings and guide future research. First, this study adopted a single-center retrospective design, using data only from the Guanggu Hospital Area of Wuhan Third Hospital in 2022; the sample thus lacks representativeness of women with unintended pregnancies and induced abortions across the entire Guanggu neighborhood or Wuhan City, limiting the generalizability of the results. Second, the study focused on analyzing demographic and reproductive-related factors but did not collect or assess detailed information on potential confounding variables such as women’s socioeconomic status, access to reproductive health services, and personal health behaviors, which may have influenced the incidence of repeat induced abortions and were not controlled for in the regression models. Third, as a retrospective analysis, the study relied on existing medical record data; potential information biases cannot be completely ruled out, which may have affected the accuracy of the statistical results. Finally, the study did not include a follow-up component, so it was unable to explore the long-term impacts of unintended pregnancies and induced abortions on women’s reproductive health or the effectiveness of post-abortion contraceptive guidance in reducing repeat abortions over time. These limitations highlight the need for future multi-center, prospective studies with larger sample sizes, comprehensive variable collection, and long-term follow-up to provide more robust evidence for optimizing reproductive health education and contraceptive intervention strategies for women of childbearing age.

Conclusion

This study identified the associated variables for repeat induced abortion following unintended pregnancy among women in the Guanggu neighborhood of Wuhan. Being married, having a history of prior childbirth, possessing non-Wuhan household registration, and notably, having given birth to two boys were related to repeat induced abortion. These findings directly address the study’s primary objective by providing a scientific basis for targeted interventions. It is imperative to prioritize patient-centered contraceptive counseling and long-acting contraceptive promotion for these high-risk groups to reduce the incidence of unintended pregnancy and repeat abortion.
