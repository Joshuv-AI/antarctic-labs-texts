Challenges in ophthalmic consultations and strategic communication
Authors: Mariantonia Ferrara, Giorgia Raguzzi, Greta Mezzetti, Antonio Simonetti, Chiara Ancona, Martina Angi, Giuseppe Querques, Mario R Romano, Carlos Rocha de lossada, Matteo Sacchi, Anna P Salvetti, Giacomo Savini, Antonino Minervino, Luca Brambilla, Vito Romano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore communication challenges in ophthalmic consultations from both patient and clinician perspectives, and to introduce ‘Strategic Communication’ as a structured, evidence-based approach aimed at enhancing patient-provider interactions and improving clinical outcomes.Methods and analysisMulticentre mixed-method study involving online focus discussion groups and surveys. Eight ophthalmologists and eight patients recruited through the Italian national ophthalmic patient association participated in focus groups moderated by a health communication expert. These discussions explored communication challenges and potential solutions, based on participants’ experience. Transcripts were thematically analysed by two independent researchers to identify key challenges and actionable insights. In parallel, quantitative data were collected via two structured questionnaires completed by 139 European ophthalmologists and 184 patients.ResultsKey themes included patients’ limited understanding of their conditions, often influenced by misleading online information, and their strong preference for clear, empathetic communication. Clinicians reported that time constraints in public healthcare limited opportunities for meaningful dialogue, while the involvement of family members further complicated interactions. The ‘Strategic Communication’ framework, particularly the O.D.I. Method (Observe, Demand, Intervene), emerged as a structured framework for tailoring communication strategies to individual needs.Conclusion‘Strategic Communication’ offers a systematic, conceptual, theory-informed approach to address communication challenges in ophthalmology, with potential relevance for patient-centred care helping structured clinician–patient communication in ophthalmology. The present findings support its practical applicability. Future studies are needed to evaluate its impact on clinical and patient-reported outcomes.

Introduction

Effective communication with patients has emerged as a critical competency in modern healthcare, where diagnosis and treatment plans require clarity, empathy and precision. While traditional approaches often rely on clinicians’ inherent interpersonal skills, such unstructured methods may fall short in addressing the diverse and nuanced challenges of patient–provider interactions.1 Research consistently highlights communication gaps during medical consultations, including difficulties in explaining complex conditions, managing expectations and promoting adherence to treatment.2 3 These challenges are particularly pronounced in ophthalmology, where the highly specialised nature of this medical specialty, the technical complexity and limited patient familiarity with ocular conditions can further hinder effective communication.4 5 Despite the well-established role of communication in improving patient outcomes, most existing models lack a structured, evidence-based framework for systematically enhancing communication skills in clinical settings.1 Miscommunication has been linked to increased patient anxiety, reduced trust and suboptimal adherence to care plans.6 Furthermore, structural constraints, such as time-limited consultations, family members’ involvement and disparities in health literacy or cultural background, further intensify these challenges, underscoring the need for more adaptable and strategic approaches. ‘Strategic Communication’ is a structured approach to clinical communication, grounded in established principles of health communication, and aims to support clear and effective interactions between clinicians and patients, moving beyond traditional, personality-driven communication styles. Distinct from unstructured communication models, it focuses on understanding not only the medical problem, but also the patient’s perspective, emotional state and the practical context of the consultation. A central element of this approach is the O.D.I. Method (Osserva, Domanda, Intervieni–Observe, Question, Intervene), which offers a structured simple framework to guide clinical communication. ‘Observe’ refers to assessing patient needs, expectations and relevant contextual factors, including symptom severity, urgency and family involvement. ‘Question’ involves the use of open-ended questions to clarify patient perspectives and concerns and align clinical assumptions with patient understanding. ‘Intervene’ focuses on jointly defining communication strategies and care plans that take into account both clinical priorities and patient preferences, supporting shared decision-making.7 8

In the light of this background, this collaborative study involving healthcare professionals and patients aimed to investigate the main communication challenges in ophthalmology. Building on these findings and grounded in the principles of ‘Strategic Communication’, the study further sought to develop practical, evidence-based questionnaires to guide ophthalmic consultations with the aim of supporting patient satisfaction, treatment adherence and clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

First phase: identification of main communication challenges in ophthalmology

To investigate communication challenges in ophthalmic consultations, a collaborative study was conducted by the ‘Accademia di Comunicazione Strategica’ (Academy of Strategic Communication, a professional training institution specialised in Strategic Communication and Negotiation), the European School for Advanced Studies in Ophthalmology (ESASO) and the Associazione Pazienti Malattie Oculari (Patients Associations Ocular Pathologies, APMO). The study involved a core team of 10 ophthalmologists with diverse subspecialty expertise and 8 patients affected by ophthalmic conditions, ensuring a comprehensive representation of both clinical perspectives and patients experience. Ophthalmologists were purposively selected based on clinical seniority, defined as having at least 5 years of experience, and subspecialty expertise, in order to represent different areas of ophthalmology and capture a broad range of communication challenges across clinical contexts. Patients were recruited on a voluntary basis through the APMO, which enabled the inclusion of participants with heterogeneous ophthalmic conditions and care pathways. This group composition was designed to enable a comprehensive evaluation of communication dynamics from both the provider and patient viewpoints. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and the study conforms to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Qualitative phase: focus group discussion

Structured focus group discussions were employed to facilitate open dialogue regarding communication challenges and experiences during ophthalmic consultations. Two separate focus group sessions were initially conducted with the ophthalmologists, moderated by two experts in health communication (GR and GM). These sessions explored specific communication barriers encountered in clinical practice and the strategies employed to address them. In parallel, one focus group session was conducted with the patient group, also moderated by the communication experts. Discussions focused on patients’ experiences of communication during ophthalmic care, including instances of misunderstanding, emotional responses to diagnoses or treatments and overall satisfaction with provider interactions. The decision to conduct the focus group sessions separately was intended to create a safe and comfortable environment, enabling participants to express their concerns and experiences freely. All sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Quantitative phase: surveys

To complement and validate the qualitative findings, two structured surveys were administered online across multiple European countries. The first survey, distributed by ESASO, targeted ophthalmologists and received 139 responses from clinicians across various subspecialties and healthcare systems. The second survey, disseminated by APMO, was completed by 184 patients diagnosed with a wide range of ophthalmic conditions. Survey items were developed following a focused review of the literature on health communication challenges in clinical medicine, with particular emphasis on issues reported in ophthalmology, with the aim to evaluate perceptions of communication effectiveness, mutual understanding, expectations and satisfaction during ophthalmic consultations. Item generation was further informed by the themes emerging from the qualitative focus group discussions, with the aim of ensuring relevance to both patient and clinician perspectives. Survey items included both closed-ended and Likert-scale questions. The questionnaires were designed as exploratory instruments and were not intended as validated scales. Only the responses of participants who provided informed consent for the use of their survey data for scientific and statistical purposes were included in the analysis.

Data analysis

For the analysis of qualitative data, an inductive thematic analysis was conducted following established qualitative research principles. Transcripts were first read in full to ensure familiarisation with the data. Initial codes were then generated independently by two independent researchers (GR and GM) and applied to meaningful units of text reflecting recurrent concepts related to communication experiences and challenges. Codes were subsequently compared, grouped into broader categories and iteratively refined through discussion, leading to the identification of higher-order themes. Discrepancies in coding or theme interpretation were resolved through interactive discussion and consensus. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics to summarise response distributions and identify overall trends. Comparisons between patient and clinician responses were therefore narrative and intended to highlight convergences and divergences in perceived communication challenges.

Application of strategic communication

To develop the ‘strategic questions’, we adopted a systematic approach integrating patient feedback on physician behaviour with physicians’ observations regarding patients’ perceived expectations. Through this analytical process, several ‘strategic questions’ emerged, designed to facilitate clinical communication, reduce the incidence of cognitive biases, enable the physician to gather information relevant to clinical practice and foster the development of trust and connection with the patient.

Results

First phase: main communication challenges in ophthalmology

The combined analysis of qualitative and quantitative data revealed four core areas of communication challenges within ophthalmic care: (1) patient understanding and information gaps, (2) emotional engagement, (3) time constraints and communication breakdown and (4) relational continuity.

Patient understanding and information gaps

This area emerged as a significant barrier to effective consultations. Patients often struggled to grasp their diagnosis or treatment options, frequently citing the use of overly technical language and insufficient explanation. This lack of clarity was often exacerbated by information they encountered online, which they described as inconsistent or misleading. These observations were echoed in the clinicians’ survey, where 65% reported that patients frequently consulted external sources, and 58.3% expressed concern about the resulting spread of misinformation. Although most physicians felt confident in their ability to understand patient concerns (91.4%), considered their communication effective (87.8%) and stated they avoided medical jargon (84.2%), only 62.6% reported routinely verifying whether patients had understood the information provided. On the other hand, only 29.3% of patients felt they received very clear explanations of their diagnosis. Visual aids, mainly hand-drawn illustrations, were used in 75.5% of consultations to support patient comprehension.

Emotional engagement

A clear disconnect between physician intentions and patient perceptions was highlighted. Patients described a wide range of emotional responses to their condition, including fear, anxiety and uncertainty, particularly when faced with complex diagnoses or unclear prognoses. Although physicians overwhelmingly acknowledged the importance of empathy to manage patients’ anxiety and fear (87.1%) and believed that addressing patients’ emotions improves adherence (98.6%), only 20.1% of patients reported consistently perceiving empathetic communication (χ²=46.2, p<0.001) and the vast majority of them reported experiencing fear or anxiety related to their condition (86.3%). Nearly half of the patients (45.1%) reported that their emotional state was only occasionally acknowledged, with 29.3% feeling inadequately supported and 14.7% stating they never felt emotionally supported during consultations.

Time constraints and communication breakdown

The negative impact of limited consultation time was widely recognised as an important concern in communication within ophthalmic care. Most physicians (58.3%) and patients (54.9%) stated that appointments were often too short to allow for meaningful dialogue and relationship. About half of the patients (52.7%) reported feeling rushed or under pressure during visits, which they believed hindered their ability to ask questions or fully process the information shared. Physicians echoed this sentiment, noting that time limitations restricted their ability to build rapport and address patient concerns. Despite recognising the importance of strong communication, 65.5% of physicians reported never having received formal training in this area. Encouragingly, a large majority (83.4%) expressed interest in developing their skills, particularly in time management, content synthesis and consultation organisation.

Relational continuity

The final theme, relational continuity, was especially important to patients. Many expressed a strong desire for ongoing relationships with their ophthalmologists, emphasising the need to feel seen, heard and understood throughout their care journey. Discontinuity, defined as changing providers or a lack of coordinated follow-up, was described as disorienting and emotionally taxing. The complexity of the healthcare system’s bureaucracy further exacerbated this issue. Trust in a certain clinician appeared to be closely tied to clinical expertise, but also to interpersonal demeanour, consistency and communication style. Survey results reflected this dynamic, and only 26.6% reported strong adherence to treatment, suggesting that trust and understanding may be influencing compliance. In terms of shared decision-making, while 78.4% of physicians stated that they involved patients in treatment planning, only 34.2% of patients reported feeling actively included in those decisions. Furthermore, 34.8% felt that the risks and benefits of proposed treatments were not clearly communicated.

Strategic communication-based questionnaires

Based on the above-mentioned findings and the O.D.I. Method, we developed a comprehensive set of strategic, context-specific, questions which can guide clinicians through various ophthalmic scenarios (tables15) and more specific conditions (online supplemental tables 1-11). These sets of questions aim to foster patient engagement, clarify expectations and strengthen shared decision-making. Regardless of the specific context and condition, at the conclusion of the consultation, we recommend offering a brief, shared summary of what has been discussed. One practical tool is to ask the patient to repeat the next steps in their own words. This not only confirms comprehension but also reveals possible misunderstandings. Additionally, providing concise, written take-home materials tailored to the patient’s situation can reinforce understanding.

Discussion

Clinician–patient interactions in ophthalmology involve unique and complex challenges. While many of the communication challenges identified are well recognised across medical specialties, this study contributes by contextualising them within ophthalmology and by integrating patient and clinician perspectives into a structured, practice-oriented framework. Communication challenges can significantly impact the quality and effectiveness of care. Many patients enter ophthalmic consultations with an incomplete understanding of their condition.9 This is often worsened by online misinformation, which can distort expectations and create confusion. Recent studies in macular degeneration care confirm that inconsistent terminology and limited explanation from providers increase uncertainty and distress.2 This confusion is often paired with anxiety and fear about their health and its consequences. In this context, managing patients’ expectations about prognosis and treatment outcomes becomes especially challenging. These unrealistic expectations can lead to dissatisfaction, even when clinical results are objectively good. Studies in glaucoma care confirm that misalignment between physician expectations and patient beliefs contributes to reduced adherence and dissatisfaction with care, even when clinical outcomes are favourable.10 It is known that cataract surgery is the most performed surgery worldwide. In modern practice, it is also considered a refractive procedure where spectacle independence is a frequent patient goal. Simultaneous vision lenses are often used to achieve this, but they are associated with photic phenomena and dysphotopsias and loss of contrast sensitivity.11 Approximately 10% of patients may report these symptoms preoperatively without optical justification.12 Therefore, effective communication is essential to counsel patients on what they can expect postoperatively.13 A similar communication imperative exists in refractive surgery, where, despite generally high success and satisfaction rates, some patients may experience dry eye or other complications, often without full preoperative awareness, creating the so-called ‘20/20 unhappy patients’.1416 Adhering to prescribed treatments adds another layer of difficulty, especially when routines are new or uncomfortable. Trust is essential but can be undermined by prior negative experiences or perceptions related to a clinician’s age, gender or demeanour. Patients consistently report valuing emotional support and feeling heard on par with clinical competence. They also emphasise the importance of seeing the same provider over time to build trust.8

For clinicians, these challenges are exacerbated by the constraints of the consultation context. Limited time, particularly in public healthcare settings, leaves little opportunity to build the rapport necessary for meaningful communication. A large proportion of ophthalmologists report never having received formal communication training, despite acknowledging its importance for patient adherence and satisfaction.1 The presence of family members or other influencers further complicates the dynamics, often introducing competing priorities or emotional tensions. Communicating complex diagnoses, especially when the prognosis is severe or symptoms are minimal, poses another layer of difficulty, demanding both clarity and empathy. Meta-analytic data confirm that effective physician communication can improve patient adherence by nearly 20%, underlining its measurable clinical relevance.6

Findings of the first phase of our study underscore the need to reconcile physician and patient perspectives and to implement systemic changes that foster more effective, empathic and time-conscious clinical communication in ophthalmology.

In addressing these persistent challenges, Strategic Communication offers a structured conceptual approach. Observation begins with careful preparation for the consultation, in which clinicians clarify their objectives and consider patient-related and contextual factors, such as consultation time, treatment costs and family involvement. This preparatory step has been shown to enhance the detection of adherence issues and foster more open dialogue.17 By considering factors such as symptom visibility, treatment urgency and prognosis, communication can be tailored to individual clinical situations. For instance, in conditions with few or no symptoms, as in glaucoma or certain ocular melanomas, particular attention should be paid to ensuring that patients understand their condition and its implications.

Strategic questioning plays a vital role in bridging gaps between clinician assumptions and patient realities. Open-ended questions such as ‘What is your main motivation for this procedure?’ or ‘What have you learnt about this condition so far?’ allow clinicians to uncover patient motivations, address misinformation, allow for the collection of rich and unexpected information, and build trust fostering a sense of recognition and appreciation in the patient. Such questions have been associated with improved medication adherence and trust-building in glaucoma care.3 By fostering genuine dialogue, clinicians can align their communication with patient needs, improving both understanding and adherence to treatment. Intervention, the final phase of the O.D.I. Method, involves co-designing solutions that balance clinical realities with patient preferences and emotional needs. This collaborative approach ensures that patients feel involved in their care while avoiding the pitfalls of entirely delegating decision-making to them. Structured shared decision-making frameworks can reduce decisional conflict, improve understanding and lead to better alignment between physician recommendations and patient values.7 Finally, the conclusion of the consultation represents a crucial moment that is often overlooked but has significant impact on patient understanding and adherence. Offering a brief, shared summary of the topics discussed and providing concise, written take-home materials reinforce understanding and serve as a point of reference. Supplementary written materials have been shown to significantly improve comprehension and retention, especially among patients with lower health literacy.2 When treatment decisions are involved, a shared planning approach, where the clinician guides but actively includes the patient’s perspective, helps balance clinical priorities with individual emotional and practical needs. This final interaction can leave a lasting impression, strengthen therapeutic alliance and reduce the risk of miscommunication after the visit. The adaptability of Strategic Communication makes it applicable across diverse clinical scenarios. For chronic conditions, building a long-term relationship takes precedence, while in urgent cases, swift yet compassionate action is prioritised.

The implications of adopting Strategic Communication extend far beyond ophthalmology. Its principles are equally relevant in specialties such as oncology, cardiology and primary care, where complex information and emotional sensitivities challenge patient-provider interactions. Given the emotional and informational complexity in chronic diseases, structured communication models like Strategic Communication are well-suited to support patient understanding and engagement across multiple disciplines.6 18 By integrating relational and technical dimensions, this approach promotes holistic, patient-centred care across medical disciplines.

Nevertheless, this study is not without its limitations. This study should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept and framework development effort rather than a validation study of an intervention. The small sample size, consisting of 10 ophthalmologists and eight patients, restricts the generalisability of the findings. The voluntary nature of participation may also introduce bias, as individuals with strong opinions or experiences are more likely to contribute. Moreover, the sample primarily reflects a European public-sector context, which may limit generalisability to other healthcare systems or private practice settings. In addition, while the survey component expands the sample size, it remains descriptive and does not allow conclusions regarding causality or effectiveness. The quantitative surveys were exploratory and descriptive in nature and were not designed to support inferential statistical analyses or causal interpretations. No outcome measures were collected to assess whether use of the proposed framework improves adherence, satisfaction or clinical results. Future interventional and longitudinal studies, involving larger and more diverse cohorts and incorporating quantitative analyses, are therefore required to evaluate the real-world impact of Strategic Communication and further validate this method in varied healthcare settings.

In conclusion, Strategic Communication may represent a paradigm shift in medical practice, offering a structured approach to improving patient–provider relationships. By aligning technical proficiency with empathy and adaptability, it may support the quality of care and foster trust and understanding. While preliminary in nature, these findings provide a structured foundation for future interventional research. Prospective studies assessing implementation and outcomes will be essential to determine whether Strategic Communication can meaningfully improve patient experience and clinical effectiveness in ophthalmology and beyond.
