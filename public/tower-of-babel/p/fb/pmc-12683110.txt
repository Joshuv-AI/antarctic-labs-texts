Can dietary protease ameliorate performance and gut integrity in broilers fed on a diet low in digestible lysine?
Authors: Shahram Golzar Adabi, Hamid Raei, Mohammad Amir Karimi Torshizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of various levels of digestible lysine (dLys) and protease (PROT) on the performance, selected serum parameters, nutrient digestibility, and gut integrity of broiler chickens. In a completely randomized design, 1,024 male Ross 308 broilers were assigned to 8 treatments in a 4 × 2 factorial arrangement, with eight replicates per treatment and 16 birds per pen. The two factors tested were dLys levels set at 100 %, 98.5 %, 97.0 %, and 95.5 % of strain recommendations and PROT supplementation at 0 or 125 mg/kg feed. Significant dLys × PROT interactions were detected for performance during the grower (body weight gain (BWG), feed intake (FI), and FCR), the finisher (FI and FCR), and the overall period (BWG and FCR). Over the entire experimental period, chicks fed 95.5 % dLys without PROT exhibited decreased BWG and elevated FCR, whereas PROT supplementation restored both BWG and FCR to values similar to those of the control group. Significant dLys × PROT interactions were observed for serum parameters (uric acid, total protein, and bilirubin), short chain fatty acid (SCFA), jejunal histomorphology, total aerobic and lactic acid bacteria populations, and dry matter digestibility. PROT in diets comprising 95.5 % dLys increased branched-chain fatty acid (BCFA) and SCFA and by 8 % and 17.12 %, respectively, in comparison to diets lacking PROT (P < 0.05). The dLys × PROT interactions suggested that diets with reduced dLys tended to impair jejunal histomorphology, whereas the inclusion of PROT appeared to alleviate this effect, as reflected by numerical improvements in the measured parameters. Furthermore, feeding 95.5 % dLys without PROT led to a 16.00 % reduction in lactic acid bacteria counts compared to birds receiving 100 % dLys without PROT (P < 0.05). The results demonstrated that reducing dietary dLys below strain recommendations, particularly to 97 % and 95.5 %, impaired growth performance, intestinal morphology, and SCFA concentrations. However, PROT supplementation in reduced-dLys diets alleviated these adverse effects and supported improved intestinal integrity and performance.Keywords: Broiler, Cecal microbial population, Low-digestible lysine, Growth performance, Short-chain fatty acid profile

Introduction

Feed accounts for nearly 69 % of the total production costs in intensive poultry systems, with 65–70 % of these costs attributed to protein sources (Mallick et al., 2020). Protein-rich ingredients are therefore the most expensive component of poultry diets, and their rising costs, coupled with concerns over nutrient excretion and associated greenhouse gas emissions, have motivated efforts to reduce dietary nutrient levels (Mohammadigheisar and Kim, 2018; Toprak et al., 2021). In addition, excessive nitrogen from high-protein or amino acid (AA) diets can impair bird health by fostering pathogenic bacteria and generating harmful metabolites such as biogenic amines, hydrogen sulfide, and phenolic or indolic compounds (Salazar-Villanea et al., 2023).

One of the most common recommendations for overcoming environmental issues and optimizing feed formulation in poultry farming is to reduce crude protein (CP) levels in feed (Toprak et al., 2021). Various studies have demonstrated that improving protein utilization in low-CP diets (by 2 to 4 % compared to NRC recommendations) can be achieved without adversely affecting poultry production traits (Angel et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2020a, 2020b; Salazar-Villanea et al., 2023).

The addition of crystalline AAs to low-CP broiler diets has frequently been employed to achieve performance levels similar to those observed in conventional diets (Pinchasov et al., 1990; Aletor et al., 2000). Nevertheless, the efficacy of this approach in enhancing broiler growth performance has been inconsistent (Bregendahl et al., 2002; Mahmood et al., 2017).

It is widely acknowledged that poultry protein requirements should be based on digestible AAs rather than CP, a principle referred to as the ideal protein concept. In this framework, lysine (Lys) serves as the reference AA, with the concentrations of all other essential AAs optimized relative to its level (Wecke et al., 2016).

Aligning dietary AA profiles with the bird’s requirements minimizes oxidation of excess AAs beyond the first limiting AA, thereby reducing nitrogen excretion and improving protein utilization efficiency (Lemme, 2003). Although recent critiques highlight the omission of certain non-essential AAs (e.g., glutamine, glutamate, glycine, proline) in the ideal protein concept (Wu and Li, 2022), the approach remains widely endorsed by nutritionists. Requirements for digestible lysine (dLys) are influenced by factors such as sex, age, genetics, strain, environmental stress (e.g., heat stress), and dietary energy concentration. Consequently, any change in dLys alters the requirements for other AAs, while the ratios of digestible AAs to dLys remain constant.

Exogenous feed enzymes represent a further tool with the potential to enhance the utilization of dietary nutrients in commercial farming contexts. For instance, protease (PROT), when applied as an exogenous enzyme, has been shown to enhance the utilization of dietary proteins and AAs. Consequently, nutritionists advocate for the incorporation of PROT into low-CP diets to ensure the sustained viability of poultry production (Amer et al., 2021).

Numerous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of PROT inclusion in low-CP broiler diets, particularly in enhancing growth performance and AA digestibility (Cardinal et al., 2019; Barbosa et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2020). Nevertheless, inconsistent findings have also been documented, with some studies showing negligible benefits or even reductions in AA digestibility following PROT inclusion (Erdaw et al., 2017; Walk et al., 2018; Borda-Molina et al., 2019). Such variability in response is likely attributable to differences in feed ingredient composition, PROT supplementation level, and the biochemical characteristics of the PROT product (Amer et al., 2021).

To the best of our knowledge, there is a paucity of information regarding the effect of simultaneously reducing CP, dLys levels, and other AAs by maintaining the desired ratios of digestible AAs to dLys, with dietary inclusion of PROT, in broiler chickens. Hence, the present study aimed to evaluate the various levels of dLys in grower and finisher diets supplemented with PROT on nutrient digestibility, jejunal histomorphology, selected cecal microbiota population, cecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations, and growth performance in broilers.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The animal experiment was conducted at the Application and Research Facility of Tarbiat Modares University Agriculture Faculty / Iran. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines set forth by the ethics committee of Tarbiat Modares University. The procedures employed in this study were in compliance with the ethical principles governing the treatment of animals.

Birds and housing

A total of 1,024 day-old male Ross 308 broiler chickens were randomly allocated to one of eight experimental treatments. Upon arrival, the chicks were distributed into 64 pens within an environmentally controlled facility, with 16 birds housed per pen. Each pen was equipped with a feeder and a nipple drinker, allowing for ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experiment. The ambient temperature was initially maintained at 33°C for the first three days, then gradually reduced to 24°C and maintained at that level for the duration of the trial. The lighting program provided 23 hours of light and 1 hour of darkness during the first three days, followed by 20 hours of light and 4 hours of darkness from day 4 to day 41.

Diets and experimental design

During the starter phase (1-10 days), all broilers were raised under identical environmental and nutritional conditions and received a standard diet formulated to meet or exceed Aviagen (2022) nutrient recommendations (Table 1). During the grower (11–22 days) and finisher (23–41 days) phases, broilers were fed one of eight dietary treatments (Table 1, Table 2).

The treatments were arranged in a 4 × 2 factorial design, comprising four levels of dLys at 100 %, 98.5 %, 97.0 %, and 95.5 % of the genetic strain recommendations, and two levels of PROT inclusion (0 and 125 mg/kg of feed). The experimental treatments consisted of: 1) L0PR0, 100 % of the Aviagen recommended dLys without PROT; 2) L0PR1, 100 % of the Aviagen dLys plus PROT; 3) L1PR0, 98.5 % of the Aviagen dLys without PROT; 4) L1PR1, 98.5 % of the Aviagen dLys plus PROT; 5) L2PR0: 97.0 % of the Aviagen dLys without PROT; 6) L2PR1, 97 % of the Aviagen dLys plus PROT; 7) L3PR0, 95.5 % of the Aviagen dLys without PROT; and 8) L3PR1, 95.5 % of the Aviagen dLys plus PROT. The levels of other essential AAs were adjusted proportionally to the level of dLys to maintain a balanced AA profile across all diets.

All diets contained equal amounts of phytase (1,000 FTU/kg activity; derived from Trichoderma reesei CBS 126897; Quantum Blue, AB Vista, United Kingdom) and an NSPase endo-1,4-β-xylanase (350 IU/g activity; EC 3.2.1.8, produced by Bacillus subtilis LMG‑S 15136; Belfeed, Belgium), providing 0.17 % of avP and Ca, and 80 kcal ME/kg, respectively. In this study, the PROT was supplied by Jefo Nutrition Inc. (1.1 U/g activity; Saint-Hyacinthe, QC, Canada) and included in the diet at a rate of 125 mg/kg.

Growth performance

Body weight gain (BWG) and feed intake (FI) were measured per pen at 10, 22, and 41 d. The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated by dividing FI by BWG. At 41 d, two chickens per replicate (16 birds per treatment) were randomly selected and euthanized for further analysis.

Blood profile

On 41 d, 2 mL of blood samples were collected from the wing vein of broilers, with two birds selected from each pen. Following centrifugation of the blood samples at 1,500 × g for 10 minutes, the serum was collected and stored at −20°C until further analysis. The concentrations of cholesterol, uric acid, total protein, creatinine, and bilirubin were determined using a spectrophotometric method, employing commercial kits (Pars Azmun, Iran) and a microplate reader (Stat Fax 3200; Awareness Technology Inc., Palm City, FL, USA), in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions.

Jejunal morphometric analysis

Following euthanasia, a 3-cm jejunal segment was collected from the distal duodenal loop to the Meckel’s diverticulum. Samples were fixed in 10 % buffered formalin at room temperature for 72 h. After the routine histological procedures, samples were embedded in paraffin wax. Five sections per slide were prepared by cutting samples in a 4 μm thick layer using a rotary-type microtome. The slides were then subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining. Slides were evaluated to measure villus height (VH), villus width (VW), crypt depth (CD), villus surface area (VSA), and number of goblet cells using a compound light microscope (Olympus-BX50, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at 40 × and 400 × magnifications. The villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was subsequently calculated (Ceylan et al., 2023, 2024; Raei et al., 2025).

Cecal SCFA composition

The cecal SCFA concentration was determined by first collecting 0.5 g of cecal content and then diluting it with 2 mL of ultrapure water. Following this, the samples were subjected to agitation and then permitted to stand for a period of 30 minutes. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 15 minutes. After this, 1 mL of the resulting supernatant was mixed with 0.2 mL of an ice-cold 25 % (w/v) metaphosphoric acid solution at 4°C, and incubated for a further 30 minutes. The samples were then subjected to a final centrifugation step at 11,000 × g for 10 minutes. The cecal SCFAs (acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, valerate, and isovalerate) were measured using gas chromatography (Varian CP-3800, Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) (Ceylan et al., 2023, 2024; Raei et al., 2025).

Selected cecal microbiota population

A total of 1 g of homogenized cecal contents was serially diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (0.1 M, pH 7.0) from 101 to 108. Aliquots (0.1 mL) from the final three dilutions were inoculated onto MacConkey agar, MRS agar, and Plate Count Agar (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to determine the quantities of coliforms, lactic acid bacteria, and total aerobic bacteria, respectively. To ensure the accuracy of the results, duplicate inoculations of each dilution were performed on the respective media and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours for enumeration. The resulting data were presented as log10 colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of cecal content (Sharifi et al., 2022; Ceylan et al., 2023, 2024; Raei et al., 2025).

Apparent ileal digestibility

On day 39 of the experiment, broilers were fed diets containing 0.5 % titanium dioxide (TiO2) as an indigestible marker for two days. On day 41, following the euthanasia, the ileal contents were collected to measure apparent ileal digestibility (AID) of CP, phosphorus (P), and dry matter (DM). The digesta samples were flushed with distilled water, frozen, and freeze-dried in preparation for the recovery of CP, P, DM, and titanium dioxide. The digesta were analyzed for these nutrients and titanium dioxide (A.T.C Scientific, North Little Rock, AR, USA). The analysis was carried out in duplicate, and the results were reported on a DM basis. The following equation was used to calculate AID (Lemme et al., 2004; Raei et al., 2025).

Data analyses

All data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA using the GLM procedure of SAS software (version 9.2; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The model incorporated the primary effects of dietary CP concentration, dietary PROT supplementation, and their interaction. Statistical significance among groups was assessed using the Tukey test, with an alpha level set at 0.05.

Feeding birds with 95.5 % of dLys, devoid of PROT, resulted in a 9.65 % and 4.27 % diminution in BWG during the grower and whole phases, respectively, compared to birds fed the control diet without PROT. On the other hand, the addition of dietary PROT to diets comprising 100 % and 98.5 % dLys resulted in significantly enhanced BWG compared to the control diet devoid of PROT, particularly during the grower and whole phases.

The FI of dLys × PROT was found to be a significant factor in the study, yet it exerted no substantial influence on the BWG of birds during the finisher phase (P > 0.05). However, a dietary intervention involving 95.5 % dLys (with a 4.50 % dLys reduction) without PROT resulted in a 5.66 % reduction in FI compared to the control diet without PROT at grower phase (P < 0.05). Interestingly, the same birds exhibited the highest FI at finisher phase (P < 0.05).

A comparison of the dLys × PROT interaction effects revealed that the whole phase’s FCR was significantly reduced by 2.60 % in birds fed a diet consisting of 100 % dLys plus PROT in comparison with birds fed a 100 % dLys diet without additional PROT. In diets without PROT, reducing dLys to 95.5 % increased FCR by 0.85 %, 5.75 %, and 3.90 % during the grower, finisher, and overall phases, respectively, compared to control birds (P < 0.05). The incorporation of PROT into the diet comprising 95.5 % of the recommended dLys during the whole period decreased the FCR by 2.5 % (1.56 vs 1.60) in the birds fed on PROT-free diets and 95.5 % dLys (P < 0.05).

Blood profile

As demonstrated in Table 4, there was no statistically significant difference among the treatments for serum cholesterol and creatinine (P > 0.05). However, an analysis of the dLys × PROT interaction revealed that uric acid, total protein, and bilirubin concentration was affected by dietary treatments (P < 0.05). The observed decrease in uric acid concentration was simultaneously associated with a decrease in dietary dLys and/or the addition of PROT to the diet. The addition of PROT led to the highest total protein levels when included in diets containing 100 % or 98.5 % of the recommended dLys. These levels were higher than those observed in birds fed the diet with 95.5 % dLys without PROT (P < 0.05). A significant dLys × PROT interaction was also observed for serum bilirubin, with birds fed diets containing 98.5 %, 97.0 %, and 95.5 % dLys plus PROT showing significantly lower serum bilirubin compared to those fed the 100 % dLys level without PROT.

Nutrient digestibility

The study revealed that neither dLys nor PROT levels, nor their interaction, had a significant impact on P digestibility (Table 7; P > 0.05). Although the dLys × PROT interaction and the level of dLys as a main factor did not affect on CP digestibility (P > 0.05), dietary PROT as a main effect increased CP digestibility by 3.7 % compared to PROT-free diets (P < 0.05). The DM digestibility was found to be influenced by the dLys × PROT interaction (P < 0.05), with birds fed on the diet containing 98.5 % of the recommended dLys plus PROT demonstrating the highest DM digestibility compared to the other birds (P < 0.05).

Discussion

In the present study, when a significant interaction between dLys and PROT was detected for the evaluated parameters, the individual main effects of these factors were not interpreted. A reduction in dietary dLys of up to 3 %, irrespective of PROT supplementation, did not exert a significant effect on broiler growth performance; however, further reduction to 95.5 % of the recommended level resulted in decreased BWG and an elevated FCR. Similar impairments in growth associated with low-CP diets, due to insufficient supply of key AAs such as dLys, has been documented in numerous studies (Walk et al., 2018; Cardinal et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020a, 2020b).

In low-CP diets, a further reduction in CP content often restricts essential AAs including methionine and Lys, which consequently depresses growth performance. Nonetheless, findings across studies are not entirely consistent. Some reports indicate that moderate reductions in CP do not adversely affect growth (Ferguson et al., 1998; Kerr and Kidd, 1999). For example, Salah (2016) and Van Harn et al. (2019) specifically showed that lowering CP by up to 3 % did not compromise performance. Conversely, diets deficient in essential AAs such as Lys, methionine, tryptophan, threonine, or arginine in a range of 0.02 to 0.20 %, have been shown to reduce BWG and increase FCR (Lin Law et al., 2019; Maqsood et al., 2022).

The reduction in BWG observed with low dLys diets may be related to limited breast muscle development during early growth, which has been associated with decreased protein synthesis and RNA content (Tesseraud et al., 1992). Conversely, excessive AA supply can increase catabolism and energy expenditure for nitrogen elimination, thereby impairing growth performance (Franco et al., 2006; Salahi et al., 2025). These findings highlight the importance of aligning AA supply with the birds’ requirements or using exogenous enzymes such as PROT to improve protein utilization and reduce nitrogen excretion.

In agreement with the current study, previous research has demonstrated that PROT supplementation in low-CP diets enhances broiler growth performance (Angel et al., 2011; Olukosi et al., 2015; Mahmood et al., 2017; Walk et al., 2018; Cowieson et al., 2019; Akter et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023). This improvement has been linked to increased digestibility of protein and fat (Mahmood et al., 2017), as well as the ability of PROT to inactivate proteinaceous antinutritional factors such as trypsin inhibitors and lectins (Cowieson et al., 2019).

The present study found no significant effect of dietary treatments on serum cholesterol and creatinine concentrations, which was consistent with the findings of previous studies (Golzar Adabi et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023).

In chickens, uric acid is the end-product of protein catabolism and serves as an indicator of protein utilization efficiency, with lower concentrations reflecting improved AA use (Golzar Adabi et al., 2019). Previous studies have shown reduced uric acid in broilers fed low-CP diets (Golzar Adabi et al., 2019; Song et al., 2023). Although some reports noted increased uric acid when low-CP diets were supplemented with PROT (Song et al., 2023), the present study did not observe this effect, possibly because PROT enhanced AA utilization and reduced nitrogen excretion.

Creatinine, a by-product of the non-enzymatic degradation of phosphocreatine in skeletal muscle, is a crucial indicator of protein metabolism. Its levels are closely associated with muscle mass, age, physical activity, and diet (Szabo et al., 2005). Notably, dietary CP has been observed to exert minimal influence on plasma creatinine levels, as evidenced by studies conducted by Golzar Adabi et al. (2019) and Lee et al. (2023).

Bilirubin, a product of hemoglobin catabolism, functions as an antioxidant, with elevated concentrations often reflecting compromised lymphatic or hepatic–biliary function (Golzar Adabi et al., 2019). In the current study, lowering dietary dLys along with other essential AAs, irrespective of PROT supplementation, led to a reduction in serum bilirubin levels. This may suggest reduced metabolic stress and improved AA efficiency, potentially reflecting a more balanced and less burdensome metabolic state in broilers.

Low VH, VH/CD, and VSA are linked to reduced nutrient absorption and growth performance (Lin Law et al., 2019). In the present study, diets containing 97.0 % and 95.5 % dLys without PROT negatively affected jejunal morphology, as indicated by reduced VH, VSA, and goblet cell counts. Supplementation with PROT partially mitigated these detrimental effects, likely by providing additional digestible peptides that promote villus development (Barbosa et al., 2020; Duque-Ramírez et al., 2023). Previous studies have reported that 6 % reductions in CP and AAs (Lys, methionine, threonine, and tryptophan) can damage intestinal structure and decrease lamina propria thickness while increasing epithelial thickness and epithelial plasma infiltration, but PROT inclusion improves intestinal health (Cardinal et al., 2019).

Furthermore, a substantial decrease in CP may lead to a decline in non-essential AAs, such as glycine, glutamine, and proline. These AAs, as the predominant component of intestinal mucins and digestive secretions, play a pivotal role in supporting the epithelial layer (Lin Law et al., 2019). In contrast, the current study demonstrated that decreasing dietary dLys and other AAs by up to 1.5 %, irrespective of PROT supplementation, did not significantly alter jejunal histomorphological parameters.

Acetate, propionate, butyrate, and lactate, the main volatile fatty acids in the chicken gut, are generated through carbohydrate fermentation, while BCFAs are produced from protein fermentation (Ndazigaruye et al., 2019). SCFA production in the anaerobic gut microbiota is critical for redox balance, and a neutral to mildly alkaline environment can favor the growth of pathogenic bacteria. In the current study, reducing dLys in diets without PROT supplementation led to marked decreases in acetate, propionate, butyrate, and isobutyrate concentrations. However, diets with up to a 1.5 % reduction in dLys did not significantly alter SCFA levels compared with diets meeting the recommended dLys content. Additionally, PROT supplementation increased SCFA levels, likely by promoting the proliferation of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus, as indicated by higher lactic acid bacteria counts. Elevated SCFAs, lactic acid, and succinic acid are widely regarded as indicators of improved gut health (Dao et al., 2022), and the increase in SCFAs with PROT may result from enhanced protein digestibility, which limits undigested proteins that would otherwise promote pathogenic bacteria like E. coli and Clostridia (Lu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020a).

The intestinal microbiota plays a crucial role in supporting poultry health and growth performance (Park and Kim, 2018). Previous studies have demonstrated that reducing dietary CP by 7 % can increase cecal Lactobacillus populations, enhancing nutrient digestion and absorption (De Cesare et al., 2019). Lactobacillus contributes to gut health through the production of extracellular proteins with adhesive properties, which influence microbiota dynamics and the synthesis of bacteriocins effective against gram-positive bacteria (Fasina et al., 2016; Borda-Molina et al., 2019). In the current study, broilers receiving diets with recommended dLys levels exhibited higher counts of lactic acid bacteria compared with those fed 95.5 % dLys, whereas diets containing 97 % dLys did not significantly influence lactic acid bacteria populations.

Essential AAs such as dLys are known to support the growth of beneficial microbiota, including Lactobacillus reuteri, which are critical for animal health (Taylor-Bowden et al., 2024). Thus, substantial reductions in dietary dLys, as observed here, may reduce beneficial bacterial populations, whereas supplementation with PROT can enhance lactic acid bacteria and improve gut health in low-CP diets (Shazali et al., 2019).

Previous studies have shown that the digestibility of CP and DM is generally unaffected when comparing diets with normal and reduced protein levels (Ding et al., 2016). In the present study, the inclusion of exogenous PROT enhanced the digestibility of CP (as the main effect) and DM (dLys × PROT), while the digestibility of P remained largely unchanged, in agreement with earlier reports (Zuo et al., 2015; Ding et al., 2016). The improvement in CP digestibility may result from the neutralization of anti-nutritive factors, such as protease inhibitors, present in feed ingredients (Zuo et al., 2015). Exogenous PROT functions by releasing peptides from these anti-nutritive components, breaking the bonds between AAs and protein complexes (López-Otín and Bond, 2008). This action not only complements endogenous peptidase activity but also reduces enzymatic secretion and protein turnover, thereby increasing the availability of AAs required for protein synthesis and deposition (Freitas et al., 2011).

Conclusions

The findings of the present study indicated that feeding diets containing 97.0 % and 95.5 % of the recommended dLys level, alongside proportional reductions in other essential AAs, significantly impaired broiler growth performance, altered the cecal microbial population, reduced nutrient digestibility, compromised jejunal morphology, and lowered cecal SCFA concentrations. Conversely, the addition of PROT to the reduced dLys diets effectively mitigated these negative effects. The inclusion of PROT enhanced growth performance, improved jejunal histomorphology, and elevated cecal SCFA concentrations. In summary, these findings suggest that a 3 % reduction in dLys and other AAs, when supplemented with PROT, does not compromise gut integrity and growth performance in broilers.
