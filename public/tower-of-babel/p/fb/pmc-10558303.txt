Introduction

Retrocaval ureter also known as circumcaval ureter is a relatively uncommon developmental abnormality of the inferior vena cava with a prevalence of 0.13 % [1]. Though it is normally described as retrocaval anatomically, it's misleading about its development; since it is not an abnormality in ureteral development but rather an anomaly in inferior vena cava development [2]. It usually presents in the third and fourth decades of life and hence a rare presentation in the pediatric population [3]. It's more common is males compared to females at a ratio of 4:1 [4]. This retrocaval ureter is mostly seen on the right side, once found on the left side, it's usually associated with other cardiovascular and renal anomalies in up to 21 % [5]; duplicated inferior vena cava being the commonest [6].

In this anomaly, the proximal part of the ureter passes behind the IVC at the level of third lumbar vertebra to lie at its medial aspect. The distal part of the ureter returns to its original normal position crossing IVC from medial to lateral aspect of the lower part of IVC. The retrocaval segment is the site at which obstruction takes place hence causing dilation of the ureter proximal to obstruction and collapse of the distal ureteric segment [7]. Obstruction happens as a result of compression of the ureter between the inferior vena cava and the third lumbar vertebra [8].

The proximal part of ureter and the renal pelvis typically appear lengthened and dilated in a fish hook shape before passing behind the IVC which is detected well on CT scan or intravenous pyelogram [9]. Retrocaval ureter used to be diagnosed with the use of intravenous urogram and retrograde pyelography, but with advancement in technology, this has shifted to the use of CT scan [10]. Non symptomatic cases of retrocaval ureter may be managed conservatively with follow-up imaging and renal function tests [11], however, symptomatic patients require surgical intervention which is primarily ureteroureterostomy [12]. We present a case of a symptomatic retrocaval ureter in a 13-year-old female diagnosed and managed surgically at a Regional Referral Hospital in a resource limited setting and has been reported in line with the SCARE 2020 criteria [13].

Case presentation

A 13 year old female presented with a 3 months history of colicky, right sided abdominal pain, radiating to the back associated with nausea, and vomiting but no history of diarrhea, fever or weight loss. She reported a history of recurrent lower abdominal pain, dysuria and urinary frequency but no reduced urinary output. She had been on different medications for urinary tract infections. There was no significant past medical or surgical history. Three weeks prior to presentation, she was advised by a general practitioner from a peripheral clinic to visit a urologist at the Regional Referral Hospital for further evaluation and management but was unable to visit the facility in time due to financial constraints. On physical examination, she was in fair general condition, fairly well nourished, with no pallor, limb swelling nor lymphadenopathy. Marked tenderness in the right lumbar area on deep palpation was elicited on abdominal examination.

The deranged laboratory measurements included aspartate transaminase (208 u/l), alkaline phosphatase (1827 u/l), and prothrombin time (18 s). The international normalized ratio, complete blood count, renal function tests, serum electrolytes and urinalysis were normal. Abdominal sonography revealed hydronephrosis of the right kidney associated with minimal loss of corticomedullary differentiation and dilation of the proximal ureter. Intravenous pyelography revealed right grade 2 hydronephrosis with dilated renal pelvis, calyces and upper ureter with a fish hook shape (Fig. 1). The left kidney and its ureter were completely normal. There was no evidence of ureteric stones on the scout film x-ray.

Intravenous pyelogram taken after 5 h showing fish hook sign (Black arrow) indicating ureteric obstruction due to retrocaval ureter.

Laparotomy, using a transperitoneal approach revealed a dilated ureter (Fig. 2), with a hook shape proximal to the site of obstruction and a collapsed ureter coursing posteriorly to the inferior vena cava (Fig. 3). The ureter was released along its course, divided, spatulated the distal collapsed segment, did ureteroureterostomy using vicryl 4–0 interrupted in a tension free and water tight meticulous technique. The ureter was stented and relocated to the anterior part of the inferior vena cava, leaving a retro-peritoneal drain in situ (Fig. 4). Intra-operatively, the ureteric stent was able to drain some urine and continued to drain post operatively. Abdomen was closed in layers and patient transferred to urology ward for immediate post-operative monitoring. The lead surgeon was a urologist. Four weeks post operatively, patient was no longer in pain, blood pressure was normal, abdominal tenderness had reduced, renal function tests were normal and hydronephrosis had resolved on intravenous urogram.

Retrocaval ureter passing behind the inferior vena cava with the distal ureter collapsed whereas the proximal ureter is dilated and forms a shape of a hook.

Discussion

Retrocaval ureter also known as circumcaval ureter is a relatively an uncommon developmental abnormality of the inferior vena cava. In Africa and Uganda, prevalence is not known and there is limited data on this rare condition, hence justifying the reason for making a case report. In Uganda, so far this is the first documented case of retrocaval ureter in a young female child that has been managed successful at a Regional Referral Hospital. Obstruction to the ureter happens due to its compression between the inferior vena cava and the third lumbar vertebra [8]. This condition in which the anomalous location of the ureter together with narrowing of the uretero-pelvic junction predisposes the patient to unilateral hydronephrosis may result in obstructive uropathy if the patient is not treated in time. In this case, the patient had unilateral hydronephrosis as seen on the abdominal ultrasound scan, however there was minimal loss of corticomedullary differentiation indicating early diagnosis of the patient and timely surgical intervention [14].

This condition usually presents in the third and fourth decades of life but in our case, it was a rare presentation in which the patient was in a second decade of life [3]. It is more common in males than females, with a ratio of 4:1 globally, but in Uganda, so far a female with this rare type of condition has not been documented [4]. Retrocaval ureter is usually seen on the right side, once seen on the left, is usually associated with other congenital abnormalities. In this case, the anomaly was on the right side, and no other congenital anomalies were identified in the child hence agreeing with the literature [6].

Retrocaval ureter used to be diagnosed with intravenous urogram where a fish hook sign would be observed [9] but with advancement in technology, CT scan is now a gold standard, but in our community since we live in a less resourced setting, we were able to rely on intravenous pyelogram and the diagnosis was confirmed at laparotomy [10]. Non symptomatic patients can be managed conservatively with serial monitoring of renal function tests and imaging, but in this case, our patient was symptomatic with symptoms of right lumbar pain that affected her school attendance, and therefore benefited from operative management [11].

The management involves active surveillance for asymptomatic patients, nephrectomy for patients with destroyed kidney and conservative surgical treatment for all symptomatic patients with preserved ipsilateral renal function [15]. The principle of conservative surgery for retrocaval ureter is the restoration of normal anatomic position by transpositioning of the ureter over the inferior vena cava [15]. This can be done by open surgery, laparoscopic transperitoneal, laparoscopic retroperitoneal, as well as by robotic assisted surgery [15]. Conventionally, open access has been considered the standard procedure for the correction of the abnormally [15]. The open approach was done for this patient.

Conclusion

Retrocaval ureter is a very rare congenital abnormality of inferior vena cava that may present with a challenge in both diagnosis and treatment. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of retrocaval ureter in a child surgically managed in Africa. Amidst of being in a less resourced setting, an intravenous pyelogram may be all that is required to make a diagnosis.
