Detrital and authigenic clay minerals in shales: A review on their identification and applications
Authors: Yuan Kang, Rukai Zhu, Kouqi Liu, Jingya Zhang, Surong Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractClay minerals are widely distributed and are important components in sedimentary rocks. The identification and quantification of detrital and authigenic clay minerals have been hot topics recently due to the needs of petroleum geology, paleoclimate, and sedimentary history research. Detrital clay minerals exhibit the phenomenon of discrete grain boundaries, while authigenic clay minerals have various specific particle shapes. The differences in crystal structure of different clay mineral origins are reflected in the polytype character of clay minerals, especially for illite and chlorite. Different origins of clay minerals inherently have different chemical characteristics. Instead of focusing only on conventional techniques (scanning electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, and geochemical analysis), here we review established approaches and introduce emerging analytical techniques (automated mineralogy techniques and artificial intelligence techniques) that can complement current methods. In addition, the applications of these techniques in illite age analysis, paleoclimate reconstruction, and reservoir properties are discussed. Further, different state-of-the-art techniques for clay mineral identification and quantification (e.g., scanning electron microscopy for specific petrographic characterization, X-ray diffraction for bulk crystal structure identification, etc.) are discussed. We propose that combining individual and bulk techniques obtained at multiple scales can provide novel insights for deciphering various clay mineral origins. Understanding the characteristics of clay minerals from different origins is of great importance to scientific issues such as shale oil and gas exploration.Keywords: Clay minerals, Characterization, Identification methods, Quantitation methods

Introduction

In sedimentary basins, hydrous aluminosilicate minerals with particle sizes smaller than 2 μm are commonly known as "clay" [[1], [2], [3]]. Clay mineral content in shale oil and gas reservoirs, including the Marcellus shale in the Appalachian Basin, the Haynesville shale in the United States, and the Upper Ordovician-Lower Silurian shale reservoirs in China, can reach as high as 60–70 % [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Clay minerals in shale may form through three distinct processes: 1) detrital origins (DO) by mechanical weathering of older rocks or soil that contain clay minerals [2,8], 2) solid-state transformation (SST) of precursor phases [9,10], and 3) precipitation from fluids (PF) formed by the dissolution of adjacent minerals such as feldspar or from transported fluids [[11], [12], [13]].

The first type of clay minerals is detrital, while the second and third types are authigenic. Detrital clay assemblages are important for reconstructing the paleoenvironment because they reflect the parent lithology and the weathering conditions [2,14,15]. For instance, smectite is associated with warm climate conditions characterized by alternating humid and dry seasons, indicating semiarid to humid paleoclimate conditions where chemical weathering is reasonably mild or slow [2,16,17]. The presence of detrital chlorite and detrital illite is suggested to correspond to relatively dry periods, while the presence of detrital kaolinite indicates a more humid and intensive weathering condition [16,18,19]. By contrast, the authigenic clay minerals can bear useful information on post-depositional alteration and diagenesis [16,20]. For instance, an increased abundance of illite with depth in shales has been interpreted to reflect gradual illitization of smectite, providing a useful tool to estimate maximum burial depths, temperatures, and the local geothermal gradient [21,22]. When studying the mineral transformation relationship during diagenesis, it is necessary to assess and quantify the contributions of detrital and authigenic minerals. This determination of detrital and authigenic mineral proportions helps in understanding the overall mineralogical changes during diagenesis. In addition, the transformation of clay minerals and their interaction with organic matter strongly influence shale reservoirs’ pore structure and oil content [[23], [24], [25]] because the characteristics of pore structure and hydrocarbon adsorption show differences in different types and origins of clay minerals [[26], [27], [28]]. Generally, smectite with more micropores (<2 nm), larger surface areas and higher CEC than illite and chlorite exhibits greater organic matter absorption [29,30].

The examples mentioned above illustrate that identifying and quantifying the detrital and authigenic clays are necessary when conducting research on shale reservoirs. However, identifying detrital and authigenic clays in shale is more challenging than identifying those in coarser sedimentary rocks such as sandstone. The challenges primarily stem form: 1) Variable particle shape: Relatively low contents of rigid particles (quartz or feldspar) lead to the deformation of clay minerals, and thus euhedral clays are rare in shale [31]. In addition, the clay and its aggregates in shale are varied in shape because of the different crystal growth habits and different responses to diagenesis [32]. 2) Variable structures: Compared to other minerals, clay minerals have more variable structures involving different types of layer interstratification (e.g., illite-smectite mixed-layer minerals), and various defects that damage the three-dimensional periodicity [[33], [34], [35]]. 3) Variable chemical composition: Variable chemical composition is caused by the isomorphous substitution in the octahedral (O) sheet which is comprised of closely packed oxygen atoms and hydroxyl groups and tetrahedral (T) sheet which is composed of individual tetrahedrons which share every three out of four oxygen atoms and the variety of interlayer cations [36,37]. These issues severely limit our ability to untangle clay mineral signals that reflect climate changes from those due to changes in provenance and diagenesis. Even though it faces many challenges, the determination of the origin of clay minerals has been a popular topic.

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) can provide secondary electron (SE) and backscattered electron (BSE) images that capture the features of crystal or particle morphology [38]. Combined with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) X-ray analysis which provides chemical data by analyzing the energy and intensity of emitted X-rays, the nature of clay minerals can be determined [39]. Many high-resolution SEM, focused ion beam (FIB)-SEM, and automated minerals mapping tools (e.g., QEMSCAN and MAPS) have recently been developed to identify detrital and authigenic clay in shales [24,[40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46]]. The development of these new technologies has made it possible to identify clay mineral sources in an efficient and accurate manner. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis is an excellent methodical procedure for robust compositional and crystallographic identification of individual clay grains and is an important complementary tool for the study of clays in fine-grained sediments [24,47]. However, its application in the identification of bulk clay minerals is limited due to the difficulty of sample preparation and the small field of view under TEM [24]. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of powder samples is the most utilized method for identifying and quantifying clay minerals [[48], [49], [50]]. It enables the differentiation of detrital and diagenetic phases based on the polytypes of clay minerals from different origins, whose crystallographic properties are sufficiently distinctive. Especially for chlorite and illite, their polytypes has been widely studied [51,52]. For example, there are distinct differences in the XRD diffraction peaks at 3.88 Å between the 1Md polytype illite derived from authigenic origins and the 2M1 polytype illite derived from detrital origins [53]. However, XRD analysis is unable to directly assess the mineral morphology and the petrographic information (replacement textures (over)growth features, mineral dissolution, etc.), due to the XRD sample requires the rock mass to be ground to a 75 μm (200-mesh) powder [24,54]. Although the chemical composition of clay minerals is complex, chemical analysis can still provide good indicators for the source of clay minerals because detrital and authigenic clay minerals have differences in major elements, trace elements, and isotope data [[55], [56], [57], [58]]. Clay analyses are carried out at different scales, including macro and micro scales, to gain insights into their origin and chemical composition. The macro-chemical analysis such as X-Ray Fluorescence (XRF) and inductively coupled plasma (ICP) provides a bulk understanding of the clay's composition and its source [57,59]. On the other hand, micro-chemical analysis (EMPA and TEM) focuses on investigating the chemical composition of individual clay minerals at much smaller scales, typically in the micro-nanometer range. Techniques such as SEM-EDS are employed to identify and characterize the chemical composition of individual clay minerals, thus investigating their origin [57,60]. This micro-chemical analysis enables a more detailed examination of the origins of specific clay minerals.

By critically evaluating the criteria and methodologies employed in prior studies on the identification of clay mineral sources in shale, this review article provides a concise overview of the crystal structure, geochemistry, and genesis of clay minerals from different origins. The morphological characteristics of clay minerals from various origins are also demonstrated using SEM images from the Gulong Shale in China as well as from the literature collection. This study further provides a summary of the principal technical tools utilized in clay mineral research from results of the authors' own experimental manipulations and the literature collection, highlighting key methods such as SEM-EDS, automated mineralogy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and chemical analysis. The methodologies are also reviewed in terms of their potential use in illite age studies, paleoclimate reconstruction, and the characterization of reservoir parameters. Additionally, by comparing the advantages and limitations of different technological approaches, this review anticipates the development of novel techniques for clay mineral source identification in future research.

Methodology

Eligibility criteria and principles for literature review process

To address the specific research questions, this study employs a systematic literature review method, guided by three fundamental principles to ensure transparency and objectivity throughout the review process. These principles help mitigate researcher bias in literature selection, offering more reliable and accurate answers to the research questions. They also enhance the scientific rigor of knowledge innovation and ensure that the selected papers align with the research objectives. This paper basically refers to Chen, Ma and Wu [61] for the methodology of literature collection, with the difference that we consider non-English language (NCE) literature. The specific criteria are shown in Table 1. The three basic principles are as follows: 1) Clear exclusion and inclusion criteria. This study established three exclusion criteria for papers: missing basic information, completely irrelevant, and irrelevant, while defining two inclusion criteria: relevant and closely relevant. Table 1 provides comprehensive information on the screening criteria and their explanation. 2) Objective assessment strategy. The selection of each paper and the collection of coding data were assessed by three assessors familiar with the basic knowledge of clay origin. In cases of disagreement among the assessors, a final judgement was made through discussion. 3) Evidence-based data collection. For subjective judgement data (e.g. The age of clay mineral), the original text of the paper was collected and used as supporting material.

Systematic literature review methods

A systematic literature review is crucial for synthesizing research findings [61]. This review adhered to the PRISMA Protocol 2020 guidelines, which outline four key phases: paper identification, paper screening and assessment, paper inclusion, and data processing and coding [61]. During the paper identification phase, relevant papers were retrieved from selected databases. The screening and assessment phase involved filtering and retaining papers with complete and relevant information on clay origin. In the paper inclusion phase, papers meeting the criteria were incorporated and categorized based on relevance. Finally, the data processing and coding phase involved preprocessing and coding the data from the selected papers (see Fig. 1).

Different stages of PRISMA.

Data selection process

The aim of this study is to analyze the sources of clay minerals, the relevant state-of-the-art identification techniques as well as their applications. We collected a total of 1425 literatures based on keywords in the WOS database and removed four duplicates. An initial screening of 1421 papers was done by reading the article titles and other basic information, and 453 papers were obtained by reading the article titles and other basic information to exclude missing basic information (FIL), and non-academic articles (ENR-1). Next, we performed an initial evaluation of the selected papers by reading the abstracts, and excluded 180 papers that were not related to origin of clay (ENR-2) and did not focus on clay charateristics research (NR). After screening and evaluating the papers, all eligible papers were examined in detail and classified according to different subcategories of inclusion criteria: relevant (R) and closely related (CR). In the end, 164 papers were collected, including 92 related, 66 closely related and 6 non English papers. In addition, we re-drawn and re-illustrated the figures and data sheets that needed to be referenced, and obtained permission to transform these figures and sheets.

Particle shape analysis

Particle shape of detrital and authigenic clay

Clay minerals of detrital origin, if not transformed, will be transported and compacted during diagenesis, exhibiting discrete grain boundaries and long-axis oriented features (Table 2., Fig. 2 f) [24,62]. Clay minerals that have been formed by the SST mechanism preserve the crystal size and particle morphology of their parental phase and display some extruded features at the edges of their contours as a result of the pre-existing support of the particle skeleton [24,63]. Simultaneously, it will also inherit pre-existing mica with obvious cleavage or the appearance of altered feldspar remnants (Table 2., Fig. 2 a, d) [24,64]. SST mechanism may occur at high temperature that do not exist on the shallow formation [9,10,24]. Clay minerals precipitated from fluids are generally smaller in size and usually occur in the form of clay aggregates, which are clusters or groupings formed by individual clay particles [24,65]. Their distinctive lath-like and hair-like morphology such as illite can be easily identified (Table 2) [24,63]. Precipitated chlorite is always in the form of a single sheet or an aggregate such as laths, plates, flakes or rosettes (Fig. 2 a, c, e) [2,65]. The precipitated smectite is mainly in the flower-like, flocculent, and honeycomb-like authigenic forms (Fig. 2 h) [2]. The precipitated kaolinite is in the shape of booket-like, vermiform or worms (Fig. 2 g), and a few are in the form of rings, which are often scattered or spotted [2]. More importantly, clay minerals precipitated from fluids are usually filled in pores with random orientation and sometimes forming bridge structures over pores [24,63,65,66].

Techniques of particle shape analysis

SEM-EDS and automated mineralogy techniques provide particle shape analysis of clay minerals to identify clay mineral origin and to quantify the proportion of various origins [24,38,42]. These two mineral analysis techniques are described below.

(1)SEM-EDS

SEM-EDS

High-resolution field emission SEM (FE-SEM) and focused ion beam SEM (FIB-SEM) are commonly used techniques in clay mineral analysis in shales. An important step in sample preparation is polishing the observation surface. As the hardness of the individual shale components is different (i.e. quartz and feldspar is harder than clay and organic matter) [68], mechanical polishing of shale samples typically runs into the following issues: Firstly, producing a smooth enough surface is a difficult task, and secondly, artifacts such as abrasion marks, grain boundary stains, and sand encrustation on the sample surface are often created [38,41,68,69]. These problems could limit the ability to obtain high-resolution FE-SEM images with advanced instruments and in some cases lead to the destruction of the original mineral morphology and the petrographic information of a shale sample. Although fine mechanical polishing may be able to obtain a surface suitable for mineralogical analysis of shale samples, it is complex and time-consuming [68]. Given the limitations of mechanical polishing in shale sample preparation, mechanical polishing coupled with argon (Ar) ion beam milling is now commonly used to polish shale sample surfaces. It allows us to achieve ultra-flat surfaces and avoid artifacts caused by mechanical polishing (Fig. 3 a, b) [38,70,71].

SEM images at same scale showing the difference in surface topography between (a) a mechanically polished surface and (b) an Ar-ion-beam cut surface. Reprinted from Ref. [71] with permission.

A set of petrographic identification criteria is required for detrital and authigenic clay minerals during SEM examination. By summarizing the descriptions of detrital and authigenic clay minerals in the previous literature and the FE-SEM experiments on Chinese terrestrial shales, this paper proposes a set of petrographic identification criteria [24,42,65,72] (Table 2, Fig. 2).

However, it still has some limitations: 1) SEM cannot distinguish clay minerals with unclear boundaries. For instance, smectite-transformed illite is often intercalated with I/S because of incomplete transformation (Fig. 4) [72]. 2) Due to the difficulty of manual counting, the quantitative analysis of clay minerals from different origins using conventional SEM-EDS alone is impractical, which cannot further meet the requirements of climate reconstruction and assessment of the phase of diagenetic evolution [24]. 3) SEM-EDS is limited on the identification of some clay species because of similar major element composition [24,73]. For instance, illite is also capable of adsorbing Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions through ion exchange, which is similar to the chemical composition of I/S mixed layer [74]. Additionally, the presence of tiny I/S mixed layers and illite mixed together in shale matrix further complicates the differentiation solely through EDS analysis. 4) Some tiny clay minerals are commonly smaller than the X-ray generating volume, which leads to difficulties in chemical composition analysis and interpretation [24,73].

The aforementioned issues in studies of clay minerals in shale with SEM-EDS highlight the critical challenge of achieving accurate identification and quantitative analysis of detrital and authigenic clay minerals [75]. One potential approach to address these challenges is through the utilization of Automated Mineralogy Techniques (AMT). This technique may contribute to improving the precision of clay mineral identification and provide a reliable foundation for subsequent quantitative analysis.

AMT were first developed in the mid-1970s based on SEM platform [76,77]. With the development of AMT, they are now able to provide more detailed information and more accurate quantitative report on phase compositions, modal proportions, textures and fabric relationships between mineral grains [78]. The AMT commonly used today are QEMSCAN, MAPS (Modular Automated Processing System), and the newly developed Maps Mineralogy/FEI Nanomin system [24,41,42,45,78].

QEMSCAN is capable of quantitative minerals identification by BSE intensity and EDS analysis and a mineral database or Species Identification Protocol (SIP) [41]. QEMSCAN system constrain the number of minerals per pixel and the resolution of QEMSCAN scanned images is typically insufficient for the accurate description of clay mineral in shale (generally >1 μm) [79]. Those limitations contribute to the complex morphology and decreased accuracy in identifying mineral contact boundaries of clay minerals (e.g., illite, chlorite, etc.) in shale using QEMSCAN imaging (Fig. 5 b) [79,80]. MAPS uses pyramid image processing to stitch together and overlay many scanned images of the sample surface, allowing the observation of structure at the centimeter scale with a large field of view [24,80]. This large-scale image acquisition technique can eliminate the effect of heterogeneity in the structure and minerals distribution of shale [24,81].

(b) The chlorite within the white dashed outline has no visible texture, and the chlorite within the red box is incorrectly recognized as illite.

Maps Mineralogy system is a newer generation of mineral analysis software system based on MAPS and QEMSCAN. Compared QEMSCAN, Maps Mineralogy system allows multiple minerals (up to 3) to be reported within a single pixel, and is optimized for mixed phases of fine-grained sedimentary rocks [24,42]. This means that Maps Mineralogy system has a much higher accuracy in mineral identification than QEMSCAN (Fig. 5). Nanomin's resolution can reach up to 1 μm, and by overlaying BSE images (100 nm pixel resolution), Nanomin can detect submicron and even nanoscale minerals. At the same time, Maps Mineralogy/Nanomin system can describe macroscale features by stitching together high-resolution quantified mineral maps up to 2 cm2 [24,45]. With higher resolution and accuracy than QEMSCAN, Maps Mineralogy/Nanomin system achieves better results when describing petrographic features of the clay minerals in shale (Fig. 5 a). Other SEM-EDS based AMTs include Tescan Integrated Mineral Analyzer (TIMA), Mineral Liberation Analyzer (MLA), and Advanced/Automatic Mineral Characterization System (AMICS), which are similar in principle to QEMSCAN, but are less capable than the Maps Mineralogy/Nanomin system of characterizing micron-submicron clay mineral profiles and surface textures [82].

Researchers have experimented with the identification of clay mineral origins through the Maps Mineralogy system [24,42,45]. The analysis process can be simplified into three main steps. First, they examined high-resolution large-area shale images obtained from the Maps Mineralogy system to describe the petrographic and mineralogical characteristics of clay minerals. This allowed them to establish identification criteria for detrital and authigenic clays in the study area (Fig. 6 a-g). Next, they used these criteria to quantitatively interpret the origin of clay minerals in representative Maps Mineralogy mineral mapping images (Fig. 7 a, b). Lastly, they determined the specific occupancy of counted detrital and authigenic clay minerals in their original form on the image (Fig. 7 c). By conducting quantitative analysis of these clay minerals, they were able to assess diagenesis, climate, and reservoir information, including pore structure of clay [24,42,45,75].

Crystal structure analysis

Crystal structure of detrital and authigenic clay

The crystal structures of detrital and authigenic clay minerals differ due to the different temperature and pressure histories they have experienced [83]. The differences in the crystal structures of illite and chlorite from different origins have been studied extensively [48,53,84,85]. Other clay minerals, such as kaolin group, also contain three polytypes in nature: kaolinite, dickite and nacrite [86,87], but there is relatively little information on their origins. Therefore, this study will focus on the polytype characteristics and origins of illite and chlorite.

Illite is a mineral that occurs naturally in different polytypes, including 2M1, 1M, 1Md, and 3T [83]. In sedimentary rocks, the prevalent polytypes of illite are 2M1, 1M, and 1Md, with the 3T polytype being uncommon [48]. Different Illite polytypes 1M/1Md and 2M1 exhibit unique stacking patterns because of different formation temperatures (Fig. 8) [24,[88], [89], [90], [91]]. Yoder and Eugster [92] concluded that the 1Md structure was the initial or metastable low-temperature form, and that transition to the 2M1 polytype occurs between 200 °C and 350 °C at 15,000 psi water pressure. In shale, the 2M1 type of illite is typically of detrital origin, while the 1Md and 1M polytypes are generally of authigenic origin [48,93,94]. There are several reasons supporting these conclusions: 1) K-Ar dating indicates that the age of 2M1 illite is older than the stratigraphic age, while 1Md and 1M illite are younger than the stratigraphic age [53,95]. 2) The proportion of 2M1 illite decreases with decreasing grain size [48,51]. 3) As the proportion of 1Md and 1M illite increases and the ages of the illite determined by K-Ar dating are younger [51,53]. 4) The 2M1 polytype is recognized as the most stable among these three polytypes [96,97]. 5) K-bentonites of sedimentary basins that have undergone diagenesis to I/S or illite do not contain detrital 2M1 dioctahedral illite [51,53]. 6) Exclusively 1M illite grows in the pore space of clean sandstone which lacks depositional clay [51,53]. 7) The spiral or step growth mechanisms of 1M illite are consistent with the AFM morphological observations of authigenic illite [51,96].

Chlorite has a similar polytype character to illite and a total of four chlorite polytypes have been identified in sedimentary rocks [84,85,99,100]: 1) Ⅱb, the polytype of common metamorphic and igneous chlorites; 2) Ⅰb (β = 90°); 3) Ⅰb (β = 97°); 4) Ⅰa. Type I chlorites form at temperatures and pressures lower than those of low-grade metamorphism during diagenesis or, less commonly, halmyrolysis [84,85]. IIb, the most stable and common polytype, tends to be of detrital origin in sedimentary rocks [84,85,99]. Similar to illite, these four chlorite polytypes have differences in stacking sequence (Fig. 9) [85,99].

Techniques of crystal structure analysis

(1)Identification and quantification of polytypism

Identification and quantification of polytypism

XRD is one of the most important analytical approaches used in the qualitative and quantitative study of clay minerals [34,[101], [102], [103], [104]]. Various illite polytypes have different XRD characteristics allowing quantitative analysis of illite polytypes by this method (Fig. 10) [48,53]. The peak diagnostics of 2M1 illite are as follows: 3.88 Å, 3.73 Å, 3.49 Å, 3.20 Å, 2.98 Å, 2.86 Å, 2.79 Å, while for 1M illite, they are 4.34 Å, 3.63 Å, 3.07 Å, 2.91 Å, and 2.67 Å (Fig. 10). In contrast, 1Md illite typically exhibits weak to no polytype-specific reflections (Fig. 10). Different chlorite polytypes also show different characteristic peak features on XRD (Fig. 11) [84,100]. The chlorite polytypes can be identified by comparison between the characteristic peaks of chlorite polytypes and the difference of two unoriented diffractograms (one from the original sample and the other from hot HCl leached sample) [99,100]. Due to the additional processing required, quantification of chlorite polytypes is difficult by XRD.

Although many methods can identify and quantify clay mineral polytypes, there are many limitations of those method. 1) quantitative polytype estimates are difficult to obtain reliable results in shales with a low content of clay minerals due to the difficulty of the clay separation [111]. 2) The quantitative measurement of polytypes does not accurately reflect the original proportion of polytypes in the rock, as the results can differ based on the grinding grain size of the bulk sample [48,53,105]. These limitations reduce the widespread use of this method in clay polytype analysis in complex shale.

Furthermore, techniques such as TEM combined with selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) and SEM combined with electron back-scattering pattern (EBSD) enable the identification of individual clay grain polytypes [24,86,[88], [89], [90], [91]]. The TEM-SAED system enables the differentiation of mica polytypes by observing crystals with the electron beam parallel to the crystal layers. This is achieved through imaging the periodicity and interlayer displacements in the stacking sequences at an atomic scale [91]. The TEM-SAED system is widely applied in illite polytype dentification. However, this method seems less effective because the intensity of the electron diffraction is strongly dependent on the specimen thickness [86]. The SEM-EBSD system is able to analyze the relationship between polytype and morphology and has demonstrated favorable outcomes in the polytypes analysis of the kaolinite group [86,112]. However, this technique is not well-suited for studying fine dioctahedral mica due to its requirement for large and thick crystals [91].

Both the SEM-EBSD system and the TEM-SAED system are powerful for polytype identification of individual clay particles, but are not as effective as XRD for quantitatively analyzing clay polytype.

(2)Calculation of crystallinity

Calculation of crystallinity

The X-ray diffraction results can also be used to calculate the crystallinity of clay minerals. The crystallinity reflects the degree of order in the crystal lattice of the crystalline substance [50]. Generally, clay minerals have a higher crystallinity when formed under anchizone and epizone conditions, compared to clay minerals formed under sedimentary-diagenetic conditions. Therefore, the diagenesis stage of clay minerals can be judged according to the crystallinity, so that the formation conditions can be reversed, the paleoenvironment can be reconstructed, and the source of clay minerals can be further judged [52].

To calculate the crystallinity of clay minerals by X-ray diffraction pattern, it is necessary to determine the special diffraction peaks of clay minerals, and then calculate the specific index of the characteristic peaks. The most used reflections are the (02,11) band of kaolinite at a range of 0.215–0.275 Å, the (001) smectite at 17 Å, the (001) illite at 10 Å, and the (002) chlorite at 7 Å, known as the Hinkley index, V/P value, the Kübler index, and the Árkai index, respectively [[113], [114], [115], [116], [117]]. In addition to indicating crystallinity, the characteristics of clay mineral crystallinity being influenced by temperature variations make these indices also valuable for paleo-temperature reconstructions [52,115]. Next, the principals involved in calculating these indices will be explained.

Hinckley Index is one of widely used indices and termed the "crystallinity index" [118,119]. As the crystallinity decreased, the proportion of random shifts between adjacent layers by ± nb/3 increased, resulting in a decrease in the resolution of neighboring peaks and an increase in the inter-peak diffraction intensity [119]. At the same time, an increase in the frequency of defects would decrease the absolute intensity of the (110) peak. The normal values range from <0.5 (disordered) to 1.5 (ordered) [120]. Due to montmorillonite peaks are usually very wide and occur at a low 2θ angle, it is difficult to establish a baseline and makes peak area measurements imprecise. To calculate the crystallinity of montmorillonite group, Biscaye (1965) proposed V/P value. The V/P value is the valley to peak (V/P) ratio of the (001) peak, where P is the maximum height of the (001) basal reflection peak in the XRD pattern and V is the minimum of the valley on the low-angle side of that peak (Fig. 13) [113]. However, the V/P ratio utilizes only the measurement of the vertical peak parameter and does not directly measure the peak width, it may not be completely independent of the montmorillonite abundance and therefore is not a true measure of crystallinity [117]. Therefore, the V/P value is not widely used as other indexes (the Hinkley index, the Kübler index, and the Árkai index).

The Kübler index and Árkai index are the most commonly used indexes to determine the state of illite ‘crystallinity’ and chlorite ‘crystallinity’ [115,122,123]. The two indexes are calculated by the half-peak-width values of the 001 illite and 002 chlorite reflections, and today the majority of researchers utilize the full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) parameter (Fig. 14) [115,116]. FWHM can be measured by hand on printed XRD chart paper [123,124] and computer software programs (e.g. MacDiff program) [50,52].

However, the peak-width-based XRD method is very sensitive to analytical variations. Even the same sample can be measured in different laboratories with significantly different results. To address this issue, a calibration approach using a rock-chip standard has been proposed [125]. This approach is widely utilized for calibrating to a standardized scale of measurement known as the Crystallinity Index Standards (CIS). Additionally, a new set of CIS samples has been introduced to replace the largely depleted set that was originally introduced in 1994 [50].

Chemical analysis

The enrichment properties of different chemical elements vary in nature [58,[126], [127], [128]]. Certain compounds and elements such as Al2O3, TiO2, Zr, and Hf are considered terrigenous elements and are enriched only in the detrital fraction [58,127], while other elements like Fe2+, Mg2+, V, Cr, and Co are enriched or adsorbed by authigenic fractions in some areas [55]. In addition, various clay mineral sources naturally have different patterns of elemental enrichment [55,[129], [130], [131]]. This distinction reflects the specific behavior of these elements in the geological context and their respective origins. Several studies investigated the variations in elemental enrichment of clay minerals from various sources (Table 4). Chemical research on shale clay mineral sources has been widely applied. In general, it is split into macro- and micro-chemical analysis.

Micro-chemical analysis

Micro-chemical analysis in geological studies is commonly conducted using an electron probe X-ray microanalyzer (EPMA). EPMA, specifically with a wavelength dispersive X-ray spectrometer (WDS), enables more accurate quantitative elemental analysis for all elements except H, He, and Li compared to energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS). EPMA has a detection limit that can reach parts per million (ppm) levels, depending on the selected elements and conditions [78,133]. It is particularly effective in analyzing trace elements such as La, Y, Sc, Cr, Th, Zr, Hf, Nb, and rare earth elements (REE), which are valuable indicators of provenance, geological processes, and tectonic settings due to their low mobility during sedimentary processes [134,135]. By comparing the trace element profiles of sediments with potential source rocks, researchers can determine the likely sources contributing to a sedimentary basin. For example, the Zr/Sc ratio of less than 10 in certain sandstone samples from Hesse, southwestern Germany, and easternmost France suggests sediment-sourcing from catchments with low zircon content or minimal heavy-mineral sorting [136].

EPMA's capability to analyze individual clay mineral particles provides valuable insights into their detrital and authigenic origins, offering better visualization of their differences [137]. However, the same problem of not being able to quantify clay minerals exists with EPMA alone [138,139]. EPMA based mapping technology may realize the quantitative of the clay mineral from different origins. Unlike SEM-EDS based mapping techniques for identifying and quantifying detrital and authigenic clay minerals, EPMA mapping is able to take precise chemical information into account [138]. However, the higher accelerating voltage used in EMPA compared to EDS results in a larger analyzed volume, which can complicate mineral mapping in fine-grained lithologies [78,140]. To address this issue, overlaying the Backscattered Electron (BSE) image can potentially provide a solution. By combining the BSE image with quantitative analysis, there is great potential for fully automated identification and quantification of clay mineral origins.

Macro-chemical analysis

Macro-scale chemical analysis is mainly used to determine the origin of clay minerals by chemical discriminant function analysis and geochemistry-clay correlation analysis. Chemical discriminant function analysis can be used to construct a reliable provenance classification plot to determine the origin of clay minerals. Many scientists have used various chemical indices to identify the provenance of sediments (Table 5). This method of analysis is based on bulk chemistry, which does not give a good indication of the origin of a particular mineral type in the mixed phase [141,142]. Dilution of the chemical information of the target mineral by other minerals makes it difficult to identify the origin of a particular clay mineral type in shale. Pure claystone is mostly used in current applications of chemical discriminant function analysis for the identification of clay mineral sources in shale [143]. There are two options that can solve this problem. One is to perform a chemical analysis of the clay minerals using mapping tools based on EMPA. The other is to separate the clay minerals in the shale and then perform a chemical analysis.

Geochemistry and clay correlation analysis can be used to estimate the proportions of detrital and authigenic clay minerals. The basic principle is that some chemical compounds and elements (e.g., Al2O3, TiO2, Zr, Hf, etc.) in some areas are considered as terrigenous elements and are enriched only in the detrital fraction [58,127]. Similarly, authigenic elements (e. g. Fe2+, Mg2+, V, Cr, Co, etc.) are enriched only during diagenesis [55]. In addition, some element concentrations (Rb, Bi, etc.) have a strong positive correlation with clay mineral contents in limestone, so such chemical element concentrations can be used to reflect clay mineral contents [58]. A series of functional equations can be derived by fitting the data to the clay mineral content, clay representative element concentrations, terrigenous element concentrations, and authigenic element concentrations (specific steps can be seen in ref. Lei, Huang, Jiang and Luo [58]). The proportion of detrital and authigenic clay minerals can be calculated by the empirical formulas obtained via the steps in Fig. 15. The strong correlation between clay content and specific chemical elements observed in limestone, as demonstrated by Lei, Huang, Jiang and Luo [58], may not hold true for shales with complex compositions. Shales contain a significant proportion of detrital or authigenic non-clay minerals such as feldspar and quartz in addition to clay minerals, and terrigenous or authigenic elements can become enriched on these non-clay minerals [57,148,149]. As a result, the establishment of a strong linear relationship between clay minerals and specific chemical elements, as seen in limestone, may be disrupted in shales. The complex mineral composition of shales introduces variability, necessitating a nuanced approach when studying the relationship between chemical elements and clay minerals in different sedimentary rock types.

Weathering indices, such as the Chemical Index of Alteration (CIA = Al2O3/(Al2O3 + K2O + CaO∗ + Na2O) × 100), Chemical Index of Weathering (CIW = Al2O3/(Al2O3 + CaO∗ + Na2O × 100), Plagioclase Index of Alteration (PIA = (Al2O3 - K2O)/(Al2O3 - K2O + CaO∗ + Na2O) × 100), and Weathering Index of Parker (WIP= CaO∗/0.7 + 2Na2O/0.35 + 2K2O/0.25 + MgO/0.9) × 100), play a crucial role in providing insights into the sediment sources [150,151]. These indices can offer valuable information about the weathering processes and sediment sources. Fedo, Wayne Nesbitt and Young [152] concluded that CIA values between 50 and 60 reflect a weak degree of weathering; CIA values between 60 and 80 indicate a moderate degree of weathering; and CIA values between 80 and 100 reflect a strong degree of weathering. However, it's important to consider that the concentration of chemical elements can be influenced by various factors such as mineral transformations during diagenesis and the mineralogical and chemical differentiation induced by hydrodynamic sorting in sediments [152,153]. For instance, the conversion of secondary aluminous clay minerals such as kaolinite to illite by K addition results in a CIA value lower than the premetasomatized one [152]. These factors can potentially mask the signals of climate-controlled weathering in sediments, leading to a decoupling of chemical weathering indices values from the actual climatic conditions [151]. Therefore, when using these indices to determine the source of sediments in clay-rich shale, additional corrections and considerations are necessary due to the specific characteristics of clay-rich shale and the potential impact of fine particle size and extensive tranformation of clay minerals on the weathering indices.

Applications

Illite age analysis (IAA)

Illite age analysis (IAA) is widely used in basin burial and thermal history calibration, especially useful in areas of complex structure, like fold and thrust belts [48,51,53,105,154]. In most shales, illite consists of a mixture of at least 2 different polytypes (e. g. 2M1, 1Md) of potentially different origins [48,53], so that a quantitative analysis of the different origins of illite is required (e.g., via a XRD study). Once the proportions of the various illite polytypes are determined, K-Ar (most used) or 40Ar/39Ar age of detrital and diagenetic end-members can be extrapolated [51,155]. The specific steps are to quantify (by XRD) the proportion of end members in each of three size fractions (<0.2 μm, <0.5 μm, <2 μm), plot the points (normalized to 100 %) as apparent K-Ar age versus percentage of detrital illite, and linearly extrapolate to 0 and 100 % detrital to obtain end member ages [51,53,156]. IAA shows good accuracy in stratigraphic age and fault age analysis [105,156]. For example, two cores in the Maquoketa group are extrapolated to the same detrital age, and different diagenetic ages reflect the diagenetic age of the layer at different depths. Three grain size fractions from two sites of the Lewis thrust are extrapolated to the same detrital and diagenetic age [48,51,53,105,154] (Fig. 16).

In addition to the commonly used bulk techniques like K-Ar and 40Ar/39Ar dating, in situ Rb-Sr dating of silicate minerals and rocks is gaining popularity as a geochronological technique [157]. This method utilizes laser ablation-inductively coupled plasma tandem mass spectrometry with a collision/reaction cell (LA-ICP-MS/MS) to determine the timing of mineral formation [46,158]. This novel and rapid dating technique allows for interference-free measurement of isobaric 87Rb and 87Sr isotopes in K- and Rb-rich minerals such as illite and glauconite [157]. The advantage of in situ Rb-Sr dating is that it allows for precise analysis of individual mineral grains, providing a more detailed understanding of the geological history [46,157,159]. Moreover, the LA-ICP-MS/MS technique offers high spatial resolution, allowing researchers to analyze multiple spots within a single mineral grain [159]. This capability provides insights into the heterogeneity of the sample, revealing variations in mineral growth or resetting ages.

Paleoclimate reconstruction

Clay minerals in sediments, especially in marine sediments, are useful indicators of paleoclimate conditions [2,16,160]. Detrital clay minerals (e.g., smectite and kaolinite minerals) used in paleoclimate reconstruction, however, are reactive materials prone to early diagenetic or burial alteration in response to changes in pore fluid composition, temperature, and pressure [16,24,42]. Therefore, when using clay minerals for paleoclimate reconstruction, the identification and quantification of detrital and authigenic clay minerals can extract information about the detrital component from the clay of mixed origins, thus optimizing clay minerals as a method for paleoclimate reconstruction. Levels relatively rich in chlorite, illite, palygorskite and quartz are interpreted as corresponding to relatively dry periods, while more humid periods lead to more intensive weathering and consequently to the dominance of clay minerals, such as kaolinite [2,16]. Smectite is taken to indicate a climate with contrasting seasons and a pronounced dry season [16]. The content of those climate indicative clay minerals may change during diagenesis. The (smectite + I/S mixed layers + kaolinite)/(illite + chlorite) index can be used to evaluate the climatic conditions, with a low index suggesting weak chemical weathering and arid climatic conditions [160]. Quantitative analysis of detrital and authigenic clay minerals can thus make the index be valid in the shale affected by diagenesis. In addition, when using chemical index (e.g., CIA index) to analyze paleoclimate, determining the proportion of detrital and authigenic clay minerals can confirm the rationality of the indices and provide a more credible result [42]. In conclusion, when using clays for paleoclimate reconstruction, the identification and quantification of clay mineral origins is a critical step in improving analytical confidence of the reconstruction result [42,160].

Oil and gas reservoir properties

(1)Clay and organic matter.

Clay and organic matter.

Clay minerals and their interaction with organic matter have been a subject of significant interest in the field of geoscience. The differing origins of clay minerals, whether detrital or authigenic, play distinct roles in the enrichment and preservation of organic matter during diagenesis. For example, authigenic illitic clays may exhibit greater reactivity compared to their detrital counterparts, as the latter are more susceptible to modification through weathering and the adsorption of organic and oxide components [63]. Detrital clays play a crucial role in the agglomeration of organic matter, while authigenic clay minerals are essential for the preservation of organic matter [161]. In addition, there is synergistic evolution of clay minerals and organic matter, e.g. H+ released by thermal evolution of organic matter can promote the growth of clay minerals such as chlorite [162]. This distinction suggests that the relationship between organic matter and different origins of clay minerals may vary across different diagenetic stages. Therefore, the identification and quantification of detrital and authigenic clay minerals are crucial for advancing research on the mechanisms of organic matter enrichment.

(2)Clay and pore structure.

Clay and pore structure.

The pore structure of unconventional hydrocarbon shale reservoirs and its correlation with shale mineralogy are crucial for gaining a comprehensive understanding of how to effectively exploit these rocks [63]. Detrital and authigenic clay minerals have been found to significantly influence the distribution and development of porosity and permeability [23,24]. It is particularly significant to analyze the particles size distribution and characterize the predominant morphology of clay minerals (Fig. 17). The mica-transformed illite exhibit more slit-shape pores, and pore diameter is over 100 nm (Fig. 17 a, a1-3). The pores size of clay minerals transformed from feldspar are smaller than mica-transformed illite, mostly below 200 nm (Fig. 17 b, b1-3). Detrital clay minerals show denser and almost no pore space while pore-filled chlorite develops many intergranular pores and can be filled by oil (Fig. 17 c, c1-3). Due to the variable relationship between clay origin and pore structure, the identification and quantification of detrital and authigenic clay minerals is important in assessing the reservoir properties of a clay-rich shale [63].

Outlooks and perspectives

SEM-EDS and automated mineralogy techniques allow the identification and quantification of detrital and authigenic clay minerals [24,42,45]. However, there are still problems with quantification, calling for the need for manual grain counting and identification. Using the information on the orientation and microstructures (cleavage and sharpness) of the clay mineral grains shown in the images, the identification and quantification of clay minerals may be able to achieve automatically. An image Analysis based code called MiCA (microstructural clay analyzer) realize quantitative analysis of the particle orientation and microstructure in clay materials [163,164]. Given this, the code could be further optimized to account for clay mineral particle orientation and microstructures to achieve automated quantitation of clay mineral origins. In addition, clay minerals can be identified and quantified from chemical aspects by EPMA mapping technique [78,138]. Clearer boundaries for authigenic and detrital clay minerals can be obtained by combining SEM and EMPA, which has great potential for quantitative analysis of clay origins [78]. XRD is very useful in identifying and quantifying clay minerals and in some cases, for identifying and quantifying the detrital and authentic origins such as the case of illite and chlorite. However, due to the large analytical errors that can occur in complex shales, the reliability of this result needs to be further verified [111]. Chemical analysis has been widely used to identify clay mineral sources, but its quantitative application has not been widespread and is limited by the chemical-clay mineral relationship [58]. It is not possible to quantitatively identify all mineralogical features, chemical and/or physical by using a single technique [78]. Instead, a multi-technique approach that uses individual and bulk techniques will improve data quality and reliability and ultimately increase the degree of confidence in the data interpretation and conclusions made [45,78]. The proportions of detrital and authigenic clay minerals obtained by automated mineralogy techniques, XRD and chemical analysis can be cross-validated by linear fitting analysis using binary plots. This multiple method is currently widely used for QEMSCAN/Nanomin and XRD quantitative data correction [42,45]. Therefore, when characterizing clay minerals, data obtained by automated mineralogical techniques, XRD or chemical analysis should be considered in combination with data acquired by other analytical methods.

Conclusions

Detrital clay minerals exhibit the phenomenon of discrete grain boundaries and long axes are (sub)parallel to the primary sedimentary structures, while authigenic clay minerals have specific particle shapes. The differences in crystal structure of different clay mineral origins are reflected in the polytype character of clay minerals. Different origins of clay minerals inherently have different elemental enrichment characteristics.

For the differences in particle shape, crystal structure, and geochemistry of the clay minerals in the reservoirs, SEM-EDS, automated mineralogy technique, XRD and chemical analysis is used to identify and quantify the origins of the clay mineral in shales. Each technique has its advantage and disadvantages. SEM-EDS and automated mineralogy techniques have the advantages in petrographic information acquisition, while having the disadvantages in the need for manual counting to obtain quantitative results of detrital and authigenic clay minerals. The automated quantitation of clay mineral origins may be achieved by the code technique. XRD techniques have more uncertainty in quantifying and identifying clay origins and are limited to clay minerals with polytype properties. Chemical analysis has been widely used to identify clay mineral sources, but its quantitative application is limited by the complex chemical-clay mineral relationship in shales. For better identification and quantification of detrital and authigenic clay minerals, in addition to improvements in the various techniques themselves, the combination of automated mineralogical techniques, XRD and chemical analysis that complement each other can also improve the accuracy and credibility of the results. Analysis of clay minerals origins is widely applied in illite age analysis, paleoclimate reconstruction, and reservoir properties analysis. It can correct the results of illite age dating and paleoclimate reconstruction, and contribute to evaluating the impact of clay minerals from different origins on shale reservoir properties. In conclusion, the analysis of clay mineral origins is still a challenge and further development of technique and more efficient analysis strategy are essential.
