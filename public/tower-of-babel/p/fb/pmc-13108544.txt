Unspecific Peroxygenases‐Catalyzed Oxidation of Pharmaceutical Compounds Considered Emerging Contaminants
Authors: Alina Torres‐Aguirre, Azucena López‐López, Mayra Avelar, Miguel Alcalde, Marcela Ayala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Unspecific peroxygenase (UPO) is versatile fungal enzyme that carries out with high efficiency a plethora of C–H oxyfunctionalizations of organic compounds, simply triggered by H2O2. With the goal of making UPO an industrial biocatalyst, it has been subjected over the years to different directed evolution campaigns focused on expression, stability, activity, and selectivity. In this work, we tested the ability of PaDa‐I and JaWa, evolved variants of the UPO from Agrocybe aegerita, to catalyze the oxidation of a series of pharmaceutical compounds, considered emerging contaminants (EC), or microcontaminants. Remarkably, these UPO mutants were able to catalyze the oxidation of 9 out of 12 EC, albeit with different efficacy. A detailed analysis of the reaction efficiency and the nontoxic nature of the reaction products point these UPO mutants as promising tools for enzymatic bioremediation schemes.

Introduction

Emerging contaminants (EC) are unregulated substances, present in everyday products, that accumulate in the environment. They have been detected particularly in water bodies, in concentrations as low as ng/L to μg/L. No lethal effects are observed at these low concentrations; however, it is known that EC exert a harmful effect on living beings upon long‐term exposure [1]. For example, some EC are endocrine disruptors, mimicking the action of hormones, perturbing fertility, and other reproductive traits in aquatic organisms [2]; exposure to other EC produces genetic defects leading to deformities in reptiles and malformations in fish embryos [3, 4]; certain EC inhibit algae growth and embryo‐larval development of aquatic animals [5, 6].

EC are present in both over‐the‐counter and prescription drugs. Analgesics, antibiotics, anti‐inflammatories, anticoagulants, antidepressants, and many other pharmaceutical compounds have been detected all over the world in domestic and industrial wastewater, streams in wastewater treatment plant (WTP), and also in natural water bodies, such as lakes, rivers, and underground aquifers [7]. The chemical nature of some of the EC renders them recalcitrant and persistent compounds, but other factors preclude their efficient biotic elimination. For instance, microorganisms in nature, and also in WTP, may not degrade EC because their concentration may be below the Ks, the substrate affinity constant controlling microbial growth kinetics. For example, it has been observed that for Escherichia coli, a very good carbon source, such as glucose, has a Ks of around 20 μg/L [8]. Less assimilable carbon sources could result in larger Ks, thus limiting their degradation rate when their concentration is low, as is the case for EC. For some environmental applications, the use of enzymes alone, without the need to sustain the whole‐cell machinery, may overcome these and other limitations.

Several physical and chemical treatments have been evaluated for the removal of EC, including photocatalytic degradation, ferrate technology, membrane filtration, and adsorption. While these methods, alongside biological processes, have achieved removal efficiencies above 90% [9, 10, 11], many of these technologies involve high electricity consumption and more importantly, the formation of toxic byproduct, which poses additional environmental risks [9, 12].

In this context, enzymatic approaches have emerged as a promising alternative for the removal of EC from wastewater. As biodegradable, highly selective biocatalysts, enzymes operate under mild conditions of temperature and pH, thereby reducing energy requirements and minimizing the formation of hazardous byproducts. These biocatalysts are naturally adapted to recognize compounds (substrates) with high affinity, reflected in the value of K M, which in some cases is as low as μM [13]. Several examples are available on the use of enzymes to catalyze the oxidation of EC, with mixed results, in terms of conversion, catalyst consumption, reaction efficiency, and importantly, a reduced harmful nature of the reaction products. In this work, we investigated the ability of fungal unspecific peroxygenases (UPOs, EC 1.11.2.1) to catalyze the transformation of selected ECs. UPOs are efficient heme‐thiolate mono(per)oxygenases that use hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as their sole cosubstrate, functioning both as electron acceptor and oxygen donor. Mechanistically, UPO catalysis largely corresponds to the “peroxide shunt” pathway of cytochrome P450 monooxygenases; however, in most cases, UPOs exhibit higher catalytic turnover rates [14]. Through a peroxygenative route involving two‐electron oxidation reactions, UPOs catalyze a wide range of transformations, including aliphatic, aromatic, and heterocyclic hydroxylations; epoxidation of aliphatic and aromatic alkenes; O‐ and N‐dealkylations; sulfoxidations; and halogenations [15, 16]. In addition, UPOs can mediate one‐electron oxidation reactions (peroxidative route) of phenolic compounds. To date, all characterized UPOs have been identified almost exclusively in fungi and can be classified into two main clades (short and long UPO families) based on phylogenetic relatedness and molecular size [17]. Overall, more than 400 organic compounds have been reported as substrates for UPO‐catalyzed biotransformations [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

The unspecific peroxygenase from Agrocybe (Cyclocybe) aegerita (AaeUPO), the first UPO to be characterized, is considered the canonical member of family of long UPOs. AaeUPO was also the first enzyme of this class to be successfully heterologously expressed in yeast in a functional, secreted form through directed evolution [24]. This laboratory evolution effort resulted in the PaDa‐I variant, a highly active and stable UPO, harbored five mutations in the mature protein (V57A, L67F, V75I, I248V, F311L) and four mutations in the signal peptide (F12Y, A14V, R15G y A21D) that is fully compatible with tandem yeast expression systems, enabling directed evolution in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and high‐level production in Pichia pastoris (Komagataella phaffii) [25]. Consequently, the prototypic AaeUPO, together with its PaDa‐I mutant, have been the subject of a large number of studies in the field. Indeed, the JaWa mutant was obtained through directed evolution of PaDa‐I with the goal to improve the oxidation of naphthalene into 1‐naphthol, which only incorporated two mutations G241D and R257K [26]. This variant also displays higher catalytic efficiency toward bulky substrates such as dextromethorphan and propranolol than PaDa‐I [27, 28]. However, despite these promising properties, it has been less extensively studied than PaDa‐I.

In this work, the ability of PaDa‐I and JaWa to catalyze the oxidation of a series of 12 pharmaceutical compounds was explored; not all of them being described substrates within this enzyme group. The substrate conversion, catalyst inactivation, and preliminary studies on the toxicity of the reaction products is reported, as well as a discussion on the advantages and disadvantages of this enzymatic treatment for EC transformation.

Results and Discussion

The studied pharmaceutical compounds, shown in Figure 1, provide a variety of therapeutic effects and are among the most frequently detected ECs in water bodies [29, 30, 31]. Their chemical structure share some common features, as all of them contain at least one aromatic ring, as well as functional groups that are amenable to oxidation, based on reports with PaDa‐I, JaWa, and other related UPOs [27, 32]. Specifically, the studied ECs contain ether groups, aliphatic chains, and/or heteroatoms such as nitrogen in heterocyclic rings and in aliphatic and aromatic amines. Both PaDa‐I and JaWa were able to catalyze the oxidation of the majority of the compounds from the studied selection.

During the quantification of the substrate conversion, we noticed that the reaction was halted, and it was only resumed upon the addition of enzyme and H2O2. This behavior has been observed before for UPOs, and it is well documented for PaDa‐I during the oxidation of challenging substrates, particularly at low concentrations. For example, in a previous work, we reported that the oxidation of aromatic ethers requires multiple additions of enzyme and H2O2 to achieve quantifiable conversion of the substrates when present at concentrations of 200 μM [33]. It has also been observed that for sulfamethoxazole [34] and propranolol [28], reactions containing low concentrations of substrate (below the KM value) become halted and can only proceed upon multiple additions of enzyme and/or H2O2. Apparently, at low substrate concentration, secondary, unproductive reactions take importance, namely enzyme suicide inactivation, and H2O2 depletion. The first reaction, the irreversible inactivation of the enzyme due to oxidative damage, has been well documented for heme‐peroxidases [35]. This effect arises from the formation of a highly unstable and reactive intermediate, compound III—an iron(III) superoxide radical complex—which ultimately promotes enzyme inactivation through at least two distinct pathways: (i) heme bleaching, involving cleavage of the protoporphyrin ring into biliverdin and Fe(III); and/or (ii) protein damage resulting from interactions with the reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated. The second reaction, H2O2 consumption, has also been observed and quantified in our group. In the presence of low concentrations of reducing agent, catalase‐like reaction predominates, resulting in peroxide depletion [33]. In these cases, increasing the substrate concentration up to mM range usually alleviates the problem. However, in this work, we were interested in characterizing the ability of the UPOs to catalyze the oxidation of EC when these are present at low concentrations. Reactions were performed by periodically adding several doses of enzyme and H2O2 until a relatively high conversion of the substrate was achieved, as detailed in Supporting Information S1 and Figures S1 to S12. We found that fluoxetine, ketorolac, and tetracycline were not substrates for PaDa‐I or JaWa, under the tested conditions. Steric and chemical reactivity issues could be the cause of the lack of enzymatic reaction. The peroxygenase activity (two‐electron oxidations) requires the substrate to come into close contact (less than 4 Å) with the activated heme group (Compound I) in order for the oxygen‐atom transfer to occur; the substrate must enter the active site cavity through a substrate access tunnel [36]. Fluoxetine bears three fluor atoms, which could render the compound too hydrophobic to enter the substrate access tunnel. Tetracycline was already reported not to be a substrate for PaDa‐I [34], and in this work, we found the same for JaWa. Tetracycline was the more voluminous molecule of the collection (Figure 1). The bulkiness and also the flatness of the compound due to the series of fused ring in its structure could result in a lack of molecular flexibility which may be necessary to enter and accommodate into the substrate access tunnel. For ketorolac, the absence of reaction is puzzling, as it is a relatively small molecule, compared to other compounds that were substrates for the enzymes, and also contains chemically reactive groups, notably the aromatic and imidazole rings. However, despite multiple additions of either enzyme and H2O2, no conversion was detected for this compound.

In the case of the 9 compounds that were substrates for the studied UPOs, both PaDa‐I and JaWa were able to catalyze the oxidation of almost all of them, although differences in the efficiency of the reactions were observed. The exceptions were propranolol and atenolol, which were oxidized in the presence of JaWa but not PaDa‐I. PaDa‐I showed a better performance than JaWa for the oxidation of sulfamethoxazole, diclofenac, sulfacetamide, and naproxen, albeit sulfacetamide and naproxen required large doses of the enzyme and the conversion was not complete. For sulfanilamide and trazodone, the best catalyst was JaWa, requiring a smaller amount of enzyme, compared to PaDa‐I, to achieve large conversions. As mentioned above, in the case of propranolol and atenolol, only JaWa was able to catalyze their oxidation, albeit with low efficiency as it required several additions of the enzyme with atenolol. For ibuprofen, the efficiency of both catalysts was comparable. An alternative measure of the efficiency of a catalyst is the total turnover number (TTN, amount of converted substrate/amount of employed catalyst), shown in Table 1. Depending on the substrate, TTN varied by 50‐fold (roughly from 90 to ca. 4500). In general, JaWa was a more versatile catalyst as it exhibited a broader substrate specificity. In previous reports, using higher concentration of pharmaceutical compounds, larger TTN are observed for different UPOs, as expected due to a higher reaction rate that helps overcome the secondary, unproductive reactions mentioned above (Table S1).

PaDa‐I and JaWa are similar enzymes, as JaWa is derived from PaDa‐I through directed evolution. Its catalytic properties toward standard substrates are similar [26]. The explanation for the different substrate specificities observed in this work between the variants could be attributed to subtle changes in key positions. For instance, the G241D mutation present in JaWa is located at the entrance of the substrate access tunnel (Figure 2) leading to the activated heme group. The presence of a negative charge potentially affects substrate access, as well as its orientation and stabilization within the catalytic site. This effect may be particularly pronounced for sulfamethoxazole, diclofenac, and naproxen, as the anionic species are predominant under the reaction conditions (pKa = 5.7, 4.15, and 4.15), thereby increasing electrostatic repulsion within the negatively charged tunnel entrance. This unfavorable interaction explains the lower efficiency of JaWa for the oxidation of these substrates.

PaDa‐I and JaWa have been studied before for the oxidation of propranolol [28]. In that work, both enzymes were shown to catalyze the oxidation of propranolol to 5‐hydroxypropranolol (Table 2); however, PaDa‐I displayed a 33‐fold lower catalytic efficiency compared to JaWa. This difference was mainly attributed to a higher K M value (K M = 2268 µM) for PaDa‐I compared to JaWa (K M = 244 µM). Thus, in our case, the lack of activity observed with PaDa‐I is explained by the low concentration of propranolol used in the reaction. Moreover, in 2018, Gomez De Santos [28] performed molecular modeling of the enzyme–propranolol complex using PELE (Protein Energy Landscape Exploration), and the results suggested that the mutation G241D in JaWa facilitates the anchoring of the substrate in a more favorable orientation for its hydroxylation. The fact that atenolol and propranolol bear chemically similar structures explains the better results observed of JaWa in terms of the substrate transformation. Nevertheless, atenolol was less efficiently transformed, compared to propranolol, as higher enzyme amounts were required to achieve 68% conversion; this compound contains polar substituents that markedly decrease its lipophilicity compared to propranolol, probably affecting the substrate–enzyme interaction and catalytic efficiency.

For sulfamethoxazole [34], propranolol [28], naproxen [27], diclofenac [37], and ibuprofen [37, 38], the nature of some products has already been reported for reactions catalyzed by PaDa‐I, JaWa, or AaeUPO, the parental enzyme of PaDa‐I. For propranolol, diclofenac, and ibuprofen, the products were identified as hydroxylated compounds, which in the case of propranolol could be further oxidized to oligomeric compounds. The enzymatic oxidation of naproxen has been previously characterized using the PaDa‐I [27, 37] and JaWa variants [27], where O‐desmethylnaproxen was identified as product, with reported conversion efficiencies of 36% and 25%, respectively, at 1 mM substrate concentration. Similarly for propranolol, previous reports described the formation of 5‐hydroxy propranolol using substrate concentrations as high as 5 mM [28]. In our case, the reactions were carried out at concentrations approximately 50‐fold lower. Notably, the JaWa variant still exhibited measurable catalytic activity under these conditions.

For sulfamethoxazole, N‐oxidation was earlier reported as the sole transformation catalyzed by the enzyme [34]. In contrast, no previous reports on the products derived from sulfanilamide and sulfacetamide are available. For these two compounds, products were detected and identified as species bearing N‐oxidation (i.e., nitro‐compound formation) and also an additional oxidation, which could be a hydroxylation, likely to occur on the aromatic ring (Table 2). Molecular ions (M‐) with mass of 283.13, 201.20, and 243.00 for sulfamethoxazole, sulfanilamide, and sulfacetamide, respectively, were detected as products of the enzymatic reactions (Figures S13A‐B and S14A). For sulfamethoxazole and sulfanilamide additional, secondary products were detected (molecular ions (M)—with mass of 517.12 and 355.13, respectively, shown in Figure S15) that hint to the formation of dimers.

On the other hand, regarding trazodone, a species with a mass of 416.11, corresponding to the molecular ion (M‐), may correspond to the introduction of at least two hydroxyl groups in the aromatic rings, and a probable C‐oxidation (Figure S14B). Unfortunately, the lack of fragmentation of the molecular ion precluded further insight regarding the position of these chemical modifications, although based on the type of modification catalyzed by PaDa‐I on other aromatic compounds, hydroxylation on the aromatic rings could be expected.

In order to test the efficiency of the enzymatic treatment in the presence of several pharmaceutical compounds, a mixture of 100 μM of each sulfanilamide, trazodone, sulfamethoxazole, and diclofenac was treated with PaDa‐I and JaWa. Under these conditions, JaWa was able to catalyze the oxidation of only sulfanilamide, in the presence of 103 nM of enzyme, after 15 min; the other pharmaceutical compounds were not transformed even after successive additions of JaWa (up to a total of 309 nM) and H2O2 (up to a total of 6 mM). In the case of PaDa‐I, the four compounds were simultaneously oxidized as shown in Table 3 (Figure S16). Upon addition of a total of 166 nM, a conversion of 89% and higher was attained for sulfanilamide, trazodone, and diclofenac. Only 60% conversion of sulfamethoxazole was obtained under these conditions. Further additions of enzyme and H2O2 increased the conversion of sulfamethoxazole to 84%. One explanation may be that the reaction products present in the mixture, which may differ from the ones observed for the single‐compound reactions, are able to inhibit the enzyme to a certain degree, thus slowing down sulfamethoxazole conversion. However, this hypothesis needs further testing.

Aiming to contextualize these results, we assessed the ability of other redox enzymes to catalyze the oxidation of the same mixture of the four compounds. Unspecific enzymes, such as a fungal high‐redox potential laccase and 2 fungal peroxidases (baylase and CPO), were assayed under their standard reaction conditions and we observed that only diclofenac was oxidized by laccase (80% conversion, after 60 min of reaction and 183 nM of enzyme) and baylase (97% conversion, after 60 min of reaction and 319 nM of enzyme). No reaction could be detected for the other 3 compounds present in the reaction for laccase and baylase, and no reaction could be detected for any of the compounds when using CPO (after 60 min of reaction and in the presence of up to 292 nM of enzyme), thus underscoring the exceptional versatility and potential of UPOs for this kind of environmental applications.

Finally, the ability of PaDa‐I to catalyze the oxidation of the mixture of four compounds was assessed in grab samples of water, at room temperature. The water samples had pH values near 7. As shown in Table 3, conversion of the pharmaceutical compounds was similar to that observed in buffer. Overall, these results demonstrate that the enzyme may be robust enough to perform reasonably well in real water samples. In a complementary work, a similar approach using the parental AaeUPO demonstrated that the enzyme is also able to catalyze the degradation of μM range‐mixtures of pharmaceuticals in a model wastewater, with the following efficiencies: diclofenac>sulfamethoxazole>metoprolol>>ibuprofen. Moreover, it was demonstrated that the combination of more than one UPO can more effectively oxidize the mixture [37].

An imperative issue in this type of study is to measure the toxic properties of the reaction products. For example, the nitrosoarene compounds derived from the oxidation of the sulfonamides studied in this work may be highly reactive, and toxic, compounds [39, 40]. On the other hand, for other enzymatic systems, the formation of adducts or oligomeric species effectively reduces the toxicity of the compounds. Seeking to preliminarily evaluate the toxicity of the enzymatically generated products, we determined the survival rate of Artemia salina upon exposure to 1 μM‐mixture of the reaction products. No acute toxicity was observed after 48 h of exposure to the mixture of the reaction products, as a high survival rate (near 90%) was determined (Table S2). This preliminary toxicity assessment must be complemented with assays at higher concentration of the reaction products and with other biological models.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the ability of two UPO variants to catalyze the oxidation of a collection of pharmaceutical compounds, described as emerging pollutants, is reported in this work. The majority of the compounds were oxidized in the presence of one or two variants. The compounds that were not transformed by the enzymes display steric hindrances or other structural hurdles that may explain its lack of reactivity. The enzyme variants show subtle differences in terms of their catalytic efficiency to transform the compounds, which can be explained by the characteristics of the substrate access tunnel. Also, the oxidation of the compounds seems to occur with low efficiency and is outcompeted by secondary reactions such as enzyme inactivation and H2O2 depletion. Thus, in some cases, significant conversion of the substrates required multiple additions of the enzyme. However, the performance of UPOs for the oxidation of the selected pharmaceutical compounds surpasses that of other unspecific redox enzymes. Another significant finding is that the reaction products do not seem to constitute a toxicological threat. In this study, we focused on demonstrating that, at least at low concentrations in the μM range, the mixture of products did not show acute toxicity for A. salina. Evaluation of the properties of the enzymatic reaction products should be included in studies such as the one presented here, and ideally, with more than one biological model, as the negative effects of the EC are multiple, subtle, and long‐term. More detailed experiments are currently in progress in our lab, using other biological models and higher concentration of the products, to deepen our understanding on the effect of the enzymatic oxidation and to perform a more integral analysis on the effects of enzymatic oxidation of EC.

Overall, these results underline the potential of UPOs, in terms of its promiscuous catalytic activity, to find application in treatment of complex systems such as those found during elimination of pollutants in different water bodies and effluents. In the case of EC, its elimination is difficult not only due to the complexity of finding dozens of pollutants, but also due to its low concentration. The broad substrate specificity of the JaWa variant, not previously reported to oxidize most of the EC studied in this work, underlines the potential of creating more active and suitable enzymes for this type of environmental application. The results in this work also shed light on the limitations of UPOs, directing further research work in strategies such as H2O2‐dosifying, which is essential to improve operational stability and TTN [37], but also protein engineering to increase UPO activity at low substrate concentrations and also the enzyme's ability to recognize challenging EC, such as sterically hindered or geometrically rigid compounds.
