Trihydroxylated Anthocyanins Potentiate Effects of Docetaxel in Mesenchymal Triple Negative Breast Cancer Cells
Authors: Mafalda Ribeiro, Miguel A Faria, Martin Vojtek, Carmen Diniz, Isabel M P L V O Ferreira, Olga Viegas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTriple negative breast cancer (TNBC) presents a significant therapeutic challenge due to its poor prognosis and limited treatment options. While anthocyanins, a group of flavonoids, exhibit promising anticancer potential, their structure–activity relationship and adjuvant properties in TNBC remain underexplored. This study evaluated the anti‐proliferative effects of five structurally distinct anthocyani(di)ns—cyanidin‐3‐O‐glucoside (C3G), delphinidin‐3‐O‐glucoside (D3G), delphinidin‐3‐O‐rutinoside (D3R), malvidin‐3‐O‐glucoside (M3G), and luteolinidin (LT)—on mesenchymal TNBC cells. Combination assays of docetaxel (DT), a first‐line chemotherapeutic agent, with the most potent anthocyani(di)ns were conducted with TNBC and MCF‐12A non‐cancerous cells. Trihydroxylated anthocyanins (D3G and D3R), particularly those with a monosaccharide at C3, demonstrated the strongest anti‐proliferative effects, reducing MDA‐MB‐231 cell proliferation by 95% and 70%, respectively, at 400 μM (IC50: 253.28 and 341.33 μM) without affecting MCF‐12A cells. These were followed by 3‐deoxy (LT), dihydroxylated (C3G), and O‐methylated (M3G) anthocyani(di)ns. In Hs 578T mesenchymal TNBC cells, the trihydroxylated anthocyanins tested also induced strong anti‐proliferative effects. Synergistic effects (combination index < 0.9) were observed for binary (D3G + DT or D3R + DT) and ternary (D3G + D3R + DT) combinations, reducing DT concentrations by up to 37‐fold and anthocyanin concentrations by up to 21.9‐fold to achieve similar inhibition. The ternary combination was the most effective, requiring the lowest concentrations of DT and anthocyanins. These results underscore the critical role of the ortho‐trihydroxylated structure in the anti‐proliferative effects of anthocyani(di)ns, highlighting trihydroxylated anthocyanins as promising adjuvants in TNBC treatment, with the potential to lower DT dosage, minimize side effects, delay resistance, and reduce treatment costs while maintaining efficacy.Keywords: adjuvant therapy, anthocyanin, anti‐proliferative, docetaxel, structure–activity relationship, triple negative breast cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer‐related deaths among women worldwide, with over 600,000 deaths reported annually [1]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) projects that the global incidence of breast cancer will increase more than 45% by 2045, rising from 2.30 million new cases in 2022 to 3.36 million [2]. Breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease, classified into distinct subtypes based on the immunohistochemical expression of three receptors: estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER‐2) [3]. Among these subtypes, triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC)—characterized by the lack of expression of ER, PR, and HER‐2—accounts for 15%–20% of all breast cancer cases. TNBC is more prevalent in premenopausal women under 40, individuals of African American or Hispanic descent, and BRCA1 mutation carriers [4, 5, 6]. Compared to other subtypes, TNBC has a poorer prognosis due to higher rates of metastasis and recurrence, being unresponsive to multiple chemotherapeutic drugs [4]. Docetaxel (DT), a taxane‐derived anticancer agent, is one of the first‐line chemotherapy drugs used to treat TNBC [7]. Despite its effectiveness, DT is associated with significant side effects, including infusion reactions, febrile neutropenia, fatigue, and peripheral neuropathy, along with the development of acquired chemoresistance, which can disrupt treatment continuity and reduce effectiveness [8]. In recent years, there is growing interest in combining DT with other drugs or less toxic natural compounds, such as polyphenols, to enhance therapeutic efficacy while reducing adverse effects [9, 10].

Anthocyanins, a subclass of polyphenolic compounds belonging to the flavonoid family, are natural water‐soluble pigments responsible for the red‐orange to blue‐purple hues in various flowers, vegetables, and fruits, particularly berries [11, 12]. These compounds primarily exist as glycosylated forms of anthocyanidins (aglycones). To date, over 500 structurally distinct anthocyanins and approximately 23 anthocyanidins have been identified, with only six of the latter—cyanidin, delphinidin, malvidin, pelargonidin, peonidin, and petunidin—widely distributed across plants [13]. Chemically, anthocyanidins are hydroxylated and methoxylated derivatives of phenyl‐2‐benzopyrylium (flavylium cation, Figure 1) [11]. Based on their structural characteristics, anthocyanidins are categorized into three groups: 3‐hydroxy anthocyanidins (e.g., cyanidin, delphinidin, and pelargonidin), characterized by hydroxylation at the C‐3 position; O‐methyl anthocyanins (e.g., malvidin, peonidin, and petunidin), which feature methylation at hydroxyl groups; and 3‐deoxy anthocyanidins (e.g., luteolinidin and apigeninidin), which lack C‐3 hydroxylation [14]. Except for 3‐deoxy anthocyanidins, such as luteolinidin and apigeninidin found in sorghum, anthocyanidins are rarely found in nature due to their instability, primarily existing in glycosylated forms [15]. Glycosylation, along with potential sugar acylation, contributes to the extensive structural diversity of anthocyanins, which are differentiated by (i) the structure of the anthocyanidin core; (ii) the identity, number, and position of attached sugars; and (iii) the degree of sugar acylation and the specific acylating agents involved [11].

The structural characteristics of anthocyanins, including the number and position of hydroxyl and methoxyl groups, as well as the degree of glycosylation and acylation, significantly influence their biological properties, particularly their anticancer potential [16]. Previous studies on structure–activity relationships have demonstrated that the anti‐proliferative activity of anthocyanins is affected by the hydroxylation pattern of the aglycone moiety [17]. Anthocyanins with an ortho‐trihydroxylated B‐ring moiety (Figure 1), such as those based on delphinidin, exhibit greater anti‐proliferative effects against ER‐positive breast cancer cells (MCF‐7 cell line) compared to their dihydroxylated (cyanidin‐based) and methylated (malvidin‐based) counterparts [18]. In a previous study by our group, similar effects were observed in TNBC cells (MDA‐MB‐231 cell line), where both delphinidin and delphinidin‐3‐O‐glucoside exhibited greater anti‐proliferative activity than cyanidin and cyanidin‐3‐O‐glucoside [19]. However, the influence of other structural variations in anthocyanins, such as the type of attached sugar, methylation of hydroxyl groups, and hydroxylation at the C‐3 position, remains largely unexplored in the context of TNBC.

Based on these findings, this study aimed to investigate the anti‐proliferative effects of four anthocyanins—cyanidin‐3‐O‐glucoside (C3G), delphinidin‐3‐O‐glucoside (D3G), delphinidin‐3‐O‐rutinoside (D3R), and malvidin‐3‐O‐glucoside (M3G)—and one anthocyanidin, luteolinidin (LT), each with distinct structural features. Indeed, the selected anthocyanins represent three structural groups: C3G, D3G, and D3R from the 3‐hydroxy group, which vary in both the number of hydroxyl groups (two in C3G and three in D3G and D3R) and sugar moieties (glucoside in C3G and D3G; rutinoside in D3R); M3G from the O‐methylated group; and LT from the 3‐deoxy group. In addition, in a set of experiments, combination treatments of DT with the anthocyanins/anthocyanidin that evidenced the most promising anti‐proliferative effects were evaluated on MDA‐MB‐231, Hs 578T, and MCF‐12A cells to assess the potential use of these compounds as adjuvants in TNBC chemotherapy, particularly in the mesenchymal subtype, with the view to minimize side effects, and lower treatment costs. Additionally, in MDA‐MB‐231 cells the potential underlying mechanisms of action induced by the combination of anthocyanins with DT have also been explored.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Anthocyanins (C3G, D3G, D3R, M3G) and the anthocyanidin (LT), all with a purity ≥ 95%, were purchased from Extrasynthese (Lyon, France), and their chemical structures are presented in Figure 1. Dulbecco's modified Eagle's high glucose medium (DMEM‐HG), fetal bovine serum (FBS; EU Approved, South American), penicillin–streptomycin solution (10,000 Units/mL—10,000 μg/mL), and trypsin–EDTA (0.25%) were obtained from Gibco (Gibco/Life Technologies, Paisley, UK). Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium and Ham's F12 cell growth medium (DMEM/F12), horse serum, human epidermal growth factor (hEGF), cholera toxin from Vibrio cholerae , bovine insulin (10 mg/mL insulin in 25 mM HEPES, pH 8.2), hydrocortisone, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 3‐[4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl]‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), DT, paraformaldehyde, Hoechst 33258, bovine serum albumin (BSA), and Triton X‐100, were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich Corp. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Rabbit anti‐α‐tubulin primary antibody was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and goat anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 secondary antibody was obtained from Invitrogen (Thermofisher, MA, USA). AlphaScreen SureFire pERK1/2 Kit and AlphaScreen SureFire pNF‐κB Kit were purchased from PerkinElmer (I.L.C., Lisboa, Portugal).

Anthocyanin stock solutions (40 mM) were prepared in 10% DMSO and stored at −20°C. Docetaxel solution at 1 μM concentration was solubilized in DMSO and diluted in PBS. During the experiments, the concentration of DMSO in the growth medium never exceeded 0.1% (v/v), and this concentration was always included as a control.

Cell Cultures

The human Caucasian triple‐negative (ER−, PR−, HER2−), claudin‐low breast carcinoma cell lines MDA‐MB‐231 (ATCC HTB‐26) and Hs 578T (ATCC HTB‐126), commonly used as representative models of the mesenchymal TNBC subtype [20], as well as the non‐neoplastic breast cell line MCF‐12A (ATCC CRL‐10782) were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). MDA‐MB‐231 cells were cultured in DMEM‐HG supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin. Hs 578T cells were maintained in DMEM‐HG supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 0.01 mg/mL bovine insulin. MCF‐12A cells were grown in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL hEGF, 100 ng/mL cholera toxin, 0.01 mg/mL bovine insulin, 500 ng/mL hydrocortisone and 5% (v/v) horse serum. Both cell lines were maintained as monolayers under sterile conditions at 37°C with 5% CO2 and subcultured at 80% confluence. Under these conditions, MDA‐MB‐231, Hs 578T, and MCF‐12A cells exhibited population doubling times of 24.9 ± 1.4, 42.8 ± 2.5, and 19.9 ± 4.8 h, respectively. Routine screening for mycoplasma contamination was performed following the protocol described by van Kuppeveld et al. [21]. All the cell viability assays were conducted during the logarithmic phase of cell growth.

Individual and Combined Treatments

To evaluate the individual effects of anthocyanins and DT on cell proliferation, MDA‐MB‐231 cells were exposed to six different concentrations of each compound: 10, 50, 125, 250, 325, and 400 μM for anthocyanins/anthocyanidin, and 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, and 200 nM for DT. Hs 578T cells were exposed to six different concentrations of D3G and D3R: 10, 50, 125, 250, 325, and 400 μM, and 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, and 200 nM for DT. Anthocyanin stock solutions (40 mM) were first diluted 1:100 in the growth medium, filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon filter, and subsequently diluted to the desired concentrations (ranging from 400 to 10 μM) in growth medium. Similarly, working solutions of DT were also prepared in complete medium. The results of MDA‐MB‐231 cell exposure to anthocyanins and DT were used to fit a concentration–response curve and calculate the 10%, 50%, and 90% inhibitory concentrations (IC10, IC50, IC90) for anthocyanins and DT.

To assess the combined effects of DT with anthocyanins in binary (D3G + DR, D3R + DT) and ternary (D3G + D3R + DT) combinations, MDA‐MB‐231 cells were exposed to five to six concentration levels calculated based on the constant combination ratio design proposed by the Chou–Talalay method [22]. The combination ratios were calculated based on the equipotent IC50 values obtained from the individual exposure experiments, to ensure that each compound contributed equally to the combination. The final concentrations of D3G, D3R and DT in each combination ranged from 2 to 360 μM, 3 to 680 μM, and 0.09 to 21.4 nM, respectively, and are described in Table 1. The concentration ratios were maintained at 23:1 for D3G + DT, 32:1 for D3R + DT, and 23:32:1 for the D3G + D3R + DT combinations. The best combination observed in the above assays was also tested using another mesenchymal line, Hs 578T cells.

Cell Proliferation Assays

The individual and combined effects of DT and anthocyanins on cell proliferation were evaluated using two distinct methods: the MTT assay and label‐free kinetic monitoring. The MTT assay measures the reduction of yellow MTT to purple formazan by metabolically active cells. The amount of formazan produced correlates with the mitochondrial activity which is proportional to cell viability [23]. Label‐free kinetic monitoring, on the other hand, tracks live cell counts in real time.

The MTT assay was performed following the method adapted by Soares et al. [24]. Briefly, 200 μL of cell suspension (9 × 103 cells/mL) was seeded in 96‐well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37°C to attach. Afterward, the growth medium was replaced by 200 μL of complete medium containing DT and anthocyanins, either alone or in combination, and the cells were incubated for another 72 h. Following the incubation period, the cells were exposed to 100 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) for 2 h. The blue formazan crystals, produced by the mitochondrial reduction of MTT in viable cells, were dissolved in 100 μL of DMSO, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a SPECTROstar Nano Microplate Reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany). Cells treated with 0.1% (v/v) DMSO were used as vehicle controls. The anti‐proliferative effects of the tested compounds were expressed as a percentage of cell viability relative to the vehicle control.

Label‐free kinetic monitoring of cell proliferation was conducted using a LionheartFX automated microscope (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA), with direct image acquisition of cells in microplates at 0 and 72 h after the addition of DT and anthocyanins. The acquired 4× images were processed using the Gen 5 Image Analysis software (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA), which enables the identification and counting of individual cells per image. Immunocytochemistry

MDA‐MB‐231 cells were seeded in 384‐well black microplates (1 × 104 cells/mL) and allowed to attach for 24 h. Cells were then treated for 24 h with the IC50 values of DT, D3G, D3R, or the ternary combination (D3G + D3R + DT). After the incubation period, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde solution (pH 7.0) for 10 min at room temperature and washed twice with PBS (100 mM NaH2PO4, 50 mM NaCl). Prior to immunostaining, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100, 0.2 M glycine, 10% FBS, 1% BSA in PBS for 1 h. After two PBS washes, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with a rabbit anti‐α‐tubulin primary antibody (1:200 dilution). After washing cells with PBS, goat anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33258 solution (5 mg/mL) for 5 min in the dark. Cells were then washed twice with PBS for 5 min and cell micrographs were immediately captured with a 20× objective using a LionheartFX automated microscope (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Image analysis was performed using Gen5 Image Prime 3.02 (Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany). To quantify α‐tubulin per cell, automated threshold‐based masks corresponding to the microtubule fluorescence staining were generated. Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified per cell. Results were expressed as a percentage of α‐tubulin‐positive cells, defined as the proportion of cells with a fluorescence intensity exceeding the mean plus two standard deviations (SD).

ERK1/2 and NF‐κB Phosphorylation Assays

Changes in phosphorylated ERK1/2 (pERK1/2) and phosphorylated NF‐κB (pNF‐κB) levels were detected using AlphaScreen SureFire pERK1/2 and pNF‐κB kits, respectively. Briefly, MDA‐MB‐231 cells were seeded in 24‐well plates (2.8 × 104 cells/mL) and treated for 3 h with the IC50 values of D3G and D3R, the IC50 and IC90 values of the ternary combination (D3G + D3R + DT), or with 2.5 mM lidocaine, used as a positive control for inhibition of ERK1/2 and NF‐κB phosphorylation. After treatment, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were lysed in 50 μL of lysis buffer. Then, 15 μL of the cell lysates were transferred to 384‐well ProxiPlates (PerkinElmer‐I.L.C., Lisboa, Portugal), and a mixture of acceptor beads and donor beads was added according to the manufacturer's instructions. Data were expressed as a percentage of control.

Data and Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using two‐tailed Student's t‐test or one‐way ANOVA followed by Holm‐Sidak's multiple comparisons test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Among the anthocyanins/anthocyanidins tested, M3G (O‐methyl anthocyanin) had no effect on cell proliferation relative to the control group and, at concentrations above 125 μM, induced significantly higher proliferation than the non‐methylated anthocyanins/anthocyanidins (C3G, LT, D3G and D3R, p < 0.05). In contrast, the non‐methylated anthocyanins/anthocyanidins exhibited significant anti‐proliferative activity (Figure 2B). Both C3G (dihydroxylated anthocyanin) and LT (3‐deoxy anthocyanidin) induced a dose‐dependent reduction in cell proliferation, reaching a maximal inhibitory response at 125 μM, which remained unchanged at higher concentrations. However, neither C3G nor LT achieved 50% inhibition (resulting in reductions of 30% for C3G and 50% for LT, compared to the control). Consequently, IC50 values for M3G, C3G, and LT could not be determined.

In contrast, trihydroxylated anthocyanins (D3G and D3R) exhibited strong anti‐proliferative effects, inhibiting cell proliferation by 95% and 70%, respectively, at 400 μM. The inhibitory concentrations (IC10, IC50, and IC90), determined by MTT, for both anthocyanins (D3G and D3R) and DT are presented in Table 2. Notably, D3G showed a significantly greater anti‐proliferative effect than D3R at higher concentrations (400 μM, p < 0.01), as evidenced by its lower IC50 and IC90 values (253.28 μM vs. 341.33 μM and 579.07 μM vs. 1258.00 μM, respectively), indicating that MDA‐MB‐231 cells are more sensitive to D3G. Consequently, D3G and D3R were selected for further studies. DT demonstrated a concentration‐dependent suppression of cell proliferation within a concentration range of 0.01–200 nM (Figure 2A). At lower concentrations (0.01 and 0.1 nM), DT had no effect on cell proliferation (IC10: 0.48 nM). However, at higher concentrations (> 1 nM) it significantly inhibited cell proliferation, reaching its maximal effect at 10 nM with 80% inhibition compared to the control (IC50: 10.70 nM; IC90: 239.49 nM).

Although D3G and D3R induced a maximal inhibitory response similar to DT (95% and 70% vs. 80% respectively; p > 0.05), they required significantly higher concentrations to achieve comparable effects, indicating lower potency compared to DT (Table 2).

Combination of D3G and/or D3R With Docetaxel

Since the trihydroxylated anthocyanins, D3G and D3R, were able to suppress MDA‐MB‐231 cell proliferation without exhibiting cytotoxicity to non‐cancerous breast cells (MCF‐12A cells), combination assays were conducted with D3G and/or D3R and DT, with the aim of reducing the concentration of DT required to effectively induce cell death in this TNBC cell line.

The combined effects of D3G and/or D3R with DT on the proliferation of MDA‐MB‐231 cells are illustrated in Figure 4, with the corresponding IC10, IC50, and IC90 values presented in Table 3. All three combinations exhibited a concentration‐dependent anti‐proliferative effect, significantly reducing cell proliferation at higher concentration ratios. The D3R + DT combination did not demonstrate a significant anti‐proliferative effect at lower doses (C1 to C2; IC10: 47.08 μM + 1.47 nM) but showed increasing activity at higher concentrations (C3–C5), ultimately achieving 96% inhibition (IC90: 490.4 μM + 15.3 nM). In contrast, at lower concentrations (C2), the D3G + DT and D3G + D3R + DT combinations exhibited anti‐proliferative effects (IC10: 5.10 μM + 0.22 nM and 5.64 μM + 7.73 μM + 0.25 nM, respectively). At higher concentrations (C4–C6), the D3G + D3R + DT combination reached a maximum effect of 96% (IC90: 148.86 μM + 203.78 μM + 6.47 nM), comparable to that of D3R + DT, whereas the D3G + DT mixture attained only 69% inhibition relative to the control (IC90: 1453.77 μM + 62.23 nM). The IC50 values for DT were significantly lower in the D3G + D3R + DT combination compared to the binary mixtures (D3G + DT and D3R + DT), with the D3G + D3R + DT combination requiring the lowest concentration of DT (1.26 nM), followed by D3G + DT (3.69 nM) and D3R + DT (4.75 nM). Moreover, to achieve a sub‐maximal effect (90% inhibition of cell proliferation), the D3G + D3R + DT combination required less than half the concentration of DT and D3R, and one‐tenth the concentration of D3G compared to the binary mixtures.

Using a similar strategy, the combination of D3G + D3R + DT was also tested in Hs 578T cells. Data demonstrated that such a combination is also capable of reducing up to 7.6‐fold the concentration of DT needed to promote at least 50% of anti‐proliferative effects (Table S2).

Effect of the Ternary Combination D3G + D3R + DT on α‐Tubulin Levels

As DT is a known microtubule‐stabilizing agent and considering the synergistic anti‐proliferative effect exhibited by the combination of trihydroxylated anthocyanins with DT, we decided to evaluate the impact of the ternary combination (D3G + D3R + DT) on α‐tubulin levels in MDA‐MB‐231 cells. As expected, treatment with DT at its IC50 led to a significant increase in the percentage of α‐tubulin‐positive cells, confirming effective suppression of microtubule depolymerization (Figure 7). In contrast, no α‐tubulin‐positive cells were detected following treatment with D3G or D3R alone, and no significant differences were observed compared to the control (data not shown). When combined with DT, the anthocyanins also induced an increase in α‐tubulin‐positive cells relative to control; however, the percentage of α‐tubulin‐positive cells obtained in the presence of the ternary combination D3G + D3R + DT was lower than that obtained with DT alone. These findings suggest that although DT retains its microtubule‐stabilizing activity, the trihydroxylated anthocyanins are unable to affect α‐tubulin levels in MDA‐MB‐231 cells. Consequently, disruption of microtubule dynamics is unlikely to be the principal mechanism driving the enhanced anti‐proliferative response of the D3G + D3R + DT combination.

Modulation of ERK1/2 and NF‐κB Phosphorylation by the Ternary Combination D3G + D3R + DT

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the synergistic anti‐proliferative activity of D3G and D3R in combination with DT, the involvement of the MAPK/ERK and NF‐κB signaling pathways, key regulators of tumor cell survival and progression [26, 27, 28], was assessed using AlphaScreen assays.

As shown in Figure 8, significant inhibition (p < 0.01) of ERK1/2 and NF‐κB phosphorylation was observed following treatment with D3G and D3R at their respective IC50 concentrations. Anthocyanins were as effective as lidocaine, a positive control, in reducing NF‐κB phosphorylation while evidencing a more effective decrease in ERK1/2 phosphorylation. However, when the ternary combination was applied, no reduction in pERK1/2 or pNF‐κB levels was observed relative to the control. This can be explained by the reduction of concentration of trihydroxylated anthocyanins applied in the ternary combination. Discussion

In this study, we provide, for the first time, an evaluation of the structural features underlying the anti‐proliferative activity of distinct anthocyani(di)ns against mesenchymal TNBC cells. We compared trihydroxylated anthocyanins (D3G and D3R), a dihydroxylated anthocyanin (C3G), an O‐methyl anthocyanin (M3G), and a 3‐deoxy anthocyanidin (LT) to establish structure–activity relationships (SARs) and assess their potential as DT adjuvants. Our findings highlight the strong anti‐proliferative activity and selectivity of trihydroxylated anthocyanins, as well as their ability to synergize with DT, offering a promising strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy while reducing chemotherapy‐associated toxicity.

The SAR analysis revealed that hydroxylation, methoxylation, and glycosylation patterns critically modulate the cytotoxic activity of anthocyanins in mesenchymal TNBC cells. Trihydroxylated anthocyanins (D3G and D3R) consistently exhibited the strongest anti‐proliferative effects, achieving more than 70% inhibition of cell proliferation and surpassing the effects of dihydroxylated (C3G), 3‐deoxy (LT), and O‐methylated (M3G) analogs. The ortho‐trihydroxylated configuration on the B‐ring (C3′, C4′, C5′) appears to function as a pharmacophore for TNBC cytotoxicity, in line with previous reports in other cancer models, including ER‐positive breast cancer [18], glioblastoma [29], and leukemia [30].

Within trihydroxylated anthocyanins, glycosylation further influenced activity. D3G (monosaccharide form) exhibited greater potency, with a lower IC50 than D3R (disaccharide form; 243 μM vs. 341 μM, respectively), suggesting that the simpler glucose moiety may facilitate cellular uptake, solubility, or interaction with molecular targets [31, 32]. Conversely, D3R may offer enhanced stability and bioavailability due to reduced enzymatic deglycosylation [33, 34]. Together, these results emphasize that both hydroxylation pattern and sugar composition influence anthocyanin activity in TNBC.

By contrast, O‐methyl anthocyanins such as M3G exhibited limited activity against MDA‐MB‐231 TNBC cells. Methylation at C3′/C5′ likely reduces hydrogen‐bonding capacity and electron‐donating potential, thereby diminishing anti‐proliferative potency. This observation is consistent with previous reports showing reduced efficacy of peonidin‐3‐O‐glucoside compared with its non‐methylated counterpart, C3G, in Hs 578T TNBC cells [35]. Interestingly, O‐methyl anthocyanins retain activity in ER‐positive breast cancer cells [36], suggesting that their anti‐proliferative effects may be at least partly modulated by estrogen receptor‐mediated pathways.

3‐Deoxy anthocyanidins exhibited moderate activity, with LT showing higher cytotoxicity than the 3‐hydroxylated analog C3G. Their enhanced stability, attributed to the absence of the C3 hydroxyl group, has been associated with improved cytotoxicity in leukemia and hepatocellular carcinoma models [37]. However, the lack of glycosylation may limit their absorption, as anthocyanin uptake in the gastrointestinal tract is thought to involve glucose transporters [38, 39]. Thus, although structurally stable, the therapeutic potential of 3‐deoxy anthocyanidins may be constrained by lower bioavailability.

Trihydroxylated anthocyanins demonstrated promising selectivity toward MDA‐MB‐231 and Hs 578T TNBC cells. D3G showed no inhibitory effect on non‐cancerous MCF‐12A cells, while D3R exerted only slight inhibition, both significantly less cytotoxic than DT. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing D3G selectivity for TNBC over non‐cancerous MCF‐12A [19] and MCF‐10A cells [40]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the selective activity of D3R against mesenchymal TNBC cells. The selective cytotoxicity of these compounds underscores their potential advantage over conventional chemotherapy, which typically affects both cancerous and healthy tissues.

Binary (D3G + DT, D3R + DT) and ternary (D3G + D3R + DT) combinations inhibited TNBC cell proliferation synergistically, reducing the effective DT concentration required (Table 5). Notably, while the binary mixtures displayed context‐dependent antagonism, the ternary combination consistently produced synergy (Figure 6A), highlighting the complementary actions of D3G and D3R. These findings support previous studies showing that anthocyanin‐rich extracts enhance taxane activity [41] and align with the clinical success of taxane‐based combination regimens, which improve outcomes while reducing systemic toxicity [42, 43, 44].

Mechanistic analyses excluded microtubule perturbation, ERK, and NF‐κB as the primary mediators of the observed synergy, indicating the involvement of alternative pathways. Trihydroxylated anthocyanins may enhance DT efficacy by promoting oxidative stress through ROS accumulation [45] and mitochondrial dysfunction [46], reprogramming gene expression via epigenetic modulation [47, 48], enforcing cell‐cycle arrest through CDK regulation [41, 46], or disrupting cancer metabolism [49]. Collectively, these data suggest that trihydroxylated anthocyanins act as multi‐targeted modulators, complementing DT to selectively suppress TNBC cell growth.

The ability of D3G and D3R to synergize with DT while sparing non‐cancerous cells highlights their potential as adjuvant agents in TNBC therapy. Given the poor prognosis and limited targeted treatment options for TNBC, these naturally occurring compounds offer a promising complementary approach. The SAR insights presented in this study provide a molecular basis for optimizing anthocyanin structures to maximize therapeutic efficacy. Future studies should validate these findings in vivo, evaluating pharmacokinetics and bioavailability and testing combination strategies in preclinical TNBC models.

In summary, our results identify ortho‐trihydroxylated anthocyanins as the most potent and selective anthocyanins against mesenchymal TNBC cells. D3G and D3R not only suppressed proliferation selectively but also synergized with docetaxel, enabling substantial concentration reductions. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of structurally optimized anthocyanins as safe and effective adjuvants in TNBC chemotherapy.
