Self-powered artificial leaf using perovskite photocathode for solar hydrogen production and hydrazine degradation
Authors: Mahmoud G Ahmed, Anupam Sadhu, Joel M R Tan, Teddy Salim, Pritish Mishra, Patrick Wen Feng Li, Hao Zhe Chun, Stener Lie, Lydia H Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBias-free photoelectrochemical devices provide a sustainable route for solar hydrogen production from alkaline seawater, however, the requirement for large potential for anodic oxygen evolution and undesired chloride oxidation in seawater limit their efficiency. By leveraging the low oxidation potential of hydrazine, a toxic pollutant, bias-free devices can achieve high-performance hydrogen production and simultaneous degradation of hydrazine, effectively avoiding chloride oxidation. Here, we design a self-powered artificial leaf device, comprising a perovskite photocathode integrated with a noble-metal-free oxide catalyst for direct solar hydrogen production and hydrazine oxidation. The device exhibits a high photocurrent density of 25 mA cm–2 and stability for 3 days under 1-sun illumination. Upscaling the artificial leaf device enables near-complete hydrazine degradation to below 1 ppb within ≈ 30 h under zero-bias operation. This study provides a scalable and sustainable approach for simultaneous hydrogen generation and pollutant removal, advancing the use of solar energy in environmental applications.Subject terms: Devices for energy harvesting, Batteries

Introduction

Bias-free, unassisted photoelectrochemical (PEC) water splitting device, mimicking artificial leaf, for producing green hydrogen (H2) is the cornerstone technology for achieving long-term, clean, and sustainable fuel production on Earth1–3. Compared to conventional water electrolysis powered by photovoltaic cells, such bias-free PEC devices directly capture solar energy without requiring additional micro-grids or AC-DC converters, thereby minimizing the complexity and cost of H2 production4. However, the reliance on freshwater as the primary electrolyte in PEC devices limits their scalability, underscoring the need to explore seawater, comprising over 95% of Earth’s water, as a more abundant alternative. The use of seawater in bias-free PEC devices, however, presents significant challenges, including the large voltage required to drive the anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and the competition with chloride oxidation reaction (ClOR, E0 = 1.72 V)5,6. ClOR, driven by chloride anions (∼ 0.5 M in seawater), reduces energy efficiency and generates hazardous chlorine species (e.g., Cl2, ClO–), which pose environmental risks and corrode the photo(anode)7. Overcoming these challenges is essential for advancing bias-free solar seawater splitting technologies, enhancing their efficiency, and ensuring long-term sustainability4.

To address these challenges, replacing OER with faster electro-oxidation reactions that require lower potential offers a promising solution8. Hydrazine oxidation reaction (HzOR; E0 = − 0.33 V versus RHE) is particularly attractive due to its low oxidation potential, rapid kinetics and its ability to entirely avoid the harmful chlorine chemistry. Hydrazine is extensively used as a deoxidant in power plant feedwater, high-energy fuels such as aircraft fuel, and raw materials across several industries9–11. Despite its widespread use, hydrazine is highly toxic, with carcinogenic and mutagenic effects, posing serious health and environmental risks through wastewater contamination, accidental spills, and improper disposal12,13. Consequently, the U.S. EPA has classified it as a potential carcinogen with a strict limit of 10 ppb in drinking water, emphasizing the urgent need for effective degradation technologies to meet the regulatory standards14,15. Therefore, integrating anodic HzOR into PEC devices presents an attractive strategy for bias-free operation, simultaneously generating solar H2 and removing toxic hydrazine from seawater, eliminating the need for external voltage, oxidants, or complex separation processes. Despite its potential, this integrated approach remains largely unexplored.

Hydrazine (N2H4) degradation in HzOR can proceed through multiple pathways. For example, N2H4 can decompose completely into harmless N2 (N2H4 + 4OH− → N2 + 4H2O + 4e–) via Faradaic electrochemical pathway. Additionally, it can undergo non-Faradaic decomposition, valorizing hydrazine by generating either H2 (N2H4 → N2 + 2H2) or NH3 (3N2H4 → N2 + 4NH3)9. However, the latter, despite involving catalytic decomposition without electron transfer, is unexpectedly potential-dependent, a phenomenon known as the electrochemical promotion effect of a chemical catalytic reaction (EPOC)9. Controlling these pathways demands electrocatalysts that reconcile kinetic constraints with stability under alkaline operating conditions. While noble metals (e.g., Pt, Pd) are commonly used as HzOR catalysts due to their low overpotentials, transition metal oxides such as single and binary systems based on Fe, Co, Ni offer distinct advantages, such as tunable electronic structures, corrosion resistance, and cost-effectiveness16–20. A major challenge, however, lies in the large onset potential for HzOR. To address this, activating the EPOC pathway alongside the electrochemical reaction is a promising strategy21. The synergy between the Faradaic (electrochemical) and non-Faradaic (EPOC-induced catalytic) processes reduces the activation barrier of surface reaction, effectively lowering the onset potential22. EPOC can be triggered in metal oxides with high reducibility (e.g., Cr-based oxides), making them attractive candidates for lowering the onset potential23. By combining the high electrochemical activity of Fe-Co oxide-based catalysts with the reducibility of Cr, Fe-Co-Cr oxides are promising candidates to reduce the onset potential for hydrazine oxidation. The reduction in onset potential of HzOR through EPOC is crucial for enhancing the performance of bias-free solar H2 production in PEC devices. Furthermore, activating the EPOC pathway enables H2 generation at the anode side, adding value to the overall efficiency of PEC devices.

To further enhance the efficiency of the PEC devices, identifying an efficient absorber materials for the photocathode which is essential for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), is equally critical24. Recently, lead halide perovskites have garnered significant attention due to their tunable bandgaps, strong light absorption, and high charge transport properties, highlighting their substantial potential either as photocathode (p-i-n architecture) or photoanode (n-i-p architecture) in PEC devices25–27. A key challenge for perovskite photocathodes is their susceptibility to water-induced degradation. Therefore, stabilizing the perovskite layer is crucial for effectively leveraging its potential in PEC devices. Encapsulation strategies play critical role in enhancing perovskite stability, ensuring long-term performance under operational conditions28,29. Importantly, such strategies not only improve operational stability in aqueous PEC environments but can also isolate lead-containing perovskites from direct human or environmental contact, enabling their safer integration into self-powered wearables, soft robotic systems, and sensor platforms30–32. The integration of stabilized perovskite photocathodes with specific anodic reactions, such as biomass reforming, is reported to be efficient in driving bias-free H2 production33. However, the broader application of perovskite-based photocathodes for bias-free solar H2 production remains largely underexplored, particularly in the context of hydrazine oxidation and degradation, as a means to address environmental pollutants.

Herein, we report a self-powered artificial leaf that couples a stable Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite photocathode with a noble-metal-free Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 spinel oxide anode to simultaneously drive hydrogen production and hydrazine degradation. This configuration exploits the photogenerated current density of the perovskite photocathode and the EPOC effect in the Cr-containing spinel oxide, enabling two distinct hydrogen evolution pathways, at both the photocathode and the anode. Our device exhibits a high photocurrent density of 25 mA cm–2, stable operation over 72 h and efficient hydrazine removal to below 1 ppb. The device shows performance competitive with previously bias-free configuration, eliminating the need for precious-metal OER catalysts while addressing environmental remediation, and representing a significant advance toward sustainable solar-to-fuels technologies.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and catalytic activity of metal oxides for hydrazine oxidation

To develop an efficient system for bias-free solar hydrogen production coupled with hydrazine oxidation, we first investigated the anodic hydrazine oxidation reaction (HzOR) on a series of metal oxides, including Fe-Co-Cr compositions, synthesized via ultrasonic spray pyrolysis on fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrates. More details on the synthesis are provided in the Methods section. The catalytic performance of the metal oxides toward HzOR was systematically evaluated and summarized in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1. As seen from Fig. 1a, a ternary Fe-Co-Cr oxide with a stoichiometric ratio of 10:30:60 exhibits the highest HzOR activity, reaching a current density of 56.5 mA cm–2 at 0.4 V vs. RHE, compared to other investigated single, binary, and ternary oxides. This reveals that Fe, Co and Cr play a key role in achieving high HzOR activity. The crystal phase of the ternary oxide, represented in Fig. 1b, matches well with the standard spinel structure (CoCr2O4, JCPDS no. 022-1084)34. The ternary spinel oxide exhibits an interconnected nanoparticle morphology as observed in Fig. 1c, d via scanning and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (SEM and HR-TEM, respectively). Figure 1e–g present HR-TEM images along with selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns, highlighting the highly crystalline nature of the spinel oxide. ICP and HR-TEM coupled with EDS element mapping (Fig. 1h, Supplementary Table 2) reveal a uniform distribution of Fe, Co, and Cr, consistent with the target atomic ratio of 10:30:60. Through XRD and ICP results, the chemical formula of the ternary oxide is confirmed as Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O434. Additionally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy reveals the co-existence of Cr3+ and Cr6+ with a high content of Co2+ and Fe3+ as well as the presence of oxygen vacancies in the ternary oxide (Supplementary Fig. 1-4). The presence of redox-active Co2+ ions is particularly significant, as they can buffer the multi-electron processes required for HzOR35. These Co2+ ions help stabilize the intermediate species formed during the reaction, facilitating efficient electron transfer and promoting a more effective oxidation process.

To clarify the role of Fe incorporation, comparative studies were conducted between Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 and Fe-free Co1.2Cr1.8O4. XRD patterns confirmed identical crystal structures (Supplementary Fig. 5a). However, synchrotron Co L-edge spectra (Supplementary Fig. 5b) revealed a stronger Co L3-edge intensity in Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4, indicating an increased Co2+ population and enhanced Co redox tunability upon Fe3+ incorporation. This electronic modulation directly results in enhanced electrochemical performance, as evidenced by J-V curves (Supplementary Fig. 6), where Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 exhibits a lower onset potential and higher current density for HzOR than Co1.2Cr1.8O4. These results demonstrate that Fe3+ acts as an electronic regulator, stabilizing Co2+ states, promoting faster electron transfer, and boosting catalytic activity.

Moreover, the Cr6+ species, with its d0 electronic configuration and high oxidative characteristics, indicates the high reducibility of the oxide36,37. This property promotes the generation of oxygen vacancies, which serve as active sites, facilitating intermediate adsorption and promoting higher catalytic activity for HzOR38.

In addition to XPS analysis, synchrotron-based soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy (sXAS) provides deeper insight into the electronic and coordination environments of the ternary oxide34. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7a, the Cr L-edge spectrum closely resembles that of Cr2O3 (Cr3+Oh), with a minor contribution near 580 eV corresponding to Cr6+Td from K2CrO4. Quantitative analysis indicates that Cr3+Oh accounts for 90.4%, while Cr6+Td contributes 9.6%. Similarly, the Co L-edge features (Supplementary Fig. 7b) align strongly with CoO (Co2+Td), with only a weak shoulder characteristic of Co3+Oh in EuCoO3, corresponding to 97.3% Co2+Td and 2.7% Co3+Oh. The Fe L-edge spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 8) confirms that Fe exists as Fe3+Oh (99%) with negligible Fe2+ contribution. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Fe3+ and Cr3+ preferentially occupy octahedral sites, while Co2+ resides mainly in tetrahedral coordination, consistent with a normal spinel configuration. Fe incorporation stabilizes a higher Co2+ population and preserves the spinel framework, thereby optimizing redox adaptability and catalytic performance. The combined XPS and sXAS analysis thus provides a comprehensive understanding of the oxidation states, redox flexibility, and coordination geometry that underpin the catalytic activity of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 for HzOR.

The high HzOR activity of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 on FTO substrates inspired us to evaluate its performance on nickel foam (NF), marking a step toward the development of an artificial leaf device, as will be discussed later. The spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF is tested for HzOR in 1.0 M KOH solution containing various concentrations of hydrazine (0.1–0.6 M) as shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 9. In the absence of hydrazine, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curve shows no discernible anodic current within the applied potential window (Supplementary Fig. 9). However, upon introducing hydrazine, the HzOR activity of the spinel oxide catalyst increases rapidly with rising hydrazine concentration, following a volcano-like trend and reaching a maximum anodic current density at 0.5 M (Fig. 2a). The subsequent decrease in performance beyond this optimal concentration is attributed to mass transport limitations, where the rapid consumption of hydrazine and the accumulation of N2 bubbles at the electrode surface begin to hinder reactant access to the active sites. Nevertheless, the peak current density achieved at 0.5 M underscores the highest catalytic performance of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF towards hydrazine oxidation.

In 1.0 M KOH containing 0.5 M hydrazine, spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF exhibits a low onset potential of – 70 mV and requires only 5 and 75 mV to achieve current densities of 100 and 600 mA cm–2, respectively (Fig. 2b). In contrast, much larger potentials (1.47–1.49 V) are required to achieve the same current densities for the sluggish OER under identical conditions in 1.0 M KOH (Supplementary Fig. 10). Moreover, the HzOR activity remains unaffected not only in the presence of 0.5 M chloride ions but also in real seawater (Fig. 2c), particularly at high current densities, operating far from the ClOR region (1.72 V). This demonstrates that hydrazine effectively reduces the large overpotentials required for anodic reactions, while also mitigating chloride crossover and corrosion, enabling stable, long-term seawater splitting39. Such a substantial decrease in the anode potential would undoubtedly contribute to a more efficient bias-free solar hydrogen production from alkaline seawater.

The HzOR activity of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF is further compared with benchmark catalysts, Pt and IrO2. The iR-corrected J-V curves of catalysts, measured in a 1.0 M KOH solution containing 0.5 M hydrazine, are shown in Fig. 2d. The onset potential (Eon), a critical parameter for evaluating catalytic performance, is –70 mV vs. RHE for Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF (Fig. 2d), as previously mentioned. This value is 80, 93, and 108 mV lower than those of Pt, IrO2, and Ni foam, respectively. Furthermore, our spinel oxide catalyst exhibits a small Tafel slope of 11.9 mV dec–1 (Fig. 2e), which is significantly lower than those of Pt/NF and IrO2/NF, highlighting its fast catalytic kinetics for HzOR. In addition, Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF requires significantly lower potentials of –33 and +5 mV to reach current densities of 10 and 100 mA cm−2, respectively, compared to Pt/NF, IrO2/NF, and NF (Fig. 2f). LSV measurements were also performed at varying scan rates to further investigate the HzOR kinetics for Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the LSV curves exhibit negligible changes in current density across scan rates between 10 and 50 mV s–1, emphasizing the effective charge and mass transport at the electrode–electrolyte–gas three-phase interface of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF, confirming its fast HzOR kinetics17,40. To further elucidate the interfacial properties of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4, the water contact angle measurements were performed on Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4, Pt, and the bare FTO substrate (Supplementary Fig. 12). The contact angle of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 ( ~ 9.07°) was significantly lower than that of Pt (72.98°) and FTO (105.46°), confirming its high wettability. This high hydrophilicity of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 promotes intimate electrode-electrolyte contact and facilitates efficient bubble detachment during catalysis, thereby contributing to enhanced catalytic performance. Compared to various reported catalysts for HzOR, Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF demonstrates one of the lowest Tafel slopes (11.9 mV dec−1), and requires a small potential (−33 mV) to reach 10 mA cm−2 (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Table 3), making it a promising candidate for hydrazine oxidation.

Mechanistic origin of HzOR activity in spinel oxide

To elucidate the origin of the high HzOR activity exhibited by spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF, we conducted an in-depth investigation combining electrochemical and surface electronic and chemical structure analysis. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed in the potential range of –0.3 to 0.8 V vs. RHE to evaluate the catalytic behavior in the absence and presence of N2H4. In the absence of N2H4, no redox peaks were observed (Fig. 3a). Upon the addition of N2H4, however, a pronounced oxidation peak appears, corresponding to hydrazine oxidation9,17. Notably, the area of this peak diminished significantly with increasing cycling number due to the progressive consumption of N2H4, indicating the high catalytic activity of spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 in driving electrochemical hydrazine degradation into corresponding gaseous products such as N2(g) and H2(g).

To further identify the gaseous products at the anode side, chronoamperometry was performed at a fixed potential of 0.2 V vs. RHE and the product was analyzed using gas chromatography (GC). As shown in Figure 3b, N2 was identified as the primary product of electrochemical HzOR (N2H4 + 4OH– → N2 + 4H2O + 4e–), with H2 detected as an additional byproduct. The detection of H2 gas can be attributed to the non-Faradaic chemical decomposition of N2H4. To further investigate this phenomenon, we analyzed the gas product at the open circuit potential (OCP), in the absence of Faradaic current flow. While GC results (Supplementary Fig. 13) confirmed the generation of H2 as a result of the chemical decomposition of N2H4 (N2H4 → H2 + N2), the amount of H2 evolved at OCP is negligible compared to that produced at 0.2 V vs. RHE (Supplementary Fig. 13).

The potential-dependent increase in H2 evolution at 0.2 V (Fig. 3c) suggests an enhanced chemical decomposition under applied potential. This observation supports the hypothesis introduced earlier, where the application of potential triggers the electrochemical promotion of chemical catalysis (EPOC), thus enhancing the chemical decomposition. The amount of evolved N2 (Supplementary Fig. 14a) and the corresponding H2:N2 ratio (Supplementary Fig. 14b) were also determined. The ratio was approximately 15%, confirming that while the Faradaic electrooxidation pathway remains dominant, a significant EPOC effect operates concurrently. These results demonstrate that the spinel oxide, Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4, facilitates HzOR via a bifunctional pathway, where the applied potential simultaneously triggers chemical decomposition of N2H4 (through EPOC) and electrochemical oxidation9.

To elucidate the origin of the EPOC effect, we hypothesize that it is triggered by the reducibility of spinel oxide, particularly due to the presence of Cr6+, which exhibits high oxidative properties. The hydrazine-induced reduction leads to changes in the electronic environment, facilitating the formation of oxygen vacancies and enhancing catalytic activity. To examine this hypothesis, we performed XPS after the complete hydrazine oxidation (see Supplementary Fig. 15) to examine the surface structure and the formation of oxygen vacancies. Interestingly, Cr 2p shows pronounced changes (Fig. 3d), with the disappearance of Cr6+ peak at 579 eV and the shift of the Cr 2p3/2 main peak to lower binding energy, indicative of a hydrazine-induced reduction of Cr species. In contrast to the pristine catalyst, the Co 2p spectrum (Fig. 3e) shows a partial oxidation of Co2+ to Co3+ after HzOR, while the Fe 2p spectrum exhibits insignificant changes35. These cationic redox transitions, reduction of Cr species and oxidation of Co, suggest dynamic surface reconstruction under reaction conditions, with surface-active Co3+ sites and reduced Cr species contributing to enhanced catalytic performance.

To further confirm the reducibility of the spinel oxide, XPS analysis was performed (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 16) and reveals a significant increase in the O 1 s peak at 531.3 eV, corresponding to an increase in chemisorbed oxygen at oxygen vacancy sites. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were further carried out to confirm and characterize the oxygen vacancies. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, a pronounced resonance signal centered at g ≈ 2.001 is observed, characteristic of unpaired electrons localized at oxygen-vacancy sites. The intensity of this signal markedly increases after the HzOR operation, verifying the generation and accumulation of oxygen vacancies during catalysis. This result aligns with previous studies on reducible oxides, where the generation of oxygen vacancies is closely associated with improved catalytic activity38. Such changes in the electronic structure of cations, along with the formation of oxygen vacancies, can tune the adsorption energy of surface intermediates, lowering the energy barrier for hydrazine oxidation. These changes can explain the activation of EPOC and its role in the observed low onset potential for HzOR9. Cross-section HR-TEM analysis further revealed that the oxide retained its structural stability and crystallinity after hydrazine oxidation, with a uniform distribution of elements across the material. The crystallinity was also confirmed by XRD (Supplementary Fig. 18). To further emphasize the importance of Cr in the catalytic process, we investigated the gas evolution on a Co-Fe oxide system in the absence of Cr species. In this case, negligible amount of H2 was observed at the anode side (Supplementary Fig. 19), confirming the indispensable role of Cr in activating the EPOC and driving the H2 evolution at the anode side. These results, together with the observed oxygen vacancy formation and surface redox properties, decisively establish the essential contribution of Cr-based spinel oxide to achieving high catalytic performance for HzOR, which is highly beneficial for bias-free devices.

Perovskite-based photocathode for artificial leaf device

The relatively low potential for HzOR demonstrated by spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF motivated us to design an artificial leaf device for simultaneous H2 production and hydrazine degradation using a state-of-the-art triple-cation perovskite, Cs0.05(FA0.83MA0.17)0.95Pb(Br0.17I0.83)3, with a bandgap of 1.6 eV (Supplementary Fig. 20)41,42. The perovskite layer absorbs sunlight, generating charges that are selectively transported to the corresponding electrodes. To enable selective hole transfer from perovskite to the FTO layer, the perovskite layer was deposited onto a p-type NiO hole transport layer, while a PCBM/BCP electron extraction layer was subsequently deposited on top of the perovskite to extract the electrons, forming a p-i-n type device architecture for photocathode applications. For electron collection, Ag layer was deposited on top of the BCP layer by thermal evaporation. Figure 4a shows the detailed architecture of the p-i-n perovskite-based photocathode. The perovskite device exhibited immediate and rapid degradation when exposed to the electrolyte solution for PEC measurements due to the susceptibility of perovskite to the aggressive chemical environment (Supplementary Fig. 21). To address this issue and to allow the perovskite photocathode to function in an aqueous solution, we developed an encapsulant using conductive Ag-Cu epoxy and Ti foil to prevent electrolyte permeation. Ag-Cu nanoparticles serve as an ohmic contact mediator by creating electrically conductive channels between the underlying Ag layer and the Ti foil. Finally, to complete the perovskite-based photocathode configuration (Fig. 4a), a Pt co-catalyst was sputtered onto the Ti foil to enhance the HER kinetics.

To evaluate the PEC performance of perovskite photocathode, we conducted LSV measurements under AM 1.5 G simulated solar illumination (100 mW cm–2) in 1.0 M KOH solution using a three-electrode PEC configuration. Two configurations of perovskite photocathode are fabricated: 1) Ti foil covering Ag-Cu epoxy-coated perovskite device (Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite), and 2) Pt HER-catalyst coated Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite (Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite). The J-V curves revealed an onset potential of 0.3 V vs. RHE for Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite (Fig. 4b), while Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite exhibited an anodic shift of 900 mV in onset potential, indicating a significantly lower required bias (as observed from the plot of the photocurrent derivative potential vs. potential curve, Supplementary Fig. 22). Furthermore, the Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite achieved a much higher saturated photocurrent density of –25 mA cm−2 at 0 V vs. RHE. Light-chopped PEC measurements (Supplementary Fig. 23) confirmed that no current was observed in the dark, verifying that the photocurrent density of –25 mA cm−2 was solely generated under light illumination. Importantly, PEC performance in alkaline saline water containing 0.5 M NaCl showed no change in photocurrent (Fig. 4c), demonstrating the feasibility of the device as a step toward artificial leaf applications in such conditions. In real seawater samples under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 24), the device also exhibited similarly stable photocurrent, confirming its robustness and applicability in practical seawater environments. Additionally, while the Pt/Ag-Cu/perovskite photocathode device (without Ti) lost its performance completely after 30 min (Supplementary Fig. 25), the Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite device (with Ti) maintained stable performance over 10 h and 72 h (Supplementary Fig. 26 and 27), emphasizing the critical role of both Ag-Cu epoxy and Ti foil in enhancing the stability of the perovskite photocathode.

Finally, to design a bias-free device, we combine the Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite photocathode with spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF anode for solar H2 production coupled with hydrazine oxidation. Figure 4d shows LSV curves of the Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite photocathode overlapped with hydrazine oxidation. As shown in Fig. 4d, the theoretical maximum photocurrent density of 25 mA cm–2 at –0.007 V is derived from the intersections of two LSV curves: one corresponding to the photocathode under light irradiation (for PEC-HER) and the other to the anode under dark (for HzOR, 0.5 M N2H4). Supplementary Fig. 28 further demonstrates the overlap of the Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite photocathode with HzOR at various concentrations of hydrazine, with the operational photocurrent density consistent at ≈ 25 mA cm–2. In contrast, the combination of the Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite photocathode with traditional OER (i.e., no hydrazine) exhibits no overlap, indicating an anticipated zero photocurrent in this configuration (Supplementary Fig. 29).

Thereafter, we measured the J-V curves in a two-compartment, two-electrode PEC configuration, both in the presence and absence of hydrazine. Under simulated 1-sun illumination, no photocurrent was detected in the absence of hydrazine (i.e., PEC-HER combined with OER, Fig. 4e), whereas a high photocurrent density of 25 mA cm–2 was achieved in the presence of hydrazine (Fig. 4e). Notably, this photocurrent aligns well with the anticipated value based on the overlap of individual J-V curves obtained from three-electrode measurements of perovskite photocathode under illumination and spinel oxide in dark (Fig. 4d). Importantly, comparable photocurrent densities (~ 25 mA cm–2) were also observed under bias-free operation in both alkaline saline (0.5 M NaCl) and real seawater electrolytes (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 30), confirming the robustness and practical seawater compatibility of the integrated PEC device. These results highlight the crucial role of hydrazine in driving efficient, bias-free solar hydrogen production while selectively suppressing chloride oxidation, thereby ensuring stable operation in saline and seawater environments. Light-chopped PEC measurements (Supplementary Fig. 31) further demonstrated that no current was generated in the dark, even in the presence of hydrazine, confirming the indispensable role of the perovskite absorber and light in driving the process. Additionally, at bias-free conditions (0.0 V), the H2 gas is collected from the photocathode side and determined by GC instrument. The hydrogen production rate at bias-free conditions was ≈ 466 μmol h–1 cm–2 with a near-unity Faradaic efficiency (Fig. 4f). The gas is also collected at the anode side, and the main product was N2 gas, accompanied with additional H2 (≈ 30 μmol h–1, Supplementary Fig. 32), consistent with the previous observations and discussions on electrochemical HzOR (Fig. 3b). Gas bubbles were clearly visible at both anode and cathode during the operation at zero-bias voltage (Supplementary videos 1 and 2). Moreover, a small artificial-leaf device was designed, demonstrating the same photocurrent density of 25 mA cm–2 at 0 V (Supplementary Fig. 33). This artificial leaf device showed no gas evolution in the dark, but H2 and N2 gas bubbles were observed under illumination (Supplementary video 3, Supplementary Fig. 34).

Moreover, our device maintained stable operation under continuous 1-sun illumination for over 72 h, without significant performance degradation (Fig. 4g). The slight decrease in photocurrent response can be attributed to the gradual consumption of hydrazine concentration and/or the accumulation of bubbles on the surface of photocathode. We believe that this exceptional stability arises from the multi-component encapsulation (Ag-Cu epoxy and Ti foil), which effectively protects the perovskite photocathode from the aggressive alkaline environment. Notably, this level of operational stability is among the most stable reported for bias-free solar hydrogen production using a perovskite photocathode (Supplementary Table 4). Additionally, the device also achieved a photocurrent density of 25 mA cm–2, among the highest reported for Pb-based perovskite photocathodes and competitive with Pb-Sn perovskite photocathodes for bias-free solar hydrogen production using a single photoelectrode43 .This achievement is consistent across various device types, including PEC and photovoltaic-assisted PEC | |electrochemical systems for water splitting, highlighting the significance of this study in advancing perovskite-based photoelectrochemical systems for sustainable hydrogen production.

Demonstration of artificial leaf device for hydrazine degradation

Despite the high efficiency of the small-scale perovskite photocathode (0.56 cm2) for bias-free solar H2 production coupled with hydrazine oxidation, practical implementation remains challenging due to the need for effective hydrazine degradation13. Therefore, a large-scale perovskite-based photocathode (6.25 cm2) was fabricated (Supplementary Fig. 35) using the same method and evaluated first in a three-electrode PEC configuration (Fig. 5a). Perovskite photocathode achieved absolute photocurrent of – 40 mA at 0 V vs. RHE. The large-area perovskite photocathode is integrated with spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4/NF anode (area: 1 cm2) for hydrazine oxidation (Fig. 5b). The device demonstrates a similar absolute photocurrent of 40 mA at zero-bias voltage under simulated 1-sun illumination. We also prepared spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 on large area nickel foam (area: 9.2 cm2) for artificial leaf device, featuring a window in the middle of Ni foam, that allows light to pass through and be absorbed by perovskite-based photocathode on the other side (Fig. 5c,d and Supplementary Fig. 35-38). The artificial leaf device exhibited the same absolute photocurrent value of 40 mA at zero-bias voltage (Supplementary Fig. 36), indicating that the device is not limited by the anode area. We therefore employed this artificial leaf device to monitor the hydrazine degradation over time under bias-free conditions. Using spectrophotometric analysis, we observed that our artificial leaf device effectively degraded hydrazine within ≈ 30 hours (Fig. 5e-g and Supplementary Fig. 39). The residual hydrazine concentration in water was reduced to 0.5 ppb (<1 ppb), significantly below the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s permissible limit of 10 ppb. These findings indicate that our device offers a significant advantage over traditional hydrazine removal processes, by efficiently degrading hydrazine, without the need for additional oxidation processes or complex separation methods44. As a proof of concept, outdoor demonstration test was also performed on such artificial leaf (Supplementary Fig. 40) under natural sunlight (Cloudy conditions, 12:00 pm, 23rd November 2024, Singapore). During this test, we observed a significant evolution of N2 and H2 bubbles, indicating the efficient operation of our self-powered artificial leaf device for solar hydrogen production and hydrazine degradation (Supplementary videos 4 and 5).

Our research advances sustainability through artificial photosynthesis by designing self-powered artificial leaf device for simultaneous hydrogen production and pollutant remediation without relying on external power sources or grids. This approach offers a scalable, low-cost platform for decentralized solar fuel production, while its adaptability to degrade a range of contaminants broadens its environmental impact. However, challenges remain for widespread adoption of solar fuel systems, including the development of floating devices and land-based technologies capable of handling the complex composition of real sewage wastewater and seawater, including varying pH values27. While these systems offer advantages in material cost and scalability, further advancements are needed to enhance stability and optimize gas collection, and explore integration with other water treatment technologies to improve contaminant removal and system efficiency.

In summary, we have developed a self-powered artificial leaf device that simultaneously produces green hydrogen and degrades toxic hydrazine, utilizing a Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite photocathode integrated with a precious-metal-free spinel oxide, Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4. The spinel oxide operates via a bifunctional HzOR pathway, enabling operation at low potential, which is beneficial for bias-free devices in alkaline seawater and avoids deleterious chlorine chemistry. The perovskite photocathode, encapsulated with conductive Ag-Cu epoxy, exhibits high performance and stability for PEC-HER. The integration of spinel Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 oxide with the perovskite photocathode in the artificial leaf device demonstrated stable and efficient PEC-HER and HzOR activity. Using this approach, we achieved a photocurrent density of 25 mA cm–2, along with stable operation over 3 days, high H2 production rate of ≈ 466 mol cm–2 h–1 and efficient hydrazine degradation to levels below 1 ppb, meeting the stringent water quality standards. This self-powered device not only addresses the challenge of wastewater treatment but also leverages daylight harvesting to enhance the economic feasibility of photoelectrochemical conversion. By purifying water and simultaneously generating green hydrogen as a clean energy carrier, our approach establishes a promising pathway for transforming wastewater into a sustainable resource for solar fuel production.

Methods

Reagents and chemicals

All chemicals were used as received without further purification. Cobalt(II) chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2·6H2O, Sigma-Aldrich, 96%), chromium(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Cr(NO3) 3·9H2O, Sigma-Aldrich, 99%), iron(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, Sigma-Aldrich, 97%), potassium hydroxide (KOH, Sigma-Aldrich, 90%), hydrazine (N2H4, Sigma-Aldrich, 80% aqueous solution), and sodium chloride (NaCl, Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) were used as received. Cesium iodide (99.999%), isopropanol (99.5%), chlorobenzene (99.8%), dimethylformamide (99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (99.9%), nickel(II) acetate tetrahydrate (>98%), ethanolamine (>99%), 2-methoxyethanol (>99.8%), and bathocuproine (BCP, 99.99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Lead iodide (99.99%) and lead bromide (>98%) were purchased from TCI. Methylammonium bromide (MABr, >99.99%) and formamidinium iodide (FAI, >99.99%) were purchased from GreatCell Solar. [6,6]-Phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM, 99.5%) was purchased from Lumtec Corp.

Synthesis of metal oxides

Fe-Co-Cr oxide compositions were prepared on fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrates via ultrasonic spray pyrolysis (USP). Before deposition, the FTO glass substrates were subjected to ultrasonic cleaning in acetone, ethanol, and deionized water for 20 min each, followed by drying with a nitrogen stream. The precursor solutions were prepared by dissolving 3.0 mmol each CoCl2, Cr(NO3)3, FeCl3 in 50 mL of absolute ethanol. During USP, these precursor solutions were loaded into separate syringes, allowing the molar ratios to be precisely controlled through USP software. The flow rate was maintained at 20 mL/min, with a constant nozzle-to-substrate distance of 8 cm. The desired compositions were sprayed at a rate of 100 mL/h onto FTO substrates mounted on a hot plate heated to 200 °C in an open atmosphere. After deposition, the samples were annealed in a furnace at 400 °C for 4 h, with ramping rate of 2°/min.

In addition to FTO substrates, Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 oxide was also deposited on nickel foam (thickness: 1.6 mm). The nickel foam was first cleaned in 3.0 mol/L HCl, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in ethanol, acetone, and deionized water for 20 min each. The deposition of these oxides on nickel foam was carried out using USP under the same conditions as for the FTO substrates, resulting in a mass loading of approximately 0.8 mg cm–2.

For comparison with IrO2 and Pt, IrO₂ ink was prepared by dispersing 5 mg of the catalyst in 2 mL of a mixture of ethanol and water (1:1 by volume), followed by the addition of 80 μL of Nafion solution. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion, and the resulting ink was drop-cast onto nickel foam for subsequent electrochemical testing. Platinum was deposited on nickel foam via sputtering at a current of 10 mA for 60 s.

Fabrication of perovskite device

To prepare p-NiO HTL layer, 0.1 × 10–3 M nickel acetate and 0.1 × 10–3 M ethanolamine were first dissolved in 1 mL of 2-methoxyethanol. The mixture was heated on a hotplate at 70 °C, until a clear and homogeneous solution was obtained. Then, this solution was used to prepare the NiO layer by placing cleaned FTO substrates in a spin coater holder, and 50 µL of the prepared solution was dispensed onto each substrate. Spin coating commenced immediately after dispensing to ensure uniform film formation. The resulting NiO films were annealed at 325 °C for 60 min under ambient conditions. Following the annealing process, the substrates were allowed to cool and subsequently transferred to a glovebox to deposit perovskite and additional functional layers.

To prepare the perovskite solution, 1.1 M lead iodide (PbI2), 0.22 M lead bromide (PbBr2), 1 M formamidinium iodide (FAI), and 0.2 M methyl ammonium bromide (MABr) were dissolved in 1000 µL of a mixture of dimethylformamide/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMF:DMSO = 4:1) solvent with a 10% excess PbI2/PbBr2. The mixture was heated at 70 °C on a hotplate until it became a clear and homogenous solution. Subsequently, 42.4 µL of 1.5 M caesium iodide (CsI) solution (dissolved in DMSO) was added to the mixture solution. This perovskite precursor solution was kept on the hotplate at 70 °C for 30 min. The perovskite film was fabricated through a 2-step spin-coating process. In the first step, the precursor solution was spread at 2000 rpm for 10 seconds. This was followed by a second step at 6000 rpm for 30 s. To coat the film, 40 µL of the perovskite precursor solution was dispensed onto the substrate, and spin-coating was initiated immediately. During the final 5 s of the second spin-coating step (i.e., 6000 rpm), 120 µL of chlorobenzene (antisolvent) was dripped onto the spinning substrate to promote the crystallization. After that, the substrates were placed on a hot plate and annealed at 100 °C for 30 min. For the deposition of ETL layer, a 20 mg/mL solution of PCBM in chlorobenzene was spin-coated at 1500 rpm for 40 s. The coated substrate was then annealed at 95 °C for 15 min. A thin BCP layer was subsequently applied by spin-coating a 0.5 mg/mL BCP solution in isopropanol at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, a silver (Ag) layer of 100 nm thickness was deposited via thermal evaporation through a defined mask, yielding an active device of geometric area of ≈ 0.7 × 0.8 cm2.

Encapsulation and preparation of perovskite-based photocathode

The conductive Ag-Cu epoxy encapsulant was prepared by mixing 60% (wt/wt%) silver-coated copper dendrites (Ag-Cu, with further details on Ag-Cu preparation provided in Supplementary Note 1) with Araldite 90 min standard in a separate bottle containing parts A and B of the Araldite epoxy. The mixture was then processed using a THINKY planetary mixer to form a homogeneous paste. The paste was subjected to mixing at 1500 rpm for 5 min, repeated twice, with manual stirring between the mixing cycles. Afterward, the contents of the separate bottles containing parts A and B, along with the Ag-Cu, were combined and thoroughly mixed using the THINKY planetary mixer to ensure the formation of a homogeneous conductive Ag-Cu epoxy paste. The paste was first spread evenly, and then a piece of cleaned Ti foil was pressed onto the surface. The Ag-Cu epoxy paste, applied to the Ag layer, functions as an electrical interface between the perovskite device and the Ti foil. The composite structure of Ag-Cu epoxy and Ti foil was left to cure for 90 min. Following the curing process, the edges of the device were sealed with a 2-component epoxy (J-B Weld) and allowed to dry for 12 h at room temperature. Finally, a Pt HER catalyst was deposited onto the Ti foil through sputtering using a Quorum Technologies Q150T instrument (sputtering at 10 mA for 90 s).

Fabrication of Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4 | |Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite artificial leaf device

To fabricate an artificial leaf device, spinel oxide, Fe0.3Co0.9Cr1.8O4, was initially deposited onto a Ni foam window as described earlier (active area of ≈ 9.2 cm2, open area ≈ 6.25 cm2). A copper wire was then attached to the oxide to provide electrical contact. The spinel oxide was subsequently fixed to the FTO-coated glass side of the Pt-Ti/Ag-Cu/perovskite photocathode using adhesive tape, ensuring proper alignment with the active area of the perovskite for optimal illumination. Supplementary Fig. 38 and Fig. 5c, d show a schematic illustration of the artificial leaf device.

Characterization

For XRD, Fe10Co30Cr60O oxide was deposited on amorphous glass to avoid the peak overlap of oxide and FTO substrate. XRD diffraction patterns of the catalysts have been recorded using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. The XRD patterns were analysed using Match software after background removal. SEM images were collected using FE-SEM (JEOL-6700F), while EDX analysis was carried out with an Oxford Instruments X-Max detector and the INCA software package. The composition of ternary oxide was measured by EDX and ICP-MS (Supplementary Table 1). HR-TEM analysis was conducted using a JEOL 2100 F TEM. Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and inverse-FFT were generated using Digital Micrograph software to analyze the crystal planes. XPS analysis and Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was performed by using AXIS Supra XPS instrument (Kratos Analytical Ltd, UK). Photoluminescence measurements were carried out using a Fluorolog FL-1057 spectrometer (Horiba Instruments) equipped with a 450 W xenon lamp. The PL spectra of the perovskite material were recorded with a 460 nm excitation wavelength.

Electrochemical measurements

LSV measurements for HzOR were conducted by Autolab (PGSTAT302N) electrochemical workstation in a 3-electrode configuration. The counter electrode was a Pt mesh, the reference electrode was an Ag/AgCl electrode, and the working electrode was the oxide catalyst deposited on an FTO substrate. The LSV scans were performed over a potential range of –1.3 to –0.1 V vs. Ag/AgCl, with a scan rate of 20 mV s–1 for 3 times to confirm reproducibility. PEC measurements

For the PEC measurements of the photocathode and artificial leaf device, solar simulator (Peccell Technologies, PEC-L15) fitted with a KG-3 solar filter was utilized to mimic the solar spectrum (AM 1.5 G). Using a standard Si solar cell, the light intensity was adjusted to 1 sun (100 mW cm–2) before initiating the PEC experiments. All PEC measurements for both 2-compartment device and photocathode, configured in a 3 electrode, were performed using an Autolab workstation. During these experiments, the perovskite photocathode was illuminated from the back. For bias-free PEC measurements in a two-compartment, two-electrode configuration, 1.0 M KOH was used as the electrolyte in the cathodic compartment, while the anodic compartment contained 1.0 M KOH with 0.5 M hydrazine. Both compartments contained 0.5 M NaCl and were separated by a Nafion membrane. The rubber septa were used to seal the PEC cell, and both compartments were purged with argon gas for 30 min prior to the measurements. An aliquot of gas from the headspace of a sealed H-cell was collected using a gas-tight syringe and injected into a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890 A) for analysis.

Measurement of residual hydrazine

Residual hydrazine in the electrolyte from bias-free PEC experiments was quantified using the spectrophotometric method based on Watt and Chrisp45. Periodic aliquots of the electrolyte were collected during the PEC measurements and diluted with HCl (10 mL, 1.0 M) to ensure optimal conditions for the colorimetric reaction. To each diluted sample, 5 mL of the color reagent—comprising 5.99 g of para-(dimethylamino)benzaldehyde dissolved in a mixture of 30 mL of concentrated HCl (37%) and 300 mL of absolute ethanol—was added in a vial wrapped in aluminium foil to protect it from light. The mixture was stirred for 30 min to allow the reaction to proceed. Subsequently, the UV-vis absorbance of the solution was recorded at 456 nm, corresponding to the absorption peak of hydrazine with the reagent. A calibration curve was prepared using standard solutions of hydrazine monohydrate in 1.0 M KOH.
