Defining causes of death‐censored kidney allograft failure: A 5‐year multicentre ANZDATA and clinical cross‐sectional study
Authors: William R Mulley, Peter D Hughes, Michael G Collins, Helen L Pilmore, Philip A Clayton, Melanie L Wyld, Darren Lee, Jane van der Jeugd, Sanduni C Fernando, Stephanie Fang‐Tzu Kuo, Sarah Tan, Sadia Jahan, Wai H Lim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimDetermining specific causes of allograft failure allows a focus on understanding and treating these conditions. Previous studies highlight chronic antibody‐mediated rejection as a leading cause of late allograft failure. We sought to define causes of allograft failure in a large cohort of kidney transplant recipients across multiple centres in Australia and New Zealand, including cases previously attributed to chronic allograft nephropathy (CAN).MethodsAll death‐censored allograft failures at 9 participating centres between 1 January 2014 to 31 December 2018 were included. Available clinical and biopsy data were reviewed and the “most likely” cause assigned.ResultsThere were 642 death‐censored allograft failures in the study period. Of these, 495 (77.1%) had an informative biopsy performed a median of 13.4 months (IQR 2.5–39.1 months) prior to allograft failure. Rejection of any type was the leading cause of allograft failure (47.5%), comprised chiefly of chronic antibody‐mediated rejection (37.4%) and chronic T‐cell mediated rejection (6.4%). Other leading causes were undifferentiated interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (10.8%), late medical and surgical complications (8.1%) and recurrent or de novo glomerulonephritis (7.0%). Polyoma viral nephropathy and calcineurin inhibitor toxicity each contributed to <2%. Causes of allograft failure previously attributed to CAN (n = 419, 65.3%) had a similar distribution to the overall cohort, with 43.9% attributed to chronic antibody‐mediated rejection.ConclusionTo prolong allograft survival, improved strategies are needed to curtail alloimmune responses. Greater understanding of the causes of undifferentiated interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy and potential treatments would also be of considerable benefit.Keywords: allografts, biopsy, fibrosis, glomerulonephritis, kidney transplantation

INTRODUCTION

Prolonging kidney allograft survival is a key current goal of transplantation. Maximizing functioning allograft duration promotes patient survival and reduces the need for re‐transplantation, thereby reducing demand for the limited number of available donor kidneys. 1 In that context, defining specific causes of kidney allograft failure that enable the identification of the underlying pathogenic processes is important to focus efforts on enhanced detection and prevention or treatment strategies. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Over the last 15 years, small audits from several single transplanting centres and 2 multicentre collaborations have reported the distribution of their causes for allograft failures. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 These identified chronic alloimmune injury, chiefly chronic antibody mediated rejection (cAMR), as a dominant cause of allograft failure. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Recurrent or de novo glomerulonephritis, undifferentiated interstitial fibrosis with tubular atrophy (IFTA), polyoma viral nephropathy (PVN) and no specific identifiable causes were also prominent. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Some previous studies have excluded cases where a biopsy was not performed preceding allograft failure, in order to increase the specificity of the diagnoses. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 This, however, does not allow attribution of cause for allograft failure in a large number of cases and introduces selection bias, resulting in an inaccurate assessment of the proportion of allograft failure for each cause due to an adjusted denominator. 10

The Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry (ANZDATA) had, until 2020, registered chronic allograft nephropathy (CAN) as the leading death‐censored cause of allograft failure within Australia and New Zealand. Internationally, there has been a transition away from this non‐specific term, 11 , 12 introduced into the Banff classification in 1993, to more pathologically defined terms, with subsequent removal from the Banff classification in 2005. 13 , 14 Allograft failure due to CAN has been associated with early acute rejection 15 and was thought to encompass allograft failures from chronic rejection (including cAMR), 11 , 16 , 17 however it is unclear what other causes of allograft failure lie within this historic category within ANZDATA and other registries.

In the current study, our goal was to define all causes of death‐censored allograft failures across multiple transplant units in Australia and New Zealand as precisely as possible, by integrating medical record assessment with biopsy and donor specific antibody (DSA) data. We hypothesised that cAMR was the leading cause of allograft failure in this patient cohort and that most cases of allograft failure due to CAN were due to cAMR.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patient inclusion

This was a retrospective multicentre cross‐sectional clinical audit. ANZDATA provided re‐identifiable data for patients who had experienced death‐censored allograft failure between 1st January 2014 and 31st December 2018 to their respective participating units. All patients with allograft failure not due to death with a functioning allograft from the participating centres (Monash Health, Royal Melbourne Hospital, Auckland City Hospital, Royal Adelaide Hospital, Westmead Hospital, Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital, Flinders Medical Centre, Austin Health and Eastern Health) were included. Data from ANZDATA included recipient age and gender, primary kidney disease, transplant date, transplant type, transplant number, peak panel reactive antibody, HLA matching, immunosuppression medication use, rejection episodes, medical comorbidities, date of allograft failure, cause of allograft failure, date of death and cause of death. Additional data retrieved from patients' medical records included whether an allograft biopsy/ies had been performed post‐transplant, date of biopsy, biopsy diagnoses, Banff rejection scores (g, i, t, v, cg, ci, ct, cv, ah, ptc, c4d, i‐IFTA) where available, whether donor specific antibody (DSA) testing had been performed before and/or after transplant, date of testing, presence of class I or II DSA and immunosuppression regimen recorded closest to allograft failure. DSA positivity thresholds were determined at the unit level. DSA screening post‐transplant was not performed routinely but rather by clinical need adjudged by each centre. Each centre de‐identified their data before it was combined for analysis. ANZDATA contains data from all patients requiring kidney replacement therapy (dialysis or transplant) across Australia and New Zealand using an opt out consent process. This study was approved by the Monash Health Human Research Ethics Committee (RES‐20‐0000‐184 L) for Australia, and the Northern B Health and Disability Ethics Committee (20/NTB/215) for New Zealand.

Determining causes of allograft failure

Transplant clinicians from each participating centre reviewed the data set described above and clinical entries in patients' medical records for their respective sites to determine the “most likely” cause of allograft failure. This involved integrating biopsy data, DSA data, primary kidney disease, trajectory of kidney function decline, proteinuria and other biochemistry results, clinical features such as blood pressure control, development of intercurrent conditions such as malignancy or infection, treatment changes and the prevailing views of the clinicians reviewing the patients, as determined from their clinical entries, to form an expert opinion. Specific diagnostic criteria were not applied for causation given that this was a retrospective assessment with significant missing data. A pragmatic view was taken to the weight given to biopsy and DSA data. Original biopsy reports were reviewed and the biopsy slides were not rescored. Biopsies taken at any time post‐transplant were reviewed and were the major determinant in deciding on the cause of allograft failure but were not the sole factor in that decision. If a biopsy was informative to allograft loss it was included regardless of how long prior to allograft loss it was performed, whilst if it was not informative or the findings were considered unlikely to cause allograft failure, that case was reported as allograft failure without an informative biopsy. Similarly, DSA data were included regardless of proximity to transplantation (pre‐transplant DSA) or allograft failure (post‐transplant DSA). In the case of multiple contributing causes, clinicians decided which was the most likely primary cause of allograft failure while a secondary cause was also recorded.

Causes of allograft failure

Causes were coded as cAMR, IFTA – not due to rejection, gradual allograft failure without a biopsy being performed, chronic T‐cell mediated rejection (TCMR)/iIFTA, calcineurin inhibitor toxicity and “other.” “Other” causes were further defined in a free text field. To allow comparison to previous studies, causes of allograft failure were further categorized as being due to: rejection; gradual allograft failure without a biopsy being performed; IFTA – not due to rejection; recurrent or de novo glomerulonephritis; primary non‐function/early surgical complication; late medical or surgical complication, and polyoma virus nephropathy.

Comparison to previous studies

Previous studies variably excluded early allograft failures due to primary non‐function and to acute surgical factors or where the cause could not be clearly determined. To allow comparison across studies, these causes were excluded for all studies. Using the data presented in each study we reallocated the causes to the broad groups outlined above and recalculated the proportion of allograft failure per cause for each study given the altered denominators.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data are presented as number and percentage. Proportions were compared using the chi‐square test and odds ratios. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Study cohort

There were 1210 episodes of allograft failure during the study period across the participating centres (Figure 1). This represents 41.2% of all allograft failures (n = 2940) reported to ANZDATA during this time. Death with allograft function accounted for 539 (44.5%) allograft failures. Of the allograft failures not due to death, 29 were unable to be assessed as they were no longer attending the participating centre or their data was not accessible. Cause of allograft failure was therefore assessed in 642 patients which represents 46.5% of all allograft failures not due to death (n = 1380) in Australia and New Zealand during this time. Of those, 494 (76.9%) had an informative allograft biopsy available, while 271 (42.2%) had both a biopsy and pre‐transplant DSA data available, and 269 (41.9%) had both a biopsy and post‐transplant DSA data available. The causes of allograft failure reported to ANZDATA at the time of failure were: CAN (n = 419, 65.3%); acute rejection (n = 34, 5.3%); glomerulonephritis (n = 43, 6.7%); non‐compliance (n = 27, 4.2%); vascular (n = 20, 3.1%); technical (n = 12, 1.9%); other (n = 70, 10.9%) and not reported (n = 17, 2.7%).

Patient characteristics

The median age of the study cohort was 40 (29–52) years at transplantation and 51 (40–62) years at time of allograft failure (Table 1). Thirty‐eight percent (38%) were female. Most patients (n = 541, 84.3%) had received their first transplant and the median duration of allograft survival was 8.9 (4.5–14.8) years. For maintenance immunosuppression, 78% of patients were receiving a calcineurin inhibitor, 74.6% were receiving mycophenolate and 86.9% were receiving prednisolone at the time of allograft failure.

Allograft failures with an informative biopsy

When considering only allograft failures where investigators had access to an informative allograft biopsy (n = 495) to supplement clinical records, cAMR was enriched as a cause of allograft failure accounting for 240 (48.5%) cases (Table 2). This was followed by IFTA not due to rejection, recurrent or de novo glomerulonephritis and chronic TCMR/iIFTA. This classification eliminated the gradual allograft failure without a biopsy group. The frequency of primary and secondary biopsy diagnoses from the closest biopsy to allograft failure are presented in Table S4 for patients in this group.

Allograft failures with DSA data and an informative biopsy

Two centres were unable to provide any DSA data and were excluded from the DSA analysis (n = 132). For the remaining centres in patients with an informative biopsy (n = 405), pre‐transplant DSA were positive in 67 patients, negative in 204 and missing in 135. For those with a pre‐transplant DSA, cAMR was the most likely cause of allograft failure occurring in 37 (55.2%) compared with 87 (42.7%) of those known to be negative pre‐transplant (OR 1.51, 95% C.I. 0.9–2.4, p = .09) (Table S5). Those with missing data had a similar proportion of allograft failure due to cAMR as those with a positive DSA.

Post‐transplant DSA were positive in 182 patients, negative in 87 and missing in 137. For those with a known post‐transplant DSA, cAMR was the cause of allograft failure in 126 (69.2%) compared with 33 (37.9%) without a post‐transplant DSA (OR 4.7, 95% C.I. 3.1–7.2, p < .0001) (Table S6). Those with missing data had allograft failure due to cAMR in 48 patients (35.0%).

From the 419 allograft failures originally attributed to CAN, 184 (43.9%) were reassigned to cAMR and in those where a biopsy had been performed (n = 314), 184 (58.6%) were reassigned to cAMR (Table 3). Allograft failures relative to time post‐transplant

Allograft failure early after transplant, particularly in the first post‐transplant year, had a quite different composition, relative to later years (Figure 2). The first year had the greatest number (n = 46, 7.1%) of allograft failures. The leading causes of allograft failure in the first year were technical or vascular causes, accounting for 58.7% of allograft failures (n = 27). Allograft failure due to acute kidney injury, infection, primary non‐function or poor kidney quality were also common. The frequency of allograft failures due to cAMR rose steadily after the first 2 years to peak at 9 years post‐transplant where it accounted for 21 cases (52.5%). This subsequently declined with IFTA not due to rejection and gradual allograft failure without a biopsy becoming the predominant causes of very late allograft failure (Figure 2).

Variation in causes of allograft failure between centres

There was substantial variation in the composition of causes of allograft failure between centres in this study (Figure 3). The proportion of allograft failures due to any type of rejection ranged from 9% to 75%. Some of the variation may relate to the proportion of allograft failures where no informative biopsy was available and allograft failure was attributed to gradual allograft failure without a clear cause which ranged between 5% and 73%.

Comparison of allograft failure composition to previous reports

Comparison to other reports of causes for allograft failure used simplified categories and excluded early allograft failures due to primary non‐function or early surgical factors and cases where the diagnosis was unknown (Figure 4). 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Across all studies, rejection was the leading cause of allograft failure accounting for between 21.1% to 66.6% of cases. Non‐specific scarring/IFTA was next most frequent (0% to 47.2%) followed by recurrent or de novo glomerular diseases (9.0%–22.8%) and medical and surgical complications (0% to 30.5%). PVN was prominent in some of the earlier studies and less prominent in the most recent studies.

DISCUSSION

This is the largest in‐depth multicentre analysis of causes of death‐censored allograft failure reported to date and the first from the Asia‐Pacific region. Combining clinical, histologic and DSA data we report that the leading cause of death‐censored allograft failure in a contemporary patient cohort was cAMR followed by gradual allograft failure without a known cause, non‐specific renal fibrosis (IFTA), recurrent or de novo glomerular diseases, chronic TCMR, late medical or surgical complications and primary nonfunction/early surgical complications. Secondary findings were that CAN was previously used as a collective term for diverse specific and non‐specific clinical and pathological conditions, and that there was significant variation in the causes of allograft failure between individual transplant units.

Chronic allograft injury due to alloreactive anti‐HLA antibodies, cAMR, has been well recognized as a major obstacle to long‐term allograft survival. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 17 , 18 , 19 Substantial efforts have been made to define pathophysiologic mechanisms of this process and to identify risk factors for antibody development to allow a focus on prevention strategies. There have been few if any effective therapies for this condition and current trials examine new therapies compared with placebo, highlighting that standard of care is currently considered to be optimising the maintenance immunosuppressive regimen. Previous reports have estimated AMR to account for up to 30% of allograft failures. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 In the current study, we found 37.4% of allograft losses were due to cAMR and 48.5% in the group with an informative biopsy. It was a particularly prominent cause from the 4th post‐transplant year. The presence of pre‐transplant DSA was not significantly associated with a greater proportion of allograft failures from cAMR in this cohort while post‐transplant DSA were more commonly identified and were associated with a substantial proportion with allograft failure from cAMR and any type of rejection, accounting for 81.3% of all causes compared with 50.6% without a post‐transplant DSA. These data are in keeping with our current understanding of the impact of preformed and de novo DSA on the risk of AMR and allograft failure. 6 , 18 , 19 Both are associated with an increased risk of AMR with de novo DSA more associated with late AMR while preformed DSA with early AMR, although progression to chronic AMR is common. Early AMR has been reported to be more responsive to therapy than late AMR and the current findings substantiate that proposition given that relatively few allograft failures (2.8%) were due to acute rejection. 17 , 19 , 20

The variation between published reports on the importance of cAMR is likely explicable by a number of factors, including study design (prospective vs. retrospective), time from transplantation, underlying immunological risk and allocation policies, immunosuppression regimens and patient factors. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 The variation between studies was also reflected in the substantial variation between transplanting units within our own study, despite similar immunosuppressive regimens. Some of this appears to relate to variation by chance with the inclusion of smaller units while the biopsy propensity of different centres prevented a more precise diagnosis in up to 38% of patients from larger centres. The lack of inter‐observer agreement in reporting transplant biopsies has been previously reported 21 and may explain some of the variability between centres, along with availability and use of anti‐HLA antibody assays, without which the diagnosis of cAMR is less certain. Despite the variation, previous studies support our findings of cAMR as a major cause of allograft failure and heighten calls for better approaches for dealing with this condition.

The significant contribution of chronic TCMR to allograft failure has not been as well appreciated. Chronic active TCMR and iIFTA are increasingly recognized as important phenomena 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 and have been included in the Banff classification schema for rejection. 26 Reports on the association of the iIFTA lesion with reduced allograft survival are proliferating. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 We found chronic TCMR to be the primary cause of allograft failure in 8.1% of cases. El‐Zoghby et al., in their seminal paper, reported 7.8% of their cases of allograft failure to be due to late TCMR. Other studies point to a similar or greater frequency of TCMR as a cause of allograft failure 2 , 3 , 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 with the exception of Sellares et al. who report that all but one of their cases of allograft failure due to rejection involved AMR. 6 This is in keeping with suggestions that iIFTA and late TCMR may portend a transition to AMR. 25 , 27

After allocating causes of allograft failure into simplified broad categories similar to those used by Van Loon et al., 1 it is apparent that rejection is the major cause of allograft loss in all included studies except those of Naesens et al. 5 and Van Loon et al., 9 2 separate reports from the same centre, although their first report notes rejection features in 52.8% of cases and the second report details a significant prevalence of DSA in their IFTA group. After excluding unknown and early causes of death‐censored allograft loss we attributed two thirds of allograft failures to rejection, in keeping with the findings of Betjes et al. and Sellares et al. 2 , 6 Hence, despite improvements in immunosuppressive regimens over time, alloimmunity remains the greatest barrier to long‐term death‐censored allograft survival. It has been proposed that much of this relates to patient non‐adherence or clinician led immunosuppression reduction. 1 , 6 While improvements in these domains are possible, non‐adherence is difficult to completely overcome 28 , 29 and a lack of reliable measures of immunosuppression adequacy prevent a scientific approach to immunosuppression reduction. Additionally, given that death remains the leading overall cause of allograft loss, over immunosuppression remains a concern given that death due to infection and malignancy are substantial in the transplant population. 8 , 10

IFTA or non‐specific allograft fibrosis remains a problem category. Scarring not due to rejection may have a range of aetiologies relating to the donor, the transplant process or post‐transplant factors. The precise cause can be difficult to elucidate if there is no ongoing histological activity as the injurious mechanisms do not necessarily generate specific histological patterns. 5 , 6 , 12 Of note 3 of the included studies 6 , 7 , 8 did not include IFTA as a category for allograft failure instead attributing such cases to medical causes or as unknown. However, in the other studies, 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 9 it comprised up to 47.2% of allograft failures making it an important cause and a key area for further investigation.

Recurrent or de novo glomerulonephritis accounted for 7% of allograft failures in this study and was a relatively consistent finding across all studies. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The timing of allograft failure due to glomerulonephritis ranged from 12 months to over 20 years post‐transplant. Similar to previous reports, we found medical or later surgical complications responsible for 8.1% of allograft failures. Predominant amongst these were acute kidney injury (particularly in the setting of infection), calcineurin inhibitor toxicity and thrombotic microangiopathy. Diabetic nephropathy was a relatively infrequent cause of allograft failure. Somewhat reassuringly, the transplant biopsy itself accounted for only 2 of 642 allograft failures, with both events occurring during the first post‐transplant year.

Polyoma virus nephropathy has received a great deal of attention since its discovery and was an important cause of allograft failure in the studies of El‐Zoghby et al. 4 and Sellares et al., 6 accounting for 7–8% of cases. Its relative importance appears to have declined over time, with the exception of Van Loon et al., 9 with our study and Betjes et al. 2 placing it at <2% of cases. This may be due to better screening and management approaches but this was not able to be analysed in this registry study.

A key issue in all studies is the lack of a clear diagnosis in a large proportion of the population. In our study this accounted for approximately 1 in 5 cases, predominantly due to the lack of an informative biopsy. This leads to false estimates of the importance of any cause if these cases are removed from the denominator. It also means that we may be missing reversible causes in this group who tend to be more remote from transplant, with long lasting allografts. There may be a reluctance to undertake a biopsy, instead assuming that the failure is due to non‐specific changes such as IFTA. Greater efforts to obtain a diagnosis in these cases may inform approaches to prolong allograft survival.

CAN had been the leading cause of death censored allograft failure reported to the ANZDATA Registry until its removal as a reportable cause of allograft failure in 2020. 10 CAN had been thought to represent chronic rejection, amongst other things, 11 , 12 and was where cAMR was thought likely to be recorded prior to its inclusion as an option. 17 This however, was not the case, with the cases originally reported as due to CAN having a similar distribution of causes to all allograft losses. Chronic AMR accounted for less than half the cases attributed to CAN. One quarter of cases were reassigned to a gradual decline without a biopsy having been performed and 13% to IFTA not related to rejection. Interestingly, a range of other biopsy proven diagnoses that would not normally be confused for CAN, such as recurrent or de novo glomerular disease, diabetic nephropathy and thrombotic microangiopathy were originally reported to the registry as CAN. At that time, the CAN category on the registry reporting form was “Chronic Allograft Nephropathy (Slow Progressive Loss of Function)”, which may have promoted the use of this category as a catchall for those with gradual allograft functional decline. In line with current thinking, our findings support the removal of CAN due to its lack of specificity. 11 , 12 , 14 Additionally, the findings of this study suggest caution needs to be used when interpreting historic registry data relating to this category of allograft failure.

Limitations

This study reports on a cross‐section of allograft failures within a 5‐year period. This means that the proportion of allograft failures by cause from the time of transplant cannot be estimated. Despite the use of histology data, allograft failure attribution remains somewhat subjective, leading to possible classification differences between observers. Allograft failure was interpreted using original local biopsy reports wherein there may be differences between pathologists from different centres in biopsy interpretation and a change in Banff criteria used over time. Additionally, some elements of histology data were not available in the original reports. Data on DSA were missing or unable to be accessed in the majority of cases meaning that the results pertaining to them need to be interpreted with caution. C4d staining was predominantly by immunohistochemistry, which provides a substantial underestimate of C4d positivity relative to immunofluorescence.

Conclusions

The major cause of allograft failure in the studied population was rejection, with cAMR being the predominant subtype. Improved strategies are needed to curtail alloimmune responses and allow prolongation of allograft survival. Greater understanding of the causes and potential treatments for IFTA that is not due to rejection would also be of considerable potential benefit.
