Smoking after the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes: Smoking-related environmental conditions and psychological dependence in temporary shelter sites
Authors: Cigdem Samanci Teki̇n, Gamze Aydin, Nur Cihan Özkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine smoking prevalence, smoking-related environmental conditions in temporary shelter sites, and psychological dependence on smoking among earthquake survivors living in the 11 provinces affected by the February 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes in Türkiye.MethodsThis cross-sectional and exploratory study was conducted in Türkiye between September 2024 and January 2025 among individuals affected by the February 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, using an online self-administered questionnaire. A total of 455 participants were included. Data were collected on smoking status, changes in smoking behavior after the earthquake, perceptions of smoking-related environmental conditions in shelter sites, and psychological dependence using the Scale for the Assessment of Psychological Dependence on Smoking. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Group comparisons were performed using chi-square tests and independent-samples t tests. Multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with psychological dependence.ResultsOverall, 25.1% of participants were current smokers. Although the study did not directly measure pre-earthquake smoking prevalence, many current smokers retrospectively reported increased cigarette consumption after the earthquake. More than half of smokers reported increased cigarette consumption, and 3.5% of current smokers initiated smoking after the earthquake. Most participants reported limited smoking regulation and a lack of designated smoking areas in temporary shelter sites. Among smokers, the mean psychological dependence score indicated moderate dependence. In the multivariable model, higher daily cigarette consumption (B = 0.180, p = 0.006) and smoking to control anxiety (B = 3.708, p = 0.032) were independently associated with psychological dependence. The model explained 25.0% of the variance in psychological dependence scores (R² = 0.25).ConclusionsSmoking after earthquakes may be influenced by both environmental and psychological factors. Integrating smoke-free shelter policies, designated smoking areas, smoking cessation support, and psychosocial services into disaster response may help reduce smoking-related health burdens among disaster survivors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28400-2.

Introduction

Globally, disasters are occurring with increasing frequency and impose a growing burden on communities, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Beyond causing loss of life, they result in the destruction of housing, infrastructure, and livelihoods, thereby adversely affecting the physical, mental, and social well-being of populations and constituting a major public health concern. In low-income settings, even a single disaster can place substantial pressure on already fragile health systems, increase health inequalities, and restrict access to essential healthcare services [1]. In this context, the ‘twin earthquakes’ —two high-magnitude seismic events (Mw 7.7 and Mw 7.6) that occurred less than nine hours apart—centered in Kahramanmaraş on 6 February 2023, became one of the most devastating disasters in the country’s history, causing widespread destruction across 11 provinces with a combined population of 14 million. More than 50,000 people lost their lives and over 107,000 were injured as a result of the disaster [2].

Tobacco use remains a major global public health challenge, causing more than 7 million deaths each year, with approximately 80% of the 1.3 billion tobacco users worldwide living in low- and middle-income countries [3]. Recent estimates indicate that the prevalence of tobacco use among individuals aged 15 years and older is approximately 19% globally [4]. In Türkiye, which is frequently struck by major seismic events, both tobacco use [5] and earthquakes [6] impose substantial public health burdens that contribute significantly to mortality and disability. Notably, the prevalence of regular cigarette smoking among individuals aged 15 years and older in Türkiye is 28.3% [7], substantially exceeding the current global average.

Changes in smoking behavior following different types of disasters —including earthquakes [8], hurricanes [9], terror attacks [10], wars [11], and pandemics [12] — have been reported to vary by context and population; these changes typically present as increases in smoking prevalence, increased consumption among existing smokers, and relapse among former smokers. Such patterns appear particularly pronounced after major earthquakes in countries with active fault lines, such as Japan and Türkiye, where notable increases in tobacco use have been observed following significant seismic events [13–15]. Increased smoking after disasters is influenced by multiple factors, including changes in living conditions, job loss or job change, psychological distress, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) and depressive symptoms, anxiety, inadequate social support, and present bias [16–20]. Consistent with these findings, our previous study conducted after the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes identified a significant association between PTSD symptoms and smoking behavior [21].

These challenges become particularly pronounced after large-scale natural disasters, especially major earthquakes with a magnitude of Mw ≥ 7.0 [22], which can generate substantial psychological distress and a range of environmental and social stressors, including those related to post-disaster shelter conditions [23–25]. In the immediate aftermath of disasters, emergency shelters are required to meet the basic physical needs of affected communities [26]. During this process, temporary shelter sites, including container settlements and tent settlements, become the primary living environments for disaster survivors until more stable housing solutions are established [27]. Although standards for temporary shelter sites have been defined [28–30], these settings often involve multiple health risks, including inadequate ventilation, exposure to hazardous insects and rodents, difficulties in accessing clean water, inadequate nutrition, and poor hygiene conditions [31, 32]. In addition, living in noisy and security-deficient temporary shelter environments after disasters may undermine a sense of safety, leading to sleep disturbances and impaired emotional regulation, which negatively affect anger control, attention, and problem-solving abilities [24, 33]. Under these highly distressing conditions, health-risk behaviors may emerge as maladaptive strategies used to cope with intense emotions, frequently resulting in increased alcohol and tobacco use [34, 35].

Moreover, in crowded and poorly ventilated temporary shelter environments, the absence of designated smoking areas or clear smoking regulations may intensify exposure to secondhand tobacco smoke among non-smokers [28–30]. Consistently, evidence from other communal living settings, such as homeless shelters, suggests that living in pro-tobacco environments characterized by high levels of stress and boredom may increase the social acceptability of tobacco use, create barriers to smoking cessation, and contribute to the normalization of smoking behaviors within shared living environments [36, 37].

Despite these documented environmental risks, limited studies have examined the psychological dependence dimension of smoking in post-disaster contexts. Psychological dependence refers to continued smoking to regulate emotions, cope with stress, and maintain perceived functioning beyond nicotine’s direct physiological effects, reinforced by psychosocial rewards such as relaxation, routine accompaniment, stress control, and social acceptance [38–42]. In this study, psychological dependence was assessed using the Scale for the Assessment of Psychological Dependence on Smoking (SAPDS), which was developed to assess psychological motivations and dimensions of smoking dependence, including emotional modulation, personality-image, and social acceptance, rather than nicotine dependence alone. Previous studies have reported strong associations between smoking dependence and depressive mood, negative affect, stress, low self-esteem, and psychological distress under normal conditions [43–47]. In the post-disaster period, weakened perceptions of control and constrained coping resources may make the role of psychological dependence in maintaining smoking behavior even more visible. Therefore, it is insufficient to consider post-disaster smoking solely in terms of frequency of use; it must be examined within the framework of psychological dependence mechanisms.

Because tobacco use and disasters have generally been examined separately in the literature, studies investigating their direct interrelationship remain scarce. Few studies have examined how smoking-related environmental conditions in temporary shelter sites interact with psychological dependence among disaster survivors, particularly in the prolonged recovery phase after major earthquakes [16, 19, 48–51]. To address this gap, this study aimed to examine smoking-related environmental conditions in temporary shelter sites after the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, assess disaster survivors’ perceptions of these conditions, and identify factors associated with psychological dependence on smoking in the post-disaster period.

Materials and methods

Setting and participants

This study employed an exploratory cross-sectional design. Data collection was formally conducted over a five-month period between September 2024 and January 2025, approximately 19 to 23 months after the main earthquake event on 6 February 2023.

The minimum sample size was calculated using OpenEpi software [52] based on the estimated population of the 11 provinces affected by the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. Because reliable prior estimates for the primary study variables were unavailable, a conservative expected frequency of 50% was used, as recommended when estimating sample size in the absence of prior prevalence data. Assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a design effect of 1.0, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 385 participants.

A Google Forms link was created for the survey instrument. Because regular daily life in the region had not yet fully resumed and the study area spanned an extensive geographical territory, an online survey approach was considered appropriate. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years and older who had directly experienced the 6 February 2023 earthquakes, were residing in one of the 11 affected provinces, and possessed internet access and literacy. Participants were recruited through online WhatsApp groups comprising disaster-management graduates, students, and volunteers. The study sample included all eligible participants regardless of whether they had stayed in temporary shelter sites. However, analyses related to smoking-related environmental conditions were restricted to participants who reported residing in temporary shelter sites. Participants who had not resided in such settings were excluded from these analyses. Consequently, the study sample is likely to overrepresent individuals who are younger, more highly educated, more digitally connected, and potentially more engaged with disaster-response networks than the broader earthquake-affected population. This characteristic was considered during the interpretation of the findings, particularly regarding prevalence estimates and the generalizability of the results. Completion of the survey required approximately 15 min, and voluntary informed consent was obtained digitally from all participants prior to entering the survey.

Different analytical samples were applied according to specific study objectives to ensure the use of appropriate denominators (Fig. 1). The total sample (N = 455) was used for general sociodemographic and descriptive analyses. Analyses of smoking-related environmental conditions were restricted to participants who reported residing in temporary shelter sites, including container settlements and tent settlements (n = 353; 77.6%). Participants who had not resided in temporary shelter sites were excluded from these analyses. Analyses of smoking behaviors and psychological dependence were restricted to current smokers (n = 114; 25.1%). The denominator used for each analysis is reported throughout the text, tables, and figures.

Temporary shelter sites in the earthquake-affected region mainly consisted of tent settlements and container settlements established by public authorities and humanitarian organizations. Tent settlements generally included family tents arranged in rows or clustered sections, with shared sanitation units, water points, and common service areas. Container settlements consisted of prefabricated container units designed for household accommodation, typically organized in blocks or compounds with internal roads, electricity, water supply, and communal facilities such as toilets, showers, laundry areas, health units, or social service spaces. Although the specific layout and infrastructure varied across provinces and sites, these temporary shelter sites represented the principal temporary living environments for many disaster survivors during the post-earthquake recovery period (Fig. 2).

Data collection instruments

The study used a questionnaire form and the Scale for the Assessment of Psychological Dependence on Smoking (SAPDS). An 18-item questionnaire prepared by the researchers (covering participants’ sociodemographic data, smoking-related conditions after the earthquake, smoking characteristics, etc.) and the 25-item SAPDS, designed to determine psychological dependence on smoking, were used.

Questionnaire form

The questionnaire was developed by the researchers in line with the study objectives. Items related to smoking characteristics were prepared with reference to the relevant literature, whereas items concerning environmental conditions and the post-disaster sheltering process were developed based on the researchers’ field experience. Prior to data collection, the shelter-environment items were reviewed by two experts, one in public health and one in disaster management, to assess their relevance, clarity, and appropriateness for the post-disaster context.

The majority of items were close-ended and consisted of categorical or multiple-choice response options; however, questions regarding age, daily cigarette consumption, and duration of smoking were open-ended. The questionnaire covered sociodemographic characteristics, smoking status, changes in smoking behavior after the earthquake, smoking duration, daily cigarette consumption, and exposure to secondhand tobacco smoke. Smoking-related environmental conditions were assessed through participants’ self-reported perceptions regarding access to cigarettes, the availability of smoking areas, exposure to tobacco smoke, and smoking-related conditions in their living environments (Supplementary File 1). Prior to the main study, a pilot study was conducted with ten participants to assess the clarity and applicability of the questionnaire. Data obtained from the pilot study participants were not included in the main analyses. Based on participant feedback, no modifications to the questionnaire items were required, as all items were considered clear and understandable. Therefore, the questionnaire was administered in its original form in the main study.

Scale for the Assessment of Psychological Dependence on Smoking (SAPDS)

The scale was originally developed by Ponciano-Rodríguez et al. in 2015 [42] and was adapted into Turkish by Bardakçı et al. in 2021 [38]. The SAPDS was developed to assess the psychological dimensions of smoking dependence, including emotional modulation, personality-image, indifference, and social acceptance, and complements rather than replaces measures of nicotine dependence. The scale comprises 25 items with a three-point Likert format. Its Cronbach’s alpha was reported as 0.93. Items are scored as 1 (Never), 2 (Sometimes), and 3 (Quite often). According to the total score, 25–41 points indicate mild dependence; 42–58 points indicate moderate dependence; and 59–75 points indicate severe dependence. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated as 0.84.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Standard Concurrent User V26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies, percentages, means ± standard deviations, and ranges, as appropriate. Age was categorized at 30 years for descriptive and bivariate analyses, as this threshold approximately reflected the median age of the sample. Skewness and kurtosis values were examined to assess the normality of the main dependent variable (SAPDS scores). The SAPDS scores demonstrated an approximately normal distribution, with a skewness of 0.513 and a kurtosis of 0.133 (both within the acceptable threshold of ± 1.5), supporting the use of parametric statistical procedures. The chi-square test was used to examine associations between categorical variables, and the independent-samples t test was used to compare mean SAPDS scores between two groups.

To identify factors independently associated with psychological dependence on smoking, variables that demonstrated statistical significance in the univariate analyses were simultaneously entered into a multivariable linear regression model using the standard Enter method. To control for potential confounding, age and gender were included in the model regardless of their statistical significance. Age and daily cigarette consumption were entered as continuous variables, whereas categorical variables (gender, smoking to control anxiety, smoking to reduce stress, and difficulty accessing cigarettes) were entered as dummy variables. The regression analysis was conducted among current smokers only. Gender was coded as male = 1 and female = 0, and the remaining categorical variables were coded as yes = 1 and no = 0. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 455 earthquake survivors participated in this study. The mean age of participants was 33.3 ± 10.7 years, with ages ranging from 18 to 66. Approximately half of the participants (50.1%) were aged 30 years or younger. With respect to educational status, 52.3% of participants were university graduates.

Overall, 25.1% of participants were current smokers. Most participants (77.6%) reported staying in temporary shelter sites after the earthquake. Among those who stayed in temporary shelters, participants were allowed to report more than one type of shelter, as many experienced transitions between different shelter types during the post-earthquake period. Accordingly, 89.0% reported staying in container settlements and 90.4% in tent settlements (Table 1).

Overall, 59.8% of participants reported being bothered by people smoking in their environment. Among non-smokers, 74.2% reported being bothered by others smoking, whereas 83.3% of smokers reported that they were not bothered. A statistically significant relationship was found between smoking status and being bothered by people smoking in the environment (χ² (1) = 117.596; p < 0.001).

Regarding the presence of smoking control in temporary shelter sites (e.g., designated smoking areas, smoke-free rules, warning signs, or supervision practices), only 12.2% of participants reported that such control existed, whereas 65.4% reported that no control was present. In container and tent settlements, participants also reported limited availability of appropriate smoking areas (Fig. 3).

Among the total study population, 25.1% (n = 114) were current smokers. Although pre-earthquake smoking prevalence could not be directly assessed because of the cross-sectional design, 51.8% of current smokers retrospectively reported increased cigarette consumption following the disaster, while 3.5% reported initiating smoking after the earthquake. More than half had been smoking for five years or more, and 67.5% reported consuming fewer than 20 cigarettes per day (Mean ± SD = 16.5 ± 11.1). In addition, 66.7% reported smoking to control post-disaster anxiety. Regarding psychological dependence levels, 67.5% of participants were classified as having moderate psychological dependence (Table 2).

A multivariable linear regression analysis was performed among current smokers (n = 114) to examine factors associated with psychological dependence on smoking. The overall model was statistically significant (F = 5.955, p < 0.001) and explained 25.0% of the variance in SAPDS scores (R = 0.50, R² = 0.25, adjusted R² = 0.21). Discussion

This study aimed to examine smoking after an earthquake by jointly addressing smoking prevalence, smoking-related environmental conditions in temporary shelter sites, and psychological dependence on smoking. The findings showed that one quarter of participants were current smokers and that more than half of current smokers retrospectively reported increased cigarette consumption following the disaster. The findings also suggest limited smoking-related environmental controls in temporary shelter sites, as participants frequently reported the absence of smoking regulations and designated smoking areas. When psychological dependence levels were examined, smoking to control post-disaster anxiety was independently associated with higher psychological dependence scores. Smoking to reduce stress or to calm down was associated with higher SAPDS scores in bivariate analyses but was not statistically significant in the multivariable model.

Smoking-related environmental conditions after the earthquake

Although evidence on active smoking after earthquakes is increasing [14, 15, 19, 55], studies directly addressing post-earthquake exposure to secondhand tobacco smoke (SHS) remain limited. In the present study, more than half of the participants reported being bothered by smoking in their environment, suggesting that SHS exposure is perceived as a significant public health concern during the post-disaster period. This perception appeared to differ according to smoking status, as most non-smokers reported being bothered, whereas a substantial proportion of smokers did not. In addition, most participants indicated an absence of smoking regulations and designated smoking areas in temporary shelter sites.

These findings are particularly relevant, as earthquake-affected areas are often associated with degraded environmental air quality, which may pose additional risks for respiratory health [56, 57]. In such settings, SHS may represent an additional and preventable health concern. Previous studies have also linked SHS exposure with various adverse health outcomes, particularly respiratory illnesses [58–61].

Our findings further suggest that smoking in disaster settings may be influenced by stress-related coping needs and potential disruptions in established social norms. One in three smokers reported using tobacco in enclosed spaces, and more than half believed that their smoking behavior did not negatively affect others. In crowded and communal shelter environments, such behaviors may be associated with higher levels of involuntary smoke exposure among non-smokers.

These results highlight the potential benefits of integrating tobacco-control measures into disaster preparedness and shelter management plans. Implementing smoke-free indoor policies, establishing designated outdoor smoking areas, providing routine monitoring of shelter smoking rules, and enhancing risk communication regarding SHS exposure may help reduce preventable health harms in temporary housing settings. Risk communication messages should emphasize the potential impacts of SHS, the protection of vulnerable groups, and the importance of compliance with smoke-free rules. Finally, the increases in cigarette consumption retrospectively reported by many smokers in this study may reflect a range of post-disaster challenges, including psychological distress, disrupted daily routines, environmental stressors, and broader psychosocial factors described in previous research [62]. Therefore, integrating preventive and regulatory tobacco-control approaches into disaster preparedness and response plans may help reduce smoking-related exposures and associated health risks in post-disaster settings [63].

Smoking characteristics of current smokers

The finding that approximately one quarter of participants were current smokers is broadly consistent with nationally reported adult smoking rates in Türkiye [7]. However, because pre-earthquake smoking prevalence was not directly assessed, comparisons with national smoking prevalence estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, the predominantly female and highly educated composition of the sample should be considered when interpreting these estimates, as they may not fully reflect the smoking patterns of the broader earthquake-affected population. More than half of current smokers retrospectively reported increased cigarette consumption following the disaster, and 3.5% reported initiating smoking after the earthquake. These findings may indicate that post-disaster changes in smoking behavior are more commonly reflected in increased consumption among existing smokers than in smoking initiation. Similar increases in tobacco use following disasters have been reported after bushfires and hurricanes [51, 64, 65].

Studies examining disasters and smoking in the Turkish context remain limited [14, 49, 66]. In a study conducted approximately two months after the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, more than half of smokers reported increased cigarette consumption, while only 6.2% reported reduced smoking [14]. By contrast, the proportion reporting reduced smoking was higher in our study (11.4%), which was conducted approximately 19 to 23 months after the earthquake. This finding may suggest that smoking reduction or cessation becomes more common during later stages of recovery and highlights the importance of maintaining smoking cessation support beyond the acute post-disaster period.

More than half of smoking participants reported economic or physical difficulties in accessing cigarettes after the earthquake. However, this factor was not independently associated with psychological dependence in the multivariable model, indicating that restricted access alone may not substantially influence dependence levels once other smoking-related and psychological factors are considered.

The present study also identified a moderate level of psychological dependence among smokers. In the multivariable model, higher daily cigarette consumption and smoking to control post-disaster anxiety were independently associated with higher psychological dependence scores. These findings suggest that post-disaster smoking behavior may be influenced not only by nicotine consumption patterns but also by anxiety-related coping needs. Previous studies have also highlighted the respiratory health burden of post-disaster conditions [66, 67] and the association of smoking with low psychological resilience and higher anxiety [49].

Recovery from post-disaster PTSD and related trauma symptoms may represent an important opportunity for smoking cessation interventions during the recovery period [68–75]. Therefore, smoking cessation interventions for disaster-affected populations should address both nicotine dependence and the psychological dimensions of tobacco use, particularly anxiety management. Integrating smoking cessation support with mental health and psychosocial services may be particularly beneficial during the recovery process.

To translate these findings into practical disaster-management actions, tobacco-control measures should be incorporated into temporary shelter planning and management. First, existing minimum standards for temporary shelter sites should be expanded to include formal smoke-free site planning principles. Designated smoking areas should be clearly identified and located away from residential units, health facilities, child-friendly spaces, and communal areas, while taking site topography, prevailing wind direction, and ventilation patterns into account to minimize smoke infiltration. Second, compliance with smoke-free regulations should be supported through routine monitoring and enforcement based on the provisions of Law No. 4207 on the Prevention and Control of Hazards of Tobacco Products [76], involving shelter administrators, local public health authorities, and disaster management agencies. Third, smoking cessation services should be integrated into post-disaster primary healthcare, mental health, and psychosocial support programs. Such services should be delivered by multidisciplinary teams, including public health specialists, family physicians certified in tobacco cessation, psychiatrists, psychologists, and social workers.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that smoking remains an important public health concern during the post-disaster recovery period. Although this cross-sectional study cannot determine causal changes in smoking prevalence, more than half of current smokers retrospectively reported increased cigarette consumption after the earthquake. Participants also reported limited smoking regulation and insufficient designated smoking areas in temporary shelter sites, indicating potential risks of secondhand smoke exposure in crowded post-disaster living environments. Furthermore, higher daily cigarette consumption and smoking to control post-disaster anxiety were associated with greater psychological dependence.

While these findings should not be interpreted as establishing direct causal links between shelter conditions and changes in smoking patterns, they highlight the need to integrate tobacco-control measures into disaster preparedness and shelter management, including smoke-free indoor rules, designated outdoor smoking areas, monitoring mechanisms, accessible cessation services, and psychosocial support throughout both the acute and recovery phases of disaster response. Disaster preparedness plans should include tobacco-control protocols as part of minimum shelter health standards.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, given its cross-sectional and exploratory design, causal inferences concerning the relationships among smoking, psychological dependence, and environmental factors cannot be drawn. Second, data collected via self-report are inherently susceptible to social desirability and recall biases. The reliance on an online survey, accessible primarily to literate individuals with internet access, may limit the sample’s representativeness; particularly older age groups, individuals with low socioeconomic status, and those with limited digital access may be underrepresented. Furthermore, the predominance of female and highly educated respondents may constrain the generalizability of the findings, especially given known demographic differences in smoking patterns in Türkiye. Third, the study was conducted some time after the earthquake, which limits our ability to directly observe changes in smoking behaviors specific to the acute phase of the disaster; consequently, these effects were assessed based on retrospective reports. Future studies should aim to address these limitations by employing longitudinal designs, utilizing face-to-face data-collection methods, and incorporating more granular measurements of long-term smoking histories.
