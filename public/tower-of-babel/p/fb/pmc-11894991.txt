Full genomic sequence characterization of the chikungunya virus from an imported case with serum viral concentration below culturable level
Authors: Mengling Jiang, Muchun Wan, Qinghong Fan, Yuyi Min, Guofang Tang, Yingfen Wen, Yaqing Lin, Ruiying He, Jiaojiao Li, Yue Tang, Yun Lan, Feng Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We identified that the imported Chikungunya fever case confirmed in Guangzhou, China, in January 2024 was caused by an Asia lineage chikungunya virus (CHIKV) infection directly originating from Timor-Leste.•Our study offers an alternative for tracing viral sources by obtaining the full-length genomic sequence of the virus from clinical samples with low viral loads, which is valuable for rapidly sequencing samples with low viral loads.•We provide a feasible and affordable alternative solution to analyze the origination of high-risk pathogens without a Biosafety Level-3 laboratory setting, which could be readily applied to most laboratories.Keywords: Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), Viral sequencing, Metatranscriptomic sequencing, Phylogenetic analysis

Introduction

Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) is a single-stranded, enveloped, positive-sense ribonucleic acid (RNA) virus classified under the Alphavirus genus within the Togaviridae family. CHIKV infection leads to an acute febrile illness known as chikungunya fever, characterized by the sudden onset of fever, often accompanied by severe joint pain, headache, muscle pain, joint swelling, or rash [1]. The potential severity of this disease, with a mortality rate ranging from 0.024 % to 0.800 %, and the heightened risk of severe outcomes in infants, elderly individuals, or those with underlying health conditions [2], emphasizes the significance of our research and the importance of our findings.

CHIKV was first identified in the Republic of Tanzania in 1952 and has since been reported in Africa, Asia, and the Indian Ocean islands. A significant outbreak occurred in Eastern Kenya in 2004, which subsequently spread to several Indian Ocean islands, India, and Southeast Asia. Since then, CHIKV outbreaks have become more frequent and widespread, with over 110 countries reporting CHIKV circulation [3]. The increasing frequency and global spread of these outbreaks, including a large-scale chikungunya fever epidemic in America in 2014 with over one million reported cases [4], and more than 10 million accumulated cases of chikungunya fever to date [4], underscores the growing threat of this disease. Unfortunately, no specific antiviral therapy for CHIKV infection is currently available, despite several preventive vaccines nearing authorization. Phylogenetic analysis has identified three distinct lineages corresponding to their geographical origins: West Africa, East / Central / South Africa (ECSA), and Asian lineages. Following the significant outbreak in eastern Kenya in 2004, the ECSA lineage gave rise to a new Indian Ocean lineage (IOL lineage), which adapted to the Aedes albopictus mosquito, thereby facilitating the global spread of CHIKV [5].

The geographical distribution of chikungunya fever is closely linked to the distribution of its vector, the Aedes mosquito. The Aedes albopictus mosquito is widely distributed in China, and its range is rapidly expanding due to accelerating urbanization, increased population movement, and a warming climate [6]. Similar to other mosquito-borne viruses such as dengue and Zika, the chikungunya virus presents a new challenge for future prevention and control efforts. Since the first imported case from Sri Lanka in 2008 [7], localized outbreaks resulting from imported cases have occurred in regions including Guangdong, Zhejiang, and Yunnan Province. The number of imported cases reported annually from 2016 to 2018 was 5, 14, and 8, respectively. This number surged to 62 cases in 2019, with most cases concentrated in Yunnan, Guangdong, and Zhejiang Province [7]. In September 2010, the first community cluster infections caused by imported cases occurred in Dongguan City, Guangdong Province, China, resulting in as many as 282 cases [8]. The southern regions of China, characterized by a subtropical monsoon climate with hot and humid weather, are particularly conducive to the transmission of chikungunya fever. Given China’s increasing global communication, facilitated by visa-free or visa-on-arrival policies, strict surveillance for imported CHIKV cases is essential.

In this study, we conducted a molecular epidemiological analysis of a chikungunya fever case imported into Guangdong Province, China, in January 2024. Our effort to promptly identify the characteristics of viral transmission provides a valuable strategy for surveilling imported cases.

Materials and methods

Study object

The subject of this study was a 53-year-old male suspected of having chikungunya fever, who was admitted to Guangzhou Eighth People’s Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University, on January 22, 2024. Epidemiological investigation and laboratory tests confirmed an imported CHIKV infection.

Nucleic acid extraction and Real-time polymerase chain reaction

The whole blood sample underwent viral RNA extraction within 4 h using a nucleic acid isolation kit (Da'an Gene Co. Ltd, China). Following this, reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed using the CHIKV viral nucleic acid detection kit (Jiangsu Macro & Micro-Test Med-Tech Co., Ltd, China). The RT-PCR targeted the CHIKV-specific conserved region and a host-specific reference gene. The reaction conditions adhered to the manufacturer's specifications, with the cycle threshold (Ct) detection limit set at 38. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and percentile methods were applied to determine the critical threshold value for kit detection. Samples with Ct values ≤38 were considered positive.

Metatranscriptomic library preparation and sequencing

The extracted 50 µL nucleic acid was initially digested with DNase I (37 °C, 20 min, MGI, China) to eliminate deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) from the human host and other microorganisms. Next, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was removed through probe hybridization, which involved annealing (95 °C to 22 °C, 0.1 °C/s), followed by RNase H (MGI, China) digestion of the rRNA (37 °C, 30 min) and a final DNase I digestion of the rRNA probes (37 °C, 30 min). This process effectively enriched the viral RNA.

The purified RNA was then reverse-transcribed using random primers, followed by second-strand synthesis. After end repair and adapter ligation, PCR amplification was conducted using adapter-specific primers as per the manufacturer's instructions (MGI, China). This unbiased amplification generated PCR products containing viral sequences. The purified products were then used to construct a library (MGI, China), followed by rolling circle amplification to create DNA nanoballs (DNBs). The DNB library was subsequently sequenced using PE100 on the MGI-SEQ2000 platform.

Metatranscriptomic sequencing analysis

Raw reads were mapped to the viral reference genome to extract viral sequences. Viral sequence alignment was carried out using BWA (version 0.7.17-r1188) with the command: bwa mem -t 25 CHIKV.ref.fa rawseq_read1.fq rawseq_read2.fq. The extracted viral sequences were then obtained using samtools (version 1.7) with the command: samtools view -F 4 -b -o CHIKV.bam rawseq bam. The viral sequences were assembled de novo using SPAdes (version v3.11.1) with the command: spades.py -1 CHIKV_read1.fq -2 CHIKV_read2.fq -o result.

MGI Metadata Analysis Software was subsequently used for raw data quality control, which included adapter removal and filtering of low-quality reads. The high-quality sequencing reads were then mapped to the CHIKV reference genome using “bwa mem” with default options [9]. Finally, FreeBayes software was employed for variant detection [10]. Mutation sites with a frequency greater than 40 % were retained for further mutation analysis.

Phylogenetic analysis

The sequence was aligned with complete genome references downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) using BioEdit software V7.0.9. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using the general time reversible and gamma substitution model with an ultrafast bootstrap value of 1,000 to assess the reliability of internal branches in IQtree software V1.6.12 [11]. The final trees were visualized using Figtree V1.4.2. A bootstrap value of 0.9 was selected as the threshold for identifying different genotypes or lineages in the ML phylogeny. Pairwise genetic distances were calculated from aligned sequences using Mega software V11.0.8 [12].

Data availability

The CHIKV consensus sequence generated and analyzed in this study has been deposited in the NCBI GenBank database (Accession number: pp798381), and is accessible at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/.

Results

Case presentation

The patient, a 53-year-old male who had recently returned from a business trip to Timor-Leste (near Indonesia), was admitted to Guangzhou Eighth People’s Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University, for confirmation of CHIKV infection by the local Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). He reported being bitten by mosquitoes during his stay aboard. On January 19, he developed a fever, which escalated to 39 °C when he arrived at China customs on January 20. On January 22, the Guangzhou CDC confirmed that his serum was positive for CHIKV viral RNA. Upon admission to our hospital on January 22, his body temperature was 36.2 °C. He presented with recurrent low-grade fever, fatigue, conjunctival congestion, and a dark red rash. Blood tests revealed normal white blood cell and platelet counts but showed reduced lymphocytes (1.01 × 109/L) and elevated levels of ALT (63.4 U/L), AST (52.2 U/L), and CK (750 U/L). RT-PCR detected CHIKV in the whole blood sample with a Ct value of 32.62. Influenza virus nucleic acid detection was negative. The patient received mosquito isolation and supportive treatment, and after nine days, he recovered, with the rash resolving.

Full-length genomic sequence of the chikungunya virus

Sequencing low viral-load samples directly from clinical specimens is challenging. Viral enrichment through cell culture, which was typically used to increase the viral concentration, was not feasible due to the biosafety regulations requiring CHIKV cultivation to be conducted in a Biosafety Level-3 (BSL-3) laboratory. We initially attempted to use the Sanger sequencing method to amplify full-length viral sequences, as previously published, but were unsuccessful. Additionally, routine metatranscriptomic sequencing yielded only a few reads (data not shown).

Subsequently, we increased the sequencing data to 336 GB with a Q30 (%) value of 96.05 by following a modified protocol that included DNAase I digestion, rRNA removal, and non-specific amplification [13]. This high-quality dataset enabled accurate variant calling and contributed to assembling the viral genomic structure. The raw reads were subjected to two rounds of alignment to assemble the full-length viral genome. First, the raw reads were aligned to the default reference sequence for CHIKV (NC_004162, S27-African prototype, NCBI database). The mapped reads were then extracted and used for de novo read assembly. After blasting the obtained consensus viral sequence against the NCBI database, we identified the JMB-172 (MT591092) viral sequence as the top hit with a 99.39 % sequence identity, while SZ1239 (MG664851) had the highest query coverage at 99 %. Since the JMB-172 strain is incomplete, we selected the SZ1239 strain as the reference genome. Next, the raw reads were realigned with the SZ1239 strain to extract all CHIKV viral sequences. The extracted viral sequences were performed using the MGI Metadata Analysis Software, which included quality control, genome assembly, and variation analysis (Fig. 1A). Ultimately, 6,622 paired reads of 100 bases in length were obtained for virus assembly and variant analysis, with an average depth of 54.3X, 98.12 % coverage at 10X depth, and 91.92 % coverage at 30X depth. The viral genome coverage was visualized using R 4.3.2 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The results were considered reliable after excluding regions with insufficient or slightly lower coverage at the termini. A total of 77 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites were identified between GZ01 and SZ1239 (Supplementary Table 1). This comprehensive approach provides a framework for analyzing virus sequences, which is crucial for understanding the genomic characteristics and variations in extremely low viral-load clinical samples.

Phylogenetic analysis

We designated the new CHIKV strain as GZ01/2024 and analyzed its evolutionary relationship with selected reference strains. Phylogenetic analysis showed that the GZ01/2024 strain belongs to the Asian genotype and forms a well-supported cluster (bootstrap value 100 %) with several reported isolates. These include MT591092 (nucleotide similarity 99.39 %), isolated from a patient in Indonesia in 2015 [14]; MG664851 (nucleotide similarity 99.35 %), from a Chinese patient in Shenzhen City who returned from Indonesia in 2012 [15]; DH130003 (nucleotide similarity 99.29 %), from an Indonesian patient in 2013; KF872195 (nucleotide similarity 99.27 %), from a Russian patient who returned from Indonesia in 2013; KY883764 (nucleotide similarity 99.25 %), from a patient in Singapore in 2013, OK316990 (nucleotide similarity 98.94 %), from a Chinese patient in Xishuangbanna Dai Autonomous Prefecture in 2019 [16], and OK655884 (nucleotide similarity 98.61 %), from an Indonesian patient in 2016 (Fig. 1B). These results support the conclusion that the imported CHIKV virus originated from Timor-Leste (a Southeast Asian country near Indonesia).

According to the phylogenetic tree’s topological structure, GZ01/2024 was closely related to the MT591092 sequence, which was isolated during the 2015 outbreak in Jambi, Sumatra, Indonesia. This strain is a part of the clade currently circulating in Indonesia [14]. Additionally, we observed that four of the seven sequences clustered with GZ01 in Fig. 1B were not native to Indonesia. KY883764 is also part of the clade currently circulating in Indonesia, as previously reported [14]; MG664851 and KF872195 were export cases from Indonesia, while OK316990 was a local case from Yunnan Province in China, not Indonesia [14]. Previous studies have indicated that Jambi is a significant source of CHIKV transmission to other regions of Indonesia.

Amino acid variation analysis

The CHIKV genome contains two primary open reading frames that encode four non-structural proteins (nsP1 – nsP4) and five structural proteins (C-E3-E2-6K-E1). We compared the GZ01 with seven closed Asian strains against the default NCBI reference strain S27(NC004162) from West Africa. The comparative analysis revealed 44 amino acid variations in the structural protein region common to all eight Asian strains, along with seven amino acid variations specific to the GZ01 strain (Fig. 1C). GZ01 exhibits mutations characteristic of the Asian lineage, such as the K211E mutation in the E1 protein and the I211T mutation in the E2 protein. Previous studies indicate that these mutations are adaptive for transmission by Aedes albopictus mosquitoes [2], [17]. GZ01 has seven specific mutations in the structural protein region that differ from those in other Asian strains, including C-Q7R, E2-H142Y, E2-S206L, E2-L210S, E2-K221R, E1-R196K, and E1-M333V. In the non-structural protein region, there are 56 common mutations among the Asian strains and five mutations specific to the GZ01 strain (Supplementary Table 2). The nsP1 and nsP2 proteins have I48V and P352S mutations, respectively. The nsP4 protein contains the K229R mutation, while nsP3 shows additional changes, including D15G and A364T. These findings offer valuable insights into the genetic variation of GZ01/2024, enhancing our understanding of CHIKV's evolution and its potential functional implications.

Discussion and conclusion

Our study offers an alternative approach for tracing viral sources by obtaining the full-length genomic sequence of the virus from clinical samples with low viral loads. Direct viral cultivation from clinical samples is technically challenging for most viruses. Specifically, CHIKV cultivation requires a BSL-3 setting, which is unavailable in many laboratories. First, we successfully obtained the full-length CHIKV sequence from a serum sample with an extremely low viral load (PCR Ct = 32.62) by increasing the sequencing depth using metatranscriptomic methods, generating 336 GB of data. Second, we developed a time-efficient read mapping pipeline to assemble the full-length sequence from this large volume of sequencing data. Given the minimal percentage of CHIKV viral reads, directly aligning all reads to multiple reference sequences to identify the closest viral strains is time-consuming and could take weeks. We combined de novo assembly with reference-based assembly. We aligned the original data to the NCBI default CHIKV reference to identify CHIKV-like reads for de novo assembly. The consensus sequence from de novo assembly was then used to find the nearest reference sequence in the NCBI database using Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). The reads mapping exactly to CHIKV were further assembled with reference to obtain the accurate genome sequence and identify SNPs. The entire analysis process can be completed within 12 h. This method represents a significant advancement in genome epidemiology research and enhances the laboratory’s capability for virus traceability, thereby improving public health efforts.

The viral glycoprotein E2 is crucial for the receptor-mediated entry of CHIKV the virus into host cells [18]. We identified four amino acid mutations in E2 that may impact the viral ability to invade host cells. The E2-L210Q mutation has been shown to facilitate more efficient initial CHIKV infection of midgut cells in Aedes albopictus[19]. The GZ01 strain has an E2-L210S mutation, which may alter the initial viral infection process. The I211T mutation in E2 affects regions critical for antibody binding and recognition, potentially allowing the virus to evade the immune system and enhance transmission. Additionally, the E1-K211E mutation is linked to improved viral adaptation in Aedes aegypti [20]. The nsP3 protein of CHIKV is involved in binding host cellular proteins, playing a significant role in interactions with the host [21]. Mutations in nsp3 may impact its ability to bind with host factors.

Chikungunya fever shares symptoms with other arbovirus infections, such as dengue and Zika, and typically has a short duration of illness. Therefore, acquiring clinical samples promptly during the symptomatic phase is crucial. We have developed a comprehensive method for sequencing and analyzing clinical samples with extremely low virus concentrations. Evidence suggests that CHIKV has been present in Timor-Leste since its independence in 2002. However, due to limited laboratory testing, its prevalence remained undefined until the first reported case in November 2023 [22]. The viral sequences and variants identified in our study are essential for understanding the circulating CHIKV strains in Timor-Leste. In the absence of specific antiviral treatments and with ongoing vaccine development, tracing the virus and controlling its vector is crucial for preventing recurrence and spread, especially for highly contagious diseases. Our study provides an effective approach for quickly obtaining full-length sequences of pathogenic viruses without requiring a BSL-3 laboratory. A limitation of this study is the significant demand for sequencing data, which results in high costs and makes it unsuitable for large-scale sample analyses.
