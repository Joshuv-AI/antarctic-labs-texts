Mutation of STAY-GREEN 1 in tomato increases volatile organic compounds during fruit ripening
Authors: Jae-In Chun, Ga-Hae Han, Seong-Yeop Kim, Seong-Min Kim, Gab-Jung Kim, Bokyeong Kim, Jae Kwang Kim, Jwa Yeong Cho, Ho-Youn Kim, Jin-Ho Kang
Published: 2026

Tomato breeding has traditionally prioritized agronomic traits, such as yield and pest resistance, thereby inadvertently reducing volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and negatively affecting flavor perception (Colantonio et al. 2022). Advances in VOC chemistry and biosynthetic gene identification have spurred efforts to restore tomato flavor (Garbowicz et al. 2018, Martina et al. 2021, Villena et al. 2023). However, the mechanisms influencing VOC synthesis remain largely unexplored.

STAY-GREEN 1 (SGR1) regulates chlorophyll degradation in the tomato (Hu et al. 2011). Although SGR1 loss improves fruit traits, including carotenoid content, antioxidants, and shelf life (Luo et al. 2013, Kim et al. 2022, Kim et al. 2024), its influence on VOCs remains unknown. To address this, we used CRISPR-Cas9 to generate SlSGR1 (Solyc08g080090) knockout (sgr1) mutants in the “Ailsa Craig” cultivar, a widely used model for tomato fruit research. Eight T₀ lines with biallelic or heterozygous mutations were obtained (Supplementary Fig. S1), and two T₂ homozygous lines (ko-1 and ko-14) with a 1-bp insertion in exon 1 (A and T, respectively), but no T-DNA integration, were selected. These mutations caused a frameshift and introduced a premature stop codon, which is predicted to produce a truncated 12-amino-acid fragment instead of the full-length 272-amino acid protein (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. S2). These lines showed a 30%–55% reduced SlSGR1 expression during fruit ripening (T–R stages) compared with that in the wild type (WT) (Supplementary Fig. S3). The mutants exhibited normal growth but delayed leaf senescence (Supplementary Fig. S4). To assess protein function, WT and mutant SGR1 were fused to a Flag tag and expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana. WT:Flag was detected and triggered senescence, whereas mutant:Flag was undetectable and caused no response (Supplementary Fig. S5), confirming sgr1 as a null mutation.

Leaf phenotype assessments and spectrophotometry revealed that sgr1 lines retained chlorophyll across young, mature, and old leaves until 11 weeks, whereas chlorophyll was fully degraded in the old leaves of the WT by this stage (Supplementary Fig. S6). Similarly, sgr1 knockout fruits maintained higher chlorophyll levels than those in the WT throughout development, with a 5-fold increase after the O stage (Fig. 1b), indicating delayed chlorophyll degradation in both leaves and fruits. VOCs were analyzed across six ripening stages in WT and sgr1 knockout fruits, leading to the identification of 31 distinct VOCs. These compounds were categorized into four groups based on their biosynthetic origins: carotenoid-, fatty acid (FA)-, phenylalanine-, and branched-chain amino acid-derived VOCs (Supplementary Table S2). Principal component analysis of the VOCs indicated that WT fruits clustered by developmental stages, while sgr1 knockout fruits were distinct from the WT at each corresponding stage. Notably, sgr1 knockout fruits at the OR stage differed significantly from WT fruits at all ripening stages (Fig. 1c). Moreover, a heatmap analysis demonstrated that the majority of VOCs accumulated more in sgr1 knockout fruits than in WT fruits during ripening (Supplementary Fig. S7). Carotenoid- and FA-derived VOCs increased markedly in the knockout fruits, peaking at the OR stage (Fig. 1d). These findings provide the first evidence of SlSGR1’s essential role in affecting VOC production in tomato fruits.

To determine the cause of increased VOC accumulation in sgr1 knockout fruits, we analyzed gene expression related to carotenoid- and FA-derived VOCs. For genes involved in carotenoid synthesis, sgr1 knockout fruits exhibited elevated expression of phytoene synthase 1 (PSY1) and lycopene-β-cyclase (CYC-B), while cleavage dioxygenase 1B (CCD1B) remained unchanged, and CCD1A was reduced at most ripening stages, likely due to feedback regulation (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. S8a). For FA-derived VOCs, among lipase 1 (LIP1) and LIP8, which contribute to FA production—precursors of FA-derived VOCs—the expression of LIP1 (Garbowicz et al. 2018) was substantially higher in sgr1 knockout fruits at the O and R stages than in WT fruits. However, the expression of LIP8 and other downstream genes did not differ between sgr1 knockout and WT fruits (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. S8b). We further investigated whether increased PSY and CYC-B expression affected carotenoid levels and found that sgr1 knockout fruits accumulated higher levels of lycopene, β-carotene, and lutein than WT fruits at the R and OR stages (Fig. 1f). Additionally, we measured the FA content to assess whether increased LIP1 expression boosted FA production. The levels of 18:0, 18:1, and 18:2 FA forms were higher in sgr1 knockout fruits than in the WT, particularly at the OR stage (Fig. 1f), suggesting that SlSGR1 deficiency leads to altered expression of multiple genes involved in the synthesis of substrates for carotenoid- and FA-derived VOC production. Previous studies have shown that tomato mutants with more plastids, like hp1 and hp2, produce more chlorophyll and carotenoids, which enhances the levels of fatty acid-derived VOCs (Kovács et al. 2009). Similarly, our study found that sgr1 knockout fruits have more chloroplasts than WT fruits throughout all ripening stages (Fig. 1g). From the orange stage onward, sgr1 fruits also exhibited faster chloroplast-to-chromoplast conversion (Fig. 1g). This suggests that the increased plastid number and accelerated differentiation in sgr1 fruits contribute to their higher levels of carotenoids, fatty acids, and related VOCs. Future research should elucidate the precise mechanism by which SGR1 deficiency affects chloroplast proliferation.

Improving flavor, a key factor in increasing tomato appeal to consumers, represents a significant challenge in plant breeding. This work provides direct evidence of the pivotal role of SlSGR1 in the production of VOCs, particularly carotenoid- and FA-derived VOCs. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the increased VOCs result from changes in the expression of genes involved in VOC synthesis, which enhances precursor production. These findings provide valuable insights into the influence of SlSGR1 on VOC synthesis and establish a crucial foundation for leveraging SlSGR1 to enhance tomato flavor and overall fruit quality, contributing to the development of high-value tomato varieties.
