Association of PM2.5 Exposure on Birthweight: Racial and Ethnic Disparities
Authors: Wei‐Jen Chen, Elaine Symanski, Kristina W. Whitworth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe few studies that have examined the impact of PM2.5 on reduced birthweight across different percentiles of the conditional birthweight distribution have produced equivocal findings, and only two assessed whether these associations varied by race/ethnicity or by racial/ethnic composition of the neighbourhood where mothers lived.ObjectiveWe evaluated racial/ethnic differences in the association between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and birthweight across the birthweight distribution in a retrospective cohort study comprising 102,986 full‐term singleton births in Harris County, Texas (2019–2020).MethodsCensus tract‐level daily PM2.5 concentrations were estimated using ‘XGBoost‐IDW Synthesis’, and averaged exposures over pregnancy. Neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition was defined by whether ≥ 50% (i.e., majority) of non‐Hispanic white residents lived in a census tract. Quantile regression models, adjusted for covariates, were applied to examine changes in birthweight [β^ and 95% confidence interval (CI)] associated with an interquartile range increase in ambient air levels of PM2.5 at selected percentiles of the conditional birthweight distribution. Stratified analyses explored differential associations by maternal race/ethnicity and neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition.ResultsAn inverted hook pattern was observed in the associations between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and reduced birthweight, with the strongest among infants born at the lowest (β^ = −14 g, 95% CI: −20, −8; 10th percentile) and highest (β^ = −11 g, 95% CI: −19, −4; 90th percentile) percentiles of the birthweight distribution, and a weaker association at the 75th percentile. In stratified analyses, the strongest association at the lowest percentile was observed among infants of Hispanic mothers or those living in neighbourhoods with less than a majority of non‐Hispanic white residents.ConclusionsThis study provides evidence that associations of prenatal PM2.5 exposure with reductions in birthweight varied among infants at the lowest, middle and highest percentiles of the conditional birthweight distribution; further, these associations varied by maternal race/ethnicity and neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition.

Background

Exposure to air pollution is associated with chronic stress during pregnancy and racial/ethnic perinatal health disparities [1, 2]. Particles less than 2.5 μm in diameter (fine particulate matter, PM2.5) are particularly pathogenic due to their large surface area, which can bind toxic compounds and readily cross the respiratory barrier and placenta [3]. Prenatal exposure to PM2.5 may increase oxidative stress and inflammation in intrauterine environments, disrupting placental function and delaying foetal growth [4]. While the epidemiological evidence supports associations between PM2.5 exposure and reduced mean birthweight, two recent meta‐analyses [5, 6] point to the need for additional large‐scale studies due to high heterogeneity observed among existing research. In addition, people of colour and those living in socioeconomically disadvantaged communities bear the most significant burdens from environmental stressors [7]. For example, two systematic reviews [8, 9] highlight that the association between PM2.5 exposure and low birthweight has primarily been observed among infants born to non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic mothers, based largely on studies conducted along the East and West Coasts of the United States (US).

Few studies have examined the varying effects of prenatal PM2.5 exposure across the birthweight distribution despite observations that infants in the lower and upper tails may be particularly vulnerable [10]. Quantile regression, which models a continuous outcome with a heterogeneous conditional distribution [11, 12], can capture differential impacts of PM2.5 on birthweight across the distribution, in contrast to traditional mean regression, which assumes a uniform effect. While some studies found stronger associations of PM2.5 on reduced birthweight among infants born in the lowest percentiles [13, 14, 15], others did not [16, 17]. Given disparities in pregnancy outcomes [8, 9], a better understanding of racial/ethnic differences in the impact of PM2.5 exposure on infants of varying birthweight is needed. A study in California [14] observed stronger associations between PM2.5 and reduced birthweight in the lower tails among infants of non‐Hispanic Black mothers compared to non‐Hispanic white or Hispanic mothers, whereas a study in Atlanta, GA [17] found no racial differences. Neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition, often linked to racial segregation [18], has drawn interest in perinatal epidemiology. However, in the only study that evaluated the impact of racial composition (based on census block group‐level proportions of Black residents) across the birthweight distribution, no differences were found [15].

Building on prior studies of particle air pollution and birth outcomes in Harris County, Texas—an area with multiple PM2.5 sources, including a large petrochemical industry and dense network of roadways [19, 20]—we aimed to evaluate the potential differential association between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and birthweight at selected percentiles of the conditional birthweight distribution. Given documented racial/ethnic perinatal health disparities in this area [7], we also examined both individual‐ and neighbourhood‐level racial/ethnic disparities in this association.

Methods

Cohort Selection

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using live birth records obtained from the Texas Department of State Health Services (TX DSHS), representing singleton births with no known foetal anomalies to mothers in Harris County, Texas, which occurred between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2020. The study period was based on the availability of the PM2.5 exposure data. A total of 126,570 births with geocoded addresses were initially available for inclusion in the present analysis (Figure S1). We excluded records that were missing for gestational age, gestational ages outside the 20–44 week range or birthweight outside the valid range for each gestational age [21]. Further, in line with existing studies [14, 15, 17], we restricted records to full term births to focus on the impact of exposure on birthweight independent of preterm birth status [22]. Additionally, we excluded records in which race/ethnicity was reported by the TX DSHS as ‘Other or Unknown’, a single heterogeneous category encompassing women who self‐identified as Asian, Native Hawaiian, Pacific Islander, American Indian, Alaska Native, non‐Hispanic multiple races or whose race/ethnicity was unknown. After these exclusions, we retained 102,986 birth records eligible for inclusion in this study.

Exposure

We obtained daily PM2.5 (μg/m3) data estimated by XGBoost‐IDW Synthesis (XIS) from 2018 to 2020 at the population centres in 1115 census tracts of Harris County (based on the 2020 US Census). XIS, developed by Just et al. [23], is a spatially adapted machine‐learning framework based on extreme gradient boosting and inverse‐distance weighting. XIS integrated the satellite‐based aerosol optical depth, along with meteorological, topographical and land use information, and makes point‐based predictions by incorporating both raster data and continuous fields (e.g., learning near‐roadway gradients) instead of relying on a gridded space. The methodology of XIS has previously been applied to reconstruct highly spatiotemporally resolved concentrations of PM2.5 at the centroids of all census tracts covering the contiguous US [23] XIS showed strong predictive performance in 10‐fold site‐wise cross‐validation stratified by year and climate region, with the mean absolute error to baseline variability consistently lower than the corresponding mean absolute deviation from the median across regions and years [23]. We linked each woman's geocoded address at delivery to the appropriate time series of census tract‐level daily PM2.5 concentrations and averaged her exposures during gestation (from conception to delivery) and for specific trimester periods: the first (gestational Weeks 1–13), second (gestational Weeks 14–27) and third (gestation Week 28 to delivery) trimesters.

Maternal race/ethnicity was evaluated as non‐Hispanic white, non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic in birth records. To evaluate neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition, we used census tracts as an approximation of neighbourhoods [24], and, using census tract‐level 2020 population composition data from the American Community Survey [25], calculated the proportion of non‐Hispanic white residents in each census tract by dividing their number by the total population [24]. This variable was then dichotomised at a 50% threshold to classify neighbourhoods as having either less than a majority or a majority of non‐Hispanic white residents [26].

Outcome

We obtained information on infant birthweight (g) and the clinical estimate of gestational age (in weeks) from the birth records.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Descriptive analyses provided summaries of maternal and infant characteristics and PM2.5 concentrations. Quantile regression was used to examine the association of average pregnancy PM2.5 exposure with change in birthweight at the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentiles of the conditional birthweight distribution. The estimated regression coefficients (β^) represent the change in birthweight associated with an interquartile range (IQR) increase in average gestational PM2.5 concentrations among infants born at a given percentile of the conditional birthweight distribution. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated through a bootstrap resampling method (500 samples) using the QUANTREG procedure in SAS [11]. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was created to guide the inclusion of confounders and variables predicting birthweight (Figure S2). Based on the DAG, maternal race/ethnicity, education level, nativity status, neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition and season of conception were included in the models. The following predictors of the outcome were also included in the models: maternal age and infant sex. We additionally constructed quantile regression models stratified by maternal race/ethnicity (non‐Hispanic white, non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic) as well as by neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition (< 50% or ≥ 50% of non‐Hispanic white residents) to explore whether the effects of PM2.5 on birthweight varied across these groups.

Missing Data

Maternal education level and nativity status were missing for 0.2% of women, while maternal age, smoking and neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition were missing for less than 0.1% (Table 1). A total of 102,520 full‐term births with complete covariate data were retained for analyses.

Sensitivity Analysis

Given that smoking is a risk factor for low birthweight [27], we conducted a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of including smoking status in the regression models. To examine potential sex differences in response to air pollution exposure [28], we performed a quantile regression model stratified by infant sex. We also conducted an analysis evaluating trimester‐specific exposures by constructing a quantile regression model that mutually adjusted for exposure during each trimester [29].

Results

The maternal and infant characteristics of study subjects included in the present analysis are summarised in Table 1. Overall, more than half of the mothers were aged less than 30 years. The majority of mothers were Hispanic, followed by non‐Hispanic white and non‐Hispanic Black. Most mothers had an education level of high school or less and were US‐born. There were some differences in maternal characteristics according to the birthweight percentiles of newborns (Table S1). Compared with mothers whose infants were born in the highest birthweight percentile (≥ 90th), those whose infants were born in the lowest percentile (< 10th) were more likely to be younger, non‐Hispanic Black, less educated, US‐born, have hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, give birth to a female newborn and live in neighbourhoods where non‐Hispanic white residents were not the majority.

The associations between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and birthweight in different birthweight percentiles are shown in Figure 1 (see corresponding effect estimates and 95% CIs in Table S2). The reduction in birthweight associated with prenatal PM2.5 exposure decreased from the 10th to the 75th birthweight percentiles and then increased at the 90th percentile, indicating stronger associations in both the lower and upper tails of the distribution. Specifically, at the 10th percentile of the conditional birthweight distribution, we observed the strongest association, with an IQR (i.e., 1.08 μg/m3) increase in average PM2.5 exposure during pregnancy on birthweight (β^ = −14 g, 95% CI: −20, −8). Additionally, an 11 g reduction in birthweight was observed at the 90th percentile (95% CI: −19, −4).

The race/ethnicity‐specific associations between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and birthweight among infants born at different percentiles of the conditional birthweight distribution are shown in Figure 2. Racial/ethnic differences were evident when compared to the overall association. Among infants born to non‐Hispanic white mothers, negative associations were observed across the entire conditional birthweight distribution, with the strongest association at the 90th percentile. In contrast, for infants born to non‐Hispanic Black mothers, negative associations of PM2.5 exposure were only observed at the lower percentiles, particularly at the 25th percentile. Among infants born to Hispanic mothers, the negative associations were strongest at the 10th percentile, gradually attenuated through the 75th percentile, and increased again at the 90th percentile, showing a pattern similar to that observed for all mothers combined.

We also observed differences in the association between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and birthweight according to the neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition (Figure 3). Among infants born to mothers living in neighbourhoods with less than 50% of non‐Hispanic white residents, we observed a pattern of negative associations similar to the overall association, with the strongest associations among infants born at the lowest and highest percentiles. This pattern was not observed among those living in neighbourhoods with a majority of non‐Hispanic white residents. Rather, the strongest associations were observed among infants at the highest (but not the lowest) percentile.

In our sensitivity analysis, the results were unchanged after additionally adjusting for maternal smoking status (Figure S3). Additionally, in evaluating trimester‐specific PM2.5 exposures (Figure S4), the associations for second trimester exposure generally followed a similar pattern to that observed in the main analysis based on average pregnancy exposure, although the magnitudes were smaller. Finally, we observed similar patterns of association between PM2.5 exposures and birthweight among male and female infants when compared to the main analysis (Figure S5).

Comment

Principal Findings

The present study adds to the limited evidence regarding variability in the associations between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and reductions in birthweight across the birthweight distribution. We observed the most pronounced reduction in birthweight among infants born at the 10th percentile of the conditional birthweight distribution; however, a reduction was also evident among infants born at the 90th percentile. Differences in association were observed by both individual‐ and neighbourhood‐level race/ethnicity. For non‐Hispanic white mothers, the greatest reduction in birthweight was observed among infants born at the highest percentile, while for non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic mothers, as well as for mothers who lived in neighbourhoods with less than a majority of non‐Hispanic white residents, the strongest negative associations were observed at the lower percentiles of birthweight.

Strengths of the Study

A strength of this study is the use of a racially and ethnically diverse birth cohort from the third most populous county in the United States (i.e., Harris County, Texas), which allowed us to explore racial/ethnic differences and contribute to the literature on understanding perinatal health disparities in the Gulf Coast region. In addition, our exposure assessment provided highly spatiotemporally resolved PM2.5 estimates, addressing a major limitation in previous quantile regression‐based studies on PM2.5 and birthweight that relied on sparse data from stationary monitoring networks.

Limitations of the Data

The PM2.5 exposure data in our study were only available at the census tract level; thus, we could not capture within‐tract variability. Nevertheless, a prior study in Texas found that residential mobility during pregnancy generally took place over short distances and did not significantly affect air pollution assessment [30]. Second, our study did not consider co‐exposures to other air pollutants or particle composition, as available monitoring data are limited in terms of their spatial and temporal resolution, and modelled data are not yet available. Third, our study was limited to 2 years of data from a single county (Harris) in Texas, which limits the generalisability of our findings. However, Harris County includes Houston—the nation's fourth largest and most diverse city—with unique exposure profiles from dense roadways, heavy industries, and a major seaport and inferences are focused on mothers living in this area. Fourth, our study was inherently conditioned on live birth due to the use of birth records [31]. Thus, the depletion of foetuses more susceptible to air pollution exposure may underestimate disparities in birthweight, particularly among non‐Hispanic Black mothers [32]. Lastly, as with other studies relying on administrative databases, our study was based on birth records and could not capture maternal behavioural factors that may influence exposure or birth outcomes, including time‐activity patterns and other potential changes related to the COVID‐19 pandemic. However, research has pointed out that using ambient air pollution concentrations as a proxy for personal exposure may help avoid confounding by individual behavioural differences [33].

Interpretation

Identifying quantile‐specific effects of environmental exposure on perinatal outcomes can help identify vulnerable populations [34]. However, only a handful of studies have applied this methodology to investigate associations between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and birthweight, and findings have been mixed. Two US‐based retrospective birth cohort studies from Massachusetts [15] and California [14] observed negative associations among infants across the entire birthweight distribution with the largest reduction in birthweight among infants at the lowest percentiles of the distribution. In contrast, associations between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and decreased birthweight were reported only among infants at the lowest birthweight percentiles in a multi‐city study in China [13] whereas a study in Atlanta, GA [17] observed associations only among infants in the upper percentiles. Additionally, a study in South Korea [16] found a negative association only among infants born in the middle percentile (i.e., 50th) of the birthweight distribution. In our study, we observed an inverted hook pattern in associations with greater reductions in birthweight at the lower (10th) and upper (90th) percentiles as compared to the 75th percentile.

Differences in results reported by studies that applied quantile regression to examine prenatal PM2.5 exposure and birthweight may be attributed to differences in the target populations, in levels and chemical composition of ambient PM2.5, and in exposure assessment approaches. Prenatal PM2.5 exposures in studies from China [13] and South Korea [16] were relatively higher than in US‐based studies [14, 15, 17], including the present study. Also, all prior studies, except for the one by Fong et al. [15], assessed PM2.5 exposure based on measurements from monitoring stations, which had limited spatial coverage in the study locations. Furthermore, our study restricted the population to full‐term births, to minimise the influence of gestational age and enable a clearer comparison of foetal growth [22], consistent with prior studies that found the greatest association of PM2.5 on birthweight reduction in the lowest percentile of the conditional birthweight distribution [14, 15].

Disparities in the associations of air pollution exposure with reduced birthweight may be attributed to the physiological manifestations of chronic stress differentially experienced by women of colour. For example, elevated inflammatory states [35] and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activation [36] may impair placental function and influence foetal growth. Our study observed negative associations between PM2.5 exposure and birthweight at the lower percentiles of the conditional birthweight distribution among infants born to non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic mothers; among infants born to non‐Hispanic white mothers, associations were observed across the distribution, with the strongest association at the 90th percentile. These findings differ from a California study [14], which reported larger reductions in birthweight among infants of non‐Hispanic Black mothers at the lowest percentile compared to non‐Hispanic white and Hispanic mothers. However, an Atlanta study found no differences in associations between Black versus non‐Black mothers [37]. Relative to larger infants, a modest reduction in birthweight among smaller infants may have clinically meaningful effects, given the significantly high rates of neonatal mortality observed in smaller infants [17, 22]. Apart from individual‐level race/ethnicity, the racial/ethnic composition of neighbourhoods where mothers live has also been associated with perinatal health disparities [38, 39]. Minoritized communities are often characterised by historical neighbourhood disinvestment, including housing discrimination, segregation, limited access to medical care, and environmental and criminal injustice [18, 40]. Yet, few studies have evaluated potential differences in the association between air pollution and birthweight by neighbourhood racial/ethnic composition. Compared with the overall results, the association between PM2.5 exposure and birthweight among infants born in the lowest birthweight percentile was only apparent among infants born to mothers living in census tracts where less than a majority of the residents were non‐Hispanic white.

Conclusions

In summary, our study of full term singleton live births in Harris County between 2019 and 2020 provides evidence that associations between prenatal PM2.5 exposure and reduced birthweight vary across the birthweight distribution. Disparities in these associations were also observed among different racial and ethnic groups at the lower tail of the birthweight distribution, particularly among infants born to Hispanic mothers, as well as among mothers living in neighbourhoods with less than a majority of non‐Hispanic white residents. Future research may benefit from considering these varying effects when evaluating the impact of environmental factors on perinatal health disparities.
