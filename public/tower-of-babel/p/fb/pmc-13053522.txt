Associations between dietary macronutrient composition and kidney stone prevalence in the U.S. adult population
Authors: Xijie Ding, Zhichao Chi, Ziyang Qiang, Xi Zhang, Jianxing Li, Guojun Chen, Chaoyue Ji, Weiguo Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe relationship between macronutrient intake and kidney stone formation remains unclear, with previous studies yielding inconsistent findings due to their focus on single nutrients. A more integrated dietary assessment may provide a clearer understanding of dietary contributions to lithogenic risk. To investigate the associations between the energy distribution of dietary macronutrients (protein, carbohydrate, and fat) and the prevalence of kidney stones among U.S. adults. This cross-sectional study included 24,235 adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018. Macronutrient intake was assessed as a percentage of total energy based on 24-hour dietary recall data. kidney stone prevalence was determined via self-reported history. Multivariable logistic regression models and restricted cubic spline analyses were used to evaluate the nonlinear relationships between macronutrient energy proportions and kidney stone prevalence, adjusting for relevant demographic and clinical covariates. A significant U-shaped association was observed between protein intake and kidney stone prevalence, with the lowest prevalence at approximately 23% of total energy. Carbohydrate intake showed a similar U-shaped pattern, with the lowest prevalence at 40–50% of energy. Fat intake demonstrated a reverse L-shaped association; the prevalence remained stable when fat comprised < 35% of energy but increased notably above this threshold. In fully adjusted models, protein intake of 15–30% of energy was associated with a lower odds of kidney stones (OR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.73–0.92), and fat intake > 40% was associated with a higher odds (OR: 1.12; 95% CI: 1.01–1.26). Our findings indicate that both insufficient and excessive macronutrient intake are associated with higher kidney stone prevalence. A balanced macronutrient profile—with moderate proportions of protein, carbohydrate, and fat—may be beneficial for kidney stone prevention and could help inform dietary recommendations. Introduction

Urolithiasis is a common and increasingly prevalent urological disorder worldwide [1]. In the United States, approximately 10.1% of adults are affected by kidney stones[2], posing a significant burden on healthcare systems, with annual treatment costs exceeding $2 billion [3]. The pathogenesis of urolithiasis constitutes a multifactorial process, associated with geographical variations, metabolic derangements, genetic susceptibilities, and anatomical-functional abnormalities [4]. Dietary factors, particularly the intake of macronutrients such as carbohydrates, proteins, and fats, are thought to modulate urinary biochemistry by influencing factors like urinary pH, citrate excretion, and supersaturation of stone-forming solutes [5–7]. These effects suggest that dietary macronutrient composition may play a key role in stone pathogenesis [8,9].

However, most existing studies have assessed individual macronutrients in isolation, leading to inconsistent or contradictory results. For example, the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines recommend limiting protein intake to 0.8–1.0 g/kg of body weight. Consistent with the concept that both insufficient and excessive protein intake may be unfavorable, Basiri et al [10]. reported a U-shaped correlation between regional total protein intake and urolithiasis incidence in Iran, with the lowest stone incidence observed at approximately 70–80 g/day of protein intake, whereas both lower and higher intake levels were associated with increased incidence. Excessive protein consumption may lead to hypocitraturia, low urinary pH, hyperoxaluria, and hyperuricosuria, all of which contribute to an increased risk of stone formation [11]. However, a 2023 systematic review that included nine cohort studies concluded that high protein intake is not specifically associated with an increased risk of kidney stone formation [12]. In studies investigating the relationship between fat intake and kidney stone formation, some have reported higher fat consumption among stone formers [13], whereas others have found no significant association between fat intake and stone risk [14]. Similarly, the impact of carbohydrate intake on the risk of kidney stone formation remains inconclusive. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis suggested that increased carbohydrate consumption may alter urinary excretion of calcium, oxalate, and pH, as well as affect uric acid metabolism, thereby potentially increasing the risk of stone formation [15]. However, other studies have reported comparable carbohydrate intake levels between stone formers and healthy controls [14].

Given the conflicting evidence surrounding the role of individual macronutrients in kidney stone formation, there is a growing need to move beyond isolated nutrient analyses [16]. In this study, we adopted a compositional approach that evaluates the proportional contribution of each macronutrient—carbohydrates, proteins, and fats—to total energy intake, rather than focusing solely on absolute amounts [17,18]. Using nationally representative data from U.S. adults, we employed advanced statistical modeling, including restricted cubic spline regression, to investigate the nonlinear associations between dietary macronutrient energy distribution and kidney stone prevalence [18]. This integrated analytical framework allowed us to better capture the complex, real-world interactions between diet composition and lithogenic risk, offering more nuanced insights into the dietary determinants of urolithiasis.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), an ongoing, nationally representative, cross-sectional survey designed to evaluate the nutritional and health status of the U.S. population. NHANES employs a complex, stratified, multistage probability sampling design to ensure national representativeness. Publicly available NHANES data and methodological documentation are accessible at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes. All participants provided written informed consent, and the NHANES protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board (Continuation of Protocol #2005–06, Protocol #2011–17, Continuation of Protocol #2011–17, and Protocol #2018-01).

For this analysis, we combined data from six consecutive NHANES cycles spanning 2007 to 2018. A total of 59,842 participants were initially identified. Participants were excluded if they were under 20 years of age, or if they lacked information on kidney stone history, dietary macronutrient intake, or relevant covariates. To improve data quality and minimize the impact of dietary misreporting, we excluded participants with implausible total energy intake (< 800 or > 4200 kcal/day for men; <600 or > 3500 kcal/day for women). Additionally, participants whose absolute intake of protein, fat, or carbohydrates exceeded three standard deviations above or below the population mean were excluded from the analysis to reduce the influence of implausible dietary reports [19]. After these exclusions, 24,235 adult participants were retained for the final analysis. A detailed flowchart of the participant selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Assessment of kidney stone prevalence

The prevalence of kidney stones was determined based on self-reported responses to questionnaire item KIQ026, which asked, “Have you ever had kidney stones?” Participants who answered “yes” were classified as having kidney stones. This variable served as the primary outcome in the present analysis [20].

Assessment of macronutrient intake

Dietary intake data were obtained through two nonconsecutive 24-hour dietary recall interviews administered by trained personnel as part of the NHANES dietary assessment component. The first recall was conducted in person at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC), and the second was completed via telephone 3 to 10 days later. Participants reported all food and beverages consumed in the previous 24 h. To minimize the potential bias introduced by differences in dietary patterns between weekdays and weekends, only recalls conducted on weekdays were included in the analysis. If both recalls occurred on weekdays, the first-day recall was selected. Energy and macronutrient intake—including total carbohydrates, protein, and fat—were estimated using the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS). For participants with two valid weekday recalls, the average intake was used; if only one weekday recall was available, the corresponding value was used. Total energy intake was calculated using standard energy conversion factors: 4 kcal per gram for carbohydrates and protein, and 9 kcal per gram for fat.

Assessment of covariates

A range of demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical variables was included in the multivariable models to control for potential confounding. Age (years), sex (male/female), and race/ethnicity (categorized as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, and other) were obtained from self-reported interview data. Socioeconomic status was assessed using the family income-to-poverty ratio (PIR) and educational attainment, which was grouped into three categories: less than high school, high school graduate (or equivalent), and more than high school.

Body mass index (BMI, kg/m²) was calculated using measured height and weight. Smoking status was defined as current smoker (yes/no) based on self-reported tobacco use. Self-reported physician diagnoses were used to define the presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and gout. All covariate definitions and measurement protocols followed NHANES analytic guidelines and are available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes. Detailed variable definitions and coding schemes are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.3; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Sampling weights were applied according to National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) guidelines to account for the complex, multistage sampling design of NHANES and to ensure nationally representative estimates. Participants were categorized into two groups based on self-reported history of kidney stones: those with and without kidney stones. Continuous variables were summarized as weighted means with standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as weighted proportions. Between-group differences were assessed using survey-weighted t-tests for continuous variables and survey-weighted χ² tests for categorical variables.

To explore potential nonlinear associations between dietary macronutrient intake and the prevalence of kidney stones, we conducted restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analyses. The percentages of total energy derived from protein, fat, and carbohydrates (%En) were modeled separately as continuous exposures, using five knots placed at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles of their respective distributions [21] . Each RCS model was adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, family income-to-poverty ratio, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and coronary heart disease.

Based on the shape of the RCS curves, we subsequently defined macronutrient intake categories for stratified analysis: protein (< 15%, 15–30%, > 30%), fat (< 35%, ≥ 35%), and carbohydrates (< 40%, 40–50%, > 50%). Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for kidney stone prevalence across macronutrient categories. Two models were fitted: an unadjusted model and a fully adjusted model including the same covariates as the spline analysis. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics by kidney stone prevalence

A total of 59,842 participants were enrolled in NHANES between 2007 and 2018. After applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria—including the removal of individuals aged < 20 years, those lacking data on kidney stone prevalence, macronutrient intake, or key covariates, as well as participants with implausible total energy intake (< 800 or > 4200 kcal/day for men; <600 or > 3500 kcal/day for women) or extreme intake of any single macronutrient (protein, fat, or carbohydrate) exceeding ± 3 standard deviations from the population mean—a final sample of 24,235 adults was included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Among them, 2,341 individuals (9.7%) self-reported a history of kidney stones and were categorized as the kidney stone group, while the remaining 21,894 participants comprised the non-kidney stone group. The associations between macronutrient energy distribution and kidney stone prevalence remained robust following these quality control steps.

The prevalence of comorbidities was also higher in the kidney stone group, including diabetes (21.9% vs. 10.9%), hypertension (47.3% vs. 30.5%), coronary heart disease (6.3% vs. 3.2%), and gout (8.4% vs. 3.6%) (all P < 0.001).

In terms of dietary intake, there was no significant difference in total daily energy consumption between the two groups. However, protein intake—both in grams and as a percentage of total energy—was significantly lower among participants with kidney stones (76.80 g/day and 15.87% En%) compared to those without kidney stones (78.90 g/day and 16.29% En%; both P < 0.001). Fat intake, when expressed as a proportion of total energy, was slightly higher in the kidney stone group (35.73% vs. 35.08%; P < 0.001), although the absolute intake in grams did not differ significantly. Carbohydrate intake showed no significant difference between the groups in either absolute (g/day) or relative (%En) terms.

Dose–response patterns between macronutrient energy intake and kidney stone prevalence

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to explore nonlinear relationships between macronutrient energy intake and kidney stone prevalence (Fig. 2).

Restricted Cubic Spline Analysis of the Associations Between Energy Intake From Protein, Carbohydrate, and Fat and Kidney Stone Prevalence in U.S. Adults

A significant U-shaped association was observed for protein intake (P for nonlinearity = 0.021; P for overall < 0.001). The lowest prevalence occurred at approximately 23% of energy from protein. Below this point, prevalence decreased with higher intake, while beyond 23%, prevalence increased progressively.

Carbohydrate intake similarly exhibited a U-shaped nonlinear association (P for nonlinearity = 0.017; P for overall < 0.001). The lowest prevalence was found at 40–50% of energy intake. Below this range, prevalence declined with increasing carbohydrate intake; however, intake above 50% was associated with a gradual increase in prevalence.

In contrast, fat intake displayed a reverse L-shaped trend, though the nonlinear association was not statistically significant (P for nonlinearity = 0.353; P for overall < 0.001). A slight decline in prevalence was noted with fat intake up to approximately 35%, beyond which the prevalence increased more noticeably.

Associations between macronutrient energy proportions and kidney stone prevalence

Logistic regression analyses were used to assess the association between the proportion of energy intake from individual macronutrients and the prevalence of kidney stones. In the crude model, participants with protein intake accounting for 15–30% of total energy had significantly lower odds of kidney stones compared to those consuming ≤ 15% (OR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.75–0.95; P = 0.003). No significant association was observed among individuals with protein intake > 30% (OR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.43–1.63; P = 0.606). After adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, family income ratio, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease, the inverse association for the 15–30% protein group persisted (adjusted OR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.73–0.92). No significant association was found for protein intake > 30% (P = 0.753). The difference in kidney stone prevalence between the lowest (≤ 15%) and highest (> 30%) protein intake categories remained nonsignificant (Table 2).

No significant associations were detected between carbohydrate intake categories and kidney stone prevalence in either the crude or adjusted models (all P > 0.05). In sex-stratified analyses, among men, protein intake accounting for 15%–30% of total energy was associated with lower odds of kidney stones compared with protein intake ≤ 15% (adjusted OR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.62–0.85; P < 0.001), whereas no significant association was observed in women (adjusted OR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.79–1.08; P = 0.290). Discussion

In this large, nationally representative cross-sectional study, we examined the association between dietary macronutrient energy distribution and the prevalence of kidney stones among U.S. adults. Previous studies on dietary factors and kidney stones have largely focused on isolated nutrients, such as protein, carbohydrate, or fat, often leading to inconsistent or inconclusive findings [11–14]. These inconsistencies may partly stem from the complex interplay between macronutrients in typical dietary patterns, where changes in one nutrient necessarily affect the intake proportions of others. To address this limitation, our study evaluated the relative contributions of protein, fat, and carbohydrates to total energy intake in an integrated manner [22,23]. By considering macronutrient balance rather than single-nutrient effects, our approach better reflects real-world dietary behaviors and allows for a more comprehensive assessment of dietary correlates of kidney stone prevalence [17]. Furthermore, the application of restricted cubic spline models enabled us to detect potential nonlinear associations that traditional categorical or linear models might have missed [24].

Our findings revealed a significant U-shaped association between protein intake and kidney stone prevalence, with the lowest prevalence observed when protein contributed approximately 23% of total energy. This observation is broadly consistent with previous population-based evidence. Basiri et al.10. reported a U-shaped correlation between regional total protein intake and urolithiasis incidence in a cross-sectional study conducted across multiple socioeconomic regions in Iran, with the lowest stone incidence observed at approximately 70–80 g/day of protein intake, whereas both lower and higher intake levels were associated with increased incidence. However, that study evaluated absolute protein intake at the regional level. In contrast, our study assessed protein as a proportion of total energy intake at the individual level, a framework that better reflects the dynamic balance among macronutrients in the overall diet. Because a relative decrease in protein intake is typically accompanied by a relative increase in carbohydrate and/or fat intake, our findings extend previous evidence by showing that the non-linear association is also present when protein is considered within the context of dietary energy distribution. This pattern suggests that both insufficient and excessive protein intake may be unfavorable in the context of stone formation. High protein intake has been associated with increased endogenous acid production, reduced urinary pH, and enhanced excretion of calcium, oxalate, and uric acid—factors that contribute to supersaturation of lithogenic salts and promote crystal aggregation [11,25]. On the other end of the spectrum, insufficient protein intake has likewise been linked to unfavorable physiological changes relevant to stone formation. Experimental evidence suggests that very low protein intake may induce systemic conditions that are unfavorable to overall metabolic balance. In a study by Yamada et al []. rats fed a very low protein diet (VLPD) developed malnutrition—reflected by decreased body weight, serum albumin, and fetuin-A levels—and exhibited elevated markers of systemic inflammation, including tumor necrosis factor-α and urinary 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine. These changes were accompanied by increased calcium-phosphate precipitates and vascular calcification. While not directly examining nephrolithiasis, such systemic alterations may influence renal mineral handling. In addition, animal models of kidney stone formation have shown that oxidative stress and inflammasome activation can promote renal crystal deposition [27], suggesting that inflammation—exacerbated by protein deficiency—may contribute to a pro-lithogenic environment. These converging mechanisms may help explain the U-shaped pattern observed in our study, where both low and high protein intake were associated with increased kidney stone prevalence.

For carbohydrate intake, a U-shaped association with kidney stone prevalence was observed, with the lowest prevalence occurring when carbohydrates contributed 40–50% of total energy. The elevated prevalence at low carbohydrate intake levels may be partly explained by compensatory increases in protein and fat consumption. Mechanistically, a controlled metabolic study by Reddy et al [28]. found that adherence to a low-carbohydrate, high-protein diet resulted in increased acid load, reduced urinary pH, decreased urinary citrate excretion, and elevated urinary calcium levels—all of which are established urinary risk factors for stone formation [1]. At higher levels of carbohydrate intake, previous studies have reported increased urinary excretion of uric acid, calcium, and oxalate—factors known to contribute to lithogenesis [29]. This may provide a potential explanation for the increased kidney stone prevalence observed at both lower and higher carbohydrate intake ranges in our study.

For fat intake, a reverse L-shaped association with kidney stone prevalence was observed in our restricted cubic spline analysis. When fat contributed less than 35% of total energy intake, the prevalence of kidney stones remained relatively stable across varying levels. However, above this threshold, higher fat intake was associated with a progressive increase in prevalence. Although the nonlinearity was not statistically significant (P for nonlinearity = 0.353), multivariable logistic regression indicated a significantly higher risk of kidney stones in individuals consuming ≥ 35% of total energy from fat. Existing evidence on the role of dietary fat in nephrolithiasis is mixed. A study by Bailly et al [30] reported no significant impact of fat intake on urinary stone risk factors among patients with calcium nephrolithiasis. In contrast, findings by Zayed et al [31] suggest that increased luminal fat may bind to calcium in the gut, reducing calcium availability for oxalate binding and thereby enhancing intestinal oxalate absorption and urinary oxalate excretion. These findings, together with our results, suggest that maintaining fat intake below 35% of total energy may be important for reducing lithogenic risk.

In this nationally representative cross-sectional study, we found that the proportional distribution of dietary macronutrients was nonlinearly associated with the prevalence of kidney stones among U.S. adults. Specifically, protein and carbohydrate intakes demonstrated U-shaped associations, with the lowest prevalence observed at approximately 23% and 40–50% of energy, respectively. Fat intake showed a reverse L-shaped pattern, with increased prevalence noted above 35% of energy. These patterns suggest that both insufficient and excessive intake of certain macronutrients may adversely affect urinary stone risk. In sex-stratified analyses, the inverse association for protein intake in the 15%–30% range appeared more evident in men than in women; however, no significant interaction by sex was observed for protein, carbohydrate, or fat intake. This lack of significant sex interaction suggests that the observed associations may reflect broadly similar underlying metabolic pathways in men and women, rather than strong effect modification by sex, although subtle differences related to hormonal milieu, body composition, and dietary source of macronutrients cannot be excluded [32]. Taken together, these findings support the view that the adverse effects of macronutrient imbalance on stone risk are likely mediated predominantly through shared metabolic and urinary mechanisms across sexes. These associations may be partly explained by the impact of macronutrient imbalance on urinary stone risk factors. Both insufficient and excessive intake can alter urinary pH, reduce citrate excretion, and increase the excretion of lithogenic solutes such as calcium, oxalate, and uric acid. These metabolic effects may arise from acid–base disturbance, enhanced enteric oxalate absorption, or low-grade systemic inflammation [33,34].

Our approach, which evaluated macronutrients as energy percentages using restricted cubic spline modeling, allowed for a more nuanced characterization of nonlinear relationships compared to conventional categorical analyses. Additionally, by considering macronutrient balance rather than isolated intake, this study reflects real-world dietary behaviors and helps explain inconsistencies reported in earlier literature. While most prior studies examined individual nutrients in general populations, stratification by intake levels or dietary patterns may yield more consistent associations and inform personalized dietary guidance for kidney stone prevention.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional nature of NHANES, causal relationships between macronutrient intake and kidney stone formation cannot be established. Second, kidney stone prevalence was based on self-reported history, which may be subject to recall bias or misclassification. Third, dietary intake was assessed using one or two 24-hour dietary recalls and may not fully reflect long-term habitual intake, which may not fully reflect long-term habitual intake and is susceptible to reporting error. Fourth, although our models adjusted for a range of demographic and clinical covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out. In particular, fluid intake—a key determinant of urinary concentration and stone risk—was not reliably captured in NHANES and thus could not be adjusted for, potentially influencing the observed associations. Finally, the absence of stone composition data limited our ability to explore macronutrient effects by stone type.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies distinct nonlinear associations between macronutrient energy distribution and kidney stone prevalence in a large, nationally representative U.S. adult population. Our findings underscore the importance of maintaining a balanced macronutrient profile within recommended dietary ranges, rather than focusing on single-nutrient restriction, in strategies aimed at reducing kidney stone risk. Our findings may inform dietary recommendations regarding macronutrient balance for kidney stone prevention, particularly in populations at elevated risk. Future prospective studies and controlled trials are needed to confirm these associations and explore underlying biological mechanisms.
