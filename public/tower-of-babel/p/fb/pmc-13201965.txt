C1–2 puncture for diagnostic CSF access is infrequently performed in neurosurgical practice but is indicated in those in whom lumbar puncture (LP) is contraindicated or technically infeasible. Common contraindications to lumbar access include prior lumbar surgery, extensive spinal fusion, overlying infection, severe spinal stenosis, arachnoiditis, neoplastic involvement, or congenital abnormalities such as tethered cord or spinal dysraphism.1 In these settings, direct access to the subarachnoid space at the C1–2 level enables diagnostic CSF sampling or contrast instillation for cervical myelography while avoiding compromised lumbar tissues.

The technique evolved from suboccipital cisternal punctures described in the early 20th century. Lateral C1–2 puncture was first reported by Kelly and Alexander in 1968 for positive-contrast myelography using an anterior subarachnoid trajectory.2 Subsequent neuroradiological descriptions in the early 1970s refined the approach by targeting the lateral aspect of the spinal canal, where the subarachnoid space is more capacious.3,4 Further technical evolution in the late 1970s emphasized posterior canal targeting to minimize the risk of vascular or spinal cord injury, establishing the foundations of the modern, image-guided technique.5,6 Presently, the optimal anatomical landmarks for safe lateral cervical puncture in adults include 1 cm inferior and 1 cm posterior to the mastoid process and, fluoroscopically, 2–5 mm anterior to the spinolaminar line posterior to the C1 posterior arch flare point and approximately 5 mm inferior to the posterior arch of C1.

Lateral percutaneous access to the C1–2 subarachnoid space has progressively evolved as a safe and effective technique in adult patients for both diagnostic and therapeutic indications. Over time, its utility expanded to include therapeutic interventions requiring direct cervical intrathecal access, such as targeted drug delivery and ablative procedures. Among these applications, percutaneous cervical cordotomy represents the most extensively studied and longest-standing use of the C1–2 puncture technique.7,8 More recently, the use of a Tuohy needle for C1–2 subarachnoid access has been described to facilitate the placement of intrathecal drug delivery catheter systems.9–11

The high concentration of collocated and critical neurovascular structures causes a perception that C1–2 puncture carries excessive procedural risk; practice pattern data suggest that the technique is universally, albeit infrequently, utilized. A national survey of neuroradiology departments demonstrated that the majority of respondents had performed at least one lateral C1–2 puncture within the preceding year, most commonly for myelography or CSF access when the preferred lumbar cisternal access was contraindicated or unsuccessful.12

The safety of C1–2 puncture has been well characterized in adult series, in which major complications are exceedingly rare. Reported adverse events are typically minor and transient, including headache, nausea, or self-limited paresthesia, while serious complications, such as spinal cord or vascular injury or clinically significant hemorrhage, occur in less than 0.05% of cases when performed under modern imaging guidance.13,14 These data support the intrinsic safety of high cervical access when appropriate technique and patient selection are applied.

The existing literature on C1–2 puncture is largely derived from adult experience, with limited application in pediatric populations. When reported in children, its use has been sparse and predominantly confined to nondiagnostic indications, such as intrathecal baclofen catheter placement or cervical myelography in the setting of complex spinal pathology.9,10 To date, there are no published reports describing C1–2 puncture in an infant for diagnostic evaluation of suspected meningitis.

Herein, we present a clinical case illustrating the application of C1–2 puncture in the pediatric population; to our knowledge, this is the first reported use of a C1–2 tap in an infant. The anatomical landmarks and redundant tissues of the infant child differ significantly from those in the adult technique. This case highlights the feasibility of high cervical CSF access in infancy when LP is contraindicated and underscores the importance of meticulous technique under imaging guidance in extending established neurosurgical approaches to selected pediatric patients.

Illustrative Case

The indication for C1–2 puncture was determined through multidisciplinary evaluation involving pediatric neurosurgery, pediatric anesthesiology, and infectious disease services, given the contraindication to LP in the setting of recent lumbar surgery and dural repair. Procedural planning incorporated review of MR images of the cervical spine and established technical principles for high cervical CSF access.

Postprocedural outcomes were assessed through neurological examinations, laboratory analysis of CSF, inpatient clinical course, and outpatient follow-up visits. No experimental interventions were performed, and all aspects of care were delivered as part of standard clinical management.

Informed consent for the procedure was obtained from the patient’s parents. Institutional review board approval was not required for this single case report in accordance with institutional policy.

History

A 3-month-old female was diagnosed with a transitional lipomyelomeningocele and associated tethering of her spinal cord at level L4–5 with associated bladder dysfunction and weakness of the left foot. She underwent surgical debulking of the lipomyomeningocele and untethering of her spinal cord with dural repair and complex wound closure at 6 months of age. A subarachnoid drain and subcutaneous drain for CSF diversion and facilitation of wound healing were placed at the time of surgery. Her subarachnoid drain was removed on postoperative day (POD) 9, and she was subsequently discharged.

On POD 12, the patient re-presented to the emergency department with a sustained fever reportedly reaching 103.9°F. At presentation, her temperature was 100.2°F with tachycardia in the 170s. The parents reported that the infant had been more lethargic since awakening that morning but denied concerns regarding wound contamination, including stool exposure, and reported appropriate wound care, bowel movements, and urine output. They also denied symptoms suggestive of upper respiratory or urinary tract infection. On physical examination, the lumbar incision appeared clean, dry, and intact, with only a normal degree of postoperative erythema and no drainage, dehiscence, or other findings concerning for surgical site infection.

Laboratory evaluation revealed marked leukocytosis (white blood cell count 29 × 109/L) with mildly elevated inflammatory markers (erythrocyte sedimentation rate 24 mm/hr, C-reactive protein 5 mg/L). Urinalysis and the respiratory pathogen panel were unremarkable, and blood and urine cultures were pending at the time of presentation. Given the urgency of evaluating for CNS infection, the multidisciplinary team, including the infectious disease service, determined that prompt CSF sampling was the most appropriate diagnostic step, followed by empiric intravenous vancomycin and ceftriaxone. Given the recent lumbar surgery with dural repair, LP was considered contraindicated; therefore, a fluoroscopically guided C1–2 puncture was planned following detailed parental counseling and informed consent.

Procedure

The patient’s identity was verified and a procedural time-out performed. The infant underwent general endotracheal anesthesia and was positioned supine with all pressure points carefully padded. The lateral neck anatomy was obscured by high-riding shoulders and tissue folds. We gently provided traction on the clavicle by taping the shoulder to the bed, which flattened the skin folds and exposed the lateral neck anatomy. The adult landmark 1 cm inferior and 1 cm anterior to the mastoid tip provided an unacceptable entry point anterior to the spinal canal. We decontaminated the skin from ear to shoulder and draped it in a sterile fashion. We identified an entry point using lateral fluoroscopy about 0.5 cm directly inferior to the mastoid tip. We measured the skin-to-dura distance to be 34 mm on axial MRI between C1 and C2 (Fig. 1) and drew this mark on the spinal needle. We then introduced the 20-gauge spinal needle orthogonal to our target and slightly anterior to the spinolaminar line at the C1–2 interlaminar space (Fig. 2). Entry angle and trajectory were guided by intermittent fluoroscopy comparing needle position to spinolaminar line and contextualizing with our expected depth of dural puncture drawn on the needle. Clear CSF was obtained with one pass. Approximately 15 mL of CSF was collected for microbiological and biochemical analysis. The stylet was replaced, the needle withdrawn, and a sterile dressing applied. The procedure was completed uneventfully with minimal blood loss (< 1 mL). Postoperative Period

The patient emerged from anesthesia without neurological deficit or respiratory compromise. CSF appeared clear and showed no growth on Gram stain or culture, while urine cultures were positive for Pseudomonas aeruginosa and renal ultrasound confirmed the diagnosis of pyelonephritis. Her fever curve and inflammatory markers improved on antibiotic therapy, and she was discharged 4 days later on 10 days of oral ciprofloxacin. She remained neurologically intact, with stable wound healing and no evidence of CSF leakage or infection at either the cervical or lumbar site.

Discussion

LP remains the gold standard for CSF sampling across both pediatric and adult populations.1 Nevertheless, C1–2 puncture represents a well-established and viable alternative when clinical or anatomical factors render lumbar access unsafe or impractical. This approach provides direct access to the cervical subarachnoid space and is supported by a favorable safety profile. Beyond diagnostic CSF sampling, C1–2 puncture has also been effectively used in the surgical management of chronic pain syndromes, facilitating safe and targeted access for procedures such as cordotomy and intrathecal baclofen pump placement.7–11,15

Anatomical and Technical Considerations in Children

Modern techniques emphasize continuous lateral fluoroscopic guidance with needle advancement confined to the posterior one-third of the spinal canal to minimize the risk of spinal cord injury.16,17 Peckham and colleagues further defined the C1 posterior arch “flare-point,” a reproducible osseous landmark approximating the dorsal margin of the spinal cord, which serves as a reliable visual boundary to guide safe subarachnoid access.16

The C1–2 interlaminar space provides a direct lateral corridor to the cervical subarachnoid compartment (Fig. 3). At this level, the spinal canal is relatively capacious, with the spinal cord occupying a smaller proportion of the canal diameter than at lower cervical segments.18 Targeting the posterior subarachnoid space posterior to the dorsal surface of the spinal cord and anterior to the ventral (inner) surface of the posterior arc (i.e., the spinolaminar line) lessens the risk of injuring the vertebral arteries if they course as expected through the transverse foramina and medially around the atlas at the sulcus vertebral artery.19 FIG. 3.C1–C2 interlaminar puncture anatomy. Safe lateral trajectories in adults begin with a starting point approximately 1 cm inferior and anterior to the mastoid tip.9 In infants, additional anatomical considerations are critical. Cranial landmarks like the mastoid tip are comparatively underdeveloped, cervical vertebrae are incompletely ossified, and bony landmarks are less distinct on imaging. We found that the lateral entry point needed to be shifted posteriorly and superiorly compared with adult measurements. The disproportionately larger head and shorter neck further influence occipito-cervical alignment, necessitating gentle positioning and modification like taping the shoulder to expose the lateral neck, and continuous imaging confirmation.20 These features make meticulous image guidance and soft tissue awareness essential for safe execution of the procedure. In all patients, preprocedural measurement from skin to dura is a critical safety step to alert one of impending dural puncture.

Decision-Making and Alternatives

Alternative cervical approaches, including midline suboccipital (cisternal) puncture, were considered but ultimately deemed less favorable. Suboccipital access is performed in the prone position, carries a potentially higher risk of medullary or lower brainstem injury, and is less amenable to precise fluoroscopic localization because of overlapping skull base anatomy and a limited procedural working corridor.21

The lateral atlanto-occipital space puncture was developed in the 1990s as a variation of high cervical and suboccipital access.22 Large retrospective series have demonstrated a high technical success rate and a low incidence of serious complications, and in select adult cases the procedure has been performed without fluoroscopic guidance.22 However, this approach relies on lateral skull base landmarks near the mastoid process and traverses a region with close proximity to the vertebral artery, lower cranial nerves, and venous structures.21 In infants, in whom anatomical landmarks are less distinct and spatial margins are smaller, these factors limit procedural precision and increase theoretical risk.

In contrast, the C1–2 interlaminar space offers more reliable midline fluoroscopic landmarks and a broader posterior subarachnoid target. The dorsal subarachnoid space widens at C1–2, providing a target for access, and the spinal cord expands inferior to C2, decreasing the ratio of the cervical spinal canal to cervical spinal cord area, which is suboptimal for access. Accordingly, C1–2 puncture represented the most direct and anatomically appropriate alternative for diagnostic CSF acquisition in this case.

An additional consideration in this case was whether empiric antimicrobial therapy alone could have been pursued without diagnostic CSF sampling. Although empiric treatment was initiated, definitive CSF evaluation was considered important given the concern for possible postoperative meningitis following recent neurosurgical intervention. While high cervical puncture carries inherent technical challenges and procedural risks, CSF analysis remains critical for confirming or excluding CNS infection, guiding targeted antimicrobial therapy, and determining the appropriate duration of treatment. Empiric antimicrobial therapy alone may lead to inappropriate antimicrobial selection or unnecessary prolonged broad-spectrum therapy, whereas microbiological confirmation through CSF analysis enables pathogen identification and targeted treatment. Current guidelines emphasize that CSF culture remains the most important diagnostic test for establishing the diagnosis of healthcare-associated meningitis and guiding management.23 In this context, the multidisciplinary team determined that the diagnostic value of CSF acquisition outweighed the procedural risks, particularly given the availability of fluoroscopic guidance and experienced operators.

Observations

In the present case, no neurological, infectious, or procedural complications were observed, further supporting the safety of this approach in a controlled pediatric setting. The patient exhibited no change in neurological examination and no evidence of CSF leakage, wound complication, or secondary infection during hospitalization or follow-up.

Several procedural factors likely contributed to this favorable outcome, including performance under general anesthesia to eliminate patient movement, use of a relatively small-gauge spinal needle, and removal of a limited CSF volume to minimize dural trauma and abrupt pressure shifts. Strict sterile technique and continuous physiological monitoring by a dedicated pediatric neuroanesthesia team further mitigated risk.

Importantly, this case demonstrates that fluoroscopically guided C1–2 puncture can provide safe and effective diagnostic CSF access in an infant when LP is contraindicated. While C1–2 puncture has been described in adults and occasionally in pediatric patients for procedures such as intrathecal baclofen catheter placement or myelography, reports of its use for diagnostic CSF acquisition in infants are lacking, making this an uncommon application of the technique.

Lessons

This case illustrates that C1–2 puncture can potentially serve as a feasible and effective alternative for diagnostic CSF access in select infant patients when LP is contraindicated. When performed under high-resolution imaging guidance within a specialized, multidisciplinary setting, high cervical access enabled timely CSF evaluation without procedural complications. Although this experience supports the potential role of C1–2 puncture in carefully selected pediatric cases, it represents a single observation and does not establish the overall safety, efficacy, or generalizability of this technique in infants. Further investigation is required to better characterize the unique anatomical and procedural considerations of infancy, including spinal canal dimensions, dural relationships, and vertebral artery anatomy, which differ substantially from those of adults. Future efforts should emphasize simulation-based training, detailed imaging and cadaveric studies, and the development of standardized procedural paradigms. Importantly, aggregation of larger multicenter datasets or registry-based analyses of pediatric C1–2 and cisternal punctures will be essential to define indications, complication profiles, and patient selection criteria. Until such evidence is available, high cervical CSF access should be considered cautiously and reserved for centers with appropriate expertise and multidisciplinary oversight.
