Florida elephant trunk hybrid aortic repair (FETHAR): a novel frozen elephant trunk technique with complete debranching sparing endograft modification
Authors: Christopher M. Bobba, Omar M. Sharaf, Fabian Jimenez Contreras, Thomas M. Beaver, Tomas D. Martin, Eric I. Jeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Treatment of aortic arch pathology is rapidly evolving. Current techniques utilize a combination of open and endovascular approaches. Existing techniques such as B-SAFER require endograft modification for complete arch debranching which may predispose the graft to failure or endoleak. We developed the FETHAR, a novel method for hybrid aortic repair and demonstrate its use in a patient who presented with an acute dissection with contained rupture. Following repair, we demonstrate excellent seal without endoleak on the post-operative CT scan. Ultimately, this repair allows for complete arch debranching, an anastomosis in zone 2 of the aortic arch, and no endograft modification.

Introduction

Hybrid aortic repair entails a combination of open surgical and endovascular techniques. Existing techniques in the aortic arch require modification of an endovascular graft to achieve this goal. We report on the Florida elephant trunk hybrid aortic repair (FETHAR), a novel method for hybrid aortic repair using a trifurcated graft, a collared Siena graft utilized in reversed fashion, a thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) stent graft, and a covered vascular graft (Fig. 1).

Case report

A 64 year-old male presented with hyperacute aortic dissection with a contained rupture into the left pleural space. Imaging indicated a type I dissection with tear in zone 4 and significant ascending and arch involvement along with large left hemothorax (Fig. 2A). The patient elected to proceed with emergent repair. We planned to repair his dissection initially without draining the hemothorax until we could assure complete coverage of his rupture. Upon sternotomy and aortic inspection there was clear hematoma and dissection consistent with the imaging. Wire access was obtained in the true lumen of the femoral artery, confirmed on transesophageal echocardiography, and left in the descending thoracic aorta (Fig. 1A). Epiaortic ultrasound identified an appropriate site for cannulation in the true lumen of the ascending aorta. Transesophageal echocardiogram did not identify aortic valve or root pathology. The patient was placed on cardiopulmonary bypass with central aortic and dual stage venous cannulation with retrograde cardioplegia and a left ventricular vent. The patient was cooled to 18˚˚ C with electrocerebral silence, placed in Trendelenburg position, and we initiated deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. A trifurcated Gelweave graft was brought to the field and anastomosis to the innominate artery was completed. Anastomosis to the left common carotid artery was then completed, and bilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion via innominate and left common carotid arteries was initiated. Visual inspection of the distal arch and proximal descending thoracic aorta revealed a large tear in zone 4. A collared Siena graft with single side arm was trimmed, reversed, and floated antegrade into zone 3. The sidearm of the reversed collared graft was then positioned into the orifice of the left subclavian artery (LSCA). The distal aortic anastomosis was then completed in zone 2 using a horizontal mattress felt sandwich technique around the diseased aorta, which was then anastomosed to the graft collar (Fig. 1B). The previously placed Amplatz femoral wire was then advanced retrograde through our distal anastomosis, such that through-and-through wire access was obtained. A TEVAR endograft was then deployed in antegrade fashion over that wire (Fig. 1C). A Viabahn stent was then deployed in antegrade fashion through the collared Siena graft and into the sidearm previously floated within the LSCA. Excellent retrograde flow was observed through the LSCA stent once deployed. There was no apparent endoleak around the stent or from the LSCA orifice. A straight Gelweave graft with sidearm was then anastomosed to the distal aortic anastomosis and flow was restored to the body. We then performed our proximal aortic anastomosis and anastomosed the trifurcated graft to the straight graft(Fig. 1D). Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest time was 19 min. Body circulatory arrest with antegrade cerebral perfusion time was 56 min. Cross-clamp time was 114 min. Total time on cardiopulmonary bypass was 196 min. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was transported to the ICU. The following day he underwent a CT scan to ensure complete coverage of his rupture within the descending thoracic aorta. We elected to extend his TEVAR on post-operative day (POD) 2 using the Knickerbocker technique to ensure complete coverage of his tear. This scan also indicated patent flow within the LSCA and no evidence of endoleak or pseudoaneurysm from the Siena graft, LSCA stent, or TEVAR stent (Fig. 2B). On POD 4, we performed left thoracoscopic washout of his hemothorax. Thereafter he recovered well and was extubated on POD 6 and subsequently discharged to inpatient rehab on POD 15. He was seen in follow-up about one month after surgery without evidence of spinal cord ischemia, vocal cord dysfunction, or stroke. Post-operative imaging indicated good seal without endoleak.

Comment

Hybrid aortic arch repair has emerged as a viable treatment strategy for complex aortic pathology involving the aortic arch and proximal descending thoracic aorta [1, 2]. Initially this repair entailed antegrade deployment of a thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) graft in conjunction with individual debranching of the head vessels via standard open surgical anastomosis, the so-called frozen elephant trunk technique. However, given the technical complexity of complete arch debranching via open surgical anastomosis, efforts have been directed toward simplification. More recently the B-SAFER has demonstrated successful modification of TEVAR endografts to allow for endovascular revascularization of the LSCA with or without the left common carotid artery, and standard anastomosis to the innominate artery [3–5]. The advantages of this technique are to move the distal anastomosis “forward” (i.e. away from zone 3 to zone 2 or 1), reduce the number of surgical anastomoses, and avoid surgical dissection injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve. These serve to reduce bleeding complications, circulatory arrest time, total operative time, and post-operative morbidity. One disadvantage, however, is that all variants of this technique require endograft modification (with electrocautery or mechanical cutting). We conjecture that this could weaken the graft and predispose it to failure or endoleak. The FETHAR technique differs from the B-SAFER technique in the utilization of a reversed Siena graft with the sidearm positioned into the LSCA orifice and deployment of a covered stent through that sidearm into the LSCA (steps 5–7 above). It is, however, important to note that our criticism of the B-SAFER technique is a theoretical concern regarding the integrity of the endograft following the burning and modification required as part of the B-SAFER technique. Various commercial devices have been developed to better facilitate hybrid repairs, but all require standard surgical anastomosis to the head vessels. We developed this technique to capitalize on the advantages of B-SAFER (i.e. allow for endovascular revascularization of the LSCA) while avoiding modification of commercially available endografts. Table 1 outlines the advantages and disadvantages of the various hybrid and classic techniques for open surgical treatment of aortic arch pathology (Table 1). In this patient we were able to visualize the orifice of the LSCA to float a short segment of a sidearm graft and then deploy a covered stent into the LSCA in antegrade fashion. Both the FETHAR and B-SAFER techniques require adequate intraoperative visualization of the LSCA orifice to facilitate antegrade LSCA stenting. Arch pathology involving the LSCA, whether dissection or aneurysm, is not an absolute contraindication for the utilization of these techniques, however, one must be able to adequately visualize the true lumen of the LSCA to confidently deploy the antegrade LSCA stent and be comfortable with the distal landing zone. It is important to note that this is an early technical experience in one patient without a study of long-term outcomes. Though this patient recovered well, medium- and long-term morbidity and mortality remain to be studied in a larger patient cohort. This approach combines the advantages of hybrid aortic repair while minimizing any necessary endograft modification and is a viable option for hybrid aortic repair.
