Quality of life in patients with heart failure and implantable cardioverter defibrillators: results from 9274 patients in 42 outpatient heart failure clinics in the National Norwegian Heart Failure Registry
Authors: Hanne Flagtvedt, Torstein Hole, Tore Wentzel-Larsen, Christer Reigstad Aase, Tone M Norekvål
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPatients with heart failure (HF) have increased risk of sudden cardiac death, and treatment with an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) has become standard treatment for selected patient groups. HF is associated with impaired quality of life (QoL), but studies on QoL in patients with HF and ICD attending follow-up in outpatient HF clinics is sparse. Hence, this study aimed to determine QoL over time in patients with HF with and without ICD using data from the first visit (baseline) and follow-up visit in 42 outpatient HF clinics.Methods and resultsLongitudinal real-world data from the National Norwegian Heart Failure Registry including 9274 patients, of which 1206 with ICD, were studied. QoL was assessed using the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire. Patients with ICD were younger, more often men, and had a higher frequency of coronary artery disease (all P < 0.001). At the first visit, no significant difference in QoL was found between patients with HF with and without ICD, and both groups had significant improvement in QoL from the first to the follow-up visit (P < 0.001). However, patients with HF and ICD reported significantly less improvement (P < 0.001).ConclusionQoL in patients with ICD improves after follow-up in an outpatient HF clinic, although not to the same extent as in patients without ICD. Thus, tailored interventions through a combined HF-ICD care programme are needed to improve QoL in this subgroup of patients with both HF and ICD.Keywords: Heart failure, Implantable cardioverter defibrillator, Outpatient clinic, Quality of life

Introduction

Sudden cardiac death is a major cause of mortality in patients with heart failure (HF), and implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICD) are recommended for patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) on optimal medical therapy and a life expectancy of over 1 year.1 Despite clear evidence-based guidelines regarding ICD implantation, a recent consensus statement from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) emphasises a considerable underutilization of ICD treatment for patients with HF in clinical practice.2 However, there is no consensus regarding the impact of ICD implantation on psychosocial factors and quality of life (QoL).3-5 Although most patients adjust well to having an ICD implanted, the potentially serious symptoms of HF1,6,7 and the fear of experiencing shock treatment may cause an excessive psychological burden, resulting in anxiety, depression, and poor QoL.3,4,8-10

Multiple studies have shown that HF can cause a moderate to severe impact on QoL.11,12 Furthermore, poor QoL can affect medication adherence, creating a vicious cycle that worsens outcomes.6 For patients with both HF and ICD, the prevalence of depression exceeds 40%, contributing to a deterioration in QoL.5 HF is characterized by a chronic course with episodic acute exacerbations, and life-long treatment is expected. Thus, maintenance or improvement in QoL is crucial.1 Therefore, to reduce mortality and improve QoL, follow-up in a multi-disciplinary HF management programme is recommended with the highest level of evidence in the European guidelines for HF management.1

Despite evidence of poor QoL among patients with HF as well as patients with ICD, knowledge regarding QoL in patients with ICD attending follow-up in outpatient HF clinics is sparse. Therefore, this study aimed to determine QoL over time in patients with HF with and without ICD by using data from the first visit (baseline) and the follow-up visit in 42 outpatient HF clinics, and to determine factors associated with QoL in both groups.

Our main hypothesis was that follow-up in an outpatient HF clinic improves QoL, regardless of ICD treatment. The secondary hypothesis was that patients with both HF and ICD report less improvement in QoL compared with patients without an ICD. The hypotheses are based on previous research and clinical experience.

Methods

Design and setting

This prospective multi-centre study was conducted using data from the National Norwegian Heart Failure Registry (NNHFR), including 9274 patients from all 42 outpatient HF clinics in Norway during 2014–22. The HF clinics are nurse-led and staffed by specialist nurses and cardiologists. The 42 HF clinics represent 89% coverage of all hospitals nationwide.

Study sample

The inclusion criteria for NNHFR were the following: (i) Adults ≥18 years of age; (ii) impaired (HFrEF), mildly reduced (HFmrEF) or preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF); (iii) patients with HF but where the left ventricle does not fail (i.e. right ventricular failure without pulmonary cause); and (iv) patients with a Norwegian social security number. Only patients with data from both visits were included in this study. Patients with foreign addresses and patients with primary right-sided HF, where the main diagnosis was lung disease, were excluded.

Data collection

Patients were referred to their respective outpatient HF clinics either while still at the hospital treated for HF, by their general practitioner in the primary healthcare service or by a cardiologist in a private practice. The referrals were assessed by a cardiologist prior to arrival at the outpatient HF clinic to ensure an accurate diagnosis based on the current HF guidelines.1 Data were entered in the NNHFR by specially trained nurses at each specific HF clinic. Sociodemographic information was obtained through patient self-report, while clinical and biomedical data were gathered from medical records. The NNHFR include three of the scheduled visits: The first visit (baseline), the final up-titration visit, and the follow-up visit conducted ∼6 months after HF treatment was optimized according to current guidelines.1 QoL assessments were conducted at the first visit and the follow-up visit; thus, the data for this study are based on two measurement points. Variations in HF-duration at baseline and visit intervals among patients arise from different treatment needs and referral sources.

Measurements

QoL was assessed using the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ). MLHFQ consists of 21 questions with a six-point Likert scale (0–5), where patients reports to which extent HF has affected their life during the last 4 weeks. The 4-week recall period was used to capture the episodic exacerbations,13 which is a common HF feature.1 A total score (0–105) is obtained by adding the scores from individual questions. The physical dimension in MLHFQ contains eight questions (score 0–40), and the emotional dimension contains five questions (score 0–25).13 A total score of <24 represents a good QoL, a score between 24 and 45 represents a moderate QoL and a score >45 represents a poor QoL.8,12,14 These cut-off values were developed based on longitudinal multi-centre data from patients attending specialized HF clinics.14 A five-point change in the total score is deemed clinically meaningful.15 The questionnaire has shown good psychometric properties with a high level of validity, sensitivity, and reliability.16

Data analysis

The results are presented as counts (n) and percentages (%) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. The population was divided into two groups: patients with and patients without ICD. A student’s t-test and χ2 test were used to compare the sociodemographic and clinical data. The potential differences in QoL were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Mann–Whitney U-test and Student’s t-tests as appropriate.

Multiple linear regression models for the MLHFQ total scores were used to identify the factors associated with QoL at both visits. The independent variables included age, gender, aetiology, marital, cohabitation, and smoking status, New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, systolic blood pressure (SBP), comorbidities, guideline recommended medication for HF, LVEF, and body mass index (BMI), which are shown to have an impact on QoL.5,7,10-12 Subcategories with three or fewer patients were excluded from the model. A composite dichotomous variable was created for patients using neither or either angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi), angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB), or angiotensin receptor/neprilysin inhibitor (ARNi). This was done in accordance with the ESC guidelines for the treatment of HF, which recommend using only one of these medications at a time.1 The results are presented as regression coefficients with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and P-value. In the regression model, interaction effects between patients with and without ICD were analysed to determine if the independent variables had a different impact on QoL across the groups. Finally, clinical significance was assessed by calculating effect sizes for the t-tests, as defined by Cohen’s d; ‘small, d = 0.2’, ‘medium, d = 0.5’ and ‘large, d = 0.8’.17 Missing data were handled using the ‘half-rule’ method, where the mean of valid items was used if at least half of the items were valid.18 Analyses were conducted on the total HF population, including HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF patients. As patients with persistent HFrEF at follow-up represent a unique HF phenotype, which encompasses distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and therapeutic responses,1 subgroup analysis for patients with HFrEF were performed and are included in Supplementary material online, Table S1.

A two-tailed P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout all the analyses. IBM SPSS statistical software for Windows version 29 (IBM Corp.) was used.

Ethical considerations

Norwegian legislation does not require consent from registered individuals in order to conduct research using the national cardiovascular health registries, including the NNHFR. The study conforms with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki19 and was approved by the Norwegian Regional Committee for Ethics in Medical Research in Western Norway (REK 480416). The identification key is blinded to the researcher, and data are stored under strict conditions on designated research servers.

A total of 9274 patients registered during 2014–22 were included in the study, of which 1206 had an ICD (Table 1).

Patient characteristics

Patients with ICD were more often men, younger, with a lower mean LVEF and higher NYHA functional class as well as a history of smoking. In both groups, the main cause of HF was coronary artery disease, but with a higher proportion in patients with ICD (64.7% vs. 43.7%, P < 0.001). In addition, the use of guideline recommended medications for HF was significantly higher in patients with ICD, but there was a significantly lower use of anti-depressants (4.7% vs. 6.8%, P = 0.006). No significant differences in terms of comorbidities (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/asthma or diabetes mellitus) were found between the groups (Table 1).

Quality of life in patients with implantable cardioverter defibrillators

Based on the MLHFQ cut-off scores, a mean total score of 34.7 (±22.1) indicated moderate QoL at the first visit. At the follow-up, significant improvement was found in the MLHFQ total score, emotional dimension and physical dimension (Table 2). According to the MLHFQ cut-off scores, a mean score of 24.2 (±19.1) indicated an improved but still moderate QoL. Overall, the largest effect size for changes in QoL was the MLHFQ total score, with a Cohen’s d of.51 (95% CI, 0.44–0.56), indicating a medium clinical effect (Figure 1).

Quality of life in patients with implantable cardioverter defibrillators compared with patients without implantable cardioverter defibrillators

At the first visit, both groups reported a moderate QoL, with no significant difference in the MLHFQ total score or mean dimension score. In the physical dimension, patients with ICD reported less difficulty related to oedema and fatigue, as well as outdoor activity. Both groups reported similar scores in the emotional dimension, but patients with ICD reported greater difficulties with sexual activity, being able to work and medication side effects (Table 2).

At the follow-up visit, patients with ICD had significantly worse mean scores in both the emotional and physical dimensions, as well as the MLHFQ total score. This result was consistently observed in both the main analysis and the subgroup analysis of patients with HFrEF (all P < 0.001) (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). Patients with ICD reported significantly more challenges related to dyspnoea and medication side effects, as well as difficulties in pursuing hobbies and sexual activity. Further, they had greater need for daytime rest compared with patients without ICD (Table 3). The HFrEF subgroup analysis revealed that patients with ICD and reduced ejection fraction also reported a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms (P = 0.028) (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). The effect size for the difference in MLHFQ total scores between groups indicated a small clinical effect with a Cohen’s d of.15 (95% CI, 0.21–0.09), which was also observed in the emotional [0.10 (95% CI, 0.16–0.04)] and physical [0.12 (95% CI, 0.18–0.06)] dimensions (Figure 1).

Factors associated with quality of life

In both groups, higher NYHA classification and BMI, as well as lower SBP, younger age and use of anti-depressants were significantly associated with worse QoL at the first visit. In addition, female gender, lower LVEF, presence of COPD/asthma, and use of aldosterone antagonists were associated with worse QoL in patients without ICD. There were no significant differences in the effects of the factors on QoL between patients with and without ICD, except for LVEF (P = 0.042) (Table 3). At the follow-up visit, higher NYHA class and BMI as well as lower SBP were significantly associated with worse QoL in both groups. No significant interaction effect was found between the groups (Table 4).

Discussion

The main findings in this large prospective multi-centre study including 9274 patients with HF were: (i) Patients with HF and ICD reported significant improvement in QoL after follow-up in an outpatient HF clinic, and (ii) although QoL in both groups improved, patients with ICD reported less improvement. According to the MLHFQ cut-off score, patients without ICD transitioned from moderate to good QoL, while patients with ICD remained at a moderate level of QoL. To the best of our knowledge, these findings have not been previously described in this subgroup of patients with HF having an ICD. Although consistent with previous studies of the general HF population, those studies did not analyse the ICD subgroup specifically.7,20 Thus, this study underscores the important contributions of outpatient HF clinics in HF management, albeit it provides new evidence on the needs of patients with ICD.

Interpreting QoL scores can be challenging due to the subjective and qualitative nature of the concept. Therefore, to further understand the clinical value of our findings, we estimated the effect size for changes in outcome within patients with ICD. A medium effect size for the MLHFQ total score, alongside a 10-point reduction in total score, indicated a clinically meaningful difference.15 In our study, the overall improvement in QoL can be attributed to treatment optimization for each patient according to the current guidelines, along with the self-care education carried out at the outpatient HF clinics.1,21 Nevertheless, patients with ICD still experience moderate QoL at the follow-up, which may reflect the unpredictability of living with an ICD. In contrast to optimal medical therapy, the ICD does not improve symptoms of HF. Instead, patients must constantly adapt to potential disruptions in their daily lives caused by ICD therapy.9 Januszkiewicz et al. found that one in 10 patients reported a significant decrease in QoL after ICD implantation. In addition, more than 40% of the patients reported feeling depressed.3 The association between depression and poor QoL in patients with both HF and ICD were previously described in a prospective single centre study of 446 patients, where more than 40% reported mild to severe symptoms of depression, which remained constant during the follow-up period of 6 months.5 This is in contrast to our study, where significant improvement was found in the MLHFQ emotional dimension as well as in the single question regarding the experience of being depressed. However, in the aforementioned study, patients were not attending follow-up in an outpatient HF clinic. The recent consensus statement from the ESC highlights that specialized HF nurses can facilitate shared decision-making and optimize QoL by providing insights into the possible impact of ICD treatment on daily life.2 Our study adds to the body of knowledge, showing that follow-up in a nurse-led outpatient HF clinic significantly improves QoL among the subgroup of patients with an ICD.

In the present study, QoL assessment at the first visit did not show evidence for differences between patients with and without an ICD. Considering the large heterogeneity of these groups, this finding was rather unanticipated. Patients with ICD were younger and more often men. In addition, their mean LVEF was lower and physical impairment was worse, which is as expected.1 Although patients with ICD were younger, there was no evidence for differences between the groups in terms of comorbidities.

Despite both groups reporting improvement, patients with ICD had significantly poorer QoL at the follow-up visit. This disparity between groups was visible in both the physical and mental dimensions. Although found to have a small clinical effect, it confirms that patients with ICD report greater challenges and poorer QoL overall. This finding was consistent, even within the subgroup analysis of patients with HFrEF. A meta-synthesis including 24 qualitative studies found that most of the patients had difficulties living with ICD and needed psychosocial support. In addition, their need for information and education was often left unaddressed.4 In the present study, patients with ICD described persistent challenges with sexual activity, concentration, work ability, and medication side effects. Furthermore, the emotional dimension, including feelings of being a burden to others and losing control over one’s life, was significantly more affected in patients with ICD. This finding indicates that, despite the clear positive effects of follow-up in outpatient HF clinics, patients with ICD require additional support to achieve the same level of QoL. Moreover, it underscores the importance of the recent consensus statement emphasizing the role of HF nurses in addressing the impact of ICD treatment on daily life and offering long-term coping strategies.2 Hence, outpatient HF clinics have a unique opportunity to optimize QoL for patients with HF and ICD.

There are some indications that QoL in patients with ICD is relative to time since ICD implantation, where a longer period since implantation are associated with feelings of safety3 and improvement in QoL.8 In the current study, a longer time since implantation was associated with worse QoL at the first visit, while no associations were found at the follow-up visit. Thus, it is possible that other factors in patients with ICD may have a greater impact on the outcome. Adequate self-care is essential in HF management, as patients with HF who report more effective self-care have better QoL and reduced mortality.1,6 In addition, one of the primary tasks of outpatient HF clinics is to provide information and education in self-care to reduce mortality and improve QoL.1 As both patients with and without ICD currently receive the same follow-up procedure in outpatient HF clinics, the differences in QoL may indicate unmet informational needs within the ICD group. The discrepancy between needs and actual information provision can have consequences for QoL, including insufficient medication adherence.22 In HF treatment, insufficient medication adherence is linked to mortality, thus directly influencing the patient’s prognosis.1 In addition, it may increase the risk of arrhythmia and ICD therapy which may further exacerbate the negative impact on both QoL and mortality.21 The experience of receiving both appropriate and/or inappropriate shock therapy has been shown to contribute to symptoms of depression and anxiety as well as poor QoL.1,8,9,23 Furthermore, post-implantation concerns have been shown to increase the risk of anxiety and depression.24 In the present study, patients responded to single questions in the MLHFQ regarding whether their HF has led to worry or depressive feelings. Although both groups had similar levels of worry and depressive feelings at the first visit, the ICD group experienced more worry at follow-up compared with the group without an ICD. Additionally, they were less frequently treated with anti-depressants, despite experiencing a comparable degree of depressive symptoms as patients without ICD. This contrasts with the current guidelines, which recommend continuous assessment of psychological status and treatment of distress in ICD patients.21 Assessment of depressive symptoms over time and referral to specialists for psychological support if indicated is important to achieve adequate self-care for patients with both HF and ICD.1,25

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is the sample size and multi-centre design. In addition, real-world data captured in real-time is considered highly representative of routine practice, enhancing external validity. The study has no upper age limit, and both older adults and frail patients with comorbidities are included. Despite the large overall heterogeneity of the population, both patients with and without ICD had the same level of questionnaire completion at both visits, which strengthens external validity. The data obtained from NNHFR have, in general, high completeness and consistency, and 90% completed questionnaires at both visits at a national level are considered a very high target achievement.26

There are some limitations of this study. As this study is based on HF registry data, details regarding ICD indication and treatment are limited. A high percentage of ventricular pacing is associated with worse long-term outcomes, including worsening of HF.2 As cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) devices are designed to improve QoL,1 the lack of data on ventricular pacing and additional CRT device may have led to an over- or underestimation of the impact of ICD on QoL, respectively. Moreover, NNHFR does not include data on ICD indication or ICD shock therapy, which could have provided valuable insights into the differences between the groups. However, there is no consensus on the contribution of ICD shocks to QoL.23,27 Although no significant association was found between LVEF and QoL, variations in the timing of LVEF measurement relative to follow-up visits may have resulted in a type II error, preventing the detection of a possible association. Still, clinical symptoms assessed by NYHA classification may reflect the clinical relevance of the LVEF impact, and no significant differences in the impact of NYHA classification on QoL were found between the groups. The lack of an observed association between the time of ICD implantation and QoL at the follow-up visit must be interpreted with caution, as the year of implantation represents a broad time span which may exhibit substantial variability within each unit (year) (see Supplementary material online, Table S2).

Not every patient diagnosed with HF is referred to an outpatient HF clinic. Therefore, this study cannot provide information on whether the study population differs from patients who are not referred. Although all HF clinics in Norway are included in the NNHFR, there are a few areas without an outpatient HF clinic (89% coverage). On the other hand, the hospitals without an outpatient HF clinic are small, with a low number of discharged patients diagnosed with HF (ranging from 33 to 154 patients in 2019). The patient population in NNHFR is comparable to the characteristics of HF patients in the North-European population.28 Additionally, treatment and follow-up procedures are in line with ESC guidelines.1 This may restrict the generalizability to other healthcare systems.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that follow-up in an outpatient HF clinic significantly improve QoL in patients regardless of ICD treatment. However, patients with ICD report less improvement in QoL compared with patients without an ICD. Outpatient HF clinics play an essential role in delivering patient-centred care aimed at mitigating the specific challenges faced by patients with HF. Therefore, tailored interventions through a combined HF-ICD care programme are needed to reduce the burden on both patients and society.
