Community-Based Accompaniment for Adolescents and Young Adults Transitioning into Adult HIV Care: Protocol of a Randomized Control Trial Evaluating the “PASEO” Intervention in Urban Peru
Authors: Molly F Franke, Renato Errea, Alyson Nunez, Milagros Wong, Hugo Sánchez, Carmen C Contreras, Jerome T Galea, Erika Gonzales, Isabel Ccota, Cristina Chávez, Alicia Ramos, Karen Finnegan, Diana Mejía, Jimena Ruiz-Cardenas, Grecia Herrera, Nicole Ocasio-Martinez, Letizia Trevisi, Martha Vibbert, Sonya Shin, Leonid Lecca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe a randomized controlled trial evaluating a nine-month community-based accompaniment intervention (PASEO) to improve HIV outcomes and well-being among adolescents and young adults living with HIV (AYALWH) entering adult-centered HIV care in Lima, Peru. Participants (ages 14–23) are individually randomized (1:1) to PASEO or a modestly enhanced standard of care, with randomization stratified by health facility and caregiver support. The PASEO intervention aims to build health-related skills, enhance knowledge, promote psychosocial adjustment, and reduce logistical, administrative, and structural barriers to care and adherence. Intervention participants are assigned a health promoter who accompanies them throughout their transition to adult HIV care and coordinates their participation in organized intervention activities. Key elements of PASEO include health system navigation, including accompaniment to appointments and support with administrative procedures; regular in-person visits to foster independent health care management and goal setting, provide social support, and address needs in the home and social environment; individualized adherence support; a low-intensity psychological intervention; peer support groups; and education sessions. Each participant is followed for 24 months. The primary outcome is the cumulative incidence of unsuccessful transition (death, loss to follow-up, or unsuppressed viral load >200 copies/mL after six months of ART) at 12 and 24 months. Secondary outcomes include additional HIV-related indicators (e.g., CD4 count, adherence, clinic attendance) and measures of well-being (e.g., social support, self-efficacy, connectedness, transition readiness, stigma, condomless sex, and depression). Participants who experience the primary outcome are invited to receive intervention services.PUBLIC HEALTH SIGNIFICANCEFor adolescents and young adults, the transition to adult-centered HIV care challenges treatment adherence and retention. This protocol describes a rigorous randomized trial design to generate robust data on the effectiveness, implementation, and costs of an intervention aimed at improving transition outcomes and well-being in an urban Latin American setting.Keywords: Support, youth, engagement, viral load, retention

Introduction

Living with HIV in adolescence and young adulthood further complicates a challenging life stage. At a time when the desire for peer acceptance is heightened, HIV-related stigma and isolation can exacerbate feelings of insecurity, low self-esteem, and depression, and cause reluctance to disclose the diagnosis to loved ones.1-3 All of these contribute to suboptimal HIV treatment adherence and engagement in care and limited social and instrumental support.1,4-7 HIV in adolescence and young adulthood is also a marker of other social and health risks, including poverty, decreased access to health services, absence of caregiver support, and sexual violence, all of which negatively impact HIV and general well-being.8-12

For young people living with HIV since birth, the transition from pediatric to adult-centered HIV care is known to be a precarious period.13,14 Less recognized is that young people newly diagnosed with HIV who initiate care in an adult-centered clinic also struggle with this transition.10,15,16 Both groups of young people entering adult HIV care for the first time experience increased rates of disengagement from care and suboptimal adherence.10,13-16 Key obstacles to success in adult-centered care include inadequate preparation to assume the level of independence expected by adult providers, lack of caregiver support (i.e., due to orphanhood, estrangement, lack of disclosure), structural and logistical barriers such as health insurance registration or other documentation required to initiate treatment, financial constraints, HIV-related stigma, perceived lower quality of care in the adult clinic, and the need for successive disclosures to multiple new health care personnel.7,17-20

From a health care delivery perspective, the heterogeneous demographics and social needs of youth entering adult-centered care underscore the limited potential of one-size-fits-all approaches.21 Interventions that provide personal navigation and support, including case management and community-based care, are among the most promising for HIV adult transition outcomes.21 Most research on HIV-related interventions for adolescents and young adults has been conducted in high-burden HIV settings of sub-Saharan Africa or middle-upper-income countries in North America or Europe.21,22 There is notably little HIV care delivery intervention research from Latin America, which tends to comprise middle-income countries with a lower HIV burden that is concentrated in key populations, such as men who have sex with men, transgender women, and individuals who engage in sex work.16 Importantly, interventions designed to help young people effectively bridge the gap to adult HIV care must address the underlying health and social needs of a heterogeneous population in a way that is culturally-tailored and youth-friendly.21,22

We previously evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of PASEO, a community-based accompaniment intervention designed to facilitate the transition to adult HIV care by building self-efficacy and a support network, while addressing short-term needs and supporting long-term goals.9,23 The concept of “accompaniment” is rooted in Latin American liberation theology, which places the lived experiences of the poor at the center of the Christian faith.24 Accompaniment was adopted and popularized by Dr. Paul Farmer, a medical anthropologist and co-founder of Partners In Health, an international nongovernmental organization that partners with governments to strengthen health care delivery and provide high-quality medical care for those most in need.25 It involves addressing the patient’s broader needs in a holistic way, rather than focusing on a single disease, and has been described as walking with someone, breaking bread with them, and simply being with them through difficult moments “…with empathy and expertise, compassion and care…”.26,27

In community-based accompaniment, the ‘community-based’ emphasizes that support occurs in settings beyond the clinic or medical facility and pursues goals that extend beyond treating a specific disease, including meeting diverse social and emotional needs. While “community” can be defined in many ways, the accompaniment team for this project defined it as shared experiences that can be leveraged positively to improve quality of life and safety. The young people targeted for this study predominantly come from lower- to middle-income backgrounds and often reside in densely populated communities characterized by high social risk and insecurity. Access to basic services, including healthcare and education, is frequently inadequate, and in some cases, water and sanitation are also limited. Overcrowded housing conditions further heighten their vulnerability, particularly among migrants who may have limited familial or social networks in Peru. Although variable, there is often a weak sense of belonging and minimal interaction or mutual acquaintance among neighbors.

While an extensive body of scientific literature highlights the effectiveness of community-based accompaniment, its impact on adolescents and young adults living with HIV (AYALWH) and over the long-term has not been evaluated.28-31 Here, we describe the protocol for a randomized trial testing the efficacy of a differentiated version of the PASEO intervention to improve HIV-related health outcomes and overall well-being among AYALWH transitioning to adult-centered HIV care (ClinicalTrials.gov registration: NCT05938803, registered July 10, 2023).

Methods

Study setting

This research is conducted in collaboration with Socios En Salud (Spanish for “Partners In Health”), a nongovernmental organization based in Lima, Peru, that has a long history of collaboration with the Peruvian Ministry of Health to support, scale, and strengthen public health services in under-served communities, with particular focus on tuberculosis and HIV. A pillar of this work has been the employment of community health promoters to provide linkages among patients, communities, and public health services.

The study takes place in metropolitan Lima, which includes Peru’s capital city of Lima and has the highest concentration of HIV in the country, reporting approximately 3,000 new cases each year.32Although HIV is rare in the general adult population—around 0.5%, the prevalence is higher in key populations: estimates suggest a prevalence of 10% among men who have sex with men and 20 to 30% among transgender women.33-36 In 2024, adolescents and young adults under 30 accounted for nearly half of new case notifications.32 Our prior work suggests that only a minority (35%) of newly-diagnosed adolescents and young adults between the ages of 14 and 21 years and living with HIV in Lima remain on treatment with evidence of viral load suppression two years after treatment initiation.10 Furthermore, AYALWH in Lima commonly experience depression, traumatic life events, housing instability, and caregiver loss, all of which may challenge adherence to treatment and engagement in care and underscore the critical importance of comprehensive and tailored support during the early stages of HIV treatment.9,10 Over the last decade, Peru’s Ministry of Health has decentralized public HIV care from large secondary or tertiary hospitals to smaller primary care health centers.

Study population

AYALWH aged 14 to 23 years and aware of their HIV diagnosis are recruited from the HIV clinics at five public hospitals and five primary health centers. Eligible AYALWH meet one of the following criteria: (1) is transitioning from a participating pediatric clinic to a participating adult clinic; (2) has a new HIV diagnosis and is initiating HIV care at a participating adult clinic and, if antiretroviral therapy has begun, has been on it for less than one month.; or (3) has disengaged from treatment within one year of their transition and seeks to reengage in treatment. Additional inclusion criteria include willingness to participate, regardless of the study arm to which they are assigned, and the ability to provide written informed consent or assent. We exclude youth living outside Metropolitan Lima, those who participated in the PASEO pilot study, and those with any condition that would render participation unsafe for the participant or study staff.

Study design and randomization

This study is a two-arm, superiority, parallel-group trial in which participants are individually randomized at a 1:1 ratio to either the PASEO intervention or a modestly enhanced standard of care (Figure 1). Randomization is stratified by health facility and by whether the AYALWH reports having a caregiver—defined as someone (such as a parent, other family member, or confidant) who typically, but not necessarily, lives with them and is able to provide broad treatment support, including financial, emotional, or logistical support. The allocation sequence is a stratified, permuted block-randomization with random block sizes, generated by the U.S.-based PI. No Peru-based study staff has access to the random allocation sequence. The sequence is recorded in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed, and stapled envelopes, stored at the corresponding enrollment site. Data management staff and analysts are blinded to allocation assignment. While data collectors recording outcome measures do not have information on allocation assignment at the time of collection, they are not formally blinded.

Overview of PASEO study design.

Enhanced standard of care

All participants receive the National HIV Program standard care for either a new HIV diagnosis or for transition from a pediatric to an adult care facility. For adolescents transitioning from a pediatric clinic within a referral hospital, the transition procedure typically includes appointments with a nurse, an infectious disease specialist, a psychologist, and a social worker prior to transition. To initiate or continue treatment at the adult clinic, individuals attend appointments with a nurse and physician (either an infectious disease specialist or general doctor trained in HIV care). They may also complete an evaluation with a psychologist and social worker, if available at the health facility. Patients are required to select a treatment supporter (agente de soporte), typically a family member or close friend, who provides accompaniment, emotional support, and assistance to strengthen adherence to treatment. Viral load and CD4 cell counts are measured at treatment initiation. Thereafter, CD4 cell count is measured at 6 and 12 months, and then annually, while viral load is measured at 3, 6, and 12 months, and then annually.37 The standard of care is enhanced by providing services that are not consistently available in public-sector HIV clinics but were deemed important for the ethical conduct of this research. These services include screening for depression, alcohol use, and other substance use. In accordance with the screening results, participants receive psychological first aid from the study psychologist and/or are referred and linked to care at a public hospital or community mental health center. In cases of suicidal ideation, in-person referrals by the study psychologist occur within 24 h. Following randomization, the study provides all participants with a brochure outlining steps for navigating and initiating adult-centered care, a pillbox, a notebook, and a folder for storing medical documents.

PASEO intervention

Participants assigned to the intervention arm receive an enhanced standard of care plus the PASEO intervention. Informed by the SMART socioecological model for transitioning to adult-oriented care and aligned with recently published recommendations from young adults with lived experience, the intervention includes elements to build health-related skills, enhance knowledge, and promote psychosocial adjustment—factors foundational to overall well-being and AYALWH’s capacity to thrive. The intervention also aims to remove/minimize logistical, administrative, and structural barriers to care and adherence, thereby improving transition outcomes.38,39

Intervention participants are assigned a health promoter (an entry-level health worker with a technical degree) who accompanies them throughout their transition to adult care and coordinates their participation in organized intervention activities (Figure 2). In the context of this work, promoters fulfill the role of accompagnateurs, described in other contexts.28,30,40 Accompaniment includes health system navigation, in which health promoters accompany participants to appointments and assist with completing administrative requirements, but extends beyond ensuring that participants attend appointments and collect medications. Promoters aim to support participants holistically, much like supporting a loved one through a difficult time. In this way, the accompaniment component of the intervention is inherently individualized, enabling health promoters to address the heterogeneous needs of AYALWH. To this end, health promoters work closely with each participant to establish trust and offer empathy, checking in with them on an ongoing basis by phone or WhatsApp, and meeting with them in person at least once a month (this occurs more frequently depending on the participant’s needs – for example, pregnant women; patients with concomitant health conditions; and those enrolled following an HIV diagnosis, prior to ART initiation tend to have more in-person support). During these visits, the health promoter addresses emotional well-being and helps the participant develop skills to manage health care independently and to set short- and long-term goals. Ideally, these visits occur in the home so that the promoter can become familiar with participants’ social environments, enabling them to identify needs and challenges related to finances, housing, and relationships within participants’ inner circles (e.g., family, partners, friends) or educational or work settings. Supportive activities may include helping individuals identify an appropriate treatment supporter; sensitizing treatment supporters and family members to how they can provide effective support; identifying additional sources of assistance within the community (e.g., community kitchens, shelters); being present for disclosure conversations; identifying and helping to resolve challenges within the home; and providing information and counseling to participants and treatment supporters about co-occurring conditions (e.g., tuberculosis).41,42 For some participants, particularly those with limited support networks or who have not disclosed their diagnosis, the health promoter serves as a primary source of nonfinancial support during the early phases of treatment, a time often marked by disruption of social networks.41

PASEO intervention components.

PM+: Problem Management Plus

Organized elements of the PASEO intervention include (1) individualized adherence support, for those who need it; (2) the World Health Organization’s low-intensity psychological intervention “Problem Management Plus” (PM+) for stress management, designed for individuals living in communities facing adversity; (3) participation in monthly peer support groups to strengthen social support; and (4) participation in monthly education sessions to fill knowledge deficits in the areas of HIV care, opportunistic infections, sexual health, substance use, legal rights and self-esteem.43

Recognizing the critical role of caregiver support in the transition process, health system navigation and adherence support are differentiated based on its presence.18 For AYALWH with caregiver support, the duration of health system navigation is two months; this is extended to six months for young people without caregiver support. At enrollment, adherence support is offered to those with a history of inconsistent adherence or with a viral load >200 copies/mL despite at least six months of ART; among those newly initiating ART, it is provided to participants who report no caregiver support. Among those who accept, an adherence practice and supervision plan is established with either the caregiver or a Ministry of Health peer supporter. This plan typically consists of six months of in-person or virtual directly observed treatment once daily, or a daily telephone call reminder, depending on the participant. Recognizing that those with suboptimal adherence prior to transition face increased risk of unsuccessful transition, the goal of this adherence support is to provide an opportunity to establish and practice a pill-taking routine.14

The standard duration of the intervention is nine months, delivered in two phases: an intensive phase (six months) and a taper phase (three months). The intensive phase intends to stabilize physical and psychosocial needs, whereas the taper phase aims to prepare individuals for independence the management of their care.44 During the taper phase, health-promotion support decreases as participants are encouraged to increasingly rely on themselves, adult healthcare providers, and their existing social networks. Supervised adherence support decreases in frequency from daily to twice weekly, to once weekly. At the end of the intensive phase, participants with unstable clinical or social circumstances (as assessed by the health promoter and health provider) may extend their intensive phase for up to six additional months before transitioning to the taper phase. During this extension period, all components of the intervention remain in place.

Endpoints

The duration of study participation for each individual participant is 24 months. The primary outcome is the cumulative incidence of unsuccessful transition (death, loss to follow-up [LTFU], or unsuppressed viral load [>200 copies/mL after six months of ART]) within 12 and 24 months. Participants are considered LTFU if 90 days have passed since their missed medication refill. Secondary outcomes include HIV-related outcomes and indicators of well-being, such as HIV-related death or LTFU, CD4 cell count, ART adherence, and clinic attendance, as well as perceived social support, self-efficacy, social connectedness, transition readiness, HIV-related stigma, condomless sexual intercourse, and depression.

Intervention availability for controls with an unsuccessful transition

Participants assigned to the control arm who experience an unsuccessful transition (e.g., LTFU or viral load > 200 copies/mL) are assigned a study endpoint and subsequently invited to enroll in the study intervention. They are not informed of this possibility prior to endpoint assignment.

Data collection

At enrollment (pre-randomization), 6, 9, 12, and 24 months, participants complete a self-administered REDCap survey (delivered via tablet) that captures sociodemographic characteristics and psychosocial and well-being indicators (see Table 1). Tablet-based surveys typically take place at the participant’s home (or other location they deem to be convenient) or during routine clinic visits. Chart reviews are conducted on an ongoing basis up to 24 months post-transition to obtain data on demographic, epidemiologic and social history, study endpoints (retention in treatment, HIV viral load, CD4 cell count), HIV-related complications and infections, pharmacy refills (medications and reasons for changes, including stock-outs) and health care utilization (hospitalizations, clinic visits). Participants lacking a routine viral load or CD4 cell count at baseline, six, nine, 12, and 24 months have one collected for study purposes.

Youth advisory board

A youth advisory board comprised of a diverse group of AYALWH in Lima ages 17-28 guided study design and reviewed all aspects of the study, including data collection instruments and recruitment materials. The board regularly meets throughout the study to advise on implementation questions and challenges and provide feedback on plans for disseminating study results.

Sample size calculation

This study is powered to detect differences in the primary outcome at 12 months. A sample size of 148 participants affords 80% power to detect a difference in the cumulative incidence of unsuccessful transition between arms of +/−20%, assuming an incidence of 35% among controls. Calculations assume two-sided testing and an alpha of 0.05. We enroll 160 participants (an 8% increase) to buffer against transfers and voluntary withdrawals, which we presume to be infrequent based on prior work.45

Statistical analysis

Primary analyses are intention-to-treat. The primary endpoint is analyzed using logistic regression to estimate the probability of an unsuccessful outcome, along with associated risk ratios and risk differences. Clinic visit attendance is modeled using a Poisson distribution and a log link. Time-to-event endpoints are modeled using Cox proportional hazards analyses, with the proportional hazards assumption assessed graphically. Changes in continuous outcomes (e.g., CD4 cell count, well-being scale scores) from baseline to each follow-up point are used to estimate the difference-in-differences, using an identity link and a normal distribution, transforming variables as needed. For all endpoints, we conduct stratified analyses to examine whether any intervention effect on outcomes is modified by the differentiated care pathway at enrollment, identifying potential interactions with an interaction p-value < 0.10.

We will conduct subgroup analyses by differentiated care pathway (caregiver support at enrollment). Exploratory subgroup analyses may be conducted (e.g., based on viral suppression at enrollment, pregnancy, or identification as a sexual or gender minority); however, because sample sizes may be small for some subgroups, we complement these data with qualitative analysis using purposive sampling. We conduct descriptive analyses of outcomes among controls who, subsequent to an unfavorable transition outcome, choose to receive PASEO services.

In all analyses, we compare the distributions of potential confounders and adjust for chance imbalances. We conduct on-treatment analyses accounting for intervention fidelity (i.e., dose). Our prior data do not suggest clustering of outcomes by site; however, we will test for intra-cluster correlation and, if present, assume a compound symmetry correlation structure to account for clustering. Model-based multiple imputation strategies are used to account for missing covariate data, as needed. Missing viral load measurements are considered unsuppressed, with sensitivity analyses conducted under alternative assumptions. We do not anticipate a need to account for permuted block randomization in analyses because the intra-block correlation is expected to be 0.46 There are no planned interim analyses or stopping guidelines.

Implementation assessment

Qualitative and quantitative indicators of intervention feasibility and implementation align with each of the five RE-AIM implementation framework tenets (reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance).47 Quantitative indicators (e.g., participation rates for intervention components, fulfillment of education session learning objectives) are assessed using participation records, study logs, and surveys, and analyzed descriptively. Qualitative indicators, such as short- and long-term impacts of the intervention and factors that challenge participation or fidelity, are determined through focus groups and in-depth interviews with a purposive sample of study participants and key informants (study implementers and health providers) once study participation has concluded. Semi-structured interview guides for sessions with study participants explore perceptions of intervention components, the quality of intervention support, and intervention impacts on treatment adherence, overall health, and life more broadly; guides for key informants focus on implementation strategies and challenges, experiences with key populations, and integration with health center services. Open-ended questions and probes allow all participants to elaborate on PASEO’s effectiveness and its potential for widespread adoption. The sessions will be conducted by a Peruvian nurse experienced in qualitative research with AYALWH who has had limited prior interaction with study participants. All sessions will be audio-recorded. Qualitative data analysts will code focus group and interview transcripts employing Framework Analysis and using a codebook derived from the interview guides.48 Coded data is extracted into thematic matrices to determine implementation facilitators and barriers.

Costing analysis

We measure the costs of the control and intervention arms in three categories: health systems costs, implementation costs, and out-of-pocket costs incurred by participants and their caregivers. Health system cost data is gathered from financial reports of participating health facilities and reviews of patient clinical records. Implementer costs are determined using study budgets and accounting records. Participant-level cost data on out-of-pocket expenses is self-reported at six, 12, and 24 months. Costs collected over time are adjusted for inflation and standardized. We report the total costs of the PASEO intervention, costs per participant, and costs per successful transition. Costs are also broken down into health system-level, implementer-level, and patient-level by medical and non-medical costs. We estimate the incremental cost of the PASEO intervention, relative to the standard of care, using a generalized linear regression model at the individual patient level.

Research ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Harvard Medical School, the Bioethics Committee of Via Libre (Peru), and the ethics committees at participating healthcare facilities and is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05938803). Participants aged 18 or older provide written informed consent, whereas those <18 years old require the written informed consent of their legal guardian, in addition to written assent. Waivers of guardian consent are approved if a minor has not disclosed their diagnosis to a legal guardian; does not have a legal guardian or is estranged from their parent or guardian; is at least 16 years of age and either married, pregnant, a mother, or financially independent; or if the legal guardian lacks the mental capacity to grant consent. Participants receive 50 soles (approximately 15 USD) for each completed survey, focus group, or interview. This remuneration was informed by discussions with the youth advisory board and amounts provided in other ongoing studies. Transportation is provided or reimbursed for any travel required for participation in the study or, as applicable, PASEO intervention activities.

Discussion

We describe the protocol for a randomized trial evaluating the efficacy of a differentiated community-based accompaniment intervention for AYALWH entering adult-centered HIV care for the first time. Strengths of this intervention include differentiation based on caregiver support and consideration of each participant’s unique needs. By offering a core set of components, including youth-friendly options that reflect participants’ developmental level, with the option to intensify support for those most in need, we seek to accommodate a population of young people diverse in terms of age, maturity, caregiver support, time living with HIV, other health needs, and social considerations. We acknowledge that tailored services may add complexity and cost and hypothesize that these investments will pay dividends in the long term.

A recent systematic review highlighted the dearth of HIV-related intervention studies addressing the transition period, as the existing literature comprises only small feasibility and acceptability studies, none of which were conducted in Latin America.22 This study will address a critical knowledge gap and key limitations of previous research. Unique strengths of this study design include the inclusion of young people with recent HIV infection (in addition to those with perinatal acquisition), collaboration with a youth advisory board, use of biological outcomes in combination with indicators of well-being, and extended long-term follow-up for 24 months (i.e., 15 months after intervention completion for most intervention participants).13 It also includes a plan to evaluate implementation and costs, to ensure that policymakers have the data-driven roadmap required for potential future scale-up, should the intervention prove effective.

We have identified several important limitations to the study design and PASEO intervention. First, young people, and particularly migrants, are highly mobile. For individuals who move outside of Peru, it is often impossible to distinguish between transfers of care and losses to follow-up. Second, given the known health and social vulnerabilities of the study population, we opted to enhance the standard of care by providing depression and substance use screening and referral to all participants. This decision was made in collaboration with the youth advisory board, in response to ethical concerns about addressing unmet mental health needs, and to facilitate study retention. However, the effect of the PASEO intervention relative to an enhanced standard of care may be less relevant than its effect relative to the "real-world" unenhanced standard of care. Third, although digital technologies may offer a convenient and cost-effective means of providing support, we chose to implement our intervention primarily in person, relying on digital technology for adherence support and in exceptional cases when in-person support was infeasible. Although more resource-intensive, this decision was driven by our experience in the pilot study, which overlapped with the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic and associated shutdowns. Pilot study participants overwhelmingly preferred in-person participation to virtual participation, both for social connection and due to privacy concerns at home (i.e., lack of disclosure to family or roommates and shared living spaces with limited privacy). Fourth, engagement with caregivers is not prescriptive but organic, occurring as needed when a caregiver is present. With participant permission, caregivers can attend monthly visits with the health promoter to review progress and discuss both short-term and long-term goals. In other circumstances, health promoters accompany participants when they disclose their HIV diagnosis to family, field questions, and provide comfort to caregivers. Finally, this version of PASEO is time-limited (9 to 15 months), intended to span the early transition period; however, some individuals may require prolonged support, which PASEO is not designed to address. Also, while the intervention may not ameliorate all health system-level challenges that contribute to unfavorable HIV outcomes (i.e., medication stock-outs or enacted stigma by healthcare personnel), the instrumental and social support provided by the intervention may buffer against their effects.

Conclusions

By incorporating robust multidimensional endpoints, extended follow-up of up to 24 months, and including key populations of AYALWH—including those who acquired HIV during adolescence and young adulthood—this study is uniquely positioned to address a critical knowledge gap in HIV service delivery: how best to support adolescents and young adults transitioning into adult-centered HIV care in urban Latin America.
