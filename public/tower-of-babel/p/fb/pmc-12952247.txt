Consequences of adverse events in cancer immunotherapy combinations – a comprehensive real-world analysis
Authors: Siv Gyda Aanes, Carsten Nieder, David Westfall Bates, Siri Wiig, Ellinor Christin Haukland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are pivotal in systemic anticancer therapy. The aim of the study was to investigate incidence, severity and consequences of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) in ICI combinations by comparing different adverse events classification systems.MethodsA retrospective multicentre cohort study included patients treated with ICI combinations. IrAEs were assessed by CTCAE v.5, NCC MERP index and SITC consensus for chronic irAEs.ResultsAmong 612 patients, 244 (40%) experienced 354 irAEs, in total 58 irAEs per 100 patients varying between treatments. Higher incidence was observed for autoimmune disease, thoracic radiotherapy, melanoma, dual ICI and ICI/TKI. Severe irAEs occurred in 147 patients, leading to treatment discontinuation and hospitalization in half of the patients. Chronic irAEs were common, with 64% persisting beyond three months after ICI discontinuation, 22% of these resolved (median 135 steroid days, 154 days to resolution). Common chronic toxicities included endocrine, pulmonary or rheumatic irAEs. Fatal irAEs affected 16 patients, primarily from pneumonitis, colitis or hepatitis.ConclusionNCC MERP better captured clinical consequences compared to CTCAE, while SITC criteria uniquely identified long-term irAEs. Severe and chronic irAEs were frequent, emphasizing the need for improved communication about irAE management and prospective studies with revised toxicity definitions.ClinicalTrials.govIDNCT06476158.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have significantly improved outcomes for cancer patients either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy or targeted therapies [1]. In general, adverse events (AE) defined as an adverse outcome emerging from medical care rather than the patient`s condition occurred twice as often in cancer patients receiving standard systemic anticancer treatment than in non-cancer patients [2]. Severe toxicity has been reported in 10–20% of patients receiving PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, with rates increasing to 55% in those undergoing treatment with combined CTLA-4 and PD-1 inhibition [3]. Fatal irAEs occur in 0.4–1.3% of cases, typically early in treatment [3,4]. This illustrates the complexity of the cancer patient were balancing positive and negative consequences of systemic cancer treatment remains a significant challenge. While ICIs enhance the immune system’s ability to target malignancies, they are associated with immune-related adverse events (irAEs).

Unlike chemotherapy-related AE, the incidence and severity of irAE are influenced by additional factors such as preexisting autoimmune conditions, genetic predispositions, microbiota or cancer type [3–5]. On the contrary, some evidence suggests a correlation between irAEs and improved survival outcomes [6–8]. However, limited data exist regarding the specific consequences of irAEs on individual patients, including treatment interruptions, quality of life, hospitalization, duration of immunosuppression permanent harm and survivorship.

Recent studies highlight the persistence of long-term irAEs other than permanent endocrine irAEs, which may last months or years post-ICI discontinuation [9–15]. These were often rheumatological, neurological, gastrointestinal or dermatological with a median duration of 180 days [10,12]. In the adjuvant setting chronic irAEs affected over 40% of patients, with 35% resolving at 18 months follow-up including half of the non-endocrine irAE [11,13]. Chronic irAEs often necessitate prolonged immunosuppressive therapy, potentially causing permanent organ damage, reduced quality of life or functional impairments. For the individual cancer patient some of the persisting irAEs may have minimal impact, while others require long-term immunosuppression with its own potential harmful side-effects. Given the persistence of chronic toxicities, it is crucial to address the limitations of pivotal ICI trials which primarily focused on acute toxicities in metastatic settings. Data about chronic irAEs and late effects have emerged through new retrospective trials and real-world data. However, as long-term survivors and adjuvant/neoadjuvant ICI settings emerge, evidence is rapidly accumulating [9–15]. These findings highlight the necessity for prolonged irAE monitoring and management of irAEs in certain patients, even after discontinuation of ICI therapy. Moreover, there is a need for real-world data to investigate how new systemic cancer treatment affect patients in a holistic perspective as clinical studies predominately focused on acute toxicities within a limited timeframe.

IrAEs are commonly graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) which guide clinical management of the specific irAE [16]. However, the current version of CTCAE lacks terminology specific to the unique nature of many irAEs. The Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) are working for changes including extended irAEs terms for CTCAE v.6 and has in 2023 published a consensus definition of chronic irAEs defined as persisting beyond three months post-ICI discontinuation [15]. SITC do not describe what consequence or severity the irAE have for the patient but incorporate the timeframe missing in CTCAE. The National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention (NCC MERP) Index for categorizing medication errors assesses the impact of adverse events (AEs) on individual patients [17]. However, the absence of a defined time frame by this categorization may lead to an underestimation of chronic irAEs eventually resolving over time.

To facilitate the identification, comparison and monitoring of irAEs there is a need for a robust classification system and a deeper understanding of all patient consequences of irAEs associated with various ICI treatment regiments. Such information could support and enhance patient communication regarding potential risks and follow-up during ICI therapy, as well as empower the patient in taking an informed choice when starting ICI treatment. Our study aims to investigate the incidence, severity and consequences of acute and chronic irAEs in a real-world cancer population treated with various ICI regimens, by comparing the different classification methods CTCAE v.5, NCC MERP index and SITC consensus for chronic irAEs.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A retrospective multicentre cohort study covering a complete healthcare region was conducted across all 12 publicly funded hospitals in Northern Norway, from January 2020 to September 2023. The region serves a population of 486,000 inhabitants and includes two oncological departments (University Hospital Tromsø and Nordland Hospital trust Bodø). Additionally, 10 rural nurse-driven outpatient clinics located in local hospitals or district medical centers. Patients were followed for a minimum of 13 months, until November 2024 or death. The study included curative or palliative patients aged ≥18 years with solid tumors, excluding gynecological, lymphoma and hematological cancers. Eligible patients received ICI monotherapy (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, durvalumab, atezolizumab, avelumab, or cemiplimab), dual immunotherapy (CTLA4/PD1), or ICI combinations with chemotherapy (carboplatin, pemetrexed, paclitaxel, vinorelbine), vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGF)- inhibitors (bevacizumab), or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sunitinib, axitinib, cabozantinib). Classification and grading of irAEs

IrAEs were classified by treatment combination, onset timing, type, severity (at onset and peak), treatment (e.g., steroid use and duration), duration and consequences for therapy. Only grade 2–5 irAEs, as defined by CTCAE v.5, were included in the analysis. Grade 1 events were excluded due to frequent underreporting in electronic health records (EHRs) and their lack of treatment implications. By assessing information from the region`s shared EHR sources, including clinical notes, discharge summaries, laboratory values and radiology reports, all irAEs were classified by the authors (SGA, EH). If the classification was inconclusive, it was discussed with the other authors.

To evaluate the duration of irAEs in relation to time the SITC consensus definition was used to categorize the irAEs into two groups: 1) Acute irAEs: defined as events occurring during ICI treatment and resolving within 12 weeks (90 days) and 2) Chronic irAEs defined as events persisting more than 3 months following the discontinuation of ICI treatment. Chronic irAEs were further subdivided into two categories: 2a) Chronic active with ongoing organ inflammation requiring immunosuppression or 2b) Chronic inactive which persist without active inflammation or immunosuppression. Recurrent irAEs after complete resolution were classified as new events. Multisystem irAEs were categorized as individual irAEs.

In addition, the NCC MERP Index was employed to categorize irAEs by harm severity into group E-I for the individual patient. Based on clinical implications documented in the EHR, irAEs were further divided into the following categories E: Temporary harm requiring intervention, F: Temporary harm requiring hospitalization or prolonging hospital stay, G: Permanent harm, H: Life-sustaining intervention required and I: Death resulting from harm.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Patient data were extracted from the region’s shared EHR, excluding cases with incomplete clinical information or missing observation periods. The region’s healthcare system ensured comprehensive coverage of all cancer treatment in hospitals and outpatient clinics. Collected data included patient demographics as cancer type and stage, local hospital, preexisting autoimmune diseases, comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index), and concurrent thoracic radiotherapy. In addition, treatment details were collected as ICI type and combination, frequency, duration, treatment lines, and treatment intent.

Descriptive statistics and data analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel – Office 365 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA), and IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Chi-square tests, t-tests, and logistic regression were used for univariate analyses where appropriate.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics Northern Norway (REK North, Tromsø, Norway, ref nr 319,277). Nordland Hospital Trust is the owner of the dataset, and permission has been obtained to use the information in the repository for the purposes of the research conducted.

Results

A total of 612 patients received at least one dose of ICI (see Figure 1 for details). The median age of the cohort was 70 years (range 25–86 years). By 1 November 2024, a total of 357 patients (58%) had deceased. The majority of patients were treated with palliative intent, and most received ICI therapy, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, as a first-line treatment. Lung cancer patients (Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer, NSCLC, and Small Cell Lung Cancer, SCLC) were the most prevalent cancer type followed by melanoma. Incidence and type of irAEs related to treatment type

In the cohort, 244 patients (40%) experienced one or more irAEs, resulting in a total of 354 irAEs. The incidence of irAEs was 58 irAEs per 100 patients across all treatment combinations, with 40% of irAEs being grades over CTCAE grade 2. The type and incidence of irAEs varied depending on the ICI treatment combination. As expected, the CTLA 4/PD1 combination had the highest incidence of irAEs (167 per 100 patients) and the highest proportion with multiple irAEs per patient (8 patients, 27% > 3 irAEs). In contrast, the ICI/VEGF combinations had the lowest incidence of irAE (29 per 100 patients), consisting of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma. Lung-related irAEs were the most common in the total population affecting 84 patients (24%). The highest incidence was observed in patients receiving ICI chemotherapy (50 patients, 32%) commonly prescribed to lung cancer patients. For the CTLA4/PD1 combination, irAEs involving the liver, biliary or pancreas were the most common (22 patients, 37%), followed by gastrointestinal (GI) irAEs (13 patients, 22%). For the TKI/ICI combination, endocrine irAEs were the most frequent (4 patients, 29%). Risk factors for irAes

A significantly higher incidence of irAEs was observed among patients with preexisting autoimmune disorder (chi-square = 10.5, p = 0.001) or those who had received concurrent thoracic radiotherapy (chi-square 5.1, p = 0.024). There were no significant differences across age groups or gender. Types of cancer were significantly associated with risk for irAEs (p < 0.001, chi-square 51.6, Nagel R2 = 0.109). Specifically, breast cancer (p = 0.043, OR 7, CI 1.0–36.6) and melanoma (p = 0.016, OR 3.6, CI 1.2–8.1) were associated with an increased risk of irAEs compared to other types of cancer. Among the treatment combinations CTLA4/PD1 (p < 0.001, OR 8.5, CI 3.5–20.7) and ICI/TKI (p = 0.016, OR 3.4, CI 1.2–9.2) were significantly associated with increased risk of irAEs. The categories were not well balanced, as the ICI monotherapy and ICI chemotherapy cohorts represented 37% and 50% of the study population, respectively. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution (Nagel R2 = 0.09). When comparing the deceased patients to survivors, the deceased population experienced significantly fewer irAEs (p < 0.001, kappa −0.141).

Severity of irAEs and consequences for patients

Assessing high grades irAEs (grade 3–5) in the total population, 147 patients experienced at least one high grade irAE, corresponding to an incidence of 24%. High grade irAEs were observed for 26 patients (72%) within the CTLA4/PD1 cohort, compared to 138 patients (24%) across the other treatment combinations. The most common treatment consequence of an irAE was discontinuation of ICI treatment (198 irAEs, 56%), particular for ICI/VEGF (8 irAEs, 100%) and CTLA4/PD1 (40 irAEs, 60%). In contrast, most irAEs for patients treated with ICI/TKI led to a temporary pause in treatment (8 irAEs, 50%), utilized mainly by patients with renal cell carcinoma.

According to the NCC MERP Index, most irAEs required hospitalization (144 irAEs, 41%, highest for the ICI/VEGF (6 irAEs, 75%) and CTLA 4/PD1 (34 irAEs, 57%) groups. In contrast, irAEs in the ICI/TKI group differed, with most irAEs being either permanent (7 irAEs, 44%) or managed in out-patient care (6 irAEs, 38%). Only 19% (3 irAEs) were admitted to hospital.

Chronic and permanent irAEs

According to SITC, the overall incidence of chronic irAEs not resolved three months after discontinuation of ICI was 64% (225 irAEs). When excluding endocrine irAEs, the incidence of non-endocrine chronic irAEs was 49% (172 irAEs). SITC identified 146 permanent non-resolving chronic irAEs (41%), of which 75 irAEs (21%) were chronic active, requiring ongoing immunosuppression. A total of 79 irAEs resolved after three months discontinuation of ICI (chronic inactive) with a median steroid duration of 135 days (mean 154, range 2–470) and a median time to resolution of 153 days (mean 189, range 92–470). Prednisone, dexamethasone, and methylprednisolone were the most commonly steroids, along with cortisone acetate for endocrine substitution.

In contrast, the 129 irAEs that resolved within three months (acute irAEs) had a median steroid duration of 42 days and a median time to resolution of 42 days (range 1–90). For both chronic active (requiring ongoing immunosuppression) and chronic inactive irAEs, the two largest treatment cohorts were ICI/TKI (14 irAEs, 88%) and CTLA4/PD1 (42 irAEs, 70%). ICI/VEGF had the lowest proportion of both chronic active (1 irAE, 13%) and chronic inactive irAEs (2 irAEs, 2%) compared to the other ICI treatment groups.

Permanent harm, as classified by the NCC MERP index (category G), was the second largest group of irAEs with 144 irAEs (32%). Permanent harm was most common in the ICI/TKI-cohort (7 irAEs, 44%) and least common in the ICI/VEGF cohort (1 irAE, 13%, by SITC classification 3 irAEs, 38%).

The SITC classification identified more than twice as many chronic irAEs (225 vs 114) compared to the NCC MERP classification. In both classifications systems, endocrine irAEs were the most common permanent irAEs, followed by lung-related irAEs. The highest proportion of endocrine irAEs was observed in the ICI/TKI-cohort (4 irAEs, 57%) and the CTLA4/PD1 cohort (6 irAEs, 46%) according to the NCC MERP classification. In contrast, SITC identified the highest proportion of endocrine irAEs in the ICI monotherapy (18 irAEs, 26%) and the ICI chemotherapy (24 irAEs, 25%) cohorts. According to NCC MERP, lung-related irAEs were the second most reported permanent irAEs, with cases divided between ICI chemotherapy (10 irAEs, 21%) and ICI monotherapy (9 irAEs, 2%). For SITC, the ICI chemotherapy cohort accounted for the largest proportion of lung-related irAEs (27 irAEs, 28%). A major difference between the classification systems was observed related to the third most common chronic irAE. Defined by NCC MERP rheumatology-related irAEs were the third largest group (15 irAEs, 13%), with the highest proportions in the ICI/TKI (4 irAEs, 29%) and ICI monotherapy (20 irAEs, 20%) cohorts. In contrast, SITC identified liver, biliary or pancreas-related irAEs (34 irAEs, 15%) as the third largest group with the highest proportion from CTLA4/PD1 (17, 41%). IrAEs causing or contributing to intensive care or death

Intervention necessary to sustain life due to an irAE, as classified by NCC MERP (category H) was required for 14 patients (2% of the total study population), involving 18 irAEs, of these, 12 patients (86%) were receiving palliative care. Compared to CTCAE grade 4, a total of 33 patients (5%) experienced a life-threatening irAE. Pneumonitis was the leading cause of life-sustaining interventions affecting 7 patients (50%).

IrAEs contributed to or caused death as classified by CTCAE grade 5 or NCC MERP category I, in 16 patients (3% of the total population, 5% of all irAEs). All 16 patients were receiving palliative care, with a median age of 67 years. Of these, 9 patients (56%) had lung cancer and 4 patients (25%) melanoma. Assessed both by NCC MERP and CTCAE, the irAEs requiring life-sustaining interventions or causing death were associated with treatments involving CTLA4/PD1, ICI/chemotherapy or ICI monotherapy. The most common irAEs leading to death was pneumonitis (6 patients), followed by colitis (3 patients) and hepatitis (3 patients).

Discussion

This retrospective, observational real-world study demonstrates that moderate to severe irAEs are common and vary across different ICI treatment combinations. One in four irAEs was a high-grade irAE, and irAEs frequently led to treatment discontinuation, hospital admission or became permanent. More than half of all irAEs progressed to a chronic state, predominantly presenting as chronic inactive conditions. Additionally, the proportion of patients requiring intensive care or experienced fatal outcomes was twice as high as reported in clinical studies [3,4]. The different classification methods each have their own value in capturing various patient consequences of irAEs, however none fully captured the complete spectrum of consequences in the patient perspective.

Incidence of moderate and high-grade irAEs

An irAE occurred in 40% of the patients, and almost one-third experienced two or more irAEs either concurrently or recurrently. This is consistent with a recent systematic review reporting a mean incidence rate for any grade irAE at 40%, with rates of 30.5% for ICI monotherapy and 45.7% for ICI combination therapy [3]. CTLA4/PD1 and ICI TKI combinations had higher irAE incidence rates, while ICI/VEGF had the lowest (18%). Moderate irAEs (grade 2) were most common with ICI monotherapy and ICI/TKI, suggesting a more favorable safety profile. Reporting irAE per 100 patients may be misleading, as a considerable proportion of the patients experienced more than one irAE.

High-grade (grade 3–5) irAEs for ICI combinations, excluding the CTLA4/PD1-cohort, had an incidence of 24%, which is slightly higher than earlier previously reported data (19.7%) or rates observed in RCTs (10–20%) [3]. This may be partially explained by the large proportion of lung cancer patients who often receive concurrent thoracic radiotherapy. The CTLA4/PD1-cohort demonstrated a significant higher incidence of high-grade irAEs, a finding in line with the mechanism of action and previous literature [3]. When comparing the incidence of irAEs to other systemic anticancer treatments as chemotherapy, a registry study from 2020 found that serious AE affected 44.5% of the patients. The rates were even higher for patients with metastatic disease (53.3%), comorbidities (51.3%) or bronchial-pulmonary cancers (59%) [18]. A systematic review and meta-analysis from 2021 investigated treatment-related AE from combinations therapies as radiation therapy, chemotherapy and targeted therapy with PD1 or PDL1 inhibitors. The incidence for grade 3 or higher AE was 68.3% in the chemotherapy combination, in the targeted therapy combination 47.3%, in the radiotherapy combination 12.4% compared to the ICI monotherapy cohort with 35.9% [19]. They concluded that patients treated with ICI and chemotherapy are at increased risk of treatment related AE compared to other ICI combinations with PD1 or PDL1 inhibition [19]. It is likely that our cohort, which includes a large proportion of lung cancer patients treated with ICI/chemotherapy may have a larger total AE burden than captured as our study only assessed irAEs. Further research should investigate real-world and prospective data including all treatment-related AE over time to better understand the total burden and consequences of iatrogenic harm for the cancer patient.

Interestingly, a significantly lower incidence of irAEs was observed among patients who died during follow-up. This finding may support evidence about an association between irAEs and better survival outcomes, particular for lower grade irAEs, as well as skin and endocrine irAEs which several clinical studies have shown [6–8,20]. However, a meta-analysis of several randomized trials from 2023 found only weak correlations between the effect of ICI therapy on overall or specific irAE rates and overall survival across different cancer types [8]. In our study, the weak association with a low kappa value indicates that other factors such as disease burden, treatment efficacy and/or patient comorbidities may have had a stronger influence on survival and could explain this result. This study was not designed to investigate the correlation between irAEs and survival but this will be explored together with other endpoints in future research.

The variation in irAE types and frequencies across treatment combinations underscores the influence of specific therapies on immune activation and organ-specific toxicity. Lung-related irAEs were the most common among the total population and for ICI chemotherapy, likely due to the predomination of lung-cancer patients. Lung cancer patients have a higher incidence of ICI-related pneumonitis compared to other cancer patients, which may be attributed to preexisting pulmonary conditions, as well as they often receive concurrent or sequential thoracic radiotherapy. In the literature, the incidence rates for ICI -mediated pneumonitis with no thoracic radiation is 12,5%. Compared to incidence rates after thoracic radiation and treatment with ICI of all grades and > grade 2 pneumonitis were 36% and 3% [21–23]. Our study cohort consist of generally more elderly and frailer patients with lung cancer, and did often receive thoracic radiotherapy which could have influenced the high rates of pneumonitis.

For the CTLA4/PD1-cohort consisting of patients with melanoma and renal cell cancer, liver and GI toxicity were predominant. Additionally, this cohort had the highest incidence of irAEs per patient and highest frequency of multiple toxicities per patient. The high irAE burden emphasizes the importance of careful patient selection, thorough patient education and close monitoring. Endocrine dysfunction was the most common irAE in patients treated with ICI/TKI, which is in line with evidence emerged as endocrine irAEs are one of the most common AE associated with TKI and ICI combinations [24]. The ability TKIs have to disrupt hormonal pathways may be amplified in combination with ICI [24,25]. Patients with hepatocellular carcinoma treated with ICI/VEGF in this cohort had the lowest incidence of irAEs. This may be explained by specific mechanism of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characteristic of hepatocellular carcinoma, or random variation due to the small cohort size [26].

The significant association between concurrent thoracic radiotherapy and irAEs can be explained by a large lung cancer population and highlights the need to be aware of the development of pneumonitis after radiotherapy. Despite the unbalanced distribution of patients in the treatment groups which necessitates cautious interpretation, some interesting results emerged. Specifically, patients with breast cancer and melanoma demonstrated a higher likelihood of developing irAEs. In melanoma patients, this likelihood can be explained by treatment with CTLA4/PD1, a more potent combination compared to ICI monotherapy [3]. In breast cancer patients other explanations are likely as they were treated with ICI chemotherapy. The limited sample size and broad confidence intervals warrants a cautious interpretation of the results. Further research is needed to explore this finding in a larger population of breast cancer patients.

Consequences for cancer treatment and hospitalization

For three out of four patients, an irAE resulted in either a pause or discontinuation of treatment. To stop a potentially lifesaving treatment can be fatal for cancer patients. The study was not designed to assess the correlation between treatment pauses or discontinuation and survival. However, previous studies suggest that favorable outcomes are possible in patients who did not complete ICI therapy due to moderate irAEs [6–8]. Psychologically, to end an effective treatment planned for a longer duration can cause stress and anxiety for the patient. Therefore, it is important to provide support and effective treatment of irAEs to ensure the best possible outcomes despite stop or pause in ICI treatment.

Although 4 out of 10 irAEs were classified as CTCAE grade 2 (moderate), only 2 out of 10 irAEs were managed without hospital admission to or progression to permanent harm. This discrepancy in grading between CTCAE and NCC MERP reflects how distinct classification systems focus on different aspects of an irAE. In a patient perspective, time spent in the hospital managing AE instead of at home can negatively impact quality of life. Furthermore, it can add potential new harm from admissions itself and reduce precious time with family and caregivers, summarized as time toxicity [9,27]. Previous research has shown that cancer patients have a significantly higher risk of iatrogenic harm when admitted to hospital compared to a general hospital population due to often longer length of stay and older age [28]. Regarding moderate irAEs, the NCC MERP index provides a more comprehensive description of the patient implications compared to CTCAE v.5.

Consequences of long-term irAEs; chronic or permanent

The incidence of chronic irAEs exceed the reported rates for adjuvant ICI monotherapy ( > 40%) across all ICI treatment combinations, except for ICI/VEGF. Published data on the incidence of chronic irAEs in the adjuvant setting reports rates of 43–46% for ICI monotherapy ICI [11,13]. There is less available data on the incidence of chronic irAEs in the metastatic setting for the different ICI combinations, possibly due to the complex clinical course of these patients. Factors such as limited survival, transitions to other systemic or regional therapies or symptoms due to progressive disease may confound the results [10]. In a study from 2022 including 161 melanoma patients in both adjuvant or metastatic setting, Tong et al., found a permanent irAE (defined by no complete resolution) in 66% of patients treated with dual immunotherapy and 35% of those treated with monotherapy [14]. Assessed by NCC MERP, irAEs classified as permanent harm (category G) occurred in approximately 40% of patients treated with monotherapy ICI and ICI/TKI, while all other treatment combinations had lower incidence. Permanent endocrine irAEs were the predominant irAEs in all treatment combinations, except for ICI/VEGF.

For all treatment combinations, NCC MERP classified fewer irAEs as permanent compared to the SITC consensus of chronic irAEs. The SITC detected more chronic irAEs overall, including more long term irAEs affecting lungs, liver/biliary and gastrointestinal system compared to NCC MERP. As a result, NCC MERP may underreport long-term irAEs and is less likely to capture irAEs requiring long-term immunosuppression or ongoing monitoring if used without the SITC consensus definition.

Need for immunosuppressive treatment

Acute irAEs were treated with steroids for a median duration of 42 days. For all ICI combinations and monotherapy, except ICI/VEGF (13%), the incidence for chronic active irAEs requiring ongoing immunosuppressive treatment was approximately 20%. In comparison, a multicentre retrospective melanoma cohort receiving adjuvant ICI monotherapy reported that 29% had persisting irAEs after 18 months with 21% requiring steroids or other management [13]. For chronic irAEs persisting over three months before resolving (chronic inactive), the median duration of steroid use in our study was 135 days, with a mean of 154 days. This is slightly longer than the median of 120 days reported in a systematic review from 2023 [12]. Classifying some chronic irAEs was challenging, as some of the palliative patients needed permanent steroids until death unrelated to the resolved irAE, a risk for confounding the results. Potential AEs related to immunosuppression such as increased vulnerability for infections, and long-term steroid use can affect multiple organ systems. These include neuropsychiatric and metabolic systems and may even lead to secondary malignancy further impairing the cancer patient’s health [29]. Therefore, it is important to monitor patients to achieve the fastest possible resolution at the lowest effective dose of immunosuppressive therapy. Neither CTCAE nor NCC MERP provide any information about the duration of immunosuppression or monitoring, whereas SITC captures the time frame of the irAE and need for immunosuppression. For chronic inactive irAEs, we observed a median time to resolution of 154 days, with a mean of 189 days and a maximum 470 days. In comparison, Barron et al. reported a median duration of non-endocrine chronic irAEs of 180 days with the longest persisting 2370 days, which exceeds the durations observed in our study [12]. Differences in follow-up durations as well as the large population of palliative patients in out cohort may have influenced the results.

Life-threatening or fatal irAEs

Mortality related to irAEs is higher compared to the mortality in previous clinical studies, but comparable to other real-world cohorts. This may reflect the more comorbid and complex patient population, which includes a disproportionately high number of palliative lung cancer patients compared to clinical studies [3,30,31]. In our cohort, 38% of the fatal irAEs and 39% of the irAEs requiring intensive care were related to pneumonitis. This finding supports emerging evidence of a higher incidence of pneumonitis in real-world patients compared to clinical trials (19% versus 3–5%) particularly in patients with NSCLC. Additionally, 35% of PD-1/PDL1-related deaths were attributed to pneumonitis, and worse outcomes were observed in patients with irAE-related pneumonitis [21,22]. The predominance of lung cancer patients with both SCLC and NSCLC in the study cohort likely explains the higher incidence of pneumonitis and the associated worse outcomes. NCC MERP and CTCAE captured the same fatal consequences in the study cohort, however, CTCAE classified more irAEs as life threatening compared to NCC MERP. This discrepancy may be explained by CTCAE identifying more serious biochemical toxicities as hepatitis or nephritis, not requiring life-sustaining interventions, but still necessitating medical interventions due to threatened organ function.

Comparing classification methods and implications

Different classification methods for grading capture varying patient consequences of irAEs. CTCAE v.5 guide clinical actions, while NCC MERP better describes patient outcomes as hospitalization or permanent harm over the course. In contrast, SITC captures the long-term timeframe of some irAEs. The NCC MERP index provided a more comprehensive assessment of the consequences of irAEs for patients, particularly regarding hospitalization or permanent harm compared to the CTCAE. Additionally, the SITC consensus identified chronic irAEs requiring long-term immunosuppression, which were not captured by either CTCAE v.5 or the NCC MERP Index. CTCAE v.5 captured more life-threatening irAEs than the NCC MERP Index. None of the classifications assess how acute or long-term irAEs can affect the patient’s quality of life, including time toxicity (time spent in health care interaction for irAE management), functional impairments, return to work and financial toxicity, harm from immunosuppression itself or other patient-centered outcome aspects.

The study highlights that moderate and severe irAEs are more common in the general real-world population compared to study populations. These irAEs frequently result in clinical implications as need for hospitalization or need for long-term follow-up that imposes time toxicity on patients or long-term harm.

In the immunotherapy era, there is a need for an updated view and vigilance on patient safety, particularly with the emergence of new types of AEs for cancer patients including long-term, permanent or severe irAEs, in the daily oncological practice. To live with irAEs including long-term irAEs can affect survivorship, quality of life, induce time toxicity and reduce ADL for both curative and palliative patients. Quality healthcare involves the delivery of safe, patient-centered care that prevents harm while addressing individual preferences, needs and values. To effectively monitor and understand the scope of AE in modern oncology, it is essential to develop relevant and valid measurement tools. These tools should address the new challenges posed by modern treatment combinations while incorporating the patient perspective – physically, socially and psychologically. Such tools could additionally support patient education and shared decision-making regarding treatment choices. Further research including prospective randomized controlled trials should include the SITC consensus definitions for irAEs, time toxicity and health related quality of life over longer timeframes to comprehensively assess the burden and consequences of new systemic anticancer treatment. Prospective studies with pragmatic inclusion criteria resembling more on a generally elderly and frailer population could be important to investigate real-world outcomes and toxicities Furthermore, integration of patient-reported outcomes as standard routine cancer care under systemic anticancer treatment could help to address this by empowering patients, reduce symptoms, increase QoL and possibly mitigate AE by proactive monitoring in real-time [32]. The goal should be to mitigate AE to minimize patient harm and improve all patient outcomes including “soft outcomes” in modern oncology.

Strengths and limitations

The inclusion of all cancer patients with solid cancers receiving ICI in a region with shared EHR is a strength of this study, enhancing the reliability of the results and providing real-world evidence for the different impacts of irAEs across different ICI treatment combinations in a standard cancer population. The study cohort primarily consists of elderly, palliatively treated patients, often diagnosed with lung cancer or melanoma, but reflects a general cancer population receiving combinations of immunotherapy treatment in Northern Norway.

Limitations of the study are several. Firstly, the study includes both palliative and curative patients with an overweight of lung cancer patients, with possibly multiple symptoms due to both anticancer treatment, progression and other treatment modalities which limits generalizability to other cancer populations [10,12]. Secondly, irAEs graded as mild or AEs from other types of cancer treatment were not collected, leading to an underestimation of the total amount of AE experienced by the included patients. Thirdly, other aspects of the SITC consensus such as multiorgan irAEs or steroid responsive/unresponsive were not assessed. Fourthly, exclusion of gynecological, lymphoma and hematological patients leads to lack of information about irAE consequences for these patient groups. Fifthly, concerns about the interrater reliability when using NCC MERP categorizing index has been raised in previous studies [33]. In our study only two doctors (SGA and EH) categorized the irAEs, whereas in a real-world setting clinicians could categorize the irAEs differently. Lastly, the observational retrospective design of the study could result in less accurate documentation of irAEs, and interventions compared to a prospective study where the objectives are set and collected in real time.

Conclusion

Moderate to severe irAEs were common, and high-grade irAEs occurred in one-fourth of cancer patients. However, in patients receiving dual ICI therapy, 7 out of 10 experienced a high-grade irAE. Most irAEs had consequences for the cancer patients as hospitalization, end of ICI treatment or became chronic, but the various ICI combinations had different toxicity profiles and implications for the patients. All three classifications methods added their own value, but no one captured the whole picture as quality of life, time toxicity, functional impairments, return to work or other important aspects of experiencing an irAE.

Prospective and real-world data about how the total burden of AE from all cancer treatments add on together with irAEs for the cancer patient is important. An updated patient-centered classification system for irAEs and AEs in cancer patients is needed to support decision-making, patient information and treatment choices both for patients and the health care system.
