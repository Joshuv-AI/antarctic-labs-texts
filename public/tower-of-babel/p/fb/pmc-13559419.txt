Clinical diagnosis and treatment of eosinophilic asthma by respiratory physicians in China: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Yongling Deng, Xuetao Kong, Yan Chen, Mei Jiang, Lin Yu, Jiaxing Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEosinophilic asthma (EA) differs from allergic asthma (AA) by later onset, higher exacerbation risk, rapid progression, pronounced airway limitation, and increased comorbidities. However, understanding of asthma phenotypes, particularly EA, remains inadequate among physicians, posing diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This study aims to investigate the current understanding, diagnostic approaches, and treatment practices for EA among respiratory physicians in China.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in the Chinese mainland from January to March 2024. A structured electronic questionnaire, developed and verified by a multidisciplinary panel from the Department of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University and related specialties, was utilized to assess respiratory physicians’ clinical attention, diagnostic-therapeutic practices, and depth of knowledge concerning EA.ResultsOf the 572 valid responses, 51.0% of respiratory physicians consistently emphasized eosinophil levels in complete blood counts during asthma assessments. While a majority of physicians demonstrated awareness of key EA characteristics, their clinical practices revealed significant gaps and inconsistencies. Only 4.4% of physicians routinely performed phenotypic analysis on diagnosed asthma patients. In the selection of biologic targeted therapy for EA, omalizumab was the most preferred choice (60.1% of respondents), followed by mepolizumab (35.7%) and benralizumab (16.4%). Notably, 92.3% of surveyed physicians agreed that China requires national diagnostic and management guidelines/consensus for EA.ConclusionsRespiratory physicians in China demonstrate inconsistent knowledge and practice regarding EA, with gaps in phenotyping, eosinophil monitoring, comorbidity attention, and targeted therapy selection. The strong consensus for guideline development highlights the need for standardized protocols to bridge these gaps.Keywords: Eosinophilic asthma (EA), asthma phenotyping, clinical awareness, targeted therapy, questionnaire

Highlight box

Key findings

• Only 4.4% of physicians routinely performed asthma phenotyping.

• Omalizumab was the preferred biologic targeted therapy for eosinophilic asthma (EA) (60.1%), despite being an anti-immunoglobulin E agent.

• A total of 92.3% of physicians supported developing China-specific EA guidelines.

What is known and what is new?

• EA is a distinct severe asthma subtype, and guidelines recommend eosinophil assessment, yet clinical awareness remains insufficient.

• This is the first nationwide survey of Chinese respiratory physicians on EA, revealing gaps in phenotyping, comorbidity recognition, and targeted therapy selection.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Urgent development of evidence-based Chinese EA guidelines is needed to standardize clinical practice.

• Enhanced continuing medical education and routine phenotyping implementation are required to bridge knowledge-practice gaps.

Introduction

Eosinophilic asthma (EA), a prevalent and severe subtype of asthma in adults (1), is defined by sputum eosinophil (Eos) levels of ≥2% of the total leukocyte count. In patients on high-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), the diagnostic threshold for this phenotype is between 2% and 4% (2). This asthmatic phenotype frequently coexists with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)-exacerbated respiratory disease (NERD), chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) with nasal polyps, and systemic eosinophilic disorders such as eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) (3), all characterized by significant peripheral blood eosinophilia. EA typically presents as late-onset asthma in adults, exhibiting poorer lung function trajectories and accelerated disease progression compared to allergic asthma (AA) (4). Additionally, these patients show inadequate responses to standard glucocorticoid therapy (5), along with increased risks of frequent acute exacerbations and hospitalization (6,7).

While EA and AA were historically considered distinct entities, recent studies highlight significant phenotypic and endotypic overlap in moderate-to-severe asthma (SA) populations. A post hoc analysis of 935 adults with moderate-to-SA not selected for any specific subtype revealed that 75.8% of patients with EA had concurrent allergic sensitization, while 39.5% of patients with AA exhibited eosinophilic inflammation (1).

AA, formerly known as extrinsic asthma, is characterized as an asthma phenotype triggered and/or aggravated by allergens (8). It is primarily mediated through allergen-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) pathways and typically presents with onset in childhood, exhibiting significant comorbidities such as allergic rhinitis (AR) and other atopic conditions, including atopic dermatitis, allergic conjunctivitis, and food/drug hypersensitivities (3). In addition to standard anti-inflammatory and antihistamine treatments, targeted biological therapies, such as omalizumab (an anti-IgE monoclonal antibody), have shown clinical effectiveness (9), particularly in severe AA cases with persistent symptoms despite conventional management (10).

Although respiratory physicians have expertise in AA, the identification and phenotyping of EA remain challenging in clinical practice. Current guidelines recommend using blood Eos counts (≥300 cells/µL) and FeNO as key biomarkers, yet standardized phenotyping protocols are not universally implemented, and biomarker interpretation is complicated by steroid treatment effects (11). Persistent challenges in EA identification and treatment underscore the need for systematic assessment of clinical cognition gaps. We conducted a nationwide cross-sectional survey to systematically assess these gaps by analyzing differences in EA-focused clinical attention, diagnostic accuracy, treatment selection, and pathophysiological understanding among respiratory physicians, categorized by professional titles, academic qualifications, and hospital tiers. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1197/rc).

Methods

Study design

The study had a cross-sectional design and was conducted in the Chinese mainland from January to March 2024. The questionnaire was developed and revised by specialists from the Department of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine at The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University and associated fields. Respiratory physicians were invited to join by targeted email distribution via the DXY.cn platform, a major Chinese online professional community and digital healthcare platform for physicians and other healthcare professionals that provides medical information, professional communication, clinical education, and related services. Targeted invitations through DXY.cn were sent to registered respiratory physicians who had subscribed to respiratory medicine-related updates on the platform. DXY.cn was therefore considered suitable for reaching respiratory physicians in the Chinese mainland. The WeChat distribution targeted members of regional respiratory physician WeChat groups across China. Therefore, the eligible survey population consisted of practicing respiratory physicians working in primary, secondary, or tertiary hospitals in the Chinese mainland. Because the survey was distributed through professional platforms and society-based networks rather than a complete national registry of all respiratory physicians, the exact total number of eligible respiratory physicians nationwide could not be determined. The response rate for the DXY.cn distribution was 55%, calculated as completed surveys divided by reached physicians. The questionnaire was also distributed through WeChat, with a response rate of 52%, calculated in the same manner as completed surveys divided by reached physicians within the WeChat distribution. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All study activities adhered strictly to the ethical norms sanctioned by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Ethics No. ES-2023-155). Participants signed informed consent forms prior to completing the questionnaire, confirming their comprehension of the survey’s objectives and content. The study aimed to evaluate respiratory physicians’ understanding of asthma phenotyping as well as diagnostic and treatment advancements.

Survey respondents

The study recruited respiratory physicians from multiple secondary and tertiary hospitals nationwide through the WeChat platform. Additionally, targeted invitations were delivered through email to respiratory physicians subscribed to respiratory medicine updates on the DXY.cn platform.

Questionnaire

This study performed a comprehensive analysis of essential clinical guidelines, including the Global Strategy for Asthma Management and Prevention 2023 Update (12), Guidelines for bronchial asthma prevent and management (2020 edition) (13) and Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Eosinophil-Associated Pulmonary Diseases (2022 version) (14), from which key recommendations were derived. To guarantee comprehensiveness and precision, we conducted several rounds of panel discussions with respiratory specialists from hospitals of all tiers and statistical experts, performing meticulous modifications of the questionnaire’s content, structure, and phrasing. Expert reviews were conducted to thoroughly assess and enhance the questionnaire. To ensure content validity, several rounds of panel discussions were conducted with respiratory specialists from hospitals of various tiers and statistical experts, leading to meticulous modifications of the questionnaire’s content, structure, and phrasing. A pilot test was conducted with a sample of 30 physicians to assess the instrument’s reliability and validity before the main survey. Reliability was evaluated by measuring internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.883, indicating excellent internal consistency and reliability for the scale (as values above 0.8 are considered good). To examine the structural validity and suitability of the data for factor analysis, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were performed. The KMO value was 0.711 (values above 0.7 indicate the data is suitable for factor analysis), and Bartlett’s test was significant (P<0.05), confirming that the correlation matrix was not an identity matrix and that factor analysis was appropriate. Based on this pilot feedback and statistical analysis, the questionnaire was finalized. The finalized questionnaire is provided in the Appendix 1.

Based on systematic literature data extraction and comprehensive panel discussions, we designed a two-part questionnaire.

❖ Part I: demographic survey. This section aims to gather physicians’ basic information, including educational background, professional title, subspecialty affiliation, province of their employing institution, hospital tier classification, and monthly asthma patient caseload. Professional title was categorized into four levels according to the standard hierarchy of the Chinese medical system: resident physician (physicians in standardized residency training or early-career physicians without independent prescribing authority), attending physician (physicians who have completed residency training and independently manage patients), associate chief physician (senior physicians with extensive clinical experience responsible for supervising junior physicians), and chief physician (highest-level senior physicians with leadership roles in clinical practice and departmental management).

Outcome measures

This study focused on four critical domains: (I) physicians were classified as prioritizing Eos monitoring if they answered “frequently” to both questions regarding routine review of historical Eos counts and performing CBC to assess Eos in newly diagnosed asthma patients. This strict dual-threshold definition was used to ensure that only those consistently and proactively engaging in Eos evaluation were identified, thereby reducing misclassification due to infrequent or context-specific practices; (II) physicians’ engagement with asthma phenotypic analysis was evaluated through their self-reported frequency of phenotypic categorization (“always”, “frequently”, “occasionally”, “never”, or “unaware of asthma phenotypes”), with responses of “always”, “frequently”, or “occasionally” indicating phenotype-aware practitioners; (III) physicians’ awareness of EA comorbidities was assessed by their identification of common comorbid conditions [AR, CRS, nasal polyposis, otologic disorders (e.g., infections, discharge, tinnitus, hearing loss), and aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD)]. Higher selection rates for these options indicated better clinical understanding; (IV) physicians’ familiarity with Eos-targeting biologics, assessed by their ability to precisely identify guideline-recommended agents (omalizumab, dupilumab, mepolizumab, benralizumab), where precise identification indicated therapeutic prioritization and incorrect selections indicated an absence of awareness about biologic therapies.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables that are regularly distributed were presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data were reported by median and interquartile range. Intergroup comparisons were performed using independent samples t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Chi-squared test, or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Sample size estimation was performed using SPSS 26 (IBM Corp.). The sample size was estimated using the single proportion formula for a cross-sectional study design. The anticipated proportion (p) for the key outcome incidence was derived from a pilot study involving 100 consecutive physicians. The calculation was performed with a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 (Z=1.96) and a margin of error (d, precision) of 0.056. Based on the formula n=Z1−α/22×p(1−p)d2, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be approximately 377 subjects. To account for a potential 10% rate of loss to follow-up or invalid data, the sample size was increased accordingly, resulting in a final target enrollment of approximately 419 participants. A P<0.05 was deemed statistically significant. All statistical analyses in this section were performed using SPSS 26.

Physicians’ emphasis on Eos and IgE

Among physicians managing newly diagnosed asthma patients, 51.0% prioritized Eos levels in CBC, with a statistically significant trend showing that higher-tier hospitals exhibited greater emphasis on Eos evaluation (P<0.05). Additionally, 38.5% of physicians reported frequently performing allergen testing (via IgE measurement or skin prick tests). Notably, 63.1% of physicians routinely measure Eos levels in CBC in patients during acute asthma exacerbations.

Physicians’ awareness of future asthma exacerbation risk indicators

A total of 90.6% of physicians agreed that higher Eos levels were associated with an increased risk of future asthma exacerbations. A total of 88.8% concurred that elevated total serum IgE levels similarly correlated with a higher exacerbation risk. Notably, only 2 respondents strongly agreed with the association between elevated Eos levels and exacerbation risk while disagreeing that higher total serum IgE levels increased future asthma exacerbation risk.

In subgroup analyses of this section, statistically significant associations were observed only for the question on serum total IgE levels (Q2.6), not for the question on blood Eos levels (Q2.5). For Q2.6, significant differences were found by hospital level and professional title. Physicians in tertiary hospitals exhibited higher agreement [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) =1.74, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.06–2.83, P<0.05] compared with those in secondary hospitals. Similarly, chief or associate chief physicians showed greater agreement (aOR =1.60, 95% CI: 1.16–2.22, P<0.05) than attending or resident physicians (Figure 1).

Physicians’ awareness of indicators of asthma-related airflow limitations

A total of 80.1% of physicians agreed that higher Eos levels were associated with more severe airflow limitation in asthma. A total of 78.1% agreed that increased total serum IgE levels were associated with greater severity of airflow limitation. Significantly, merely 7 respondents agreed with the association between elevated Eos levels and the severity of airflow limitation, while disagreeing that elevated total serum IgE levels exacerbated airflow limitation.

In subgroup analyses of this section, statistically significant association was observed exclusively for the question on blood Eos levels (Q2.7), with no significant associations found for the question on serum total IgE levels (Q2.8). For Q2.7, the significance was confined to the educational attainment subgroup, where participants with a Master’s or Doctoral degree were more likely to agree (aOR =1.57, 95% CI: 1.14–2.17, P<0.05) (Figure 1).

Physicians’ phenotyping practices in asthma patients

A total of 4.4% of physicians consistently performed phenotypic analysis on diagnosed asthma patients, while 33.6% reported frequent phenotyping.

Physicians’ awareness of asthma phenotype differentiation

EA vs. AA in disease control

In response to the questioning, “In comparison to AA, do you agree that EA is more challenging to control?” Overall, 77.1% of physicians agreed that EA is more challenging to control than AA, comprising 33.7% who expressed strong agreement and 43.4% who indicated moderate agreement.

EA vs. AA in age of onset

To the question, “Compared with AA, do you agree that EA has a later age of onset?”, 25.3% of physicians strongly agreed and 42.0% somewhat agreed.

In subgroup analyses of this section, significant associations were confined to the education stratum. Participants with Master’s or Doctoral degrees were more likely to agree that EA is more difficult to control (Q2.10: aOR =1.37, 95% CI: 1.00–1.89, P<0.05) and that it has a later onset age (Q2.11: aOR =1.48, 95% CI: 1.08–2.04, P<0.05) (Figure 1).

Differentiation between airflow-limited EA and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

A total of 67.9% of physicians concurred that airflow-limited EA is challenging to distinguish from COPD, with 24.5% strongly agreeing and 43.4% somewhat agreeing.

Physicians’ awareness of Eos-targeting biologic therapies for SA

Physicians could select all applicable options, and the reported percentages reflect the proportion of respondents who included each therapy in their answer. Among biologic-targeted therapy for EA in SA, 60.1% of physicians selected omalizumab, followed by dupilumab and mepolizumab (38.3% and 35.7%, respectively), with the lowest recognition for benralizumab (16.4%). Physicians with doctoral degrees demonstrated the highest awareness of mepolizumab, dupilumab, and benralizumab (69.4%, 61.1%, and 36.1%, respectively) (Figure 5).

A total of 92.3% of surveyed physicians agreed that China requires national diagnostic and management guidelines/consensus for EA, while 7.0% expressed a neutral stance.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first cross-sectional survey investigating respiratory physicians’ awareness, clinical management practices, and knowledge regarding EA. Our findings showed that 51% of physicians prioritized the evaluation of Eos levels in CBC when assessing newly diagnosed asthma patients, while 63.1% reported routinely assessing Eos levels through CBC during acute asthma exacerbations.

Asthma is typically driven by allergic inflammation and/or eosinophilic inflammation, with severe cases often exhibiting both pathological processes concurrently (1,15). EA and AA demonstrate considerable overlap in both phenotypic manifestations and underlying endotypic mechanisms, which makes their distinct classification challenging in routine clinical practice (1,16). In our survey, 77.1% of physicians agreed that EA is more challenging to manage than AA, and 67.3% affirmed that EA tends to have a later age of onset compared with AA, indicating a majority consensus on these features. The existing literature indicates that EA patients, in comparison to AA patients, generally experience an older age of onset (2,6), higher exacerbation frequency (17,18), worse lung function (19,20), more severe airflow limitation (21), and greater comorbidity burden (e.g., nasal polyps, sinusitis, NERD) (3,21). Late-onset EA is recognized as a predominant phenotype in SA (21,22).

Our survey also found that only 4.4% of physicians routinely performed phenotypic analysis on asthma patients, while 33.6% did so frequently. The difference between the high recognition of EA’s clinical features and the relatively infrequent routine use of phenotypic analysis may be attributable to considerations such as the necessity of phenotyping mainly in complex or severe cases, resource constraints, and workflow limitations, rather than a uniform lack of awareness.

Current evidence highlights a discrepancy between perceived clinical challenges and actual diagnostic practices. Notably, elevated blood Eos levels strongly predict future asthma exacerbations (23-25), while total serum IgE levels exhibit limited prognostic value for acute exacerbations (26-28). In our questionnaire, most physicians nonetheless agreed that raised blood Eos and total IgE levels both indicate a higher risk of future exacerbations (only 0.3% disagreed). Similarly, the majority linked higher Eos and total IgE to more severe airflow limitation, with just 1.2% dissenting. These findings may reflect a common conflation of Eos and IgE roles among clinicians, potentially influenced by overlapping clinical presentations, educational emphasis on IgEmediated mechanisms in AA, and guideline formulations that do not sharply differentiate prognostic utility across asthma subtypes.

During the management of asthma patients, 42.3% of physicians frequently focused on EA comorbidities, while 35.3% exhibited moderate attention. However, in this survey, when asked about EA-associated comorbidities, more physicians selected AR (80.4%) than CRS (75.5%), with fewer than half acknowledging nasal polyps, otologic disorders, or NERD. Notably, sinusitis, nasal polyps, NERD, and otologic complications are recognized as primary EA comorbidities (3,29). This pattern may reflect conventional teaching emphases that highlight AR as the principal comorbidity of AA, whereas the comorbidity spectrum specific to EA has been less prominently featured in clinical education and training programs. As a result, physicians may be more inclined to recall AR when considering asthma comorbidities, which could influence the recognition and assessment of EA-specific comorbidity profiles in practice. To address this, continuing medical education initiatives could place greater emphasis on EA-specific comorbidity recognition, integrating recent evidence and phenotype‑based teaching to complement existing asthma management training.

Asthma and COPD differ in their pathophysiology, treatment strategies, and prognostic outcomes, yet they share considerable similarities in spirometry results and clinical manifestations, complicating routine diagnosis. Confusion in distinguishing between airflow-limited asthma and COPD is most prevalent among older patients with a history of smoking or occupational exposures (30), particularly in primary care settings that lack advanced diagnostic resources (31). In our survey, 67.9% of physicians agreed that airflow-limited EA is difficult to distinguish from COPD, with higher agreement among those in lower-tier hospitals. This perception may reflect real-world diagnostic challenges, such as limited access to advanced pulmonary function testing or imaging in primary care, greater prevalence of smoking history, and overlapping spirometric patterns, rather than solely a deficit in knowledge.

Biologic-targeted therapies are recommended for managing severe, uncontrolled asthma to prevent exacerbations and improve symptoms and health-related quality of life (32,33). Although the number of monoclonal antibodies for SA continues to grow and understanding of treatment responses has advanced, selecting the optimal agent remains challenging (34,35). Mepolizumab and benralizumab are commonly employed biologics targeting Eos (36-39). Dupilumab, an IL-4Rα-targeting monoclonal antibody, dually blocks IL-4/IL-13 pathways, effectively reducing pulmonary symptoms in EA (39-41). Omalizumab, which targets IgE, is primarily utilized for AA (36,37).

According to Guidelines for bronchial asthma prevent and management (2020 edition) (13) and Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Eosinophil-Associated Pulmonary Diseases (2022 version) (14), anti-IL-5/IL-5R and anti-IL-4Rα biologics are recommended as add-on therapies for severe eosinophilic/Type 2 asthma, particularly in patients with elevated blood Eos and/or other Type 2 inflammatory biomarkers.

Among Eos-targeting biologics for EA, omalizumab was the most frequently selected by surveyed physicians (60.1%). This finding is broadly consistent with the current biologic uptake pattern in China, where omalizumab was first approved in China in 2017, thereby establishing the earliest clinical exposure and accumulating more extensive domestic experience in asthma management. In contrast, dupilumab was first approved in China in 2020 for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis, and its use in asthma remained off-label during the early post-approval period, with its indication for asthma subsequently approved in China in 2023. Similarly, mepolizumab was approved in China in 2021 for EGPA, with its use in asthma remaining off-label at that time, before being later approved for severe eosinophilic asthma (SEA) in 2024. Benralizumab was first approved in China in 2024 for SEA, representing its initial approved indication in the Chinese market. Therefore, IL-5/IL-5R-targeted biologics and IL-4Rα-targeted biologics have only more recently entered formal asthma indications and remain in a comparatively early stage of clinical adoption for asthma. In addition to differences in approval timing and clinical experience, biologic selection in China is also influenced by real-world access factors, including reimbursement coverage and regional variability in drug availability, particularly during the early adoption phase of newer biologics. These health system and access-related factors are especially relevant in the 2023–2024 transition period of biologic expansion in China. These regulatory and health system factors may have further contributed to the more frequent selection of omalizumab, despite guideline recommendations supporting anti-IL-5/IL-5R and anti-IL-4Rα therapies for severe eosinophilic/Type 2 asthma. Therefore, the relatively limited use of IL-5-targeted agents observed in this survey should not be interpreted as a deviation from guideline recommendations, but rather as a potential implementation gap between guideline-based biologic selection and real-world prescribing conditions in China.

Physicians with doctoral degrees demonstrated the highest awareness of mepolizumab (69.4%) and dupilumab (61.1%). This difference may reflect systematic differences in clinical care pathways rather than individual prescribing preference alone. In stratified clinical practice in China, doctoral-degree physicians are more commonly concentrated in tertiary hospitals and teaching hospitals, where SEA is preferentially managed and biologic-targeted therapies are embedded within structured SA clinics. Within such settings, patients are more likely to undergo comprehensive phenotyping (including blood Eos, FeNO, and IgE), which directly increases eligibility for anti-IL-5 and anti-IL-4/13 therapies. In addition, access to biologics is strongly influenced by institutional formulary inclusion, hospital tier, and reimbursement policies, which are typically more favorable in tertiary hospitals where doctoral-level physicians practice. This creates a higher treatment opportunity density, resulting in increased real-world exposure and subsequent familiarity with mepolizumab and dupilumab. Furthermore, these physicians are more likely to participate in multicenter clinical trials, guideline committees, and academic case discussions, which accelerates translation of evidence-based recommendations into clinical practice. Conversely, physicians with lower professional titles, academic qualifications, or working in lower-tier hospitals were more likely to report unfamiliarity with Eos-targeting biologics, suggesting disparities in access to updated training, specialist networks, and institutional biologic availability. Overall, the observed prescribing preferences and familiarity gaps should be interpreted in the context of guideline implementation, drug accessibility, indication scope, reimbursement, and clinical experience, rather than as a simple knowledge deficit.

The current questionnaire survey indicates that respiratory physicians encounter a limited number of SA patients in clinical practice. Of the respondents, 62.9% of physicians indicated that they diagnosed 1–10 new cases of SA within a six-month period (with 40.0% concentrated in 1–5 cases). The survey revealed knowledge gaps regarding the clinical value of biomarkers such as blood Eos and total IgE. A substantial proportion of physicians reported diagnostic difficulty in asthma phenotyping, characteristics/complications of EA and distinguishing airflow-limited EA from COPD. Contradictory responses were observed in distinguishing AA from EA, potentially leading to uncertainties in selecting biological targeted therapies. Overall, physicians with higher professional titles, academic qualifications, and those working in higher-tier hospitals demonstrated better understanding of EA. Although numerous high-quality reviews and guidelines for SA have been published internationally, the absence of specific guidelines or expert consensus focusing solely on EA, both domestically and globally, may contribute to these knowledge deficiencies. Approximately 92.3% of surveyed physicians supported the establishment of Chinese diagnostic and treatment guidelines or expert consensus for EA.

However, it is important to recognize the inherent limitations of self-report data in this study. Questionnaire-based assessments of knowledge and practice may not fully capture actual clinical behavior, as studies in medical education have consistently shown discrepancies between what physicians report they do and what they actually do in practice (42). Respondents may overestimate adherence to guidelines or inflate familiarity with certain procedures owing to social desirability bias or recall inaccuracies. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as indicative of perceived challenges and attitudes, rather than definitive measures of real‑world practice patterns.

The electronic questionnaire demonstrated a high response rate, showing greater efficiency compared to traditional paper-based surveys and helping reduce selection bias (43). However, our study still has several limitations. First, the survey results were based on self-reported data from physicians, potentially introducing recall bias. Second, the instrument did not distinguish whether reported practices stemmed from a genuine knowledge gap or were constrained by local resource availability. This conflation limits our ability to attribute suboptimal management solely to cognitive factors. Third, while enriching the sample with experts, the overrepresentation of physicians whose subspecialty focus was chronic airway diseases (72.6%) may introduce selection bias, potentially yielding estimates of EA awareness that are higher than those among the general population of respiratory physicians in China and thus limiting the generalizability of some findings. Additionally, while participants encompassed all seven major geographical regions of China, the uneven geographical distribution of physicians may influence the generalizability and representativeness of the results.

Conclusions

The study indicates that respiratory physicians exhibit an inconsistent and heterogeneous pattern of clinical practice and knowledge regarding EA. While a majority of physicians demonstrate awareness of key EA characteristics and support the development of national guidelines, significant gaps and disconnects between knowledge and routine practice are evident in four critical domains: the underutilization of routine asthma phenotyping, insufficient monitoring of Eos levels in clinical assessments, inadequate attention to EA-specific comorbidities, and a preference for biologic therapies not primarily targeting the eosinophilic pathway. The current lack of specific, dedicated guidelines or expert consensus for EA contributes to this clinical heterogeneity. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop evidence-based diagnostic and treatment protocols or expert consensus for EA. This standardization is essential to bridge the identified knowledge-practice gaps, harmonize clinical management, and ultimately facilitate more informed, patient-centered decision-making.
