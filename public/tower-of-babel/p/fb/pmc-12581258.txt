Spatial analysis of depression and anxiety among frontline health workers during Nigeria’s COVID-19 pandemic: cross-sectional study
Authors: Piwuna Christopher Goson, Uchechukwu Levi Osuagwu, Bernadine N Ekpenyong, Chundung Asabe Miner, Margaret Ojeahere, Tanko Ishaya, Khathutshelo P Mashige, Godwin Ovenseri-Ogbomo, Esther Awazzi Envuladu, Emmanuel Kwasi Abu, Chikasirimobi G Timothy, Raymond Langsi, Onyekachukwu M Amiebenomo, Richard Oloruntoba, Deborah Donald Charwe, Palash Basak, Kingsley Emwinyore Agho

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic caused considerable panic and anxiety among health workers in all countries of the world, including Nigeria. This study aimed to identify the spatial clustering for prevalence and factors associated with anxiety and depression among frontline health workers (FHWs) during COVID-19 in the 36 states of Nigeria.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study conducted in COVID-19 hospital treatment centres (one in each state) across 36 Nigerian states and Federal Capital Territory. A total of 3,353 FHWs were recruited using convenient sampling technique. The main outcomes were depression and anxiety symptoms examined using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and categorised into binary. The sequential modelling approach in Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with anxiety and depression among FHWs. The spatial distribution of anxiety and depression across Nigerian states was examined, and prevalence and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for mental health outcomes were visualized using ArcGIS Desktop 10.86, with graduated color symbols representing aORs adjusted for confounders.ResultsFHWs in the Southeastern States of Anambra, Enugu, Abia, and Imo, experienced higher odds of depression and anxiety compared with other states. For anxiety alone, higher odds were observed among FHWs in Benue, Cross River, Ebonyi, and Edo states. Logistic regression analyses revealed higher odds for anxiety and depression among Nurses, while FHWs who have practiced for 1–6 years had significantly higher odds for depression (adjusted odds ratios (AOR) = 3.21, 95%CI: 1.90, 5.43 for 5–6 years; and AOR = 2.33, 95%CI: 1.27, 4.26 for 1–4 years). The odds for anxiety and depression were significantly lower among FHWs with a prior history of psychological distress compared to those without such a history.ConclusionsThis study highlights significant spatial clustering of anxiety and depression among FHWs in Nigeria, with the highest burden in Southeastern states. Nurses and FHWs with fewer years of practice were particularly vulnerable, indicating the need for targeted mental health interventions. Those with a prior history of psychological distress had lower odds of experiencing anxiety and depression, suggesting potential resilience or coping mechanisms. These findings underscore the urgency for tailored mental health support and policy interventions to mitigate the psychological impact of future public health crises on healthcare workers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24941-0.Keywords: COVID-19, Mental health, Depression, Anxiety, Healthcare workers, Hospital, Psychological distress

Background

The Coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic, with its rapid spread and high death rate, caused considerable panic and anxiety in countries around the world, including Nigeria, during its outbreak [1, 2]. Health workers the world over faced unprecedented circumstances and pressure during the pandemic [3, 4]. The pandemic also took a toll on the emotional, physical, and psychological wellbeing of healthcare workers (FHW) globally [5, 6]. The emotional, physical, and psychological well-being of frontline staff (such as hospital workers) [7] was brought under serious strain due to the increased demand for services from individuals with emerging or current mental health conditions [8, 9]. The impact of pandemics on healthcare workers is particularly severe in conflict-affected countries [10], where pre-existing stressors and limited resources worsen mental health challenges. In Nigeria, as in many African countries, the healthcare system is underdeveloped [11, 12]with a shortage of healthcare personnel [11, 13–15].

Healthcare workers are a national strategic resource, having to make impossible decisions and work under extreme pressure. The decisions they make may include how to allocate scarce resources to equally needy patients, how to balance their own physical and mental healthcare needs with those of patients, how to align their desire and duty to patients with those to family and friends, and how to provide care for all severely unwell patients with constrained or inadequate resources [16]. This may cause some mental health problems [17]. As a result, the likelihood of a worker experiencing mental health challenges may be influenced by the way that they are supported before, during, and after a challenging incident such as those faced by the Frontline FHW during the pandemic [18].

Globally, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that between 80,000 and 180,000 health and care workers may have died from COVID-19 between January 2020 and May 2021, with the most likely scenario indicating around 115,500 deaths [19]. A systematic review put the overall case fatality rate of COVID-19 at 9 deaths per 1000 infections, with an infection rate of 14.5% among healthcare workers [20]. FHWs treating patients with COVID-19 have greater risks of mental health problems, such as anxiety, depression, insomnia, and stress [16]. These FHWs are vital resources for every country including Nigeria. In the first 30 days of the pandemic in Nigeria, it spread from person to person to community transmission, with 255 (19.2%) positive cases and 40(3%) deaths. As such, the health and safety of the FHWs are crucial, not only for continuous and safe patient care but also for control of any future pandemics. Consequently, there was an urgent need to understand the possible emotional and psychological impacts of this outbreak on Health Care Workers at the time.

The COVID-19 outbreak provided researchers with a unique opportunity to explore these impacts among health workers in Nigeria to enable preparedness for combatting future outbreaks. Findings from this study may help clinicians and health policymakers improve their understanding of the mental health and psychological impact of such exposure to an outbreak of a fast-spreading, life-threatening infectious disease like COVID-19 and strengthen preparations for responding to possible future outbreaks in Nigeria. Therefore, this study aimed to (1) Examine the prevalence of anxiety and depression among frontline hospital workers (FHWs) in Nigeria during the COVID-19 pandemic and (2) Identify risk factors associated with these mental health problems during the COVID-19 pandemic across the 36 Nigerian States.

Materials and methods

Study team and collaboration

This study was conducted in collaboration with a broad range of experts, including epidemiologists, biostatisticians, psychiatrists, community physicians, anthropologists, communication experts, and social researchers.

Participants

Participants were physicians, nurses, and other FHWs in hospitals across the 36 states in Nigeria and the Federal Capital Territory, Abuja. Other FHWs included laboratory scientists/technicians, ward attendants/cleaners, and ambulance drivers. Participants were recruited from a hospital treatment centre providing direct care and treatment for patients with COVID-19. Participants were recruited using a sampling technique. First, one COVID-19 hospital treatment centre was randomly selected from each state. Subsequently, 100 hospital employees were conveniently selected from each centre using the hospital employee roster. All 36 States, including the Federal Capital Territory (FCT), participated except for Kogi state, where the health care professionals declined to participate to not contradict the State governor’s claim that there was no COVID-19 in the State at the time. Variations in years of work experience, number of support days, and hospital employment were considered to obtain diversity in the experiences of caring for patients with COVID-19.

Study design and sampling

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April and June 2022 using online questionnaires designed on Survey Monkey for data collection. The questionnaires were self-administered, and participants were provided with clear instructions and support materials to ensure they understood the questions and response options. Data quality was ensured by incorporating validation checks within the survey platform to prevent missing data and inconsistent responses. One COVID-19 hospital treatment centre in each state of the 36 states and Abuja in Nigeria and each COVID-19 hospital treatment centre was selected for data collection. The selection of one COVID-19 hospital treatment center per state was a pragmatic decision based on resource constraints and the need to ensure coverage across all states.

Power calculation and sample size

According to previous research [21], about 13% and 30.4% of gynaecological cancer patients treated at a University Teaching Hospital in Nigeria reported severe anxiety and depression, as measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). While data on the prevalence of anxiety and depression among FHWs during COVID-19 would have been ideal, such data were not readily available at the time of the study design. Therefore, we opted to utilize data from a previous study on gynecological cancer patients as a proxy. The sample size was calculated based on a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error for a two-sided test. This gave a sample size of 62 hospital workers for each Nigerian state. Using an online sample size calculator (G*Power version 3.1.9.7), although a formal sample size calculation might not be strictly required for convenience sampling, this calculation was done to provide an estimate of the number of participants needed to potentially observe statistically significant differences and conduct subgroup analyses. We aimed for a sample size of around 3000, factoring in a potential 30% non-response rate, which is a conservative estimate based on previous studies [22].

A multistage sampling method was employed to ensure representation across the country, one COVID-19 hospital treatment center was randomly selected from each state. Then, within each selected centre, convenience sampling was utilised to recruit 100 hospital employees using the hospital employee roster. This approach balanced representativeness across states with the feasibility of data collection within each centre. The convenience sampling technique was chosen due to practical considerations such as limited resources, time constraints, and the urgency of gathering data during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Recruitment approach

To facilitate recruitment, research assistants (RAs) were deployed across different geographical zones. These RAs visited each isolation center within their assigned zones, distributing hard copies of the questionnaire to eligible participants. They remained at the centers for at least two days to collect completed responses. However, initial recruitment faced challenges due to slow response rates, particularly in states such as Plateau, Adamawa, Taraba, and Niger, where only a handful of responses (as low as five in some cases) were received after four weeks. In certain regions, especially in the Southeast and Southsouth states, participants expressed reluctance to complete the questionnaire without some form of incentive. These challenges necessitated additional engagement efforts by the research team to encourage participation. Participants were included if they were employed at a COVID-19 isolation center during the study period. While direct patient care was not explicitly defined in the methodology, it was inferred that all individuals working in these centers played a role in COVID-19 management.

For the key informant interviews, participants were purposefully selected based on their leadership roles in isolation centers. This included Chief Medical Directors (CMDs), Heads of Nursing Services, Heads of Intensive Care Units (ICUs), and selected attendants. Their inclusion was aimed at addressing policy-related aspects of COVID-19 management, particularly regarding federal guidelines and support from agencies such as the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) and the Federal Ministry of Health. This structured recruitment process ensured that a diverse range of FHWs and key decision-makers contributed valuable insights to the study.

Outcome variables

The outcome variables were anxiety and depression, which were measured using the hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS) developed by Zigmond &Snaith [23]. The HADS uses 14 items divided into anxiety and depressive subscales. The decision to use HADS was due to its brevity, ease of administration, and focus on psychological distress without overlap with physical symptoms [24]. Its widespread use and consistent validity among healthcare workers in high-stress contexts further support its suitability for this study [25–27].

HADS - anxiety subscale

There are seven items each for the depression and anxiety scales. Responses to each item were rated from 0 (not at all) to 3 (most of the time). Items 1–7 consisted of the anxiety subscale, and the total score for all items was 21. As recommended [23] the total subscale score was calculated to determine the final score, which was then categorized into normal (0–7), mild-moderate anxiety (8–10), and severe anxiety (11–21). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used to test the validity of the anxiety subscale. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient score was 0.79, with the individual items’ scores ranging from 0.76 to 0.79, indicating a satisfactory level of reliability (Supplementary Table 2). For the analysis, anxiety scores >10 were classified as 1, otherwise 0.

Depression subscale

Like the anxiety scale, items 8–14 consisted of the depression subscale, and the total score was 21. The response options vary from one item to the other, as presented in Supplementary Table 1. The sum of scores per individual was calculated to derive a final score, which was then used to derive the categories of normal (0–7), borderline depression (8–10), and severe depression (11–21). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient testing the validity of the anxiety subscale showed scores ranging from 0.65 to 0.77, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The depression score was dichotomised as binary, with depression scores > 10 considered as 1, otherwise 0.

Potential confounders and risk factors

The potential confounders shown in Table 1 include the history of psychological distress and the type of frontline healthcare profession classified as doctors, nurses, or others. The risk factors investigated in this study included age group, gender, marital status, religion, years of practice experience (categorised as (1–4 years, 5–6 years, ≥ 7 years based on previous study [28]), level of education, and previous history of psychological distress (identified through an item that asks whether or not they have had a previous history of psychological distress) were also based on previous studies [29–31].

Data analysis

Data was carefully cleaned and screened for outliers and inconsistencies before analysis. The sample characteristics were presented using frequency tabulations, this was followed by the prevalence of anxiety and depression by confounding factors. To identify the factors associated with anxiety and depression, we tested the association with independent variables by odds ratios (OR) using univariate.

We employed a sequential modelling approach in our multivariable regression analyses. This exploratory technique examines how various confounding factors influence the outcome variables and illustrates how the strength of these relationships changes as additional variables are incorporated into the analysis [32]. This approach allows for comparing the relationship between each set of factors in examining their association with depression and anxiety. In the first stage of the model (Model 1), the state and basic factors, including age group, gender, marital status, religion, and the highest level of education, were entered into the model. In the second stage model (Model 2), professional experience factors, such as years of practice and type of profession, were added to the second stage. The third and final stage (Model 3), included all variables from the previous stages plus a psychological factor – a previous history of psychological distress.

We used the ‘logistic’ command in STATA to estimate OR and 95% CIs, All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA/MP Version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) obtained from the adjusted multiple logistic regression model were used to measure the factors associated with depression and anxiety. The test of statistical significance was set at 5%. Reporting of this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for cross-sectional studies.

Spatial analysis

The spatial distribution of the two outcome measures (anxiety and depression) was analyzed, and maps illustrating prevalence and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) were generated using ArcGIS Desktop 10.8 [33]. Average levels of depression and anxiety across Nigerian states were calculated by comparing maps and conducting regression analysis to establish thresholds for mental health variables. Data were geocoded to assign geographic coordinates to healthcare facilities, COVID-19 treatment centers, and population density areas. Layers representing datasets such as healthcare worker distribution, patient numbers, and states COVID-19 case rates were created. Spatial analysis tools, including kernel density estimation and proximity analysis, identified patterns and clusters of healthcare worker deployment relative to population needs and COVID-19 hotspots. Choropleth maps displayed aORs, adjusted for confounders in Model 3, using graduated color symbols. All maps were standardized to a consistent coordinate reference system, ensuring accuracy. Spatial analysis tools in ArcGIS, such as kernel density estimation and proximity analysis, were employed to identify patterns and clusters of healthcare worker deployment relative to population needs and COVID-19 hotspots. Choropleth maps were used to visualize the intensity of key variables, while overlay analyses highlighted correlations between healthcare resource allocation and disease burden.

Ethical consideration

This study was carried out in full compliance with the principles of the 1967 Helsinki Declaration. The National Health Research Ethics Committee of Nigeria approved the study (NHREC/01/01/2007-08/03/2022), including permission to use the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) through the Ministry of Health. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation in the study. Participation in this study was voluntary, and participants were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses.

Results

Details of the demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. A total of 4,534 FHWs were invited to participate in the study. Of these, 3,364 (74.2%) completed the study, however, only data for those who completed the depression and anxiety questionnaire items (n = 3353) were used in the analysis. More than half of the FHWs were women, married, and the majority were in the age groups of 31 to 40 and 41 to 50 years (Table 1). About 2/3rd were Christians, nearly all completed at least a bachelor’s degree (tertiary education), and the majority have between 3 and 6 years of practice experience. The distribution of participants by state is presented in the supplementary file (S1) and shows a higher representation of FHWs in Plateau (5.0%), Katsina (4.3%), Taraba, and Abia states (3.6%, each). About one in every three participants reported that they experienced psychological distress in the past (Table 1).

Table 2 also shows the prevalence and unadjusted odds ratios for factors associated with threshold-level anxiety and depression, together with their 95% CIs. Significant findings have been bolded for easy identification. Among those who identified as Muslims, the unadjusted odds for threshold-level depression (OR 0.72, 95%CI: 0.58, 0.91) and threshold-level anxiety (OR 0.79, 95%CI: 0.65, 0.97) were significantly higher compared to those identifying as Christians. In addition, females were 1.19 times (95% CI: 1.01, 1.42) more likely to experience threshold-level depression than men. FHWs with a tertiary education reported higher odds of anxiety (OR 1.75, 95%CI: 1.16, 2.65) and depression (OR 2.18, 95%CI: 1.30, 3.66) than those who attained primary, secondary, or had no formal education (Table 2).

The state prevalence of threshold-level anxiety and depression among FHWs in Nigeria

The GIS maps presented in Figs. 1A and B show the prevalence of the mental health problems of anxiety and depression, respectively, for the participating states in Nigeria. There were significant variations in the prevalence of mental health problems between the Nigerian states such that FHWs in the Eastern States of Nigeria including Abia (49.2% and 47.5%), Anambra (42.7% and 42.7%), Enugu (36.7% and 35.8%) and Imo (63.1% and 36.9%) had the highest rates of threshold-level anxiety (Fig. 1A) and depression (Fig. 1B), respectively compared with FHWs in the FCT. In addition, FHWs in other states including the northern state of Niger (30%) and the Southern state of Ogun (30.7%) showed significantly higher rates of anxiety while higher threshold-level depression was observed among FHWs in the Southern state of Delta (31.0%) compared with the FCT Abuja (Fig. 1B).

Predictors of mental health problems of anxiety and depression among FHWs in Nigeria

Table 3 presents the results of hierarchical modelling for the factors associated with threshold-level anxiety and depression, while the distribution by state is shown in Figs. 2(A and B), respectively, after adjusting for all the potential confounders in Model 3. Across all the models in the table, adjustments were made for the state variations. In both Model 2 and 3, nurses were more likely to report threshold-level depression and anxiety, while FHWs with fewer years of experience (< 7 years) were more likely to report threshold-level depression. Those who were aged between 41 and 50 years were more likely to experience threshold-level anxiety and depression (Model 3). FHWs who had experienced psychological distress in the past were more likely to report threshold-level depression (aOR 0.63, 95%CI: 0.50, 0.80) and anxiety (aOR 0.57, 95%CI: 0.46, 0.70) compared to those who had not. Although gender and religion had some impact on the prevalence of anxiety (aOR 1.12, 95%CI: 0.90, 1.39 and 3.58, 95%CI: 1.28, 10.07), and depression (0.96, 95%CI: 0.75, 1.23 and 0.78, 95%CI: 0.24, 2.57), only the relationship between religion and anxiety reached statistical significance after adjusting for all potential confounders in Model 3 (Table 3).

When compared to FCT, FHWs residing in the Southeastern states of Abia (15.21, 95% CI: 5.07, 45.63), Anambra (10.86, 95% CI: 3.56, 33.16), Enugu (9.08, 95% CI: 3.00, 27.55), and Imo (24.71, 95% CI: 8.11, 75.28) experienced the highest odds for anxiety (Fig. 2A). However, the odds were lower in Bauchi, Kano, Taraba, Edo, Bayelsa, Osun, Kwara, Oyo, and Lagos states compared to FCT. In comparison to FCT, FHWs residing in the North Central states of Benue and Niger had approximately three times higher risk of depression.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on frontline hospital workers (FHW) in Nigeria through statistical and spatial analysis. It is the first nationwide, state-level study in Nigeria to examine depression and anxiety among FHWs during the pandemic. The state level is very important in the context of Nigeria because health policies, monitoring, and resources are allocated based on state-specific demographic and cultural diversity. In this study, it was found that FHWs from Anambra, Enugu, Abia, and Imo states, those aged between 41 and 50 years, and nurses are common factors associated with experiencing threshold-level anxiety and depression. FHWs who reported a previous history of psychological distress were likely to experience threshold-level anxiety and depression.

There is evidence from past research that both doctors and other FHWs at the forefront of giving care for COVID-19 patients had an increased risk of anguish and negative health outcomes, including loneliness, anxiety, and disturbed sleep [4]. In this current study, participants with several years of practice experience exhibited high rates of threshold-level anxiety and depression. Nonetheless, the study observed that some FHWs in the forefront experienced better mental health outcomes. This could be attributed to the fact that such workers had control over the environment while they were at work and had a sense of satisfaction from their professional duties [34].

While initial data was limited, a growing body of research has explored the impact of the pandemic on healthcare workers in developing countries. Most studies support a strong relationship between gender, depression, anxiety, and COVID-19 [31, 35, 36]. For example, a 2016 survey of doctors in a tertiary care hospital in Pakistan found that female doctors experienced higher rates of anxiety (6.6%) and depression (7.69%) compared to their male counterparts [37]. This finding aligns with our study, where women reported a significantly higher rate of threshold-level anxiety compared to men. Other studies have also reported a higher risk of psychological effects in women and clinicians [38–40]. Additionally, our study found that healthcare workers with more years of service (particularly between five and six years) had higher odds of experiencing anxiety and depression, consistent with previous studies [37]. FHWs with more service years may face increased odds of psychological distress due to prolonged stress, burnout, greater responsibilities, and disillusionment with systemic challenges, such as inadequate resources and support. Their heightened sense of duty, combined with a lack of recognition and insufficient mental health support, likely exacerbates feelings of frustration and helplessness.

In the current study, higher threshold levels of anxiety and depression were found among individuals who identified with religions outside of Christianity or Islam, reflecting the challenges faced by religious minorities. This finding is consistent with past studies showing that not only the virus itself but also the measures implemented to contain it often have a disproportionately negative impact on people from minority ethnic and religious groups, thereby further exacerbating health inequalities for these communities [41]. Minority religious groups may experience heightened psychological distress during a pandemic due to social marginalization, reduced access to religious and social support networks, and potential discrimination, which can intensify feelings of isolation and stress. Additionally, people from ethnic and religious minority groups were more likely to work in high-risk occupations that involve frequent contact with others, further increasing their exposure to the virus and contributing to their heightened anxiety and depression. These factors, when combined, can amplify mental health challenges, highlighting the importance of targeted interventions that address both the health risks and the unique psychological needs of minority groups during a crisis. The finding that FHWs who identified as Muslims had significantly lower odds of threshold-level depression and anxiety compared to those identifying as Christians was nullified after adjusting for the confounders. Although not significant, this finding is consistent with prior research [42]; it can be explained by the fact that Christians may experience higher existential anxiety related to status and death than Muslims [42], potentially influencing their mental health differently. Additionally, Muslims may be more likely to attribute misfortunes to divine will, which can foster acceptance, whereas Christians may engage in the rationalization of such events, potentially influencing their coping mechanisms. Further research is needed to explore the complex interplay of religious beliefs, coping styles, and mental health outcomes.

Notably, FHWs in the Southeastern states of Abia, Anambra, Enugu, and Imo experienced significantly higher odds for both anxiety and depression after adjusting for all potential confounders. This is in agreement with a past study [43], which observed that, compared to workers who reported living in areas with a low incidence, healthcare workers living in high-incidence areas had a significantly higher prevalence of clinically significant levels of depression and anxiety symptoms. The southeastern states, in particular, faced a higher burden of COVID-19 during the pandemic [13]. The observed variations in the rates of anxiety and depression among frontline healthcare workers across different states in Nigeria highlight the complex interplay of state factors that influence mental health outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic. These heightened rates could be attributed to various contextual factors, including the effectiveness of local public health measures and the socio-economic situation in these states. The Southeastern states of Nigeria have faced several challenges during the COVID-19 pandemic, such as healthcare infrastructure strain, higher infection rates, and increased community transmission, contributing to the elevated mental health burden among FHWs [44].

Conversely, FHWs in Kano, Bauchi, Kwara, Oyo, Osun, Lagos, Bayelsa, and Edo states reported lower odds of experiencing anxiety, indicating a potential resilience or effective coping mechanisms in these areas. The disparities between states could be explained by the differences in the local healthcare system’s capacity [45], community adherence to preventive measures, and the overall public health response [46]. Localities with more robust healthcare infrastructure and better pandemic management strategies might have provided better support systems for FHWs, reducing the mental health impact. While grouping states into larger geopolitical zones would reduce the number of levels and account for regional differences, this approach was not feasible for our study due to the design and granularity of the data. The primary aim of this study was to examine state-level variations in anxiety and depression among healthcare workers, necessitating the retention of individual states to preserve specificity and accuracy. For the current study, maintaining state-level analysis was essential for providing actionable insights at a more granular level. Aggregating states into zones would have limited our ability to capture localised disparities and may have introduced bias. We acknowledge the value of this approach and recommend it for future studies with broader sampling frameworks or higher-level regional analyses.

Across the states, featured nurses were more likely to report threshold-level depression and anxiety, while FHWs with fewer years of experience (< 7 years) were more likely to report threshold-level depression. This result was consistent with a recent study by Chen and colleagues [47], who examined the development of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)-like symptoms in nurses working with COVID-19 patients. In that study, women nurses working in intensive care units (ICUs) and COVID-19-designated health centres had the highest odds of developing PTSD-like symptoms. In a meta-analysis that assessed the prevalence of anxiety, depression, and insomnia during COVID-19 [48], nurses and female healthcare providers (HCPs) reported a higher rate of affective symptoms. These findings indicate that healthcare centres should focus on appropriate psychosocial support and treatment for these “at-risk” HCP groups. Healthcare administrators should provide nurses with information about coping skills and treatment for anxiety, depression, PTSS/PTSD, and other mental health disorders. The nurses should be made aware of the initial signs and symptoms of mental illnesses. Early intervention is vital, as mental health disorders can lead to dysfunction, internal suffering, and in the most extreme instances, result in death if not properly cared for. Furthermore, institutions should stress to nurses and other HCPs that they are not alone, that there is hope, and that mental health would bring about improvement in the seeking of help and time. Because of isolation and social distancing, it was not uncommon to feel alone and hopeless during the COVID-19 pandemic. Nonetheless, as a community, nurses should be supported; in addition, it should be ensured that they have access to and support for whatever care they may need. Our analysis revealed that in the univariable analysis, there was no association between age and levels of anxiety and depression. However, after adjusting for factors such as states in Nigeria, region, gender, education, and religion, we found that FHWs aged between 41 and 50 years were more likely to experience threshold-level anxiety and depression. This finding may be related to marital status, as a sub-analysis indicated that 65% of the participants were married, and among them, approximately 21% were in the 41 to 50 age group, while only 7% were aged between 19 and 30 years. This is similar to results from a past study [49], which observed that older age was a common risk factor for anxiety, depression, and social stress. Several past studies have explained that, compared with younger adults, older adults are usually predisposed to experiencing physical discomfort and chronic physical diseases that could contribute to mental illness; moreover, several older adults have been found to have many household responsibilities and economic burdens [50–53].

The psychological well-being of healthcare workers is very important for providing universal quality care. Since such workers play a vital role in response to the pandemic crisis, they require comprehensive psychological support for their mental well-being, to mitigate the negative effects of anxiety and stress due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The current study revealed that a prior experience of psychological distress was associated with lower odds of anxiety and depression. This finding suggests a potential coping or adaptation effect, where previous psychological distress may have led to greater resilience or coping mechanisms in the face of ongoing stressors. Similar associations have been observed in past studies, although in the opposite direction. For instance, a past study [54] has shown that a history of psychological distress increases the odds of developing stress-related symptoms due to COVID-19 by 1.51 times, suggesting that previous mental health challenges may exacerbate the impact of new stressors. This contrast highlights the complex nature of the relationship between prior distress and current mental health outcomes, which may depend on individual coping strategies and the severity of ongoing stressors.

Strengths and limitations of the study

One of the key strengths of this study is its use of well-established psychological measures, such as the HADS, ensuring that the findings are based on validated tools for accurately assessing anxiety and depression. This enhances the reliability of the results and contributes to the robustness of the conclusions. Additionally, the study’s focus on Africa, a continent with limited high-quality data on COVID-19-related mental health issues, provides valuable insights that are essential for informing public health efforts in the continent. The study also benefits from a comprehensive analytical approach, particularly the use of spatial analysis, which helps identify geographic patterns and variations in mental health outcomes. By highlighting the states with a higher burden of anxiety and depression among healthcare workers, the study offers critical data for designing targeted interventions and optimizing resource allocation. This approach not only provides a deeper understanding of where frontline healthcare workers (FHWs) are most affected but also strengthens the significance of the findings for improving mental health support for FHWs in Nigeria and similar settings.

The study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. One key limitation is the potential for response bias due to the self-reported nature of the data, which could affect the accuracy of the reported levels of anxiety and depression. Additionally, the use of an online survey may have excluded healthcare workers without internet access, potentially limiting the representativeness of the sample. The study also lacks data from Kogi state, where healthcare professionals declined to participate, introducing potential bias and limiting the generalizability of the findings to the entire Nigerian population. FHWs in Kogi, as well as those not directly working in the selected COVID-19 treatment centres but still in contact with affected patients, may have had different experiences that could influence their responses on the measured scales. Another limitation is the estimation of sample size using data from a prior study on gynaecological cancer patients, which may have introduced some inaccuracy. While this proxy was reasonable given the lack of local data on anxiety and depression among healthcare workers during COVID-19, it could have affected the precision of the sample size estimation. Furthermore, the use of a convenience sampling technique, while providing quick access to FHWs in hospitals, carries the risk of selection bias. The sample may not fully represent the broader population of healthcare workers, particularly those from different states, job roles, or working conditions, and factors such as willingness to participate may have influenced the results. Finally, the selection of only one COVID-19 treatment center per state limits the representativeness of the sample, as it excludes the varied experiences of FHWs working in different healthcare settings within each state. Despite these limitations, it is important to note that the operational dynamics in most state hospitals across the country are similar, which strengthens the relevance of the study’s conclusions. While these limitations should be acknowledged, they do not undermine the overall validity of the findings.

Conclusions

This study highlights the substantial psychological distress experienced by frontline health workers (FHWs) in Nigeria during the COVID-19 pandemic, with significant disparities in anxiety and depression prevalence between Nigerian states. Southeastern states exhibited the highest burden, while nurses and those with fewer years of professional experience were particularly vulnerable. Interestingly, FHWs with a prior history of psychological distress had lower odds of experiencing anxiety and depression, suggesting potential resilience or adaptive coping strategies.

Healthcare providers are vital resources in Nigeria, and their sense of duty often drives them to continue working despite significant challenges. Addressing these challenges requires urgent and targeted mental health interventions. Health authorities and stakeholders must prioritize exploring factors associated with psychological distress, such as anxiety and depression, and implement targeted interventions. Addressing specific risk factors identified in this study, such as gender, professional experience, and disparities between states, can guide more focused and effective interventions. For instance, gender-specific support programs, mentorship opportunities for less experienced healthcare workers, and tailored support for states with higher stress levels could significantly improve mental health outcomes. Encouraging healthcare workers to utilize existing support systems, such as Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs), peer support groups, and mental health hotlines, is effective in similar contexts. Administratively, measures such as limiting shift durations, ensuring regular breaks, and providing mental health training for healthcare managers to better support their teams can help alleviate stress and burnout. Future research should continue exploring the mental health of healthcare workers and evaluating the effectiveness of these specific interventions, particularly those addressing the identified risk factors, to enhance support and outcomes for healthcare workers in Nigeria.
