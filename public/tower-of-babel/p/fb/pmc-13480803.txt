Health Screening and Post‐Arrival Services for Refugee Children From Afghanistan
Authors: Amy Williamson, Eva Sudbury, Rija Khanal, Jade Woon, Jennifer Schaefer, Georgia Paxton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine refugee health screening and services for Afghan children in the unique context of emergency expedited humanitarian resettlement in Melbourne, Australia.MethodsRetrospective audit of Afghan children who attended a specialist child refugee health service between August 2021–April 2024.ResultsParticipants included 218 children aged 2 months—18 years, 57.8% male. Most (82.6%) were part of an intact family unit, 11.5% were unaccompanied minors. At first clinic visit, more than half held a temporary humanitarian visa with variable medicare access, and 51.4% were in hotel quarantine or short‐term accommodation due to the Covid‐19 pandemic. Only 17.4% children reported offshore healthcare related to travel. While 65.1% children were linked with a general practitioner, 90.8% still required refugee health screening and 92.7% required catch‐up vaccinations. The most common health issues were dental caries (28.4%), sleep concerns (17.0%), infections (14.2%) and developmental concerns (15.1%). Twenty children (9.2%) required hospital admission in the early post‐arrival period, 10.1% had intellectual disability and 8.7% had complex physical disability. Refugee health screening identified high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency (64.7%), B12 deficiency (44.7%), iron deficiency (29.5%) and anaemia (25.5%), and a range of rare diagnoses and communicable diseases.ConclusionsRefugee health screening remains essential in post‐arrival healthcare, allowing early identification and treatment of infections and micronutrient deficiencies and support for complex cases. Fragmented systems for refugee health screening in Melbourne are not meeting this need, and there is a strong case for specialist paediatric screening given the range of medical, developmental, disability, educational and social needs identified.Keywords: Afghanistan, health screening, refugee

Introduction

Australia accepts 20 000 humanitarian entrants annually, and has committed to resettlement of more than 30 000 Afghan refugees to 2026 [1]. Around 40% of humanitarian entrants to Australia are children 0 to 17 years [1].

In August 2021, the Taliban regained control of Afghanistan, resulting in an emergency humanitarian uplift of more than 120 000 foreign nationals and Afghan citizens. More than 4100 Afghans were evacuated to Australia, in the midst of the Covid‐19 pandemic [2]. At the time, Melbourne was experiencing some of the longest and most restrictive lockdowns in the world, with a complex system of hotel quarantine and disruptions to healthcare. International borders had been closed for 18 months, then reopened suddenly, and people arrived on temporary humanitarian visas without access to Medicare. Reception and settlement processes in Australia were profoundly impacted by the pandemic.

Standard processes for permanent migrants to Australia include an offshore Immigration Medical Examination (IME). Humanitarian entrants are offered an additional (voluntary) Departure Health Check (DHC) in the week before travel. The emergency uplift meant Afghan evacuees arrived without an IME or DHC, and the IME was completed after arrival through a contracted provider (BUPA).

Victoria uses a primary care model for refugee health screening (RHS), with general practitioner (GP) screening supported by nurses from the Refugee Health Program. Child RHS is fragmented, sitting within several areas of the healthcare system, including community health, private general practice and hospital outpatient services. Access to RHS is neither guaranteed nor followed up through any central process, so uptake is unknown. The RHP supports coordination of settlement healthcare but does not confirm RHS completion by medical providers. While consensus guidelines recommend comprehensive refugee health assessment for refugee arrivals [3, 4, 5], available evidence suggests few children have recommended screening or catch‐up immunisation in Victoria [6, 7, 8, 9]. Consequences of missed or incomplete screening and vaccination can be significant at individual and community levels [10].

The Royal Children's Hospital Immigrant Health Service (RCH IHS) is the oldest and largest child refugee health service in Australia. All children are offered comprehensive RHS, medical and developmental assessment, vaccination, mental healthcare and ongoing care/linkage to other services as required. Assessments are undertaken by experienced paediatricians, and the service includes mental health clinicians, social work and a dental therapist.

Ongoing assessment of key issues in refugee children and emerging cohorts is essential to: ensure health and developmental needs are addressed; plan healthcare and settlement supports; appraise screening guidelines; and understand whether existing programs for RHS and settlement support are meeting their intended goals.

This study aimed to report on presenting issues and results of post‐arrival RHS for new Afghan humanitarian arrivals aged 0–18 years. Additional objectives were to examine the proportion of children who had access to timely and complete RHS and immunisation catch‐up in primary care and the provision of settlement supports and school enrolment for this cohort.

Methods

We completed a retrospective audit of medical records for Afghan children seen in the RCH IHS between August 2021 and April 2024. Inclusion criteria were: Afghan background (country of birth or country of origin recorded as Afghanistan); attended at least one appointment (including face‐to‐face, telehealth or phone); arrived under offshore humanitarian pathways; and 0–18 years at the time of first visit. Electronic Medical Record (EMR) clinic lists were reviewed to ensure eligible children were included.

Clinicians used a standard EMR proforma for initial visits, recording migration, demographics, history, examination findings, and service access at the initial appointment. Parent/child concerns were recorded as provided. Routine care includes obtaining any external pathology results to avoid duplications in RHS.

Study data were collected and managed using secure REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the Murdoch Children's Research Institute. A REDCap survey reflecting the clinic proforma facilitated systematic data extraction, with participants assigned unique REDCap record numbers. A single investigator (AW) extracted data from the EMR into the secure REDCap database, including information from the initial clinic visit, pathology results and immunisation records. Results of RHS were included from RCH pathology or EMR scanned external pathology results were available, missing results were marked ‘not available’. Where date of arrival was listed as month/year, the mid‐point of the recorded month was imputed, and queries were resolved through consensus (AW, GP). Data were analysed using REDCap and Microsoft Excel (2023), using descriptive statistics to summarise key variables. Ethics approval was granted on 20 December 2022 through the RCH Human Resources Ethics Committee (HREC QA/90891/RCHM‐2022).

Results

Demographics

Overall 218 children (57.8% male) were included; demographics are shown in Table 1. Median age at first appointment was 7.8 years (IQR 4.0, 13.5 years; range 2 months–18 years). Most children were Afghan born (94.0%), Dari speaking (80.1%), and most visits (94.0%) required interpreting assistance. Families were generally large (median 6, IQR 5, 7), and 82.6% children were part of an intact family unit, although separation from extended family was common (66.1%). A total of 25 (11.5%) children were unaccompanied humanitarian minors (UHM), although a number dispersed interstate before direct review. Three quarters of the cohort arrived in the last 4 months of 2021, and 37.3% were part of the direct emergency evacuation.

Referral pathways to RCH IHS varied, but were most commonly from other RCH departments, reflecting the proximity of short‐term accommodation to the hospital and case complexity. Most children (89.0%) had an allocated case worker, although few (11.3%) reported meeting them directly. Around two thirds had Medicare, a similar proportion were linked with a GP, and 30.7% reported they were linked with a refugee health nurse.

Less than 10% children had completed refugee health screening in primary care, 25.2% had partial screening, and 64.7% had not had screening after arrival. Of children referred by a GP, 7/39 (17.9%) had completed RHS. BUPA IME had been completed for 35.8% children; however, results were only available for one child (via their GP). Catch‐up vaccinations had been commenced for 56.0% children and 52.8% had immunisations recorded on the Australian Immunisation Register (AIR). No children declined RHS at RCH, although some families moved area before screening tests were completed.

Education Access

The cohort included 148 school‐aged children (6–18 years); 83 (56.0%) had been to school overseas (median 1 year (IQR 0, 6), mean 3.1 years (SD 3.9) education). At first visit, 64 (43.2%) children were enrolled in school in Australia (37 language, 15 primary, 7 secondary, 5 specialist education). Overall, 21/148 (14.2%) children needed specialist education for complex disability, with most experiencing delays of 6–12 months in school enrolment. Only 7/27 (25.9%) 3‐ and 4‐year old children were enrolled in kindergarten.

Refugee Health Screening Results

Results from available RHS are presented in Table 5. Nutritional deficiencies were common, including anaemia (25.5%), iron deficiency (29.5%) and Vitamin D deficiency (64.7%); 10.5% of children had biochemical rickets (raised ALP), although only 1 child had hypocalcaemia. The prevalence of Vitamin B12 deficiency was 44.7%, despite high reported daily meat/dairy intake. No children had macrocytosis; 9/48 had elevated homocysteine; urine methylmalonic acid testing was usually not feasible. Helicobacter pylori faecal antigen testing was positive in 24/39 (61.5%) of those screened.

Tuberculosis (TB) screening mainly utilised interferon gamma release assays (IGRA) due to pandemic restrictions and practicalities of Mantoux testing. No children had active TB; 5.0% had latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI). There were 2 cases of Plasmodium vivax malaria in siblings, and 2 children had acute hepatitis A requiring admission. There were no cases of leishmaniasis or blood‐borne viruses, and only one child had Strongyloides (direct travel). Children were treated for micronutrient deficiencies and infections where these were identified (including H. pylori , parasites and LTBI).

Onward Referrals

The complexity of the cohort necessitated linkages and referrals to other services, including to primary care. Our service referred 118 (54.1%) children to GPs. All children were offered referrals to refugee nurses and trauma counselling, 50 (22.9%) and 9 (4.1%) accepted these linkages. Immunisation pathways included RCH (84, 38.5%), GPs (69, 31.7%), and other (39, 17.9%). Other referrals included dental (75, 34.3%), other RCH specialist services (64, 29.4%), audiology (46, 21.1%), school enrolments (46/148, 31.1%), nurse led clinic B12 injections (41, 18.8%), other paediatricians (40, 18.3%), optometry (28, 12.8%), NDIS (28, 12.8%) and mental health services (5, 2.3%).

Discussion

This was a unique refugee population due to direct evacuation from Afghanistan and arrival during Covid‐19 lockdowns for more than half the cohort, resulting in hotel quarantine, reduced access to early direct assessments, and delayed linkage with community and health organisations. For the first time in decades, large‐scale emergency accommodation was used after arrival, located close to RCH. Even with this proximity, more than half the first visits were completed by phone due to pandemic restrictions, and interpreting needs added complexity. There was a high proportion of UHM (11.5%), although in most cases, UHM status only became apparent during hotel quarantine, and this group dispersed across Victoria and interstate. First visits with UHM were especially complex given their experiences and support needs [11], and higher likelihood of mental health disturbance [11, 12].

Victorian primary care systems for undertaking RHS in children continue to face significant challenges. Similar to our previous findings in Syrian and Iraqi [6] and asylum seeker [8] cohorts, few children had completed recommended RHS in primary care, despite 89% having a case manager and 65.1% being linked with a GP. While guidelines recommend RHS within 1 month of arrival [3, 4], more than 90% of children still needed RHS and immunisation catch‐up at their first visit to our service, a median of 3 months after arrival.

Onshore contracted IME resulted in a universal lack of medical handover. Clinicians were unable to access BUPA IME summaries or results for their patients, and families were not provided with copies of IME testing, resulting in inefficient care, additional stress for children, unnecessary travel during lockdowns and higher Medicare expenditure on duplicated testing.

Our findings emphasise the importance of RHS for children, and the need for ongoing appraisal of screening guidelines for new cohorts. Screening detected multiple treatable medical conditions, facilitated immunisation and linkages to medical, dental and mental health care, and highlighted the complexity of this cohort. Micronutrient deficiency was common, with many children requiring treatment for iron, vitamin D and B12 deficiencies. Vitamin D deficiency was highly prevalent (64.7%) and 10.5% of children had biochemical rickets, similar to other studies in refugee children [6, 13, 14, 15], highlighting the need for screening and/or consideration of universal supplementation. Likely contributors include cultural and religious practices of reduced sun exposure, and for infants, low maternal vitamin D and screening represents an opportunity for education and planning for self‐management. The prevalence of B12 deficiency (44.7%) was striking and higher than previously reported in Afghan cohorts in the US [16], Australia [17] and Pakistan [14]. Multiple contributors are plausible, including over‐reporting of animal‐sourced foods, predominant intake of goat milk/meat with lower B12 content [18], low maternal vitamin B12 in breastfeeding infants and H. pylori infection, which is associated with B12 deficiency [19, 20, 21] with B12 levels improving after eradication [20]. Given the neurodevelopmental implications of low B12, it is important to identify, treat and prevent deficiency, especially in infants and breastfeeding mothers, and based on our findings, we recommend B12 screening for all Afghan arrivals.

Malaria and hepatitis A infections required hospital admission. Tuberculosis screening identified similar prevalence of LTBI to other studies of Afghan children [16], with relevance to individual and public health [10], noting major cost and access barriers to TB screening in primary care. While well described in Afghan populations [22, 23, 24], blood‐borne viruses, haemorrhagic fever and leishmaniasis were not identified, and strongyloidiasis was rare, which could be factored into future screening guidelines. Blood lead levels were not routinely tested as per guidelines [3, 4], however, subsequent US studies have identified elevated blood lead in 32% [16] and 61% [25] Afghan refugee children, and lead screening should be considered in RHS for Afghan children in Australia.

This was a complex cohort with significant health needs and a range of genetic, metabolic, and neurological diagnoses. Many children had complex disabilities requiring aids and equipment, and some required nutritional support and/or hospital admission for stabilisation. Children with disabilities experienced long delays in school enrolment and access to the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS). Supports for young people with disabilities sit across multiple sectors, without clarity on who holds the responsibility for facilitating timely access to equipment (wheelchairs, other aids), NDIS access or schooling. Consequences include system inefficiencies, avoidable hospital admissions, delayed supports and inappropriate school or housing placement.

Limitations of this study include sample size and missing data. Unknown screening results for many children will affect prevalence data, but in most cases mean our findings will be an underestimate. Cross‐sectional analysis only allows inferences about service access and provision at a point in time, although most children were seen more than 1 month after arrival, when screening should have been completed. Generalisability of findings to other Afghan refugee cohorts has limitations due to the unique context of the emergency evacuation and Covid‐19 pandemic. It is plausible that due to the urgency of travel for many of these families, their pre‐travel health status may differ from (and be better than) broader Afghan refugee populations awaiting humanitarian settlement, strengthening the case for screening. Given the specialist hospital setting, selection bias is possible, however this pattern of high complexity has continued in Australian refugee cohorts, with 30%–40% of all new arrivals having complex issues. (AMES settlement services personal communication, November 2025).

Finally, it is difficult to overstate the impact of pandemic‐related interruptions to every dimension of settlement and services—accommodation, casework, Medicare access (offices were closed), healthcare, interpreting format, vaccinations, pathology testing, and even hospital attendance as a whole family group with Covid‐19 screening. Post‐arrival care was fragmented and chaotic. Pandemic restrictions had substantial impacts on families' experience and understanding of key individuals and organisations supporting them. Often families had no recall of their case manager and did not know who had been calling them for healthcare. Restrictions on face‐to‐face contact and the implications for rapport‐building cannot be underestimated, and under‐reporting of physical and mental health symptoms is likely.

Conclusion

Afghan refugee children had a range of treatable conditions, and a significant proportion had complex medical conditions and/or disability requiring paediatric care and intensive specialised support. We saw significant inequity for children with disability, with delays to school enrolment and disability support, necessitating considered responses at service and policy levels.

Refugee health screening and immunisation remain fragmented in Victoria, with poor completion in primary care and significant inefficiencies, resulting in avoidable costs to families and the healthcare system. Current RHS and post‐arrival IME models should be examined and alternatives considered. Our findings highlight the ongoing need for early and complete post‐arrival RHS and catch‐up immunisation. There is a strong case for specialist paediatric assessment given the range of medical, developmental, disability, educational and social needs identified, alongside, and working with GPs, refugee nurses and well‐coordinated settlement support to enable a healthy and successful transition to life in Australia. Australia's commitment to humanitarian settlement must be met with a commitment to provide the necessary support and infrastructure for our newest arrivals.
