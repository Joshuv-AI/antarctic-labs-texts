Pedestrian Crashes in the Northern Territory, Australia: A Detailed Epidemiological Analysis From 2013 to 2023
Authors: S Clifford, K K S Lee, G Lamba, A Dadi, R Canty, F Wardle, R Ramsamy, C J C Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesPedestrian crashes are a major concern in the Northern Territory (NT), Australia, but the factors associated with these crashes differ from those in other Australian jurisdictions. This study aims to provide a comprehensive investigation of pedestrian crashes in the NT.MethodsUsing police crash records and hospitalisation data, we conducted a retrospective observational analysis. We describe crash factors and calculate standardised rate ratios to compare involvement by gender, age and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status. Stepwise logistic regression was used to explore crash factors contributing to fatal and ‘severe injury collision’ outcomes.ResultsMale pedestrians were 1.43 times (95% CI: 1.22–1.66) more likely to be involved in crashes and 1.38 times more likely to be hospitalised (95% CI: 1.23–1.56). Across all age groups, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander pedestrians had higher crash involvement and fatality rates. Among those aged 35–44 years, the likelihood of being involved in a fatal crash increased 35‐fold (95% CI: 8.81–305.88), while severe injury risk increased 14‐fold (95% CI: 8.83–23.81). Most crashes occurred during clear weather and light traffic, on straight, flat, sealed and dry roads. Factors that influence the severity of injuries in a pedestrian crash are visibility, road speed limit and driver and pedestrian alcohol and drug use.ConclusionImproving lighting on roads with speed limits ≥ 70 km/h should be prioritised, or speed limits reduced where lighting upgrades are not feasible. Keeping people safe while they are intoxicated is a priority for reducing pedestrian injuries and deaths.

Introduction

Of all road users, pedestrians are the most at risk of injury or death during a crash [1]. Reducing pedestrian deaths is a goal of the World Health Organisation, with a 25% global decline in the past decade [2]. Although this reduction has been disproportionately driven by declines in mortality in middle‐ and high‐income countries [2], in Australia, pedestrian deaths have reduced very marginally (1% decline) [3]. Within high‐income countries, the social determinants of health play an important role, with people of lower socioeconomic status and from historically marginalised ethnic groups over‐represented in pedestrian mortality [4, 5, 6, 7]. Meta‐analyses indicate that key risk factors contributing to pedestrian trauma include infrastructure (lack of dedicated facilities for pedestrians; lighting), pedestrian characteristics and behaviour (clothing visibility; intoxication), driver characteristics and behaviour (speeding, failing to give way, and drink/drug driving) and vehicle design [1]. The presence of these risk factors tends to be correlated with lower socioeconomic status and historical marginalisation [5, 7].

In 2022, pedestrian deaths accounted for 13.7% of road fatalities in Australia [3] but 23.1% of road fatalities in the Northern Territory (NT) [8]. The NT is a vast, yet sparsely populated jurisdiction located in the central north of the Australian continent. The NT population only accounts for 1% of the total Australian population, the majority of whom (60%) reside in the Greater Darwin area [9]. Compared to the national population, the NT is younger (median age: 33 vs. 38 nationally), with slightly more men than women and a greater proportion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples (26.3% vs. 3.2% nationally) [9]. These demographic characteristics are worth noting, because as a result of historical and contemporary colonial practices, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples experience greater structural inequity [10]. Reflecting these inequities, four of the top 10 most disadvantaged local government areas in Australia are located in the NT [11]. Despite the higher prevalence of pedestrian fatalities in the NT, there has been limited work examining the issue in this context. Previous work in this jurisdiction found the death rate of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander pedestrians was 10 times higher than that of their non‐Indigenous counterparts [12]. Compared to other Australian jurisdictions, the rate of pedestrian fatalities in the NT is 21 times greater than in South Australia, 7 times greater than in New South Wales and 3.5 times greater than in Western Australia (the state with the second‐highest rate of fatalities) [13].

Dempsey's [12] study used data from 1999 to 2007, and Tiemensma and Byard [13] only examine pedestrian deaths (not all pedestrian crashes). In recent years, there has been an increase in public interest in this issue, following a number of high‐profile cases and calls from Aboriginal leaders to address this issue [14, 15, 16]. This study aims to describe and analyse the demographic, behavioural, environmental, and collision characteristics of pedestrian crashes in the NT, with the objective of identifying patterns, contributing factors, and contextual conditions that shape pedestrian injury risk. We note that earlier findings are presented in the associated technical report [17] with this paper extending this work to provide greater methodological specificity and a fuller analytical interpretation tailored for an academic audience.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted retrospective observational analyses of two datasets comprised of routinely collected administrative data to quantitatively measure epidemiological factors associated with pedestrian crashes. These included police‐recorded crash records and records of hospitalisations related to pedestrian crashes. We assessed a 10‐and‐a‐half‐year period from January 2013 to June 2023 to identify any changes over time in the rate of pedestrian crashes. We considered a 10‐year period to be an appropriate timeframe for a contemporary and comprehensive investigation of pedestrian crashes, providing enough data to identify patterns while remaining reflective of current conditions.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the NT Department of Health and Menzies School of Health Research Human Research Ethics Committee (2024‐4825), which included review by the Aboriginal Ethics Sub‐Committee.

Datasets

Police Crash Data

Police crash records (1 January 2013–30 June 2023) were provided by the NT Government Department of Infrastructure Planning and Logistics via the Vehicle Accident Database. Records with the Crash Type Description of ‘Hit Pedestrian’ were supplied. Records included (where available) demographics and circumstances of pedestrian(s) and driver(s), details and location of the crash, details of the vehicle, temporal and environmental factors and outcomes. The unit types (i.e., driver, pedestrian, cyclist, etc.) involved in each crash were reviewed, and 24 records were identified as not involving a pedestrian; this was discussed with the data custodians, who confirmed the Crash Type Description had been a miscode and, as such, were excluded before analysis.

Hospitalisations

Hospitalisation data (1 January 2013–30 June 2022) was provided by NT Health. Hospitalisation is defined as an episode of hospital care that starts with a formal admission process and ends with a formal separation process. Records were included if they included ICD‐10 codes relevant to pedestrian injury (for the full list, see Table S1). Records included the demographics of patients, date and time of admission, and all ICD‐10 codes associated with the hospitalisation.

Analysis

We used information from the Australian Census to standardise the data by gender, age, and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status and calculate population rates (per 10 000) [18]. Specifically, 2016 Census information was used for data from 2013 to 2015 and 2021 Census information was used for data from 2018 onwards [18]. This standardised data was used to calculate the incident rate ratios by demographics. We assessed statistically significant trend changes in quarterly population rates per 10 000 for all pedestrians involved in police‐recorded crashes, pedestrian crashes resulting in severe injury (death or hospitalisation as recorded by police), and hospitalisation (as recorded by the hospitals) using Poisson regression models. Differences are reported as statistically significant when p < 0.05. Crash factors are presented descriptively. Subsequently, stepwise logistic regression was used to explore factors contributing to fatal and ‘severe injury' outcomes (fatality or serious injury requiring hospital attendance combined) in the police dataset, with independent variables included in the multivariate analysis if a significant association was found in the bivariate analyses (p < 0.05). Bivariate analysis is presented in Table S2. All data cleaning and analysis were conducted in Stata v17.0.

Results

From 1 January 2013 to 30 June 2023, there were 709 pedestrian crashes recorded by police, involving 727 pedestrians and 723 drivers. Four in five pedestrian crashes resulted in an injury to the pedestrian (81%; n = 570), with just over half (52%; n = 367) resulting in a hospitalisation. Approximately one in 10 crashes resulted in a fatality (11%; n = 82). Two of the fatal crashes each involved more than one pedestrian fatality, resulting in a total of 84 pedestrian deaths over the 10‐year period. Over the same period, 1154 pedestrians were admitted to NT hospitals as a result of injuries sustained in a traffic crash. Data discrepancy is not uncommon when using police and hospital data to investigate pedestrian crashes. In Victoria, the police‐recorded serious injuries (pedestrian recorded as transported to hospital) underestimated the number of actual hospitalisations for pedestrian injuries (only 44.1% of hospitalisations were recorded as such in police records) [19]. In contrast, other research from the NT (which probabilistically linked police recorded crash data and hospitalisation data) found that only 48% of traffic injuries (for all road users) identified by police as serious were in fact admitted to hospital [12]. The inconsistency likely reflects the different legislative requirements and core business of the agencies. For example, the NT Traffic Act requires blood samples to be collected ‘if a person enters a hospital or health centre for examination or treatment of injuries which may have been received in a crash’ (Traffic Act 1987 (NT), s.29AAK). It is possible that this may contribute to an overcount of injuries that could have resulted from traffic crashes in health care settings to ensure compliance with legislation regarding blood collection.

Pedestrian Demographics—Police and Hospital Records

Male pedestrians were 1.43 times more likely (95% CI: 1.22–1.66) to be involved in pedestrian crashes (34.20 per 10 000 males compared to 23.98 per 10 000 females) (Table 1). Males were also 1.38 times more likely (95% CI: 1.23–1.56) to be hospitalised as a result of a pedestrian crash. Across all age bands in both datasets, rates (standardised by age and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status) were higher for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander pedestrians compared to non‐Indigenous pedestrians. Those aged between 35 and 44 years were 14 times (95% CI: 8.83–23.81) more likely to be involved in a crash and 35 times (8.81, 305.88) more likely to be fatally injured than their non‐Indigenous peers. People aged between 45 and 54 years were 19 times (95% CI: 10.42–36.54) more likely to be involved in a crash and 37 times (95% CI: 5.70–1584.73) more likely to be fatally injured. We saw the same pattern in hospitalisations: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander pedestrians aged between 35 and 44 years were 19 times (95% CI: 13.34–28.99) more likely to be hospitalised for crash‐related injuries and 45‐ to 54‐year‐olds were 12 times (95% CI: 9.05–18.45) more likely. Police records showed a slightly lower proportion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples (68%; 12% unknown) compared to hospitalisation records (74%; less than 1% unknown). However, police‐recorded fatal pedestrian crashes were substantially higher for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples (86%).

The magnitude of the change, however, was very small (< 2% for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander pedestrians and < 3% for non‐Indigenous pedestrains). Rates of pedestrian crashes recorded in hospitalisation, however, did not significantly change during the time period (Figure 3).

Road Characteristics—Police Records

Table 3 summarises infrastructure and environmental factors for police‐recorded crashes. Although more than two‐thirds (70%) of crashes occurred on roads with speed limits between 40 and 60 km/h, the majority of fatalities (64%) occurred on roads with a speed limit of 70 km/h and over. Traffic density was typically recorded as being light at the time of the crash, and the weather was typically clear. The vast majority of crashes occurred on straight, flat, sealed roads that were dry. Half of pedestrian crashes occurred on divided roads.

These data do not differentiate between the alcohol use of drivers or pedestrians.

Factors Contributing to Fatalities and Severe Pedestrian Injuries—Police Crash Data

Table 6 presents the multivariate logistic regression identifying which factors were associated with crashes being fatalities and severe injuries (fatalities and injuries requiring hospitalisation combined) in the police crash dataset. Poor visibility (i.e., darkness with no street lighting) resulted in 10.9 times the odds of a crash resulting in a pedestrian fatality (95% CI: 1.66–71.37) and 2.7 times the odds of a crash resulting in a severe injury to a pedestrian (95% CI: 1.41–5.11). Reduced light was also associated with increased odds of crashes: with a 10.9% increase in odds of fatality at dawn and dusk (95% CI: 1.66–71.37) and a 1.6% increase in odds of a severe injury in darkness with street lighting (95% CI: 1.06–2.36). For every 1 km increase in the road's speed limit, the odds of a pedestrian crash resulting in a fatality increased by 1.03 (95% CI: 1.01–1.05) and the odds of the crash resulting in a severe injury increased by 1.02 (95% CI: 1.01–1.03). Crashes being recorded as alcohol‐related were significantly associated with both fatalities (OR: 65.38, 95% CI: 23.79–179.68) and severe injuries (OR: 3.34, 95% CI: 1.55–7.19), while being recorded as drug‐related was only associated with fatalities (OR: 13.2, 95% CI: 3.12–55.80). The traffic density, the vertical and horizontal features of the road and whether the crash was speeding‐related or occurred in a rural location had a nonsignificant effect on the crash outcome.

Discussion

This study provides a detailed analysis of 10.5 years of pedestrian crashes and fatalities in the NT due to traffic collisions, based on police and hospitalisation data. In the NT, pedestrian crashes typically happen when traffic is light and the weather is clear, on flat, sealed roads that are dry. Although neither traffic density nor weather was significantly associated with crash outcomes in our data, research from the United States indicates that rain may deter pedestrian activity, leading to fewer interactions with vehicles [20]; however, as indicated by research from Hong Kong, when pedestrian crashes do happen in the rain, the severity of outcomes is greater [21]. Although rates of all pedestrian crashes and those resulting in severe injury significantly decreased over the 10‐and‐a‐half‐year period, the absolute magnitude of these changes was very small (1.64% and 1.36%, respectively), suggesting that risk factors remained relatively stable over the study period. Factors which influence the severity of injuries in a pedestrian crash in the NT are visibility, road speed limit, and alcohol and drug involvement. Visibility is a major factor in pedestrian injuries, with increased odds of a fatal crash occurring at dawn/dusk and in the darkness without streetlights. Road speed limits are also very important for crash outcomes, as the odds of a pedestrian crash resulting in a fatality on NT roads increase by 3.3% (95% CI: 1.01–1.05) for every 1 km/h increase in designated road speed. Since only 2% of crashes and 6% of fatalities were recorded as speed‐related by police, this is not a result of speeding but of high‐speed limits. Although we did not have the variables to examine impact speed in these data, we know from meta‐analyses that it is an important factor that increases the risk of fatality. For example, on average, as the estimated impact speed increases by 1 km/h, the odds of a pedestrian fatality increase on average by 11% [22]. As the majority (64%) of pedestrian fatalities in the NT occur on roads where the speed limit is 70 km/h and above, understanding the combined impact of high‐speed limits and poor visibility is an important consideration for road safety improvements. These should include both targeted speed reduction measures and improvements to visibility through good quality lighting.

Alcohol and drug involvement is a key contributor to NT pedestrian crashes. We found the odds of a pedestrian crash being fatal were 65 times greater if alcohol was involved (95% CI: 23.79–179.68) and 3 times greater for severe injuries (95% CI: 3.12–55.80). The crash being drug‐related increased the odds of fatality by 13 times. In the NT, alcohol‐related harms are an ongoing concern, estimated to cost the government $1.3 billion a year [23]. Regarding pedestrian intoxication specifically, previous work has highlighted a phenomenon of pedestrians in the NT sleeping on the warm road surface while intoxicated from alcohol [13]. This also occurs in Okinawa, Japan (called ‘rojo‐ne’, which translates to ‘sleeping on the road’), and is reportedly related to the consumption of a local brewed rice‐based spirit combined with warm weather conditions [13]. Although a higher proportion of the overall NT population consumes alcohol above the recommended national guidelines (33% drink more than 10 standard drinks per week compared to 25% nationally; 34% drink more than four drinks on an occasion compared to 24% nationally) [24], alcohol‐related harms are higher among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples [25]. There is clear evidence that this disproportionate burden of harms is a result of the coalescence of alcohol consumption with other risk factors related to colonisation; however, public and political discourse typically does not recognise this. Keeping individuals who are intoxicated away from roads should be a priority to reduce pedestrian deaths. This may include responses like the provision of safe spaces for intoxicated pedestrians (including sobering‐up shelters and managed alcohol programmes) and increased drink‐driving policing.

Pedestrian crashes predominantly affect middle‐aged, Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander men. The gender difference is consistent with what we see in other Australian states [19] and overseas in similar high‐income countries like the United States of America [26] and Canada [27]. This gender difference is hypothesised to stem from greater risk‐taking behaviours among men, including walking late at night and in poorly lit areas, which women are more likely to avoid due to personal safety concerns [26]. The over‐representation of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples is not an NT‐specific issue. Nationally the fatal injuries rate for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander pedestrians is 5.2 times higher than non‐Indigenous pedestrians, while the rate of serious injuries is almost double [28]. However, because Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples account for a smaller proportion of the population in other jurisdictions, the inequity is more noticeable in the NT. Similar racial disparities are seen in the United States of Ame, with Black Americans and Native Americans both disproportionately involved in pedestrian crashes [6]. Native Americans and Alaskan Natives have the highest pedestrian fatality rates, with those living in rural areas experiencing double the fatality rate compared to those in urban areas [5]. Increased risk was greatest in those aged 35–54 years. This is congruent with national data, which demonstrates that although rates of fatal injury for non‐Indigenous pedestrians are highest in early adulthood, the rates of fatal injuries for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander pedestrians peak in middle age [28]. This pattern is the same for fatal injury for car drivers and car passengers. Dempsey [12] demonstrated a similar demographic pattern using data up to 2007, and here we highlight that the same group remain at risk. In high‐income countries, pedestrian deaths are more common among people with lower incomes, as they are more likely to rely on walking as their main form of transport [4]. This data suggests that pedestrian deaths in the NT are an equity issue—as is also seen for First Nations peoples in North America. That is, the factors that create and facilitate an unsafe built environment are likely influenced by systemic racism. For example, research from the United States of America has found that a structurally racist policy from almost 100 years ago (‘redlining’, a policy of grading neighbourhoods and denying residents in ‘low grade’ neighbourhoods financial credit and loans implemented in the 1930s) continues to influence pedestrian fatalities [29]. As the historical neighbourhood ‘grade’ worsened, modern pedestrian fatality rates increased, with ongoing high poverty rates, reliance on public transit or active transportation and limited, unsafe, high‐speed roadway infrastructure [29]. This means that targeted improvements that make the environment safer, like decreased speed, improved lighting and safer footpath networks and pedestrian crossings, are warranted.

Limitations

Regarding BAC, we note that a large proportion of drivers and pedestrians did not have their BAC recorded in the Vehicle Accident Database. This data is provided by the police to the Vehicle Accident Database, which is managed by the Department of Logistics and Infrastructure. Police may collect roadside alcohol (breath) and drug (oral fluid) tests and if positive, follow this by collecting an evidential sample. If the road users are injured and attend a hospital or health centre, blood must be taken (per legislative requirements noted above). There are some exceptions to this (i.e., if it would be detrimental to the patient's medical condition) and there is no specific requirement for these bloods to be tested. Police typically collect blood for testing if required to support a prosecution or coronial inquest. The forensic testing of blood can take some time to complete, particularly if samples are being sent in from remote locations. We also note that alcohol recording in major datasets is a recognised problem in other jurisdictions, for example, although BAC was recorded by the Victorian State Trauma Registry, no test results were available for 41.3% of cases [30].

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of over a decade of pedestrian crashes and fatalities in the NT. Crashes are most likely to occur under clear weather conditions on straight, sealed roads with high‐speed limits. Poor visibility significantly increases the odds of fatalities and severe injuries occurring. Alcohol and drug involvement also dramatically elevate the risk, highlighting the need for targeted harm‐reduction strategies to keep people safe while intoxicated. Middle‐aged Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander men are disproportionately affected, reflecting broader inequities in road safety and systemic factors.

Policy implications from this work include addressing systemic inequities, particularly regarding improving road infrastructure, appropriate urban planning and implementing culturally safe interventions for intoxicated pedestrians. These approaches are essential to reducing pedestrian fatalities and fostering equity in road safety outcomes.
