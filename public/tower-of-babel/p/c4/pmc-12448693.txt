ECG challenge: a double speed heart
Authors: Hong Yee Lim, Yew Chung Chan, Lee Yee Lim, Stefano Bordignon
Published: 2025

Case

An 80-year-old gentleman presented with palpitations and dizziness for several days. His past medical history was notable for dyslipidaemia, for which he was on atorvastatin. He had no other medical conditions and was not taking any other medications, particularly antiarrhythmic drugs.

Question 2

Vagal-mediated

Vagal-mediated

Bradycardia-dependent (phase 4) AV block

Bradycardia-dependent (phase 4) AV block

PVC with concealed retrograde conduction into the AV node

PVC with concealed retrograde conduction into the AV node

Intrinsic AV node decremental property

Intrinsic AV node decremental property

Infra-nodal disease

Infra-nodal disease

The answer is B.

Discussion and explanation

Wenckebach is most commonly due to intrinsic AV nodal decremental conduction, sometimes influenced by increased vagal tone or concealed retrograde conduction from PVCs.1 In this case, the presence of interpolated PVCs may result in retrograde conduction into the AV node, progressively slowing antegrade conduction and eventually blocking a P wave2 (refer Supplementary material online, Figure S1 for ladder diagram demonstration). Although rare, infra-nodal disease can present with a Wenckebach pattern.3 The overall heart rate is not markedly slow, and AV conduction actually improves after a pause, making bradycardia-dependent (phase 4) AV block unlikely as the primary mechanism. In our case, Holter monitoring was performed and revealed disappearance of the Wenckebach phenomenon during PVC-free segments, further supporting that it was PVC-induced.

Question 3

What is the likely mechanism of the PVC?

Automaticity

Re-entry

Delayed afterdepolarization (DAD)

Depolarization abnormality

Depolarization abnormality

The answer is C.

Discussion and explanation

A constant coupling interval of the PVC suggests re-entry or triggered activity, as automaticity usually produces irregular coupling intervals. The PVCs disappear when there is no preceding normal QRS complex, indicating that their initiation depends on the preceding depolarization rather than spontaneous firing from enhanced automaticity. Both re-entry and triggered activity require a preceding beat, however:

Re-entry: Once initiated, can sustain itself independently.

Re-entry: Once initiated, can sustain itself independently.

Triggered activity: Requires a preceding QRS each time.

Triggered activity: Requires a preceding QRS each time.

EAD occurs before full repolarization and is typically associated with prolonged QT intervals and Torsades de Pointes. DAD occurs after full repolarization, often triggered by high intracellular calcium, and matches the mechanism in this case.
