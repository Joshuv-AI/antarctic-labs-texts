Umbilical hernia rupture with small bowel evisceration in advanced cirrhosis (flood syndrome)
Authors: Martin Dubovský, Arpád Panyko, Marián Vidiščák, Marianna Hajská
Published: 2026

A 36-year-old male with decompensated liver cirrhosis presented in extremis with spontaneous rupture of an umbilical hernia and small bowel evisceration. Clinical examination revealed profound jaundice, severe cachexia, and approximately 40 cm of congested, ischemic ileal loops protruding through a ruptured umbilical defect, with active mesenteric hemorrhage (Fig. 1).

Ruptured umbilical hernia (U) with evisceration of congested ileal loops (arrow) in a patient with decompensated liver cirrhosis. Active hemorrhage is evident.

The patient was in profound hemorrhagic shock, with undetectable blood pressure, severe anemia (hemoglobin 48 g/L), and coma. Laboratory evaluation confirmed severe anemia (hemoglobin 48 g/L, erythrocytes 1.24 × 1012/L, hematocrit 0.14), leukocytosis (20.66 × 109/L), marked hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin 207 μmol/L, conjugated bilirubin 194 μmol/L), elevated aspartate aminotransferase (14.10 μkat/L), and severe coagulopathy (APTT ratio 4.97; prothrombin time not measurable).

A massive transfusion protocol with concurrent coagulation reversal was initiated, and emergent damage control laparotomy was performed under high-dose vasopressor support. Intraoperatively, an incarcerated, ischemic, non-peristaltic segment of ileum was identified, with active hemorrhage originating from the mesentery. Mesenteric bleeding was controlled, necrotic umbilical tissue was excised, and approximately 40 cm of ileum was resected. A stapled side-to-side anastomosis was constructed. Hemostasis was achieved despite severe coagulopathy. Advanced macronodular liver cirrhosis was confirmed intraoperatively (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative view demonstrating macronodular morphology of the cirrhotic liver.

Despite aggressive resuscitation with blood products, surgical intervention, and maximal intensive care support, refractory hypotension developed, and the patient died one hour postoperatively.

Correct answer: C. Flood syndrome.

Flood syndrome arises in cirrhotic patients with long-standing, large-volume ascites, which increases intra-abdominal pressure and weakens the umbilical region, leading to hernia formation in ~20% of such patients [1]. Rupture is often triggered by a sudden pressure rise (coughing, vomiting, straining, heavy lifting) or local trauma, especially when skin over the hernia shows ulceration, erythema, or necrosis. Ascites itself reflects advanced portal hypertension and is linked to poor prognosis. Presentation typically involves a “gush” or continuous leakage of ascitic fluid from an umbilical defect, sometimes after a prodrome of skin breakdown. Complications include peritonitis, bowel incarceration, evisceration, hemodynamic instability, renal injury, and sepsis [2]. Reported overall mortality for Flood syndrome is about 30% [3].
