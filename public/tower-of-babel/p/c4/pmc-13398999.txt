Memorizing the future: Encoding anticipated interactions enhances human memory for dynamic stimuli
Authors: Gonzalo Aparicio-Rodríguez, Daniel Ruiz Navalón, Paloma Manubens, Abel Sánchez-Jiménez, Carlos Calvo-Tapia, José Antonio Villacorta-Atienza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurvival in nature requires responding to rapidly changing situations, where memory is crucial for fast and reliable decision-making. This work explores human memory modulation under the hypothesis that in dynamic scenarios critical information is encoded as static maps of future interactions. Results show that dynamic stimuli containing future interactions are recalled 36% better than equivalent stimuli that do not and even 23% better than simpler dynamic stimuli. This aligns with a central prediction of the time-compaction hypothesis, suggesting that stimuli involving anticipated interactions may be more efficiently memorized and recalled because they are compatible with simplified static representations of future interactions—their maps of interactions. This effect is modulated by stimulus complexity: in simple scenarios, recall differences appear only in men, while in more complex situations, no gender bias emerges. This work reveals novel mechanisms by which memory can cope with dynamic situations, suggesting that those critical to survival—fighting, chasing, or fleeing—are preferentially remembered, enabling efficient learning and decision-making in a complex changing world.

Adaptive behavior in a changing world requires efficiently encoding and recalling dynamic events. This study shows that human memory more effectively retains dynamic events that involve future interactions—such as potential collisions—than those without them. These results align with the hypothesis that the brain encodes such dynamic situations through static maps that spatially organize predicted interactions. By supporting this idea, our findings suggest a complexity-modulated mechanism by which memory can efficiently extract and store the most behaviorally relevant information. This work reveals how the human brain may prioritize events critical for prediction and action, offering new insight into the cognitive foundations of learning and decision-making in a dynamic world.

Introduction

Memory is an essential cognitive process inextricably linked to spatial encoding of the environment (1) and, thus, to navigation (2). In particular, dealing with dynamic situations (which imply the spatial change over time of the subject and/or elements in the environment) requires a central role of memory, as it entails retaining the temporal and spatial location of the elements around the subject (3, 4).

Working memory, besides its relevance in dealing with static scenarios (5, 6), is of paramount importance to overcome the daily challenges of dynamic situations (7), playing a key role in maintaining situation awareness (i.e. the ability to perceive and interpret a situation correctly to respond accordingly) (8), allowing visual integration and representation of mobile object characteristics in humans (9), and providing a more efficient and safer navigation in dynamic environments when implemented to AIs (10).

A critical aspect of working memory is its restricted capacity, as only a limited amount of information can be retained for a limited time (11). Thus, aspects such as energy, time, and structural efficiency are crucial for memory (12, 13) and consequently, information complexity often needs to be reduced for a proper encoding and recalling (14, 15). This is especially important in time-changing situations, which pose specific challenges to working memory (9), since dynamic stimuli comprise complex information, as the subject needs to process his own and the other elements’ position, directions, velocities, and accelerations for each moment. Therefore, in dynamic situations, complexity reduction is mandatory to encode and retrieve information in real time for efficient decision-making (16).

The complexity of real-world information imposes constraints not only on memory but also on processing in general. In living beings, rapidly interpreting the immediate environment is essential for adaptive behavior (17), particularly in dynamic contexts where opportunities and threats arise from other individuals. In such contexts, efficient behavior depends not only on perceiving ongoing events but also on anticipating their future consequences in order to guide rapid and adaptive responses. To explain humans’ ability to efficiently process dynamic environments, it has been proposed that the brain optimizes incoming information through a mechanism termed “time compaction” (18). Time compaction refers to a representational process by which dynamic situations are internally transformed into simplified predictive models emphasizing future interactions, such as encounters or collisions involving the observer. The resulting constructs, termed compact internal representations (CIRs), are simplified time-independent representations in which anticipated interactions are spatially encoded. Thus, through time compaction, dynamic situations are transformed into static maps—the CIRs—that preserve relational information about predicted interactions while reducing the need to explicitly encode the full spatiotemporal dynamics of the scene (Fig. 1A).

Within a CIR, future interactions are spatially encoded without explicitly representing the full temporal dynamics of the scene. This framework shares conceptual similarities with broader theories of dynamic cognition. In particular, by emphasizing anticipated future interactions, time compaction is consistent with predictive approaches proposing that cognition relies on the generation and updating of internal predictions (19–22). Likewise, the compression of continuous dynamic information into CIRs parallels event segmentation accounts, according to which ongoing experience is organized into discrete cognitive units (23–26). More generally, time compaction provides a representational framework linking predictive processing, dynamic event perception, and memory optimization in behaviorally relevant contexts.

Evidence suggests that time compaction supports the processing of dynamic stimuli in humans (27) and other species, including rats (28) and bats, whose hippocampal activity encodes prospective self-positions at potential collision points (29). Whether this mechanism extends to higher-order cognitive processes such as learning (16) and memory remains an open question. The memory issue is particularly relevant given that encoding and recall are critical for adaptive behaviors like navigation (30). Notably, the information reduction observed in other memory processes parallels the principles of time compaction (31), suggesting that the relationship between stimulus simplification and recall efficiency (32, 33) may provide a useful framework to investigate the interplay between memory and time compaction.

With this aim, we implemented a recognition memory task (34, 35) using visual stimuli with two moving spheres that either approach to collide—collision is not displayed—(collision stimuli) or that move apart (noncollision stimuli). According to the time compaction hypothesis, a collision stimulus is encoded as a CIR (18) (Fig. 1B), while noncollision stimuli, lacking future interactions, are encoded through diverse spatiotemporal attributes (Fig. 1B). As time compaction reduces cognitive load, collision stimuli should be more stable and easier to recall (32, 33).

Consistent with this prediction, the results obtained here show that dynamic stimuli with future interactions were recalled better than those without, and even better than simpler dynamic stimuli (a single moving sphere), supporting their encoding as simplified static representations. Interestingly, this effect appeared only in men during simple tasks but in both genders during complex tasks, reflecting previously reported gender differences in time compaction salience (27). These results reveal new mechanisms by which memory operates in dynamic situations, supporting time compaction as a key mechanism in human cognition.

Results

To study the role of time compaction in working memory, we employed a recognition memory task to test the above-mentioned hypothesis. During the encoding phase, participants viewed a series of collision and noncollision stimuli (old stimuli) and were asked to memorize them (Fig. 2A). After a brief distractor video, the recalling phase began, where participants indicated whether each presented stimulus—either old or new—had been seen before (Fig. 2B). Two task versions of varying complexity were used in separate groups, termed simple and complex recognition memory tests. A looming recognition memory test, using a single moving sphere, served as a control to compare with the memorization of simpler dynamic stimuli and to isolate the specific contribution of time compaction from general motion-related effects.

Structure of the experimental procedure, consisting of two consecutive phases. A) Encoding phase. Participants were asked to memorize a set of stimuli, termed old stimuli. Each stimulus was followed by a gray screen with a static white Greek cross on its center, so participants could discriminate between consecutive stimuli. B) Recalling phase. A set of old stimuli and a set of new stimuli were displayed. After each stimulus, the participant was asked, if they had already seen that stimulus in the encoding phase.

The working hypothesis—that collision stimuli are better recalled than noncollision stimuli—was tested using two spheres moving in a bidimensional plane. In old stimuli, the red spheres’ summed trajectory vector aligned with the bisector of an xy-plane quadrant, whereas in new stimuli, it was parallel to either the x- or y-axis (Fig. 3A). During encoding, participants viewed eight stimuli (four collision and four noncollision) in pseudorandom order; during recall, they were randomly presented with 12 (three old and three new of collision and noncollision). A total of 237 people took part in this test.

New stimuli were designed to be easily distinguishable by orientation, serving as a baseline to confirm attention and task compliance. Hence, participants performed better for new than for old stimuli (generalized linear mixed model [GLMM]—includes random effect [random intercept] for participant—triple interaction: odds ratio [OR] = 0.20, 95% CI [CI95] = 0.07–0.62, P = 0.005, Table S5; GLMM—phase: OR = 0.63, CI95 = 0.48–0.82, P < 0.001, Table S6). No gender (GLMM—gender: OR = 0.64, CI95 = 0.36–1.14, P = 0.126, Table S7) or stimulus-type (GLMM—type: OR = 1.22, CI95 = 0.80–1.85, P = 0.351, Table S7) differences were found when only new stimuli were analyzed.

For old stimuli, performance differed significantly by gender (GLMM—interaction: OR = 0.33, CI95 = 0.16–0.69, P = 0.003, Table S8). Men showed higher recall for collision stimuli (GLMM—type: OR = 3.05, CI95 = 1.62–5.71, P = 0.001, Table S9), whereas women performed similarly across stimulus types (GLMM—type: OR = 0.93, CI95 = 0.61–1.42, P = 0.744, Table S10, Fig. 3B). These effects were not influenced by experimental conditions (GLMM—setup: OR = 0.79, CI95 = 0.56–1.11, P = 0.171, Table S11).

No effect of trial on success was observed (GLMM—trial: OR = 0.97, CI95 = 0.92–1.03, P = 0.364, Table S12), indicating that neither fatigue nor learning or forgetting occurred during the task. Similarly, no influence of trajectory orientation was detected beyond the collision vs. noncollision distinction (ANOVA from GLMM—P = 0.108, Table S13).

Complex recognition memory test: both men and women remember collision stimuli better than noncollision stimuli

The gender-related effects observed in the simple recognition memory test should be interpreted cautiously. However, they are consistent with previous evidence, suggesting that strategy selection during dynamic processing may depend on task demands and complexity (27). Based on this prior work, we hypothesized that increasing cognitive demands could reduce potential strategy-related differences between participants by promoting reliance on time-compaction–based processing. To explore this possibility, we implemented the complex recognition memory experiment.

In this task, two red spheres moved within a three-dimensional (3D) space, varying along the z-axis to simulate approach or recession. Their motion in the xy-plane followed trajectory vectors at 22.5° intervals from the x-axis, yielding 16 stimuli: eight collision and eight noncollision stimuli (Fig. 4A). The increased complexity of the complex recognition memory task arose primarily from the introduction of subtle trajectory orientations that reduced the perceptual differences between old (memorized) and new (distractor) stimuli. In the simple task, the trajectories of old and new stimuli differed in a visually salient manner, allowing them to be readily distinguished based on their global motion configuration and orientation (Fig. 3A). In contrast, in the complex task, both memorized and novel stimuli were randomly drawn from the same set of trajectories, differing only by small angular deviations (Fig. 4A). Consequently, stimuli presented during encoding and recall shared highly similar visual and dynamic properties and could no longer be reliably discriminated using simple perceptual cues. Successful performance therefore required finer relational processing and increased working memory demands in order to distinguish previously encoded interactions from highly similar distractors. According to the time compaction hypothesis, these conditions should favor reliance on CIR-based encoding, as CIRs may facilitate discrimination between dynamic situations lacking salient distinguishing features.

During encoding, participants viewed four random collision and four random noncollision stimuli randomly selected from the set of 16 stimuli. During recall, all 16 possible stimuli (eight collision and eight noncollision, half old and half new) were presented randomly. A total of 99 people took part in this test.

Viewing the stimuli during the encoding phase significantly improved recall, with old stimuli remembered better than new ones (generalized linear model (GLM)—does not include random effects for participant—interaction: OR = 1.60, CI95 = 1.04–2.46, P = 0.031, Table S14; GLM—phase: OR = 1.85, CI95 = 1.49–2.29, P < 0.001, Table S15). This contrasts with the simple recognition test, confirming the increased difficulty of recognition.

For old stimuli, recall success again did not depend on gender (GLM—gender: OR = 0.85, CI95 = 0.63–1.16, P = 0.320, Table S16) but did depend on stimulus type, as collision stimuli were recalled more accurately than noncollision ones, and the effect was modulated by trial (GLM—interaction: OR = 0.92, CI95 = 0.85–0.99, P = 0.036, Table S17). In the first trial, collision recall exceeded noncollision recall with a 36% higher success probability (Fig. 4B; GLM—type: OR = 2.57, CI95 = 1.40–4.77, P = 0.002, Table S17). Recall for collision stimuli then declined across trials (Fig. 4C; GLM—trial: OR = 0.92, CI95 = 0.86–0.97, P = 0.002, Table S18), consistent with forgetting, whereas noncollision recall remained stable (GLM—trial: OR = 1, CI95 = 0.94–1.05, P = 0.853, Table S19). By the final trials, recall for both stimulus types converged. Trajectory orientation did not significantly affect performance (ANOVA from GLM—P = 0.241, Table S20).

Looming recognition memory test: better recall of complex collision stimuli than of simpler dynamic stimuli

The static representation of dynamic stimuli proposed by time compaction suggests that collision stimuli should be more stable than simpler dynamic stimuli. To test this, we implemented the looming recognition memory test, identical to the complex version but using simpler stimuli with a single moving sphere. In this task, the sphere moved in 3D space, either toward (looming) or away from (receding) the participant (Fig. 5A). Each stimulus matched an equivalent stimulus from the complex test in terms of orientation and motion. Looming and receding stimuli thus paralleled collision and noncollision conditions from the complex test. Apart from the single-sphere simplification, all parameter-trajectory orientations, the absence of visual cues, number of stimuli, and randomization remained unchanged. In addition to exploring the memorization of simpler dynamic stimuli, this design also controlled for the looming effect, as approaching objects naturally attract attention (36). A total of 104 people took part in this test.

The stimuli have the same structure as those in the complex test but show a single red sphere moving either toward (looming) or away (receding) from the observer. As in the complex experiment, participants recalled old stimuli better than new ones (GLM—phase: OR = 1.43, CI95 = 1.16–1.76, P = 0.001, Table S21). Among old stimuli, neither stimulus type (looming vs. receding; GLM—type: OR = 1.01, CI95 = 0.75–1.37, P = 0.938, Table S22) nor gender (GLM—gender: OR = 0.96, CI95 = 0.70–1.31, P = 0.795, Table S22) affected performance (Fig. 5B), indicating that the collision advantage in the complex test is not due to the looming effect.

Complex and looming stimuli differed only in complexity, so better recall for looming–receding stimuli (Fig. 5B) than for collision stimuli (Fig. 4B and C) would be expected due to their simplicity. However, in the first trial—before the forgetting effect shown in Fig. 4C—collision stimuli in the complex test were recalled significantly better than their corresponding receding stimuli (GLM—experiment: OR = 0.21, CI95 = 0.05–0.72, P = 0.018; Table S23, Fig. 5C), with a 23% higher success rate. This indicates that dynamic stimuli containing future interactions are better remembered than simpler dynamic stimuli. Finally, trajectory orientation had no significant effect on recall (ANOVA from GLM—P = 0.781, Table S24).

Discussion

Dynamic situations are intrinsic to animal life and accurately encoding and recalling them is essential for survival. The time compaction hypothesis proposes that dynamic situations may be internally transformed through a representational process termed “time compaction,” whereby future interactions are spatially encoded into simplified time-independent predictive structures, the static predictive maps termed CIRs. From an adaptive perspective, prioritizing anticipated interactions may provide substantial behavioral advantages, as events involving potential collisions or encounters often require rapid responses for successful navigation, avoidance, or interception. Within this framework, time compaction may optimize dynamic cognition by preserving behaviorally relevant predictive information while minimizing representational load, optimizing the processing, learning, and memory of behaviorally relevant situations (16).

Here, we explored the interplay between memory and time compaction, emphasizing that in rapidly changing contexts such as predation or escape, swift and reliable retrieval of critical information is vital for adaptive decision-making.

The simple recognition memory tests revealed that stimuli containing future interactions—those representable through a CIR—were recalled more accurately than those not containing impending interactions. In the complex test, collision stimuli were remembered better not only than noncollision stimuli but also than simpler dynamic (looming) stimuli, despite involving a single moving element. This paradox is consistent with the possibility that collision stimuli are processed through compact static representations of predicted encounters (Fig. 1A), as proposed by the time compaction hypothesis. By potentially embedding temporal information into spatial form, such compaction may simplify encoding, reduce cognitive load, and enhance memory stability for rapid decisions and flexible behavior—key for survival in both humans and animals (37). In contrast, stimuli lacking predicted interactions are not compacted and thus demand greater processing resources. Our results show that this mechanism also supports observational learning, allowing knowledge acquisition without direct exposure to risk (38, 39).

The present findings also raise the important question of whether the observed memory advantage reflects genuinely compacted representational encoding, rather than alternative explanations related to attentional prioritization, perceptual salience, or the enhanced behavioral relevance of collision-like stimuli. Although previous studies have shown that humans and other animals internally represent dynamic situations involving impending collisions as CIRs (27, 28), it remains possible that such interactions preferentially attract attention or are perceived as behaviorally significant, thereby facilitating memory performance independently of representational compression. However, several observations suggest that these factors alone cannot fully account for the present results. First, if the memory advantage observed in the complex recognition task were primarily driven by attentional prioritization or stimulus salience, looming stimuli—known to strongly engage attentional resources (36)—should have produced a comparable memory advantage relative to receding stimuli. This effect was not observed in our design. Importantly, the noncollision stimuli themselves incorporated looming-related depth cues and should, therefore, under a purely attentional account, have been preferentially remembered over collision stimuli, opposite to the pattern reported here. Nevertheless, it should be noted that attentional modulation by looming stimuli exists along a continuum that depends on the specific characteristics of the approaching motion (40, 41).

Furthermore, an attentional bias would likely affect the processing of both old and new stimuli; however, the lack of significant differences between collision and noncollision trials among new stimuli suggests that the advantage is specific to the encoding and consolidation of the representation. The gender-dependent effects observed in the simple task, together with their disappearance under increased discrimination difficulty in the complex protocol, are difficult to reconcile with purely attentional or salience-based accounts and instead suggest the engagement of higher-order representational strategies during dynamic stimulus processing. An additional alternative explanation is that participants may have preferentially remembered the final spatial positions of the moving spheres, since collision stimuli end with the spheres closer together than noncollision stimuli. However, if memory performance were primarily driven by this factor, the looming-test stimuli—which involve only a single moving sphere and therefore constitute the simplest dynamic configuration—should have shown superior recall performance than the two-sphere stimuli, contrary to the observed results. Importantly, collision stimuli were remembered better than their equivalent looming-test stimuli despite being objectively more perceptually complex. This paradoxical result supports a representational interpretation of the findings, consistent with the hypothesis that anticipated interactions are encoded through simplified static representations of future events.

Although the present findings are consistent with the representational framework proposed by the time compaction hypothesis, the behavioral nature of the current experiments does not allow direct observation of the underlying representational format. Future studies combining behavioral paradigms with neurophysiological or neuroimaging approaches will be necessary to directly characterize the neural implementation of CIRs.

Differences in memory for compacted and noncompacted stimuli likely arise at the engram level. Engrams—neural assemblies with strengthened synapses—represent the physical substrate of memory (42–46). Cognitive maps are thought to rely on these engrams (47, 48), with place cells serving as their spatial correlates (30, 49–51). Engram allocation depends on neuronal excitability (52) and is continuously reshaped through learning and forgetting (53). Under the time compaction framework, stimuli with future interactions may be spatially encoded, recruiting smaller, more specific neural ensembles than noncompacted stimuli, which demand representation of multiple spatiotemporal variables. Such compact engrams would experience less interference, promoting greater memory stability consistent with our results.

This enhanced efficiency may also affect memory consolidation. Long-term memories arise through multiple mechanisms (54), including integration of similar experiences (55) and replay (56, 57). Time compaction could thus facilitate consolidation by prioritizing dynamic situations involving interactions, improving adaptability in changing environments (37).

Time compaction embeds the “when” into the “where” (18), consistent with the close relationship between spatial and temporal encoding in memory. Hippocampal time cells provide temporal frameworks for episodic events (58), while replay supports both recall and navigation (59). Though debated whether memory emerges from spatial cognition (60) or vice versa (61), both processes converge in the hippocampal formation (47, 62, 63) alongside medial prefrontal regions housing engrams (49). Evidence suggests the hippocampus may underpin time compaction (28): studies in bats suggest the implication of place cells (29), and modeling shows this process arises from network dynamics consistent with hippocampal circuitry (64). Object-vector cells in the medial entorhinal cortex, which encode distances and orientations to objects, may also contribute (65). Based on this, it has been proposed that the predictive representation proposed by time compaction—the CIR—extends the classical cognitive map, traditionally limited to static environments, to time-dependent contexts (66), supporting adaptive behavior in dynamic environments.

In this sense, time compaction converges with the ecological psychology framework proposed by Gibson (67), according to which perception, cognition, and action are tightly coupled through affordances, namely, opportunities for action provided by the environment. Within the time compaction framework, a situation involving converging trajectories may not be processed merely as two objects approaching each other, but as a behaviorally meaningful interaction affording responses such as interception, avoidance, or escape (18, 27, 37). From this perspective, the enhanced memorization of collision stimuli is also consistent with the ecological view that events carrying actionable consequences are prioritized (67, 68), particularly in survival-relevant contexts requiring rapid behavioral responses. However, unlike ecological psychology, which traditionally neglects the role of internal representations (67, 69, 70), time compaction places them at the center of cognition (16). Therefore, time compaction bridges ecological psychology with representational theories of cognition, offering a mechanistic account of how actionable environmental information is internalized, compressed, and stored, thus providing a formal representational grounding that ecological approaches have historically lacked.

Time compaction also establishes conceptual links with predictive coding and event segmentation theory. Event segmentation theory proposes that continuous experience is organized into discrete cognitive units whenever internal situation models are updated (23–26). Within the present framework, CIRs may operate as such units by compressing the temporal unfolding of an interaction into a single static predictive structure. In this way, anticipated interactions become represented as compact event-centered blocks emphasizing predicted consequences rather than continuous spatiotemporal details.

These compact event structures are also compatible with predictive coding frameworks (19–22), according to which cognition relies on the continuous generation and updating of predictions. Under time compaction, predictive processing may operate over CIR-defined units: incoming dynamic information is compared against previously memorized CIRs (16), and prediction errors emerge when anticipated interactions fail to match ongoing sensory evidence. Importantly, predictive refinement can still occur within each CIR-defined event structure as the interaction unfolds. Such hierarchical organization may provide substantial processing advantages by reducing representational demands while preserving behaviorally relevant predictive information.

An important aspect of our findings is the gender differences observed in working memory. In the simple test, men recalled collision stimuli better, while no differences appeared for noncollision. This aligns with reports that men tend to rely on time compaction, whereas women integrate multiple strategies (27). However, with increasing complexity, this sex bias disappears, supporting its role in optimizing processing and memory of dynamic events. Crucially, the gender differences were also stimulus specific: men outperformed women only for collision stimuli, while no gender differences emerged for noncollision stimuli in the same task. This asymmetry argues against a general male advantage in visuospatial working memory as an explanation and instead points to a selective effect tied to the use of time compaction. Gender differences are frequently reported in visuospatial memory tasks, often with men outperforming women (71–75), although the opposite pattern has also been observed in specific paradigms such as object location memory tasks (76, 77). Importantly, individuals may rely on different cognitive strategies during dynamic visuospatial processing (27, 76, 78, 79), with strategy selection adapting to task demands and complexity (80–83). Previous studies further suggest that gender-related performance differences are strongly influenced by the strategies adopted to solve the task (84) and that such differences tend to emerge at intermediate difficulty levels while disappearing in low or high difficulties (85–88). Within this framework, our results suggest that the use of time compaction may depend on the cognitive demand of the task, indicating that increasing task complexity promotes reliance on time compaction in both women and men.

In consequence, the present findings do not suggest that time compaction intrinsically favors one gender over the other. In ecological contexts, adaptive behavior depends on successfully solving cognitively demanding dynamic tasks (27, 89)—such as hunting, escaping, or fighting—in which the gender differences should disappear, as observed in our setup. This interpretation is consistent with recent evidence indicating that gender differences are not associated with general cognitive ability or intelligence but rather emerge under specific task conditions and strategic demands (90). The origins of these strategic preferences remain unclear, and caution is warranted when broadly interpreting gender-related differences in cognitive performance. Hormonal influences show inconsistent evidence (91, 92), while sociocultural and experiential factors may also contribute. Potential modulators include familiarity with computer-based tasks (93), visuospatial abilities (94), or stereotype-related effects on performance (78). Moreover, gender likely represents only one of several factors influencing cognitive strategy selection and task performance (95). Although the present results do not allow us to identify additional sources of heterogeneity, substantial within-group variability was observed in both men and women. Individual differences in working memory capacity (96), prior experience (97, 98), or cognitive style (99) may therefore account for an important proportion of the observed variance. More generally, cognitive strategies are highly flexible and context dependent at the individual level, and binary gender comparisons may obscure this variability (100). Accordingly, the group-level effects reported here should not be interpreted as reflecting fixed or universal cognitive profiles but rather as context-dependent differences emerging under specific task conditions.

In summary, this work shows that memory for dynamic stimuli involving imminent interactions is more stable—and better recalled—than noninteractive or simpler dynamic stimuli. These results are consistent with the time compaction hypothesis, highlighting its relevance for understanding how humans learn and act in time-changing environments. Once learned, CIRs can be efficiently retrieved to guide real-time behavior (16). Moreover, evidence from bats (29) and rats (28) suggests that these predictive encoding and memory mechanisms extend across species. Time compaction may thus represent a fundamental cognitive process supporting adaptive behavior and survival in dynamic worlds.

Materials and methods

Experiments

The experimental design was implemented in a computer using R software (101) and its extension Shiny (102), through the RStudio environment (103), which allowed us to perform the tests online to reach a great number of participants. To validate this data collection and rule out any bias coming from this, we also performed the simple recognition memory test in person using the software MATLAB (104). Both implementations followed the same specifications.

In both encoding and recalling phases, each stimulus consisted of either two moving spheres (simple and complex recognition memory tests) or one moving sphere (looming recognition memory test). The presentation of each stimulus consisted of two consecutive repetitions of the sphere movements, with a duration of 1 s per repetition. During the first 0.7 s, the two moving red spheres were displayed, disappearing for the remaining 0.3 s. The presentation of each stimulus was followed by a 3-s fixation interval consisting of a gray screen with a white Greek cross displayed at the center. This latter screen served as a separator between stimuli and forced the participant to center the view in the middle point of the screen, so all stimuli were visualized starting from the same point.

Shadows and shines were added to the spheres to give them realism and ensure that participants would perceive future interactions—operationally defined here as predicted trajectory convergence leading to an impending collision—rather than simple geometric crossings between circles. In the simple recognition memory test, to prevent biases when recalling collision or noncollision stimuli, we randomly showed each participant one of two pseudorandom sequences of old stimuli that alternated between collision and noncollision stimuli (see Supporting Information). In the complex and looming recognition memory tests, the order of appearance of the stimuli was completely randomized.

In the simple and complex recognition memory tests, collision stimuli consisted of converging trajectories predicting an impending interaction, whereas noncollision stimuli followed diverging trajectories that prevented future interaction between the spheres. In the looming recognition memory test, stimuli consisted of a single sphere moving either toward the observer (looming stimuli) or away from the observer (receding stimuli). These stimuli were designed to reproduce the depth-motion component present in the complex recognition memory test, with looming and receding stimuli corresponding to the noncollision and collision conditions, respectively in that task.

In the simple recognition memory test, sphere trajectories were constrained such that the vectorial sum of the trajectories of the two spheres aligned either with the bisector of an xy-plane quadrant (old stimuli) or with the x- or y-axis (new stimuli). In the complex and looming recognition memory tests, sphere trajectories were constrained to predefined orientations in the xy plane. Specifically, the vectorial sum of the sphere trajectories in the complex recognition memory test, and the trajectory of the sphere in the looming recognition memory test, resulted in vectors oriented 22.5°, 67.5°, 112.5°, 157.5°, 202.5°, 247.5°, 292.5°, and 337.5° relative to the x-axis.

The answer buttons from the recalling phase appeared at the center of the screen next to each other and their left or right position was randomized between participants. Once the recalling phase was completed, participants were requested to introduce their gender and age, which were the only personal information collected.

At the beginning of the experiment, participants received written and oral instructions describing the memory task while avoiding any information that could bias performance (e.g. no mention of potential collisions). They were informed that the experiment consisted of an encoding phase, during which dynamic stimuli had to be memorized, followed by a recalling phase in which previously presented (“old”) and novel (“new”) stimuli were displayed and participants had to identify whether each stimulus had been shown during encoding. Any participant questions were answered exclusively by referring to these instructions.

Further details regarding the experimental design and implementation are provided in the Supporting Information.

Participants

Participants were enrolled according to two inclusion criteria: normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no prior exposure to studies conducted by our group. Recruitment was carried out on the university campus, and consequently most participants were college students. No participants were excluded from the analyzed sample. The simple recognition memory test was performed by 237 participants who completed 2,370 trials (1,180 observations/trials from 66 women and 52 men, for the R software setup; and 1,190 observations/trials from 70 women and 49 men, for the MATLAB software setups). For the complex recognition memory test, 99 volunteers participated in the experiment, completing 1,386 trials (51 women and 48 men). Finally, the looming recognition memory test was performed by 104 participants who completed 1,456 trials (67 women and 37 men). Participant age ranged from 18 years old to 41 years old, with similar distributions for both genders and for each test and setup (Tables S1–S4).

All participants for each test were different (no person participated in more than one test) and provided informed consent to anonymously take part in the experiments. The full study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Committee of Bioethics, National Distance Education University). All experiments were performed following the guidelines and regulations set forth by the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed simple, complex, and looming recognition memory tests separately. First two trials of each test were intended to serve as an adaptation to the setup and task and were not included in the analysis. We performed analysis of success (answering correctly during the recalling phase) by fitting individual probabilities of successful answers to random intercept mixed models with logit link (i.e. logistic regression; GLMMs), using the individual (i.e. participant) as the random intercept. When the variance related to the upper level was not large enough to justify the use of mixed models, we used GLMs instead. The initial set of variables analyzed were gender, stimulus type (i.e. collision or noncollision stimulus for the simple and complex recognition memory tests; looming and receding stimulus for the looming recognition memory test) and whether it was an old or new stimulus. From a model with these variables and their interactions, we followed a backwards stepwise elimination procedure to select the set of variables with explanatory power following P-value criterion. When interactions between variables were found, we disentangled them by analyzing and interpreting the interaction terms separately. Model fit was performed via maximum likelihood. We used Bobyqa controller (105) when needed, to avoid convergence problems.

We also analyzed the influence of trial (i.e. ordinal position in which the stimulus is shown), checking for significant interactions of trial and stimulus with gender, stimulus type and whether the stimulus is an old or a new stimulus. In order to separately estimate the effect of these factors on each trial, we used pairwise comparisons to the selected model. The effect of stimulus (i.e. each individual stimulus, with its particular orientation of sphere trajectories) on success was also assessed through the same procedure used for the effect of trial, to check that there was no bias coming from this factor.

For every model, we checked residuals and distributions’ assumptions. For all mixed models used, intraclass correlation indexes were large enough to justify the use of multilevel regression (see Supporting Information). All statistical analyses were performed by means of R software (101) under RStudio IDE (103) using the libraries lme4 (106) (computation of mixed models) and emmeans (107) (pairwise comparisons of the selected model—only used for graphic representational purposes, not for signification tests). All variables analyzed were either binary or quantitative, except for the case of stimulus, but as models including this variable resulted nonsignificative, we did not perform any multiple comparisons corrections.
