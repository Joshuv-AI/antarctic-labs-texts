Deep Learning Echocardiographic Trajectories of Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction
Authors: Erick Romero, Hitalo Oliveira da Silva, Padmini Sirish, Javier E. Lopez, Nipavan Chiamvimonvat, David A. Liem, Martin Cadeiras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a dynamic chronic disease. Specific disease trajectories remain unclear.ObjectivesThis study aims to investigate cardiac structure and function trajectories measured by echocardiography and their relationship with clinical outcomes.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort from a single center of HFpEF patients with follow-up echocardiogram at ≥ 1 year. Seven longitudinal echocardiographic variables of cardiac geometry and function were utilized to identify trajectory. Longitudinal phenomapping analysis was conducted with machine and deep learning longitudinal clustering. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, while the secondary outcome consisted of changes in B-type natriuretic peptide levels overtime. A panel of longitudinal laboratory biomarkers was used for exploratory outcomes.ResultsIn total, 1,626 HFpEF cases were included; training and validation sets were randomly derived. Echocardiographic trajectories were identified: Echo-Trajectory #1, a stable remodeling trajectory; Echo-Trajectory #2, an increased remodeling course; and Echo-Trajectory #3, decreased remodeling trajectory with right ventricular dysfunction. Baseline clinical characteristics varied significantly among Echo-Trajectories by sex, age, blood pressure, obesity, comorbidities, and left ventricular mass index (P < 0.05). Compared to Echo-Trajectories #2 and #3, Trajectory #1 had a better all-cause mortality outcome (P < 0.001). Similarly, Echo-Trajectory #1 presented favorable trajectories of natriuretic peptides, sodium, and renal function (P < 0.05).ConclusionsLongitudinal phenomapping resulted in echocardiographic trajectories of cardiac structure and function with different clinical, laboratory, and survival characteristics. HFpEF is a dynamic condition, and longitudinal phenomapping may improve trajectory characterization and guide treatment strategies.

Heart failure (HF) with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) causes disability and death in millions of individuals.1 Studies have underscored the heterogeneous character of the HFpEF syndrome and revealed the existence of various phenotypes.2,3 However, HFpEF is also a heterogeneous longitudinal syndrome. As HFpEF progresses, dynamic changes occur in cardiac structure and function, reflecting the complex, heterogeneous pathophysiology of the disease.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Previous research on longitudinal geometry and function has primarily focused on left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) or left ventricular structures using predefined categories. Therefore, there is a need for more comprehensive and longitudinal phenotyping that is not dependent on predefined subtypes. Echocardiograms, which provide a global assessment of cardiac structure and function, are routinely used in clinical practice. Using unsupervised phenotyping strategies along with longitudinal echocardiographic data presents a unique opportunity for a comprehensive longitudinal characterization of HFpEF.11,12 Mechanistically, cardiac structure and function trajectories in HFpEF are affected by adverse remodeling during disease progression.9,10 Identifying these trajectories can help pinpoint patients at high risk of disease progression. We hypothesize that using longitudinal phenomapping methodologies with longitudinal echocardiography will enable the identification of distinct trajectory patterns and related outcomes. The objective of this study is to investigate the cardiac structure and function trajectories measured by echocardiography and their relationship with clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and cohort data source

In this single-center retrospective cohort study, data were collected from the University of California-Davis Medical Center. The university’s Institutional Review Board approved the study, and all data used were deidentified before analysis.

Data were collected through the institution’s electronic health record data warehouse between January 2014 and December 2022. The study population consisted of adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with HFpEF. We followed a previously validated identification criteria, including inpatient or outpatient International Classification of Diseases (ICD) HF diagnoses (ICD-9 or ICD-10), at least 1 echocardiogram, all recorded LVEF ≥50%, and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) ≥100 pg/mL or N-terminal-pro BNP ≥225 pg/mL or loop diuretic prescription (sensitivity 88%, specificity 96%, positive predictive value 96%, and negative predictive value 87%).13,14 To avoid misclassification and longitudinal inconsistency, authors further include exclusion criteria. Incomplete cases were defined as less than 2 time points with available left ventricular internal dimension at end-diastole (LVIDd) and interventricular septum thickness at end-diastole (IVSd), ICD codes for cardiac or valve transplant, left ventricular assist device implantation, those with only 1 LVEF value in the electronic health record, those with any LVEF <50%, those using endothelin receptor antagonists, and those with less than a 1-year interval between their first and second echocardiogram.

Baseline definitions

Baseline was defined as the index date of the first LVEF ≥50% or first BNP ≥100 pg/mL. The demographic information extracted included self-reported sex, race, and ethnicity. Baseline laboratory, echocardiogram, and electrocardiogram characteristics were defined as the first value within 90 days of the index date or the first available value. Comorbidities were defined as diagnoses prior to the index date, and a validated comorbidity identification algorithm was employed as proposed by the Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse.15, 16, 17 Medical therapy utilization was defined as the prescription of medications recorded in the electronic medical record prior to the index date and within 6 months afterward.

Longitudinal data

Longitudinal echocardiogram and clinical laboratory biomarker variables were extracted, starting from baseline until the last available data point recorded on follow-up. Compared to echocardiogram full reports, the tubular data from electronic health record do not have the full variables. Raw longitudinal data were preprocessed into equally spaced time series. Multivariable longitudinal echocardiographic characterization in HFpEF remains sparse, likely due to follow-up intensity efforts (∼42% at multiple years is rarely reported).4,6,18 To address this barrier, the echocardiographic variable selection rationale was longitudinal completeness and cross-temporal consistency to accurately represent the cohort. Complete cases were defined as LVIDd and IVSd in at least 2 time points. The first 4 years displayed a higher frequency of nonmissing data, 7 echocardiographic had the fewest missing values and were clinically relevant (Supplemental Table 2A). Variables with < 15% missing at baseline and <50% at follow-up were excluded (ie, systolic and atrial variables). Thus, we selected 7 echocardiographic variables that represented cardiac geometry and function based on clinical relevance and had the fewest missing values. These were IVSd, LVIDd, left ventricular posterior wall (PW), pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP), tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), left ventricular mass (LVM) index, and LVEF. The LVM was calculated as follows: LVM = 0.8 × 1.04 × ([IVSd + left ventricular end-diastolic dimension + PW3 − LVIDd3) + 0.6 g.19 The LVM was indexed to height2.7.20 Thus, 7 echocardiographic parameters (IVSd, LVIDd, PW, LVEF, LVM Index, PASP, and TAPSE) were used to explore cardiac geometry and function trajectories.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome was BNP levels overtime. Creatinine, eGFR, Na+, K+, and QTc intervals were considered for exploratory outcomes (biomarker panel). All-cause mortality data were obtained from the clinical data warehouse, the institution did not collect specific cause mortality.

Statistical analysis and data science approach

The longitudinal phenomapping analysis utilized unsupervised machine and deep learning algorithms for trajectory clustering of longitudinal echocardiographic data (IVSd, LVIDd, PW, LVEF, LVM index, PASP, and TAPSE), potentially revealing diverse trajectories using the following steps. Refer to supplementary material Trajectory clustering pipeline for full details and Supplemental Figure 1A for a visual representation.

Step 1 data partitioning

The data set was randomly split into training and validation sets (2:1).

Step 2 preprocessing

To ensure robust longitudinal analysis, we restricted the primary modeling to complete cases with at least 2 time points of LVIDd and IVSd measurements and applied a power transformation to normalize variable scales.21

Step 3 longitudinal imputation

Change overtime trajectories require complete baseline and follow-up data. Dense multivariable longitudinal echocardiographic data beyond LVEF in HFpEF are rare. Thus, missing longitudinal echocardiographic values were expected due to the data source (tubular data from electronic health records vs full reports) and 4 years of follow-up. We imputed using a self-attention–based deep learning model (self-attention-based imputation for time series [SAITS]) that accounts for temporal dependencies.22

Step 4 encoding

Patient-level longitudinal trajectories were encoded into low-dimensional representations using a deep learning time-series encoder (Clustering Representation Learning on Incomplete time-series data [CRLI]), resulting in embeddings to correct biases from imputation that might skew clustering results.23

Step 5 dimensionality reduction and clustering

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection was used for further embedding dimensionality reduction. Then, we applied unsupervised clustering models to group them based on trajectories similarity.24 The optimal number of clusters was determined using a combination of assessment indices.

Step 6 internal validation and robustness

A traditional complete-case sensitivity analysis was infeasible since only 67 patients (6.6%) had complete data across all 4 years (Supplemental Tables 1A to 1C). Nevertheless, we performed a multistep sensitivity and internal validation strategy to assess robustness. The trajectory clustering pipeline was applied independently within each training and validation data set. Then, clinical association analyses were conducted independently in the training and validation data sets using only observed data (nonimputed), including baseline characteristics, longitudinal echocardiography, longitudinal biomarkers (secondary and exploratory outcomes), and the all-cause mortality primary outcome.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Between-group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables after testing for normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Chi-squared was used for categorical variables. Associations between baseline characteristics and trajectories were explored using logistic regression with stepwise selection and backward elimination. The echocardiographic trajectories identified were visualized using piecewise B-spline curves, a smoothing spline that constructs a curve through piecewise segments.

Outcome analysis

The primary outcome was evaluated in a time-to-event fashion using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. Longitudinal continuous outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed models that included fixed effects for time, trajectory group, and their interaction, with random intercepts for patients. Assumptions of linearity, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity were assessed.

Analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS institute) and the programming language Python version 3.12 (Python Software Foundation) in conjunction with the libraries’ pandas (version 2.2.2), NumPy (version 1.26.0), scikit-learn (version 1.4), SciPy (version 1.12.0), and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (version 0.5.6).

Results

Cohort identification and echo-trajectories

Over 9,000 cases were retrieved from our warehouse database. After applying exclusion criteria, 3,504 cases were identified. Of these, 1,626 had at least 2 time points with complete variables for analysis. Supplemental Table 2 provides a comparison between included and excluded cases. Overall, the study cohort is balanced with modest increased proportion of females and comorbidities. The data set (n = 1,626) was randomly split into training (n = 1,089, ∼67%) and validation (n = 537, ∼33%) sets. Baseline characteristics and associations to echo-trajectories

Overall, the cohort had a median age of 69 years (IQR: 59-78). White was the predominant race, with 842 patients (51.1%). The cohort exhibited multiple comorbidities burden, particularly hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity, and diabetes (Table 1). Between-group trajectory comparisons revealed that Echo-Trajectories #1 and #2 were younger compared to Echo-Trajectory #3. Echo-trajectory #2 had a higher proportion of males and characterized by higher blood pressure, body mass index, and greater comorbidity burden of hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and kidney disease. In contrast, Trajectory #1 had a higher proportion of hyperlipidemia, while Echo-Trajectory #3 had a higher proportion of atrial fibrillation. Biomarker and echocardiogram profiles also differ. Echo-Trajectory #1 had the lowest levels of BNP, K+, creatinine, and QTc, whereas Trajectory #2 presented the highest LVM index. Trajectory #3 was characterized by worse PASP and TAPSE. Discussion

Our investigation exploring longitudinal phenomapping of HFpEF revealed 3 echocardiogram-derived trajectories associated with different clinical characteristics, biomarker, and mortality outcomes (Central Illustration). This longitudinal perspective contributes to understanding the intricacies of the HFpEF syndrome. Prior studies have characterized HFpEF heterogeneity using single point in time phenotyping (cross-sectional).2,3,25 Longitudinally, characterization is typically limited to a single parameter based on LVEF.4,5,18 Yamanaka et al9 explored longitudinal changes using more than 1 parameter, LVM index, and LVIDd; however, the limited follow-up to 1 year and predefined groups may restrict characterization of the natural evolution of HF. In contrast, our study extends prior work by jointly using an unsupervised phenotyping strategy with multiple time-dependent echocardiographic parameters, longitudinal phenomapping. In addition, we propose a deep learning–based phenomapping pipeline to address sparse and limited follow-up real-world echocardiography data, providing a more practical framework for phenotyping in routine clinical settings.

Echo-trajectories and clinical significance

The identification of 3 trajectories within the HFpEF cohort underscores the heterogeneous nature of this syndrome. Each trajectory exhibited a unique echocardiographic pattern. Independent of phenomapping trajectory group assignment, clinical characteristics, longitudinal biomarkers, and mortality outcomes also differed. Likely, reflecting underlying biological differences in disease severity and progression. However, between-group trajectories differences predominated, with relatively modest temporal change (time x group vs group effects, Figure 3 and Supplemental Table 4). This suggests that baseline trajectories largely define temporal remodeling patterns. Findings were consistent in the validation cohort.

Echo-Trajectory #1 intermediate remodeling pattern was associated with lower mortality rates compared to the other trajectories. This trajectory was associated with predominantly female, atrial fibrillation prone phenotype with lower cardiometabolic and pulmonary burden and less LV remodeling (Figure 4). By echocardiography, Echo-Trajectory #1 demonstrated an intermediate remodeling pattern, suggesting a more stable cardiac geometry and function overtime, when compared with trajectories #2 and #3 (Figure 3). Biochemically, this trajectory presents longitudinal low BNP and creatinine, high eGFR and Na+, possibly reflecting less severe disease (Figure 6).

Echo-Trajectory #2 increased remodeling pattern was associated with higher mortality rates compared to Trajectory #1 and similar to #3. This trajectory included a male predominant hypertensive remodeling profile with increased LVM index compared to Trajectories #1 and #3 (Figure 4). Based on echocardiography, Echo-Trajectory #2 presented an increased remodeling pattern over time suggesting an initial and sustained worse remodeling vs #1 and #3 trajectories (Figure 3). Based on longitudinal biomarker trends, this trajectory was characterized by high BNP, creatinine, along with low eGFR and Na+, potentially reflecting greater disease severity (Figure 6).

Echo-Trajectory #3 pulmonary hypertension and right ventricle dysfunction pattern (reflected by PASP and TAPSE trajectories) was related to higher mortality rates compared to #1 and similar to #2. This trajectory was characterized by an obesity-associated phenotype with lower LV hypertrophy and atrial fibrillation prevalence compared to Trajectories #1 and #2 (Figure 4). Biochemically, this trajectory presented high BNP, creatinine, along with low eGFR and Na+. This biochemical pattern shares similarities with trajectory #2 but with less overall disease severity (Figure 6). Together, these trajectories suggest sex- and comorbidity-associated pathways of cardiac remodeling.

The prevalence of ventricular geometric patterns has demonstrated the heterogeneity in ventricular remodeling found in HFpEF.26,27 However, longitudinal changes in geometry and ejection fraction also occur. For instance, studies across the literature highlight various subtypes of LVEF trajectories across HFpEF, where patients can experience an increase, stability, or a decline in LVEF. These longitudinal changes have been associated with distinct outcomes.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Furthermore, Yamanaka et al9 identified different HFpEF subtypes based on predefined left ventricular structure and remodeling changes at 1-year follow-up. Temporal structural changes can occur bidirectionally in terms of the progression or regression of LV hypertrophy enlargement, and these changes were related to cardiovascular death or HF admissions. Consistent with Yamanaka’s findings, our study also demonstrated temporal changes associated with clinical outcomes. Echo-Trajectory #2 and #3 demonstrated worse remodeling and right ventricle dysfunction associated with higher mortality rates compared with Echo-Trajectory #1 (Figures 3 and 5).

Early recognition of patients belonging to these trajectories could be critical for implementing aggressive management strategies aimed at mitigating disease progression and reducing mortality risk. For example, early echocardiographic changes could be instructive for interventions targeting underlying mechanisms of disease progression, such as blood pressure control, weight management, and optimization of cardiac function.

Study Limitations and future directions

Limitations in our study warrant consideration. The retrospective design of our analysis limited causal inferences, so our findings should be interpreted as associations. Despite applying systematically exclusion and inclusion criteria, risk of HFpEF misclassification exists in retrospective studies. Echocardiography is an operator-dependent study, while performed as per single institution protocol, variability may exist. Cohort identification required follow-up and complete echocardiographic data. This may introduce selection bias, as we may be indirectly selecting patients with better insurance, compliance, and less severe HF. Consequently, patients with more severe HF or limited or no insurance may not be well represented because of the limited follow-up. This follow-up limitation can only be addressed in a prospective design with active follow-up. Additionally, our findings may not be generalizable to other populations, as the study was based on a single center. Missing data, such as diastolic parameters, may limit the ability of the derived trajectories to capture the full HFpEF spectrum. Future studies should aim to validate our findings in larger, multicenter cohorts with longitudinal echocardiograms and to explore the utility of incorporating additional imaging modalities and biomarkers to further refine trajectory stratification.

Conclusions

We identified echocardiographic trajectories associated with different clinical characteristics and outcomes in an HFpEF using longitudinal phenomapping and time-dependent echocardiography. Further validation of our findings in large multicenter cohorts could facilitate trajectory characterization to guide treatment strategies.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In this study, echocardiography longitudinal data combined with deep learning for longitudinal phenomapping identified cardiac structure and function trajectories of HFpEF.
