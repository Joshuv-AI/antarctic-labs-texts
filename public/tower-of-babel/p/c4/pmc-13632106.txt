Circulating metabolites in plasma reveal potential target of lung cancer prevention: insights from fatty acids pathway
Authors: Yunyun Liu, Qinghan Liu, Tong Sun, Honghao Yang, Zheng Ma, Liuxin Li, Chao Ji, Huanhuan Chen, Yang Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProspective evidence regarding the associations between circulating fatty acids (FAs) and lung cancer risk-particularly regarding their interplay with exposome factors and genetic susceptibility-remains limited. Furthermore, the predictive utility of FAs as biomarkers for lung cancer has not been fully characterized. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the associations of circulating FAs metabolism with lung cancer risk, focusing on their interactions with smoking and genetic risk profiles, while further assessing their potential as predictive biomarkers.MethodsA total of 107,954 participants from the UK Biobank study were included in analyses. The associations of circulating FAs, as well as their interactions with polygenic risk score (PRS), smoking status, and FA-related genetic variants, with lung cancer were examined. The differential expression of genes and hub gene involved in FAs metabolism in lung cancer were explored using RNA-sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas. 10-year lung cancer risk prediction models were developed and evaluated incorporating FAs, PRS, and traditional risk factors.ResultsA total of 965 lung cancer cases occurred during a mean follow-up of 12.12 years. Circulating saturated FAs (SFAs), monounsaturated FAs (MUFAs) and n-6/n-3 PUFAs ratios were positively associated with lung cancer, whereas polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs)/n-3 PUFAs were negatively associated with the outcome (all P for trend < 0.05). Moreover, specific FAs presented significant interaction effects with smoking history, lung cancer-PRS, and FAs-associated alleles on the risk of lung cancer. RNA-sequencing of tumor and normal tissue indicated differential gene expression on FAs metabolism (FDR < 0.05). Models incorporating FAs and traditional risk factors significantly improved lung cancer risk prediction, achieving an area under the curve of 0.788.ConclusionOur study identifies significant associations between circulating FAs and lung cancer, characterized by interplays with smoking and genetic risk profiles. These metabolic markers provide incremental predictive value, offering new insights into risk stratification and individualized lung cancer prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-026-01355-1.

Background

As the most common malignancy and leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, lung cancer brings huge health damage globally with 2.5 million new cases (12.4% of all cancers) and an estimated 1.8 million deaths (18.7%) in 2022 [1]. This increasing burden drives the need to identify individuals at high risk for lung cancer to enhance prevention efforts. As is well known, lung cancer can be driven by the long-term interactions of genetic predisposition and behavioral factors like smoking [2]. Reprogramming of fatty acids (FAs) also has profound effects on tumorigenesis and progression [3]. Based on the number and position of carbon–carbon double bonds, FAs are broadly classified into saturated fatty acids (SFA), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA, including n-3 and n-6 series). While several population studies have explored the roles of dietary or circulating FAs-specifically the protective effects of PUFAs and the risk-elevating potential of SFAs and MUFAs-in lung cancer etiology [4, 5], several critical gaps remain. The interplay between FAs and genetic polymorphisms, as well as the potential in lung cancer risk prediction, remain to be fully elucidated. Genetic polymorphisms in key FAs metabolic enzymes-notably fatty acid desaturases (FADS1/2), stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD), and elongases (ELOVL)-modulate systemic and tissue-specific FA availability, thereby influencing metabolic homeostasis and disease susceptibility [6–8]. It remains unknown whether the effects of specific FAs on lung cancer could be modified by genetic variability in FA metabolism or by genetic susceptibility to lung cancer itself. Second, the biological relevance of these systemic associations to transcriptomic dysregulation within lung tumor tissues remains largely unexplored. Finally, the value of integrating FA-based biomarkers with traditional factors for long-term risk stratification has not been fully evaluated [6, 9].

Thus, we hypothesize that associations exist between circulating FAs and lung cancer development, potentially modified by genetic susceptibility, variants in FA metabolism enzymes, and smoking behaviors. We further explore the clinical utility of FAs as risk prediction biomarkers, in combination with these factors, for lung cancer in the UK Biobank.

Methods

Study participants

The UK Biobank is an ongoing cohort recruiting over 500,000 participants from 22 centers across the United Kingdom from 2006 to 2010. All participants signed up consent for their involvement in the study at baseline, and the research has received ethical approval from the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data collection at the initial assessment visit included a self-administrated questionnaire, physical measurements, and blood samples for genotype and biomarker analyses. The current study included 117,932 participants with circulating FAs measured at baseline using nuclear magnetic resonance. We excluded individuals with a diagnosis of any cancer at baseline (n = 9,649) and those with a follow-up duration of less than one year (n = 329). The final analytical cohort comprised 107,954 participants for the primary analysis of associations between circulating FAs and lung cancer risk. For subsequent interaction analyses and prediction model construction, participants with missing genetic data for the variants of interest were further excluded (n = 717). Details of the study design and methodology are shown in Figure S1.

FAs measurement

The quantification of 249 metabolic biomarkers, including 168 absolute measures and 81 ratios, was performed between June 2019 and April 2020 using a high-throughput NMR-based profiling platform (Nightingale Health Ltd.) for each EDTA plasma sample [10]. In the present study, total FAs, SFAs, MUFAs, PUFAs, n-3 PUFAs (including docosahexaenoic acid, DHA), and n-6 PUFAs (including linolenic acid, LA) were measured; all have demonstrated high repeatability over time [11]. The primary exposures in these analyses were these plasma FAs, expressed as percentages of total plasma FAs (%TFA).

Lung cancer cases ascertainment

Cancer cases at baseline were identified through national cancer registries and self-reported medical history. Incident lung cancer outcomes were ascertained using ICD-10 code C34. Major histological subtypes-including adenocarcinoma (LUAD), squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and small cell lung cancer (SCLC)-were further classified using ICD-O-3 morphology codes [12]. Death records were retrieved from the death registry, with the date and underlying cause of death classified using ICD-10 codes. Participants were followed from the time of initial assessment (between 2008 and 2010) to the date of lung cancer onset, loss to follow-up, or censorship (30 September 2021 in England; 28 February 2018 in Wales; 31 July 2021 in Scotland), whichever occurred first.

Genetic data

The genotyping procedures, imputation, and data cleaning in UK Biobank have been previously detailed elsewhere [13]. The weighted polygenic risk score (PRS) of lung cancer was generated using 18 SNPs (Table S1) from a large-scale GWAS meta-analysis performed by the International Lung Cancer Consortium and the Transdisciplinary Research in Cancer of the Lung. The participants were predominantly of European ancestry, involving over 29,000 cases and 56,000 controls. Detailed information regarding the study populations, recruitment protocols, and genotyping procedures can be found in the original report [14].

For FAs, genetic association data were sourced from three GWAS studies. Data for n-3 PUFAs were obtained from Lemaitre et al. (n = 8866) [8], data for MUFA and SFA were sourced from Wu et al. (n = 8961) [15]. Additional genetic influences on n-6 PUFAs were obtained from the blood metabolite atlas by Guan et al. (n = 8631) [16]. All fatty acid datasets were restricted to individuals of European ancestry. See details in Table S2.

Pathway gene list (Table S3) and RNA-Sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/; accessed May 2025) were used to explore lung cancer associated FAs metabolism genes. The data generation and project standards were based on the foundational framework described by The Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network [17]. Details can be found in Supplementary materials.

Covariates

Sociodemographic data included age (years), sex (man or woman), education levels (college/university degree or higher [yes or no]), ethnicity (Caucasian or non-Caucasian), and Townsend deprivation index. Lifestyle factors included smoking and drinking status (never, former, current, or unknown), and a healthy diet score based on a previously validated dietary index [18]. Physical activity levels were assessed by the metabolic equivalent task (MET) minutes based on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire, categorized into regular exercise (≥ 500 Mets minutes/day), non-regular exercise (< 500 Mets minutes/day) and unknown [19]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated with the standard formula (weight [kilograms]/height [meters]2). Medication history (yes or no) was ascertained by self-report involving any of treatment of lipid-lowering, hypoglycemic, or hypotensive drugs using data field 6177. Detailed information can be found on the UK Biobank Website (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/).

Statistical analyses

Association analyses

Baseline characteristics were described as means (± standard deviations) for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Incidence density was calculated per 1000 person-years. Differences in participant characteristics across specific FAs quartiles were examined using general linear models or logistic regression models. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for fatty acid (FA) levels, including quartiles of exposure, in relation to lung cancer risk, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox model was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residuals test. HRs for continuous variables represent the risk associated with each 1% increase in the proportion of the fatty acids. The P-value for trend was calculated by treating the median value of each quartile as a continuous variable. Further, dose–response associations between FA levels and lung cancer incidence were evaluated using restricted cubic splines (RCS) based on Cox proportional hazards models, with three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles, and the median (50th percentile) set as the reference point. Model 1 was adjusted for sex, age, and BMI, while model 2 included additional adjustments for the Townsend deprivation index, smoking and drinking status, education level, physical activity, and lung cancer PRS. Total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG) were further adjusted in the Model 3. Interaction analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification by genetic risk and smoking status on the associations. The robustness of our findings was verified through several sensitivity analyses. Further exploratory analyses regarding differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

Risk models construction

The lung cancer prediction model and FAs score were constructed using Elastic Net (ENET)-regularized Cox proportional hazards regression (alpha = 0.5). The study population was randomly partitioned into training and testing datasets at a 7:3 ratio. The optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was determined through 10-fold cross-validation using the glmnet R package, where lambda.min was selected to minimize the cross-validation error. Based on the features with non-zero coefficients at lambda.min, a weighted FAs score was calculated for each participant as a linear combination of the selected FAs and their regression coefficients. Subsequently, Cox proportional hazards models were used to predict lung cancer integrating the FA score, the lung cancer PRS, and traditional risk factors (including age, sex, BMI, and smoking status). Model performance was evaluated using the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (10-year risk AUC). The statistical significance of the difference in AUCs across models was determined using 1000 bootstrap iterations to generate the 95% CIs and corresponding P values.

All data analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.3.2 (2023–10–31). The Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons for the primary associations involving the six FAs subclasses, with a significance level set at P < 0.05/6 (0.008). For interaction and subgroup analyses, which were considered exploratory, a nominal P-value of < 0.05 was used to identify suggestive associations. All statistical tests were two-sided. Missing values for continuous covariates (all < 5%) were addressed via multiple imputation by chained equations, while categorical missingness was handled by creating a distinct 'Unknown' category.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics according to specific FAs quartiles. Participants with higher levels of SFAs and MUFAs were generally older, more likely to be Caucasian, current smokers, and had higher TG or TC levels. These individuals also exhibited higher BMI, a higher Townsend deprivation index, and a lower healthy diet score. Unlike those in higher quartiles of SFAs, individuals with higher levels of MUFAs were more likely to be women and ever drinkers. Individuals with higher levels of PUFAs were younger, more likely to be women, regular exercisers, and non-smokers and non-drinkers. These individuals were also more likely to have a college education or higher and less likely to be on medication for lipid-lowering, hypoglycemic, or hypotensive treatments (all P < 0.001). Circulating fatty acids and lung cancer

Over a mean (SD) follow-up period of 11.93 (1.62) years, a total of 965 participants developed lung cancer with an incidence density of 74.93 per 100,000 person years. The Schoenfeld residuals test demonstrated no significant departure from the proportional hazard assumption across Cox models (all P > 0.05). Table 2 showed associations were attenuated after adjusting for additional covariates, with marginal significance for continuous SFAs, and no significant association for n-6 PUFAs and N-6/N-3 ratio in the fully-adjusted model. Compared with individuals in the first quartile of PUFAs and n-3 PUFAs, significant reductions in lung cancer risk were observed in individuals in the fourth quartile of PUFAs and n-3 PUFAs, with HRs (95% CIs) of 0.60 (0.47, 0.76) and 0.73 (0.60, 0.89), respectively. In contrast, individuals in the higher quartiles of SFAs and MUFAs had increased lung cancer risks, with HRs (95% CIs) of 1.25 (1.03, 1.52) and 1.72 (1.33, 2.22), respectively. Additionally, circulating DHA and LA were inversely associated with lung cancer risk (Table S4). Figure 1 shows significant linear associations between circulating PUFAs, n-3 PUFAs, n-6/n-3 PUFAs, MUFAs, and lung cancer risk (P for non-linearity > 0.05). Additionally, all sensitivity analyses did not substantially alter the results (Tables S5-S8). The analyses (Table S8) across three major subtypes of lung cancer showed similar directions, although it did not reach statistical significance in some FAs-specific subtypes pairs. Specifically, we observed stronger associations of FAs with squamous cell carcinoma compared to other subtypes, and also, a stronger inverse association of N-6 PUFAs with small-cell lung cancer was Prediction model construction using FA Scores, lung cancer-PRS, and traditional risk factors

The ENET algorithm successfully performed variable selection at the optimal parameters (lambda. min = 2.30E-05), 6 robust features (including SFAs, N-3 PUFAs; N-6/N-3 ratio, MUFAs, LA and DHA) were maintained with non-zero coefficients. Then, the FA Score was calculated as the linear predictor using coefficients weights derived from the ENET model. The predictive performance for 10-year lung cancer risk prediction was evaluated in both the training and validation datasets. As shown in Fig. 4a and Table S11, the predictive performance of lung cancer-PRS was lower than that of FA scores in validation datasets (AUC: 0.527 [0.502, 0.552] vs. 0.638 [0.611, 0.665]). The AUC increased significantly when FA scores were added to the model containing traditional risk factors (AUC: 0.788 [0.768, 0.808] vs. 0.773 [0.753, 0.793], P < 0.001). Differential expression of FAs associated genes

A total of 5,707 DEGs were identified between lung tumor and normal tissues (FDR < 0.05). Among 82 FAs metabolism-related genes, 16 were significantly dysregulated in tumor tissues and intersected with the identified DEGs, as detailed in Table S12 and Fig. 5a. Among these, 11 genes were significantly upregulated and 5 were downregulated, indicating altered fatty acid metabolism in lung cancer (Fig. 5b). Functional interactions using the PPI network demonstrated the high correlation of these overlapping genes (Fig. 5c). The resulting network comprised 15 nodes and 58 edges, reflecting a highly interconnected molecular landscape. The global clustering coefficient was 0.55, suggesting a significant modular structure within the FA metabolic pathway. As illustrated in the network diagram, EHHADH exhibited the highest degree and centrality, with a degree more than twice the network average, suggesting a pivotal role in regulating FA metabolic flux. Discussion

In this large cohort study of 107,954 participants, circulating PUFAs and n-3 PUFAs were found to be inversely associated with the risk of lung cancer, while SFAs, the n-6/n-3 ratio, and MUFAs were positively associated. Additionally, the associations between circulating FAs and lung cancer may be modified by lung cancer PRS, smoking status, and genetic variants involved in FA metabolism. A FA score was constructed, which improved lung cancer risk prediction beyond traditional risk factors and lung cancer PRS.

Prior epidemiological studies on dietary FAs intake and lung cancer risk have yielded inconsistent findings. A large pooled analysis of 1,445,850 participants from the US, Europe, and Asia found that high intake of SFAs was associated with an increased lung cancer risk, particularly in smokers [20]. Conversely, high PUFAs intake was linked to a reduced risk, while MUFAs showed no association. However, studies from the US and China found no significant associations between SFAs [21] or PFA [22] and lung cancer risk. Dietary FAs need to undergo metabolism within the body to exert their biological effects, making them an inaccurate reflection of actual internal exposure levels. Thus, it’s essential to explore associations of objectively measured circulating FAs with health outcomes considering endogenous synthesis. Recent studies utilizing the UK Biobank have established a comprehensive map of lung cancer risk, ranging from broad-spectrum metabolomic screenings [5, 23] to the exploration of interplays between fatty acids and PRS [4]. Furthermore, large-scale modeling has demonstrated the potential of metabolite risk scores in predicting an array of common diseases, including lung cancer [24]. Collectively, these works underscore the significant role of systemic metabolic alterations in lung cancer. The current study provides a multi-layered extension by specifically interrogating the FAs metabolic pathway and lung cancer, offering both biological plausibility and clinical utility for personalized risk stratification. Beyond association analysis of FAs and lung cancer, the DEGs analysis using transcriptomic data provides complementary evidence by uncovering the dysregulation of FA-related pathways within lung tumor tissues. The finding enhanced the biological plausibility of the observed associations and supported the hypothesis that aberrant FA metabolism may be associated with the process of lung oncogenesis. The established evidence showed that the EHHADH maintains metabolic homeostasis by facilitating peroxisomal fatty acid beta-oxidation and long-chain fatty acid clearance [25]. Its downregulation or deficiency triggers metabolic reprogramming and impairs peroxisomal proteostasis, leading to ROS accumulation and tissue damage [26], which may provide a mechanistic link between FAs-pathway dysregulation and increased lung cancer risk. Blood lipids are essential for energy storage, membrane synthesis, and oncogenic signaling in tumor [27]. Mechanistically, SFAs and MUFAs facilitate de novo lipogenesis and membrane stabilization, rendering tumor cells more resilient to lipid peroxidation and promoting progression [28–30]. In contrast, PUFAs exhibit dual roles: n-3 PUFAs exert anti-carcinogenic effects by inhibiting NF-κB-mediated inflammation [31–33], while excess PUFAs can trigger ferroptosis [34]. Furthermore, a higher n-6/n-3 ratio, a known marker of systemic inflammation, was positively associated with increased lung cancer risk in our study, consistent with its link to various adverse clinical outcomes [11, 35]. Since both n-6 and n-3 PUFAs are essential fatty acids that must be obtained through the diet, maintaining a balanced n-6/n-3 ratio (ideally close to 1:1) in daily intake is recommended for potential health benefits.

When considering the genetic background, we found that participants with a higher predisposition had a greater lung cancer risk associated with each IQR increment of SFAs. This suggests a potential modification effect of PRS on the association between SFAs and lung cancer. We also observed interactions between smoking status and FAs, with significant associations only seen in current or former smokers. These findings remind us that individuals with high genetic risk and a history of smoking should manage their plasma FA levels by reducing dietary SFAs (e.g., carbohydrates, butter, alcohol) and increasing PUFAs (e.g., leafy green vegetables, walnuts, oily fish) for lung cancer prevention. Polymorphism in gene variants at key enzymes, such as FADS1/2, SCD and ELOVL2, can influence tissue FAs availability [36, 37]. We also found that the protective effect of n-3 PUFAs on lung cancer was more accentuated among rs2236212 G/C carriers than G/G and CC carriers. The C allele (effect allele) indicated a higher genetically determined ELOVL2 activity involved in the synthesis of long-chain n-3 PUFAs [8, 15]. These results suggest that individuals with appropriate PUFA synthesis activity may be benefit from higher plasma n-3 PUFA levels, though the precise mechanism needs further exploration. Additionally, adverse effects of MUFAs are more pronounced in rs603424 A/A and G/G carriers, and diminished in G/A carriers. The G allele (effect allele) carriers exhibited a higher genetically determined SCD activity involved in the synthesis of MUFA from SFAs [8]. Greater MUFAs availability (G/G genotype) or a higher degree of FA saturation (A/A genotype) may be a stronger carcinogenic effect.

We additionally developed and validated a FAs score using a weighted sum of the lung cancer-related FAs. Compared to lung cancer-PRS, the FAs score significantly improved the accuracy of lung cancer risk prediction beyond traditional risk factor model in both the training and validation datasets. Considering that circulating FAs reflect not only dietary intake but also endogenous synthesis and genetically determined metabolic capacity, they offer a more accurate potential for predicting lung cancer risk compared to the lung cancer-PRS alone. While previous studies suggested the potential clinical implications of FAs for lung cancer diagnosis [38, 39], our findings firstly demonstrated that FAs could serve as a valuable complementary tool for lung cancer prediction, alongside traditional risk factors or as intervention targets for future cancer prevention.

Despite these advantages, some limitations should be noted. First, although multiple adjustments were made for confounding factors, residual bias due to the observational nature of the study cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, although interaction analyses were pre-specified as exploratory and interpreted using a nominal significance threshold, the number of tests performed increases the likelihood of chance findings, and therefore these results should be interpreted with caution. Second, the study population was predominantly of European ancestry, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Finally, due to data limitations, we were unable to explore more specific FA subtypes on lung cancer.

Conclusion

We identified significant associations between circulating FAs and the risk of lung cancer using metabolomics. These associations were modified by genetic risk and smoking behaviors, suggesting that high-risk individuals may benefit more from FAs management for lung cancer prevention. Circulating FAs enhanced the prediction model for lung cancer incidence beyond traditional risk factors. Further validation in diverse populations and animal models is needed to confirm the generalizability of our findings and to elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.
