Childcare challenges among surgical trainees and faculty: a single-institution study
Authors: Emma G Burke, Sarah Peiffer, Melissa Koci, Lindsay Dean, Kristy L Rialon, Michele Loor, Alice King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have demonstrated the challenges of parenthood in surgical training and careers. However, certain aspects of childrearing for surgeons remain understudied. We aim to leftacterize the complexity and costs of childcare amongst surgical trainees and faculty.MethodsAn anonymous, self-administered survey was conducted within the Department of Surgery at a large academic medical center. Both male and female trainees and faculty were included in the analysis. Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact test was performed.ResultsForty-six respondents provided information about childcare costs, with 37% of respondents being residents or fellows. Trainees worked longer workhours (p = 0.003) than their faculty counterparts. Despite trainees having fewer (p = 0.001) and younger (p = 0.001) children, there was no statistically significant difference in the annual childcare costs (p = 0.6). Over half of trainees and faculty report spending more than $20,000 annually. Childcare costs seem to place a greater burden on trainees, with 89% of trainees and 46% of faculty reporting that they are worried about the cost of childcare (p = 0.04). In addition to the expense, coordinating childcare is also challenging, with most respondents reporting multiple sources of childcare.ConclusionConcerted efforts are needed to support surgeon-parents at all career stages. The cost of childcare places an increased burden on surgical trainees compared to their faculty counterparts. Management of multiple sources of childcare is also a source of stress for both faculty and trainees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09507-x.Keywords: Childcare, Parenting, Residency, Faculty development, Career planning

Background

A surgeon’s training and early career often overlap with peak fertility. This time of immense professional and personal growth places unique demands on both physicians and their families. While the high rate of infertility and pregnancy complications in female surgeons compared to the general population is well-documented, surgeons continue to face difficulties balancing clinical demands with parenting responsibilities long after overcoming the challenges associated with pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum reintegration to work [1]. A better understanding of the unique challenges faced by surgeon-parents beyond the peripartum stage is needed to create impactful policies and support networks.

Previous research has highlighted the difficulty surgical trainees face in obtaining and managing childcare [2]. Availability of on-site childcare or off-site options that accommodate the often unpredictable and variable resident schedule is uncommon [3, 4]. In addition, the cost of childcare has been shown to have a negative effect on residents’ decision to pursue parenthood during training [2]. This challenge extends to practicing surgeons with many female surgeons reporting that they alter practice due to difficulties finding appropriate childcare [4].

While previous work identifies difficulties in obtaining and affording reliable childcare, little is known about how the trainee experience compares to that of surgical faculty [2–4]. Understanding these unique perspectives is important for talent recruitment and retention across the continuum of surgical training and practice. The aim of this study is to better understand the complexities and cost of childcare among both surgical trainees and faculty to identify shared barriers, unique challenges, and opportunities for targeted institutional support by career stage.

Materials & methods

Survey development

The survey was developed to leftacterize gender- and role-based priorities, perceptions, and experiences related to family planning and parenting within a single academic department of surgery. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained for this study and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Informed consent was implied through participation in the survey; a waiver of written consent was granted by the institutional IRB. Data on demographic information and work hours were collected. Questions related to spouse or partner work obligations were included to better understand the challenges of dual-career families. Branching logic was used to guide respondents through relevant questions about family planning, childcare, pregnancy, parental leave, and personal values. Within the survey, all questions were optional. Participants could skip questions or select ‘prefer not to answer’ as desired. Results of questions pertaining to demographics and childcare were analyzed for this study. Survey questions relevant to this manuscript can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Study participants

Voluntary, anonymous responses were collected from trainees, advanced practice providers, and faculty within the department of surgery from January through March 2024. The survey was sent via the department email listserv with weekly reminders over the collection period. Trainees were defined as residents or fellows while faculty were comprised of attending physicians. The sampled department included the following subspecialties: abdominal transplantation, cardiothoracic, thoracic, pediatric, plastic and reconstructive, surgical oncology, trauma, bariatric, vascular, and acute care surgery. Advanced practice providers were inclusive of physician assistants and nurse practitioners, but this subgroup was excluded from the analysis as the nature of their work, including schedules, is fundamentally different from trainee and faculty physicians.

Statistical analysis

Data was collected and stored via Microsoft Forms. All statistical analysis was conducted using Stata 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests were employed to identify potential relationships in the categorical data. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was utilized as the significance value for all tests. Free response questions were included to capture potential missed options in multiple choice questions. Exploratory qualitative content analysis was performed to gain insight into future areas of study.

Results

Demographics

Ninety-four individuals completed the survey with 56 reporting having one or more children. Forty-six respondents answered one or more of the questions about childcare costs, sources, or concerns and were included in this analysis. Responses from these 46 were included in the final analysis. The true number of parents within the department of surgery was, and still is, unknown. As such, a target sample size or response rate for this study could not be calculated. Of those meeting inclusion criteria, 47% were female and 63% were faculty (Table 1). There was no significant difference in the proportion of females in the trainee or faculty groups (p = 1). Trainees were younger (p < 0.001) with 82% under 36 years of age. A significant difference was noted in the number of hours worked per week (p = 0.003) with 94% of trainees reporting working > 60 h per week compared to 48% of faculty members.

Respondent demographics

Almost all (98%) respondents were married or in a domestic partnership with 80% reporting spouse employment. There was no significant difference in employment rates of (p = 0.265) or number of hours worked (p = 0.343) by partners of trainees or faculty. Partner work hour estimates excluded domestic obligations, which were beyond the scope of this study. Faculty had more children than trainees (p = 0.001) with two-thirds having two. Meanwhile, trainees tended to have younger children (p < 0.001) than their faculty counterparts, with 94% of trainees having a child under 5 years of age compared to 41% of attendings.

Childcare

Sources of childcare are included in Table 2. Notably, the majority of all surgeon-parents relied on multiple sources of childcare with 71% of trainees and 66% of faculty reporting at least two care sources. The most common sources of childcare utilized by faculty were spouse or self (45%), after school programs (38%), family members (34%), and daycare (34%). Trainees most often relied on spouse or self (76%), daycare (47%), and family members (41%). There was no significant difference between the types of childcare used by trainees and faculty (p = 0.289). Managing childcare was a common concern for both faculty and trainees (p = 0.20).

Childcare data

The majority of all respondents estimated spending over $20,000 annually on childcare. Despite faculty tending to have more children, there was no statistically significant difference in annual childcare cost between trainees and faculty (p = 0.6). However, trainees did report experiencing a greater financial burden associated with this expense. 89% of trainees worried about the cost of childcare compared to 46% of faculty (p = 0.04).

In free-response questions asking how the institution could better support parents, the lack of on-site childcare options and desire for a childcare stipend or reimbursement was cited by multiple respondents. Trainees reported difficulty finding childcare that covered their long work hours while some faculty noted a lack of drop-in care options for coverage while on-call. Many also recommended that early morning or late afternoon meetings should have a virtual option to afford the surgeon-parent the opportunity to participate in childcare drop-off or pick-up.

Departmental support

Outside of routine childcare needs, trainees (71%) and faculty (87%) felt their colleagues would be supportive if they needed time off to care for sick children (p = 0.18). Both groups also felt department leadership would be supportive of needing additional time off (faculty 83% vs. trainees 76%, p = 0.24). One-third of faculty and 62% of trainees were concerned about financial loss associated with needing additional time off to care for ill children; however, this difference was not significant (p = 0.173).

Despite a culture of support, 70% of trainees and 33% of faculty were concerned about potential for stigma due to the need for additional time off (p = 0.06). Most trainees (88%) and faculty (59%) felt guilty about needing time off to care for their children. The difference between these groups was not significant (p = 0.076).

Discussion

While there has been significant focus on the interplay of a surgical career and pregnancy in recent years, less is known about the issues faced after childbirth by trainees and faculty. The results of this single institution study highlight the financial and emotional burden of childcare among surgeons of all stages. Most trainees and faculty in our sample have working partners, leading to dependence on childcare outside of the home. Our work is consistent with the work of Magudia et al., which found 63% of trainees required multiple sources of childcare [5]. We identified similar trends among our faculty, indicating that childcare management challenges are not unique to residents and fellows.

Among our sample, daycare, family members, and after-school programs were the most common sources of childcare after spouses or oneself. Coordination of multiple resources likely contributes to the worry many respondents feel about managing childcare, particularly for those who do not have the safety net of nearby family. Unlike a traditional job search where applicants may weigh offers before deciding on the best opportunity for themselves and their family, medical and surgical trainees are required to obtain positions through the National Resident Matching Program Match® process. This process utilizes rank-order matching to determine the training location of an applicant; aspiring residents can match to any program listed on their rank list [6]. One analysis of general surgery residency applicants found that only 36% had a geographic connection to the region in which they ultimately matched [7]. In addition, an average of two-thirds of general surgery residency graduates pursue fellowship training [8]. The associated fellowship match process often necessitates trainees and their families again move away from support networks that may be able to provide additional childcare at low or no cost. With 41% of our trainees and 34% of our faculty utilizing family for care, future work should aim to better understand the unique needs of surgeons without local family support.

Another source of stress accompanying childcare is identifying options that can cover the irregular work hours of surgical trainees and faculty. Trainees and faculty expressed similar levels of concern regarding management of childcare and most used multiple sources of care. We found that trainees report working more hours per week than faculty while work hours of trainee and faculty spouses were similar. Trainees noted the lack of local childcare options that were available during their scheduled work hours. Faculty referenced needing drop-in care options for assistance while on-call. This reflects a nationwide paucity of safe childcare options for families. Few residency programs offer childcare support, and many options fail to accommodate the irregular hours of a surgeon’s schedule [3]. Of the 78% of training institutions that do not offer on-site childcare to residents or fellows, only 42% of these assist trainees in locating childcare [9].

To ensure adequate coverage for the schedule demands of surgical training, the Association of Women Surgeon’s (AWS) Initiative for Surgical Families During Residency and Fellowship recommends extended hour on-site childcare at hospitals, access to back-up childcare, and preferential enrollment at select facilities [10]. While our institution provides access to emergency childcare through institutional benefits, multiple respondents noted a need for these types of services in open-ended questions. Future iterations of this survey should aim to determine if the offered resource is inadequate for the needs of our department or if the existing service should be better publicized by inquiring about familiarity with the resource and frequency of use.

Most participants reported spending over $20,000 annually on childcare. Not unexpectedly, trainees expressed greater distress than faculty related to this financial burden. While respondent income was not queried in our study, this higher financial burden is likely accounted for by the greater proportion of income spent by trainees compared with faculty. Nationwide data for 2024 shows an average salary of $66,712 for PGY 1 residents with the average trainee salary peaks at $90,562 for PGY 8 residents [8]. Meanwhile, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports an average attending surgeon annual salary of $343,990 [11]. A recent analysis comparing publicly available resident salary data to average expenditures, including childcare, around the country found that current trainee salaries are inadequate to support a family as the sole source of income and can consume up to 35% of a trainee’s salary [12, 13]. Future work should assess the level of distress associated with childcare expenses and management as they relate to total household income in surgical families.

Consistent with prior research demonstrating PGY-1 residents spend over 40% of pretax income on childcare, our findings reiterate the need for support policies that extend beyond maternity or paternity leave [5]. AWS’s Initiative for Surgical Families During Residency and Fellowship recommends programs consider offering discounts or other subsidies to assist in offsetting these costs [9]. Multiple faculty and trainees advocated for similar policies in our survey. A pilot program of childcare stipends for residents at the Medical University of South Carolina was well-received with all eligible parents participating [14]. For systems like ours that do not have an existing on-campus childcare option, this is a viable alternative to construction of a new on-site facility.

Limitations of this study include response bias due to reliance on self-reported data of a small sample size. While all responses were voluntary and anonymous, some participants may have been wary of sharing overtly identifying information or inflammatory opinions. This also limited the number of additional subgroup comparisons that could be performed with this dataset; it is likely that childcare costs, and associated challenges, differ significantly depending on family structure and childcare arrangements. Participants may also have interpreted questions differently, which is a limitation of survey-based research. Future multi-institutional studies using a combination of qualitative methodologies should be performed to increase the statistical power of additional subgroup analyses. Additionally, the number of parents within our department is unknown. It was therefore impossible to estimate an appropriate response rate. It is possible that trainees and faculty without childcare or family planning challenges did not respond to the survey, influencing our findings. Finally, this study was conducted at a single academic institution and may not be reflective of trainees and faculty within other systems. These findings may not be generalizable to other practice settings. Despite these limitations, our study offers insight into childcare-related challenges faced by surgeon-parents in academic surgery. However, these findings likely represent only a small fraction of the issues that shape the parenting experience of surgeons. Future research should incorporate a more detailed assessment of the division of domestic labor, including the cognitive and logistical tasks, which may help identify additional opportunities for workplace supports and policies.

Conclusions

Surgical trainees and faculty experience emotional distress and financial burden related to childcare management. Notably, residents and fellows report greater concern over the cost of childcare than surgical faculty. Given the reported distress in our survey reported and desire for institutional support, exploration of interventions such as childcare stipends, on-site facilities, and drop-in care options should be considered to improve the lives of surgeon-parents.
