Comparison of inflammatory and nutritional markers obtained at the time of diagnosis in patients diagnosed with renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Hatice Basaran Goksen, Alaettin Arslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to compare the prognostic significance of inflammatory and nutritional parameters, calculated based on hemogram and biochemical values at the time of diagnosis, in patients diagnosed with renal cell carcinoma (RCC). A total of 120 patients diagnosed with RCC and receiving diagnosis and/or treatment at our center were included in the study. Among the inflammatory and nutritional parameters, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), modified Glasgow prognostic score (mGPS), and Memorial Sloan Kettering prognostic score (MPS) were calculated and analyzed. The most common pathological subtype was the clear cell variant (69.2%). 25.8% of the patients were at the metastatic stage. Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that tumor size, tumor grade, sex, stage, presence of metastasis, number of metastases, IVC involvement, history of radiotherapy (RT), hemoglobin, NLR, PLR, PNI, albumin, C-reactive protein (CRP), MPS, and mGPS had a significant impact on survival. Furthermore, stage, number of metastases, and PNI were identified as independent prognostic factors for survival. When clinical parameters were excluded from the analysis, NLR, LDH, and mGPS emerged as independent prognostic factors for survival.As demonstrated by the results of the multivariate analysis, stage, number of metastases, and PNI were found to be more influential independent prognostic factors. This study suggests that nutritional parameters have greater prognostic value than inflammatory markers in RCC, and that a simple nutritional scoring system performed at the time of diagnosis may provide improved prognostic accuracy. Additionally, our findings indicate that the MPS, which was evaluated for the first time in RCC in this study, also has prognostic significance, and it may potentially become an independent prognostic marker in future studies with larger patient populations.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma, Inflammatory markers, Prognostic nutritional index, Mortality, Memorial sloan kettering prognostic score, Glasgow prognostic scoreSubject terms: Renal cancer, Cancer microenvironment, Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Although kidney cancers account for 2–4% of all adult cancers, they most commonly present as renal cell carcinoma (RCC) (60–85%)1,2. At diagnosis, 40–50% of patients have localized disease amenable to surgery, while 25–30% present with distant metastases. Despite initial localized presentation in approximately half of the patients, recurrence rates of up to 20–30% can occur even in early-stage disease following surgical intervention3. Given its occurrence in a relatively younger patient population and its life-limiting nature, the development of scoring systems and prognostic parameters to aid in outcome prediction has gained importance. The feasibility of such assessment methods without incurring additional costs or time is a significant advantage. While numerous laboratory-based parameters are already well-known, comparative analyses of these grouped parameters in terms of prognostic impact are uncommon, which forms the central hypothesis of our study.

For prognostic assessment in localized disease, standardized models such as tumor stage, size, grade, and necrosis score (SSIGN) are employed, whereas no widely accepted nomogram exists for advanced disease4. Given the high cost of treatment and the short survival time associated with advanced-stage RCC, it is crucial to utilize parameters that can predict survival and evaluate treatment responsiveness. The use and clinical utility of immunological, inflammatory, and nutritional scores based on hematological and biochemical values have become increasingly prominent with the emergence of novel therapies such as targeted agents and immunotherapy in advanced disease. Although the International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) score developed by Heng et al. provides high predictive accuracy in metastatic RCC, additional scoring systems are needed to better reflect the patients’ nutritional and inflammatory status5. The most well-known laboratory-based parameters and scoring systems include C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), and Glasgow prognostic score (GPS)6–11. These parameters have been reported in the literature to possess both prognostic and predictive value in localized and metastatic RCC and are considered cost-effective and time-efficient tools.

Additionally, according to our review of the literature, the Memorial Sloan Kettering prognostic score (MPS), which was first shown to have prognostic significance in metastatic pancreatic cancer, has not yet been evaluated in RCC12. MPS is a relatively new prognostic tool and has started to be investigated in advanced gastric cancers as well13.

In this study, we aimed to assess the prognostic significance of MPS, which has not previously been specifically studied in RCC, and to compare it with routinely obtainable laboratory-based nutritional and inflammatory scores.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the local ethics committee (Date: 08/10/2024, Decision no: 215).

Patients’ selection

The study included 120 patients diagnosed with and/or treated for RCC at our center between January 2014 and June 2024. Inclusion criteria included being 18 years of age or older, having medical imaging at the time of diagnosis, knowledge of current laboratory values before oncological treatment/surgery, knowledge of the date of death/last follow-up, no additional malignancy, and no additional autoimmune disease or medication use that could affect laboratory values. Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Calculation of scores

PNI calculation was performed as [10 × serum albumin (g/dL)] + [0.005 × lymphocyte count/microliter]. mGPS scoring was performed as follows: patients with elevated CRP levels (> 1 mg/dL) and hypoalbuminemia (< 3.5 g/dL) were given a high-risk score of 2, patients with only abnormal CRP levels (> 10 mg/L) were given a moderate-risk score of 1, and those with normal CRP levels (≤ 10 mg/L) regardless of albumin levels were given a low-risk score of 0. An MPS scale of 0–2 was used: MPS = 0, low risk for albumin ≥ 4 g/dL and NLR ≤ 4 g/dL; MPS = 1, moderate risk for albumin < 4 g/dL or NLR > 4 g/dL; and MPS = 2, high risk for albumin < 4 g/dL and NLR > 4 g/dL.

Statistical analysis

Age, gender, disease stage, Fuhrman grade, tumor size, metastasis status, IVC involvement, number of metastases, radiotherapy history, hemoglobin, albumin, creatinine, CRP, LDH, NLR, PLR, PNI, mGPS, and MPS variables were analyzed for factors affecting survival. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed for cutoff values of numerical variables, and these values were accepted as cutoff values. Data are presented as frequencies, percentages, and medians (min-max). Normal distribution of the data was checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox regression tests were also used to evaluate the effect of variables on survival. Hazard ratios were calculated with 95% confidence intervals. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 21 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). A p < 0.05 level was considered significant.

Results

A total of 120 patients diagnosed with RCC were included in the study. Among them, 76 patients were alive, while 44 had died (63.3% and 36.7%, respectively). The median overall survival (mOS) was 40.5 months (range: 2–120 months). Forty of the patients were female (33.3%) and 80 were male (66.7%). According to the 8th edition of the AJCC staging system, most patients were classified as Stage I–II (65%). In terms of Fuhrman grading, the majority of tumors were Grade I or II (61.7%). The dominant pathological subtype was clear cell variant (69.2%). There were 29 metastatic patients (24.2%), most of whom were diagnosed with multiple (>4) metastases (79.3%). Among those who were metastatic at diagnosis or became metastatic later, a considerable proportion received radiotherapy or palliative medical treatment (20% and 32.5%, respectively). Discussion

In cancer types with limited survival in advanced stages, such as RCC, the implementation of rapid and cost-effective prognostic methods in outpatient settings appears advantageous for both clinicians and patients in terms of time and effort. The findings derived from routine blood parameters, particularly nutritional markers, have primarily been explored in gastrointestinal, lung, and head and neck cancers and have been validated through meta-analyses14–16.

The rationale for using these parameters lies in the biological behavior of molecules such as albumin, CRP, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, and blood cells included in the calculations. Albumin is featured in the scoring systems of PNI, mGPS, and MPS; it not only plays a crucial role in maintaining hemostatic balance but also serves as a nutritional indicator and is essential for the structural integrity of blood cells. Lymphocytes, representing anti-tumoral immunity, contribute to the calculation of PNI, NLR, PLR, and MPS. In contrast, neutrophils and platelets are associated with poor prognosis due to their roles in promoting angiogenesis, facilitating metastasis, and suppressing anti-tumoral immune responses.

Studies evaluating PNI, NLR, PLR, and mGPS specifically in RCC are frequently encountered in the literature, and several meta-analyses involving these variables have been published8–11,17. However, no prior study has assessed the prognostic value of MPS in RCC, which makes the present study novel in this respect. Furthermore, comparative studies that group and analyze these yet unexamined parameters remain scarce.

In a study conducted by Ma et al., the predictive value of the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was investigated in patients with RCC18. This study, conducted in a surgically treated patient cohort, also calculated NLR, PLR, and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR). SII, PNI, CRP, tumor size, tumor necrosis, surgical modality, Fuhrman grade, AJCC stage, and pathological type were identified as independent predictors of postoperative mortality. Five-year overall survival (OS) was reported as 89% in the low SII group and 64.5% in the high SII group; in the high PNI group, OS was 87.9%, whereas it dropped to 43.4% in the low PNI group. While both SII and PNI were shown to affect OS, PNI had a greater impact on survival curves. In our study, multivariate analysis also identified PNI as an independent prognostic factor for OS. However, our patient population was more heterogeneous, and in addition to PNI, we also evaluated mGPS and MPS scores.

In another study by Makino et al., the prognostic value of inflammatory and nutritional indices in non-metastatic RCC was assessed19. This study evaluated NLR, PLR, the CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR), PNI, and the geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI). While CAR was found to be an independent prognostic factor for metastasis development, NLR was identified as an independent prognostic factor for OS. The study concluded that inflammatory indices had greater prognostic significance. In contrast, our univariate analysis showed that inflammatory indices had a dependent impact on OS, whereas PNI remained an independent prognostic factor. Although CAR and GNRI were not included in our evaluations, we incorporated both mGPS and MPS in our analysis.

Another study by Ren et al. investigated the relationship between the PNI- monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR)-PLT (PMP) score and postoperative outcomes in non-metastatic clear cell RCC patients20. This three-parameter score was found to influence both OS and metastasis-free survival, and a high PMP score emerged as an independent prognostic factor for tumor-related survival. In our study, while PLR, NLR, mGPS, and MPS values were observed as variables affecting survival, PNI emerged as the strongest independent factor affecting survival.

In a different study, Higgins et al. examined the predictive value of preoperative sarcopenia and mGPS in patients with localized RCC21. Patients were stratified into risk groups based on radiologically assessed sarcopenia, and skeletal muscle index (SMI) and mGPS values were evaluated together. These risk groups were found to have a statistically significant impact on recurrence-free survival. In our study, while mGPS was found to be a significant factor for mOS, PNI, which serves as an indirect marker of sarcopenia and nutritional status, also emerged as a significant variable for mOS.

Particularly in metastatic patients, evaluating the efficacy and response to targeted therapies and immunotherapy is critical, especially considering the high cost of these treatment modalities. Although radiological analysis remains the standard method for objective response evaluation (i.e., Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors—RECIST), additional patient-based supportive parameters are needed. A study by Saal et al. addressed this issue22. The study reported that mGPS monitoring, particularly at the 6th week of treatment, served as a superior and practical marker in predicting treatment prognosis. Compared to RECIST evaluations performed between weeks 8 and 12, mGPS provided an earlier and more cost-effective approach. In our study, mGPS was found to be an effective prognostic marker for overall survival, especially when clinical factors were excluded. Additionally, we evaluated other parameters, including MPS, PNI, NLR, PLR, LDH, CRP, hemoglobin, albumin, and creatinine.

As supported by the studies discussed above, inflammatory and nutritional parameters are crucial in disease monitoring and patient follow-up. Some of these studies did not include clinical or tumor characteristics in their multivariate analyses. In our study, when evaluating the patient holistically, nutritional status emerged as the more prominent factor, whereas, in isolated analyses of laboratory parameters, inflammatory markers became more prominent.

The limitations of our study include its single-center, retrospective design, the limited sample size, and the small number of patients who did not receive radiotherapy and/or systemic treatment, which prevented subgroup analyses to evaluate treatment impact. Furthermore, the study cohort was heterogeneous in terms of disease stage. Our results need to be validated through randomized, prospective, multicenter studies involving more homogeneous patient groups. The original contributions of our study include the simultaneous evaluation and comparative grouping of multiple scores, as well as, to the best of our knowledge, the first investigation of the MPS in RCC.

Conclusion

In RCC, patient characteristics, in addition to tumor characteristics, are also of prognostic importance. When considering the patient holistically, the importance of nutritional parameters emphasizes the importance of monitoring the patient’s nutritional status from the beginning of treatment and providing support as needed. Nutritional screening from the moment of diagnosis and subsequent follow-up are crucial in this patient group. As can be seen from this study, clinicians’ comprehensive patient monitoring and support are invaluable for both treatment response and disease prognosis.
