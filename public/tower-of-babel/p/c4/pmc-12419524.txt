Incidence, treatment, and survival of patients with appendiceal adenocarcinomas and low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms: linked Swedish national registry study
Authors: Joel Johansson, Roland E Andersson, Per Loftås, Stefan Redéen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAppendiceal adenocarcinomas and low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms (LAMNs) are rare tumours. Much of the existing knowledge is derived from registry-based studies, particularly the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database in the USA.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the Swedish Cancer Registry, Swedish Cause of Death Registry, and the National Patient Registry to analyse demographic characteristics and outcomes of patients diagnosed with appendiceal adenocarcinoma or LAMN between 2005 and 2019. Kaplan–Meier survivor function, multivariate Cox regression analysis, standardized mortality ratio, and net survival were used to assess survival. Incidence was estimated by direct standardization from 2005 to 2019.ResultsIn all, 1159 patients with appendiceal neoplasms were included, with a mean age at diagnosis of 63.3 years. The incidence of adenocarcinomas was stable, whereas the incidence of LAMNs increased from 2012 onwards. Patients with non-mucinous adenocarcinomas who underwent colonic resection had better survival outcomes than patients treated with appendicectomy alone. For mucinous adenocarcinomas, colonic resection did not improve survival outcomes compared with appendicectomy. Patients with non-mucinous adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, or LAMN who underwent cytoreductive surgery and heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy (CRS-HIPEC) had favourable overall and net survival.ConclusionColonic resection increased survival only for patients with non-mucinous adenocarcinomas. Since 2012, the incidence of LAMN has increased, most likely due to changes in diagnostic and coding practices, but the incidence of appendiceal adenocarcinomas has remained stable. The survival benefit of CRS-HIPEC is observed in a very specific patient population, emphasizing the importance of careful patient selection.

Introduction

Appendiceal adenocarcinomas (AAs) and low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms (LAMNs) are rare tumours, and their low incidence has significantly hindered prospective studies. Much of the knowledge of these tumours comes from registry-based studies, which are almost exclusively based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database or other US-based registries. A previous study has reported on the incidence of AA in Sweden and its temporal trends from 1970 until 20121.

Studies from SEER have shown a trend for an increased incidence in AA since the early 2000s, with improved survival observed for the period 1994–2014 compared with 1973–19932,3. Treatment remains mainly surgical, with right hemicolectomy (RHC) recommended operation in most cases. T1 tumours with favourable characteristics (tumour grade G1–2, no angiolymphatic invasion, R0 resection) and LAMN can be treated with appendicectomy alone. Patients with peritoneal dissemination are sometimes treated with cytoreductive surgery and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (CRS-HIPEC)4. In Sweden CRS-HIPEC is centralized to a few university hospitals, and patients are discussed at multidisciplinary conferences.

This approach follows the treatment of the more common colon cancer, where RHC is the treatment of choice for right-sided colon cancer. Although AAs have a similar morphology to colon cancer, they are genetically distinct entities5,6. Studies on the efficacy of RHC for treating AA have shown mixed results, and benefit has only been demonstrated in specific patient subgroups7–13. Small case series have previously demonstrated that AA frequently presents with acute and subacute abdominal pain14–16.

The aims of the present study were to: assess the incidence of AA and LAMN in Sweden from 2005 until 2019 and to evaluate temporal changes; analyse overall and net survival for AA and LAMN in relation to temporal trends, age, histological subgroups, and cancer stage; and examine how surgical treatment and co-morbidities affect survival.

Methods

The Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare maintains the Swedish Cancer Registry (SCR)17, the National Patient Register (NPR)18, and the Swedish Cause of Death Register (SCDR)19. These registers contain data of all tumours diagnosed in Sweden; this includes diagnosis and interventions for all hospital admissions and specialist outpatient care, localization and histopathological characteristics, and the date and cause of all deaths occurring in Sweden. These registries can be linked using the Swedish personal identity number, which uniquely identifies all Swedish residents. A linked registry extract containing the above-mentioned data from the SCR, SCDR, and NPR was requested from the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare. This study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board at Linköping University. The RECORD checklist for the reporting of observational studies was used (see Appendix S1).

Using the linked registry extract, all individuals diagnosed with a tumour in the appendix between 2005 and 2019 were identified from the SCR and SCDR using the International Classification of Diseases, Revision 7 (ICD-7) code 153.4. Further information on date of birth, date of tumour diagnosis, histopathological type, and tumour node metastasis (TNM) stage was obtained from the SCR. Surgical procedures and their corresponding hospital admission dates were obtained from the NPR. The date of death was obtained from the SCDR. The SCR uses the 3 digit C24/histology codes17 as well as ICD-codes to encode diagnosis. Using the ICD-7 code 153.4 together with histology codes for adenocarcinoma (096) and LAMN (093) from the SCR, all patients with a diagnosis of AA or LAMN were identified. Patients diagnosed at autopsy were excluded from the study. Patients were followed from the date of diagnosis until they died, emigrated, or to the end of the follow-up period. Those who did not re-immigrate before 1 January 2022, were censored at the date of emigration. All remaining patients were followed up to 1 January 2022, and were censored at that date. The dates of last emigration and re-immigration were obtained from the NPR. The Swedish population was used as the reference population. Data on the composition of the Swedish population according to age and sex were obtained from the public database provided by Statistics Sweden20.

AA and LAMN are subclassified according to the International Classification of Disease for Oncology, 3rd edition (ICD-O3) and C24/histology codes in the SCR. In this study, these tumours were subdivided into five histological subtypes: LAMN, non-mucinous adenocarcinoma (NMA), mucinous adenocarcinoma (MA), Goblet cell cancer, and Signet ring cell cancer. ICD-O3 and TNM classifications were included in the SCR in 2005. For this study, all patients were staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control’s TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours, 8th edition21. In the SCR, the TNM classification is registered either when there is sufficient evidence for a treatment decision or after surgical treatment, and only the last of these TNM classifications that are reported is included in the registry. For patients with NMA and MA, registration in practice always occurs after surgical treatment (Regional Cancer Center in Southeast Sweden, pers. comm.). For patients with Goblett cell cancer, Signet ring cell cancer or LAMN, at what point the TNM classification is reported to the SCR is up to the treating physician.

The ICD-O3 codes used were as follows: LAMN, 84801; NMA, 81403, 82633, 82613, 84403, 82113, 82203; NMA, 84803, 84703; Signet ring cell cancer, 84903; and Goblet cell cancer, 82433.

Open and laparoscopic operations were defined using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes as appendicectomy (JEA00, JEA10, JEA01, 4510, 4511, 0058), ileocecal resection (JFB20, JFB21, 4642), colonic resection (JFB30, JFB31, JFH00, JFH01, JFH10, JFH11, 4641, 4649, 4650, 4651), exploratory surgery (JAH00, JAH01, JAH21, 4010, 4042, 4043, 4044, 4080), and CRS-HIPEC (JAQ00, JAQ10). In this study, the surgical treatment was defined as the most extensive operation that a given patient underwent within 180 days of diagnosis. The date of diagnosis in the SCR was defined as the date of the first examination/sampling that is the basis of diagnosis. Patients with no surgery within 180 days of diagnosis were classified as not having undergone surgery.

Co-morbidities were identified using the NPR. Heart disease was identified using truncated ICD-10 codes, and was defined as having been diagnosed the year before the diagnosis of AA with any of the following codes: I3, I4, I5, I6, I20, I21, I22, I23, I24, and I25. Diabetes was identified using truncated ICD-10 codes, and was defined as having been diagnosed the year before the diagnosis of AA with any of the following codes: E10, E11, and E14.

The study period was divided into three equal periods: 2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2019. Age was categorized into four groups (0–49, 50–69, 70–80, and ≥ 80 years) to distinguish between very young patients, middle-aged adults, young elderly, and old elderly.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were made using STATA 15.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Survival analyses were conducted by histological subtype on the entire study population and a subgroup analysis of patients with cancer stage I–III NMA or MA treated with appendicectomy, ileocecal resection, or colonic resection. Five-year overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method according to age, sex, time period, cancer stage, and surgery for each histological subtype. The Pohar Perme method was used to estimate survival as cumulative 5-year net survival (NS) according to age, sex, time period, cancer stage, and surgery for each histological subtype. A subgroup analysis of OS and NS was performed on stage I–III patients who underwent appendicectomy, ileocecal resection, or RHC, assessing survival by surgery type for each histological subtype. The Pohar Perme method was used because it provides unbiased estimates by accounting for the effect of population mortality through weighted survival ratios22, using the Swedish population as reference population.

Cox proportional hazards models were created for each histological subtype to assess statistical independence between the prognostic factors of age, sex, stage, time period, type of surgery, and co-morbidities. Additional Cox proportional hazards models were conducted for subgroup analyses of patients with NMA and MA who had stage I–III disease and had undergone appendicectomy, ileocecal resection, or colonic resection.

A step-down (backward elimination) approach was used for variable selection in all Cox proportional hazards models. Patient-factor variables (heart disease and diabetes) were initially included but were sequentially removed if not significant (P ≥ 0.05). At each step, the simpler model was compared to the previous model using the likelihood ratio test, and the simpler model was retained if the test was not significant (P ≥ 0.05), indicating no meaningful loss of model fit.

The remaining variables (age, sex, time period, cancer stage, and surgery) were retained in all models regardless of significance level because they were considered essential based on clinical and epidemiological reasoning.

Kendall's τb was calculated for the correlation of stage and extent of surgery; no surgery was excluded from this analysis. To handle missing values for cancer stage in the survival analyses, multiple chained ordered logistic regression imputation was used.

Incidence estimates were calculated for AA and LAMN. The incidence rate was calculated using direct standardization adjusted for age, sex, year, and histological subtype, with the Swedish population as the reference population. The incidence rate ratio (IRR) was calculated using Poisson regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, year, and histological subtype. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic details

In all, 1273 patients met the inclusion criteria. After excluding those diagnosed at autopsy, 1159 patients remained. The mean age at diagnosis was 63.3 years. LAMN was the largest subgroup (371, 26.7%), followed by NMA (357, 28.3%), MA (313, 24.8%), Goblet cell cancer (67, 5%), and Signet ring cell cancer (37, 3%); no histological subtype was recorded for 14 patients (1%). Detailed demographic information for the LAMN, NMA, and MA groups is provided in Table 1. Due to low numbers of patients, the Goblet cell cancer, Signet ring cell cancer, and no recorded histological subtype groups were excluded from the survival analysis; demographic details for these three groups are provided in Table S1. The high proportion of patients with missing TNM data in the LAMN group (87.5%) meant that stage could not be imputed for these patients. Due to the high frequency of missing stage classification among LAMN patients, it was decided not to perform Cox regression analysis for this group. Subgroup analyses of patients with stage I–III who underwent appendicectomy, ileocecal resection, or RHC were performed for patients with NMA (n = 184) and MA (n = 131). Kendall’s τb indicated a correlation between stage and the type of surgery for LAMN (τb = 0.46, P < 0.001), NMA (τb = 0.26, P < 0.001), and MA (τb = 0.35, P < 0.001).

For patients with LAMN, OS decreased with age, but NS did not. There were no differences in OS or NS according the type of surgery for patients with LAMN (Table 2).

Incidence

LAMNs are non-existent in the first decade of life, with zero cases registered. The incidence of AA increases throughout life, with a peak incidence of 7.41 per 106 person-years in the eighth decade of life. LAMN peaked in the seventh decade of life, with an incidence of 7.81 per 106 person-years. The age-adjusted incidence of AA did not differ between men and women (2.91 versus 3.13, respectively). However, for LAMN, the age-adjusted incidence was significantly higher among women than men (3.01 versus 1.94, respectively); this was congruent with the Poisson regression (IRR 1.65; P < 0.001). The incidence of AA has remained stable from 2005 onwards, whereas that of LAMN increased markedly from 2005 to 2019 (IRR 10.80; P < 0.001; Table 5; Fig. 1).

Discussion

For the patients with NMA in the present study, colonic resection conferred improved survival over appendicectomy alone in multivariate analysis, whereas patients with MA did not experience the same improvement with colonic resection. This is in line with previous studies that reported that patients with MA do not benefit from colonic resection, whereas a subset of patients with NMA do7,9,11,12. The mechanism for this difference may be that regional lymph node metastasis is more common in NMA than MA and that adequate lymphadenectomy is important for the outcome among patients with NMA7,8,13.

In patients with LAMN, there was no improvement in survival with additional surgery beyond appendicectomy, consistent with the literature23. In summary, colonic resection increased survival only for patients with NMAs. Since 2005, the incidence of LAMN has increased, most likely due to changes in diagnostic and coding practices, whereas the incidence of AA has remained stable. A survival benefit of CRS-HIPEC was observed in a highly selected patient population, emphasizing the importance of careful patient selection.

In the subgroup analysis of patients with stage I–III, neither the NMA nor MA models had ten or more events per variable, which is a common rule of thumb in regression analyses. However, this has been challenged, with Vittinghoff et al.24 reporting that models with five to nine events per variable should not be systematically discounted. Both subgroup analysis models had five or more events per variable, and are in line with the main models. Although some caution in interpretation is warranted, these models add further support to the conclusion that colonic resection has distinct impacts on patients with NMA and MA.

In the multivariate analysis, patients with NMA and MA selected for CRS-HIPEC appeared to have significantly improved survival than patients selected for appendicectomy alone. This is probably due to them being almost 10 years younger, on average, decreasing the expected mortality. It is important to note that there is an indication for CRS-HIPEC only if there is evidence of peritoneal dissemination, which differentiates these patients from the larger population with AA.

Previous population studies often note that the incidence of appendiceal carcinomas increases over time2,3. In the present study, the incidence of invasive AA remained stable from 2005 onwards, whereas the incidence of LAMN increased markedly. This is probably due to changes in the diagnosis and/or classification of pathological findings due to the very rapid increase in incidence around 2014, after which there was a large increase in the number of LAMNs. The incidence of appendicectomy in Sweden has remained stable (at around 120 per 100,000 person-years) during the study period according to statistics from the National Board of Health and Welfare25, with data available for the number of cases per year. The Epidemiology Strengthened by Histopathology Reports in Sweden (ESPRESSO) study compiled all gastrointestinal histopathology data from 1965 to 2023, including appendicectomy specimens26. A more detailed picture of the increase in LAMN can be obtained by comparing the number of appendicectomy specimens sent for histopathological analysis per year from 2005 until 2019 from the ESPRESSO study (pers. comm.) to the total number of appendectomies per year reported to the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare's Patient Register25. In 2005, 57.7% of all appendicectomy specimens were sent for histopathological analysis. The incidence of LAMN only started to increase in 2012. One year prior, in 2011, the proportion of appendix specimens sent for histopathology had increased to 68.3%, with no increase in the incidence of LAMN. By 2019, 91.3% of all appendicectomy specimens were sent for histopathological analysis. Thus, the reason for the increase in the incidence of LAMN is not necessarily due to a sampling effect, because there was a delay of several years between the increase in the proportion of appendicectomy specimens sent for histopathological analysis and the increase in the incidence of LAMN. Changes in coding and detection rate at histopathological analysis may have also contributed to the increased incidence of LAMN. To determine how these factors are related, follow-up data are needed to further examine the relationship between the incidence of LAMN and the proportion of appendicectomy specimens sent for histopathological analysis. It appears that some population-based studies have included the ICD-O3 code 8480 (which includes in situ lesions such as LAMN) among MAs, perhaps explaining some of the increase in the incidence of AA in previous studies2.

The main strength of this study is the use of data from validated population-based registries held by the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare, where all individuals are identified by their personal identity number consistently across all registries and are used by all healthcare providers. This study covers all patients diagnosed, regardless of surgery, and reflects clinical practice.

Despite this, the study has several weaknesses. Compared with the SEER database, the registries used in this study have a smaller population to draw and have limitations regarding their design. For example, there is less information concerning tumour biology, but data on patient factors, such as co-morbidities, are available. Classifications of histopathological diagnosis and diagnostic methods have changed over time, meaning that it is not known whether the subgroups are truly consistent over time. A specific concern is that there are reports of non-specialized pathologists overinterpreting LAMN as adenocarcinomas compared with expert gastrointestinal pathologists27.
