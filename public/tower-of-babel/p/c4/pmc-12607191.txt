Patterns and trends in antimalarial dispensing practices in Ghana: a retrospective analysis of outreach training and supportive supervision data (2018–2022)
Authors: Esenam Mawufemor Tagboto, Michael Opoku-Mireku, Jocelyn Laryea, Timothy Agandah, Paul Boateng, Joel Jeffrey Idun-Acquah, Keziah Laurencia Malm, Harriet Affran Bonful
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing production of antimalarials and the growing emergence of partial resistance to artemisinin necessitate effective antimalarial stewardship. The Outreach Training and Supportive Supervision (OTSS) provides an effective quality assurance framework to foster such stewardship. OTSS data has been analysed and utilized to strengthen various aspects of malaria care. However, improvements in dispensing practices remain undocumented. This study sought to describe dispensing practices and to characterize their temporal trends in Ghana from 2018 to 2022.MethodsAn analytical observational study was designed to analyse secondary data from Ghana’s annual OTSS visits from 2018 to 2022. The visits covered selected health facilities in all 16 administrative regions. Data was extracted from the Electronic Data System, a digital tool used for OTSS. Indicators included region, level of service delivery, facility name, and all pharmacy-related indicators; 3 under labelling and 12 under adherence. Dispensing practice was defined as the median-dichotomized composite of labelling and adherence. After data cleaning, there were 7193 valid records of visits to 4471 facilities (2722 were repeat visits). Dispensing practices across regions and service levels were reported annually. Mixed-effects logistic regression was used to predict the odds of dispensing practice by year and over time.ResultsThe highest rates of adequate dispensing practices for the regions in each year were 85.7% in 2018, 70.8% in 2019, 58.8% in 2020, and 66.0% in 2022. By level of service, the highest was 57.7% in 2018, 56.3% in 2019, 57.8% in 2020, and 45.8% in 2022. Eight regions had similar levels of appropriate dispensing practices as Greater Accra. Three regions exhibited lower, and four had higher levels. Dispensing practices were frequently appropriate in Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds compared to hospitals (Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) = 0.48), clinics (AOR = 0.37), and maternity homes (AOR = 0.33).ConclusionDispensing practices remained consistently low across regions and service delivery levels over the assessed years. However, CHPS compounds and health centres demonstrated comparatively better dispensing practices than higher-level facilities. Continuous investment in training, supervision, and resources is needed to ensure equitable improvements in dispensing practices.

Background

Malaria remains a disease of public health concern globally despite being a preventable and treatable disease. It is a major cause of death among pregnant women and children, particularly in Africa [1]. In 2023, the global malaria incidence rate increased to 60.4 per 1000 population at risk, with a disproportionate 94% of the cases recorded in sub-Saharan Africa. That same year, there was a global increase in the production of artemisinin-based combinations, reaching 4.4 billion globally, mostly distributed by national malaria programmes in sub-Saharan Africa [2].

While this increase suggests improved access to treatment, it is a cause for concern amid the growing emergence of partial resistance to artemisinin [3]. This highlights the need for deliberate and guided stewardship of artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT). In this vein, the Outreach Training and Supportive Supervision (OTSS) was implemented as a mechanism to enhance the proper use of ACT.

With a disproportionately high number of malaria cases in sub-Saharan Africa and emerging artemisinin resistance, Ghana’s dispensing practices are pivotal for global malaria control. OTSS has been operating in Ghana since 2009, enhancing adherence to malaria policy guidelines while improving preventive, diagnostic, and treatment outcomes [4–6]. It became a necessary approach for assuring high-quality malaria services following the introduction of ACT and the mandate for parasitological confirmation before treatment [7]. OTSS aims to enhance health facility capacity and provider expertise by combining facility-level training with supportive supervision [8].

During OTSS visits, trained supervisors employ standardized, competency-based, and digital checklists to collect data through healthcare provider observations and records review. Nine checklists were developed based on the six thematic areas: two for case management, one for malaria in pregnancy, two for laboratory assessment, one for pharmacy, two for data management, and one for general observation [8]. Real-time services and procedures performed are observed and assessed, and action plans are developed to address gaps identified at the health facility [9].

There is growing evidence to show the use of OTSS in enhancing health. Data from OTSS has been used to generate practice and research evidence in most of the thematic areas [9–11]. In Zambia, for example, Worges and colleagues revealed the value of OTSS in enhancing malaria diagnostic capacity and case management practices [12]. Similarly, the OTSS approach has been shown to improve healthcare provider competence in malaria rapid diagnostic testing (RDT), microscopy, management of malaria in pregnancy, clinical case management, guideline compliance, and the provision of intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy [6].

Due to the increase in ACT production, stewardship of antimalarials must reach beyond responsible diagnosing and prescribing to include appropriate dispensing practices. Unfortunately, malaria dispensing practice is rarely studied in Ghana, with few studies found in the literature. Studies found are limited and often localized to specific parts of the country [13–16]. The pharmacy section of the malaria OTSS checklist assesses dispensing practices, pharmacy infrastructure, stock management practices, and availability of malaria commodities, providing a useful opportunity for quality improvement in antimalarial dispensing. Given the success of OTSS in improving malaria diagnostic capacity and case management, it is reasonable to conceive of an extension to dispensing practices [6, 11]. Without such improvements in sub-Saharan Africa where poor dispensing practices are widespread [13, 17, 18], counterproductive health outcomes such as the emergence of resistance are inevitable [19]. Improving dispensing practice requires understanding the current dispensing practices, sources of inappropriate practices, and how these change with time.

This is the first national-level study of antimalarial dispensing practices in Ghana using OTSS data. It addresses a gap in the literature focused on facility-level antimalarial dispensing practices by region and level of service delivery. Specifically, it aims to describe these practices and characterize the trends from 2018 to 2022.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study analyzed secondary data from Ghana’s annual OTSS visits from 2018 to 2022. Employing a repeated cross-sectional design, facility-level antimalarial dispensing practices were assessed annually. Data was extracted from the Electronic Data System (EDS) application from June to July 2023. The EDS digitizes OTSS, enhancing malaria case management by replacing paper-based tools. It enables real-time data collection, checklist-based assessments, trend monitoring, and device optimization for improved healthcare supervision. Data for the period 2018 to 2022 were extracted to assess trends and patterns of antimalarial dispensing practices across Ghana's public and private healthcare facilities. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2021 visits were conducted in 2022.

Study area

The study was conducted in Ghana, a lower-middle-income country in West Africa. Ghana’s population in 2021 was 31 million and a land mass of 238,537 km2 [20]. The country comprises of 16 regions: Ahafo, Ashanti, Bono, Bono East, Central, Eastern, Greater Accra, North East, Northern, Oti, Savannah, Upper East, Upper West, Volta, Western and Western North.

The healthcare system in Ghana comprises both public and private sectors, offering outpatient and inpatient services through facilities across primary, secondary and tertiary care levels. The primary care level serves as the initial point of contact for patients which includes Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds, maternity homes, clinics, health centres, and polyclinics. The secondary level comprising district and regional hospitals, offers more specialized care whilst the tertiary hospitals, provide advanced specialized services [21, 22]. Healthcare services also span urban, rural and peri-urban settings, including public, private, mission, and community-based providers.

Selection of facilities

Annually, facilities with poor operational performance are selected purposively for OTSS. Poor performance was determined through composite scoring of routine indicators used for all health facilities’ assessment. These indicators include testing rate, proportion of cases treated with ACT, proportion of pregnant women receiving the third dose of intermittent preventive treatment, and proportion of pregnant women provided with long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLIN). In Ghana, OTSS visits target a broad set of facilities across all regions. However, due to resource and logistical constraints, not all districts are visited each round. To maximize reach, facilities already visited in previous rounds are typically excluded from follow-up visits. This implies an implicit prioritization of unvisited or under-supported facilities when allocating OTSS rounds [23].

Dataset

The OTSS dataset contains secondary data of malaria treatment practices at selected healthcare facilities across Ghana. The National Malaria Elimination Programme of the Ghana Health Service collects the data with the EDS application. The dataset encompasses six thematic categories: case management, malaria in pregnancy, laboratory, pharmacy, data management, and general observation. This study focused on the pharmacy component, which is organized into dispensing practices, infrastructure, commodity availability, and stock management. Additional data included the level of service delivery and region of facility.

Data collection

The dataset was downloaded in Microsoft Excel format in June 2023. Data downloaded included all available indicators for the pharmacy management thematic category; 3 under labelling, 12 under adherence, 4 under infrastructure, and 3 under stock management. The individual-level data acquired from observing 3 health workers at each facility per OTSS visit were also downloaded. Data management and analysis

The unit of observation for this study was a health facility visited for OTSS in Ghana from 2018 to 2022. A total of 7834 observations encompassing data on all facilities visited during OTSS from 2018 to 2022 were downloaded in Microsoft Excel format. All observations with more than 10% missing values were deleted to minimize excessive data loss during the analysis, resulting in 7732 records. Duplicate records were assessed and cleaned leaving 7193 observations which remained valid upon further assessments for inconsistencies. The number of unique observations out of these records was 4471 (57.8%) while the rest (2722, 42.2%) were repeated measurements across the years (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Data management algorithm

Data management algorithm

Summary statistics were presented to describe the number and type of facilities in each region for each year and dispensing practice. Using chi-square tests, the associations between dispensing practice and the independent variables were explored for each of the years studied. Mixed effects logistic regression was used to predict the odds of dispensing practice simultaneously for each year and over time. The choice of this model was based on the need to adjust for dependence in the data due to repeated measurements on facilities. Fixed effects for region, level of service delivery, and time, and random effects for facilities (observations) were estimated. Two interaction terms were added, one between region and time and the other between level of service delivery and time. They were added to determine the trend of dispensing practice for each region and level of service delivery. In building the model, a null model including only dispensing practice was first fitted while estimating random effects for facilities (observations). The intra-class correlation was 0.159, suggesting significant clustering in the data. Region, level of service delivery, time, and the interaction terms were added to the model one variable at a time, and each model was compared with the previous one using the likelihood ratio test. Despite its complexity, the final model (consisting of region, level of service delivery, time and the interaction terms region x time, and level of service delivery x time) fit the data significantly better than the other models, as indicated by the likelihood ratio test (p = 0.009). The model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.85, indicating that it could correctly distinguish between outcomes 85% of the time. This was further confirmed by comparing the Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) values of the models. The final model had the smallest AIC (9520.34) which differed only slightly from the next best model (9517.63). For ease of interpretation, the adjusted odds ratios for the main effects (region, level of service delivery, and time) and average predicted probabilities for the interaction terms were reported.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was received from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC:044/01/24). The National Malaria Elimination Programme granted administrative approval for the use of the data for this study. The data was anonymized to protect the identity of supervisors, supervisees, and health facilities.

CHPS compounds (42.9%) were the most common level of service delivery where dispensing practices were assessed, while maternity homes (4.2%) were the least common. Of all the patients, 63.1% received clear prescription explanations, while 57.8% of medications were adequately labelled. However, only 40.7% of patients understood their prescriptions, 35.2% of children under five received Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) for the first ACT dose, and 31.5% were informed about potential side effects.

Table 4 presents the results of bivariate analyses examining relationships between anti-malaria dispensing practices and geographic location and healthcare level of service delivery. Anti-malaria dispensing practices varied significantly across the different years and healthcare levels of service delivery. In 2018, 6 out of 7 (85.7%) facilities in the Central region had adequate dispensing practice while no facility in the North East region (0 out of 3; 0.0%) and Savannah region (0 out of 3; 0.0%), and 1 out of 27 (3.6%) in the Northern region had adequate dispensing practice. Each year, the region with the fewest facilities showing adequate dispensing practices had small sample sizes, often including three facilities or fewer. From 2018 through 2022, the regions having the highest proportion of well-performing facilities were Central (85.7%), Upper West (70.8%), Northern (75.0%), and North East (66.0%). Health centres had a bigger proportion with adequate dispensing practice each year (56.3% in 2019, 57.8% in 2020, and 45.8% in 2022) compared to the other levels of service delivery, except in 2018 when CHPS facilities had a higher proportion (57.7%). The highest proportion of facilities with adequate dispensing practices was recorded was 2020 (50.1%) and the lowest in 2022 (41.2%). Discussion

Our analysis of Ghana’s OTSS data revealed persistent gaps in antimalarial dispensing practices. Adequate dispensing practices peaked at 85.7% in 2018 but declined to 58.8% in 2020 before rising slightly to 66.0% in 2022. By facility-level, the highest was 57.7% in 2018, 56.3% in 2019, 57.8% in 2020, and 45.8% in 2022. Four regions (Bono, Bono East, Savannah, and Upper West) had adequate dispensing practice comparable to Greater Accra, whilst three (Ashanti, Northern, Upper East) performed poorly, and remaining six (Ahafo, Central, Eastern, North East, Oti, Volta, Western, Western North) performed better. Only poorer or comparable levels of performance were observed in each of the years relative to the 2018 performance. CHPS compounds outperformed hospitals (AOR = 0.48), clinics (AOR = 0.37), and maternity homes (AOR = 0.33) in dispensing practice.

The observed low rate of adequate dispensing practices is comparable to findings from a study conducted in Pakistan, which found dispensing practices to be unsatisfactory [25]. Another study with similar findings is a survey done in community pharmacies and drug retail outlets in Ethiopia. Both identified poor labelling, inadequate counselling and frequent dispensing errors [26]. In this study, critical steps such as explaining potential side effects (31.5%) and direct observation of the first dose of treatment for children under five (35.2%) were poorly adhered to. These gaps may be due to poor knowledge of dispensing staff, many of whom are not trained/skilled dispensers, as well as suboptimal supervision [26, 27]. Ghana’s pharmacist-to-patient ratio of 1:10,000, which is below the WHO-recommendation of 1:2,000, necessitates reliance on non-professionals [28, 29].

While the primary source of drug information is standard treatment guidelines [30], their use is inconsistent among non-professional staff [27, 31]. Although the OTSS model is valuable, it may not yield the desired outcomes without increased frequency of supervision and consistent follow-up on action plans, as emphasized by Ashton et al. [6].

Studies elsewhere have found that up to 73% of patients in hospitals poorly understand the instructions given along their dispensed medication [32, 33]. This confirms findings of low rates of prescription explanations provided by health facilities in this study. Similar findings have been made in Ethiopia [26] and Ghana [13]. This can contribute to patients taking inappropriate doses, thereby increasing the risk of side effects, which will in turn reduce adherence and select resistant strains while delaying or not achieving a cure.

These findings mirror the broader challenge of infrastructure and health inequity. The significant differences in dispensing practices across the regions and facility levels suggest uneven dispensing capacity or workload across the country. The gaps in health facilities and workforce distribution in Ghana have been documented, with some parts having less-resourced facilities than expected [34].

Inversely, CHPS compounds and Health Centres outperformed hospitals, clinics, and maternity homes. This could be attributed to the personalized care provided by these facilities in smaller catchment area with community-based service delivery models i.e. home visits, community outreaches, and regular community durbars. These may contribute to better adherence to dispensing protocols.

On the contrary, hospitals, despite having more resources, including trained staff, are overburdened with large patient numbers vis-à-vis limited personnel [34, 35]. The clinics and maternity homes, which are usually privately owned, prioritize speed to minimize patient waiting time. This can compromise dispensing practice to the trend of adequate dispensing practices, the lack of evidence of improvement, as well as the worsening observed in comparison with the base year at the national level over time, and the limited progress in only 2 regions, suggests a lack of a deliberate strategy to improve dispensing outcomes. Currently, OTSS is the only identifiable national intervention with an even remote focus on dispensing. This raises questions about its long-term impact and sustainability. The current sampling technique is based on facilities’ operational performance in the ensuing year. This approach may not support a year-on-year performance improvement and may need to be reconsidered. Improving dispensing practices will facilitate the achievement of malaria elimination in Ghana. In this regard, a global trend in pharmaceutical care that recommends a re-orientation from product-centered to patient-centered care will strengthen long-term outcomes [36, 37]. Additionally, the effectiveness of interventions to improve dispensing practices, such as the Lean Six Sigma methodology [38], a context-specific educational intervention [39], or a multifaceted approach [40] could be tested in future studies.

To our knowledge, this is the first study in Ghana that assesses facility-level dispensing practices using a nationally representative dataset. Limitations however exist. In years with 0% adequate dispensing practices, regions had small sample sizes (3 or less), limiting reliability. This raises questions about the method used for selecting facilities in the regions. Ensuring a representative sample of facilities per region would enhance the reliability of regional estimates and strengthen leadership engagement. It is acknowledged that the tools used to assess facility-level dispensing practices, as well as the dichotomization approach applied to the composite scores, are not formally validated. Further, there are no established standards for analyzing dispensing data within the OTSS dataset. While this may introduce some limitations regarding the reliability of the findings, these methods provide a useful initial framework and reasonable estimates to guide further research and interpretation. Further studies should consider developing standard tools for measuring anti-malarial dispensing practice to help improve surveillance.

Conclusion

The present study described the antimalarial dispensing practices of health facilities by region and level of service delivery and characterized the temporal trends from 2018 to 2022. The rates of dispensing practices were generally low across regions and levels of service delivery in each year assessed. Eight regions in the country had similar levels of appropriate dispensing practices as the Greater Accra region, which has the highest density of health facilities and workforce. Three regions had lower levels of appropriate practices, while the four exhibited higher levels. Furthermore, dispensing practices declined in the years following 2018 compared to 2018. CHPS compounds and health centres had superior dispensing practices than the other levels of health service delivery.

Continuous investment in training, supervision, and resource allocation in facilities with poor performance in case management is crucial for equitable improvements in dispensing practices. For these facilities, strengthening dispensing staff capacity through targeted training, increasing supervisory visit frequency, and ensuring follow-up on OTSS action plans are recommended. Addressing workforce shortages may be helpful. The opportunities to explore in this vein include recruiting and deploying more professional dispensers to specialized health facilities (hospitals). For sustainability, implementing a robust system for monitoring and evaluating dispensing practices may ensure continuous quality improvement. Future OTSS visits should employ a robust facility sampling approach that captures a comprehensive view of dispensing practice. Exploring the effectiveness of context-specific educational interventions and other multifaceted strategies to enhance dispensing practices could yield valuable insights for sustainable improvement.
