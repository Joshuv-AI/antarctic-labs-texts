Epidemiologic and clinical features of Alzheimer’s disease: a hospital study based on the memory clinic in Abidjan, Côte D’ivoire, Sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Kouamé Léonard Kouassi, Frédérique Reine Emmanuella Adjeman Ehoulan, Affoué Marie Roxane Beuseize, N’guessan Yves Constant Broh, Ahya Nancy Tanya Essoin-De Souza, Nawa Samuel Yeo, Ange Stéphane Abbé, Mariam Doumbia-Ouattara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This is a retrospective 4 year, cross-sectional hospital based study describing the main epidemiological and clinical characteristics in a cohort of 36 patients with Alzheimer’s disease. The AD patients were identified out of a series of 295 patients attending a Specialized Memory clinic. The main epidemiolocal findings were a mean time to consultation of 3.3 years, equal M/F sex ratio, mean age 70.5 years, ≥ 65 years 72.3%, and had a higher overall level of education 52%. The main clinical findings as assessed by a Mean Montreal Cognitive Assessment score of 10.9, report a mean loss of memory capacity of 89.2%, loss of visuospatial and executive functions of 64% and of attention of 61.5%. The main radiological finding on MRI investigation was bilateral hippocampal atrophy (63.9%) with radiologic lesions significantly more pronounced in the elderly than in the young. On univariate analysis, moderate Alzheimer’s disease was associated with age < 75 years, higher education and cortical atrophy. A history of diabetes and cortico-subcortical atrophy were associated with severe AD. In conclusion, this study provides an initial database on this disease in Côte d’Ivoire, but also enriches the existing database in sub-Saharan Africa. The authors state that the main findings of the study are similar to those described in the literature. However, they note the predominance in young subjects < 65 years old (27.7%) and the strong association with higher education (university) of 52%. Furthermore, the authors highlight a diagnostic delay and difficulties in diagnostic management related to the costs of paraclinical investigations for this disease. Government interventions to better characterize this disease using larger sample sizes.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia worldwide, accounting for 80% of cases [1]. In Africa, AD affects 2.67 million people, the majority of whom live in sub-Saharan Africa. According to WHO estimates, the prevalence of this disease is set to rise considerably, reaching around 150 million people worldwide by 2050 [2]. The prevalence of dementia and cognitive disorders in sub-Saharan Africa varies considerably. In East Africa, prevalence rates vary from 6% to 23% for dementia and from 7% to 44% for cognitive disorders [3]. In West and Central Africa, the prevalence of dementia ranges from 0% to 10.1% in Nigeria, and that of cognitive disorders from 6.3% in Nigeria to 25% in the Central African Republic [4]. A population study carried out in sub-Saharan Africa in Ghana reported a prevalence of AD of 16% [5].

The cause of AD has yet to be identified, but part of the biological cascade leading to neuronal death is known. It involves the accumulation of abnormal proteins in the brain: the beta-amyloid protein and the Tau protein. These accumulations form the two brain lesions of the disease, called amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary degeneration [6]. These lesions are linked to genetic and environmental risk factors. Several genes, such as APP, PSEN1 and PSEN2 mutations, have been identified as increasing Aβ aggregation in AD. In addition, cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes and certain lifestyle factors also increase the risk of AD [7].

Clinically, there are two phenotypes of the disease: the amnestic form and the non-amnestic form. In the amnestic form, the disease begins with reduced memory for recent events or conversations (episodic memory) and impaired temporal orientation. In the non-amnestic form, the disease begins with behavioral changes, depression, language difficulties, orientation difficulties or visual disturbances. Over time, both types develop global cognitive deficits, various behavioral and psychological symptoms and functional impairment. Most present motor symptoms and autonomic dysfunction [8]. Diagnosis is based on post-mortem anatomopathological examination of the human brain. However, during the patient’s lifetime, diagnosis is based on clinical presentation meeting several criteria, as well as on fluid biomarkers and imaging [1]. To date, there is no curative treatment, and the disease progresses progressively and inevitably to decline [9].

The average age of onset of AD is 65, and in Côte D’ivoire, life expectancy is increasing and the population of elderly subjects (≥ 65 years) is growing [10, 11]. In the coming years, therefore, we are likely to see increasingly frequent cases of AD. Unfortunately, AD and dementia in general are insufficiently understood and remain stigmatizing in our resource-limited countries; this could be a hindrance to quality care. Limited studies have shown that stigma is rooted in belief systems, usually cultural or supernatural, where people with dementia are regarded as witches.

While the role of traditional healers, community leaders and religious healers in health promotion cannot be overlooked, there is nevertheless evidence that these community opinion leaders do not generally view dementia as a specific disease, but rather as a feature of normal aging. Sometimes, caregivers and even healthcare professionals share similar beliefs, an observation that suggests a need for education of both the general population and healthcare professionals [12].

The aim of this study was therefore to describe and analyze the epidemiological and clinical aspects of this disease in the hospital setting, in order to contribute to the evidence base needed in Africa in general and Côte D’ivoire in particular.

Patients and methods

Type and setting of study

This was a retrospective, descriptive and analytical study of patients identified in the memory consultation unit of the neurology department of the Centre Hospitalier et Universitaire (CHU) de Yopougon in Abidjan, Côte D’ivoire from November 01, 2018 to October 31, 2022.

It is also important to emphasize that this memory unit only receives patients who request a neuropsychological assessment.

Inclusion criteria

This study included patients diagnosed with major (dementia) or mild neurocognitive disorder due to probable or possible AD, as determined by DSM-5 [13] and NINCD-ADRDA [14].

The clinical criteria in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) for major or mild neurocognitive disorder due to Alzheimer’s disease, after disregarding genetic criteria, are based on:

Non-inclusion criteria

A review of patient files was carried out and incomplete files were removed.

Data collection procedure

Insidious onset was determined on the basis of anamnesis.

Neurocognitive impairment was determined by history-taking and documented using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) test. This is a practical screening tool for mild to severe neurocognitive disorders, not specific to Alzheimer’s disease. It was developed by Nasreddine et al. in 2005 [15]. It has since become widely used in clinical and research settings, often supplanting the Folstein Mini Mentel State (MMSE). It was designed, among other things, to control the ceiling effect observed with the MMSE (difficulty in detecting people with milder impairment). There are now 75 versions covering some 50 languages. It assesses attention/concentration, executive functions, conceptual thinking, memory, language, calculation and orientation. Completion time is approximately ten minutes. The maximum number of points or score is 30. A score ≥ 26/30 indicates no neurocognitive impairment.

Neuropsychological assessments are carried out in the Memory Consultation. Patients are assessed by two neurologists holding a University Diploma in Neuropsychology. This assessment is carried out once a week, in the morning. Several tests may be used, but the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is systematically used for all patients. Completion time is approximately ten minutes. The maximum number of points or score is 30.

A score < 26/30 reflects neurocognitive impairment, which comprises three levels: mild impairment (18 ≤ score ≤ 25); moderate impairment (10 ≤ score ≤ 17) and severe impairment (score < 10) [16]. It performs at least as well as the MMSE, including in screening for dementia. It has been widely translated. Although it does not replace in-depth neuropsychological testing, the MoCA correlates well with more comprehensive formal neuropsychological testing [17].

In addition, patients who had never attended school were assessed subjectively and on the basis of anamnestic data. But it only concerned one patient, who was self-taught.

The neurocognitive disorder was classified as “mild” or “major” according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) [13]. Mild or majorʺ was determined on the basis of the patient’s autonomy in activities of daily living. Autonomy was assessed on the basis of the anamnesis of the patient’s relatives on instrumental activities of daily living such as managing finances, using the telephone, shopping, cooking and managing transport, etc.

Steady progressive decline in cognition, without prolonged plateaus, was determined on the basis of the patient’s family history.

The absence of evidence of a mixed etiology was based on: a normal neurological examination, a cranioencephalic MRI that showed no secondary lesions, and a normal plasma biology work-up consisting of a complete blood count, C-reactive protein, sedimentation rate, liver, kidney and thyroid function tests, vitamin B12 and B9 levels, and HIV and syphilis serologies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was carried out using Spss version 24 software. Descriptive data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and percentage (proportion). The relationship between various epidemiological and clinical variables and Alzheimer’s disease was analyzed using the Fisher or Chi-square test, with correction for categorical variables. A univariate analysis with the Chi-square test was performed to identify risk factors for severity associated with Alzheimer’s disease. A significance level of P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to analyze AD cases observed in Côte D’ivoire, in order to contribute to knowledge of this disease on the African continent in general, and in Côte D’ivoire in particular

It revealed the following characteristics: AD affects both men and women. It predominates in the elderly, with the number of AD cases increasing with age. In addition, there was a significant proportion of young subjects under the age of 65. Most patients had a high level of education. Past history was mainly High blood pressure and diabetes. The delay in consultation was late, and the stage of the disease was moderate to severe in most cases. Radiological lesions were dominated by bi-hippocampal atrophy, and AD severity factors were age < 75, university education, diabetes, cortical atrophy and cortico-subcortical atrophy.

In sub-Saharan Africa, AD prevalence ranges from < 1% to 10.1% (0.7% to 5.6%) in population-based studies and from < 1% to 47.8% in hospital-based studies [18]. Our study reported a frequency of 12.2% and that of Napon et al. in 2009 in Burkina Faso, reported a similar frequency of 9.7% on 72 cases of dementia [19]. Furthermore, in a recent hospital-based study in Nigeria, AD was the most common dementia, accounting for 60.5% of a population of 1,216 people [20]. We conclude that the frequency of AD varies from study to study, where methodologies differ.

The lack of female predominance in our study confirms that this predominance in AD is not clearly established and varies from study to study [21–23]. The mean age of our patients was 70.5 years, with a majority ≥ 65 years, thus confirming AD as a disease of the elderly [24, 25]. In a Moroccan study, the mean age of AD patients was 64 years, with a high prevalence of subjects aged over 60 [21]. Moreover, frequency increased with age, as also reported by Niu et al. [26]. However, the frequency was low from the age of 85 onwards, certainly due to the rarity of this age group in our hospitals, and also to their low proportion at national level. Young-onset AD, which accounts for less than 5% of cases in general [27, 28], was observed in 27.7% of our study. This high proportion of young subjects should be confirmed by biological markers of AD, which could not be done for financial reasons. Nevertheless, their clinical phenotypes were characteristic, in contrast to some studies which describe atypical phenotypes [27]. Moreover, Chiari et al. noted that the typical clinical phenotype of young-onset AD is the most frequent [29]. In Egypt, Tallawy et al. reported a low rate of 7.6% of AD cases in young subjects [30]. This was a population-based study with a large sample size (12,808 people). More than half of our cases were retired. Indeed, the retirement age in Côte d’Ivoire is set at 60 for some and 65 for others. This coincides with the age of onset of AD in general. Moreover, as these people are no longer really active, they lose their economic and social power and find themselves “abandoned”, sometimes even “devalued”, with an impact on their health. The majority of patients had a high level of education; this is contrary to the literature, which states that dementia predominates in subjects with a low level of education [31–33]. This paradox could be justified by the size of our sample on the one hand, but also by the source population of our study. This population represented patients who had consulted us for memory disorders, most of whom had a high level of education.

In the history of our patients, hypertension and diabetes were the main vascular risk factors associated with AD. These are now recognized as modifiable risk factors for AD [30].

The mean time from symptom onset to diagnosis of AD was excessive in our study (3.3 years). In a country like ours, this delay could be due to insufficient knowledge of dementia or neurocognitive disorders by our populations and also by medical staff. To our knowledge, there are no awareness-raising programs on these disorders in Côte D’ivoire. As a result, a number of prejudices may arise, such as equating these disorders with normal aging, especially in the presence of elderly subjects, or with behavioral disorders, leading to referral to psychiatry, or as linked to metaphysics (witchcraft), leading patients to seek traditional care [34]. Neurocognitive disorders are also difficult to perceive at first, and only attract the attention of family and friends when they begin to interfere significantly with the patient’s daily life. Lastly, the memory consultation unit is not sufficiently well known among nursing staff and the general public.

This delay in diagnosis is similar to that reported by Chu in Hong Kong, with a delay of 3 years [35]. On the other hand, this delay is longer than that of Caucasian patients, which is one year [35], and Brazilian patients, which oscillates between 1.5 and 1.8 years [28]. This suggests a lack of awareness and perception of AD in our populations.

The predominant and severe impairment of memory, visuospatial and executive functions in our study would indicate a sporadic form of AD in almost all our patients. This form is generally manifested by memory disorders, associated with visuospatial and dysexecutive deficits [36]. Moreover, this severe impairment could be the consequence of the advanced (moderate to severe) stages of AD, as observed in our study. These stages confirm the late consultations, suggesting that our patients consult at the stage of dementia, when loss of autonomy has already begun. Furthermore, the absence of deficits on neurological examination is in favour of AD, and these appear to be in the terminal stage of the disease.

Bihippocampal atrophy was the main radiological lesion observed in our patients. Although non-specific, it is characteristic of AD and has been confirmed by atrophic changes in the medial temporal lobe associated with AD stages and mild cognitive impairment [37]. Leukopathy was the second most frequent radiological lesion in our study. It is common in the elderly, in vascular dementia and in AD. In both types of dementia (vascular and AD), the characteristics of leukopathy are similar; both diseases are considered part of a pathological continuum [37, 38]. Its prevalence differs considerably from one study to another, with rates ranging from 5% to 90% [38]. According to Hu in China, leukopathy is responsible for 25% of AD cases [39].

In the Democratic Republic of Congo, Ikanga et al. observed high rates of hippocampal atrophy and leukopathy in patients with probable AD [40]. Cortico-subcortical atrophy is recognized as part of the physiological and pathological aging process [41]. In our study, the main radiological lesions (bihippocampal atrophy, leukopathy and cortico-subcortical atrophy) were significantly more frequent in the elderly than in the young, in line with the literature [41, 42]. Indeed, early-onset AD is characterized radiologically by significant temporoparietal or posterior parietocingular atrophy, with absence or minor involvement of the medial temporal region.

Factors associated with stages of AD severity vary from study to study. Nourhashémi et al. [43] identified age and level of education in their study. In addition to these factors, our study identified diabetes and radiological factors (cortical atrophy and cortico-subcortical atrophy). Other studies have looked at factors associated with rapid cognitive decline in AD or other dementias, such as a high level of education [44–46].

Limitations

This preliminary study may be subject to recruitment bias due to its small size, since it only included records from patients who had requested a neuropsychological assessment.

Although subjects aged under 65 met our inclusion criteria, their high proportion (27.7%) would require confirmation by Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers, which could not be carried out for financial reasons (the cost of Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers amounts to over 370 euros in our context).

Strengths

Despite certain limitations, this study is the first to focus specifically on Alzheimer’s disease in Côte d’Ivoire. It thus provides a first database on AD in Côte D’Ivoire hospitals. In addition, it contributes to enriching the database on AD in hospitals in sub-Saharan Africa.

It also calls on public authorities to support the management of AD in our country, especially its diagnosis, in order to better characterize this disease.

Conclusion

This study is a first in Côte D’ivoire and one of the few in sub-Saharan Africa to address the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of Alzheimer’s disease in a hospital setting. It provides an initial database of this disease in Côte D’ivoire, but also enriches the existing database in sub-Saharan Africa. It appears that, from an epidemiological perspective, the characteristics of AD in Côte d’Ivoire are similar to those described in the literature, apart from a few specificities related to a significant frequency of young subjects and a predominance of subjects with a high level of education. Furthermore, this study highlights diagnostic delays and difficulties in diagnostic management related to the financial costs of paraclinical examinations (brain MRI and biological biomarkers). Public intervention is necessary to undertake studies with larger sample sizes to better characterize this disease.
