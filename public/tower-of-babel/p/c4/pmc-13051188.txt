Antifungal Drug Susceptibility Profiles and Molecular Mechanisms of Azole Resistance of Candida tropicalis Isolated from a Tertiary Teaching Hospital in Xuzhou
Authors: Jingfang Sun, Xinyi Liang, Yan Chen, Yi Guo, Yinhai Xu, Haiquan Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCandida tropicalis (C. tropicalis), a WHO high-priority pathogen, causes invasive multi-system infections and is associated with rising azole resistance. Given that its resistance patterns exhibit significant geographical variation, conducting regional and institutional resistance monitoring is of critical importance. Currently, there is limited research on drug resistance testing of C. tropicalis in Xuzhou, China. Here, we aimed to analyze the strain distribution characteristics and in vitro drug susceptibility profiles of C. tropicalis in a tertiary hospital in Xuzhou, and to explore the mechanisms of azole resistance.MethodsAntifungal susceptibility testing was performed on 86 clinical isolates of C. tropicalis using Sensititre Yeast one YO10 panels to determine the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs). Quantitative Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was employed to measure the expression levels of the CDR1, MDR1, and ERG11 genes. Sanger sequencing was conducted to analyze mutations at the ERG11.ResultsThe 86 clinical isolates were predominantly isolated from male patients, with the highest distribution observed in intensive care unit (ICU) departments. Primary specimen sources included urine, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and blood. Overall, 25.6% of isolates were resistant to fluconazole, with 81.8% of these showing cross-resistance to voriconazole. The expression level of ERG11 was significantly upregulated in fluconazole-resistant strains compared to susceptible and susceptible-dose dependent (SDD) strains (P < 0.05). In contrast, no significant differences in the expression of CDR1 or MDR1 were observed among the groups. None of the assessed mutations (T374C (V125A), A395T (Y132F), C461T (S154F), T769C (Y257H), G1390A (G464S)) were found in susceptible or SDD strains. In contrast, among fluconazole-resistant isolates, the A395T and C461T mutations were present in 63.6% (14/22) and 68.2% (15/22) of strains, respectively, whereas the T374C, T769C, and G1390A mutations were absent.ConclusionOverexpression and point mutations of ERG11 contribute to azole resistance in C. tropical in Xuzhou.

Introduction

C. tropicalis is a significant fungal pathogen responsible for invasive infections in clinical settings, affecting the respiratory, genitourinary, thoracic/abdominal, and bloodstream systems. It has been classified as a high-priority pathogen by the World Health Organization (WHO) in its “Fungal Priority Pathogens List” published.1 Currently, the primary antifungal drugs used in clinical practice are polyenes, echinocandins, and azoles. The clinical application of polyenes is limited by nephrotoxicity, while that of echinocandins is restricted by high cost and the lack of oral formulations. Azole drugs exert their antifungal effect by inhibiting lanosterol 14-alpha demethylase (Erg11), leading to the disruption of ergosterol synthesis and destabilization of the cell membrane. Fluconazole is widely used in the clinic due to its low cost, high bioavailability, and few side effects.

However, the rising incidence of invasive fungal infections and the widespread use of antifungal agents have led to an increase in drug-resistant strains, including multidrug-resistant ones. This situation poses significant challenges to clinical diagnosis and treatment, complicates public health containment efforts, and creates major obstacles in patient management. A multicenter study showed that the fluconazole resistance rate of C. tropicalis in the Asia-Pacific region was 9.2%, significantly higher than that in Europe (2.5%), Latin America (1.1%), and North America (2.9%).2 Research from CHIF-NET indicates that the resistance rates of C. tropicalis also vary across different regions of China.3 These geographic variations underscore the importance of resistance surveillance at the regional and institutional levels. Such monitoring is crucial for guiding patient management, antifungal stewardship, and overall public health control. Data on antifungal resistance in C. tropicalis is limited in the Xuzhou region. We therefore performed drug susceptibility testing on 86 non-duplicate C. tropicalis isolates from the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University and investigated their resistance mechanisms. Our findings are expected to provide a reliable foundation for rational antifungal therapy and effective infection control.

Materials and Methods

Strains

For the performance of the study, we selected 86 clinical isolates of C. tropicalis obtained from the Microbiology Laboratory of the Department of Laboratory Medicine, the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, between July 2023 and November 2024. The 86 C. tropicalis clinical isolates separated from different patients, which not received zole drug treatment. Duplicated isolates from the same body site of a patient at different times were excluded. The strains causing invasive infections, and recovered from urine samples with clone count >103 cfu/mL, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid samples (BALF) simultaneously detected in sputum specimen were included in this study. C. parapsilosis ATCC22019 and C. krusei ATCC6258 were used as quality control strains for drug susceptibility testing, while C. tropicalis ATCC750 served as the control strain for gene expression analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Approval No.: XYFY2024-KL178-01).

Main Reagents and Instruments

The Yeast DNA Extraction Kit and Yeast RNA Extraction Kit were obtained from Omega Bio-Tek (USA). The Sensititre YeastOne YO10 Antifungal Susceptibility Panel was sourced from Thermo Scientific (USA). Premix Taq™ and the PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit were purchased from TaKaRa (Japan). ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix was acquired from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). The MALDI-TOF Mass Spectrometer was supplied by Zybio (Chongqing, China). The PCR Thermal Cycler and the Real-Time Quantitative PCR System were procured from Bio-Rad (USA) and Roche (Switzerland), respectively. A Water-Jacketed CO2 Incubator was obtained from Yuejin (Shanghai, China). All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Methods

Strain Identification and Drug Sensitivity Testing

All clinical isolates were identified using MALDI-TOF MS. Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using Sensititre Yeast one YO10 panels to determine the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of antifungal agents. MICs were read on color change according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Interpretive criteria followed the 2022 CLSI guidelines M27M44S-Ed34 and M57s-Ed4.5 According to these guidelines, isolates were classified as susceptible (S), susceptible-dose dependent (SDD), intermediate (I), resistant (R), wild-type (WT) and non-wild-type (NWT) based on their MICs, while WT isolates without acquired resistance mechanisms and NWT isolates harboring acquired resistance mechanisms.

ERG11 Gene Amplification and Sequencing

Genomic DNA of C. tropicalis was extracted using a commercial kit. The reaction system was prepared and the reaction conditions were set according to the Premix Taq™ instructions to amplify the ERG11 gene. The PCR products were sent to Shanghai Sangon Biotech for Sanger sequencing. According to previous study,6 nucleotide mutations at the T374C (V125A), A395T (Y132F), T769C (Y257H), and G1390A (G464S) of ERG11 can lead to a decrease in the sensitivity of C. tropicalis to azole drugs. The C461T (S154F) mutation has a significant co-occurrence phenomenon with the A395T mutation. Therefore, nucleotide mutation analysis was conducted for these five mutations. Primer sequences for the C. tropicalis gene were presented in Supplementary Table 1. The sequencing results were BLAST-ed against the C. tropicalis ERG11 reference gene sequence (XM_002550939).

Analysis of Gene Expression Levels

The expression levels of the ERG11, MDR1, and CDR1 genes were assessed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Total RNA was isolated from each strain using a commercial yeast RNA extraction kit, and its concentration and purity were determined. Subsequently, RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit. Amplification of the target genes (ERG11, MDR1, CDR1) and the internal reference gene (Actin) was performed using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix, following the manufacturer’s instructions for reaction setup and thermal cycling conditions. All reactions were run in triplicate with the reference strain ATCC 750 serving as the control, and gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0, and graphical representations were generated with GraphPad Prism 10. Gene expression levels were compared using Mann–Whitney tests, with a P-value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

General Information

A total of 86 non-duplicate C. tropicalis isolates were included in this study. The majority (59/86, 68.6%) of isolates were obtained from male patients, with a higher prevalence observed in elderly individuals. These isolates were primarily recovered from urine samples (37/86, 43%), BALF (17/86, 19.8%), and blood specimens (14/86, 16.3%). The majority of isolates were obtained from various intensive care units (ICUs) (61/86, 70.9%), including the critical care ICU, emergency ICU, respiratory ICU, and neurological/neurosurgical ICU (Figure 1). Discussion and Conclusion

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a significant global public health challenge. Compared to the arsenal available for bacterial infections, clinicians are faced with a narrow range of antifungal agents to treat invasive fungal infections. The rise of drug-resistant fungal strains further complicates the therapeutic landscape, limiting effective options for patients. C. tropicalis is a prominent fungal pathogen causing invasive infections, leading to infections in multiple body sites. It is classified as a high-priority pathogen by the World Health Organization in its 2022 “Fungal Priority Pathogens List”.1 The CHIF-NET study analyzed the antifungal resistance of 3702 C. tropicalis isolates from 87 hospitals nationwide. The results indicated a continuous increase in resistance to fluconazole, reaching 31.8% by 2018,3 which has garnered significant attention from researchers in China. CHIF-NET data also revealed regional variations in C. tropicalis resistance patterns.3 These geographical differences underscore the need for comprehensive understanding and monitoring of local epidemiological trends and antifungal susceptibility to inform treatment strategies and antifungal stewardship programs for C. tropicalis infections.

Eighty-six non-duplicate C. tropicalis isolates were included in this study. The majority of cases were observed in elderly patients, indicating that this population is highly susceptible to C. tropicalis infection. This susceptibility may be attributed to the higher prevalence of underlying medical conditions, weakened immune systems, and prolonged hospital stays in this demographic. Regarding the gender distribution, male patients represented twice the number of female patients. Prior research has investigated the role of gender in invasive fungal infections. A meta-analysis of nine studies revealed that seven studies reported that males accounted for 52% to 60% of candidiasis cases.7 Furthermore, animal studies have shown that male mice are more susceptible to C. albicans infection than female mice.8 These findings indicate that male sex is associated with a higher risk of invasive candidiasis. The majority of C. tropicalis isolates were obtained from various ICUs, which may be related to the severity of illness in ICU patients, the use of antibiotics, extended hospital stays, and the frequent use of invasive procedures.

In this study, C. tropicalis primarily exhibited resistance to azole drugs, with no non-wild-type strains demonstrating resistance to echinocandins or amphotericin B. The overall resistance rate to fluconazole was 25.6%, which was lower than the 31.8% reported by CHIF-NET3 and the 32.5% reported in East China.9 Among the fluconazole-resistant strains, 81.8% exhibited cross-resistance to voriconazole. Low wild-type proportion for posaconazole of C. tropicalis was consistent with other multi-center study,3,10 but the mechanism needs to be further explored. The distribution of C. tropicalis resistant strains was generally consistent with the overall strain distribution, predominantly originating from elderly, male, and ICU patients. Fluconazole is primarily cleared through the kidneys, resulting in high urine concentrations, which may act as a selective pressure, promoting the evolution and spread of C. tropicalis resistance. However, the detection of Candida in urine is not definitive evidence of urinary tract infection due to the possibility of contamination or colonization; therefore, some resistance monitoring studies have excluded isolates from urine samples.3,9 Based on the “One Health” concept, this study included C. tropicalis isolates from urine samples for resistance analysis. Of the 22 resistant strains isolated, 14 originated from urine samples, indicating the necessity of monitoring the resistance of C. tropicalis in urine samples. Ongoing research into the resistance mechanisms of C. tropicalis is driven by its high prevalence of resistance. Azole resistance inC. tropicalis is mediated by multiple mechanisms,6,10–15 including upregulated efflux transporter genes, mainly Candida drug resistance 1 (CDR1) and multidrug resistance 1 (MDR1) genes, enhanced expression and copy number variation of the ERG11 gene, and nucleotide mutations. In this study, CDR1 and MDR1 did not show significant upregulation in resistant strains, suggesting that the upregulation of efflux transporter genes is not the primary mechanism of resistance in C. tropicalis. The ERG11 gene was significantly upregulated in resistant strains, and the A395T mutation was detected in 63.6% of the resistant strains, while the C461T mutation was detected in 68.2% of the resistant strains. In this study, most of the strains that presented A395T mutation also had the C461T mutation, consistent with other studies.6,10–12 These results indicate that the overexpression and nucleotide mutations of the ERG11 gene are closely related to the azole resistance of C. tropicalis in our region.

Several limitations in this study warrant further investigation: (1) The study’s findings may be subject to bias due to the limited sample size and short duration; therefore, continuous monitoring of azole resistance in C. tropicalis remains warranted. (2) It was not possible to differentiate between colonizing and infecting strains in the urine samples included in this study. (3) Further genetic research is required to investigate the resistant strains of C. tropicalis in order to elucidate the relationships and transmission patterns among them. (4) The gene expression-level analysis reflected the overall level of stains, no replicates for RT-qRCR may cause variable within strains, so we only analyze the overall level of stains, rather than individual stain.

In conclusion, our research indicates a high rate of azole resistance in C. tropicalis isolates within our institution. Overexpression of ERG11 and nucleotide mutations are the primary mechanisms driving azole resistance in C. tropicalis in our institution, combining the milti-center study of China.6,12 In light of the “One Health” concept, monitoring the azole resistance trends in clinical isolates of C. tropicalis is crucial for strengthening hospital infection control and ensuring the rational application of antifungal drugs.
