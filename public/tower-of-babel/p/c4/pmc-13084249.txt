Structural and morphological modulation of the myocardium by Dioscorea bulbifera saponins in experimentally induced cardiotoxicity
Authors: Olusola S Saka, Omobola A Komolafe, Oluwadare Ogunlade, Linus A Enye, Alice A Saka, Mufutau O Oladimeji, Babatunde E Arayombo, Olateju S Ayegbusi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the ameliorative potential of a saponin derived from Dioscorea bulbifera bulbils in mitigating experimentally induced cardiotoxicity in adult male Wistar rats.MethodsForty-eight rats were divided into eight groups (n = 6). Group A received distilled water, and Group B received doxorubicin (10 mg/kg). Groups C and D received SRF (50 or 100 mg/kg) for 14 days. Groups E and F received doxorubicin with SRF, while Groups G and H were pretreated with SRF before doxorubicin on day 15. Blood and heart tissues were collected for analysis after euthanasia.ResultsRats in the doxorubicin-only group (Group B) exhibited significant elevations in serum cardiac injury markers, including lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), along with increased systolic and diastolic blood pressures and elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Conversely, activities of key antioxidant enzymes—superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT)—were markedly reduced. Enhanced glycogen accumulation, Caspase-3 activation, and CD4 expression further indicated heightened oxidative stress and apoptosis. Treatment with SRF, particularly in the pre- and co-administration protocols, significantly attenuated these alterations.ConclusionThe saponin-rich fraction of Dioscorea bulbifera bulbils demonstrated substantial cardioprotective potential against doxorubicin-induced cardiac injury, likely through its antioxidant and anti-apoptotic mechanisms.Keywords: Dioscorea bulbifera, Saponin-rich fraction, Doxorubicin, Cardiotoxicity, Oxidative stress, Apoptosis

Introduction

Anthracycline-based therapies are widely employed in cancer treatment protocols. Among these, doxorubicin remains one of the most potent and effective chemotherapeutic agents; however, its clinical utility is constrained by the development of cardiomyopathy, which often proves resistant to conventional therapeutic interventions [1]. Experimentally induced cardiotoxicity may manifest acutely, typically within two to three days of treatment initiation, and can progress to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM).

The underlying mechanisms responsible for this cardiotoxicity are believed to involve excessive oxidative stress, as reflected by increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation products [2,3]. Furthermore, doxorubicin inflicts substantial mitochondrial damage in cardiac tissue, leading to enhanced ROS generation and intracellular hydrogen peroxide accumulation, both of which have been implicated in cardiomyocyte apoptosis across multiple studies [4,5].

Additional pathogenic mechanisms include the downregulation of critical genes involved in maintaining cytoskeletal integrity, sarcomere organization, and calcium homeostasis—such as α-actin, desmin, troponin I, myosin light and heavy chains, and SERCA2a. These molecular alterations collectively contribute to impaired myocardial contractility and diastolic dysfunction [6]. Consequently, the therapeutic application of anthracyclines like doxorubicin is challenged by their dose-dependent cardiotoxic effects, which can culminate in severe cardiomyopathy. Although several aspects of this adverse effect remain incompletely elucidated, accumulating evidence suggests that doxorubicin treatment significantly alters the expression of genes encoding cytoskeletal and sarcolemmal proteins in the heart [2].

Dioscorea bulbifera contains bioactive constituents, including steroids and diosgenin, is also rich in flavonoids and isoflavonoids—compounds known to exert diverse biological activities, including estrogenic modulation, cardioprotection, antioxidant action, and anticancer effects. Additionally, several studies have shown that these phytochemicals possess anti-tumor, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory properties [7].

In Asian traditional medicine, Dioscorea species are widely employed in the management of several metabolic and cardiovascular disorders. They have been reported to reduce postprandial glycemic response, promote sustained energy release, and provide protective effects against obesity and diabetes mellitus. Furthermore, Dioscorea has been utilized to manage hypercholesterolemia [7,8].

Based on this evidence, the present study aims to investigate the cardioprotective effect of the saponin with particular emphasis on its potential to mitigate experimentally induced cardiotoxicity.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The plant material was authenticated by a qualified taxonomist in the Department of Botany, Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, Osun State, Nigeria. A voucher specimen (IFE-18037) was deposited in the university's herbarium for future reference.

Animal care and experimental procedures

Forty-eight healthy male Wistar rats (170–200 g) were used in this study. The animals were randomly assigned to eight groups (A–H) with six rats per group. Prior to treatment, rats were acclimatized under standard laboratory conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, temperature 22 ± 2 °C) and provided with standard rat chow and water ad libitum.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Health Research and Ethics Committee (HREC) of the Institute of Public Health, Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, Nigeria (approval number: IPH/OAU/12/2406). All experimental procedures adhered to the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals as outlined by the National Research Council (NRC, 1996).

Drug administration

Doxorubicin was obtained from the Pharmacy Department, Afe Babalola Multi-System Hospital, Ado-Ekiti, Nigeria. The drug was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight to induce cardiotoxicity.

Preparation of the saponin-rich fraction of Dioscorea bulbifera

A saponin-rich fraction of Dioscorea bulbifera was prepared through a multi-step process. The dried and powdered tuberous roots of Dioscorea bulbifera were first subjected to solvent extraction using a mixture of methanol and chloroform (1:1, v/v) at room temperature for 72 h. The resultant extract was filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to obtain a crude saponin-rich fraction. The crude fraction was then subjected to column chromatography using silica gel as the stationary phase and a mixture of petroleum ether and ethyl acetate (9:1, v/v) as the mobile phase. Elution with the mobile phase resulted in the separation of saponin-rich fractions, which were further purified through repeated recrystallization from ethanol. The final saponin-rich fraction was obtained after freeze-drying, yielding a pale yellow powder with a saponin content of 85% as determined by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis. The saponin content was determined using a modified method of El Aziz et al. [9].

Blood pressure determination

Systolic and diastolic blood pressures were measured using a CONTEC 08A VET sphygmomanometer. Each rat was gently restrained in an upright position, and the cuff was secured around the left forelimb. The device was calibrated to ensure proper cuff fitting. Blood pressure readings were automatically recorded and displayed on the monitor [10,11].

Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) staining

Glycogen deposition was visualized using the Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) method (Fu & Campbell-Thompson) [12]. The preparation of the Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) reagent involves dissolving 0.5 g of periodic acid in 100 mL of distilled water to create a 0.5% solution, which is then mixed thoroughly to ensure complete dissolution. Subsequently, the solution is filtered to remove any particulate impurities and stored in an amber-colored bottle to protect it from light degradation. Prior to use, the reagent is equilibrated to room temperature and gently inverted to ensure homogeneity. This prepared PAS reagent is subsequently employed for histochemical staining procedures to detect polysaccharides and mucosubstances within tissue sections, providing a reliable and consistent reagent for laboratory applications.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The immunohistochemical analysis for CD4 and Caspase-3 markers was performed using a standardized protocol. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were first deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions. Antigen retrieval was then conducted using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave oven for 20 min to unmask epitopes. Following cooling and washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. The sections were subsequently incubated with a blocking solution containing normal serum to minimize nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies against CD4 and Caspase-3 were applied separately at optimized dilutions and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After thorough washing, sections were exposed to appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies, followed by incubation with streptavidin-peroxidase complex. The immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the slides were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for microscopic examination [13].

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

SOD activity in microsomes was determined using the method of Fridovich [14]. The assay is based on the inhibition of epinephrine autoxidation at pH 10.2 by SOD. The conversion of adrenaline to adrenochrome by xanthine oxidase generates superoxide radicals, and the degree of inhibition of this reaction reflects SOD activity.

Creatine Kinase–MB (CK-MB) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assays

Serum CK-MB activity was measured using a commercial diagnostic kit (Stanbio Laboratory, TX, USA), as described by Komolafe et al. [15]. The increase in absorbance at 340 nm was monitored spectrophotometrically. LDH activity was determined according to the method of Danese and Montagnana [16], following the manufacturer's instructions.

Photomicrography

Histological images were captured using an Olympus binocular microscope equipped with a 5.1 MP MV550 research camera. The system was calibrated for optimal sharpness and resolution. Images were acquired and processed using proprietary imaging software for quantitative and qualitative analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9. Data were first checked for normality and homogeneity of variances. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences among the groups. When the ANOVA results indicated statistical significance (p < 0.05), Tukey's multiple comparison test was applied to perform pairwise comparisons between groups. This approach ensured a comprehensive evaluation of the data, with results expressed as mean ± standard error of the Means (SEM) and significance levels set at p < 0.05.

Effect of saponin on cardiac markers in wistar rats

Serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity

A significant variation in LDH activity was observed among the experimental groups (p < 0.0001) (see Fig. 2A). Doxorubicin administration markedly elevated LDH levels in group B (1223 ± 11.80 U/L) compared with the control (106.8 ± 6.66 U/L). In contrast, the saponin-only groups (C and D) exhibited LDH activities (113.3 ± 6.65 U/L and 111.3 ± 6.65 U/L, respectively) comparable to control values and significantly lower than those in group B. LDH levels in the anaphylactic (E and F; 418.1 ± 5.05 U/L and 417.2 ± 8.99 U/L) and prophylactic groups (G and H; 263.0 ± 31.90 U/L and 230.3 ± 9.70 U/L) were also significantly reduced relative to group B, indicating dose-dependent cardioprotection (Fig. 2 A).

The findings indicated that saponin modulated the activity of serum lactate dehydrogenase in doxorubicin-treated rats, suggesting potential protective effects against doxorubicin-induced toxicity.

Serum creatine kinase-myocardial bound (CK-MB) activity

CK-MB activity differed significantly among groups (p < 0.0001). Doxorubicin treatment significantly increased CK-MB in group B (1141 ± 70.40 U/L) compared with the control (73.66 ± 3.45 U/L). Saponin-only groups (C and D; 80.30 ± 5.55 U/L and 75.41 ± 2.78 U/L) displayed activities similar to control. Anaphylactic groups (E and F; 251.0 ± 9.07 U/L and 252.0 ± 7.55 U/L) and prophylactic groups (G and H; 99.00 ± 0.58 U/L and 84.67 ± 2.60 U/L) showed markedly lower CK-MB activities than group B, confirming a protective effect (Fig. 2 B).

Effect of saponin on oxidative stress markers in wistar rats

Serum superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

SOD activity differed significantly between groups (p = 0.0001). Doxorubicin markedly suppressed SOD in group B (0.36 ± 0.21 U/mL) compared with control (5.00 ± 0.32 U/mL). Saponin-only groups (C and D; 4.65 ± 0.21 U/mL and 4.59 ± 0.18 U/mL) maintained near-normal SOD activity. Although the anaphylactic groups (E and F; 1.57 ± 0.29 U/mL and 1.40 ± 0.40 U/mL) showed modest improvement, their SOD levels did not significantly differ from group B. Prophylactic administration (G and H; 2.35 ± 0.22 U/mL and 2.87 ± 0.07 U/mL) significantly elevated SOD relative to group B, suggesting antioxidant restoration (Fig. 3 A).

Serum malondialdehyde (MDA) levels

A significant difference in MDA levels was recorded across groups (p < 0.0001). Doxorubicin significantly increased MDA in group B (35.16 ± 2.92 μmol/L) compared with control (9.56 ± 0.97 μmol/L). Saponin-only groups (C and D; 10.59 ± 0.41 μmol/L and 10.12 ± 0.49 μmol/L) exhibited MDA levels comparable to control, while both anaphylactic (E and F; 24.75 ± 2.06 U/mL and 25.24 ± 2.56 μmol/L) and prophylactic groups (G and H; 17.33 ± 1.45 μmol/L and 14.33 ± 0.88 μmol/L) showed significant reductions compared with group B (Fig. 3 B).

Serum catalase (CAT) activity

CAT activity also varied significantly among groups (p = 0.0047). Doxorubicin markedly suppressed CAT in group B (2.45 ± 0.33 U/mL) relative to control (12.12 ± 0.87 U/mL). Saponin-only groups (C and D; 9.40 ± 0.15 U/mL and 10.28 ± 0.21 U/mL) maintained higher CAT levels than group B. Both anaphylactic (E and F; 6.83 ± 0.71 μ/mL and 6.11 ± 0.58 U/mL) and prophylactic groups (G and H; 7.08 ± 0.53 U/mL and 7.74 ± 0.22 U/mL) exhibited significant increases in CAT activity, demonstrating partial reversal of doxorubicin-induced oxidative impairment (Fig. 3C).

Systolic and diastolic blood pressure

Significant alterations were observed in blood pressure parameters across the treatment groups (p = 0.0010). Doxorubicin elevated both systolic (168.7 ± 14.44 mmHg) and diastolic (139.0 ± 23.03 mmHg) pressures relative to control (123.0 ± 2.52 mmHg and 79.93 ± 10.34 mmHg, respectively). Saponin-only groups (C and D) maintained normal pressures (125.3 ± 14.53 mmHg/83.0 ± 3.33 mmHg and 126.7 ± 0.88 mmHg/83.33 ± 3.33 mmHg). The anaphylactic and prophylactic groups (E–H) showed significant decreases in both systolic and diastolic pressures compared with group B, indicating improved cardiovascular stability (Table 1).

Histochemical and immunohistochemical findings

Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) reaction for glycogen demonstration

Quantitative analysis using ImageJ revealed significant differences in glycogen deposition among the treatment groups. Rats in the doxorubicin-only group (B) exhibited markedly higher glycogen accumulation in the left ventricular tissue compared with the control and all other treated groups (Fig. 4). In contrast, animals receiving saponin either alone or in combination with doxorubicin showed reduced glycogen intensity, approaching control values, indicating a protective modulation by saponin.

Immunochemical detection of activated Caspase-3 in the left ventricle

Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated a pronounced increase in activated caspase-3 expression in the myocardium of doxorubicin-treated rats (group B) compared with other groups. ImageJ quantification confirmed a significant difference in caspase-3 expression among groups (p < 0.0001). Caspase-3 immunoreactivity was lowest in the control (group A, 1.11 ± 0.07) and saponin-only groups (C and D, 1.30 ± 0.05 and 1.12 ± 0.05, respectively) (Fig. 5A–C, D). Doxorubicin markedly elevated troponin I expression (group B, 6.04 ± 0.09), which was substantially attenuated in the saponin-treated anaphylactic groups (E and F) and further reduced in the prophylactic groups (G and H, 1.91 ± 0.13 and 1.78 ± 0.12, respectively) (Fig. 5). These findings indicate that saponin mitigated doxorubicin-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis.

Immunochemical detection of CD4 expression in the left ventricle

CD4 immunoreactivity was significantly increased in the myocardium of doxorubicin-treated rats (group B) compared with all other groups (p < 0.0001). ImageJ quantification revealed low CD4 expression in the control (A, 0.26 ± 0.02) and saponin-only groups (C, 0.22 ± 0.04; D, 0.25 ± 0.06) (Fig. 6A–C, D). In contrast, group B (6.85 ± 0.36) exhibited a marked elevation in CD4 expression, which was partially reversed in the anaphylactic groups (E and F) and preserved at near-control levels in the prophylactic groups (G and H) (Fig. 6). These results suggest that saponin treatment attenuates doxorubicin-induced myocardial inflammation.

Discussion

This study investigated the potential cardioprotective effects of saponins against experimentally induced cardiotoxicity in adult male Wistar rats. Various saponin formulations and dosing protocols were administered either prophylactically or concurrently with doxorubicin (DOX). The evaluation included histochemical and immunohistochemical analyses of left ventricular tissue, assessment of serum biomarkers such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and catalase (CAT), as well as blood pressure measurements to provide a comprehensive evaluation of cardiac function.

The cardiotoxic effects of doxorubicin are well established across multiple animal models and are primarily attributed to increased oxidative stress resulting from excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, lipid peroxidation, and depletion of endogenous antioxidant defenses [2,17,18]. In addition, DOX causes mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to the accumulation of ROS and hydrogen peroxide, which aggravates cellular damage [4]. Numerous studies have further confirmed that DOX promotes cardiomyocyte apoptosis, contributing to impaired cardiac function and structure [5].

Biochemical markers are essential for detecting tissue damage and monitoring therapeutic outcomes [19]. Serum LDH serves as a sensitive indicator of tissue injury, as this enzyme is released into circulation following membrane disruption [20]. In this study, LDH activity was markedly elevated in the doxorubicin-only group, consistent with previous findings by Zilinyi et al. [21], confirming substantial cellular injury. Conversely, co-administration of saponins significantly reduced LDH levels compared to the doxorubicin-only group, suggesting that saponins protect cardiomyocyte membranes from DOX-induced damage. Importantly, saponins alone did not significantly alter LDH activity, implying that their effects are specifically protective rather than cytotoxic.

Creatine kinase myocardial bound (CK-MB), a cardiac-specific isoenzyme involved in ATP regeneration [22]. The significant elevation of CK-MB in the doxorubicin-only group aligns with previous observations by Xu et al. [23] and reflects oxidative membrane damage caused by ROS-mediated lipid peroxidation [24]. Treatment with saponins markedly reduced CK-MB levels, likely due to their antioxidant capacity, which helps preserve membrane integrity and attenuate myocardial injury [25].

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) are key antioxidant enzymes that collectively protect cells from ROS-induced injury. SOD catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide, while CAT converts hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen [26,27]. In the present study, both SOD and CAT activities were significantly reduced in the doxorubicin-only group, indicating impaired antioxidant defense mechanisms and increased oxidative stress [28,29]. Saponin treatment, particularly in prophylactic groups, restored the activities of these enzymes, suggesting that saponins effectively counteract oxidative damage and enhance endogenous antioxidant capacity.

Malondialdehyde (MDA), a well-established biomarker of lipid peroxidation, was significantly elevated in doxorubicin-treated rats, indicating extensive membrane lipid damage [30,31]. Saponin co-administration markedly decreased MDA levels, particularly under prophylactic conditions, consistent with reports by Lin et al. [32]. These findings indicate that saponins suppress lipid peroxidation, thereby preserving the structural integrity of cardiomyocyte membranes [33,34].

Histochemical findings further support the biochemical evidence. Glycogen accumulation, indicative of metabolic remodeling, was observed predominantly in the doxorubicin-only group, consistent with previous findings by Saka et al. [35] and Linhart and Elliott [36]. Increased glycogen deposition may result from impaired glycolytic flux and enhanced fatty acid oxidation [37,38]. However, excessive glycogen accumulation can disrupt cardiac function [39]. Notably, saponin-treated groups exhibited reduced glycogen deposition, suggesting that saponins help maintain normal energy metabolism, possibly through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms [40].

The immunohistochemical analysis revealed that CD4 expression was markedly elevated in the doxorubicin-only group, indicating increased myocardial inflammation. This finding aligns with reports that cardiac-resident macrophages and activated dendritic cells can promote CD4+ T-cell infiltration, contributing to myocarditis and further cardiac dysfunction [41,42]. Saponin treatment significantly reduced CD4 immunoreactivity, suggesting that saponins may exert immunomodulatory effects by limiting inflammatory T-cell activation [43].

Caspase-3 expression, a key marker of apoptosis, was significantly upregulated in the doxorubicin-only group, corroborating the role of oxidative stress in promoting cardiomyocyte apoptosis [44,45]. Saponin treatment significantly downregulated caspase-3 expression, confirming its anti-apoptotic and cardio-protective potential. These effects may be attributed to the multiple pharmacological properties of saponins, including antioxidant [46], anti-hypoxic [47], calcium regulatory [48], anti-apoptotic [49], vasodilatory [50], and pro-angiogenic activities [51]. Collectively, these mechanisms may underlie the observed cardioprotective effects of the saponin-enriched from Dioscorea bulbifera against experimentally induced cardiotoxicity. The bioactive components diosgenin and dioscin are likely responsible for these effects, given their established antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [45,52].

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that it experimentally induces cardiotoxicity through oxidative stress–mediated mechanisms involving reactive oxygen species overproduction, inflammation, apoptosis, and structural myocardial damage. Treatment with the saponin, significantly mitigated these effects by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, reducing lipid peroxidation, preserving membrane integrity, and attenuating inflammatory and apoptotic responses.

These findings suggest that saponins derived from Dioscorea bulbifera possess substantial cardioprotective potential and may be beneficial in preventing or managing experimentally induced cardiotoxicity such as doxorubicin and related cardiovascular disorders. Further studies involving molecular pathway analysis and clinical translation are warranted to validate these promising outcomes.
