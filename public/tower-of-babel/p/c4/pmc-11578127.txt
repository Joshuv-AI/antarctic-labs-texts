Physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling of long‐acting extended‐release naltrexone in pregnant women with opioid use disorder
Authors: Babajide Shenkoya, Mathangi Gopalakrishnan, Ahizechukwu C. Eke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOpioid use disorders (OUD) are a major issue in the U.S. Current treatments for pregnant women, like methadone and buprenorphine require daily dosing and have adverse effects. Monthly injectable naltrexone (XR‐NTX) mitigates these adverse effects but is not recommended during pregnancy due to limited pharmacokinetic and safety data. This study developed a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model to describe XR‐NTX pharmacokinetics during pregnancy, and to predict dosing recommendations. Model predictions were successfully validated with observed data. Maternal plasma XR‐NTX profiles were simulated for 400 non‐pregnant virtual females at the approved dose of 380 mg, then randomized to continue with either 380, 285, 190, or 95 mg during pregnancy. The non‐pregnant virtual females had a mean predicted C max, AUC0‐7days, and AUC0‐28days of 23.3 ng/mL, 142 ng·d/mL, and 148 ng·d/mL, respectively. Maternal XR‐NTX exposure (AUC0‐28days) were predicted to increase by 1.37, 1.43, and 1.72 times during the first, second, and third trimester of pregnancy. However, the fetal‐to‐maternal exposure (AUC0‐28days) was lower in the first (15%), second (7%), and third (9%) trimesters. A dose of 285 mg of XR‐NTX in pregnancy during the first/second trimester and dose of 190 mg in the third trimester were predicted to provide maternal exposures that were comparable to non‐pregnant levels at the standard dose. This study provides crucial insights into XR‐NTX pharmacokinetics and proposes a dosing strategy during pregnancy, potentially aiding further clinical investigations and decision making regarding XR‐NTX use during pregnancy.

INTRODUCTION

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a significant global health challenge affecting over 16 million people worldwide, including 7.5 million adults in the United States alone. 1 Among those affected, complex populations, such as women of reproductive age and pregnant women are particularly at higher risks, with OUD accounting for at least 10% of all pregnant and postpartum deaths. 2 Currently, medication‐assisted therapy with opioid agonists like methadone or buprenorphine remain the standard of care for managing OUD during pregnancy. While these medications reduce the risk of overdose, relapse, and adverse pregnancy outcomes, their abuse potential remains a concern. 3 Additionally, trans‐placental transfer of these medications in concentrations matching maternal levels often leads to neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) in newborns. 4

Given the association of NAS with high neonatal morbidity and hospitalization rates, 5 alternative medications that manage prenatal OUD while mitigating these risks are needed. Naltrexone, an opioid antagonist, offers a unique pharmacological approach to OUD treatment. Its strong affinity for opioid receptors blocks other agonists, and its effective blockade of these receptors inhibits the euphoric and drug‐seeking behaviors typically associated with opioid agonists. Importantly, monthly administration of extended‐release injectable naltrexone (XR‐NTX) eliminates the adherence concerns with daily oral naltrexone for OUD. As a result, XR‐NTX can reverse opioid overdose, reduce physical dependence and tolerance, and facilitate full recovery from OUD, making it a desirable medication‐assisted therapy for OUD.

Naltrexone is an attractive option for patients motivated to cease all opioid use, including pregnant women seeking optimal outcomes for themselves and their newborns. 6 A recent survey among pregnant and postpartum individuals enrolled in a comprehensive OUD medication‐assisted therapy program demonstrated strong interest in naltrexone treatment during pregnancy. 7 In addition, recent data show no major congenital abnormalities in fetuses exposed to naltrexone in the first trimester, and a significantly reduced incidence of NAS in mothers on naltrexone. 8 Therefore, pregnancy presents an opportune time for healthcare professionals to support pregnant women in overcoming OUD, 9 making naltrexone an attractive option.

Despite the pharmacological and social appeal of naltrexone, its utilization in pregnancy remains low due to high discontinuation rates associated with the oral dosage form. This is attributed to its tendency to precipitate withdrawal symptoms in individuals who are currently using or have opioids in their systemic circulation. 10 Consequently, a 7 to 10‐day opioid‐free period is recommended before initiating naltrexone. Additionally, oral naltrexone undergoes extensive first‐pass metabolism, thereby reducing circulating levels and resulting in poor adherence and high relapse rates. The maternal and fetal uncertainties surrounding the withdrawal symptoms during these opioid‐free period constitute the greatest barrier to naltrexone utilization in pregnancy. Nevertheless, renewed interest in naltrexone has emerged following the FDA approval of extended‐release injectable naltrexone (XR‐NTX). XR‐NTX is well‐tolerated, has a modest safety profile, and is associated with a low incidence of maternal relapse, while also improving adherence to treatment. 8 , 11 , 12

An extensive literature search identified only one clinical study evaluating the pharmacokinetics of naltrexone during pregnancy. 8 In this study, participants were administered oral naltrexone, and both mother and fetus tolerated the drug well, with no NAS observed in newborns. Despite these findings, XR‐NTX is not currently recommended during pregnancy due to limited pharmacokinetic and safety data. Ethical concerns and the potential for drug‐related adverse events typically exclude pregnant women from clinical trials. In light of this challenge, Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models offer a powerful tool for simulating the physiological changes in pregnancy and their impact on drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. Therefore, this research aims to utilize the PBPK modeling approach to address the current gap in our understanding of the pharmacokinetics of XR‐NTX during pregnancy.

METHOD

Pharmacokinetics data for PBPK model evaluation

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across Google Scholar, Cochrane, and PubMed to gather clinical studies on plasma concentration–time profiles of intravenous (IV) or intramuscular (IM) naltrexone in both adult and pregnant populations. The concentration–time data retrieved from the studies were extracted using Plot Digitizer 2.6.6 software (Free Software Foundation, Boston, MA, USA).

Software and model development approach

The physiological parameters for the PBPK system and the differential equations governing drug disposition were implemented in Pumas® (version 2.5.1, Pumas‐AI, Inc., Centreville, VA, USA). The model development began by combining system‐ and naltrexone‐specific parameters to build an adult IV base model. These parameters were then updated for IM administration to create an adult IM model of XR‐NTX. Model predictions were validated against observed data at each step. Following validation of the adult IM model, further refinements including feminization of the model, integration of pregnancy‐induced system and physiological changes, and addition of a fetoplacental compartment to obtain a pregnancy model of long‐acting naltrexone. PBPK model for extended release IM naltrexone in adults

To adapt the adult IV PBPK model into an IM PBPK model, physicochemical properties of naltrexone hydrochloride were adjusted to those of naltrexone base, including molecular weight, logP, and pKa. This was necessary to reliably predict tissue partition coefficients for XR‐NTX, which utilizes base naltrexone instead of naltrexone hydrochloride form used in IV formulations. Subsequently, a depot compartment was introduced to model naltrexone release from the injection site into the muscle tissues. XR‐NTX, formulated as microspheres, releases naltrexone in three phases: (a) an initial release phase on day 1, (b) a hydration phase within the first week as microspheres erode, and (c) a sustained release phase through polymer erosion. Detailed equations for the absorption process are in the Data S1.

PBPK model for extended release naltrexone (XR‐NTX) in pregnant adults

Several modifications were made to the adult IM model to develop the pregnancy PBPK model. Initially, the adult model was feminized by incorporating female‐specific organs (breasts and uterus) and anthropometric characteristics. Pregnancy‐associated compartments, including the placenta and fetus were added, with the fetus lumped together as a single compartment (See Figure 2).

Pregnancy PBPK model structure. Unidirectional arrows represent organ blood flows and bidirectional arrows represent drug transport by diffusion.

Pregnancy‐induced physiological changes were integrated into the model parameters, including modifications to plasma protein, hematocrit levels, blood volumes, glomerular filtration rate, organ weights, and blood flows. These changes were implemented using published gestational age‐dependent equations derived from a meta‐analysis previously. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Plasma‐protein binding in pregnancy and the fetus was determined using the equation described by McNamara and Alcorn. 20

Naltrexone is extensively metabolized in the liver via aldo‐keto reductases (AKR1C4, AKR1C1, and AKR1C2) to 6β‐naltrexol, which is pharmacologically active and excreted by the kidneys. 21 , 22 While AKR1C4 expression was assumed constant during pre‐conception and pregnancy due to lack of data, AKR1C1 and AKR1C2 expression extends beyond the liver to the placenta, myometrium, and cervix during pregnancy. 23 , 24 , 25 These enzymes are crucial for steroid metabolism in these tissues, with studies showing that steroid metabolic activity of placental tissues may increase by up to fivefold from the first trimester to term. 23 , 26 , 27 Therefore, the placenta and myometrium were considered as additional eliminating organs in the pregnancy PBPK model. Due to the lack of enzyme kinetics data for these tissues, two assumptions were made. Firstly, it was assumed that the AKR1C1 and AKR1C2 enzyme kinetics values reported for liver hepatocytes were applicable to both the placenta and myometrium. Secondly, it was postulated that the gradual increase in steroid metabolic activity correlates strongly with increasing naltrexone metabolism. To account for the second assumption in the model, a linear gestational age‐dependent enzyme activity for placenta was incorporated as follows: (1) CLplacentaGA=1+5−140·GA·CLplacenta0, where CLplacentaGA is the placental clearance at any given gestational age, GA is the gestational age, and CLplacenta0 is the baseline placental clearance combining AKR1C1 and AKR1C2 activities.

To model naltrexone transfer across the placenta, it was divided into maternal and fetal sides to reflect their separate blood vasculature. The transfer was modeled as a bidirectional passive exchange using two independent approaches that combine drug and membrane (placenta) properties. The first approach utilized Fick's diffusion equation, where the transfer rate is proportional to the concentration gradient between maternal and fetal compartments and placental villi surface area, but inversely proportional to placenta's thickness. 28 The second approach was more mechanistic, incorporating inter‐compartmental sub‐structure within the fetal side of the placenta (See Figure 2). In the mechanistic approach, the materno‐fetal diffusion rate was derived from the optimized midazolam in vivo diffusion rate and naltrexone's membrane permeability, while the inter‐compartmental diffusion rate combined membrane characteristics with both lipophilicity and effective molecular weight 29 (See Data S1).

Model evaluation

The populations from the clinical studies were virtually reproduced, and a virtual study design replicating the original study design was implemented within the PBPK model framework. Plasma concentration versus time profiles for 100 virtual subjects were simulated, and the model's predictive performance was evaluated by visually comparing the simulated mean profile to the observed data. Additionally, pharmacokinetic parameter ratios and absolute average fold errors were calculated. The PBPK model validation criterion was met if the model‐predicted summary pharmacokinetic parameters were within twofold of the observed values.

Virtual study design

A cohort of 400 virtual non‐pregnant female individuals was generated, using anthropometric parameters representative of women at pre‐conception. Each participant received a 380 mg IM XR‐NTX dose 1 month before conception. Post‐conception, individuals were divided into four treatment arms with doses of 380, 285, 190, and 95 mg, respectively. Maternal plasma and fetal naltrexone concentrations were assessed in the first, second, and third trimesters (See Figure 3). Comparative analyses of pre‐conception and pregnancy, as well as maternal and fetal pharmacokinetic profiles were conducted to propose an optimal dose of IM XR‐NTX during pregnancy.

RESULTS

Clinical data

In the literature, only one study administered IV XR‐NTX to healthy adults, 30 while two studies involved IM XR‐NTX administration to healthy adults. 31 , 32 Plasma profiles from the FDA clinical pharmacology review document for Vivitrol (IM XR‐NTX) were also digitized for validation. 33 Notably, no clinical study currently evaluates and reports the plasma concentration versus time profile of IM XR‐NTX in pregnancy.

Naltrexone IM formulation release

While the specific amounts of naltrexone released during each phase were not reported, the cumulative release profile was documented in the FDA product submission document. 33 These data were digitized and fitted using both quadratic and trinomial functions, as well as an Emax model. The quadratic model provided the best fit based on prediction assessment and R2 value. The quadratic function is represented as: (2) Cumulative release%=−2.292+3.647t−0.0324t2, where t is time in days.

Using this function, the cumulative XR‐NTX release at the end of the initial phase (at t = 1 day), hydration phase (at t = 7 days), and sustained release phase was determined to be 1.3%, 21.6%, and 77.1%, respectively.

Adult IV and IM PBPK model evaluation

Visually, the model‐predicted concentration–time profiles were in good agreement with the observed profiles (See Figure 4). The predicted naltrexone concentrations in the adult IV model differed by less than twofold from the observed values, indicating successful validation and suitability for extension into an adult IM model. After incorporating formulation characteristics and adjusting for intramuscular administration, the adult IM model predicted naltrexone concentrations with good accuracy, with all pharmacokinetic parameters showing less than twofold deviation from the observed values. Each virtual individual's predicted concentration also fell within the acceptable 1–2 absolute average fold range (Table S1). Thus, the adult IM model was validated and deemed suitable for extension to a pregnant IM model. Detailed characteristics of the clinical study used for model evaluation are provided in Table S2 of the Data S1.

Simulations

Considering the lack of IM XR‐NTX clinical data available for validating the pregnancy model, a comparative analysis of crucial system parameters defined by gestational age during pregnancy was conducted. These parameters include body weight, blood flow, organ weights, plasma proteins, and hematocrit levels. The system parameters in the final pregnancy model closely align with those observed in pregnant women during the first, second, and third trimesters of pregnancy (data not shown).

To ensure that the virtual study subjects represented women of reproductive age, the average demographic characteristics of women at pre‐conception was sourced from literature references. 16 , 34 , 35 , 36 Based on this data, the cohort of 400 virtual females has a mean (SD) age, body weight, and height of 23.1 (5) years, 61.1 (8.9) kg, and 1.64 (0.063) m, respectively. The mean and standard deviation of gestational age (in weeks) used for simulating the first, second, and third trimesters were 8 (3), 20.5 (10), and 34.5 (12), respectively, and each trimester was bounded between 1–14, 14–28, and 28–40 weeks for the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively.

The predicted pharmacokinetic data using both the simple and mechanistic placental diffusion models at pre‐conception and each trimester of pregnancy are presented in Tables 2 and 3 respectively. Maternal plasma predictions from both methodologies are comparable, and the pharmacokinetics of naltrexone demonstrated dose proportionality across the explored dose range. Compared to pre‐conception levels, the predicted maternal plasma exposure for a 380 mg dose of IM XR‐NTX was approximately 36%, 43%, and 72% higher in the first, second, and third trimesters of pregnancy, respectively. Furthermore, the 285 mg dose showed the closest exposure levels to pre‐conception values in the mother.

Sensitivity analysis

The impact of assumptions about model parameters on fetal concentration–time profiles was examined. Specifically, the sensitivity analysis focused on myometrial and placental clearance. Using a simulated dosage of 380 mg IM XR‐NTX, the average profiles across all the trimesters of pregnancy were analyzed, with virtual subjects having a mean gestational age of 24 (8) weeks. Despite the minimal influence of both myometrial and placental clearance on maternal predictions, fetal exposure is most sensitive to placental clearance in both models (See Figure S4).

DISCUSSION

Proper management of OUD during pregnancy is crucial, as it minimizes risks for both the mother and the fetus, including reducing the potential for hypertensive diseases of pregnancy (pre‐eclampsia, gestational hypertension), preterm birth, intrauterine fetal growth restriction, low birth weight, and NAS. Effective medication‐assisted therapy for OUD during pregnancy improves maternal health outcomes and supports the overall well‐being and long‐term development of the fetus. While medication‐assisted treatments for OUD exist with methadone and buprenorphine, naltrexone is a well‐tolerated alternative that blocks opioid effects, prevents development of maternal tolerance, and aids full recovery. Several reassuring case reports regarding naltrexone use during pregnancy exist. 37 , 38 The off‐label use of naltrexone during pregnancy, whether by continuing treatment initiated before pregnancy (in well‐motivated patients) or switching to naltrexone under medically supervised withdrawal, has not been associated with any notable adverse maternal outcomes or NAS. 8 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Although XR‐NTX has improved adherence issues associated with the use of oral naltrexone, the limited understanding of XR‐NTX pharmacokinetics, safety, and dosing during pregnancy contribute to its underutilization by clinicians for the management of OUD in pregnancy. This study successfully developed a pregnancy PBPK model to describe the pharmacokinetics of XR‐NTX during pregnancy and proposed dose recommendations for XR‐NTX during pregnancy.

The adult IM PBPK model was adequately validated using clinical data from single and multiple doses of XR‐NTX in adult populations, encompassing both the approved dose of 380 mg and additional data for a lower dose of 190 mg. Given the zwitterionic nature of naltrexone, the Rodgers and Rowland method was preferred for predicting tissue partition coefficients, although other methods by Berezhkovskiy and Poulin and Theil yielded similar results. To address the multiphasic release nature of XR‐NTX, a lag‐time was introduced to the model to control the release of naltrexone from the depot compartment. A first‐order absorption model at the initial and hydration phase, as well as a transit absorption model at the final sustained phase, best fit the XR‐NTX data. The predictions of the adult IV and IM PBPK models were within the acceptable fold changes of all the observed data gathered (1.01–1.85).

Adequate steps were taken to ensure the reliability of the results despite limited data for validating the pregnancy PBPK model. The model's system parameters were validated to align with observed parameters in pregnant populations. Additionally, drug‐specific parameters influenced by pregnancy and new pregnancy‐specific parameters were calculated using previously established validated equations for various drugs. 15 , 19 , 20 , 45 Naltrexone primarily undergoes metabolism by liver cytosolic enzymes AKR1C1, AKR1C2, and AKR1C4, with minor contributions from AKR1C1 and AKR1C2, 21 rather than cytochrome P450 enzymes. Although pregnancy can alter enzyme expression, recent research showed no increase in non‐CYP drug metabolizing enzymes in liver cytosol with varying progesterone levels. 46 Therefore, it was assumed that the hepatic enzyme activities remained unchanged. However, this study included the myometrium and placenta as additional metabolizing organs in the pregnancy PBPK model due to the expression of AKR1C1 and AKR1C2 in these tissues, with their activity increasing throughout gestation. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27

A higher naltrexone exposure was predicted during pregnancy, with pregnancy and preconception AUC0‐τ ratios of approximately 1.37, 1.43, and 1.72 during the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively, at the approved dose of 380 mg. Model outputs for both preconception and pregnancy states were assessed to understand the potential reasons for the elevated concentrations observed in pregnancy. Firstly, blood flow to the muscles, where the drug is depot, increases with gestational age, 15 , 19 potentially resulting in rapid absorption. Secondly, plasma proteins were lower in pregnancy and continue to decrease as pregnancy advances, which may lead to a higher unbound fraction and consequently more circulating naltrexone if there is inhibition of the metabolizing enzymes, or the metabolic pathways become saturated. Lastly, the extraction ratio of the liver decreases with gestational age, allowing more drugs to escape the liver unmetabolized. Despite accounting for myometrium and placenta as additional eliminating organs, the sustained elevation in naltrexone maternal plasma exposure may be due to the limited involvement of AKR1C1 and AKR1C2 in its overall metabolism. Therefore, the combined effects of altered drug absorption, protein binding, and hepatic extraction may outweigh the increased metabolic activity of AKR1C1 and AKR1C2, leading to higher naltrexone levels that may quickly saturate the AKR enzymes involved in its metabolism. In a prior study of 50 mg oral naltrexone, 8 the reported maternal naltrexone concentrations were consistently higher than that reported for the non‐pregnant population by Dunbar et al. for the same 50 mg oral naltrexone. 31 For example, naltrexone concentrations were approximately 0.4 ng/mL in non‐pregnant subjects after 12 h post‐dose, while during pregnancy, levels were notably higher at 4.9, 1.4, and 0.79 ng/mL after 10‐, 11‐, and 13 h post‐dose, respectively, in pregnant subjects. Similarly, the current study, based on a pregnancy PBPK model, showed consistently higher maternal naltrexone levels during pregnancy compared to non‐pregnant individuals.

Three mechanisms of fetal‐placental transfer of naltrexone were explored: simple diffusion (SD), mechanistic intercompartmental diffusion (MID), and a perfusion‐limited (PL) model. While the results of the PL model are detailed in the Data S1 (Table S3, Figure S3), the discussion will focus on the SD and MID models, as it has been established that most drugs, particularly small‐molecule drugs like naltrexone, cross the placenta bidirectionally via diffusion. 47 , 48 At the standard dose of 380 mg approved for XR‐NTX, the predicted fetal‐to‐maternal (F/M) AUC0‐τ ratio increased with gestational age, reaching values of 0.70, 0.92, and 1.37 in the first, second, and third trimester, respectively, in the SD model (Table 2). However, the F/M AUC0‐τ ratio remained consistently lower (0.15, 0.07, and 0.09) across all trimesters with the MID model (Table 3).

Currently, there is limited data on XR‐NTX to support our fetal predictions, with the only available study being an oral naltrexone study that reported F/M concentration ratios between 0.29 and 1.08. A key limitation of this study is that maternal and fetal blood draws were not taken simultaneously. 8 In two subjects with the shortest interval between draws (30 min), F/M ratios were 0.80 and 1.08. However, since neonatal metabolism can differ significantly from that of the mother, F/M ratios may be unreliable unless samples are taken at delivery and simultaneously. This makes direct comparison with our F/M predictions challenging due to post‐delivery timing differences. Nonetheless, our SD model aligned more closely with the reported values, whereas the MID model predicted much lower ratios. The MID model, being more mechanistic, may better represent the actual physiology of drug transfer across the placenta; however, further clinical or in vitro evaluations may be necessary for confirmation.

In 2019, the Society for Maternal Fetal Medicine, American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and American Society of Addiction Medicine jointly held a workshop, where it was concluded that there was insufficient data to develop meaningful recommendations for naltrexone use in pregnancy. 49 Since then, only one study, focusing on oral naltrexone, has evaluated naltrexone exposures during pregnancy. 8 As a result, the current study presents the first description of the pharmacokinetics of XR‐NTX during pregnancy using the PBPK approach. The simulation results for the currently FDA approved 380 mg XR‐NTX show that pregnant women had higher plasma exposures of naltrexone compared to non‐pregnant women, and that these exposures increase with gestational age.

To come up with a pregnancy dose of XR‐NTX that matches exposure in non‐pregnant women, simulations were conducted with three reduced dose levels, each decreasing the standard dose by 25% down to 95 mg. The 285 mg resulted in maternal exposures that were comparable to non‐pregnant levels during the first and second trimester, with lower fetal exposures. A 50% reduction (190 mg) led to maternal day 28 concentration (C 28d) below the minimum effective concentration of naltrexone (2 ng/mL) in the first and second trimesters, 50 but above this threshold in the third trimester. Thus, a 25% dose reduction in the first and second trimesters, followed by a 50% reduction in the third trimester, appears reasonable for managing OUD in pregnancy with XR‐NTX. While the findings suggest that dose adjustments could be beneficial, further dose adaptive studies are needed to confirm the proposed dose reductions.

This study, for the first time, describes the pharmacokinetics of XR‐NTX during pregnancy using a PBPK modeling approach. It demonstrates the utility of PBPK models in assessing the probability of target attainment and identifying an optimal dosage regimen for special populations. Furthermore, the results provide valuable insights for the planning and designing future prospective investigations into XR‐NTX pharmacokinetics during pregnancy. However, we acknowledge the limitations of our study, particularly the limited data, which is typical of any bottom‐up pharmacometric approach. Specifically, there is insufficient data regarding aldo‐keto reductase enzyme expression during pregnancy, the enzyme kinetics of AKR1C1 and AKR1C2 in myometrium and placenta, and the role of transporters in naltrexone placental transfer. These limitations were addressed to the best of our ability based on the available literature at the time of writing this manuscript.

In conclusion, this work describes the pharmacokinetics of XR‐NTX during pregnancy, offering valuable insights for clinicians managing OUD in pregnant individuals and helping to inform appropriate dosing in this population.
