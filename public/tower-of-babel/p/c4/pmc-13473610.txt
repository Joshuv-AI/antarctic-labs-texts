Functional and hypoglycemic dietary fiber from Queen pineapple peel waste by ultrasound-assisted alkaline extraction
Authors: Muneera Altayeb, M. G. M. Johar, Jasgurpreet Singh Chohan, Smita D. Panchal, Vipasha Sharma, Divya Singhal, Subhashree Ray, S. Usha Nandhini, A. Bhowmik, Yalew Tamene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sustainable utilization of agro-industrial residues for the development of value-added functional ingredients has gained increasing attention; however, limited studies have explored the ultrasound-assisted recovery of dietary fiber (DF) from Queen pineapple peel, particularly with respect to its structure–function relationships. In the present study, peel waste from Queen pineapple cultivated in Tripura was investigated as a renewable source for DF extraction using an alkaline-assisted ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) technique. The extraction parameters were systematically optimized through response surface methodology (RSM), considering sonication time, ultrasonic amplitude, and solid–liquid ratio. Under optimal conditions (23 min sonication time, 47% amplitude, and 28 g/L solid–liquid ratio), the maximum DF yield reached 88.12%, which was substantially higher than that obtained through conventional alkaline extraction (65.21%). Comparative evaluation revealed that UAE-derived DF exhibited superior functional properties, including water-holding capacity (13.05 g/g), swelling capacity (7.62 mL/g), and cation exchange capacity (6.58 meq/g). The enhanced functionality was attributed to cavitation-induced structural disruption, increased porosity, and improved surface accessibility generated during ultrasonic treatment. Structural characterization through FTIR, XRD, and SEM analyses confirmed the presence of characteristic polysaccharide functional groups, enhanced crystallinity, and a well-developed porous honeycomb-like morphology. Thermal analysis further demonstrated improved thermal stability of the UAE-extracted DF. Moreover, the extracted fiber exhibited promising hypoglycemic potential through enhanced glucose adsorption capacity and glucose dialysis retardation index. Overall, the study demonstrates that alkaline-assisted UAE is an efficient and sustainable strategy for enhancing both the recovery and functional performance of DF from pineapple peel waste. Nevertheless, further in vivo studies and food application trials are necessary to validate its long-term nutraceutical potential and industrial applicability.

Introduction

The rapid growth of the food processing industry has generated substantial quantities of agro-industrial waste, creating major environmental and sustainability concerns. In recent years, food waste valorization has received significant attention as a sustainable approach for converting underutilized biomass into value-added functional ingredients and nutraceuticals1. Such strategies support circular bioeconomy strategies by improving resource efficiency, minimizing waste generation, and reducing environmental pollution2. Furthermore, the utilization of fruit-processing residues aligns with several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly responsible consumption and production (SDG 12), climate action (SDG 13), and sustainable industrial innovation (SDG 9). Therefore, the recovery of valuable bioactive compounds from agro-industrial by-products has emerged as an important research area for sustainable food system development3. The increasing focus on sustainable food systems and the circular bioeconomy has stimulated significant interest in converting agro-industrial residues into high-value functional ingredients. Ananas comosus, a member of the Bromeliaceae family, is a widely cultivated tropical fruit valued for its high nutritional content, desirable sensory properties, and significant health-promoting properties, including antioxidant and blood glucose–lowering effects4. As reported by FAO, global pineapple production surpasses 26 million tonnes per year, resulting in a large volume of processing waste, including peels, cores, crowns, and pomace. These residues constitute nearly 50% of the total fruit mass and, if not managed effectively, can lead to serious environmental issues5,6. Hence, the valorization of pineapple waste is crucial for promoting both environmental sustainability and economic viability.

Commonly regarded as agro-industrial waste, pineapple peel is in fact a rich source of valuable bioactive compounds, including dietary fiber, complex polysaccharides, essential minerals, vitamins, and enzymes7,8. Among these components, dietary fiber (DF) has attracted considerable interest because of its significant contribution to human health and well-being. Dietary fiber represents the non-digestible component of plant-derived foods that is not degraded by enzymes in the small intestine and is instead partially or fully fermented by microorganisms in the large intestine9. Dietary fiber is broadly classified into two types—soluble dietary fiber (SDF) and insoluble dietary fiber (IDF)—with each type providing unique physiological benefits. SDF plays a key role in regulating post-meal blood glucose levels, lowering serum cholesterol, and supporting beneficial gut microbiota, while IDF aids in increasing stool bulk and mitigating conditions such as constipation, obesity, and colorectal disorders10,11. Because of its beneficial functional characteristics and positive effects on health, dietary fiber is extensively incorporated as a key component in the formulation of functional food products.

The extraction technique plays a crucial role in determining the yield, chemical composition, and functional properties of dietary fiber. Traditional approaches, including chemical, thermal, and enzymatic methods, have been widely employed; however, they are often associated with prolonged processing time, high solvent consumption, and possible degradation of heat-sensitive bioactive compounds11. These limitations have encouraged the development of more efficient and environmentally sustainable extraction technologies. Among the emerging techniques, ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has gained considerable attention because of its eco-friendly nature, shorter extraction time, reduced solvent requirement, and improved extraction efficiency12,13. The effectiveness of UAE is primarily attributed to acoustic cavitation, where the formation and collapse of microbubbles generate localized shear forces, turbulence, and microjets that disrupt plant cell walls, enhance solvent penetration, and accelerate mass transfer. Such cavitation-induced effects can also modify the structural properties of dietary fiber by increasing porosity, reducing particle size, and improving surface accessibility, thereby enhancing its functional performance. In recent years, several advanced extraction methods, including microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), enzyme-assisted extraction (EAE), subcritical water extraction (SWE), and UAE, have been explored for recovering bioactive compounds from agro-industrial residues14. MAE provides rapid heating but may negatively affect heat-sensitive constituents, whereas EAE offers high selectivity under mild conditions but often requires longer processing time and higher operational cost. SWE exhibits high extraction efficiency; however, the requirement for elevated temperature and pressure may limit its industrial applicability15. Compared with these techniques, UAE offers a favorable balance between extraction efficiency, operational simplicity, and sustainability, making it a promising approach for dietary fiber recovery from plant-based waste materials16.

The choice of raw material is an important factor influencing the quality and functionality of dietary fiber. In this study, Queen pineapple peel from Tripura, India, was selected because of its regional abundance and commercial importance17,18. Despite the wide cultivation and processing of this pineapple variety, its peel waste remains underutilized, particularly for dietary fiber recovery using advanced extraction techniques such as UAE. The valorization of this agro-industrial by-product therefore offers both scientific and sustainability significance. In addition, dietary fiber is recognized for its role in regulating postprandial glucose levels by slowing glucose diffusion and enhancing intestinal viscosity19. Parameters such as glucose adsorption capacity (GAC) and glucose dialysis retardation index (GDRI) are commonly used to evaluate the hypoglycemic potential of dietary fiber20. Therefore, improving the functional characteristics of dietary fiber through suitable extraction approaches may enhance its applicability in functional food formulations targeting metabolic health.

Although several studies have reported the recovery of dietary fiber from fruit processing by-products such as citrus peel, mango peel, and commercial pineapple residues, research specifically focused on Queen pineapple peel remains extremely limited, particularly in the context of ultrasound-assisted extraction and structure–function characterization. Existing studies have primarily emphasized conventional extraction approaches with limited attention to process optimization, microstructural modification, and functional performance of the recovered fiber. Moreover, a comprehensive comparative evaluation between ultrasound-assisted extraction and conventional alkaline extraction in terms of extraction efficiency, physicochemical properties, structural transformation, and hypoglycemic potential has not yet been systematically reported for Queen pineapple peel. Therefore, the novelty of the present study lies in the integrated application of alkaline-assisted ultrasound extraction combined with response surface methodology (RSM) optimization for efficient dietary fiber recovery from Queen pineapple peel waste generated in Tripura. Furthermore, the study establishes detailed structure–function relationships through comprehensive physicochemical, structural, thermal, and hypoglycemic characterization of the extracted fiber and compares its performance with conventionally extracted dietary fiber. The findings are expected to provide new scientific insights into the valorization of underutilized pineapple processing waste for the development of sustainable functional food ingredients while simultaneously contributing to agro-industrial waste management and circular bioeconomy strategies.

Materials and methods

Raw materials and chemicals

Fully ripened Queen variety pineapples, harvested at optimal maturity, were obtained from a certified agricultural farm in Tripura, India—an area recognized for its conducive agro-climatic conditions for cultivating this variety17. This particular cultivar was selected owing to its pronounced sweetness, characteristic aroma, and rich compositional profile, which are likely to enhance the functional properties of the derived dietary fiber. Prior to processing, the fruits were thoroughly washed to eliminate surface impurities, after which the peels were manually separated. The collected peels were then subjected to drying in a controlled hot air oven maintained at 40 °C until a constant (equilibrium) moisture level was attained, thereby minimizing the loss of heat-sensitive constituents. Subsequently, the dried material was finely ground using a laboratory grinder and passed through a 100-mesh sieve to ensure uniform particle size distribution. The resulting dried powder (DP) was further defatted using petroleum ether to remove residual lipophilic substances that could interfere with subsequent analyses. The treated samples were then stored in airtight containers under refrigerated conditions until further use. All reagents and chemicals employed in the study, including hydrochloric acid (HCl), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ethanol, and other analytical-grade solvents, were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals Pvt. Ltd., ensuring adherence to standard analytical practices.

Extraction of dietary fiber

Conventional alkaline extraction

Dietary fiber was extracted using a modified conventional alkaline extraction method adapted from previously reported studies8,21. Briefly, defatted pineapple peel powder was dispersed in 0.5 M NaOH solution (pH ≈ 13.7) at a fixed solid-to-liquid ratio to ensure consistent solid loading throughout all experiments. The suspension was continuously agitated using a magnetic stirrer at 1000 rpm for 35 min at 50 °C to maintain homogeneous mixing and uniform exposure of the biomass particles to the alkaline medium. The extraction duration was slightly extended compared to the original protocol to promote enhanced solubilization of non-fibrous components and improve extraction uniformity.

Following alkaline treatment, the slurry was neutralized using 0.5 M HCl. Subsequently, 85% (v/v) ethanol was added in a 2:1 ratio to facilitate dietary fiber precipitation. The mixture was allowed to stand undisturbed for 1 h to ensure complete precipitation, followed by centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 30 min to separate the fiber-rich precipitate. The recovered residue was then dried overnight at 40 °C, designated as alkali-extracted dietary fiber (AEDF), and stored under refrigerated conditions for subsequent physicochemical and structural analyses.

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE)

Ultrasound-assisted extraction of dietary fiber was performed based on previously reported methods with slight modifications to improve extraction efficiency and process reproducibility22. Briefly, defatted pineapple peel powder was dispersed in 0.5 M NaOH solution and subjected to treatment using a probe-type ultrasonicator (Q700-220 Digital Sonicator, Qsonica LLC, USA) operating at a fixed frequency of 20 kHz with a maximum nominal power output of 700 W. The ultrasonicator was equipped with a titanium alloy probe of 13 mm diameter, providing high-intensity acoustic cavitation during extraction. The synergistic effect of alkaline solubilization and ultrasonic cavitation facilitated enhanced disruption of the lignocellulosic matrix and improved dietary fiber release.

The extraction variables, including sonication time (10–30 min), ultrasonic amplitude (20–50%), and solid-to-liquid ratio (10–30 g/L), were systematically optimized through response surface methodology. Ultrasonic energy delivery was regulated through controlled amplitude variation and processing duration to ensure consistent cavitation intensity and reproducible extraction conditions. During sonication, the extraction temperature was carefully maintained below 60 °C using intermittent pulse sonication along with an external ice-bath cooling system to minimize thermal degradation of fiber constituents. Following ultrasonication, the suspension was neutralized using 0.5 M HCl, and ethanol precipitation and centrifugation were subsequently performed following the same procedure as conventional alkaline extraction. The recovered residue was then dried at 40 °C to obtain ultrasound-assisted extracted dietary fiber (UAEDF), which was stored in airtight containers for further physicochemical and structural characterization.

Experimental design and optimization

The ultrasound-assisted extraction conditions were optimized using response surface methodology (RSM) based on a Box–Behnken design (BBD), following previously established methodologies reported in the literature23. Three independent process variables, namely sonication time (A), ultrasonic amplitude (B), and solid-to-liquid ratio (C), were selected and evaluated at three different levels to determine their influence on dietary fiber extraction yield (%). A total of 17 experimental runs were generated by the BBD matrix and performed in randomized order to minimize experimental bias. Proximate composition analysis

The chemical composition of pineapple peel powder and its extracted dietary fiber was evaluated using standard procedures recommended by the AOAC24. Key components, including moisture, crude protein, crude fat, ash, and crude fiber, were systematically measured, while the total carbohydrate content was calculated using the difference method.

Structural characterization

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR characterization was conducted using a Nicolet 410 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) to examine the functional groups present in the samples. The spectra were collected over a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm⁻1 at a resolution of 4 cm⁻1, enabling the identification of characteristic bonding features associated with dietary fiber constituents8.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The surface characteristics and microstructural alterations of pineapple peel powder, AEDF, and UAEDF were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (JEOL JSM-6390LV, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Micrographs at various magnifications were obtained to assess the structural transformations resulting from ultrasonication.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD analysis was carried out using a Bruker D8 FOCUS diffractometer (Germany) to evaluate the relative proportions of crystalline and amorphous phases present in the dietary fiber. The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 5° to 80°, and the crystallinity index was evaluated using established analytical methods21.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermal stability of the samples was assessed using TGA performed on a NETZSCH TG 209F1 Libra instrument (Germany). The analysis was carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere within a temperature range of 30–600 °C, maintaining a uniform heating rate of 10 °C/min25.

Functional properties of dietary fiber

The functional characteristics of the material—namely water-holding capacity (WHC), oil-holding capacity (OHC), swelling capacity (SC), cation exchange capacity (CEC), emulsion activity (EA), and emulsion stability (ES)—were evaluated following established standard procedures26–28. These parameters serve as key indicators in assessing the suitability and performance of dietary fiber in various food applications.

Evaluation of hypoglycemic activity

Glucose adsorption capacity (GAC)

The glucose adsorption capacity was evaluated following the procedure reported by Peerajit et al.29. In this method, the samples were exposed to glucose solutions with concentrations ranging from 1 to 200 mM, and the extent of glucose adsorption was measured after the incubation period.

Glucose dialysis retardation index (GDRI)

The GDRI was determined based on the procedure reported by Nsor-Atindana et al.20. This method involves monitoring the rate of glucose diffusion across a dialysis membrane over specified time intervals in the presence of dietary fiber, thereby evaluating its ability to slow down glucose absorption.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and the obtained results are presented as mean ± standard deviation to demonstrate experimental variability and reproducibility. Statistical analysis of the experimental data was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to determine the significance of differences among treatment groups at a confidence level of 95% (p < 0.05). For post hoc multiple comparison analysis, Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) was employed to identify statistically significant differences between mean values. Duncan’s test was selected because it is widely adopted in food science and agricultural research for its higher sensitivity in detecting treatment differences among multiple experimental groups while maintaining acceptable control of Type I error. Compared with more conservative methods such as Tukey’s or Bonferroni correction, Duncan’s test provides improved discrimination among closely related treatment means, which was considered appropriate for the present experimental design. Furthermore, statistical reliability and consistency of the experimental observations were assessed through standard deviation analysis and graphical error representations wherever applicable. The relatively low variability among replicate measurements confirmed the reproducibility and reliability of the experimental procedures and analytical methods employed in this study.

Results and discussion

Optimization of UAE parameters

Experimental design and effect of process variables

The process parameters for alkaline-assisted ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) were optimized to enhance DF recovery from Queen pineapple peel waste using response surface methodology (RSM) integrated with a Box–Behnken design. Three key factors—sonication time (A), ultrasonic amplitude (B), and solid-to-liquid ratio (C)—were selected based on their influence on cavitation intensity, alkaline-assisted solubilization, and mass transfer efficiency during extraction23. A total of 17 experimental trials were conducted to systematically evaluate the individual and combined effects of these variables on DF yield. Table 1 clearly indicates that sonication time strongly influenced the DF yield. Based on the RSM response surface analysis, a notable increase in yield was predicted when the sonication duration increased from 10 min toward the optimized region of approximately 22–25 min. This improvement may be attributed to intensified cavitation effects that enhanced cell wall disruption, solvent penetration, and mass transfer efficiency during extraction. Such behavior is associated with improved disruption of lignocellulosic structures under moderate cavitation intensity. However, excessive ultrasonication at prolonged durations could partially degrade fiber components, thereby limiting further enhancement in extraction yield. Similar trends have been reported for ultrasound-assisted extraction of dietary fibers from fruit by-products11,13. Although 30 min was included as an experimental level in the Box–Behnken design, extending the sonication time beyond the optimized region did not produce proportional improvement in DF yield and, in some cases, resulted in slight reduction, possibly due to degradation of fiber structures under prolonged ultrasonic exposure13.

In a similar manner, ultrasonic amplitude significantly influenced extraction efficiency. The RSM model predicted that moderate amplitude conditions within the range of approximately 35–47% favored improved DF recovery due to enhanced cavitation intensity and structural disruption of plant tissues. However, amplitudes approaching 50% did not consistently improve the extraction yield and occasionally resulted in slight reduction, suggesting that excessive ultrasonic intensity may adversely affect fiber integrity. This trend is consistent with earlier reported studies. The optimized extraction conditions predicted by the developed RSM model were 23 min sonication time, 47% ultrasonic amplitude, and 28 g/L solid-to-liquid ratio, which produced a validated DF yield of 88.12%, closely matching the predicted value (87.85%). The solid-to-liquid ratio was another important factor affecting extraction performance. Optimal yields were achieved at moderate ratios (20–30 g/L), where solvent penetration and diffusion were more effective. However, ratios outside this range resulted in lower extraction efficiency, possibly due to limited mass transfer and inadequate interaction between the solvent and the material11.

Among the investigated variables, sonication time exhibited the most significant influence on DF yield, followed by ultrasonic amplitude and solid-to-liquid ratio. The strong contribution of sonication time may be attributed to prolonged exposure of the biomass matrix to ultrasonic cavitation, which enhances disruption of cell wall structures and facilitates improved release of dietary fiber components into the extraction medium. Similarly, ultrasonic amplitude governs cavitation intensity and shear force generation, thereby improving solvent penetration and mass transfer efficiency. In contrast, the solid-to-liquid ratio primarily influences solvent accessibility and concentration gradients during extraction.

The quadratic terms (A2, B2, and C2) were statistically significant, confirming the presence of nonlinear extraction behavior and supporting the curvature observed in the response surface plots. However, the interaction terms (AB, AC, and BC) were statistically insignificant (p > 0.05), indicating that the investigated process variables did not exhibit strong interaction effects within the selected experimental range. Therefore, the extraction response was predominantly governed by the independent linear and quadratic contributions of the individual process parameters rather than synergistic interactions among them.

The relatively weak interaction behavior may be explained by the distinct mechanistic role of each parameter in the extraction process. Sonication time primarily determines cavitation exposure duration, ultrasonic amplitude regulates acoustic energy intensity, and solid-to-liquid ratio affects solvent diffusion and contact efficiency. Since these mechanisms operate comparatively independently under the investigated operating conditions, statistically significant interaction effects were not prominently observed.

In addition, the non-significant lack-of-fit result confirmed that the developed model adequately represented the experimental system and was suitable for prediction and optimization purposes. The optimum UAE conditions predicted by the developed model were 23 min sonication time, 47% ultrasonic amplitude, and 28 g/L solid-to-liquid ratio. Under these optimized conditions, the experimentally validated DF yield reached 88.12%, which closely matched the predicted value (87.85%), thereby confirming the robustness and predictive accuracy of the developed RSM model.

Chemical composition of extracted dietary fiber

The compositional characteristics of Queen pineapple peel powder, alkali-extracted dietary fiber, and ultrasound-assisted extracted dietary fiber were evaluated to determine the influence of different extraction approaches on dietary fiber enrichment and compositional modification. Among the investigated samples, UAEDF exhibited the highest total dietary fiber content (88.12 g/100 g), which was substantially greater than that of AEDF (65.21 g/100 g). This enhancement demonstrates the superior efficiency of ultrasound-assisted alkaline extraction in recovering fiber-rich fractions from lignocellulosic biomass. Similar increases in dietary fiber yield following ultrasound-assisted treatment have also been reported for pineapple pomace, citrus peel, and papaya peel fibers, where cavitation-assisted disruption significantly improved extraction efficiency and polysaccharide recovery8,11,30.

The increased TDF content in UAEDF may be attributed to the synergistic effects of alkaline solubilization and ultrasonic cavitation. Alkaline treatment facilitates the removal of lignin, proteins, and other non-fibrous components, whereas ultrasonic cavitation generates localized shear forces, microjets, and turbulence capable of disrupting the plant cell wall matrix. This process weakens intermolecular hydrogen bonding within lignocellulosic structures and promotes partial depolymerization of matrix polysaccharides such as hemicellulose and pectin. Consequently, bound soluble polysaccharides become more accessible and extractable, leading to enrichment of soluble dietary fiber fractions.

A substantial increase in insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) content was observed in UAEDF (83.90 g/100 g) compared to AEDF (63.10 g/100 g). This increase may be associated with enhanced recovery of cellulose-rich structural polysaccharides following efficient removal of soluble non-fiber constituents during ultrasound-assisted processing. Comparable increases in IDF have been reported in ultrasound-treated fruit byproduct fibers due to improved separation of cellulose and hemicellulose fractions from surrounding matrix materials. Furthermore, the soluble dietary fiber (SDF) fraction also increased in UAEDF, which may be linked to ultrasonic disruption of pectic substances and partial hydrolysis of hemicellulosic components into lower-molecular-weight soluble fractions. Such structural modifications are known to improve hydration behavior and adsorption-related functional properties of dietary fibers.

The moisture content of both AEDF and UAEDF decreased noticeably compared with raw DP, with UAEDF showing the lowest moisture level. Reduced moisture content is advantageous for improving storage stability, minimizing microbial deterioration, and extending shelf life. Similar moisture reductions have been reported in ultrasound-assisted extraction systems due to enhanced removal of free and bound water during cavitation-assisted processing. In addition, crude protein and fat contents were significantly reduced after extraction, with UAEDF exhibiting lower values than AEDF. The reduction in protein content may be attributed to alkaline-induced solubilization and denaturation of proteinaceous materials, which are further enhanced by ultrasonic cavitation and mechanical disruption. Likewise, the decrease in lipid content can be associated with improved solvent penetration and enhanced release of lipid fractions during ultrasound treatment. Comparable compositional reductions have been reported in ultrasound-assisted extraction studies involving fruit and vegetable byproducts, indicating greater purification efficiency of the extracted dietary fiber fraction30. These observations suggest that UAE not only increases fiber recovery but also improves extraction selectivity toward fiber-rich components.

The ash content of AEDF and UAEDF was higher than that of raw DP, indicating concentration of mineral constituents after removal of organic non-fibrous materials during extraction. However, UAEDF showed slightly lower ash content than AEDF, possibly due to more effective removal of inorganic residues during ultrasonic processing. Structural characterization of extracted dietary fiber

The DF obtained from Queen pineapple peel waste was structurally characterized using FTIR, SEM, XRD, and TGA analyses. These characterization techniques collectively offer detailed information on the functional groups present, surface morphology, crystalline structure, and thermal behavior of the samples. Such insights are essential for evaluating the suitability of the extracted fiber for potential applications in functional food formulations.

FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis was performed to identify the major functional groups present in pineapple peel-derived DP, AEDF, and UAEDF, and the corresponding spectra are presented in Fig. 3. All samples exhibited characteristic absorption bands typical of polysaccharide-rich lignocellulosic materials. A broad absorption band centered around ~ 3400 cm⁻1 was observed in all spectra, corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations associated with hydrogen bonding among cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectic polysaccharides. Compared with DP and AEDF, UAEDF exhibited a slight broadening and minor shift in this band, which may indicate alterations in intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions caused by ultrasonic cavitation during extraction. SEM analysis

The surface morphology of DP, AEDF, and UAEDF was examined using scanning electron microscopy, and the corresponding micrographs are presented in Fig. 4. The untreated pineapple peel powder exhibited a relatively compact and dense surface morphology with limited visible pores and a comparatively smooth structural arrangement, indicating the presence of an intact lignocellulosic matrix. Following alkaline extraction, AEDF displayed partial disruption of the compact structure along with the appearance of fibrous fragments and surface irregularities, suggesting removal of non-fibrous components and partial degradation of cell wall constituents during alkaline treatment. More pronounced morphological modifications were observed in UAEDF, which exhibited a highly irregular, porous, and honeycomb-like surface architecture characterized by visible cracks, cavities, and disrupted fibrous networks. These structural changes are likely associated with the combined effects of alkaline swelling and ultrasonic cavitation. During ultrasonication, the formation and collapse of cavitation bubbles generate localized shear forces, microstreaming, and mechanical shock waves capable of disrupting the lignocellulosic matrix. Such cavitation-induced stresses may weaken intermolecular associations among cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin components, thereby promoting formation of microchannels and increased structural disintegration within the fiber matrix.Figure 4SEM micrographs showing morphology of (a) DP, (b) AEDF, and (c) UAEDF.

Functional properties of extracted dietary fiber

The functional properties of dietary fiber are critical determinants of its applicability in food formulations, particularly with respect to hydration behavior, lipid interaction, emulsification performance, and ion-binding capacity. In the present study, important functional parameters including water-holding capacity (WHC), oil-holding capacity (OHC), swelling capacity (SC), emulsifying activity (EA), emulsion stability (ES), and cation exchange capacity (CEC) were evaluated for pineapple peel-derived DP, AEDF, and UAEDF samples. Among all samples, UAEDF consistently demonstrated superior functional performance, indicating that ultrasound-assisted extraction substantially modified the physicochemical behavior of the dietary fiber matrix.

The observed improvements cannot be attributed solely to increased porosity and surface area. Ultrasound-assisted treatment likely induced disruption of lignocellulosic interactions and partial rearrangement of polysaccharide networks, resulting in enhanced accessibility of hydrophilic functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl moieties. In addition, the altered balance between SDF and IDF fractions appears to play a significant role in determining the functional behavior of the extracted fiber. The increased SDF fraction in UAEDF may contribute to greater hydration, gel-forming ability, and water affinity, whereas the elevated IDF fraction provides structural rigidity, adsorption sites, and improved bulk characteristics.

WHC is an important parameter that reflects the ability of dietary fiber to retain water against external forces such as centrifugation or compression. In this study, UAEDF exhibited substantially higher WHC (13.05 g/g) compared with AEDF (8.72 g/g) and raw DP (6.42 g/g). This enhancement may be associated with increased accessibility of hydrophilic groups and loosening of the compact lignocellulosic matrix following ultrasonic cavitation. The partial disruption of hemicellulose–cellulose interactions likely exposed additional hydroxyl-containing sites capable of forming hydrogen bonds with water molecules. Furthermore, the increase in SDF content may have contributed to improved water retention because soluble polysaccharides possess greater hydration and gel-forming tendencies. Higher WHC is particularly advantageous in food systems as it contributes to improved texture, moisture retention, processing stability, and shelf-life extension32.

Similarly, UAEDF demonstrated markedly improved OHC, increasing from 3.62 g/g in AEDF to 5.35 g/g. This enhancement may be linked not only to increased porosity but also to modifications in fiber conformation and exposure of nonpolar regions within the polysaccharide matrix. Structural disruption induced by ultrasonication may create additional capillary spaces and adsorption sites capable of physically entrapping oil molecules. Moreover, the higher IDF content in UAEDF may contribute to improved lipid retention due to the rigid and porous structural framework of insoluble fiber components. Enhanced OHC is beneficial in food applications such as meat products, bakery formulations, and fat-rich food systems where oil retention contributes to flavor preservation and texture improvement.

The swelling capacity of UAEDF (7.62 mL/g) was also significantly greater than that of AEDF (4.35 mL/g). Swelling behavior is strongly influenced by the hydration properties of polysaccharides and the ability of fiber networks to absorb and retain water within their structure. The increase in SC may therefore be associated with both increased SDF content and greater exposure of hydrophilic functional groups following ultrasound-assisted extraction. Partial depolymerization of hemicellulose and pectin fractions may have increased matrix flexibility and facilitated greater volumetric expansion upon hydration27. Such enhanced swelling behavior is important for promoting satiety, regulating intestinal transit, and improving digestive health.

Both emulsifying activity and emulsion stability improved after extraction, with UAEDF exhibiting comparatively better performance than AEDF and DP. Nevertheless, the EA and ES values remained below 50%, indicating relatively limited emulsifying capability of the extracted dietary fibers. This behavior may primarily be attributed to the low residual protein content of the extracted materials, as proteins are generally responsible for stabilizing oil–water interfaces during emulsion formation33. However, the slight improvement observed in UAEDF may result from increased surface activity and improved hydration behavior associated with structural modification of the fiber matrix.

CEC, which reflects the ion-binding ability of dietary fiber, was markedly higher in UAEDF (6.58 meq/g) than in AEDF and DP. This enhancement may be associated with greater accessibility of negatively charged functional groups such as carboxyl and phenolic moieties following ultrasonic disruption of the fiber structure. The increase in SDF content may also contribute to improved ion-exchange behavior because soluble polysaccharides often contain more accessible charged groups capable of interacting with ions in aqueous systems. Elevated CEC values suggest improved potential for bile acid binding, cholesterol reduction, and adsorption of undesirable metal ions or toxins34.

Overall, the results indicate that ultrasound-assisted extraction substantially enhances the functional behavior of dietary fiber through interconnected compositional and structural modifications. The simultaneous increase in SDF and IDF fractions, disruption of lignocellulosic interactions, improved accessibility of hydrophilic functional groups, and development of porous microstructures collectively contribute to the enhanced hydration, adsorption, and ion-binding properties of UAEDF.Figure 7Functional properties of DP, AEDF, and UAEDF.

These findings highlight the strong structure–function relationship governing the performance of ultrasound-modified dietary fiber and support the potential application of UAEDF in functional foods, nutraceutical products, and health-oriented formulations. The improved hydration behavior, lipid interaction, and ion-binding capacity may be particularly beneficial in bakery, dairy, and meat-based food systems as well as in dietary formulations targeting digestive health and metabolic regulation. Overall, UAE represents an efficient and sustainable strategy for simultaneously improving dietary fiber recovery and functional quality from pineapple peel waste.

Hypoglycemic activity of extracted dietary fiber

The hypoglycemic efficacy of DF is an important functional property that influences its application in the development of health-focused food products. In the present study, the hypoglycemic behavior of pineapple peel-derived DP, AEDF, and UAEDF was assessed through GAC and GDRI. These in vitro analytical approaches are commonly employed to evaluate the capacity of dietary fiber to modulate glucose release and slow its absorption within the gastrointestinal system20.

Glucose adsorption capacity (GAC)

The glucose adsorption capacity of DP, AEDF, and UAEDF was evaluated over a glucose concentration range of 1–200 mM to investigate the hypoglycemic potential of the extracted dietary fibers. For all samples, GAC increased progressively with increasing glucose concentration, indicating a concentration-dependent adsorption phenomenon. This behavior may be associated with gradual occupation of available adsorption sites within the dietary fiber matrix as glucose concentration increases. Among all tested samples, UAEDF consistently exhibited the highest GAC values throughout the entire concentration range, followed by AEDF and DP.

The enhanced glucose adsorption performance of UAEDF may be attributed to multiple structural and physicochemical factors rather than solely increased porosity. Ultrasound-assisted extraction likely induced partial disruption and rearrangement of lignocellulosic components, resulting in greater accessibility of hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups capable of interacting with glucose molecules through hydrogen bonding and weak electrostatic interactions. In addition, the higher swelling capacity and water-holding capacity of UAEDF may facilitate formation of a highly hydrated fiber network capable of entrapping glucose molecules within its matrix. Such hydration-induced expansion can reduce glucose mobility and enhance physical retention of glucose within the fiber structure.

The increased soluble dietary fiber fraction observed in UAEDF may also contribute significantly to glucose adsorption behavior. Soluble polysaccharides possess greater hydration ability and viscosity-forming characteristics, which can promote molecular entanglement and interaction between glucose molecules and the hydrated fiber matrix. Furthermore, the porous and disrupted microstructure observed in SEM analysis may increase accessibility of internal adsorption sites and diffusion pathways, thereby improving overall glucose-binding efficiency. Similar improvements in glucose adsorption behavior have been reported in ultrasound-modified dietary fibers where structural modification enhanced accessibility of functional groups and hydration-related properties.

The variation of GAC with glucose concentration is illustrated in Fig. 8, where UAEDF consistently demonstrated superior glucose adsorption performance compared with AEDF and DP at all investigated concentrations. Glucose dialysis retardation index (GDRI)

The GDRI was employed to evaluate the ability of dietary fiber to retard glucose diffusion across a dialysis membrane, thereby simulating glucose transport behavior under gastrointestinal conditions. For all samples, GDRI values increased progressively with time, indicating enhanced restriction of glucose movement during the dialysis process. Among the investigated samples, UAEDF consistently exhibited the highest GDRI values at all measured time intervals, followed by AEDF and DP, demonstrating superior glucose-retarding capability after ultrasound-assisted extraction. The improved GDRI behavior of UAEDF may be explained through multiple interacting mechanisms involving hydration, viscosity development, diffusion kinetics, and glucose–fiber interactions. The higher WHC and SC values of UAEDF suggest the formation of a more hydrated and expanded fiber network capable of retaining larger amounts of water within the matrix. Such hydration behavior may increase the viscosity of the surrounding medium and create a gel-like environment that reduces glucose mobility and slows mass transfer across the dialysis membrane. Increased viscosity is known to decrease diffusion coefficients of glucose molecules, thereby delaying glucose transport and reducing the rate of glucose release.

Furthermore, the increased SDF content in UAEDF likely contributes significantly to GDRI enhancement because soluble dietary fibers are capable of forming viscous polysaccharide networks that physically entrap glucose molecules and retard their diffusion. The hydrated gel-like matrix formed by soluble polysaccharides may act as a diffusion barrier, thereby reducing glucose transport kinetics across the membrane. Simultaneously, the elevated IDF fraction and porous microstructure may provide additional physical adsorption and entrapment sites that further restrict glucose mobility within the fiber system. In addition to physical entrapment mechanisms, exposed hydroxyl and carboxyl groups within the modified fiber matrix may contribute to transient molecular interactions with glucose molecules through hydrogen bonding. Such interactions may further reduce glucose diffusion rates and enhance glucose-retarding behavior. The enhanced cation exchange capacity (CEC) observed in UAEDF also suggests greater accessibility of charged functional groups, which may indirectly contribute to improved interaction between the hydrated fiber matrix and glucose molecules. As illustrated in Fig. 9, UAEDF maintained consistently higher GDRI values compared with AEDF and DP throughout the dialysis period. The gradual increase in GDRI over time indicates that the modified dietary fiber effectively delays glucose diffusion and release, which may be beneficial for reducing postprandial glucose spikes and improving glycemic regulation. In addition to the observed functional and hypoglycemic properties, the present study also demonstrates the potential for valorization of Queen pineapple peel waste as a value-added dietary fiber source. Pineapple peel is commonly generated as an agro-industrial byproduct and is often underutilized or discarded, leading to disposal-related environmental concerns. The conversion of this lignocellulosic residue into functional dietary fiber therefore represents a promising approach for improving biomass utilization and supporting circular bioeconomy principles through recovery of high-value food ingredients from processing waste streams. The application of ultrasound-assisted extraction may further contribute to process intensification compared with conventional alkaline extraction methods. In the present study, UAE achieved higher dietary fiber recovery within a comparatively shorter extraction duration, indicating improved extraction efficiency and enhanced mass transfer behavior. Previous studies have suggested that ultrasound-assisted processing may reduce extraction time and improve resource utilization due to cavitation-enhanced solvent penetration and disruption of plant cell wall structures. However, it is important to note that a comprehensive quantitative evaluation of sustainability indicators such as energy consumption, solvent reduction, carbon footprint, water usage, and overall environmental impact was not performed in the current investigation. Therefore, definitive conclusions regarding the environmental superiority of UAE over conventional extraction methods cannot be established solely based on the present results.

Furthermore, although UAE demonstrated improved extraction efficiency and functional performance, additional techno-economic assessment and life cycle analysis would be necessary to accurately determine the industrial feasibility, energy efficiency, and environmental sustainability of the proposed extraction system. Such analyses would provide more reliable insight into the trade-offs between processing efficiency, operational cost, and environmental impact during large-scale implementation. The enhanced hypoglycemic behavior and improved functional characteristics of UAEDF suggest potential applicability in the formulation of health-oriented food products targeting glycemic regulation and digestive wellness. Nevertheless, further in vivo studies, process optimization, scalability evaluation, and sustainability assessment will be essential before practical industrial implementation can be fully established. Overall, the present study provides a promising foundation for transforming pineapple processing waste into value-added functional ingredients through ultrasound-assisted extraction. While the obtained results indicate potential technological and environmental advantages, future investigations incorporating quantitative sustainability metrics and techno-economic validation are required to comprehensively assess the long-term feasibility and industrial applicability of the proposed approach.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that alkaline-assisted ultrasound-assisted extraction is an effective approach for the recovery and functional enhancement of dietary fiber from Queen pineapple peel waste. Optimization through response surface methodology established that sonication time, ultrasonic amplitude, and solid–liquid ratio significantly influenced DF yield, with the optimized extraction conditions producing a maximum yield of 88.12%, substantially higher than that obtained through conventional alkaline extraction. Structural characterization confirmed that ultrasonic cavitation induced notable microstructural modifications, including increased porosity, partial disruption of lignocellulosic interactions, and relative enrichment of ordered cellulose domains, which collectively contributed to improved functional performance. The UAE-derived DF exhibited superior water-holding capacity, swelling behavior, cation exchange capacity, and enhanced hypoglycemic potential through improved glucose adsorption and glucose diffusion retardation characteristics.

The findings highlight the potential of ultrasound-assisted processing as a promising strategy for valorizing pineapple peel waste into value-added functional food ingredients while supporting sustainable agro-industrial waste utilization and circular bioeconomy approaches. Nevertheless, the study also presents several limitations that should be acknowledged. The hypoglycemic activity was evaluated only through in vitro assays, and therefore the physiological efficacy of the extracted fiber under in vivo conditions remains unverified. In addition, although FTIR, SEM, XRD, and TGA analyses provided useful structural information, detailed molecular-level characterization of polysaccharide interactions and compositional transformations was not performed. Furthermore, the sustainability advantages of UAE were interpreted qualitatively, while quantitative assessment of energy consumption, solvent reduction, environmental impact, and economic feasibility was beyond the scope of the present work. The scalability of the extraction process under pilot-scale or industrial-scale operating conditions also remains to be validated.

Future investigations should therefore focus on detailed rheological and molecular interaction studies to better understand the structure–function relationships of the extracted dietary fiber. In vivo glycemic response evaluation and gastrointestinal functionality studies are necessary to confirm its nutraceutical potential. Additionally, life cycle assessment and techno-economic analysis should be performed to quantitatively evaluate the environmental and industrial feasibility of the UAE process. Further research should also explore the incorporation of the extracted dietary fiber into real food matrices, including bakery, dairy, and beverage systems, to validate its functional performance, processing stability, sensory acceptability, and commercial applicability.
