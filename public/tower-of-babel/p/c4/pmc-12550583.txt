Environmental and social injustices impact dementia risk among older adults with end-stage kidney disease: a national registry study
Authors: Yiting Li, Gayathri Menon, Jane J Long, Malika Wilson, Byoungjun Kim, Sunjae Bae, Mario P DeMarco, Wenbo Wu, Babak J Orandi, Terry Gordon, George D Thurston, Tanjala S Purnell, Roland J Thorpe, Sarah L Szanton, Dorry L Segev, Mara A McAdams-DeMarco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRacial/ethnic dementia disparities among older adults with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) are likely driven by environmental and social injustices. We quantified the impact of fine particulate air pollution exposure (PM2.5; environmental injustice) by racial/ethnic segregation (social injustice) on dementia diagnosis in ESKD.MethodsWe leveraged the US national registry to identify 884 950 older adults (age ≥55 years) who initiated dialysis (2003–2019). Adjusted Cox regression models with shared state-level frailty quantified the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of dementia by PM2.5 concentrations (annualized and matched to older adults' residential ZIP code at dialysis initiation) and by segregation scores (Theil's H method).FindingsOlder ESKD adults residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 (n = 598,778, 59,515 with dementia) experienced a 1.44-fold (95% confidence interval (CI):1.41–1.47) higher risk of dementia; this association differed by race and ethnicity (Pinteraction<0.0001). Dementia risk was exacerbated for those residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 and segregation (n = 232,708, 29,959 with dementia; aHR = 2.28, 95% CI: 2.21–2.36). Within these neighborhoods, older adults in predominantly minority neighborhoods (aHR = 2.85, 95% CI: 2.75–2.95) had a significantly higher risk of dementia compared to those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 and segregation.InterpretationOlder adults with ESKD in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 experienced an increased risk of dementia; this risk was particularly pronounced among individuals in high segregation and predominantly minority neighborhoods. Environmental and social injustices likely drive racial and ethnic disparities in dementia for older adults with ESKD, underscoring the need for interventions and policies to mitigate these injustices.FundingNational Institutes of Health.Keywords: Air pollution, Segregation, Dementia, End-stage kidney disease

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a literature search on May 18, 2023, using PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science with the following search terms: “air pollution dementia”, “racial and ethnic segregation dementia”, “residential segregation dementia” “air pollution dementia end-stage kidney disease”, “segregation dementia end-stage kidney disease”, “air pollution dementia kidney failure”, and “segregation kidney failure”. Most studies we identified were either regional or national, focusing on the association between air pollution and dementia risk in the general older population and community-dwelling adults, or on the association between segregation and dementia in both the general older population and individuals with ESKD. However, we did not identify any studies examining the synergistic effect of air pollution and segregation on the risk of dementia and its subtypes in the older ESKD population. Furthermore, existing studies have primarily focused on Black and White individuals, with fewer examining other racial and ethnic minorities. Additionally, very few studies have focused on examining air pollution and dementia in individuals residing in high-segregation neighborhoods, particularly predominantly minority neighborhoods.

Added value of this study

In this study, we quantified the synergistic effects of PM2.5 exposure (environmental injustice) and racial and ethnic segregation (segregation hereafter; social injustice) on dementia among older adults (age ≥55 years) who initiated dialysis between 2003 and 2019. We found that older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 had a higher risk of being diagnosed with dementia. This risk was exacerbated in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 and segregation. Specifically, older adults with ESKD residing in predominantly minority neighborhoods with high PM2.5 and segregation had a significantly higher risk of dementia diagnosis compared to those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 and segregation. Similar associations were observed for high PM2.5 levels, both alone and in combination with segregation, in relation to Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, and other/mixed-type dementia.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our study shows that, among older adults with ESKD, residing in neighborhoods with high levels of segregation and PM2.5 is associated with an increased risk of dementia diagnosis. This highlights the potential role of environmental and social injustices in driving racial and ethnic disparities in dementia among older adults with ESKD. It underscores the need for national policies and tailored, multifaceted interventions for this population, targeting both environmental factors (e.g., reducing vehicle traffic and increasing green spaces) and social factors (e.g., enhancing support networks, promoting cultural practices, and addressing socioeconomic inequities) to preserve cognitive function.

Introduction

Older adults with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) are at increased risk of dementia and Alzheimer's disease, likely due to comorbidities and the effects of long-term dialysis.1,2 Dialysis may increase dementia risk through blood fluctuations (resulting from fluid shifts),1,3,4 neurological damage (including cerebral atrophy, recurrent ischemia, brain injury, and edema),4, 5, 6 and the accumulation of uremic metabolites and toxins.4,7 Racial and ethnic disparities in dementia diagnosis are well-documented among older adults with ESKD.1 Older Black adults with ESKD experience a 1.70-fold higher likelihood of any-type dementia and a 1.78-fold higher likelihood of Alzheimer's Disease compared to their White counterparts.1 These disparities are likely driven by environmental and social injustices, including environmental exposures (e.g., air pollution) and structural racism (e.g., racial and ethnic segregation).

Air pollution, an environmental exposure associated with cardiovascular disease and stroke,8,9 disproportionately impacts older community-dwelling adults,10 and is associated with an elevated risk of dementia in this population.11,12 Specifically, exposure to fine particulate matter (aerodynamic diameter <2.5 μm [PM2.5]),13 contributes to oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation,11,12 and blood coagulation—factors that accelerate cognitive decline and dementia.11,12 Older adults with ESKD undergoing dialysis are particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of PM2.5, often a result of environmental injustice,11,14,15 on dementia due to their advanced age and comorbidities.11

Furthermore, minoritized and low-income communities bear a disproportionate burden of environmental air pollutants, a differential exposure rooted in social injustice.16 Racial and ethnic segregation (segregation hereafter), a form of structural racism, has historically perpetuated racial hierarchies and exclusions,17 that particularly affect Black populations,18,19 contributing to significant health disparities, including dementia.20, 21, 22, 23 Segregated neighborhoods often face disinvestment, resulting in limited resources,24 fewer opportunities,24 and increased environmental injustices, such as elevated PM2.5 levels.17,25, 26, 27, 28 These conditions can foster isolation, and hinder cognitive development and preservation of cognitive function.29,30 Additionally, a lack of resilience to environmental injustices may amplify the impact of PM2.5 exposure, driving racial and ethnic disparities in dementia diagnosis among older adults with ESKD.1

In this national study, we quantified the association between PM2.5 and dementia diagnosis among older adults with ESKD and assessed whether this association differed by race and ethnicity. Additionally, we quantified the synergistic impact of PM2.5 and segregation on the risk of dementia diagnosis.

Methods

Data source and study population

We leveraged data from the US national registry of ESKD, United States Renal Data System (USRDS), to identify a cohort of 884,950 older adults (age ≥55 years) diagnosed with incident ESKD in the United States (US), who initiated dialysis between 1/1/2003 and 12/31/2019. The USRDS, a national registry, provides comprehensive information on all adults with ESKD in the US, including details from Medicare billing claims.31 Accessing data from this national registry requires approval from the USRDS. Patient characteristics and comorbidities were extracted from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Medical Evidence form (CMS-2728) at dialysis initiation.31 Dementia diagnoses were identified using the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Ninth or Tenth Revision (ICD-9/ICD-10) diagnosis codes from Medicare billing claims.1,32 Medicare is federally funded health insurance for older adults in the US,33 and all adults with ESKD are eligible for Medicare Part A (hospital insurance, such as inpatient visits), B (medical insurance, such as outpatient visits), and D (prescription insurance).33 The study population was limited to older adults with ESKD who had no prevalent dementia diagnoses at dialysis initiation based on the 1-year lookback, whose primary payer was Medicare, and whose race and ethnicity were categorized into one of the following categories: non-Hispanic (NH) Asian (Asian hereafter), NH Black (Black hereafter), Hispanic or NH White (White hereafter). We performed a complete case analysis, excluding patients with missing comorbidities or causes of ESKD (3.2%; Appendix p3 and p22), as well as those whose race was categorized as Other (N = 11,090; Other, Multiracial, American Indian or Alaska Native) or missing race and ethnicity (N = 2095). The Other race category is collected in USRDS; however, it was not included in our analytical cohort.

Our study was reviewed by the New York University Grossman School of Medicine Institutional Review Board and considered exempt under s22-00535, as participants could not be identified directly or indirectly.

Air pollution (PM2.5)

We obtained daily PM2.5 levels from 2003 to 2019 through the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Environmental Public Health Tracking Network.34 We used yearly averages of PM2.5 levels by census tract, which were further averaged at the ZIP code level. Subsequently, we linked these data to the residential 5-digit ZIP code of older adults with ESKD at dialysis initiation.35 These ZIP codes served as proxies for older adults’ residential neighborhoods. PM2.5 levels were categorized as either low (≤9 μg/m3) or high (>9 μg/m3) concentrations, following the recently modified standards set by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for National Ambient Air Quality Standards for Particulate Matter.36

Residential racial and ethnic segregation

To quantify segregation, we used the Multigroup Entropy Index (Thiel's H) method, known for its robustness in simultaneously evaluating segregation across multiple racial and ethnic groups (Asian, Black, Hispanic, White, and Other).23,37, 38, 39, 40 First, we merged American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates with USRDS data to obtain population counts for each race and ethnicity within ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs). Then, we assessed the diversity within ZCTAs, and used the distribution of racial and ethnic populations within counties to quantify the relative racial and ethnic composition of ZCTAs (Appendix p24).23,37, 38, 39, 40

We assigned segregation scores to older adults with ESKD based on their 5-digit ZIP code at the time of dialysis initiation. In line with prior research,18,41, 42, 43, 44, 45 segregation scores were categorized into tertiles: low (<3.2), medium (≥3.2 and < 11.2), and high (≥11.2).

Predictors of dementia diagnosis

Based on prior research, we selected the following covariates that have been identified as potential risk factors for dementia diagnoses1,23,46, 47, 48: patient demographics (age [55–65, 66–75, 76–85, >85], sex, body mass index [BMI in kg/m2; ≤25, 26–30, >30], and employment status), dialysis-related factors (year of dialysis initiation, pre-ESKD nephrology care, cause of ESKD), comorbidities (cancer, hypertension, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease [PVD], cerebrovascular disease [CVD], atherosclerotic heart disease [ASHD], congestive heart failure [CHF], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], drug use, alcohol use, and tobacco use), and assessments of functional status (ability to walk, residence in an institutionalized setting, and the need for assistance with daily activities). Additionally, we considered the following neighborhood-level factors obtained from the ACS 5-year estimates: percentage of residents with ≥ high school degree (≤84.2%, 84.3%–91.5%, >91.5%), percentage of residents with ≥ bachelor's degree (≤22.1%, 22.2%–36.7%, >36.7%), and median household income (≤$53,197, $53,198–$74,434, >$74,434).

Dementia diagnosis

We identified incident cases of diagnosed dementia and its subtypes (Alzheimer's Disease, vascular dementia, and other/mixed types), using ICD-9/ICD-10 codes from Medicare claims after dialysis initiation among older adults with ESKD,31 which has shown high sensitivity and specificity for detecting dementia and Alzheimer's disease (Appendix p4).49 For the outcome of dementia diagnosis, we followed older adults with ESKD from their date of dialysis initiation to the date of their first dementia diagnosis, censoring at the earliest occurrence of the following events: death, end of Medicare coverage, or the end of the study observation period (12/31/2019).

Statistical analyses

We used the Kaplan–Meier method to estimate the unadjusted cumulative incidence of dementia and its subtypes. For clarity, we omitted the medium segregation tertile from the unadjusted cumulative incidence figures. We used complementary log–log plots and Schoenfeld residuals to test the proportional hazards assumption. We then used multivariate Cox proportional hazard models with shared state-level frailty to quantify: 1) the association between PM2.5 and dementia diagnosis (including subtypes); 2) the difference in these associations by race and ethnicity and by sex using interaction terms and Wald tests; 3) the synergistic impact of PM2.5 and segregation on the risk of dementia diagnosis (including subtypes); and 4) within-group differences in high PM2.5 and high segregation neighborhoods, comparing predominantly White (≥70% White residents), mixed (31%–69% White residents) and predominantly minority neighborhoods (≤30% White residents) to low PM2.5 and low segregation neighborhoods. We examined the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to quantify the ZIP code level variability and found it to be < 0.05, indicating that ZIP code-level variability contributed only a small proportion to individual-level variance.50 Therefore, we used a shared state-level frailty for all analyses. This also allowed us to account for state-level variations in policies (e.g, emission standards),50, 51, 52 built environment (e.g, higher concentration of polluting industries),53,54 and access to health care (e.g., Medicaid expansion).55

Sensitivity analysis

We assessed the robustness of our findings through the following sensitivity analyses: 1) using prior EPA cutoff values to define high (>12 μg/m3) concentrations of PM2.5; 2) restricting the analysis to individuals aged >66 years to allow an adequate period of claims data prior to dialysis initiation and to exclude those with Medicare eligibility due to disability (individuals aged 55–66)31,33; 3) evaluating the correlation matrix of coefficients and variance inflation factor (VIF; VIF>10 indicates high multicollinearity)56,57 to identify potential correlations between PM2.5, segregation, and neighborhood-level variables; 4) treating PM2.5 concentrations as a continuous variable (scaled per 1 μg/m3 and 10 μg/m3); and 5) assessing frailty-covariate independence.58

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS (v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC)), Stata 17 MP (StataCorp LLC), and RStudio (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P-value <0.05.

Role of funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the manuscript.

Results

Study population

Among 884,950 older adults with ESKD, the mean age was 70.5 years (standard deviation [SD] = 9.4), 391,312 (44.2%) were female, 33,202 (3.8%) were Asian, 217,167 (24.5%) were Black, 97,483 (11.0%) were Hispanic, and 537,098 (60.7%) were White. Additionally, 420,809 (47.6%) had diabetes, 274,740 (31.0%) had hypertension as the cause of ESKD, and 470,127 (53.1%) had pre-ESKD nephrology care (Table 1).

Residential neighborhood PM2.5

Residential neighborhoods with high PM2.5 were characterized by a higher proportion of females (high PM2.5 = 271,952 [45.4%], low PM2.5 = 119,360 [41.7%], P < 0.0001), and Black older adults with ESKD (high PM2.5 = 169,883 [28.4%], low PM2.5 = 47,284 [16.5%], P < 0.0001). Those residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 were more likely to be unemployed and less likely to have pre-ESKD nephrology care than those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 (Table 1).

Residential neighborhood PM2.5 and incidence of dementia

We identified 79,165 older ESKD patients who were diagnosed with dementia over a median follow-up time of 2.2 years (interquartile range [IQR]:0.8–4.6). The unadjusted cumulative incidence of dementia diagnosis was higher for older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 (5-year cumulative incidence: high PM2.5 = 13.4%, low PM2.5 = 11.8%; PLog-rank<0.0001; Appendix p23).

After adjusting for demographic, clinical, and neighborhood-level factors, older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 (n = 598,778, 59,515 with dementia) had a significantly higher risk of dementia diagnosis (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 1.44, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.41–1.47) than those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 (n = 286,172, 19,650 with dementia). This association differed by race and ethnicity (Asian: aHR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.16–1.36; Black: aHR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.57–1.68; Hispanic: aHR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.20–1.30; White: aHR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.40–1.48; Pinteraction <0.0001; Table 2). However, this association did not differ by sex (male: aHR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.39–1.47; female: aHR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.41–1.49; Pinteraction = 0.41; Table 2).

Residential neighborhood PM2.5 and segregation

Residential neighborhoods with high PM2.5 and segregation were characterized by a higher proportion of older females (high PM2.5 and high-segregation = 106,644 [45.8%]; low PM2.5 and low segregation = 49,781 [42.2%], P < 0.0001) and Black (high PM2.5 and high-segregation = 85,402 [36.7%]; low PM2.5 and low-segregation = 15,768 [13.4%], P < 0.0001) adults with ESKD compared to residential neighborhoods with low PM2.5 and segregation (Appendix p7).

Synergistic impact of residential neighborhood PM2.5 and segregation on incidence of dementia

The 5-year unadjusted cumulative incidence of dementia diagnosis was higher for older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 and segregation compared to those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 and segregation (high PM2.5 and segregation = 17.0%; low PM2.5 and segregation = 9.2%; PLog-rank <0.0001; Fig. 1).

Cumulative incidence of (A) all-type dementia, (B) Alzheimer's disease, (C) vascular dementia, (D) other/mixed-type dementia stratified by residential neighborhood PM2.5 and segregation levels among older adults (age ≥55) with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) who initiated dialysis (2003–2019).

After adjustment, older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 and segregation (n = 232,708, 29,959 with dementia) had a significantly higher risk of dementia diagnosis (aHR = 2.28, 95% CI: 2.21–2.36) than those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 and segregation (n = 118,080, 6223 with dementia; Table 3; Pinteraction<0.0001). Specifically, older adults residing in predominantly White (aHR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.62–1.77) or predominantly minority neighborhoods (aHR = 2.85, 95% CI: 2.75–2.95) with both high PM2.5 and high segregation had a higher risk of dementia diagnosis compared to those residing in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 and segregation, with the most pronounced association observed in predominantly minority neighborhoods.

Dementia subtypes

Alzheimer's disease

The 5-year unadjusted cumulative incidence of Alzheimer's disease was higher among older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 compared to those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 (Appendix p23). After adjustment, older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 (n = 598,778, 13,660 with dementia; aHR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.22–1.34) had a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease diagnosis compared to those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 (n = 286,172, 4358 with dementia). This association differed by race and ethnicity (Pinteraction <0.0001; Table 2) and by segregation (aHR = 2.23, 95% CI: 2.07–2.39; Table 3) but not by sex (Table 2).

Vascular dementia and other/mixed-type dementia

The 5-year unadjusted cumulative incidence of vascular dementia and other/mixed-type dementia was higher among older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 compared to those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5 (Appendix p23). After adjustment, older adults with ESKD residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5, either alone or combined with segregation, had a significantly higher risk of vascular dementia (PM2.5: aHR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.46–1.61; PM2.5+Segregation: aHR = 2.35, 95% CI: 2.18–2.54) and other/mixed-type dementia (PM2.5: aHR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.42–1.49; PM2.5+Segregation: aHR = 2.28, 95% CI: 2.20–2.36) diagnoses compared to those in neighborhoods with low PM2.5, with or without segregation (Table 2, Table 3). Similar to all-cause dementia, the associations of PM2.5 with vascular dementia and with other/mixed-type dementia differed by race and ethnicity (Pinteractions <0.0001, Table 2), but not by sex.

Sensitivity analysis

Our findings were robust to the following methods: 1) using the previous EPA cutoff for defining high concentrations of PM2.5 (Appendix p10-11); 2) restricting the study population to those aged >66 years (Appendix p12-13); 3) VIF, with values < 5 indicating mild multicollinearity, as well as the correlation matrix of coefficients. Both methods showed a mild correlation between residential neighborhood PM2.5, segregation, and neighborhood-level variables such as median household income, percentage of residents with ≥ high school degree, and percentage of residents with ≥ bachelor's degree (Appendix p14); 4) treating PM2.5 as a continuous variable (Appendix p15); and 5) percent changes in HR for covariates before and after adding the frailty term were <5% (frailty-covariate independence; Appendix p16-19).

Discussion

In this national study of 884,950 older adults with ESKD spanning over 17 years, we found that residing in neighborhoods with high PM2.5 levels, both alone (1.44-fold) and in combination with high segregation (2.28-fold), was associated with a higher risk of dementia diagnosis. This highlights a potential mechanism by which environmental and social injustices drive racial and ethnic disparities in dementia among older adults with ESKD.

In this study, we identified elevated PM2.5, a result of environmental injustice, as an important risk factor for dementia among older adults with ESKD. Specifically, exposure to high levels of PM2.5 alone increases the risk of dementia diagnosis, particularly among older Black adults with ESKD. Our findings are consistent with prior research, suggesting that community-dwelling older adults residing in neighborhoods with elevated levels of PM2.5 experience compromised cognitive function, particularly in episodic memory, and may also face up to 21% acceleration in cognitive decline and dementia onset among those aged 65–79.59,60 Air pollutants, such as PM2.5, are a modifiable risk factor for dementia that impacts the nervous system through multiple mechanisms.60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66 Its infiltration into lung tissue can induce oxidative stress and inflammation,63, 64, 65 leading to systemic inflammation and neurotoxicity through the bloodstream.63, 64, 65 PM2.5 can also cross the blood–brain barrier,66 triggering inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, cytokine production, and cerebrovascular damage, all of which contribute to cognitive decline.60,62,65 Additionally, the pronounced association we observed between high PM2.5 exposure and increased dementia risk among older Black adults, compared to other racial and ethnic groups, may reflect the enduring effects of structural racism, including underinvestment in communities, limited access to education, employment, and quality healthcare, and fewer resources to support healthy lifestyles.23,24,67,68 These systemic inequities contribute to lower socioeconomic status, a greater burden of cardiovascular disease and related comorbidities, ultimately elevating dementia risk in this population.23,24,26,69,70 These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to reduce PM2.5 exposure and its associated risk to cognitive health in the older ESKD population.

While PM2.5 alone disproportionately affects older adults with ESKD, its synergistic effect with segregation further increases the risk of dementia diagnosis, particularly for older adults residing in predominantly minority neighborhoods. These predominantly minority neighborhoods, where Black, Hispanic, or Asian residents are highly concentrated, often face structural disadvantages that may contribute to elevated dementia risk.17,18,23,24,40,71 This helps explain why dementia risk was higher in high-segregation neighborhoods even at lower levels of PM2.5. Our findings are consistent with prior research suggesting that residence in such neighborhoods is associated with lower cognitive scores, including measures of episodic memory and executive function, and an increased risk of developing dementia.29,72 Segregation, rooted in discriminatory zoning practices such as redlining,40,71 has created an uneven distribution of resources and opportunities,40,71 leading to neighborhoods marked by social and economic disadvantage.40,71 Within highly segregated neighborhoods, stressors such as material and social deprivation and inadequate neighborhood investment are common.17,18,24 These conditions, when coupled with high PM2.5 exposure,17 may profoundly impact the health and cognitive function of older adults with ESKD.73 This is especially evident among older adults in predominantly minority neighborhoods with both high PM2.5 and segregation, where socioeconomic inequalities (e.g., education, employment, housing, income, access to care), limited healthcare access, environmental injustices,17,23,29,40,72 and lifelong exposure to discrimination72 contribute to racial and ethnic differences in dementia and cognitive function.17,29,72 Therefore, while PM2.5 exposure is an important risk factor, our findings indicate that the social and structural conditions of predominantly minority, high-segregation neighborhoods also play a critical role in dementia risk. Addressing neighborhood-level environmental and social injustices, such as high PM2.5 and segregation, is essential to mitigating cognitive decline and reducing racial and ethnic disparities in dementia among older adults with ESKD.

In February 2024, the EPA lowered the air quality annual standard of PM2.5 concentrations from 12 μg/m3 to 9 μg/m3.36 We found that exposure to PM2.5 concentrations using previous EPA standards had a less pronounced association with the risk of dementia diagnosis. Utilizing prior EPA standards (12 μg/m3) for air quality may decrease public health protections,36 fail to adequately identify at-risk populations, and potentially contribute to worsening racial and ethnic disparities in dementia diagnoses. Addressing the impact of PM2.5 and segregation on diagnosed dementia among older adults with ESKD requires state or national level policies that: 1) identify the most vulnerable population at risk74; 2) establish an environmental and social brain health consortium to bring together key stakeholders, researchers, and clinicians to evaluate and develop new policies informed by this study's findings74; 3) promote education and awareness through local or national campaigns, and engage communities in disseminating information74; 4) design multifaceted interventions targeting built environment factors (e.g., reducing vehicle traffic and increasing green spaces),75, 76, 77, 78, 79 and addressing socioeconomic inequities)80, 81, 82; and 5) create publicly available outdoor and indoor air pollution monitoring tools for its residents. Ultimately, these potential targets for intervention could strengthen community resilience, promote environmental and social justice, and reduce racial and ethnic disparities in dementia.

Our study has several strengths, particularly the use of a large cohort and national registry data to examine the association between PM2.5 exposure, its synergistic effects with segregation, and dementia diagnosis. We also used ICD-9/ICD-10 codes to identify dementia diagnoses, which will not capture mild cognitive impairment. Furthermore, individuals with ESKD may have frequent interactions with the healthcare system, thus making them more likely to obtain a dementia diagnosis compared to the general population of older adults. Nonetheless, previous research has demonstrated the validity and reliability of this method to identify incident dementia diagnoses.49

However, it's important to acknowledge several limitations. First, we used ZIP codes as proxies for neighborhoods due to data constraints, which may introduce spatial misclassification and systematic biases.83 Nevertheless, ZIP codes are commonly used for defining neighborhoods.84,85 Second, we used outdoor PM2.5 measurements, which may not fully capture the total PM2.5 exposure of older adults with ESKD. Despite this, outdoor PM2.5 remains a significant contributor to overall exposure, including in workplaces and during daily road travel.59 Third, we only considered PM2.5 and segregation levels at dialysis initiation, assuming that levels did not vary over time. However, this assumption may be challenged if older adults with ESKD relocate to new residential neighborhoods characterized by varying PM2.5 and segregation levels. Fourth, our segregation definitions rely on aggregated demographic data from ACS and may not fully reflect the multifaceted nature of structural racism within neighborhoods.23 Fifth, due to data constraints, measures of cognitive impairment were unavailable, and education was assessed at the neighborhood level rather than the individual level, even though low individual-level educational attainment is a well-established risk factor for dementia.86

In conclusion, our study reveals a significant association between high PM2.5 levels and an increased risk of dementia diagnosis among older adults with ESKD. While high PM2.5 levels disproportionately affect older adults with ESKD, their synergistic effect with segregation further exacerbates the risk of dementia. Notably, older adults in predominantly minority neighborhoods faced a higher risk of dementia, highlighting a potential mechanism driving racial and ethnic disparities in dementia. Addressing these environmental and social injustices requires both national policies and targeted, multifaceted interventions tailored specifically to older adults with ESKD. Multipronged and collaborative efforts should focus on the harmful effects of both air pollution and segregation to address these injustices and mitigate dementia risk in this population.
