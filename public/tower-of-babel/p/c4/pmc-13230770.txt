Ranolazine as an adjunct to standard therapy for angina in myocardial bridging: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Alireza Abdi-Ardekani, Nasim Kheirandish, Seyed Yaser Rahbarian, Mohammad Hossein Imani, Alireza Arzhangzadeh, Amirhossein Saem, Maliheh Kamali, Helia Bazroodi, Sana Neisi, Mitra Mojibpour, Amir Hossein Imani, Mehdi Bazrafshan, Hamed Bazrafshan Drissi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Myocardial bridging (MB) is a common anatomical variation in which myocardial fibers traverse over an epicardial coronary artery, potentially leading to angina or angina- equivalent symptoms. Ranolazine, known for its efficacy in chronic stable angina, might offer benefits in symptomatic MB, where traditional treatments often fall short. This prospective, parallel-group, double-blind, randomized add-on clinical trial was conducted at Al-Zahra Cardiovascular Teaching Hospital, Shiraz, Iran, from 2023 to 2024. We included patients with MB and angina symptoms who had normal epicardial coronary arteries. Participants were randomized into two groups: one receiving standard therapy (β-blockers) and the other receiving standard therapy plus ranolazine (500 mg twice daily). Outcomes were assessed using the Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) classification of angina, echocardiographic left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and electrocardiogram (ECG) parameters. Trial registry number: IRCT20230801059005N2 (08/11/2023). Among 52 participants, ranolazine addition significantly improved angina severity, with more patients transitioning to CCS grade I (p = 0.001 after adjusting for confounders). While univariate analysis showed no differences in ECG parameters, multivariate analysis revealed a modest but statistically significant QTc prolongation in the ranolazine group (OR = 1.055, 95%CI:1.012–1.100, p = 0.012). PR and QRS intervals remained unaffected. Post-treatment LVEF was comparable between the two groups. Ranolazine, as an adjunct to β-blockers, significantly improved angina symptoms in patients with myocardial bridging and was associated with a mild but significant QTc prolongation, without causing substantial changes in LVEF or other ECG parameters. Introduction

Myocardial bridge (MB) is a benign condition and is defined by the presence of myocardial fibers over an epicardial coronary artery—known as intra-myocardial or tunnelled coronary artery1. Based on autoptic investigations, MB is a highly prevalent anomaly that affects over 30% of the population2. But, coronary computed tomography angiography (cCTA) documents MB prevalence as approximately 20%3. The proximal or middle segment of the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) is the main site of MB and the right and circumflex coronaries are less frequently affected4. MB is usually asymptomatic, which may incidentally be found during coronary angiography. Even though the vast majority of MBs are normal variants, numerous case reports and series have demonstrated the link between MB and angina or angina-equivalent symptoms5. Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), vasospastic angina, and stable or unstable angina can all be complications of MB6. Although pharmacologic therapy is the mainstay of treatment for symptomatic patients, PCI, myotomy, or CABG may be considered for certain cases who are not responding to maximal medical therapy7. Beta-blockers are the primary treatment for symptoms of MB by reducing the heart’s pumping force and the compression of the coronary arteries8. Coronary artery stent implantation can also be used to treat symptomatic patients by decreasing the compression of the coronary artery and normalizing blood flow9. Surgical intervention for MB involves either supra-arterial myotomy or CABG10. Stent implantation, myotomy, or CABG are effective treatments for MB, but each procedure has its own unique risks, including stent fracture or stent restenosis, wall perforation or ventricular aneurysms, or graft failure, respectively7. Therefore, medical therapies that have the same outcomes as surgical intervention are beneficial for patients who cannot undergo surgery7. Ranolazine, a non-nitrates antianginal, when used at clinically effective doses, blocks the flow of sodium and potassium through ion channels and reduces the overload of calcium ions inside the myocardial cells11. So, the improvement in the heart’s ability to relax and fill with blood during diastole would be achieved. This drug is mainly approved for the treatment of chronic stable angina11,12. The recent widespread use of ranolazine for stable angina has led to the hypothesis that it may also be beneficial for symptomatic MB who do not have any other CAD13.

The Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) grading system is a commonly utilized method for characterizing the intensity of angina pectoris symptoms14. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of ranolazine as an adjunct to standard therapy in improving angina symptoms (by using the CCS grading system) in patients with MB, through a prospective randomized controlled trial.

Methods

A prospective, parallel-group, double-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted between January 2023 and January 2024 at Al-Zahra Cardiovascular Teaching Hospital, affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Iran. The study population comprised adult patients presenting with exertional chest pain suspicious for myocardial ischemia, referred for diagnostic coronary angiography. All procedures were performed via the femoral approach, and angiograms were assessed for obstructive CAD and the presence of MB. Two experienced interventional cardiologists independently interpreted the angiograms. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the potential benefits of adding ranolazine to the standard management regimen of beta blockers for symptomatic patients with MB. The full study protocol is publicly accessible at https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/73353. No significant changes were made to the methodology after the trial commenced.

Eligibility criteria, trial design, and allocation ratio

The inclusion criteria for the study required participants to be at least 18 years old, exhibit symptoms of dyspnea or angina, either at rest or upon exertion, and possess angiographic evidence of myocardial bridge with less than 50% stenosis of the epicardial coronary arteries. Exclusion criteria included a history of heart failure, liver failure, or renal failure; prolonged QT interval on ECG; current use of QT-prolonging medications or CYP3A inhibitors (such as diltiazem, ketoconazole, and macrolides); pregnancy or lactation; Parkinson’s disease; a life expectancy of less than six months; or any participant who declined to participate or withdrew during the follow-up period. Written informed consent was obtained for both participation and publication. Participants were made aware of their right to withdraw from the study at any time or to change their allocated group without any consequences, thus adhering to a per-protocol strategy for handling any deviations.

This study was designed as an add-on trial with two parallel groups, utilizing a 1:1 allocation ratio within a superiority framework. The control arm received only beta-blocker therapy, beginning with orally administered bisoprolol at a dose of 2.5 mg once daily, which was subsequently up-titrated to achieve a target heart rate of 50–60 beats per minute. The experimental group received Ranolazine at a dosage of 500 mg twice daily, in addition to beta-blocker therapy (with bisoprolol adjusted to maintain the same target heart rate). Both treatment arms continued their respective medical therapies for the following six months and were monitored accordingly. Outcome evaluations were performed at baseline and at the end of follow-up.

Randomization, implementation, and concealment mechanisms

Randomization was implemented at the individual participant level through a simple randomization method. The randomization sequence was derived from a two-digit table of random numbers, starting at row 3 and column 10, and proceeding row-wise to the right, as provided by Chakrabarty et al.15. Numbers greater than 52 and any duplicates were excluded, with the first half of the number set assigned to the control group. All participants, primary outcome assessors (N.Kh. and H.B.), and cardiologists responsible for patient monitoring, performing transthoracic echocardiography, and interpreting electrocardiograms (secondary outcome assessors: H.B.D. and A.A.), remained blinded to group allocation until the study’s completion. To maintain allocation concealment, the trial coordinator (S.Y.R.) entered the electronic prescriptions for each patient, directing them to the predetermined drugstore at Imam-Reza Specialty and Subspecialty Clinic in Shiraz, Iran. Furthermore, concealed bottles of medications were provided to the aforementioned drugstore. At both the pre- and post-treatment time points, two independent outcome assessors conducted telephone interviews to evaluate the severity of each participant’s angina according to the Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) classification14. This process ensured that both the participants and outcome assessors remained unaware of group assignments throughout the study. Beyond the trial coordinator, no one had access to the random number sequence or the drug allocation groups throughout the study.

Outcome measurements

The study’s primary outcome is change in angina severity, which is assessed using the Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) classification of angina pectoralis. In this classification, Grade I indicates the presence of angina only during strenuous, rapid, or prolonged exertion related to work or recreation, while ordinary physical activities, such as walking or climbing stairs, do not trigger angina. Grade II is characterized by a slight limitation in ordinary activities, where angina may occur during rapid walking or climbing stairs, walking uphill, or engaging in these activities after meals, in cold conditions, in windy conditions, or under emotional stress; it may also be triggered within a few hours of awakening, or by walking more than two blocks on flat terrain or climbing more than one flight of stairs at a normal pace in typical conditions. Grade III indicates marked limitation in ordinary physical activity, where angina can be triggered by walking just one or two blocks on level ground or climbing a single flight of stairs under normal conditions and pace. Finally, Grade IV denotes an inability to perform any physical activity without discomfort, with the possibility of experiencing angina even at rest14.

The secondary outcomes of the study include (I) changes in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), assessed through transthoracic echocardiography, and (II) changes in electrocardiogram (ECG) parameters, including corrected QT intervals (QTc), PR intervals, and QRS duration. No significant changes were made to the outcome measurements after the trial commenced.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using an online sample size calculator from the Department of Quantitative Health Science, Cleveland Clinic, accessible at https://riskcalc.org/samplesize/16. The clinical design was chosen to be a parallel randomized controlled trial with a superiority design and evaluating a dichotomous outcome (whether CCS improved over a six-month follow-up period). A two-sided alpha value of 0.05, an 80% study power, an allocation ratio of 1:1, and an anticipated dropout rate of 2% were incorporated into the calculations. The expected proportions for the desired outcome in the control group and the experimental group were established at 0.7 and 0.95, respectively. Ultimately, the final sample size was determined to be 26 participants in each group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software versions 25 and 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared between the two studied arms using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Additionally, these categorical variables were assessed across two time points in the study using McNemar tests. Continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U-test, given the non-normal distribution of the evaluated variables.

For multivariate analysis on primary outcome, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were utilized to examine CCS classifications at both pre- and post-treatment time points, comparing the experimental and control groups while also considering the potential effects of confounding variables, such as age, gender, medical history, tobacco or alcohol use, and previous angiography interventions. A cumulative logit link function was implemented, with a multinomial distribution assumed for the primary outcome variable. Additionally, multivariate analysis of secondary outcomes was performed using Generalized Linear Models (GLM), with the distribution set to Gamma and the Log link function applied. This GLM model was selected due to the numerical characteristics and non-normal distribution of the secondary outcomes. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1402.290). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki concerning research involving human subjects, as well as the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Furthermore, the clinical trial was registered on November 8, 2023, with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials under the registration number IRCT20230801059005N2 (08/11/2023).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 52 participants met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and were randomly distributed between the control group (beta-blocker only, n = 26) and the experimental group (beta-blocker plus Ranolazine, n = 26). Fortunately, all participants tolerated both treatments well and successfully completed their six-month follow-up assessments, with no dropouts or significant deviations from the predefined protocol. Furthermore, neither group experienced any significant harm or adverse effects.

The mean age of the study population was 46.75 ± 11.67 years, and 57.7% were female. Baseline characteristics, including the prevalence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and angiographic findings, were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences identified, as detailed in Table 1 (all p-values > 0.05). This baseline comparability supports the internal validity of the subsequent outcome analysis. As presented in Table 1, 10 participants (19.2%) had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, and 12 participants (23.1%) had hypertension. None of the participants had a history of myocardial infarction (MI); however, one participant (1.9%) reported a previous stroke. Concerning habitual behaviours, 10 participants (19.2%) were smokers, while 3 participants (5.8%) reported alcohol consumption. Angiographic findings revealed that all 52 participants (100.0%) had bridge anomalies, with 8 participants (15.4%) showing stenosis in the right coronary artery (RCA) and 7 participants (13.5%) exhibiting stenosis in the left circumflex artery (LCX). Notably, none of the observed stenosis in the RCA or LCX exceeded 20%. (Table 1).

Change in angina severity: Canadian cardiovascular society (CCS) classification

Prior to both treatments, the majority of participants were classified as having CCS grade II angina (35 participants, 67.3%), while a smaller proportion had grade III (15 participants, 28.8%), and only a minor fraction had grade I (2 participants, 3.8%). None of the participants were classified as having grade IV angina. This trend changed significantly after 6 months of either medical therapy: most participants were reclassified as CCS grade I (37 participants, 71.2%), 12 participants (23.1%) exhibited grade II, and only 3 participants (5.8%) had grade III (McNemar p-value < 0.001). Analysis of class transitions revealed that the most common shift was from grade II to grade I, experienced by 31 participants (59.6%). Notable improvements also included 8 participants (15.4%) transitioning from grade III to grade II and 4 participants (7.7%) moving from grade III to grade I. Conversely, no participants moved from grades I or II to grade III, indicating an overall improvement in angina status following both treatments.

A substantial improvement in angina severity classification was observed among the majority of participants (n = 45, 86.5%), while the remaining 13.5% experienced no change in their perceived angina severity. These findings highlight the effectiveness of both treatment regimens in improving angina severity among study participants. Subgroup analysis revealed that both the Ranolazine and control arms experienced significant improvements in angina severity following six months of both medical treatments, with both McNemar p-values being < 0.001. Among participants receiving only beta-blockers, the percentage of patients with grade III angina decreased from 26.9% to 11.5%. Similarly, the proportion of patients with grade II angina also dropped from 69.2% to 30.8%, while the percentage of patients with grade I angina increased from 3.8% to 57.7%. Left ventricular ejection fraction

Echocardiographic assessments evaluated the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of study participants six months after treatment. Following a six-month regimen of Ranolazine combined with a beta blocker, the median LVEF was recorded at 52.5%, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 51% to 54%. In contrast, the LVEF observed after treatment with a beta blocker alone was 52% (IQR: 50–53). The difference between the two treatment groups was not statistically significant in univariate analysis, as indicated in Table 4 (Mann-Whitney U-test p-value = 0.129).

Parameters of ECG

Following a six-month treatment period, ECG parameters—including the corrected QT interval (QTc), PR interval, and QRS duration—were evaluated and compared between the two groups. As presented in Table 4, the median (IQR) values for QTc and QRS duration in the Ranolazine plus beta blocker group were slightly higher than those in the control group, while the PR interval was marginally shorter. However, these differences did not achieve statistical significance in the univariate analysis, as indicated by Mann-Whitney U-test p-values of 0.176, 0.707, and 0.474, respectively. Specifically, the median QTc was 397.5 milliseconds (IQR: 360.2–414.8) in the Ranolazine plus beta blocker group compared to 376.5 milliseconds (IQR: 348.0–408.2) in the beta blocker-only group.

In the GLM analysis, controlling for cofounding variables revealed that adding Ranolazine to the standard regimen resulted in a slight prolongation of the QTc, with an OR of 1.055 and 95% CI of 1.012–1.100 (beta intercept = 0.054, standard error = 0.021, and p-value = 0.012). Conversely, the results from GLM analysis for both QRS duration and PR interval remained non-significant, even following adjustment for cofounders (p-values = 0.359 and 0.529, respectively).

Discussion

Numerous studies and systematic reviews over the years have shown that Ranolazine can be effective in relieving angina in CAD patients, whether used alone or with other therapies17–21. Evidently, the evidence supports its benefit in CAD-related angina, while its effects beyond CAD remain unclear and require further research. Although many previous studies reported substantial improvements in angina symptoms with Ranolazine in patients without obstructive CAD22–24, few specifically targeted myocardial bridging (MB). By focusing on this rarely represented group in Ranolazine research, our study extends the previous research and contributes novel clinical insight and expands the potential application of this treatment. Using the CCS classification of angina, our results revealed a notable angina improvement in patients, with a greater number of individuals moving from higher CCS classes (II and III) to class I following treatment with Ranolazine. Given the underlying pathophysiology of the condition—marked by systolic compression and persistent diastolic narrowing of the coronary artery that impair perfusion and reduce coronary flow reserve25—Ranolazine is effective in MB patients because it directly addresses these hemodynamic disturbances. By inhibiting the late sodium current, Ranolazine reduces intracellular sodium and calcium overload, which improves diastolic relaxation and enhances myocardial perfusion without affecting heart rate or blood pressure26. Given that diastolic dysfunction is central to ischemia in MB, this mechanism likely underlies the clinical improvements observed in our study.

Our results are also consistent with the most recent systematic reviews, which examined the effects of Ranolazine on coronary microvascular function in patients without obstructive CAD, revealing considerable angina improvements27,28. However, some trials have reported inconsistent findings in other populations. Alexander et al. (2015) did not observe a meaningful advancement in angina frequency or quality of life with Ranolazine compared to placebo in patients with incomplete revascularization post-PCI29. Similarly, Bairey Merz et al. (2015) found no statistically significant difference in angina outcomes with ranolazine in a substantial group of women with evidence of coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) and no obstructive CAD30. These discrepancies may reflect differences in patient pathophysiology and trial design. Unlike those studies, our population consisted specifically of patients with dynamic, mechanically-induced ischemia due to MB, where ranolazine’s action on diastolic tone and ion homeostasis may be particularly relevant. This highlights the importance of matching therapeutic mechanisms with underlying disease physiology when evaluating treatment efficacy. Notably, while these studies relied on the Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ) as a metric for evaluating improvements in angina, our use of the CCS angina classification offers a more precise and functional viewpoint, because of its clinician-based methodology. Despite the differences between these assessment tools, both are designed to quantify the influence of angina on patients’ quality of life, allowing us to compare the results of the two.

Among previous studies, only one article assessed the effects of ranolazine on patients with MB31. Although Pargaonkar reported noticeable symptom improvement in both Ranolazine and standard care groups, no significant difference was found between the two groups31. Our study, focusing specifically on angina class, found notable improvements following ranolazine treatment. While Pargaonkar et al. included a broader cohort that also included patients with endothelial dysfunction and microvascular dysfunction, our study exclusively concentrated on patients diagnosed with MB. These findings imply that ranolazine may have similar effects on various aspects of cardiovascular health depending on the underlying pathology. By isolating MB as the sole condition, our study may have better captured the specific therapeutic effect of Ranolazine in this anatomical variant, underscoring the need for further research to clarify its role across distinct patient subsets.

Overall, our research reveals a beneficial effect of Ranolazine on patients with MB. This conclusion is based on improvements observed in angina symptoms, as measured by the CCS angina classification system, which, while not a direct physiological measure of functional capacity, provides a validated assessment of symptom burden and activity limitation. This discovery not only enhances our current knowledge but also highlights the potential of Ranolazine as a therapeutic option for this particular condition. Further investigation is needed to validate these conclusions and investigate the underlying mechanisms responsible for its efficacy.

In the present study, we also evaluated Ranolazine’s influence on Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and electrocardiographic (ECG) parameters (including QTc, PR, and QRS intervals) in patients with MB. While univariate analysis showed no significant differences in ECG parameters between groups, GLM analysis—adjusting for potential confounders—revealed a modest but significant increase in QTc. This finding suggests that Ranolazine may exert a measurable effect on ventricular repolarization, likely linked to its inhibition of the late sodium current and subsequent modulation of intracellular calcium dynamics. Notably, previous studies have reported no significant changes in QTc, PR, or QRS intervals following Ranolazine treatment24,32,33, which makes our finding particularly relevant for this specific patient group. While the clinical relevance of this mild QTc prolongation remains to be determined, it highlights a potential electrophysiological impact that warrants further investigation in longer-term or higher-risk patient cohorts.

Moreover, although baseline LVEF was not assessed in our study, between-group comparisons at the six-month follow-up revealed no statistically significant differences in LVEF between patients treated with Ranolazine plus beta blockers and those treated with beta blockers alone. These findings are consistent with studies by Tagliamonte et al. (2014) and Shah et al. (2017), which showed no difference in LVEF improvement with ranolazine compared to control groups34,35. In contrast, Murray and Colombo (2014) documented a significant LVEF improvement with 36 months of Ranolazine use among CHF patients, suggesting that longer follow-up may be necessary to detect such changes36. These modest effects may be due to Ranolazine’s mechanism, which targets cardiac ion channels and metabolic pathways37,38, and may not directly influence LVEF or ECG parameters unless severe ischemia is present. Methodological differences, such as follow-up duration, may also explain the variability. In summary, these results highlight the necessity for further studies to thoroughly evaluate Ranolazine’s impact on cardiac function in various patient groups.

Limitations and recommendations

One limitation of the study is the relatively small sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader patient populations. Although the study employed a randomized controlled trial (RCT) design, the absence of a placebo group restricts the ability to fully isolate the therapeutic effects of ranolazine from natural fluctuations in symptoms or placebo responses. Additionally, the follow-up period was limited to six months, which may not be sufficient to evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of ranolazine in patients with MB. Future studies should explore whether the improvements in angina status and cardiac function persist over longer periods. Moreover, while efforts were made to enhance participant recruitment, the initial unwillingness of some patients to participate posed a challenge. This was addressed through thorough patient education, including detailed explanations of the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential benefits, which ultimately facilitated informed consent and retention.

Despite its limitations, the study has notable strengths. First, it used a randomized controlled trial design, which is the gold standard in clinical research, to evaluate the effects of adding ranolazine to beta-blocker therapy against beta-blocker monotherapy. This methodology boosts the validity and reliability of the findings. Additionally, the comprehensive assessment of multiple outcomes, including angina status, EF parameters, and ECG, provides a thorough evaluation of the treatment regimens’ effects on various aspects of cardiac function. Furthermore, the inclusion of detailed baseline characteristics and standardized outcome measures enhances the robustness of the study results and facilitates comparison with other research in the field. Overall, these strengths contribute to the credibility and clinical relevance of the study findings.

To build on these results, future research should aim to conduct RCTs with larger cohorts and extended follow-up periods to improve statistical power and generalizability. Furthermore, examining potential interactions between ranolazine and other commonly prescribed cardiovascular medications could provide valuable insights into its safety and efficacy in real-world clinical settings. Additionally, utilizing advanced imaging techniques and biomarker assessments could offer deeper insights into the underlying mechanisms of ranolazine’s therapeutic effects and its impact on cardiac function. Finally, evaluating the cost-effectiveness of ranolazine treatment and its potential role in optimizing healthcare resource utilization would be valuable for informing treatment decisions and healthcare policies.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the potential benefit of adding ranolazine to beta-blocker therapy in improving angina-related symptoms and daily activity tolerance in patients with myocardial bridging, as assessed by the CCS angina grading system. The findings demonstrate significant improvements in angina class for most participants receiving ranolazine and underscore its potential as an adjunctive therapy in managing cardiac symptoms. Although no significant differences were observed in LVEF and ECG parameters between treatment groups, the overall improvement in angina status suggests a clinical benefit of combining ranolazine with standard therapy for patients with myocardial bridging. Further research with larger sample sizes, objective functional capacity assessments, and longer follow-up periods is necessary to confirm these findings and elucidate the long-term impact of ranolazine treatment on patient outcomes.
