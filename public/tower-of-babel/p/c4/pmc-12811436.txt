Influence of diet-induced obesity and voluntary exercise training on cardiac lipids and mitochondrial function in mice
Authors: Nimna Perera, Minh Deo, Surafel Tegegne, Yow Keat Tham, Natalie A. Mellett, Anida Velagic, Alex M. Parker, Oliver K. Fuller, Lauren V. Terry, Casey L. Egan, Peter J. Meikle, Rebecca H. Ritchie, Mark A. Febbraio, Miles J. De Blasio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•High fat feeding decreases cardiolipins in the heart.•Voluntary exercise training reverses these effects and induced physiological cardiac hypertrophy.•High fat feeding reduces markers of cardiac mitochondrial function but these effects ae somewhat reversed by voluntary exercise training.

Introduction

Obesity is a multifaceted metabolic disease emerging as a global health crisis, impacting over 650 million adults worldwide.1 This condition, characterized by elevated blood glucose and lipids as well as insulin resistance, poses a significant risk for the development of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, stroke, and coronary artery disease.2 Moreover, cardiovascular mortality (including myocardial infarction, heart failure, and atherosclerosis) stands as the primary cause of death among people living with obesity.3 The cardiac aberrations associated with chronic overnutrition can impact the heart directly, or indirectly as a result of obesity and associated pathologies. A shift in myocardial substrate utilization, marked by increased fatty acid oxidation and decreased glucose utilization, is often a hallmark of obesity.1 Consequently, this shift in substrate utilization produces oxidative stress, which contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction, decreased oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondrial uncoupling.4

The regulation of mitochondrial dynamics is tightly controlled through the processes of fusion and fission. Outer mitochondrial membrane fusion is primarily facilitated by mitofusin (MFN) 1 and 2, whilst inner mitochondrial membrane fusion and maintenance of cristae structure are regulated by optic atrophy 1 (OPA1).5 Comparatively, fission is regulated by dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) and fission protein 1 (FIS1).5 Feeding rodents a high fat diet (HFD) decreases cardiac mitochondrial fusion but increases cardiac mitochondrial fission genes while concomitantly decreasing enzymes related to fatty acid and carbohydrate oxidation, as well as respiratory chain complex proteins.6 Feeding mice an HFD, phosphorylates DRP1 and promotes cardiac mitochondrial fission, contributing to myocardial insulin resistance, contractile dysfunction and, ultimately, cardiomyocyte death.7 Chronic consumption of an HFD alters substrate availability for mitochondria, requiring adaptations in their molecular machinery to efficiently generate ATP. Mice fed an HFD have demonstrated reduced mitochondrial respiration as well as decreased activity in complexes I–III and citrate synthase, leading to impairments in cardiac function.6

Exercise training has emerged as a beneficial treatment strategy for various cardiovascular diseases.8 The capacity for chronic exercise to induce physiological cardiac hypertrophy is a well-known phenomenon.9 This type of hypertrophy, characterized by mild heart growth and normal or enhanced cardiac function, is considered adaptive and not a risk factor for heart failure.9 During exercise, the energy consumption of the heart significantly increases, leading to higher oxygen consumption and an increased rate of mitochondrial ATP production. For example, swim training in mice induced a state of physiological hypertrophy with enhanced mitochondrial respiration and ATP production.10 Exercise training not only promotes physiological hypertrophy but also induces adaptive remodeling of mitochondria.11 An 8-week voluntary exercise conditioning regimen in mice was found to increase the number of myocardial mitochondria, consequently enhancing their function.12 The dynamic response of cardiac mitochondria to exercise is imperative for maintaining cellular homeostasis and metabolic energy supply,13 which involve the processes of fusion and fission. Furthermore, chronic exercise leads to cardiac upregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), a transcriptional coactivator that promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and enhances fatty acid oxidation capacity.14,15

In the current study, we investigated the effects of prolonged high fat feeding on cardiac morphology and cardiac mitochondria function, also examining whether voluntary exercise training (VET) could attenuate cardiac impairments in HFD-fed obese mice. We found that prolonged high fat feeding impaired cardiac mitochondrial membrane structure and mitochondrial electron transport chain function, effects which were somewhat rescued by VET.

Methods

Experimental animals

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Monash Institute of Pharmaceutical Sciences Ethics Committee. All mice were monitored weekly in accordance with the Australian Code of Practice regulations for care and use of animals for scientific purposes. Male C57Bl/6 J mice, at 6 weeks of age, were dual-housed in open top cages and maintained on a reverse 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to either a chow (n = 23; 8.5% crude fat, 20% crude protein) or HFD (n = 23; 36% total fat, 17.9% crude protein; SF03-002; Specialty Feeds, Western Australia, Australia) as well as water ad libitum for the full duration of the study. At 10 weeks of age, cages were randomized to either a locked (chow n = 11, HFD n = 11; sedentary) or unlocked (chow n = 12, HFD n = 12; VET) running wheel, and dual-housed mice were studied for a further 20 weeks. For each 24-h period, the total number of wheel rotations for each cage (with 2 wheels per cage) was measured, and these data were averaged over 3 consecutive weeks. The 24-h period was divided into light and dark cycles for analysis. To calculate the distance travelled, the following equation was applied: distance (in meters) = (28.9 × wheel rotations)/100. Body composition was assessed with magnetic resonance imaging (EchoMRITM, Houston, TX, USA) and cardiac function was assessed via echocardiography (Vevo3100®; VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada). Two days prior to study endpoint, mice were fasted for 5 h and a tail prick was performed to measure fasting blood glucose via Accu-Chek (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). At study endpoint, non-fasted mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane (Covetrus, Portland, ME, USA) for whole blood collection via cardiac puncture and excision of the heart. Whole blood was centrifuged at 2500g for 10 min at 4°C and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis. The whole heart was dissected, and the left ventricle was snap-frozen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis.

Echocardiography

Left ventricle diastolic and systolic function was assessed by echocardiography in anaesthetized mice. Mice were anaesthetized with 4% isoflurane (Covetrus) via inhalation and placed in the supine position (maintenance 1%–4% isoflurane) for image acquisition using a Vevo3100® system (VisualSonics). Following echocardiography, mice were moved to a recovery heat pad and monitored for reversal of isoflurane anesthetic and returned to their home cage. An apical 4-chamber view was used for acquisition of Doppler images for E wave velocity, A wave velocity, deceleration time, ejection time, and isovolumic contraction and relaxation time. Long axis M-mode was used for chamber wall dimensions and B-mode was used for chamber volumes, areas, and global longitudinal strain. All images were analyzed using VevoLab® software (V5.7.1; VisualSonics). All images were acquired by individuals blinded to experimental groups, and blinded quality control validation was performed post analysis.

RNA extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction from left ventricle

Left ventricle gene expression was measured via quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Total RNA was extracted from frozen left ventricle using Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Isolate II RNA Kit (Bioline, Cincinnati, OH, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Synthesis of cDNA was performed using Tetro cDNA synthesis kit (Bioline) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using high-capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA), and qPCR was performed using TaqMan™ probes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 machine (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Relative mRNA expression of gene transcripts was calculated from the comparative threshold cycle values relative to 18S housekeeper gene. The following TaqMan assay gene transcripts were used: fatty acid-binding protein 3 (FABP3, Mm02342495_m1), platelet glycoprotein 4 (CD36, Mm00432403_m1), beta myosin heavy chain (β-MHC, Mm00600555_m1), atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP, Mm01255747_g1), OPA1 (Mm01349707_g1), DRP1 (Mm01342903_m1), robosomal18S (18S, Mm03928990_g1).

Protein extraction and Western blotting

Left ventricle protein expression was determined via Western blotting. Left ventricle samples were combined with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 130 mM sodium chloride, 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% Triton, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 10 mM sodium fluoride, 1:100 phosphatase inhibitor, and 1:100 protease inhibitor) and homogenized using a TissueLyserII homogenizer machine (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) at 30 Hz for 90 s. Homogenates were incubated for 30 min on ice prior to centrifugation at 14,000 rotations per minute (rpm) for 20 min at 4°C. Supernatants were extracted and a protein assay was performed using a colorimetric bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay according to manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Diluted samples were loaded into a tris-glycine extended (TGX) FastCast Acrylamide (Bio-Rad) gel and run for 20 min at 60 V, followed by 70 min at 100 V using an electrophoresis cell (Bio-Rad). Protein was transferred from the gel onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane via a wet transfer for 70 min at 100 V using an electrophoresis cell (Bio-Rad). The transferred membrane was blocked in 5% w/v bovine serum albumin for 60 min at room temperature and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4°C on an orbital shaker. The following day, the membrane was incubated with a secondary antibody for 60 min at room temperature. Blots were covered in Clarity Max Chemiluminescent reagent (Bio-Rad) and visualized using the ChemiDoc XRS+ Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Densitometry was analyzed using ImageLab software (V6.0.1; Bio-Rad). Immunoblotting was performed with antibodies against lysocardiolipin acyltransferase 1(LCLAT1, PA5-25627; Thermo Fisher Scientific), PGC-1α (ab191838; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), MFN2 (PA5-118059; Thermo Fisher Scientific), ANP (sc-18811; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα, 3707; Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), phosphorylated adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK, 2531; Cell Signaling), AMPKα (2532; Cell Signaling), Perilipin 5 (PA1-46215; Thermo Fisher Scientific), translocase of outer mitochondria membrane 70 (TOM70, 65675; Cell Signaling), cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 (COXIV, 4844; Cell Signaling), and calnexin (208880; Merck, Rahway, NJ, USA). All primary antibodies were diluted to 1:1000 and secondary antibodies to 1:3000.

Lipidomics analysis of left ventricle tissue

Left ventricle lipid profiles were analyzed from frozen left ventricle samples and measured by lipidomic analysis. Left ventricle tissue was homogenized in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and sonicated using a probe tip sonicator. A BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions, and 5 mg/mL of homogenate was aliquoted and used for lipid extraction. An internal standard and a chloroform–methanol (2:1) mixture was added to each sample, followed by water bath sonication for 30 min and centrifugation at 16,000g for 10 min. Lipid containing supernatants were dried under a stream of nitrogen and resuspended in 50 μL H2O-saturated butanol, followed by water bath sonication for 10 min, before 50 μL of 10 mM ammonium formate in methanol was added. Resuspended extracts were centrifuged at 3350g for 5 min and supernatants were transferred to mass spectrometry glass vials. Prior to mass spectrometry analysis, samples were vortexed and sonicated for 15 min and left to sit at 20°C for 120 min. Analysis of lipid extracts was performed on an Agilent 6495C triple quadrapole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with an Agilent 1290 series high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent Technologies) with the following settings: temperature 150°C, gas flow rate 17 L/min, nebulizer gas pressure 20 pound per square inch (psi), sheath gas temperature 200°C, capillary voltage 3500 V, and sheath gas flow 10 L/min. Isolation widths for Q1 and Q3 were set to “unit” resolution (0.7 atomic mass unit (amu)). The Agilent 1290 Infinity II HPLC conditions were as follows: The composition of running solvents comprised of 50:30:20 (Solvent A) and 1:9:90 (Solvent B) water, acetonitrile, and isopropanol, respectively. Solvent A was buffered with 10 mM ammonium formate with 5 μM medronic acid, while Solvent B was buffered with 10 mM ammonium formate. A ZORBAX eclipse plus C18 (Agilent Technologies) column was used. Solvents were run at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min with the column compartment temperature set to 45°C. Solvent B gradient started at 15%, increasing to 50% over 2.5 min, then to 57% over 0.1 min, to 70% over 9.4 min, to 80% over 0.5 min, to 90% over 3.5 min, and finally 100% over 0.1 min. The solvent was then held at 100% B for 0.9 min and returned to 15% B over 0.1 min and held for 2.9 min. Raw mass spectrometry data was analyzed using MassHunter Quant 10.0 (Agilent Technologies). Peak area of lipid species was normalized to their respective internal standards to generate relative concentration data per sample. Data analysis was performed with a one-factor statistical analysis method using MetaboAnalyst software (V5.0; MetaboAnalyst, https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/), and analysis involved data filtration and normalization. Variables with relative standard deviations exceeding 25% were excluded, followed by exclusion of features with more than 50% missing values from all treatment groups. A logarithmic transformation was performed, followed by median normalization and auto scaling. All lipid species were normalized to phosphatidylcholine (PC) levels of each sample as no statistical difference was observed in PC levels between treatment groups, and concentrations of samples are detailed as pmol/μmol of PC.

Proteomic analysis of left ventricle tissue

Approximately 25 mg of left ventricle tissue was lysed in lysis buffer containing 6 M urea, 2 M thiourea, and 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) in 0.1 M 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES). Lysates were precipitated in ice-cold methanol and chloroform and resuspended in lysis buffer. Samples were sonicated using a tip sonicating probe 3 times each for 30 s, vortexed for 10 min, and centrifuged at 20,000g for 20 min before quantification using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein was diluted to 30 μg in lysis buffer before reduction in 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), shaking at 800 rpm for 60 min. Samples were then alkylated in 20 mM iodoacetamide (IAA), protected from light, and shaken at 800 rpm for 60 min. Samples were quenched in 10 mM of DTT, followed by digestion in LysC (Lysyl endopeptidase; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) at 1:50 ratio for 5 h at room temperature. Samples were diluted in 5 volumes of 0.1 M HEPES and further digested in trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) overnight at 37°C at a ratio of 1:50 with 1 mM calcium chloride. The following day, samples were acidified to a final concentration of 1% trifluoracetic acid and desalted on in-house-made styrenedivinylbenzene-reversed phase sulfonate (SDB-RPS) (3 M, St. Paul, MN, USA) stage tips. Peptides were resuspended in loading buffer containing 2% acetonitrile, 0.5% acetic acid and loaded onto a 50 cm × 75 mm inner diameter column packed with 1.9 mm C18AQ particles using an ultra-HPLC operated in single-column mode loading at 700 bar. Peptides were separated using a 180 min linear gradient at a flow rate of 200 mL/min. Mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid (Buffer A) and 0.1% formic acid, 80% acetonitrile v/v (Buffer B). Mass spectrometry was performed on a Q Exactive HF-X Orbitrap system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the following settings: resolution = 70,000, m/z range: 300–1750, automatic gain control target value = 3e6, and maximum injection time = 100 ms. Data analysis was performed using the Andromeda search engine in MaxQuant software (V2.5.1; Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Munich, Germany) against the Uniprot database with peptide tolerance of 20 parts per million (ppm) for the first search and 4.5 ppm for the main search. Search was performed with full trypsin/P digestion and allowed a maximum of 2 missed cleavages on the peptides analyzed from the sequence data base. For all downstream analysis, Perseus software (V2.0.11; Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry) was used by filtering the raw data for contaminants followed by log2 transformations. Proteins with missing values in more than 70% of at least 1 group were removed, and false discovery rate (FDR) was set to 0.05 and 1.00.

Picrosirius red histological staining of left ventricle

A portion of the left ventricle fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was paraffin embedded, sectioned (4 µM), stained with picrosirius red to measure collagen deposition in regions of the left ventricle, and imaged at 20× by the Monash University Histology Platform. Quantitation was performed by ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), using a customized macro calculated as collagen area relative to total tissue area (and perivascular collagen was calculated as collagen area relative to total tissue area within regions of marked blood vessels).

Oil red O histological staining of left ventricle

A portion of the left ventricle was embedded in Tissue-Tek optimum cutting temperature (OCT) compound, sectioned (10 µM), stained with Oil red O solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to measure lipid deposition in regions of the left ventricle, counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich) and p-Xylene-Bis-pyridinium bromide (DPX) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) mounted. Sections were imaged at 40× with an Olympus BX60 widefield microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitation was performed by ImageJ software, using a customized macro calculated as lipid droplet area relative to tissue area.

Hematoxylin and eosin histological staining of left ventricle

A portion of the left ventricle fixed in 10% NBF (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was paraffin embedded, sectioned (4 µM), stained with hematoxylin and eosin to measure cardiomyocyte size (cross-sectional area and width) and imaged at 40× by the Monash University Histology Platform. Quantitation was performed by ImageJ software using a customized macro that specifically selects cardiomyocytes to determine cell area and width. Approximately 1000 cardiomyocytes were measured per image.

Seahorse assay of mitochondrial respiration from frozen left ventricle tissue

Approximately 5 mg of snap frozen left ventricle tissue was added to mitochondrial assay solution (MAS) buffer (70 mM sucrose, 220 mM mannitol, 5 nM monopotassium phosphate, 5 mM magnesium chloride, 1 mM EGTA, and 2 mM HEPES) with 0.25 mg/mL collagenase type II (Worthington Biochemical Corp., Lakewood, NJ, USA) for 30 min at 37°C. Homogenates were centrifuged at 1000g for 10 min at 4°C. Supernatant was extracted, and a BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions. Homogenates were loaded into Seahorse XF96 (Agilent Technologies) culture microplate at 20 μg/mL in 40 μL of MAS buffer. The plate was centrifuged at 500g for 5 min at 4°C, and 140 μL of MAS buffer containing 10 μg/mL cytochrome C (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was pipetted to each well. Into each well, 1 mM nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) + hydrogen (H) (NADH) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (complex I) or 5 mM succinate and 2 μM rotenone (complex II), 4 μM antimycin A, 0.5 mM N,N,N’,N’-tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine (TMPD), and 1 mM ascorbic acid, 50 mM azide were sequentially pipetted into the cartridge and placed into Seahorse XF96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies) before oxygen consumption readings were measured. Assay design and data analysis was performed using Seahorse Wave Pro Software (V2.6.3.8; Agilent Technologies).

Mitochondrial isolation from frozen left ventricle tissue

Approximately 15 mg of snap frozen left ventricle tissue was minced in ice-cold PBS and homogenized using 150 μL of ice-cold MAS buffer using 20 strokes in a glass-Teflon Dounce homogenizer. Homogenate was incubated with 0.25 mg/mL collagenase type II (Worthington Biochemical Corp.) in MAS buffer for 30 min at 37°C. Samples were centrifuged at 1000g for 5 min at 4°C and supernatant extracted and centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min at 4°C. Mitochondrial pellets were washed twice with MAS buffer and centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min at 4°C. Final mitochondrial pellets were resuspended in MAS buffer and a BCA protein assay was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions to determine protein concentrations for Western blotting.

Plasma triglyceride assay

To assess non-fasting plasma triglyceride concentrations in mice, a triglyceride assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed as per manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 3 μL of Multi Calibrator Lipid standards (Fujifilm), MilliQ water (Sigma-Aldrich), and plasma samples were pipetted into respective wells, in duplicate, in a 96-well clear-bottom plate, followed by 300 μL of Infinity Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Microplate was incubated for 5 min at 37°C, and absorbance was determined using a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany) at a wavelength of 500 nm and analyzed against calibrator standards.

Plasma non-esterified fatty acid assay

To assess non-fasting plasma free fatty acid concentrations in mice, a non-esterified fatty acid (NEFA) assay (Fujifilm) was performed as per manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, Multi Calibrator Lipid (2 mmol/L) standards (Fujifilm) were prepared by serial dilution, and samples were pipetted into respective wells, in duplicate, in a 96-well clear-bottom plate, followed by 100 μL of Reagent A and incubated for 10 min at 37°C. Following incubation, 200 μL of Reagent B was pipetted to each well and incubated for 10 min at 37°C, and absorbance was determined using a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech) at a wavelength of 550 nm and analyzed against calibrator standards.

Plasma cholesterol assay

To assess non-fasting plasma cholesterol concentration in mice, a cholesterol assay (Fujifilm) was performed as per manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 3 μL of standard solution, MilliQ water (as a blank), and plasma samples were pipetted into respective wells, in duplicate, in a 96-well clear-bottom plate. Color reagent was dissolved in buffer solution, and 300 μL of this color solution was added to each well. The microplate was incubated for 5 min at 37°C, and absorbance was determined using a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech) at a wavelength of 600 nm and analyzed against calibrator standards.

Statistical analysis

Repeated measures, two-way and three-way analyses of variance were performed in GraphPad Prism (V9.3.1; GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). A Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Left ventricle lipidomics analysis was conducted using the one-factor statistical analysis method from MetaboAnalyst software (V5.0; MetaboAnalyst). Searching for raw mass spectrometry files and statistical analyses were carried out using Maxquant (V2.5.1) and Perseus (V2.0.11) (Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Munich, Germany). FDR values of 0.05 and 1.00 were used for left ventricle proteomic analysis.

Results

HFD feeding increases fat mass, which is attenuated with voluntary exercise

Body weight was measured weekly, and body composition was monitored monthly via magnetic resonance imaging. Body weight was increased in HFD sedentary mice when compared with chow sedentary mice, and this was rescued in the HFD VET group (Fig. 1A). As expected, consumption of an HFD increased (p < 0.001) adiposity compared with chow-fed animals at 3 months, and this remained elevated (p < 0.05) for the remainder of the study. In addition, VET attenuated (p < 0.05) fat mass when comparing HFD VET groups with HFD sedentary mice at 2 months, and this was also sustained (p < 0.05) for the remainder of the study (Fig. 1B), while lean mass was not different between experimental groups (Fig. 1C). Absolute heart weight did not increase (p = 0.11) in chow VET groups compared with chow sedentary groups but increased (p < 0.05) in HFD VET groups compared with HFD sedentary groups (Fig. 1D). On average, mice in the chow exercise and HFD exercise groups ran 1700 m/day and 1400 m/day, respectively. Mice were housed on a reverse 12-h light/dark cycle and, as expected, mice in the VET groups ran further in the dark cycle compared with the light cycle in both HFD (p < 0.01) and chow-fed (p < 0.05) groups (Fig. 1E and 1F). At 26–28 weeks of age, mice in the chow exercise and HFD exercise groups ran 880 m/day and 730 m/day, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). HFD induces diastolic dysfunction, and voluntary exercise promotes a hypertrophy phenotype

To investigate the effect of a chronic HFD and VET on left ventricular (LV) cardiac function, transthoracic echocardiography was performed following 24 weeks of HFD feeding and 20 weeks of VET. E wave velocity (early LV filling) was unaltered (Fig. 2A); however, A wave velocity (late diastolic LV filling) was increased in HFD mice with VET when compared with chow VET mice (p < 0.05) and HFD sedentary mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2B). E/A ratio, a commonly used diastolic measure of early (E) and late (A) ventricular filling velocities, was not different between groups (Fig. 2C). LV deceleration time, a common measure of LV chamber stiffness and resultant relaxation time, was prolonged (p < 0.001) in HFD sedentary mice when compared with chow sedentary mice, indicating mild diastolic dysfunction; this tended (p = 0.05) to be rescued in HFD VET mice (Fig. 2D). Isovolumic contraction (p = 0.06) and relaxation time (p = 0.09), both measures of chamber stiffness and hence relaxation, were not different in HFD sedentary mice when compared with chow sedentary mice, and this was not affected by VET (Fig. 2E and 2F). Furthermore, B-mode and M-mode echocardiography showed that LV anterior and posterior wall dimensions as well as systolic function, a key indicator of cardiac contractility, remained unchanged between groups (Supplementary Table 1). As expected, in settings of physiological hypertrophy, LV area and volume in systole and diastole was increased at study endpoint in chow and HFD VET groups compared to their sedentary counterparts at baseline (Fig. 2G–2J). This is indicative of a physiological hypertrophy phenotype, which as detailed above, is adaptive, develops in response to chronic exercise stimulation, and exerts cardioprotective effects.9 Furthermore, in the present study, at endpoint alone, LV area and volume in diastole were elevated (p < 0.01) in chow VET when compared to chow sedentary mice, which also highlights a physiological hypertrophy phenotype (Fig. 2G and 2I). This is supplemented by an increase (p < 0.05) in heart weight in HFD VET mice compared with their sedentary counterparts (Fig. 1D). In addition, the lack of interstitial and perivascular cardiac collagen accumulation in response to HFD is consistent with the absence of pathological remodeling (Fig. 3A, 3D, and 3E). Although no difference was observed in cardiomyocyte width or area in hematoxylin and eosin-stained LV sections, there was an overall VET effect with a trend towards and increase in cardiomyocyte width (p = 0.10) and area (p = 0.08) in VET mice when compared with sedentary mice (Fig. 3B, 3F, and 3G). Furthermore, mRNA expression of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) and β-myosin heavy chain (β-MHC), both pathological hypertrophic genes, were decreased (p < 0.05) in chow VET mice compared with sedentary mice, which is again consistent with a physiological hypertrophy phenotype (Fig. 3K and 3L). HFD feeding and voluntary exercise alter LV cardiac lipidome

To investigate the effect of HFD and VET on the LV lipidome, we performed a comprehensive targeted lipidomic screen on LV tissue. The principal component analysis demonstrated that the cardiac lipidome between chow and HFD samples was markedly different, but the separation was to a lesser degree in HFD VET samples when compared with HFD sedentary samples (Fig. 4A). Volcano plots demonstrated differentially expressed lipid species when comparing HFD sedentary with chow sedentary mice and HFD VET with HFD sedentary mice (Fig. 4B and 4C). We examined the major lipid classes that are often dysregulated with HFD feeding and VET, including sterols, free fatty acids, and glycerolipids; however, these were not different between experimental groups (Supplementary Fig. 2A–2C). Interestingly, cardiolipins (CL) were decreased (p < 0.0001) in HFD sedentary mice compared with chow sedentary mice, and this decrease was attenuated (p < 0.01) in the HFD VET group (Fig. 4D). CL are a key mitochondrial phospholipid class localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane required for oxidative phosphorylation.16 The tetra-linoleic form, CL (18:2)4, is the most abundant species in heart, skeletal muscle, and other metabolic tissues.17 Interestingly, CL (18:2)4 was decreased (p < 0.0001) in HFD sedentary mice when compared with chow sedentary mice, with no apparent effect (p = 0.10) of VET (Fig. 4E). Phosphatidylglycerols (PG) are an intermediary lipid class driving the biosynthesis of CL and are, therefore, critical for the normal development of mitochondrial membranes.18 However, there were no significant differences in PG between any of our treatment groups (Fig. 4F). Other lipid classes that have been hallmarked in the mitochondrial membrane structure include acylcarnitines, diacylglycerols, and ceramides. Following consumption of an HFD, these lipid classes were elevated when compared with chow sedentary mice; however, this increase was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 2D–2F). Furthermore, we examined basic plasma lipid profiles for non-esterified fatty acids, triglycerides, and cholesterol on non-fasted plasma at study endpoint. There were no changes (p = 0.10) in non-esterified fatty acids in chow VET mice compared to sedentary mice, and there was a decrease (p < 0.05) in triglycerides in HFD VET mice compared to HFD sedentary mice (Fig. 1H and 1I). Plasma cholesterol was increased (p < 0.0001) in both HFD groups compared to their chow counterparts (Fig. 1J). Gene expression of CD36 and FABP3 remained consistent between groups (Fig. 3I and 3J). Lipid accumulation is often a key feature of an obese heart. We, therefore, examined LV lipid deposition via Oil red O histological staining. Cardiac lipid deposition was increased (p < 0.0001) in HFD sedentary mice compared with chow sedentary mice, and this was attenuated (p < 0.0001) in the HFD VET group (Fig. 3C and 3H). Aerobic exercise is known to exert beneficial effects on circulating lipid profiles of individuals with obesity.20

To gain insight on the effect of HFD and VET on the LV proteome, we performed a proteomic screen. We identified 4035 proteins present; however, the principal component analysis did not exhibit clear separation, and with a false discovery rate of 0.05, no proteins were differentially expressed between any of the groups (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Using a less conservative false discovery rate of 1, there were 216 proteins differentially expressed between HFD sedentary and chow sedentary, but only 7 proteins differentially expressed between HFD VET and HFD sedentary. The top enriched proteins ranked by Student’s t test–log (p values) are mapped comparing HFD sedentary to chow sedentary and HFD VET to HFD sedentary (Supplementary Fig. 3B and 3C). Of interest was perilipin 5, which is uniquely associated with mitochondria in oxidative cells and tissues and has distinctive properties of recruiting mitochondria at the lipid droplet surface.24 Perilipin 5 regulates the amount of fatty acid release from lipid droplets, therefore protecting the heart from oxidative stress.24 Perilipin 5 expression was not different between experimental groups in LV tissue protein extracts assessed via western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

Our data demonstrated that consumption of an HFD led to an increase in body weight, fat mass, and mild LV diastolic dysfunction. Further, VET contributed to an LV physiological hypertrophic phenotype, a finding which is commonly observed in models of physical activity. Our lipidomics analysis revealed that LV CL expression was downregulated following consumption of an HFD, and VET ameliorated the decrease in CL. Given CL are instrumental in the structural integrity of mitochondrial membranes, we probed further into the dynamics and biogenesis of cardiac mitochondria. We highlighted that consumption of an HFD reduced electron transport chain complex activity, which may be due to alterations in mitochondrial dynamics and biogenesis; however, VET had no effect on mitochondrial complex activity.

Our data provide evidence of cardiac physiological hypertrophy following VET in our model. Specifically, we observed significant increases in both diastolic and systolic chamber area and volume, alongside a reduction in gene expression of pathological hypertrophy markers, such as ANP and β-MHC, coupled with an increase in absolute heart weight. In a swim training mouse model, 4 weeks of exercise resulted in an increase in the heart weight-to-body weight ratio, along with activation of phosphorylated protein kinase B (Akt), a key marker of cellular signaling involved in hypertrophy.25 Similarly, in a rat model of high intensity treadmill running, 1 week of training led to significant increases in left ventricular end-diastolic diameter and left ventricular end-systolic diameter indicating enlargement of the ventricular chamber when compared with sedentary controls.26 Cardiac hypertrophy often involves an increase in heart size, chamber volume or wall thickness, but does not necessarily require a proportional increase in individual cardiomyocyte size.27, 28, 29 Although we did not observe histological changes in cardiomyocyte width and area, we acknowledge that our voluntary exercise model might not have been sufficiently intense to induce detectable alterations in cardiomyocyte cellular morphology. Although our echocardiography analysis only revealed mild diastolic dysfunction, characterized by a prolonged LV deceleration time following HFD feeding, this may indicate that even modest alterations in deceleration time and diastolic function may serve as early indicators for more advanced cardiac dysfunction.

Our initial observation in the LV lipidome revealed a substantial decrease in CL concentration induced by an HFD, with VET attenuating this deficit. This finding aligns with current research suggesting that the reduction of CL and subsequent impaired mitochondrial function are considerable factors in the progression of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus.11,23,30,31 CL are phospholipids that play a pleiotropic role in modulating bioenergetic processes of mitochondria and maintaining the stability of the inner membrane, which includes cristae formation, ATP synthesis, and respiratory chain complexes.32 Pathological adaptations in the CL profile in the context of disease, commonly fall into 3 categories: a reduction of CL content, CL peroxidation, and alterations in acyl chain composition.19 In most research, however, the prevailing pathological change in the CL profile is a decrease in mitochondrial content of CL. This selective decline in CL within mitochondria could be attributed to increased CL degradation or downregulated de novo synthesis, a consequence of compromised enzymatic activity associated with its synthesis or reduced availability of CL precursors.33 Additionally, when evaluating CL in whole tissue homogenates, as was conducted in our study, a reduction in CL content may merely indicate a decrease in mitochondrial volume.34 When we consider the changes in CL species in our lipidomics data set, we observe a decrease in CL (18:2)4, the most predominant form, following consumption of an HFD. In the initial stages of type 2 diabetes, there is often a marked reduction in total cardiac CL.35 Furthermore, within the diabetic cardiac milieu, there appears to be a shift from the dominant fatty acyl species (18:2) towards longer and polyunsaturated fatty acids, a phenomenon attributed to the aberrant remodeling of CL.35,36 In mice subjected to an HFD for 20 weeks, there was a significant reduction in shorter saturated acyl chains and an increase in polyunsaturated acyl chains in cardiac mitochondria.37 The changes in CL acyl profiles are observed in the etiology of pathological conditions such as diabetes, obesity, heart failure, and aging.23,36,38 Activities of complexes I, III, and IV show decline in a model of ischemia-reperfusion; however, exogenous administration of CL restored complex activity.39 Depletion of tafazzin, an enzyme in the remodeling of CL, was adequate to induce cellular hypertrophy, reduce intracellular ATP content, and elevate expression of the heart failure marker B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) in cultured neonatal ventricular rat cardiomyocytes.40 Furthermore, a reduction in CL has also been observed in hypertrophied hearts from rats subjected to abdominal aortic constriction.41 To our knowledge there have been limited studies examining the effects of VET on the LV CL composition in models of diet-induced obesity, although there is current research pertaining to CL expression in skeletal muscle, also an oxidative tissue. In a 12-week aerobic and resistance training regimen in obese women, exercise resulted in a significantly altered skeletal muscle lipid profile with increased content of CL and phospholipids but no changes in diacylglycerols, ceramides, or triglycerides.42 Increased total content of CL with exercise training was associated with increased mitochondrial respiratory capacity, a finding consistent with our findings in the heart. In another study in patients with type 2 diabetes, there were increases in skeletal muscle mitochondria density, CL content, and mitochondrial oxidation enzymes following a 16-week long moderate intensity regimen.43 In a similar study, elderly participants in a 12-week exercise program had increased skeletal muscle mitochondrial DNA and CL content and enhanced mitochondria electron transport chain activity.44

CL plays a vital role in the structural integrity and enzymatic function of all electron transport chain complexes.32 In atrial tissue from patients with type 2 diabetes, citrate synthase activity and complex IV activity remained unchanged when compared to non-diabetic tissue extracts, but there was a significant reduction in complexes II and III activity.45 Our investigation demonstrated a decrease in complex II and IV activity following consumption of an HFD, and this prominent finding prompted us to look deeper into the dynamics and function of cardiac mitochondria. Synthesis of electron transport chain protein subunits is part of the mitochondrial biogenesis that is governed by a set of transcription factors. Notably, nuclear respiratory factor 1 (Nrf1) is activated by PGC-1α through physical interaction, thereby stimulating the expression of genes encoding both electron transport chain protein subunits and nuclear transcription factors.46 CL actively contribute to the shaping of the mitochondrial network through their role in regulating mitochondrial fission and fusion. The processes of fission and fusion are under the control of a group of dynamin-related GTPases, with CL acting as a modulator of the activity of DRP1 and OPA1.47 A study in Zucker diabetic rats,48 revealed increased DRP1 phosphorylation and decreased protein expression of fusion proteins OPA1 and MFN2. Similarly, in a db/db mouse model of type 2 diabetes, reduced LV ejection fraction and fractional shortening were observed at 12 weeks and 16 weeks of age.49 Additionally, hearts from mice with type 2 diabetes displayed excessive mitochondrial fission at 12 and 16 weeks, compared with control hearts, and a reduction in mitochondrial size and MFN2 expression.44 Decreased PGC-1α expression in a diabetic heart led to downregulation of downstream factors Nrf1 and mitochondrial transcription factor A (Tfam).50 In a db/db mouse model of type 2 diabetes, long-term moderate exercise training has been shown to reduce myocardial fibrosis and apoptosis through activation of PGC-1α and Akt pathways, thereby enhancing mitochondrial biosynthesis and maintaining normal cardiac function.50 In addition, a 5-week moderate treadmill exercise intervention in a db/db mouse model of type 2 diabetes reported an attenuation of DRP1 protein expression levels, associated with an increase in the number of mitochondria and improved function.51 In our study, however, the absence of differences in protein expression associated with fusion and fission may be attributed to distinctions in animal models, where the above models focused on diabetes, a condition characterized by hyperglycemia, which is known to accelerate mitochondrial dysregulation.52

The characterization of our diet-induced model of obesity showed elevated blood glucose and plasma cholesterol, combined with elevated cardiac lipid deposition, all which are typically observed in models of obesity and can lead to the formation of lipid droplets.53 These serve as reservoirs for neutral lipids, such as triacylglycerols, aiming to sequester fatty acids in an inert form to mitigate the potential lipotoxic effects.53 The excessive accumulation of triacylglycerols and subsequent abnormal development of lipid droplets often, however, leads to lipotoxicity in the heart, resulting in subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction.54 Interestingly, perilipin 5 has been identified to localize within mitochondria and is proposed to enhance fatty acid utilization.55 Our investigation into mitochondrial function revealed a reduction in respiratory chain activity, particularly in complexes II and IV, following consumption of an HFD. The pathogenesis of mitochondrial electron transport defects in obesity is multifaceted, involving either a decrease in quantity or loss of catalytic activity of their subunits. Excessive uptake of fatty acids by cardiomyocytes can often lead to reduced expression of mitochondrial fusion genes, thereby inhibiting the activity of respiratory chain complexes and oxidative phosphorylation.56 Recent research has elucidated that an excess of lipids activates DRP1 through acetylation, promoting its mitochondrial translocation and subsequently causing myocardial cell dysfunction.7 In vitro experiments have also established that elevated free fatty acids in the myocardium significantly alter the mitochondrial network, leading to changes in DRP1 and OPA1.57 Indeed, cardiac lipotoxicity has been linked to lipids, including free fatty acids, ceramides, diacylglycerols, and acylcarnitines.58,59 In our study, the observed decrease in complexes II and IV subsequent to consumption of an HFD could potentially be attributed to an increase in these toxic lipid species in the myocardium, although the increase in these species was not statistically significant.

An argument can be proposed that cardioprotection against diet-induced obesity is contingent upon the type and intensity of physical exercise performed. For instance, a study in 4-month-old rats aimed to evaluate the impact of a single bout of treadmill exercise on mitochondrial function in cardiac tissue.60 While no discernible differences were detected in the proteins associated with mitochondrial dynamics, there was enhanced mitochondrial function. This emphasizes the notion that the short duration may not be sufficient to elicit changes in mitochondrial biogenesis, potentially necessitating a high-intensity protocol.60 In a chronic forced treadmill exercise model involving sessions 5 days per week over an 8-week period, there were no significant differences in expression of OPA1, MFN2, DRP1, FIS1, or markers of mitophagy, but increased MFN1 expression when compared with cardiac tissue from sedentary rats.61 Furthermore, a 6-week swim training protocol in mice demonstrated an upregulation of mitochondrial biogenesis in the heart, with an increase in PGC-1α, Nrf1, Tfam, MFN2 expression, alongside an increase in mitochondrial volume density.62 Conversely, a 3-month treadmill running regimen in mice, conducted 5 days per week, resulted in upregulated expression of complexes I and IV in the gastrocnemius muscle, but no significant changes were detected in cardiac samples.63 Additionally, there were no differences in Nrf1 and Tfam mRNA or the impact on mitochondrial density within the LV.63 In a rat model of endurance exercise, following 54 weeks of treadmill exercise, complexes I–V expression in the heart did not change.64 However, ATP synthase F1 subunit beta (ATP5B), a marker of complex V was elevated in isolated cardiac mitochondria from trained animals. Additionally, PGC-1α, often elevated post exercise, exhibited significantly lower expression in the LV of exercised mice compared with sedentary animals, indicating reduced mitochondrial biogenesis.64 Given the inherently forced nature of treadmill and swim exercise protocols, a valid concern arises regarding the potential for a stress-induced type of cardiac growth. While mice are usually acclimated to the exercise regimen, it can be appealed that swim or treadmill protocols stray from natural activity in rodents, thereby instigating a stress-induced cardiac growth phenotype.65,66 The utilization of a voluntary treadmill exercise model, which is considered high volume and intermittent, as conducted in our study presents an opportunity to leverage the natural tendencies of activity in rodents.

Limitations

Although this study provides insights into the opposing effects of diet-induced obesity and exercise, there are some limitations. Given the robust effects of high fat feeding and voluntary exercise on cardiac CL and its effects on mitochondrial membrane integrity and complex activity, visualizing cristae formation and mitochondria organelle morphology would be beneficial to this study. Following consumption of an HFD, cardiac mitochondria are often reduced in size and have fewer cristae structures in the inner membrane.6 Of interest to this study would be determining the role of reduced CL expression on mitochondrial membrane potential and morphology. The use of preserved tissue, rather than fresh samples, likely compromised the structural integrity of organelles within our cardiac tissue samples, limiting our understanding of the role of CL in mitochondrial membrane potential and morphology assessed through electron microscopy or stains such as MitoTracker Green. Another limitation of this study is the use of an HFD largely rich in dietary lipids. Firstly, measuring the variation in caloric consumption and, consequently, in micronutrient concentrations and fatty acid composition across our 2 diets may provide insight into the observed differences in CL levels and their potential influence on mitochondrial function. Secondly, high glucose diets or settings of diabetes mellitus often drive a more severe cardiac dysfunctional phenotype and metabolic disarray than seen in this study. Perhaps the use of a diet rich in fructose or glucose or the use of a diabetic mouse model would provide a stronger obesity phenotype that better aligns with the human disease pathology. Several other factors remained unexplored in this study regarding the use of voluntary running wheels, including the potential variability in exercise between individual mice due to dual-housing and, furthermore, the lack of control over the timing of exercise bouts prior to conducting intervention experiments such as echocardiography and tissue collection. The current study utilizes male mice, but studies examining sex differences of CL remodeling might provide evidence for the mitochondrial-related mechanisms under physiological and pathological conditions. Finally, it would be of interest to this study to decipher whether different modalities of exercise (e.g., resistance, forced, or interval training) and varying velocities heed varied levels of cardioprotection in this chronic model of overnutrition, and to explore the possible effects on mitochondrial dynamics and structure.

Conclusion

In summary, this work provides new knowledge regarding the implications of a chronic HFD and VET on cardiac and mitochondrial physiology. We determine that chronic consumption of an HFD remodels mitochondrial CL composition with reduced electron transport chain complex activities, a possible consequence of impaired mitochondrial biogenesis and structure. Our VET regimen, which leads to physiological cardiac hypertrophy, may potentially mediate HFD-induced cardioprotection. This work signifies the importance of targeting mitochondrial health and dynamics in chronic models of diet-induced obesity and identifies the potential for VET to attenuate metabolic dysregulation.
