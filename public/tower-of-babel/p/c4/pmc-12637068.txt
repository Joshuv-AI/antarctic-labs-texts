Population responses in V1 encodes stimulus visibility in backward masking
Authors: Itiel Dennis, Hadar Edelman-Klapper, Gal Gadi Raveh, Osnat Bar-Shira, Shany Nivinsky Margalit, Hamutal Slovin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe visibility of a briefly presented stimulus is diminished when followed by a mask, a phenomenon known as backward masking (BM). As the interval between the stimulus and the mask (stimulus-to-mask onset asynchrony [SOA]) becomes shorter, the stimulus visibility decreases. Yet, the neural mechanism underlying BM remains poorly understood. To investigate this, monkeys were trained to discriminate oriented targets in a pattern BM paradigm. Using voltage-sensitive dye imaging, we measured the population responses in the primary visual cortex (V1). The behavioral performance in short SOAs was lower, and reaction times were slower. Population response in V1 showed a figure-ground modulation (FGm) with lower values for short SOAs. A classification analysis revealed the differences in temporal dynamics of the model accuracy in relation to stimulus visibility and spatial rearrangements of the informative neural patterns. These results suggest that the mask interferes with FGm in V1, possibly leading to diminished stimulus visibility.Subject areas: Natural sciences, Biological sciences, Neuroscience, Sensory neuroscience

Introduction

The paradigm of visual masking has been used for over a century to disrupt visual perception by presenting a masking stimulus that is in close proximity in time and space to a target stimulus. Visual masking is achieved either by presenting a masking stimulus that partially (metacontrast masking) or entirely (pattern masking) overlaps the target stimulus or by introducing the masking stimulus shortly before (forward masking) or after (backward masking [BM]) the stimulus.1,2,3,4,5

In BM, the suppressed visibility of the target stimulus is not simply attributed to the brief presentation of the target stimulus, because when it is not followed by a mask, the stimulus can be clearly perceived. Behavioral studies reported that stimulus visibility in BM depends on the time interval between stimulus onset and the mask onset, termed as stimulus-to-mask onset asynchrony (SOA).6,7 As SOA becomes shorter, the stimulus visibility decreases.6,8,9,10

While the effects of BM have been linked to the neural activity in early visual cortex,6,7,11,12,13,14,15,16 the neuronal mechanisms in the visual cortex underlying BM are still not well understood. Several theories have been suggested to explain the process of visual masking, including the temporal integration of sensory signals causing target-mask merge,1,16,17,18,19 lateral inhibition in the feedforward visual pathway,4,11,13,20 and disruption of cortical feedback.14,21,22,23 Lamme12 extended the latter theory to suggest that the mask disrupts reentrant processing from higher visual areas back to the V1 of monkeys, i.e., the masking interferes with the ability to distinguish an object (the figure) from its surrounding background (ground), resulting in decreased stimulus visibility. Yet, how the figure-ground (FG) processing is interrupted in V1 and the relation to behavioral performance and stimulus visibility in BM is poorly understood.

To investigate this question, we used voltage-sensitive dye imaging (VSDI) to measure the population responses in the primary visual cortex (V1) of monkeys trained on pattern BM. We found that the animal’s behavioral performance decreased in short SOAs, and reaction times became slower relative to long SOAs. The V1 population response showed a figure-ground modulation (FGm) where the FGm in late time (∼200 ms after stimulus onset) was higher in longer SOAs, which corresponded with better stimulus visibility. A classification analysis revealed spatial reorganization of the informative regions in V1 that could discriminate between trials with high and low visibility. Our results suggest that the mask interferes with figure-ground segregation in V1, possibly leading to diminished stimulus visibility.

Results

Behavioral performance in the backward masking task

Two monkeys (F and T) were trained on a texture discrimination task (TDT) with pattern BM (Figure 1). The monkeys were required to discriminate between two texture stimuli that included either a horizontal or a vertical target (Figure 1Ai). The texture stimuli were comprised of oriented line segments, while a target consisted of three horizontally or vertically aligned lines that were segmented from the background lines only by their orientation difference (Figure 1Ai). In each trial, the monkeys acquired and then maintained fixation on a small fixation point, for a random time interval (Figure 1Aii). Next, a texture stimulus, either a horizontal or vertical stimulus, appeared briefly (40 ms) on the screen. Then, after a short interval, termed SOA, a mask stimulus appeared for 100 ms (Figure 1Ai, right). The SOA duration varied (50, 75, 100, and 150 ms) across the sessions. Following mask offset, the monkeys were required to report the target orientation by making a saccade (Figure 1Aii).

Next, we assessed the monkeys’ behavioral performance in the BM task and found that it depends on the SOA duration, as was previously reported. The detection rate of horizontal and vertical stimuli (defined as: the percentage of correct trials out of the total number of trials, see STAR Methods) was significantly higher in the longest SOA tested (150 ms) compared to the shortest one tested (50 ms; Figure 1B): 93.01% ± 4.84% (mean ± SEM) vs. 79.35% ± 5.80% in monkey F and 92.47% ± 1.41% vs. 67.44% ± 8.05% in monkey T (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 respectively; Wilcoxon rank-sum test). An inverse trend was observed when comparing the reaction time (RT; defined as the time interval from the mask offset until the reporting saccade has landed): as the SOA increased, the RT became shorter (Figure 1C). In monkey F, the RT increased from 117.25 ± 8.44 ms when the SOA was long (150 ms) to 171.47 ± 3.74 ms when the SOA was short. In monkey T, the RT increased from 207.28 ± 7.45 ms when the SOA was long to 234.45 ± 7.34 ms when the SOA was short (p < 0.001 for both monkeys). These behavioral results are consistent with previous reports from humans6,24,25,26,27,28 and monkeys12,29,30,31 that performed a BM task. Moreover, the RTs in error trials (trials in which the animal has reported on perceiving the opposite target to that presented) were significantly longer than the RTs in the correct trials (Figures S1A and S1B) by an average of 57.6 ms and 38.7 ms, in monkey F and T, respectively (p < 0.001 for both monkeys, for all SOAs combined). Moreover, when dividing the RTs into bins according to their lengths, we found that the trials with faster RTs were associated with higher fraction of correct trials within each SOA (Figures S1C and S1D). The longer RT in error trials, as well as the dependency of the percentage correct trials on the RT length, support the notion that trials with longer RTs reflect poorer visibility of the target.

While the animals performed the BM task, we recorded the population response in area V1 at high spatial (mesoscale, 1702 μm2/pixel) and temporal resolution (100 Hz) using voltage-sensitive dye imaging (VSDI32). The fluorescence dye signal of each pixel reflects the sum of membrane potential from a population of neurons (rather than single cells), not only emphasizing subthreshold membrane potentials but also reflecting suprathreshold membrane potentials (i.e., spiking activity of neuronal populations33,34).

Mapping the targets and background parts of the stimulus onto V1

To study population responses in the BM task, we first mapped the different stimulus’ parts (target and background) onto the V1 imaging area (see STAR Methods). Therefore, on each recording day, the monkeys initially performed a simple fixation paradigm, where a vertical (Figure 2Ai) or horizontal (Figure 2Bi) target alone, without the background elements, was briefly presented. The two targets shared one common element (Figure 2Ci, yellow element), while the other elements did not overlap (Figure 2Ci, red and blue elements for the vertical and horizontal target, respectively). Next, we used VSDI to obtain the retinotopic mapping, i.e., the spatial pattern of the population response in V1 to each of the targets. Figures 2Aii and 2Bii show the population response maps for the vertical target and horizontal target at peak response amplitude, whereas Figures 2Di and 2Dii show the temporal evolvement of the distinct activation patterns in response to each of the targets in V1. Using the retinotopic activation maps, we defined the vertical and horizontal region of interests (ROIs) by fitting an elliptical contour to the activation patches in V1 evoked by the vertical or horizontal targets (Figures 2Aii and 2Bii, black contours). Next, we defined a common ROI, which corresponds to the shared element by the two targets: vertical ROI and horizontal ROI overlapping V1 region (Figure 2Cii, yellow contour). The remaining non-overlapping parts between the vertical and horizontal ROI were defined as vertical-only and horizontal-only ROIs (Figure 2Cii, red and blue contours, corresponding to the non-overlapping elements in the two targets). The V1 area outside the target ROIs was defined as the general background region (Figure 2Cii).

The stimulus-alone and mask-alone evoked V1 responses and their relation to the backward masking paradigm

To investigate the neural response to the stimulus from that of the mask, the monkeys performed a fixation paradigm while the stimulus alone (40 ms duration; not followed by a mask; Figure 3Ai) was presented. In a different set of fixation trials, the mask alone appeared (100 ms duration, as in the BM task; Figure 3Bi; see STAR Methods). Figures 3Aii and 3Bii show the temporal evolvement of the VSD maps following stimulus or mask onset (the time points of the maps are depicted on the time courses [TCs] of the V1 VSD signal in Figure 3Aiii and 3Biii, respectively). Unlike the target-alone stimulus, which elicited distinct patches of activation within the V1 region (Figure 2), the stimulus-alone, i.e., the full-texture stimulus and mask-alone stimulus, elicited widespread activation across the entire imaged V1 area. Within this widespread activation (which is expected due to the multiple oriented bars and the stimulus size), the population activity evoked by the target elements could not be easily discriminated from that evoked by the background elements. Next, we computed the TC of the response (mean overall V1 pixels and across trials) for the stimulus-alone condition (Figure 3Aiii) and mask-alone condition (Figure 3Biii; the TC of the evoked population response is longer than the stimulus duration itself, which is typical for a V1 response in studies using very brief stimuli).12 In addition, as the stimuli flashed over a dark background, the population response decayed slowly to the baseline level within the time window (see STAR Methods).

Figure 3Ci shows the TC of the VSD response (mean across trials) to the stimulus-alone (black curve) and to the mask-alone (orange-to-brown curves). The onset time of the mask in the mask-alone trials was varied to match the mask onset in the different SOAs, from short to long SOAs. The VSD TC was normalized to the peak response of each condition. The results in Figure 3Ci show, as expected, that as the mask onset occurs earlier, the mask-alone-evoked response appears closer in time to the stimulus-alone response, resulting in larger overlap between the two responses. Figure 3Cii shows the TCs of the VSD signal for the stimulus-alone condition and the mask-alone condition, separated for the different onset times of the mask. While for the late-onset mask (150 ms) there is only a small overlap between the stimulus-evoked and the mask-evoked response (Figure 3Cii, light-orange area), as the mask onset time starts earlier, the mask-evoked response shifts earlier, and there is a larger overlap with the stimulus-evoked response. When mask onset occurs at 50 ms (equivalent to SOA 50 ms), there is a large overlap between the mask-evoked and the stimulus-evoked response (Figure 3Cii). To quantify the overlap between the two responses, we computed the enclosed area between them and divided it by the response to the stimulus-alone, for each SOA (Figure 3Ciii; see STAR Methods). In the longest mask onset condition (equivalent to SOA 150 ms), the overlap area covers 16.5% ± 0.62% of the stimuli-related area, as opposed to a 40.46% ± 1.23% overlap in SOA 100 ms and a 66.0% ± 1.02% overlap in SOA 50 ms (p value range: p < 0.01 to p < 0.001, signed-rank for difference from 0 and rank-sum for the difference between mask onset times). These results suggest that as the SOA shortens, the neural response to the mask becomes more disruptive due to a larger overlap with the stimulus-related response. Yet, it appears that the early part of the stimulus-evoked response (<100 ms) is not affected by the mask.

Next, we investigated the V1 response in the BM task, which elicited wide-spread activation throughout the V1 imaging area. The sequence of VSD maps in Figure 3D shows the spatiotemporal activation patterns in V1 evoked by a vertical stimulus followed by a mask (SOA 150 ms) in an example session. The VSD maps show two temporal phases of increased neural activation, corresponding to the population response evoked by the stimulus (response window: ∼60–180 ms) and by the mask (response window: ∼220–340 ms). Next, we computed the TCs of the population response in the SOA 150 ms and SOA 50 ms conditions for the same example session in Figure 3D. Figure 3Ei shows that the TCs of the population response in the two SOAs conditions exhibit an early peak of activation (∼110–130 ms after stimulus onset) with a similar amplitude (2.10 × 10−3 ± 2.3 × 10−5 and 2.0 × 10−3 ± 2.4 × 10−5 mean ± SEM across trials, respectively; non-significant difference between the SOAs; p ≪ 0.001 for the difference from zero for both SOAs). Interestingly, the response to the SOA 150 ms has a second peak of activation at ∼280–300 ms (1.60 × 10−3 ± 3.7 × 10−5 amplitude; p ≪ 0.001 for the difference from zero), evoked by mask onset (150 ms after stimulus onset). However, for SOA 50 (i.e., mask onset is 50 ms after stimulus onset), there is no clear second phase of increased activation that corresponds to mask onset. The VSD TC suggests the response to the mask at SOA 50 was merged with the stimulus-evoked activity. The population response to SOA 50 at 280–300 ms was 9.97 × 10−4 ± 4.6 × 10−5, which is significantly lower than the response SOA 150 (p < 0.001) and significantly higher from zero (p < 0.001; signed-rank test).

Next, we investigated the VSD response to the BM in relation to the stimulus-alone and mask-alone responses. Figure 3Eii shows the population response TC (mean across trials) from the BM condition along with the population response to the stimulus-alone and mask-alone at matching times to the SOAs. In the VSD response to the BM, the initial response fits with the stimulus-alone response, for all SOAs (black curve vs. brown-orange curves). For SOA 150 ms, the second phase of increased activation clearly fits with the response to the mask-alone of the matched SOA time. Yet, the population response in the BM task, at the time corresponding to the mask, shows a lower response of activation than that evoked by the mask-alone, suggesting a stimulus-mask interaction in the BM. To quantify this interaction, we computed an interaction index (Figure 3Eiii; see STAR Methods). This index measures the relative difference between the VSD responses to the stimulus and mask alone versus the response to the mask in the BM task. Larger values of the interaction index indicate that the response to the mask in the BM was more similar to the individual VSD responses to the mask alone and stimulus alone. The results in Figure 3Eiii show that the interaction index increased significantly from 0.43 ± 0.006 at short SOAs to 0.51 ± 0.01 in long SOAs (p ≪ 0.001). As Figure 3Eii shows, the response to the mask in the BM paradigm was suppressed relative to the response to the mask alone. For an additional analysis of this “mask suppression” effect, see Figure S2.

In summary, the early part of the stimulus-evoked response (<100 ms) is not affected by the mask at any SOAs. However, it appears that the mask becomes more disruptive at shorter SOAs, affecting mainly the later time of stimulus processing. Neuronal activity at early time after stimulus onset was previously reported to involve information processing of basic stimulus features (e.g., orientation and motion35). In contrast, neural responses at later time have been reported to also involve feedback influences and carry information on figure-from-background segregation, crucial for the texture discrimination and differentiating between horizontal and vertical stimuli.

The mask interferes with the figure-ground modulation in V1

The segregation of the target elements from the background in each texture stimulus is a crucial step toward discriminating between horizontal and vertical stimuli. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the visual system needs to segregate each target from its background, a process known as FG segregation. The neural signature of this process in V1 was previously reported in monkeys36,37,38,39,40,41,42 and can be quantified by the neural response difference between the figure ROI and the background ROI. To demonstrate the existence of this response difference, termed FGm,37 in the texture discrimination task, we analyzed the V1 response when the animal needed to report the target orientation in the stimulus-alone trials (without mask appearance; see STAR Methods). Figure 4A shows a sequence of VSD maps from an example session, for a horizontal stimulus condition. The contour depicts the vertical-only (gray) and horizontal-only (black) ROIs. Following stimulus onset, a widespread increase in neural activation in V1 (map = 100 ms) was observed in the VSD maps. Interestingly, ∼200 ms after stimulus onset (stimulus duration was 40 ms as in the BM paradigm), the VSD maps showed increased population activity in the horizontal-only ROI, i.e., the “figure” region for the horizontal stimulus, while the population response in the surrounding background regions was lower. In particular, the neural response in the vertical-only ROI, which is part of the background region for the vertical stimulus, was lower. Figure 4B shows the VSD TC of response in the figure (horizontal-only) ROI vs. background (vertical-only) ROI for the data in Figure 4A. The VSD signal shows increased activity in the target relative to the background (the response difference between the figure and background is 1.28 × 10−4 ± 2.51 × 10−5) at early times (80–100 ms, p < 0.01 for the difference between the figure and the background) and a further increase at later times (5.7 × 10−4 ± 4.3 × 10−5, 180–200 ms; p≪0.001 for the difference between the figure and the background). These results, together with the spatial pattern of activation shown in the VSD maps, reveal the neuronal signature of FGm in V1 for the textured stimulus (see Figure S3 for FGm in a texture stimulus with a different background).

Next, we performed a grand analysis (i.e., analysis for all trials) of the FGm in the BM paradigm. In the vertical-stimulus trials, we defined the vertical-only ROI as the figure ROI and the horizontal-only ROI as the background ROI. The opposite was used for the horizontal-stimulus trials, where the horizontal-only ROI was defined as the figure and the vertical-only ROI was defined as the background ROI (see STAR Methods; this enabled us to use exactly the same ROIs for the figure and the background in any of the stimuli). To compute the FGm as a function of time, we randomly paired horizontal and vertical trials and calculated the population activity difference between the response in the figure ROI and the background ROI. Results were averaged across all trial pairs (see STAR Methods). Next, we pooled together all SOA 150 and 100 trials and termed them as “long SOAs” and all SOA 75 and 50 trials as “short SOAs.” Figure 4Ci shows that the TCs of the FGm for monkey F (Figure 4Ci) show an early peak at around 80–100 ms after stimulus onset, reaching a level of 7.03 × 10−5 ± 1.08 × 10−5 for the long SOAs (p < 0.001 for the difference from zero; p ≪ 0.001 for the difference between the real vs. label-shuffled data) and 4.98 × 10−5 ± 8.31 × 10−6 for the short SOAs (p = 0.025 for the difference from the long SOAs, p < 0.001 for the difference from zero, and p < 0.001 for the difference from label-shuffled data). A second peak appears in the long SOAs at around 180–200 ms, reaching an amplitude of 6.45 × 10−5 ± 1.53 × 10−5, which is much larger than that for the short SOAs, which is −1.67 × 10−5 ± 1.41 × 10−5 (Figure 4Cii; p < 0.001 for the comparison between long and short SOAs). In monkey T, the FGm as a function of time does not show two distinct peaks (monkey T was presented with a lower luminance stimulus; see STAR Methods) but does show a similar deviation of the long SOAs from the short SOAs at late times. In the long SOAs at ∼180–200 ms, the FGm reached a level of 6.11 × 10−5 ± 1.18 × 10−5, as opposed to 1.82 × 10−5 ± 1.58 × 10−5 for the short SOAs (Figure 4Dii; p < 0.01). The early population response peak (80–100 ms) for the long SOAs reached 5.43 × 10−5 ± 8.31 × 10−6, whereas in the short SOAs it reached 5.31 × 10−5 ± 1.28 × 10−5 (Figure 4Dii; no significant difference between short and long SOAs).

The results of the FGm analysis suggest that the mask mainly interferes with late FGm processing by significantly reducing it, which might translate into the suppression of targets’ visibility and the ability to discriminate one from each another. The decreased FGm in the short SOAs also aligns with the monkeys’ reduced behavioral performance when comparing long to short SOAs (an average decrease of ∼12%). Figure 4E shows the pooled FGm response for the two animals (mean across the two animals), separated into the different SOAs. The first FGm peak is similar in all SOAs and therefore has very low correlation with the SOA duration and animals’ behavioral performance. At a later time, from ∼150 to 250 ms, the curves of the FGm in the different SOAs clearly deviates from one another and seem to arrange from high to low FGm according to the SOA duration. It is also noticeable that the FGm curve for each individual SOA declines toward the baseline after mask onset (plus ∼50 ms delay due to the delay of propagation from the retina to V1). Next, we used a linear fit for the FGm at the early and late peak times as a function of the SOA (Figure 4F). Interestingly, the late FGm highly correlated with the SOA duration (Pearson correlation coefficient, r = 0.995, p = 0.005), while the early FGm’s correlation with the SOA was weaker and also non-significant (r = 0.574, p = 0.426). In addition, the late FGm had a significantly larger slope compared to the early FGm (9.36 × 10−7 and 8.13 × 10−8, respectively, p = 0.0012; see STAR Methods).

To examine whether the temporal phases in the FGm curves (Figures 4C and 4D) can be described by independent components, we established a descriptive mathematical model (Data S2 and Figure S4, see two different approaches). We defined three distinct components comprised of two temporal phases, corresponding to an early FGm and a late FGm, and a third component that describes the “disruptive” effect of the mask on the FGm. Using this approach, we were able to obtain a good fit between the model and FGm data (Figures 4C and 4D), while implementing the early and late FGm as independent components and confirming that the response to the mask interferes with the late FGm. The early FGm may reflect mainly the orientation processing in V1 that initiates first, at early time after stimulus onset, whereas the late FGm arises later and reflects the top-down loops carrying FGm information from higher visual areas to lower visual areas.35

SVM trained on VSD maps successfully classified trials with a horizontal or vertical target

While the FGm analysis suggested that the mask interferes with FG segregation processing in V1, it was computed based on the responses in the target ROIs and was also averaged across multiple trials. To investigate the spatial patterns at higher resolution, i.e., the contribution of single-pixel responses to the stimulus discrimination, as well as to investigate the robustness at the single-trial level, we applied an SVM (support vector machine) classifier to the single trials.

To verify the applicability of an SVM classifier to the VSD data, we first implemented it on the simple task of discriminating between fixation trials in which only a horizontal or vertical target was presented to the monkey (see STAR Methods). Figure 5A shows the VSD maps from V1, for an example session in monkey F: Figures 5Ai and 5Aii show the population response map evoked by a vertical and horizontal target, respectively (see target stimuli in Figures 2Ai and 2Bi). The VSD maps were averaged at the time of peak response (70–110 ms after target onset). Our assumption was that in order to discriminate between the horizontal and vertical targets, the SVM model will converge to opposite weights (i.e., negative and positive weights) for pixels in the non-overlapping target ROIs.

Next, an SVM classifier was trained with a set of VSD maps (70–110 ms after target onset) from the same example session as in Figure 5A. Here, each frame in a single trial was used as a single example for the classifier (see STAR Methods). Figure 5B shows the accuracy of the SVM model aligned on stimulus onset (gray rectangle indicates the time window used for training the model). As expected, the model accuracy at baseline, i.e., before the stimulus-evoked response in V1, is near-chance level, 51.7% ± 6.01% (mean ± SEM; there is no significant difference between real and shuffled data). However, following target onset, the model’s accuracy reached 100% (within the training window; p < 0.01, rank-sum test between real and shuffled data). Figure 5Ci shows the weight map generated by computing the mean weights across all models (LOOCV, see STAR Methods), where warm/cold colors denote pixels with positive/negative weights. As expected, the largest absolute weights of the model appear at the non-overlapping target ROIs, while in the common ROI of the two targets, the weight values are closer to zero. This is demonstrated in the histograms in Figure 5Cii, showing the weight distributions in the non-overlapping ROIs (horizontal-only ROI: −4.28 × 10−4 ± 4.31 × 10−6; vertical-only ROI: 4.59 × 10−4 ± 4.84 × 10−5 [mean ± STD], p < 0.001, for the difference between the ROIs; rank-sum test). Figure 5D shows the SVM grand analysis across all target-alone trials (n = 62, all sessions of monkey F): the model accuracy shows a near-chance level (p value is non-significant between real vs. shuffled data, rank-sum) before stimulus onset and high accuracy (∼100%) after stimulus onset (p < 0.001). Figure 5E shows the grand analysis of the mean weights in the non-overlapping ROIs and in the common ROI. In the horizontal-only ROI, the mean weight value is −6.05 × 10−4 ± 1.95 × 10−5 (mean ± SEM), in the vertical-only ROI 5.73 × 10−4 ± 2.13 × 10−5, and in the common ROI 4.75 × 10−5 ± 2.13 × 10−5. These values differ significantly from each other (p < 0.001; rank-sum test).

In monkey T (Figures 5F–5J), the SVM reaches 100% accuracy in the example session (Figure 5G), and 93.9% ± 2.2% accuracy in the grand analysis model (Figure 5I). The mean weight value (Figure 5J) in the horizontal-only ROI was −1.16 × 10−3 ± 9.47 × 10−5, in the vertical-only ROI 9.10 × 10−4± 6.75 × 10−5, and in the common ROI 5.16 × 10−4 ± 7.00 × 10−5 (p < 0.001).

These results demonstrate that the SVM model can proficiently distinguish between trials evoked by horizontal and vertical targets alone, with a high degree of accuracy, and that the temporal and spatial properties of the model align with the expected results. Interestingly, the SVM weight maps of the target-alone trials also offer the most informative pixels for solving the BM paradigm, because the horizontal and vertical stimuli in the BM task differ mainly in the non-overlap target elements that correspond to the vertical-only and horizontal-only ROIs.

SVM of the BM data shows higher accuracy for long SOAs and higher accuracy for correct vs. error trials

We next applied the SVM model to the VSD data from the BM paradigm, where the target stimulus is comprised of oriented target elements embedded in a textured background, followed by a mask stimulus. A sliding window of 50 ms was used to train and test data (grand analysis across all trials; see STAR Methods). This approach allowed us to generate a time-dependent curve of classification accuracy as a function of time from stimulus onset. Figure 6Ai shows the TC of accuracy for the SVM model of long and short SOAs in monkey F. Two peaks of accuracy are apparent in both SOAs. The first peak appears during the initial response of V1 activation (∼80–100 ms), which previously was suggested to encode basic stimulus features. Interestingly, the second accuracy peak occurs approximately at the same time in which we observed the late phase of the FGm (∼180–200 ms in long SOAs). Figure 6Aii shows that there is a non-significant difference in accuracy between the long and short SOAs during the first peak (0.89 ± 0.01 for long SOAs and 0.88 ± 0.011 for short SOAs, no significant difference between the short and long SOAs, p < 0.001 for the real and shuffled data; rank-sum test). In the late peak, the accuracy for the long SOAs is significantly higher than for the short SOAs: 0.88 ± 0.015 vs. 0.79 ± 0.017, p < 0.001 for the difference between the real and shuffled data and between the long and short SOAs, rank-sum test). Figure 6C shows the same analysis for monkey T with similar results: there is a significant accuracy difference between the long and short SOAs at the late time, although the accuracy values are smaller (see STAR Methods; the stimulus luminance in monkey T was lower). We hypothesize that the decrease in accuracy for the short SOAs at the late time is caused by the mask, which may interfere with stimulus processing and the FGm. In an additional analysis, we implemented a unified SVM model (trained on all SOAs trials, grouped together; Figure S5) that confirmed the early peak of accuracy (Figure S5A). However, the late phase of the unified SVM model showed mixed results of the accuracy difference between the short and long SOAS in the two animals. This is probably due to the fact that the late phase of the VSD response reflects mixed population responses across the SOAs (i.e., a population response to a mask or a black screen).

Next, using the SVM models for correct trials only, we tested the model’s accuracy on error trials (see STAR Methods). Error trials were defined as trials in which the animal was presented with a horizontal stimulus but reported on a vertical stimulus and vice versa. Figures 6B and 6D show that the model accuracy was significantly lower in error trials at both time points, by 8%–11% in monkey F (p < 0.05–p < 0.01) and by 9% in monkey T (p < 0.01). Finally, the top panel in Figure 6E shows the TC of the VSD signal (mean across trials) in V1 as a function of time. Superimposed on the TC with color coding is the SVM model’s accuracy (see the bottom panel of Figure 6E for the TC of the model accuracy). It is interesting to note that the peak accuracy of the model’s performance does not coincide with the peak response (i.e., highest activity) of the VSD signal. In particular, while the first peak of accuracy aligns with the rising phase of the VSD signal, the late phase of accuracy aligns with a decreased VSD response after stimulus onset. This result suggests that a high level of neural activity does not necessarily mean high accuracy in the model.

The model weight maps reveal distinct spatial organization for early and late times

To gain insight into how the model solves the BM task, we examined the weights assigned to each pixel in V1, i.e., the weight map (grand analysis across all trials; see STAR Methods). Figure 7A shows the weight maps of the model trained on SOA 150 and SOA 50 trials at early and late times, which correspond to the SVM accuracy peaks. The early maps reveal similar patterns for both the long and the short SOAs. A mixture of positive and negative weight patches is observed across the V1 area and also with the target ROIs. These patches are relatively small and may relate to individual elements in the texture stimulus or their interactions. Importantly, in SOA 150, at the late time, the vertical-only ROI shows mainly positive weights and the horizontal-only ROI mainly negative weights, indicating a clear spatial arrangement reminiscent of the SVM weight maps for the target-alone stimuli (Figure 5C). In contrast, the model weight map for SOA 50 has a less clear pattern. Figure 7B shows the distribution histograms of the pixel weights in the horizontal-only and vertical-only ROIs. The weight difference between the two ROIs is largest for SOA 150 at the late time: −1.43 × 10−3 ± 2.45 × 10−3 in the horizontal ROI vs. 1.38 × 10−3 ± 1.98 × 10−3 in the vertical ROI (mean ± STD), with a difference of 2.81 × 10−3 (p < 0.001 for the comparison between the two distributions, rank-sum test). Moreover, the area under the curve (AUC; see STAR Methods) shows a value of 0.81 (Figure 7B, top right, inset). In contrast, the weight difference for the same SOA at the early time is much smaller: −1.12 × 10−3 ± 4.06 × 10−3 vs. 1.08 × 10−4 ± 3.66 × 10−3, respectively, with a difference of 1.23 × 10−3 (p < 0.001 for the two weight distributions, rank-sum test). In this case, the AUC is 0.58, almost at chance level (Figure 7B, top left, inset). As to SOA 50, similar small weight differences were observed in the weight distribution at the early (−4.62 × 10−4, AUC 0.476) and late (−5.23 × 10−4, AUC 0.433) times. Figure 7Ci shows the pooled weight difference of the two monkeys as a function of time. While at the early time (80–100 ms) there seems to be a small separation between the SOAs, in the late time (180–200 ms) there is a clear separation, revealing that longer SOAs show a larger difference between the weights in horizontal-only and vertical-only targets. Figure 7Cii shows a linear fit for the weight difference of early and late times as a function of the SOA. While both the early and late peak show high correlation values, only that of the late peak is statistically significant (r = 0.99, p = 0.009). Moreover, the slope of the weight difference was significantly larger at late time compared to the early time (3.33 × 10−5 and 1.22 × 10−5 respectively; p = 0.015). Figure 7Ciii shows the AUC analysis for the weights’ distributions in the early and the late time. In an additional analysis, we implemented a unified SVM model (trained on all SOAs trials, grouped together) and compared the performance to the SVM model that was trained on each SOA separately. The weight map for the first peak of the accuracy (Figure S5B, left) was similar to the SOA specific model. However, the weight maps of the late accuracy peak showed a decrease in weight differences between the vertical-only and horizontal-only pixels in the two animals, with lower AUC values for the pixels in each target (Figure S5B, right; Figure S5C). This is probably due to the fact that the VSD response in the late temporal phase includes mixed population response that vary across the SOAs (depending on the visual input on the screen: a mask stimulus or a black screen).

To further examine whether the SOA weight maps are similar to those from the target-alone trials (Figures 5C and 5H), we computed the correlation between the weight maps of the different SOAs and the weight maps of the target-alone trials. The linear regression of the correlation as a function of time (Figure 7Di) is similar to the results shown in Figure 7Ci. The correlation analysis shows a larger separation between the SOAs at the late time, with the highest correlation values for SOA 150 and lowest for SOA 50. Moreover, as can be seen from Figure 7Dii, the correlation at the late times is strongly dependent on the SOA (r = 1, p = 0). In addition, there was a significantly larger slope for the correlation at the late time compared to the early time (3.31 × 10−3 and 6.88 × 10−4, respectively, p = 0.00078). We suggest that the spatial pattern at the late time reflects a top-down feedback influence into V1, which encodes the late FGm, representing the target and its segregation from the background region.

Classification analysis using a neural network model supports the SVM results

To test whether the SVM results derived the VSD data can be replicated using a different approach, we used a convolutional neural network (CNN) model (see supplementary material). Unlike SVM, which treats each pixel as an independent feature, CNNs are better suited for image classification as they can capture spatial patterns and spatial correlation between the image pixels. Utilizing the same VSD dataset and time windows as in the SVM analysis, we first tested the ability of the CNN to classify VSD data using the target-alone data. The accuracy of the CNN model on the target-alone trials showed high values following stimulus onset (99.7% and 83% for monkey F and T, respectively; Data S4 and Figures S6A and S6B). Analysis of feature importance (SHAP, see supplemental information) confirmed the strong contribution of vertical and horizontal ROI pixels to the classification task in both animals. In monkey F, significantly different mean SHAP values were obtained for the horizontal-only, vertical-only, and common ROIs: −1.71 ± 0.04 (mean ± SEM), 1.42 ± 0.033, and 0.04 ± 0.035, respectively (p ≪ 0.01, rank-sum test). A similar pattern was observed in monkey T: −1.52 ± 0.07, 1.67 ± 0.06 and −0.137 ± 0.09, respectively (p ≪ 0.01, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). In the BM data, the TC of model accuracy for the horizontal and vertical trials showed a temporal pattern similar to that observed in the SVM model: an early peak followed by a late peak in time (Figure S6C). Using the CNN model, we confirmed a deviation between the short and long SOAs at the late time, with higher accuracy for the long SOAs. The SHAP analysis revealed similar spatial patterns to those revealed by the SVM analysis (Figure S6D).

Discussion

Using VSDI, we investigated the V1 population responses in pattern BM. The neural activation revealed a FGm that was lower for short SOAs in comparison to the long SOAs. An SVM classification revealed the difference in the temporal dynamics of the model accuracy in relation to stimulus visibility and spatial reorganization of the informative pattern of activation in long vs. short SOAs. Our results suggest that the mask interferes with the late neuronal activity in V1, which is corresponding to the late FGm in V1 and can lead to reduced stimulus visibility.

The monkeys’ behavioral performance at shorter SOAs was reduced (Figure 1), which is in accordance with previous studies in human6,25,28 and monkeys,12,29,30,31,43 and in rodents the behavioral effects in BM are under debate.44 In addition, the monkeys’ RT increased as the SOA shortened, suggesting that a weaker stimulus visibility requires extended processing times for report (and may also reflect a lower confidence of the taken decision). These results are consistent with a study in humans by Bacon-Mace et al.,27 who showed that RTs were significantly slower in the short SOAs in comparison with the long ones (see also Bacon-Macé et al. and Schubert et al.45,46). A few other studies have investigated the relationship between stimulus visibility and RTs in monkeys, using tasks such as contrast detection47 or motion detection.48 These studies reported on longer RTs under conditions of reduced stimulus visibility. These observations are aligned with our findings, where shorter SOAs were associated with longer RTs and reduced visibility (i.e., poorer performance rates).

We found that the V1 responses to the mask-alone and stimulus-alone showed an increasing overlap in time as the SOA became shorter, while the early phase of the response to the stimulus was unaffected (Figure 3C). These results are in accordance with a previous ERP study in humans28 and suggest that while the feedforward transfer of stimulus information to V1 was preserved at all SOAs, the mask overlap, and thus interference with the stimulus processing, was larger in short SOAs. By precisely mapping the different stimulus parts, i.e., the targets (the “figures”) and background regions onto the V1 surface, we computed the FGm in the BM paradigm and studied the effect of the mask on the FGm for the different SOAs. Interestingly, the TCs of the FGm for short and long SOAs showed a deviation at late time (∼200 ms after stimulus onset), with higher FGm values for long SOAs. This observation was consistent with the no-mask condition, which revealed the spatial signature of FGm (Figures 4A, 4B, and S3; increased activity in the target ROI and decreased activity in the background ROI) and showed an elevated FGm from early to late time.

The emergence of FGm at late time was previously reported36,37,38,39,41,42 along with additional studies that described an early time component of the FGm. The latter appears during the rising phase of the stimulus-evoked activity and relates to information processing of basic stimulus features (e.g., orientation35). Our results show that the mask did not interfere with the early phase of FGm (up to ∼100 ms after stimulus onset; Figures 3C, 4E, and 4F); however, it disrupted the late FGm (Figures 4E, 4F, and S4 ). Moreover, we found a higher correlation and larger slope between the FGm and the SOA duration for the late FGm as compared with the early FGm. This is in accordance with Lamme et al.,12 who recorded the spiking activity of V1 neurons in monkeys and showed that the FGm signals are selectively and strongly suppressed at SOAs that induced invisibility of the stimulus. Several studies have proposed that FGm depends on feedback from extra-striate areas.37,38,39,41,49,50 Therefore, one can suggest that the masking interrupts the recurrent interactions between V1 and higher visual areas.

To investigate the most informative pixels in V1 for the BM task, at the single trial level, we applied an SVM classifier for each time point along the VSD TC. We used the SVM decoding accuracy to quantify the amount of task-related information that existed in V1 as function of time. The accuracy for the target-alone stimuli increased at the rising phase of the population response to the stimulus and persisted in V1 long after the stimuli was turned off, suggesting that the residual activity in V1 continues to hold information about the stimulus features. The weight maps showed, as expected, that the neural activity in the non-overlapping target ROIs carry the information to distinguish between the two stimuli. This analysis showed that SVM is proficient in discriminating single trials of horizontal and vertical targets trials and suggested also the spatial solution for solving the BM paradigm. In the BM task, the decoding level at early time was similar across SOAs; however, the accuracy at the late time revealed difference between long and short SOAs that fits with the behavioral report on stimulus visibility (Figures 6A and 6C). We suggest that the decoding accuracy at early time reflects the feedforward information and V1 local processing of the element’s orientation.12,35,37 The late time of accuracy seems to reflect feedback influences from higher brain regions that drives the late FGm and was shown to be linked with behavioral report.12,37,38,41,49,50,51 Figure 4E further showed a mismatch between the VSD response amplitude and the decoder accuracy at the late time. A previous study reported that the RH VSD dyes stain mainly the upper cortical layers,52 thus the VSD signal sums the membrane potential from all the neuronal elements in the upper layers (mainly 2–3) of the cortex. This staining pattern cannot support the option of different neuronal contributions from different cortical layers and therefore cannot explain the mismatch between the VSD response amplitude and decoding accuracy. Rather, we suggest that this mismatch can be explained by the fact that the late FGm, which is a small neural modulation, arrives at late time to V1, when the stimulus was turned off and the large population response due to the feedforward processing of the stimulus is washed away.

Additional analysis showed a lower level of decoder accuracy for the error trials relative to the correct trials, in both the early and late time phases. This result suggests that the error trials may emerge due to degraded neural activity either at the early or at the late time of information processing in V1. The first one may emerge from impaired feedforward transfer of stimulus information and its processing, and the late one may result from disruptions in feedback influences.

Interestingly, an analysis of the SVM weight maps revealed for long SOAs (where stimulus visibility is better) a spatial re-arrangement of the weights as function of time from stimulus onset. In the early time of the neural response, the weight map showed multiple distributed clusters all over V1, whereas at the late time the weights tended to accumulate in the target ROIs, with opposite sign in the two target ROIs. The ROC analysis in Figure 7B confirmed that the weight distributions showed a higher separation at the late time of the long SOA. Figure 7C showed that the weight difference as function of time was larger at late times, and also the correlation with the SOAs duration was higher (along with a larger slope for the linear regression fit) for the late time as compared with the early time. In addition, there was a higher correlation between the weight maps at late time and the weight maps obtained for the target-alone trials. Interestingly, the weight maps of the target-alone trials also suggested the pixels that are most informative for solving the BM paradigm: because the horizontal and vertical stimuli differ only in the non-overlap target elements reflecting the vertical-only and horizontal-only ROIs. Therefore, the SVM model uncovered the spatial rearrangement of the task-related informative activity in V1, and this spatial rearrangement matched the non-overlapped target ROIs. This rearrangement of the weights did not occur for short SOA, which suggest that the mask indeed disrupts the top-down influences.

In summary, our results support the view that feedforward processing alone is not sufficient for stimulus percept and offers a better support to the theory of disrupted cortical feedback in BM. While decoding levels during the early phase were high in all SOAs, the behavioral performance of the animal was substantially lower in short SOA, despite all information being represented in V1. We found that the animal behavioral performance was better correlated with the late phase of FGm and SVM at late time (accuracy and weight maps), which we suggest to be driven by top-down influences and is susceptible to mask interruption.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of the current study is that the neural recordings were performed in V1 only. While our findings strongly support the theory that the mask interferes with the late component of the FGm emerging from cortical feedback of higher visual areas, we did not simultaneously record the neural activity from these extrastriate regions. Future research combining recordings in V1 with areas such as V4 would be beneficial to further investigate the disruption of these recurrent interactions. Another limitation is that VSDI measures the summed subthreshold and suprathreshold membrane potentials from the population of several hundred neurons per pixel. While the VSDI technique offers a high spatial (at the meso-scale) and temporal resolution of population dynamics, it lacks single-cell resolution. We, therefore, could not dissect the neural circuitry underlying BM and distinguish between the responses of different neuronal subtypes such as excitatory and inhibitory cells or uncover the role of different cortical layers. Additional studies are required along these lines.

Method details

Behavioral task and visual stimuli

The monkeys were trained on a texture discrimination task (TDT) with pattern backward masking. The stimuli were computer generated textures, made of short line segments (Figure 1Ai). Visual stimuli were presented on a 21-inch Mitsubishi monitor at 85 Hz, placed 1 m from the monkey’s eyes and occupied 10 ° × 10 ° (line segment length: 0.5°, width: 0.1°, mean distance between line elements’ center: 0.75°). A horizontal or a vertical stimulus refers to a horizontal or a vertical target consisted of three horizontally or vertically aligned lines, that were embedded in a texture background (Figure 1Ai left and middle). The targets were segregated from the background elements by their orientation difference only. Background elements were jittered randomly (±0.1–0.3°). The mask stimulus (same size as the stimulus) was made of random oriented V-shaped elements (Figure 1Ai right). The luminance of all elements (target, background and mask elements) was 74 cd/m2 for monkey F and 23 cd/m2 for monkey T. The background luminance for both monkeys was near zero. The luminance levels of the stimuli enabled us to investigate whether the neural modulations are preserved across variable luminance conditions of the stimulus.

A trial started when the animal fixated on a small fixation point. After a random time interval (3000–4000 ms) a horizontal or vertical stimulus appeared on the screen for 40 ms. Then, after a variable period of time, a mask appeared for 100 ms (Figure 1Aii). The time interval between the stimulus onset to mask onset, is defined as stimulus-to-mask onset asynchrony (SOA). Following the mask offset (the fixation point and mask were turned off together), two small lateral points appeared and the monkey had to report the target orientation (horizontal or vertical) by making a saccade either to the right or the left, within a brief time window (500 ms). A trial was classified as correct, only if the monkey reported the target orientation correctly. The animal was rewarded with a drop of juice or water for each correct trial. The stimulated trials were interleaved with fixation-only trials (blank condition; no stimulus was presented). This condition was used to subtract the heartbeat artifact (see basic VSD analysis) and was used as baseline activity condition.

The animals were also trained on a simple fixation task, where they were required to maintain fixation throughout the entire trial (no report was requested) and were rewarded for holding the fixation. On each recording day, the animals performed a fixation task where a stimulus comprised of the horizontal or vertical target alone was turned on (Figures 2Ai and 2Bi). The duration of the target stimuli was identical to the stimulus duration in the BM task. This approach enabled us to identify in V1 the regions evoked by the targets alone and background alone, i.e., to retinotopically map the targets and background regions onto the imaging V1 area (Figures 2A–2C). In another set of experiments, the monkeys performed a fixation task, while they were presented with the stimulus-alone image (i.e., the texture stimulus: target embedded in the background as shown in Figure 1Ai; Figure 3A shows the VSD response to the stimulus-alone) or the mask-alone stimuli (Figure 1Ai; Figure 3B shows the VSD response the mask-alone). The stimulus duration was as in the BM task and mask onset time and duration was identical to the different SOAs conditions in the BM paradigm. These imaging sessions served as control conditions, to measure the VSD signal evoked by either the stimulus-alone or mask-alone (Figure 3) and enabled us to compare the V1 activity in the BM task to the VSD signal in the stimulus and mask-alone conditions (Figure 3E). In another set of experiments, the stimulus-alone appeared (for 40 ms) without the mask (Figures 4A, 4B, and 4E) and the monkey needed to report which stimulus it perceived, i.e., a vertical or horizontal stimulus, by performing a saccade either to the right or the left. The datasets for all behavioral paradigms are detailed under ‘VSDI dataset and analysis’.

Data acquisition

Two linked computers controlled the visual stimulation, data acquisition, and the monkey’s behavior (CORTEX software package). The protocol of data acquisition in VSDI has been described in detail elsewhere.32 Single trials were saved on separate data files to enable single trial analysis.

Eye-position recording

Throughout the experimental paradigm, the monkey’s eye positions were monitored by an infrared eye tracker (Dr. Bouis Device, Kalsruhe, Germany), sampled at 1 KHz and recorded at 250 Hz. Only trials with tight fixation, where the animal maintained within ±1 deg window until the report time (in the TDT with or without the BM) or until end of trial (in the simple fixation paradigms), were used for the analysis.

Surgical procedure and VSD imaging

Antibiotics and analgesics were applied before, during and after surgical procedures and adequate measurements were taken to minimize pain and discomfort. The surgical procedure has been reported in detail elsewhere.32,53 Briefly, a craniotomy was performed under full anesthesia and aseptic conditions, and the dura mater was removed, exposing the visual cortex. Then, a thin and transparent artificial dura made of silicone was implanted over the visual cortex, to enable long-term imaging. The anterior border of the imaged area was typically 3–6 mm anterior to the Lunate sulcus. For the retinotopic position of the imaged V1 see ‘Retinotopic mapping of the V1 area and the border between V1 and V2’.

We stained the cortex with voltage-sensitive-dyes: RH-1691 or RH-1838 supplied by Optical Imaging, Israel. VSDI was performed using a MiCAM Ultima system with sampling rate of 10 ms/frame (100Hz) and a spatial resolution of 10,000 pixels/frame. Each pixel sums the neuronal activity from an area of 1702 μm2 that includes a few hundred neurons. The exposed cortex was illuminated using an epi-illumination stage with an appropriate excitation filter (peak transmission of 630 nm, width at half-height of 10 nm) and a dichroic mirror (DRLP 650), both from Omega Optical, Brattleboro, VT. A barrier post-filter above the dichroic mirror (RG 665, Schott, Mainz, Germany) collected the fluorescence and rejected stray excitation light.

Retinotopic mapping of V1 and V1/V2 border

For the retinotopic mapping of the imaged V1 and the border of V1/V2, we used we used 6 sessions of optical imaging of intrinsic signals (OIIS) in monkey F and 7 sessions OIIS in monkey T. After the animal acquired fixation and maintained fixation for a random time interval (2–3 s), a stimulus appeared over the screen for 2 s. The animal was required to maintain fixation for additional 4 s after the stimulus offset. The stimuli were a 0.5° vertical bar or horizontal bar of moving square gratings. The bar position varied at increasing distances (deg) from the vertical meridian (VM) or from the horizontal meridian (HM), correspondingly. In Monkey T, the center of the imaged chamber was ∼2.25° deg below the horizontal meridian and 1° from the vertical meridian. In Monkey F, the center of the imaged chamber was ∼1.5° below the horizontal meridian and ∼2° from the vertical meridian. To map the border between V1 and V2 we performed OIIS and obtain the orientation domains and ocular dominance maps. Using these functional domains, we could map the border between V1 and V2 areas.32,53

Quantification and statistical analysis

Behavioral analysis

To study the behavioral performance in the BM experiments, we computed the performance of the animal as the percent correct trials in each session, i.e., the fraction of correct trials relative to the total number trials (correct and error trials) in the same session. Trials with early break fixation (the monkey did not keep fixation until the mask and fixation point offset) were excluded from analysis. A correct trial was defined as a trial in which the monkey identified correctly the target’s orientation and reported within 500 ms whether the target was vertical or horizontal by a saccade. Error trials were defined as trials with wrong responses, i.e., a horizontal stimulus was presented but the animal reported on a vertical stimulus and vice versa. We computed the percentage of correct trials for each SOA session separately and created a psychometric curve for each monkey (Figure 1B; Monkey F: n = 8, 6, 8 & 6 sessions, Monkey T: n = 6, 12, 10 & 12 sessions for SOAs 50, 75, 100 & 150 ms respectively). We also computed the reaction time (RT) of the monkeys that is defined as the time interval from fixation point and mask offset to the onset of the reporting saccade. The RT analysis was done at the single trial level for correct trials (Figure 1C; Monkey F: n = 173, 111, 171, 106 trials for SOAs 50, 75, 100 & 150 ms respectively. Monkey T: n = 124, 354, 358 & 399 correct trials for SOAs 50, 75, 100 & 150 ms respectively). An RT analysis was done at the single trial level also for error trials and compared to the correct trials (Figure S1). This single trial analysis at the behavioral level was done to fir with the grand analysis of the VSD data was done on the pooled dataset of single trials. While it is possible to compute RT at the single trial level, it is not possible when computing percentage of correct trials, which is done at the session level.

VSDI dataset

VSDI data was obtained from the right hemispheres of two adult monkeys (Macaca fascicularis; F and T, 9 and 12 Kg). A total of 27 and 28 VSDI sessions were analyzed in monkey F and T respectively (11 imaging days). This dataset is a little smaller than the behavioral dataset because it includes removal of VSD trials with high noise37 and matching trial numbers between vertical and horizontal stimuli for SVM and FGm analysis. The dataset for VSDI retinotopic mapping of the target and background regions in V1 (Figure 2) was comprised from 62 trials (4 sessions) in monkey F, and 100 trials (6 sessions) in monkey T. The same dataset was used for training the SVM model of the target-alone trials, i.e., the retinotopic data (Figure 5). The VSDI dataset for BM paradigm was comprised from: Monkey F: 14 sessions that included 158, 98, 150, 84 trials for SOAs 50, 75, 100 & 150 ms respectively. Monkey T: n = 20 sessions that included 70, 208, 190 & 248 correct trials for SOAs 50, 75, 100 & 150 ms respectively (Figures 3, 4, 6, and 7). The dataset for the stimulus-alone and mask-alone was comprised of 33 trials for stimulus-alone (3 sessions) and 29, 26 & 15 trials for the mask-alone stimulus (5 sessions), using onset times that were matched to the mask in those in the BM task: SOAs 50, 100 & 150 ms respectively (Figure 3). For the example session of the TDT paradigm without the mask, we used 29 trials from one session in monkey F (Figures 4A and 4B). For the grand analysis of the no mask condition in the FGm analysis (Figure 4E) we used 26 trials (from 3 sessions) for monkey F, and 86 trials (2 sessions) for monkey T. For the error analysis in Figure 6B (comparison of SVM accuracy in error and correct trials) we used a total (short and long SOAs) of 72 and 125 error trials in monkey F and T respectively.

Basic VSDI analysis

All data analyses were done using MATLAB software. The basic analysis of the VSDI signal is detailed elsewhere.32,34 Briefly, to remove the background fluorescence levels, each pixel was normalized to its baseline fluorescence level (average over few frames before stimulation onset). The heart beat artifact and the photo bleaching effect were removed by subtraction of the mean blank signal (fixation only trials) from stimulated trials. Thus, the imaged signal (Δf/f) reflects the changes in fluorescence relative to the blank trials. The VSD maps in Figures 2, 3, 4, and 5 were low pass filtered (2D Gaussian filter, sigma = 1 pixel) for visualization purposes only.

Defining ROIs: Target and background

On each recording day, the animals performed a fixation task (see Behavioral tasks and visual stimuli) where a horizontal-alone or vertical target-alone was turned on (for 40 ms, as in the BM task). This approach enabled us to retinotopically map the targets and background regions onto the imaging V1 area (Figure 2). To study the population response in the target and background regions and their relations in the various behavioral tasks, we defined region of interests (ROIs) for each region. First, we computed the activation patterns evoked by the target-alone conditions (Figures 2A and 2B) to define separate ROIs for vertical and horizontal targets by fitting an elliptical shape ROI to the activation map of each target (80–100 ms after stimulus onset). Figure 2Aii shows the ROIs for the targets: vertical ROI (ver-ROI) and Figure 2Bii shows the horizontal ROI (hor-ROI). Figure 2Cii shows the background and the common target ROI as well as the non-overlapping parts of the target ROIs: ver-only ROI and hor-only ROI.

Stimulus and mask responses: Overlap and interaction

To compute the overlap between the stimulus-alone evoked response and the mask-alone evoked response in V1, we first averaged the response across V1 for all trials in each condition and session, separately. An example session is shown in Figures 3A and 3B. Next, the TC of each single trial was normalized to the peak response of the trial-averaged signal (of the same condition and the session), a normalized example session appears in Figure 3C. The shaded area (orange-pink) in Figure 3Cii depicts the overlap between the stimulus-alone response and mask-alone response. This was computed as the area underneath the joint curves of the stim and mask-alone responses, from mask onset until t = 230 ms after stimulus onset. Figure 3Ciii depicts the grand analysis of this response overlap, which was computed for all trials in all sessions. Figure-ground modulation

The figure-ground modulation (FGm) is defined in equation no. 2:

The FGm is calculated as the response difference between the figure and background regions for pairs of horizontal and vertical trials (taken from the same imaging session). In each trial, the ‘figure’ ROI was defined as the region activated by the target elements of that orientation, excluding the ‘common element’ region (i.e., verticalfg and horizontalfg). The ‘background’ ROI (verticalbg and horizontalbg) was defined as the region which the opposite target activated (excluding the ‘common element’). Thus, for the vertical trial, the verticalfg and the verticalbg are the vertical-only and horizontal-only ROIs and for the horizontal trial the opposite applies, the horizontalfg and the horizontalbg are the horizontal-only and vertical-only ROIs (Figures 2Cii and 4A). After this calculation, all FGm pairs from all recording sessions were pooled together according to their SOA-condition (Figures 4C, 4D, and 4E). This approach for computing the FGm enabled us to use exactly the same brain regions for computing the FGm in both horizontal and vertical stimuli and thus account for any variation of VSD staining across recording days.

To estimate the expected FGm by chance, we randomly shuffled the trials label within an SOA session, and assigned vertical/horizontal labels such that the ROIs now represent ‘figure’ and ‘background’ regions at random. This control condition showed a near zero value of the FGm (Figures 4C and 4D dashed lines). To assess the significance level of the results, we performed the label shuffling 100-fold, and created a distribution of this shuffled FGm. We then performed a Wilcoxon rank-sum Test with the values of the real data vs. the shuffled ones.

Spatial registration of the VSD maps

The cortical images obtained from the different recording days showed slight variations in the exact chamber location and orientation relative to the camera frame. This occurred due to slight changes in camera position relative to the imaging chamber. Accounting for these variations was crucial for effective training of the linear SVM model, where each pixel is a feature and across the recording days a given pixel should represent the same site in V1. To address this, we selected one recording day as a fixed reference and registered all other recording days to it by aligning the blood vessels pattern of the cortical tissue in each session with those of the reference session (in a previous study we showed that the functional columns in V1 remain constant in space relative to the blood vessels32). In addition, for monkey T, there was a minor shift (0.5°) of the stimulus position on the display between the first 3 recording days and the last 3 days. We therefore divided the data into two datasets of 3 sessions each, that were separately registered and aligned. The SVM analysis was performed on the VSD registered maps and all results were verified on original data (i.e., before registration).

An SVM model on the target-alone trials

For the SVM analysis, we normalized the VSD data relative to baseline noise. This was done by normalizing the activity in each pixel to Z score, with the mean and standard deviation calculated from baseline frames, i.e., before stimulus onset (220–70 ms before stimulus onset, from all trials of a single session). This normalization is a standard approach for SVM analysis.

For training the SVM on the VSD maps of the target-alone trials, we used a training window of 70–110 ms (i.e., 5 frames) after stimulus onset, as this is the time when the stimulus evoked activity becomes detectable in the VSD maps. We balanced the training data by selecting an equal number of trials from the horizontal and vertical conditions – 12 & 16 trials total for monkeys F and T respectively in the example sessions analysis (Figures 5B, 5C, 5G, and 5H) and 62 & 100 trials total for monkeys F and T respectively in the grand analysis (Figures 5D–5I). The Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) method was employed, meaning that in each training iteration, one horizontal and one vertical trial were left out for evaluation while the remaining trials were used for training the model. The 5 frames of the training window from each trial were pooled together across all training trials and used to train the SVM. We then tested the model on the two trials left out for each time point and this procedure was repeated for all pairs of trials. Next, the model’s performance was averaged across all pairs of trials to create the accuracy TC shown in Figure 5. This entire process was conducted separately for monkeys F and T, initially using an example session (Figures 5B and 5G) and later expanding to the entire dataset (Figures 5D and 5I). Finally, we averaged the weights of all trained models to produce one single weight map, and color-coded it such that warm colors represent high positive weights and cool colors represent low negative weights (Figure 5Ci and 5Hi). Using the horizontal and vertical ROIs that were defined previously, we calculated the weight distributions (Figure 5Cii and 5Hii). The mean weights in the vertical-only, horizontal-only and common ROIs of the grand analysis is shown in Figures 5E and 5J. When training the data on the SVM we equalized the number of trials for the horizontal and vertical stimuli.

To assess the statistical validity of the SVM model, we performed label shuffling on the training set before training. We then used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare the distribution of model performances within the training window between the shuffled and unshuffled data, as well as to compare the mean weight values of the ROIs within the weight maps.

SVM on backward masking data

To investigate the temporal dynamics of the model accuracy with the BM data, we used a sliding window of 5 frames (50 ms) with a stride of 1 frame (10 ms). For each window the SVM model was trained on the VSD maps of the BM data using a LOOCV approach (as described above in ‘An SVM model on the target-alone trials’) and tested using a 5-frame window on one pair of trials: one horizontal and one vertical stimulus. Model accuracy was averaged across these frames and all trials. Each SOA was trained and tested independently, allowing for a comparison of the model’s performance across different SOAs (Figure 6). When training the data on the SVM we equalized the number of trials for the horizontal and vertical stimuli. To assess the statistical significance of the results, we performed label shuffling of the trials prior to training and used Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare the real data to the shuffled data. Rank-sum was used to compare models’ accuracy across the different SOAs at the early and late time of accuracy.

Finally, we averaged the weights of all trained models to produce one single weight map that is color-coded (Figure 7A) as for the SVM on the target-alone data. The weight maps were low pass filtered (2D Gaussian filter, sigma = 0.5 pixel) for visualization purposes only. Using the horizontal and vertical ROIs that were defined previously, we calculated the distribution histograms of the weights in the vertical-only and horizontal-only ROIs (Figure 7B). To measure how much the weights distributions are different between the ROIs, we calculated the ROC curves and the AUC between horizontal-only and vertical-only weights distributions (Figure 7B insets and 7Ciii). We averaged the mean weight differences between the horizontal and vertical ROIs across the two monkeys to produce the TC of the weight difference in Figure 7Ci. In Figure 7Cii we performed a linear fit and computed the Pearson Correlation (r) between the value of weights-difference in the early time (80–100 ms) and the SOA duration, and between the weight difference at the late time (180–200 ms) and the SOA duration. In a similar manner, we calculated separately for each monkey and each SOA the Pearson Correlation (r) between the weight maps obtained for the BM data and the weight maps obtained from the target-alone data (e.g., Figures 5C and 5H; each monkey to its own retinotopic weight-map). Next, we averaged the correlation coefficient across the monkeys to produce the TC of correlation (Figure 7Di). We then calculated the Pearson Correlation and linear fit with the SOA duration, for the early time and late time (Figure 7Dii). Finally, we note that we obtained similar results to those obtained by the SVM classifier by using other classifiers e.g., random forest.

Statistical analysis

All statistical details can be found in the Results section and within the figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± sem (unless indicated otherwise). Nonparametric statistical tests were used: The Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare between two medians of two populations or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare the population’s median to 0. Statistical significance values were corrected for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni correction). In addition, we generated the shuffle condition from the real data as explained in specific parts of the methods. To compute the slope differences for the linear regression of the FGm (Figure 4F) as function of the SOA as well as the weight difference and correlation to the weight target-alone maps as function of the SOA (Figure 7Cii and 7Dii) we used a linear regression model with an interaction term.
