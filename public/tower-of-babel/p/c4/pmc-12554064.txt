Heart transplantation in special patient populations
Authors: Kathryn R. Armstrong, Anne I. Dipchand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this review of heart transplantation (HTx) in special patient populations, the indications for HTx for patients with underlying genetic conditions, myopathies, mitochondrial conditions, connective tissue disorders (CTDs), and intellectual developmental delay will be explored along with the outcomes and survival. A key principle for cardiologists looking after these patients who may develop end-stage heart failure and require HTx evaluation is to determine what is in the child’s best interest from a medical, physical, emotional, developmental, and psychosocial perspective with a goal of providing the child not only survival but an acceptable quality of life within their underlying circumstances.This review highlights that much like clinicians do for any pediatric patient put forward for HTx and as the International Society of Heart Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) endorses an evaluation of the patient’s cardiopulmonary status, comorbidities and psychosocial factors that may affect graft/patient survival should be considered for patients with underlying genetic conditions, myopathies, CTDs, and intellectual delays prior to transplantation. More research is required to better understand the risk and long-term outcomes of HTx in those with underlying mitochondrial conditions where HTx has accelerated the progression of disease in other organ systems and in conditions such as Noonan’s syndrome, where patients have a higher risk of developing post-transplant complications such as post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder.

Background

Heart transplantation (HTx) continues to be the standard of care for infants and children in end-stage heart failure of any cause. With advances in congenital heart disease (CHD) surgery more children with underlying genetic syndromes, chromosomal abnormalities or developmental disabilities are being offered corrective or palliative surgery. These children may go on to develop advanced heart failure, where consideration for HTx becomes a potential discussion.

Prior to transplantation, the International Society of Heart Lung Transplantation endorses an evaluation of the patient’s cardiopulmonary status, comorbidities, and psychosocial factors that may affect graft/patient survival.1 The ethics of denying a child with an underlying intellectual or chromosomal abnormality a transplant is complex and challenging; however, as clinicians push the boundaries for their patients and as more knowledge is gained about genetic disease questions around eligibility for HTx in the pediatric population continue to be raised. Wall et al surveyed pediatric transplant centers to determine criteria for transplant suitability based on underlying genetic disease and intellectual disability and found that pediatric centers were more likely to consider transplant in these populations than their adult counterparts.2 Pediatric patients and their families with underlying genetic conditions and intellectual disability have extensive experience of living with uncertainty often for years, have extensive experience of the medical system, medical therapies and may have developed coping strategies for their child that need to be taken into consideration for each individual child when making decisions about heart transplant candidacy in these higher-risk special patient populations.3

The aim of this review is to evaluate the indications and outcomes for children with underlying genetic syndromes, myopathies, metabolic conditions, connective tissue disorders (CTDs), or developmental delay undergoing HTx.

HTx in downs syndrome (DS)

Up to 40% of children with DS have CHD.4 Despite the high burden of heart disease, transplant rates in patients with DS remain low. A case series from the United Kingdom reviewed four patients (aged 3-30 years) with DS who went on to receive a heart transplant, the indication in 3 of the patients was for anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy. They report that the younger two patients transplanted at 3 and 11 years are both alive more than 15 years post-transplant, the older two patients are still alive and being followed over 3 years post-transplant.5

In the largest retrospective review of post-transplant outcomes in patients with DS, Godown et al report data on 26 patients listed for transplant from 17 centers across Canada, United Kingdom, United States and Sweden. The median age at listing was 8.5 years, with failed repair of CHD (62%) and cardiomyopathy secondary to anthracycline administration (19%) the leading reasons for listing. Nine patients were bridged to transplant with ventricular assist device (VAD), 88% survived to transplantation. Post transplant all patients survived to hospital discharge and overall survival was similar between patients with DS and aged-matched non-DS counterparts. The rates of post-transplant-related infection requiring hospitalization, rejection, and PTLD were also similar to other pediatric HTx recipients.6

Despite these good outcomes in small numbers of patients, the unknowns include how many children with DS approach end-stage heart failure. More thought provoking, however, is how many are not referred for consideration of HTx and the rationale, which could be as wholistic as an informed decision between caregivers and family regarding risk, outcomes, and quality of life to as paternalistic as presumed lack of candidacy, which is not supported by the existing albeit sparse literature.

HTx in noonan syndrome (NS)

NS and Noonan spectrum disorder are a genetically heterogenous group of malformation syndromes caused by mutations in the genes encoded by proteins of the RAS/MAPK pathway. The term Rasopathy includes NS and NS with multiple letignes. Patients can present with short stature, neuropsychological challenges, and CHD along with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, which often presents in infancy and is a leading cause of mortality and primary indication for transplant.7 McCallen et al reports the outcomes on 18 patients with NS/NS with multiple letignes who underwent HTx, 3 patients died during the initial transplant hospitalization, 15 patients survived until hospital discharge with a mean follow-up time of 6 years, a further 3 patients died during the follow-up period, one had a viral gastrointestinal illness and developed an ischemic bowel, one cause of death un known, and one died 3.5 years following transplant during their third episode of rejection. Of the 6 surviving patients, 50% have had an episode of rejection, rates of PTLD were reported to be around 22%, which is higher than in the general pediatric HTx cohort, which reports a risk of 9.5% at 10 years post-transplant.7

For those patients with NS who are being put forward for HTx careful consideration and counseling should be undertaken around the long-term outcomes rather than focusing on short-term survival. The risk of PTLD and acute rejection is significantly higher than what is reported for the general pediatric cohort. This raises the potential for consideration of more novel immunosuppression strategies and surveillance screening for this population.

Heart transplant in other selected genetic syndromes

Williams – Beuren Syndrome (WBS)

This condition is caused by a deletion in chromosome 7 which contains the Elastin gene. Children present with typical facial features, have neurodevelopmental delay and cardiovascular defects, the commonest of which is supravalvar aortic stenosis (SVAS).8 There are isolated case reports of transplant in WBS, two in infancy: one in a 2 month old with an underlying diagnosis of an ischemic cardiomyopathy, at 18 month follow-up he was alive with narrowing in the descending aorta despite extensive repair at the time of transplant and residual peripheral branch pulmonary artery stenosis9; the second in a 14-month old with ischemic left ventricular dysfunction following surgery for SVAS; this child was successfully discharged home on day 23 post-HTx and was still alive at early follow-up.10 There is also a case report of an adolescent with WBS who was transplanted for refractory heart failure and who died in the early postoperative period due to cytomegalovirus infection.8

DiGeorge syndrome

22q11 deletion, also known as DiGeorge syndrome, is associated with developmental delay, immunologic compromise, and CHD. Woolman reviewed outcomes of 17 patients with DiGeorge syndrome following HTx (aged <1-month to17 years). Primary cardiac abnormalities were conotruncal defects, left sided obstructive lesions, including hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) and double outlet right ventricle (DORV)/tetralogy of fallot (ToF). Two patients were supported on VAD prior to transplant. Overall survival and freedom from rejection or infection were not different between HTx recipients with and without DiGeorge syndrome.11 Due to the small number of patients who were transplanted in the review by Woolman et al the authors recognize that selection bias may have excluded those who had significant immunocompromise, they also found that induction and maintenance immunosuppression reflected current practice rather than an individual approach to an immunocompromised patient.11 The impact of underlying immunodeficiency on HTx outcomes remains unclear. In other cardiac populations, such as those with Fontan palliation who undergo HTx, Richmond et al compared those who underwent HTx for failed Fontan palliation who had lymphopenia or hypogammaglobulinemia to those without and did show an increased risk of infection post HTx.12 This continues to support the need for alternative immunosuppression strategies for individuals who are deemed to be at higher risk.

Turners syndrome (TS)

Monosomy X (45X, O) is a genetic syndrome affecting 1 in 2100-2500 live female births.

Common features include growth failure, pubertal delay, and CHD, usually left-sided heart lesions, including HLHS, which is the commonest indication for transplant. A retrospective review of the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients from 2002 to 2016 identified 14 female HTx recipients with TS, 71% of whom had an underlying diagnosis of HLHS. Tow patients were supported with VAD at the time of transplant, 6 patients died all in the first year following transplant, 5 within the first month, 2 from primary graft failure, 2 from a cardiac arrest and one from an anoxic brain injury. Patients who undergo HTx with TS have an early risk of mortality; however, reported numbers of cases of TS undergoing HTx remain low.13

What is clear from the literature is that more children with underlying genetic syndromes are surviving initial CHD surgeries and going on to develop end-stage heart failure, and while the indications for transplant are diverse and primarily linked to the genetic syndrome itself, there is growing interest in the post-transplant outcomes for these patients, especially given the constellation of accompanying features, which often include neurodevelopmental, growth, endocrine, immunologic, vascular, and other challenges potentially relevant to common post-transplant co-morbidities.

A United Network for Organ sharing (UNOS) review between 2009 and 2019 found that up to 9% of patients who undergo HTx have an underlying genetic syndrome, though a “select” population, there appeared to be no differences in listing clinical characteristics, waitlist time, post-transplantation complications or post-transplantation survival between those with and without a genetic syndrome.14

Heart transplant in myopathies

Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy (MD) are the most prevalent forms of MD. Approximately 90% of these patients will develop cardiomyopathy, with heart failure being the leading cause of death.15 Cardiac transplantation has been limited in patients with MD due to their comorbid conditions, the challenges that may exist around their ability to perform physical rehabilitation, and the potential to develop cardiomyopathy in the transplanted heart.15 In a review of patients transplanted for MD from 1987 to 2016 Wells et al searched the UNOS database and found there were 81 patients transplanted for MD during the study period, outcomes were compared to all other types of cardiomyopathies (CMO). Becker MD accounted for more than half of the cases, the remaining muscular dystrophies were Emery-Dreifuss (14) %, Limb-girdle (5%), Duchenne (3%), Myotonic (2%), Charcot-Marie-tooth (1%) and the remaining muscular dystrophies were not specified. Fewer patients with MD were on VAD prior to transplant compared to the general CMO group (16% vs 30%), post-operative transplant complications except for acute rejection were similar between groups, and post-transplant survival did not differ significantly between groups. They conclude that a multidisciplinary approach to review patients and their comorbidities should be encouraged when considering transplant in a patient with a MD.16 In a single-center review looking at transplants in inherited myopathies, indications for HTx were end-stage heart failure secondary to cardiomyopathy, accounting for a total of 2% of transplants; likewise, they found that post-transplant outcomes were similar between those transplanted for end-stage heart failure in MD and those transplanted for other indications.17

Barth syndrome, an X linked disease characterized my cardioskeletal myopathy and neutropenia, often has children presenting in severe heart failure. Li et al reviewed data from the Pediatric Heart Transplant Study Registry and reported data on 47 patients with Barth syndrome, 43 of whom underwent HTx (41 primary and 2 r transplants) compared to matched males listed for HTx with dilated cardiomyopathy. Dilated cardiomyopathy was the commonest indication for HTx in the patients with Barth syndrome. The patients with Barth syndrome had less acute rejection compared to children transplanted for other indications; there was no difference in survival, no difference in serious infection or malignancy. The authors concluded that when indicated, HTx for patients with Barth Syndrome is appropriate.18

Heart transplant in mitochondrial conditions

Primary metabolic mitochondrial diseases are genetic disorders that impact the structure or function of the mitochondrion and the process of oxidative phosphorylation. Children with mitochondrial diseases may develop cardia, renal, or hepatic failure at times triggered by a metabolic stressor such as illness or prolonged fasting.

The number of patients transplanted with an underlying mitochondrial condition is low, due to uncertainty about the underlying genetic or metabolic condition and how it may have an impact on post-transplant morbidity and mortality. Parikh et al reviewed HTx indications and outcomes across 17 Mitochondrial Disease centers. A total of 11 patients across the 17 centers underwent HTx, all for progression of disease related to their underlying disorder (6 for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, 4 for dilated cardiomyopathy, and one for worsening heart failure symptoms after a cardiac arrest). The majority (82%) survived post-transplant with a range of follow-up between 6 months to 13 years. Rejection was seen in 1 of the 11 patients. Of the surviving patients 82% did not have worsening of their mitochondrial disease due to the HTx procedure itself; however, 55% had eventual worsening of their mitochondrial disease-related symptoms such as seizures, strokes, or myopathy. This is a significant finding, as it appears that while HTx may resolve the cardiac disease it may ultimately lead to more comorbidities and a worsening of their underlying disease and therefore ultimately decrease their quality of life.19

CTDs and indications for HTx

CTDs are associated with mitral valve disease and aortic root dilatation. In the largest published adult series, ten patients with Marfan’s syndrome underwent HTx for type A aortic dissection and ascending aortic aneurysm. They report long-term survival to be similar to those who underwent HTx without Marfan’s.20 There are few cases reports published for pediatric patients with CTD who go on to require HTx. Laks reports on an infant with neonatal Marfan’s who after mitral and tricuspid valve repair, developed biventricular systolic dysfunction requiring BiVAD support until the time of HTx. The short and intermediate post-HTx follow-up was notable for significant co-morbidities. Finally, the patient developed early coronary artery vasculopathy at 3.5 years post-HTx and passed away.21 Indication’s for transplant in the teenage Marfan’s population may relate to the risk of aortic root dilatation; a 14-year-old who underwent a Bentall procedure for an aortic dissection resulted in postoperative ventricular failure requiring Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and underwent HTx 5 days later.22

Heart transplant in children with intellectual disability

In many settings intellectual disability (ID) has been thought to be a contraindication to HTx, with concerns over non-compliance and poor improvement in quality of life cited as reasons not to pursue HTx. Since 2008, UNOS began collecting data on the academic development of children undergoing organ transplant23 Goel reports on 565 children with ID who underwent HTX between 2008 and 2015, the majority of whom had underlying CHD. He compared them to those without ID who underwent HTx and found that graft survival and transplant survival did not differ between groups22 A more recent review continued to find that CHD was the indication for HTx in patients with ID and motor disability and demonstrated an increase in the number of those with ID undergoing HTx.24 Recent studies have shown that there is no difference in outcomes after pediatric HTx in patients with ID.25 ID should not be considered a contraindication to HTx in children.

Conclusion

In this review of indications for HTx for patients with underlying genetic conditions, myopathies, metabolic conditions, CTDs, and intellectual developmental delay, the indications for HTx were underlying CHD or CMO, which is similar to the underlying indications in the general pediatric population. Although the number of patients with the above listed conditions being put forward for transplant appears to be much smaller than those without, the outcomes in some of the subsets described, such as those with ID or Down Syndrome have transplant survival that is similar to the general pediatric population. There are a number of nuances though when consideration transplant in patients with NS who are at increased risk of post-transplant related complications such as PTLD, the risk of transplant in patients who may have underlying immunodeficiency, such as those with Di George syndrome and in patients with underlying metabolic conditions where HTx may cure their cardiac disease but increase disease progression in other organ groups and have a detrimental effect on a patients overall quality of life.

This review highlights that much like clinicians do for any pediatric patient put forward for HTx and as the International Society of Heart Lung Transplantation endorses an evaluation of the patient’s cardiopulmonary status, comorbidities, and psychosocial factors that may affect graft/patient survival should be considered for patients with underlying genetic conditions, myopathies, CTDs, and intellectual delays. A careful evaluation of the psychosocial environment is also encouraged for each individual patient or family, as each individuals coping strategies and experience of the medical system to date may also impact the patient’s quality of life following transplant.
