The impact of Trem2-mediated macrophage polarization on the mechanisms of allergic rhinitis
Authors: Rong Hu, Kan Ze, Yu Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study aimed to investigate the impact of Trem2-mediated macrophage polarization on the pathogenesis of allergic rhinitis (AR), with a focus on how Trem2 influences macrophage polarization and its downstream effects on nasal epithelial cells and eosinophil activity. A series of in vivo and in vitro experiments were conducted using C57BL/6J mice and primary macrophages. Mice were sensitized and challenged with ovalbumin (OVA) to induce AR, and various experimental groups were treated with or without Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccine and Trem2 gene manipulation. Macrophage-specific Trem2 knockout mice (Trem2-cko) were used to assess the role of Trem2 in macrophage polarization and allergic inflammation. Flow cytometry, ELISA, qRT-PCR, histological staining, and immunohistochemistry were performed to analyze macrophage polarization, cytokine levels, epithelial cell proliferation, and eosinophil activity. The BCG vaccine effectively reduced inflammation in OVA-induced AR mice, significantly downregulating Trem2 expression in macrophages, which corresponded with reduced epithelial damage and mucus secretion. Trem2 deletion in macrophages enhanced the M1 polarization, resulting in greater anti-inflammatory effects following BCG treatment. In vitro studies showed that Trem2 overexpression in M2-polarized macrophages inhibited nasal epithelial cell proliferation and survival, whereas Trem2 silencing restored these functions. Furthermore, Trem2 promoted M2 polarization and enhanced eosinophil survival and proliferation, further exacerbating allergic inflammation. The regulation of these processes by Trem2 was mediated through DAP10 and DAP12 signaling pathways, which modulated macrophage polarization and eosinophil activity. Trem2-mediated macrophage polarization plays a crucial role in exacerbating allergic inflammation in AR by promoting M2 polarization and enhancing eosinophil function. The findings suggest that targeting Trem2 or its signaling pathways, particularly through DAP10 and DAP12, could offer novel therapeutic strategies for managing allergic rhinitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46399-9.Keywords: Trem2, Macrophage polarization, Allergic rhinitis, Eosinophils, M1/M2 polarization, Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG), DAP10, DAP12, Inflammation, CytokinesSubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Immunology, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a common inflammatory condition of the nasal mucosa, typically triggered by exposure to allergens such as pollen, dust mites, and animal dander1. Characterized by symptoms such as nasal congestion, sneezing, and itching, AR represents a significant public health burden, affecting a large portion of the global population2. The global prevalence of AR varies by region, but it is estimated that between 10 and 30% of the adult population and up to 40% of children are affected, with the incidence rising in urban areas and regions with higher environmental pollution2. In developed countries, the prevalence of AR has been increasing over the past few decades, possibly due to changes in environmental factors, lifestyle, and hygiene practices2. The pathogenesis of AR involves a complex interplay between immune cells, cytokines, and inflammatory mediators, with a prominent role played by macrophages, T helper cells, and eosinophils3,4. The hallmark of AR is an exaggerated Th2 immune response, which leads to the release of cytokines like IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, fostering inflammation and tissue damage5,6. These immune responses contribute to the characteristic symptoms of AR and are closely linked to the recruitment and activation of eosinophils, mast cells, and other inflammatory cells in the nasal mucosa5,6. The rising prevalence of AR and its significant impact on quality of life, work productivity, and healthcare costs underscore the urgent need for better understanding of its underlying mechanisms and the development of more effective therapeutic strategies.

Macrophages, a key component of the innate immune system, are central to the development of allergic inflammation. These cells exhibit plasticity in their activation states, generally classified into two main subsets: M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory)7. M1 macrophages are involved in the initiation of inflammation, whereas M2 macrophages promote tissue repair and immune regulation7. The balance between M1 and M2 macrophages is crucial in determining the outcome of inflammatory responses. Macrophage polarization is heavily influenced by various signaling pathways, including the Trem2-mediated signaling axis, which has recently gained attention in immune regulation8,9. TREM2 (triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2) is a receptor primarily expressed on macrophages, microglia, and dendritic cells10,11. It has been implicated in modulating macrophage polarization, phagocytosis, and inflammation8,9,12,13. In the context of allergic diseases, recent studies suggest that TREM2 plays a role in regulating macrophage polarization and modulating the inflammatory response14,15. However, the precise mechanisms through which TREM2 influences allergic rhinitis remain poorly understood. The role of TREM2 in AR has not been extensively explored, and while studies have suggested that TREM2 could promote M2 polarization and enhance the anti-inflammatory response14, its specific contribution to the regulation of macrophage polarization in allergic inflammation is still unclear. Furthermore, the potential interaction between TREM2 and other signaling pathways, such as DAP10 and DAP12, which are involved in macrophage activation, warrants further investigation. This lack of detailed understanding underscores the necessity of examining the function of TREM2 in the context of AR, particularly in relation to macrophage polarization and eosinophil activity.

Recent therapeutic strategies for allergic rhinitis, including the use of Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccination, have shown promise in modulating immune responses16, but the involvement of macrophage-mediated mechanisms in this effect remains underexplored. BCG vaccination has been proposed to promote a shift towards a more regulatory immune response by modulating macrophage polarization, but whether this involves TREM2-mediated pathways in macrophages is yet to be determined. Given the complexity of immune responses in AR, particularly the intricate regulation of macrophage polarization and eosinophil activity, further investigation into the role of TREM2 in these processes is critical. The present study aims to elucidate the impact of TREM2-mediated macrophage polarization on the mechanisms of allergic rhinitis, with a focus on its influence on nasal epithelial cell proliferation, eosinophil function, and overall inflammatory responses.

Methods

Animal modeling and grouping

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old) were obtained from Vital River (Beijing, China) and acclimatized for one week under controlled conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 22 °C, and 50–60% humidity). All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated TCM Hospital. For AR modeling, mice were sensitized with 20 µg of ovalbumin (OVA) in 200 µL of aluminum hydroxide gel via intraperitoneal injection on days 0, 7, and 14. On days 21–23, mice were challenged with 1% OVA solution via intranasal instillation. Control mice received PBS instead of OVA. For BCG treatment, mice in the OVA + BCG group were administered 1 × 106 CFU of BCG vaccine on days 0, 7, and 14. Experimental groups included: (1) Control group: PBS without OVA challenge; (2) OVA group: OVA sensitization and challenge without BCG; (3) OVA + BCG group: OVA sensitization, challenge, and BCG vaccination; (4) Trem2-cko group: Macrophage-specific Trem2 knockout mice with OVA sensitization and challenge; (5) OVA + Trem2-cko group: Trem2-cko mice with OVA sensitization, challenge, and BCG vaccination; (6) OVA + BCG + Trem2-cko group: Trem2-cko mice with OVA sensitization, challenge, and BCG vaccination. All treatments followed the described protocols, and animals were euthanized used 150 mg/kg pentobarbital on day 24 after the final OVA challenge. Upon sacrifice by cervical dislocation under anesthesia, nasal mucosa was collected for histology (HE, PAS staining). BALF was used for cytokine analysis (IgE, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13, IFN-γ) by ELISA and cell differential counts. Blood was collected for serum cytokine analysis, and macrophages were isolated for flow cytometry and gene expression analysis. Eosinophils were also isolated from nasal mucosa for further analysis.

Cell culturing and grouping

Primary macrophages were isolated from the bone marrow of C57BL/6J mice and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL M-CSF for 7 days to differentiate. M2 polarization was induced using IL-4 (20 ng/mL). For Trem2 manipulation, macrophages were transfected with oe-TREM2 or sh-TREM2 plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000. Human nasal epithelial cells (HNEpCs) were cultured in keratinocyte serum-free medium with bovine pituitary extract and EGF. To assess macrophage polarization effects, HNEpCs or Eosinophils were co-cultured with polarized macrophages by 0.4 μm Transwell (Corning, USA, 3401). Eosinophils were isolated from the BALF of OVA-induced allergic rhinitis mice using MACS eosinophil isolation kits. The experimental groups were as follows: (1) HNEpCs and Macrophage Co-culture: Control co-culture: HNEpCs cultured alone without macrophages; (2) IL-4 + Control co-culture: HNEpCs co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages; (3) IL-4 + oe-NC co-culture: HNEpCs co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages transfected with control plasmid; (4) IL-4 + oe-TREM2 co-culture: HNEpCs co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages overexpressing Trem2; (5) IL-4 + sh-NC co-culture: HNEpCs co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages transfected with control shRNA; (6) IL-4 + sh-TREM2 co-culture: HNEpCs co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages silenced for Trem2; (7) Control co-culture: Eosinophils co-cultured with non-polarized control macrophages; (8) IL-4 + Control co-culture: Eosinophils co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages; (9) IL-4 + oe-NC co-culture: Eosinophils co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages transfected with control plasmid. (10) IL-4 + oe-TREM2 co-culture: Eosinophils co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages overexpressing Trem2. (11) IL-4 + sh-NC co-culture: Eosinophils co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages transfected with control shRNA. (12) IL-4 + sh-TREM2 co-culture: Eosinophils co-cultured with IL-4-polarized macrophages silenced for Trem2. (13) Control: Non-polarized macrophages. (14) Φ oe-TREM2: Macrophages overexpressing Trem2. (15) Φ sh-TREM2: Macrophages with Trem2 knocked down. (16) IL-4 + Φ oe-TREM2: Macrophages overexpressing Trem2 and polarized with IL-4. (17) IL-4 + Φ sh-TREM2: Macrophages with Trem2 knocked down and polarized with IL-4. (18) EOS + Φ co-culture: Eosinophils co-cultured with control macrophages. (19) EOS + Φ oe-TREM2: Eosinophils co-cultured with macrophages overexpressing Trem2. (20) EOS + Φ oe-TREM2 + oe-DAP10: Eosinophils co-cultured with macrophages overexpressing Trem2 and DAP10. (21) EOS + Φ oe-TREM2 + oe-DAP12: Eosinophils co-cultured with macrophages overexpressing Trem2 and DAP12. (22) EOS + Φ sh-TREM2: Eosinophils co-cultured with macrophages with Trem2 knocked down. (23) EOS + Φ sh-TREM2 + sh-DAP10: Eosinophils co-cultured with macrophages with Trem2 knocked down and DAP10 knocked down. (24) EOS + Φ sh-TREM2 + sh-DAP12: Eosinophils co-cultured with macrophages with Trem2 knocked down and DAP12 knocked down.

Gene knockout

Trem2-conditional knockout mice (Trem2-cko) were generated by crossing Trem2 floxed mice (Trem2^f/f) with LysM-Cre mice, where Cre recombinase expression is driven by the LysM promoter, ensuring macrophage and neutrophil-specific deletion of the Trem2 gene. The Trem2^f/f mice possess loxP sites flanking exons 2 and 3 of the Trem2 gene, allowing for tissue-specific deletion of Trem2 when Cre recombinase is expressed. The LysM-Cre transgenic mice express Cre recombinase specifically in myeloid lineage cells, including macrophages and neutrophils, which results in the excision of the floxed region of the Trem2 gene in these cells. To establish Trem2-cko mice, heterozygous Trem2^f/f and LysM-Cre transgenic mice were bred, and the resulting offspring were genotyped to confirm the presence of both the floxed Trem2 allele and the Cre recombinase transgene. Genotyping was performed using tail DNA samples, and PCR was conducted with specific primers to detect the Cre transgene and the presence of the floxed Trem2 allele. The wild-type control mice (Trem2^f/f) were also genotyped to confirm the absence of Cre recombinase. After confirming the genotype, Trem2-cko mice were used for subsequent experiments.

Gene silencing and overexpression

Gene silencing and overexpression were performed in macrophages to modulate Trem2 expression and assess its impact on macrophage polarization and eosinophil activity in allergic rhinitis. For gene silencing, shRNA targeting Trem2 was transfected into macrophages using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Macrophages were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well in 6-well plates and transfected with 1 μg of shRNA plasmid in 250 μL Opti-MEM medium. After 24 h, Trem2 knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting using anti-Trem2 antibody (Abcam, UK) and qRT-PCR, showing significant reduction in Trem2 expression compared to controls. For overexpression, macrophages were transfected with plasmids encoding Trem2 (oe-Trem2) using the same transfection method. After 24 h, Trem2 overexpression was verified by Western blotting and qRT-PCR, which showed elevated Trem2 levels in the transfected cells. The effects of Trem2 silencing and overexpression on macrophage polarization and eosinophil activity were further examined using CCK-8, EdU staining, and ELISA assays.

Histopathological evaluation

To assess the histopathological changes in the nasal mucosa of allergic rhinitis mice, Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining were performed. The nasal tissues from mice were harvested and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h. After fixation, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%) for 2 h each, followed by xylene treatment for 30 min. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin, and 5 μm thick sections were cut using a microtome. For H&E staining, the paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a descending ethanol series (100%, 95%, and 70%). The sections were then stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by eosin for 2 min. After staining, the slides were dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series (70%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and mounted with a resinous medium. For PAS staining, after deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were oxidized in 0.5% periodic acid for 10 min, followed by staining with Schiff’s reagent for 15 min to highlight mucus and glycoproteins. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin for 2 min. After dehydration and clearing, the slides were mounted with neutral resin. Histological analysis was performed by examining the slides under a light microscope at 400× magnification. Goblet cell hyperplasia was quantified by counting PAS-positive cells, and the area occupied by these cells was measured using ImageJ software (NIH). Epithelial damage was quantified by assessing the length and area of epithelial disruption.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

To examine Trem2 expression and macrophage polarization markers (CD86 and CD206) in the nasal mucosa of allergic rhinitis mice, IHC was performed on paraffin-embedded sections. After fixation in 10% formalin for 24 h, tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 µm thickness. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Trem2 (1:200; Abcam), CD86 (1:200; BioLegend), and CD206 (1:200; BioLegend). After washing, sections were incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody (1:500; Thermo Fisher) for 1 h. Signal amplification was achieved using the avidin–biotin complex method, and peroxidase activity was visualized with diaminobenzidine (DAB). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted for analysis. Trem2, CD86, and CD206 staining intensities were quantified by measuring optical density (OD) in at least five randomly selected fields per section using ImageJ software. The mean OD value for each sample was used to represent staining intensity.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to analyze macrophage polarization, F4/80 + Trem2 + macrophage proportions, and apoptosis in cell cultures from allergic rhinitis mice. Single-cell suspensions were prepared from nasal tissues by enzymatic digestion with collagenase and dispase, followed by filtration through a 70 µm mesh. Cells were stained with antibodies against F4/80 (1:100; BioLegend, USA) and Trem2 (1:200; Abcam, UK) for macrophage analysis, and CD86 (1:200; BioLegend) and CD206 (1:200; BioLegend) for M1 and M2 polarization, respectively. To assess apoptosis, cells were stained with Annexin V (1:100; BioLegend) and 7-AAD (1:500; BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After primary antibody incubation, cells were washed and incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min at 4 °C. The stained cells were analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, acquiring at least 10,000 events per sample. Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (TreeStar, USA) to quantify F4/80 + Trem2 + macrophages, M1 (CD86+) and M2 (CD206+) macrophage proportions, and apoptotic cells.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA was used to quantify various cytokine levels in serum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and eosinophils from allergic rhinitis mice and supernatant from cell culturing. Serum OVA-specific IgE levels were measured using an ELISA kit (BioLegend, USA). The levels of IL-5, IL-6, IL-13, and IFN-γ in BALF and eosinophil supernatants were also quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (BioLegend, USA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Briefly, 100 µL of each sample or standard was added to pre-coated 96-well plates and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. After washing, biotinylated detection antibodies were added, followed by streptavidin-HRP conjugate. The plates were developed with a substrate solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Cytokine concentrations were determined based on the standard curves.

Differential cell count by Diff-Quick staining of BALF

To determine the total and differential cell counts in BALF, Diff-Quick staining was performed on the collected BALF samples. After euthanizing the mice, BALF was collected by flushing the lungs with 1 mL of sterile PBS. The BALF samples were then centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the cell pellets. The cells were resuspended in PBS, and cytospin slides were prepared by centrifuging the cells onto microscope slides at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The slides were air-dried and stained using the Diff-Quick stain kit (Dade Behring, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the slides were dipped sequentially in the Diff-Quick fixative, eosin, and methylene blue solutions, with each dip lasting for 30 s, followed by rinsing in distilled water. The stained slides were then examined under a light microscope at 400× magnification. The total number of cells was determined by counting the cells in five randomly selected fields per slide, and differential cell counts (including macrophages, eosinophils, neutrophils, and lymphocytes) were performed by identifying and categorizing cell types based on their morphology.

CCK-8 assay

To assess cell viability, CCK-8 assays (Dojindo, Japan) were performed on cell cultures co-cultured with macrophages overexpressing or silencing TREM2. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated according to the experimental conditions. After incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). The cell viability was calculated as a percentage of control group viability. For co-culture studies involving eosinophils, similar procedures were followed to assess the viability of eosinophils cultured with macrophages under different conditions.

EdU proliferation assay

To evaluate cell proliferation, an EdU (5-ethynyl-2ʹ-deoxyuridine) incorporation assay was conducted. Nasal epithelial cells (HNEpCs) or eosinophils co-cultured with macrophages were treated with IL-4 to induce M2 polarization and incubated with 10 µM EdU for 2 h. Following incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min. EdU incorporation was detected using the Click-iT™ EdU Imaging Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were then counterstained with DAPI to visualize the nuclei. Proliferation rates were determined by quantifying the EdU-positive cells using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) at 400× magnification. Data were analyzed by counting the number of EdU-positive cells in at least five randomly selected fields per sample.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess macrophage polarization (CD86 for M1 and CD206 for M2) in the nasal mucosa of allergic rhinitis mice. Paraffin-embedded 5 µm tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD86 (1:200; BioLegend) and CD206 (1:200; BioLegend), followed by incubation with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min, and sections were mounted with a coverslip. Fluorescent images were captured at 400 × magnification using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus). Fluorescence intensity of CD86 (green) and CD206 (red) was quantified using ImageJ software, with at least five randomly selected fields per section analyzed for each group.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from macrophages using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and its concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). One μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The resulting cDNA was used for quantitative PCR, performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The PCR reaction system consisted of: 10 µL SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA); 1 µL cDNA (template); 1 µL forward primer (10 µM); 1 µL reverse primer (10 µM); 7 µL nuclease-free water. The amplification conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Primers specific for Trem2, Dap10, and Dap12 were used, and GAPDH served as the internal control. The relative expression levels of Trem2, Dap10, and Dap12 were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Data were presented as fold change in gene expression relative to the control group. Significant differences in gene expression were evaluated between experimental groups.

Western blot

For protein expression analysis, macrophages were lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) using a semi-dry transfer system (Bio-Rad, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk (Biorad, USA) in TBST for 1 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Trem2 (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), CD86 (1:1000, Abcam, UK), CD206 (1:1000, Abcam, UK), DAP10 (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), and DAP12 (1:1000, Abcam, UK). The membranes were then washed three times with TBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). GAPDH was used as a loading control for normalization. Protein expression levels were compared between experimental groups and analyzed using densitometric analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05, and differences were considered statistically significant if the p-value was less than 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version X, GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

BCG vaccine downregulate Trem2 expression to relieve inflammation in OVA-induced allergic rhinitis mice

To assess whether BCG vaccine modulates allergic rhinitis via Trem2-mediated macrophage responses, nasal mucosa from control, OVA, and OVA + BCG groups was examined. HE and PAS staining showed that OVA challenge caused severe epithelial damage, goblet cell hyperplasia, and mucus hypersecretion, all of which were markedly alleviated by BCG treatment (Fig. 1A and B). IHC revealed elevated Trem2 expression in the OVA group, which was significantly reduced by BCG (Fig. 1C and D). Flow cytometry confirmed that OVA increased the proportion of F4/80+Trem2+ macrophages, while BCG treatment reversed this effect (Fig. 1E and F). qRT-PCR showed increased Trem2 mRNA in the OVA group and significant suppression by BCG (Fig. 1G). ELISA analysis of BALF demonstrated that BCG lowered IgE, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13 and IFN-γ levels compared to OVA (Fig. 1H). These results suggest that BCG alleviates nasal mucosal inflammation and mucus hypersecretion, downregulates Trem2 in macrophages, and rebalances the Th2/Th1 cytokine profile in OVA-induced allergic rhinitis.

(B) PAS staining indicating goblet cell hyperplasia and mucus secretion. (C) IHC images of Trem2 expression. (D) Quantification of Trem2 IHC intensity. (E) Flow cytometry plots of F4/80^ + Trem2^ + macrophages. (F) Statistical analysis of F4/80^ + Trem2^ + frequency. (G) Trem2 mRNA levels by qRT-PCR. BCG vaccination attenuates allergic inflammation by promoting M1 polarization via suppressing TREM2

To assess whether BCG vaccination mitigates allergic rhinitis via suppression of TREM2-mediated macrophage polarization, nasal mucosa from control, Trem2-cko, OVA, OVA + Trem2-cko, OVA + BCG vaccine, and OVA + BCG vaccine + Trem2-cko mice was analyzed. HE and PAS staining showed that OVA-induced epithelial disruption, goblet cell hyperplasia, and mucus hypersecretion were alleviated by BCG, with comparable improvements in Trem2-deficient mice (Fig. 2A). IHC and Western blotting revealed that OVA decreased CD86 (M1) and increased CD206 (M2), changes reversed by BCG and further enhanced toward M1 polarization in Trem2-deficient, BCG-treated mice (Fig. 2B–E). To further evaluate the stability of Trem2-mediated macrophage polarization, we conducted time-course analyses at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h after stimulation. The qRT-PCR analysis revealed that OVA stimulation significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of M2-type marker CD206, while downregulating the mRNA expression of M1-type marker CD86. Overexpression of Trem2 further enhanced the mRNA expression of CD206, while Trem2 gene knockout significantly reduced the mRNA level of CD206 and increased the expression of CD86. These results indicate that Trem2 helps maintain the M2-type macrophage polarization state during allergic inflammatory responses, confirming the stability of Trem2-mediated macrophage phenotype throughout the progression of allergic inflammation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Flow cytometry confirmed BCG-induced M1 shift, further enhanced by Trem2 deletion (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). BALF counts showed that BCG reduced OVA-induced leukocyte, macrophage, eosinophil, neutrophil, and lymphocyte increases, with greater reductions in Trem2-deficient mice (Supplementary Fig. 2D). ELISA demonstrated that BCG lowered IgE, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13 and IFN-γ levels; similar changes occurred with Trem2 deficiency, indicating that BCG’s anti-inflammatory and Th2-suppressing effects are associated with inhibition of TREM2 activity (Supplementary Fig. 2E).

Macrophage TREM2 overexpression inhibits nasal epithelial cell proliferation and survival

HNEpCs were co-cultured with macrophages overexpressing or silencing TREM2. Western blotting confirmed efficient modulation of TREM2 expression, with markedly higher levels in oe-TREM2 macrophages and significantly lower levels in sh-TREM2 macrophages compared with their respective controls (Fig. 3A and B). IL-4 was used to induce M2 macrophage polarization. CCK-8 assays showed that IL-4–induced M2 polarization significantly reduced epithelial cell viability compared with control co-cultures. This suppression was further enhanced by TREM2 overexpression, whereas TREM2 silencing markedly restored viability relative to controls (Fig. 3C). Similarly, EdU staining revealed that IL-4-induced M2 polarization uppressed HNEpC proliferation. TREM2 overexpression further decreased proliferative capacity, while TREM2 knockdown significantly enhanced proliferation compared with respective controls (Fig. 3D and E). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that IL-4–polarized macrophages increased epithelial cell apoptosis. This pro-apoptotic effect was further strengthened by TREM2 overexpression, whereas TREM2 silencing reduced apoptosis below control levels (Fig. 3F and G). These findings indicate that IL-4–induced M2 macrophage polarization inhibits proliferation and enhance apoptosis. TREM2 positively reinforces these effects, whereas its silencing counteracts them, potentially modulating epithelial responses in allergic rhinitis.

Trem2-mediated M2 macrophage polarization improve eosinophil activity in allergic rhinitis

Macrophages transfected with oe-TREM2 or sh-TREM2 plasmids were polarized toward an M2-like phenotype using IL-4, and subsequently co-cultured with eosinophils isolated from OVA-induced allergic rhinitis mice. In accordance with the enhancing effects of IL-4-induced M2 macrophages, CCK-8 assays demonstrated a significant increase in eosinophil viability compared to control co-cultures. This effect was further amplified by oe-TREM2 overexpression, while sh-TREM2 transfection resulted in a significant reduction in eosinophil viability relative to controls (Fig. 4A). Similarly, EdU assays revealed that IL-4-polarized macrophages promoted eosinophil proliferation. TREM2 overexpression further enhanced proliferation, while TREM2 silencing markedly inhibited it when compared to controls (Fig. 4B and C). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that IL-4-induced M2 macrophage polarization led to a reduction in eosinophil apoptosis. This anti-apoptotic effect was further enhanced by TREM2 overexpression, whereas TREM2 knockdown led to an increase in apoptosis, surpassing control levels (Fig. 4D and E). ELISA analysis confirmed that IL-4-polarized macrophages elevated the secretion of Th2-associated cytokines, including IL-5, IL-6, and IL-13, while simultaneously reducing IFN-γ levels. TREM2 overexpression amplified these cytokine changes, whereas TREM2 silencing reversed them, reducing IL-5, IL-6, and IL-13 levels and increasing IFN-γ production (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these results suggest that IL-4-induced M2 macrophage polarization exerts activating, anti-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory effects on eosinophils, and that TREM2 potentiates these effects, while its silencing counteracts them.

Macrophages transfected with Trem2 overexpression (Φ oe-Trem2) or knockdown (Φ sh-Trem2) plasmids were stimulated with IL-4 to induce M2 polarization. These results indicate that Trem2 promotes M2 and inhibits M1 polarization in macrophages, with IL-4–induced polarization further modulated by Trem2 expression levels.

Trem2-mediated M2 macrophage polarization regulates eosinophil function in allergic rhinitis

Macrophages transfected with Trem2 overexpression (Φ oe-Trem2) or knockdown (Φ sh-Trem2) plasmids were stimulated with IL-4 to induce M2 polarization and co-cultured with eosinophils from OVA-induced allergic rhinitis mice. CCK-8 assays showed that IL-4-induced M2 macrophages enhanced eosinophil viability, further enhanced by Trem2 overexpression and restored by Trem2 knockdown (Fig. 6A). Flow cytometry revealed decreased eosinophil apoptosis with M2 polarization, enhanced by Trem2 overexpression and attenuated by Trem2 knockdown (Fig. 6B,C). EdU staining indicated that M2 macrophages promoted eosinophil proliferation, with Trem2 overexpression amplifying and Trem2 knockdown reversing this effect (Fig. 6D,E). ELISA showed elevated IL-5, IL-6, IL-13, and IFN-γ in M2 co-cultures, further increased by Trem2 overexpression and reduced by Trem2 knockdown (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest Trem2 strengthens M2 macrophage–mediated suppression of eosinophil proliferation and survival while enhancing pro-inflammatory cytokine release in allergic rhinitis.

Discussion

The present study aimed to elucidate the impact of Trem2-mediated macrophage polarization on the pathogenesis of AR. Our findings provide novel insights into the regulatory role of Trem2 in modulating macrophage polarization and its subsequent effects on nasal epithelial cell and eosinophil function during allergic inflammation. Specifically, our results demonstrate that Trem2 mediates a shift towards M2 macrophage polarization, which influences both epithelial cell proliferation and survival, as well as eosinophil activation in the context of AR. Macrophage polarization plays complex roles in allergic inflammation. Although M2 macrophages are traditionally associated with tissue repair and resolution of inflammation, emerging evidence suggests that they may also contribute to pathological type 2 immune responses in allergic diseases. M2 macrophages can promote eosinophilic inflammation through the secretion of cytokines such as IL-4, IL-10, and IL-13, which may amplify Th2-mediated immune responses in allergic rhinitis. Therefore, the functional role of M2 macrophages appears to be context-dependent, and Trem2-mediated polarization may shift macrophage activity toward a phenotype that facilitates eosinophil survival and allergic inflammation. Moreover, we found that the modulation of Trem2 expression in macrophages, both by genetic deletion and overexpression, has significant implications for inflammatory cytokine production and immune cell interactions, underlining its critical role in allergic responses.

This study underscores the critical role of TREM2 in modulating immune responses in AR. In the OVA-induced AR mouse model, TREM2 expression in macrophages was upregulated, and BCG treatment significantly downregulated it. This reduction in TREM2 was associated with improved epithelial damage and decreased mucus secretion, hallmark features of AR. TREM2 deletion (using Trem2-cko mice) enhanced BCG’s anti-inflammatory effects, shifting macrophage polarization toward M1, reducing Th2-associated cytokines (IL-5, IL-6, IL-13), and decreasing IgE levels and eosinophil infiltration. These results suggest that TREM2 plays a central role in macrophage M2 polarization and the inflammatory cascade in AR, potentially contributing to persistent allergic inflammation. TREM2 (Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells 2) is a receptor predominantly expressed on various myeloid cells, including macrophages, microglia, and dendritic cells10,17–19. It plays a crucial role in immune modulation by regulating cellular responses to external stimuli and facilitating the resolution of inflammation10,17–19. TREM2 functions by binding to a variety of ligands, including apoptotic cells, bacteria, and lipids, activating downstream signaling pathways mediated by adaptor proteins such as DAP10 and DAP1220–22. These adaptor proteins are integral in the transmission of signals that regulate various cellular functions, including macrophage polarization. Macrophages are highly plastic cells that can adopt different phenotypic states in response to environmental cues. The M1 phenotype is associated with pro-inflammatory responses, while M2 polarization is generally considered anti-inflammatory and involved in tissue repair and immune regulation23,24. TREM2’s primary role is to promote M2 macrophage polarization, which is associated with immune suppression, tissue repair, and fibrosis9,25,26. Under normal conditions, M2 macrophages help resolve inflammation and promote healing, but in the context of chronic inflammation and autoimmune diseases such as allergic rhinitis, the overactivation of M2 macrophages can disrupt immune tolerance and exacerbate allergic responses27,28. The Th2-driven inflammatory environment in allergic rhinitis, characterized by cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, could potentially be influenced by TREM2-mediated macrophage polarization, contributing to the persistence and severity of allergic reactions29,30. Thus, TREM2 may play a pivotal role in the progression and regulation of allergic diseases by modulating the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophage subsets. Although substantial research has been conducted on TREM2’s role in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, where it modulates microglial activity to maintain homeostasis31,32, its involvement in allergic diseases, particularly allergic rhinitis, has been less explored. Most existing studies have focused on TREM2’s role in chronic inflammation, cancer immune evasion, and infections33–35, but the molecular mechanisms behind TREM2’s regulation of macrophage polarization in allergic inflammation remain poorly defined. This knowledge gap presents a critical opportunity to investigate the function of TREM2 in allergic rhinitis. Our findings highlight the crucial role of TREM2 in regulating immune responses in allergic rhinitis, particularly through macrophage polarization. We showed that TREM2 enhances M2 polarization and promotes eosinophil survival and activation, contributing to persistent inflammation in allergic rhinitis. Furthermore, we observed that the BCG vaccine alleviates allergic inflammation by downregulating TREM2, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in AR. Future studies should explore the molecular mechanisms underlying TREM2’s role in allergic inflammation. Targeting TREM2 could provide a promising strategy for developing novel treatments for allergic rhinitis and other allergic diseases, aiming to restore immune balance and reduce chronic inflammation (Table 1).

Furthermore, the study examined the role of DAP10 and DAP12 in TREM2-mediated immune modulation in AR. We found that DAP10 and DAP12 protein levels were elevated in TREM2-overexpressing macrophages and reduced in TREM2-knockdown macrophages. This modulation correlated with changes in macrophage polarization and eosinophil activity. DAP10 or DAP12 overexpression in TREM2-overexpressing macrophages amplified M2 polarization and eosinophil activation, while DAP10/DAP12 knockdown in TREM2-deficient macrophages further inhibited eosinophil vitality. These results highlight the critical roles of DAP10 and DAP12 in mediating TREM2’s effects on macrophage polarization and eosinophil function in AR. DAP10 (DNAX-activating protein of 10 kDa) and DAP12 (DNAX-activating protein of 12 kDa) are signaling adaptor proteins that associate with several immune receptors, including TREM2, to mediate cellular responses20,36,37. Both proteins lack intrinsic enzymatic activity and instead recruit kinases such as Syk and ZAP-70 through immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) in their cytoplasmic tails38. Upon receptor engagement, DAP10 and DAP12 initiate downstream signaling pathways that regulate immune cell activation, polarization, and survival36. DAP10, for example, has been implicated in promoting macrophage polarization and enhancing cellular responses to inflammatory stimuli39, while DAP12 is more often associated with the regulation of immune cell activation and cytokine production40,41. Their role in myeloid cells, particularly in macrophages, has been shown to influence the resolution of inflammation and tissue repair, suggesting that they may serve as key mediators of immune responses in both acute and chronic inflammatory conditions42,43. Recent studies have highlighted the significance of DAP10 and DAP12 in various diseases, particularly in chronic inflammation and autoimmune diseases44,45. For example, DAP12 has been shown to regulate microglial activation and contribute to neuroinflammatory processes in neurodegenerative diseases46,47. DAP10 and DAP12 signaling also plays a role in macrophage polarization in cancer, where it has been associated with the promotion of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) that support tumor growth and immune suppression48,49. In the context of allergic diseases, DAP10 and DAP12 have been less studied, but their involvement in macrophage polarization and eosinophil activation suggests they may play a critical role in exacerbating or resolving allergic inflammation36. This highlights the need for further investigation into how these adaptor proteins mediate immune responses in allergic conditions, particularly in the context of TREM2 signaling. This study highlights the importance of TREM2 in AR, revealing that DAP10 and DAP12 are key mediators of TREM2’s function in macrophages. These adaptor proteins influence macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype and eosinophil activation. By identifying DAP10 and DAP12 as critical downstream effectors of TREM2, this research sheds light on the molecular pathways driving allergic inflammation.

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, several limitations must be considered. First, while we utilized mouse models of allergic rhinitis to investigate the role of TREM2, the direct translation of these findings to human pathology requires caution, as human immune responses may differ significantly from those observed in murine models. The precise molecular mechanism linking BCG signaling to Trem2 repression remains to be fully elucidated. BCG stimulation has been shown to activate Toll-like receptor signaling pathways and interferon-associated transcriptional programs that broadly reshape macrophage gene expression profiles. It is therefore plausible that BCG-induced immune activation indirectly suppresses Trem2 transcription through these regulatory pathways. Further studies will be required to identify the specific transcription factors responsible for Trem2 regulation in this context. Second, while we examined the impact of TREM2 modulation on macrophage polarization and eosinophil activity, other immune cells and cytokines involved in allergic inflammation were not extensively explored, which may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the immune network. Lastly, while the study highlights the importance of DAP10 and DAP12 in TREM2-mediated immune regulation, the exact signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms through which these adaptor proteins mediate their effects remain to be fully elucidated, warranting further detailed investigation. Due to experimental limitations, eosinophil degranulation was not assessed in this study, which will be further explored in future work. While batch effects and inter-experimental variability in primary cell cultures may influence the interpretation of subtle cytokine changes in Figs. 4 and 6, the overall trends and conclusions of this study remain unchanged.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underscores the critical role of TREM2 in macrophage-mediated immune modulation in AR. We demonstrated that TREM2 promotes M2 macrophage polarization, which enhances inflammatory responses and contributes to the severity of AR. Additionally, modulating TREM2 expression, either through BCG treatment or macrophage-specific knockout, altered macrophage polarization and eosinophil activity, leading to significant changes in AR pathology. Furthermore, we identified DAP10 and DAP12 as key mediators of TREM2 signaling in macrophages, regulating eosinophil function and immune responses in AR. These findings suggest that targeting TREM2 and its associated signaling pathways could provide a novel therapeutic approach for managing allergic rhinitis, potentially in combination with other immune-modulating therapies.
