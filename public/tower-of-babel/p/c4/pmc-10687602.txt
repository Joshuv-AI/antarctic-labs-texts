Cost of Medically Attended RSV Among Medicaid Beneficiaries ≤2 Years of Age by Underlying Risk Condition
Authors: Yoonyoung Choi, Lynn Finelli
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated costs for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) medical attention in children aged <24 months using MarketScan® Medicaid Multi-State claims database 2015–2019. Average cost was highest for RSV hospitalization with intensive care unit (ICU) admission ($23 514–24 835), followed by no ICU admission ($8039–8990), ED visits ($463–482), and outpatient visits ($145–151). Cost was higher for those with comorbidities.Keywords: Medicaid, RSV, cost, hospitalization, outpatient, emergency visit

INTRODUCTION

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the most common cause of acute lower respiratory infection (ALRI) in infants and children [1, 2]. In the United States, it was estimated that RSV caused approximately 1.5 million outpatient (OP) visits, 500 000 emergency department (ED) visits, and 50 000 hospitalizations among children <2 years old [3, 4].

Extended half-life monoclonal antibodies and maternal vaccination are likely to replace palivizumab (Synagis®) for RSV prevention and will provide an opportunity for prevention for all infants [5]. Information about the cost of medically attended (MA)-RSV will play a critical role in determining the cost-effectiveness of these new prevention strategies [6].

In the United States, it is known that the cost of RSV hospitalization is dependent on underlying medical conditions such as preterm birth, chronic lung disease (CLD), or congenital heart disease (CHD) as well as type of health insurance [7]. For instance, infants born extremely preterm had 5.6 times higher average cost of RSV hospitalization compared with full-term infants; among those born extremely preterm, infants with commercial insurance coverage had 1.5 times the average cost of RSV hospitalization compared with infants covered by Medicaid [7]. However, such data are sparse for the cost of RSV emergency and OP visits which, in fact, far exceed the number of RSV hospitalizations [3, 4]. Costs specific to insurance coverage type and comorbidities are not available for ED and OP visits and are essential for accurate cost of illness estimation.

In this study, we aim to provide a comprehensive cost of MA-RSV cost for hospitalization, OP, and ED visits for RSV lower respiratory tract infection stratified by risk conditions for Medicaid-covered children under 2 years of age.

METHODS

We established a retrospective cohort of Medicaid beneficiaries aged <24 months of age using MarketScan® Medicaid Multi-State database 2015–2019. The database captures records of inpatient and OP services and prescription drug claims in multiple geographically dispersed states, which are not disclosed in the data set. We limited the study population to those covered under fee-for-service for the study period and included beneficiaries in the 2019 data whose cost data were deemed complete (85%). We linked claims data of children with their mothers to estimate date of birth as well as gestational age using a previously validated pregnancy algorithm [8]. To mitigate the sample size shrinkage due to the aforementioned conditions, we did not require any continuous enrollment. Instead, we ascertained all encounters year-round using the following the International Classification for Disease—Version 9 or 10—Clinical Modification (ICD-9 or 10-CM) codes in a principal or secondary diagnosis field: RSV Pneumonia (480.1 or J12.1), RSV Bronchiolitis (466.11 or J21.0), RSV Bronchitis (J20.5—available for ICD-10-CM only), and RSV (079.6 or B97.4).

We included direct medical costs only and excluded direct nonmedical and indirect costs. The unit of cost in our study is an individual encounter, not an episode that can potentially include multiple encounters (eg, 1 RSV episode consisting of an RSV OP visit followed by an RSV hospitalization). The cost per encounter was calculated by combining the number of net payments reported by Medicaid and payments made by beneficiaries. We calculated the average and standard deviation (SD) for costs for ED visit and OP visits and stratified cost for children by intensive care unit (ICU) admission during hospitalization. We inflated the cost to 2021 US dollars using Consumer Price Index for medical care.

In addition, we stratified the cost by age and underlying risk conditions that are considered in the American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines for the use of palivizumab prophylaxis [9] as follows: CLD of prematurity, hemodynamically significant CHD, preterm birth, other comorbidities (anatomic pulmonary abnormalities, neuromuscular disease, profoundly immunocompromised conditions or chemotherapy, cystic fibrosis, or Down syndrome). Risk conditions were defined using diagnostic, procedural, and drug codes, and operational definition details can be found in our earlier study [10].

RESULTS

We analyzed a total of 11 525 encounters derived from our analytical cohort of 6376 children aged less than 2 years. The average age of children in the cohort was 7.60 months (SD of 6.48) of whom 54.72% were male, 40.2% were White, 13.4% were Hispanic, and 22.10% did not have any underlying risk conditions including preterm births.

As reported in Tables 1 and 2, the highest average cost per encounter among infants was observed for RSV hospitalizations with ICU admission ($24 835 ± SD of 50 102) followed by those without ICU admission ($8039 ± 47 405), RSV ED visits ($482 ± 476), and RSV OP visits ($145 ± 190). The second-year average cost by health care setting was similar to the first-year cost, but the number of RSV health care encounters in the second year is much less than that in the first year.

Among those hospitalized, the average cost was highest among infants with comorbidities such as anatomic pulmonary abnormalities, neuromuscular disease, profoundly immunocompromised conditions or chemotherapy, cystic fibrosis, or Down syndrome. Cost for this group for those with and without ICU admission were $42 171 ± 70 838 and $24 809 ± 132 635, respectively. The average cost of an RSV ED visit was highest among infants with CHD at $707 ± 1238 and the average cost of an RSV OP visit was highest among second year children with other comorbidities at $234 ± 240.

DISCUSSION

We described the cost of MA-RSV by healthcare setting, age, and underlying risk conditions among Medicaid beneficiaries aged ≤2 years. As expected, the average cost was highest for RSV hospitalization with ICU admission, followed by that without ICU admission, ED visits, and OP visits. Interestingly, there was only a slight decrease in average cost per encounter from the first year to the second year of life. Infants with other comorbidities or CHD had the highest cost per encounter and their costs were 1.5–3 times higher than infants without these conditions depending on the setting.

Our cost for children with Medicaid is expected to be lower than equivalent costs among commercially insured children under 2 years of age [7, 11]. A recent review paper included 13 studies that reported cost data for RSV hospitalization by insurance type. The average cost per RSV hospitalization was ~50% higher for commercially insured children compared with those with Medicaid ($15 289 vs $10 394, as of 2020 USD) with a larger cost gap among preterm infants than term infants. The lower cost among Medicaid beneficiaries may be related to lower Medicaid payment rates for providers [12], but not to differential services received.

While there are limited cost data for ambulatory settings, the average cost for RSV emergency or urgent care visits was $501 (2020 USD), and that for RSV-associated OP visit was $73 [7]. Given the fact that these data are based on a very small sample (15 subjects), our findings of $482 for ED and $145 for OP are not inconsistent.

It was notable that the average cost of MA-RSV in the second year did not decrease much from that in the first year. It may be that children who required RSV-associated medical attention in the second year of life experienced a severe form of RSV disease or have underlying medical conditions. Consistent with this is that the number of MA-RSV encounters in the second year was disproportionately higher among those with underlying risk conditions.

Our study has several strengths and limitations to note. Our study provided detailed cost estimates among Medicaid beneficiaries stratified by a variety of risk factors. These data will help policymakers generate accurate cost of illness estimates for economically underserved children whose risk for acquiring RSV disease is in fact higher than the commercial population [11]. However, in an effort to accurately estimate cost, we applied multiple selection criteria and constructed a subset of beneficiaries, which could limit the generalizability of study findings. In addition, we needed to exclude costs for a several strata and combine risk conditions and comorbidities categories for which cost per encounter may have been heterogeneous. Finally, our study population is not generalizable to the overall infant population in the United States, among which most MA-RSV cases (~70%) are attributable to otherwise healthy term infants [1]. Hence, the unstratified costs could be overestimated as derived from oversampled infants and children with underlying risk conditions.

CONCLUSION

The cost of MA-RSV is substantial among Medicaid beneficiaries <2 years of age with notable variation across settings and underlying risk conditions. It would be important to ensure the access to newer RSV preventive strategies such as maternal vaccination and extended half-life monoclonal antibodies for this population.
