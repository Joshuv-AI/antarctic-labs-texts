Antibiotics in Hand Surgery: Guidelines and Review
Authors: Jason Strelzow, Joel S Solomon, Sonia Chaudhry, Aviram M Giladi, Chris Jehle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurgical site infection (SSI) remains an important concern in hand surgery, despite low baseline infection rates, especially in clean, elective soft tissue procedures. This manuscript synthesizes current evidence and guidelines on preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis in hand surgery, focusing on procedure type, patient-specific risk factors, and implications for antimicrobial stewardship. Contemporary guidelines advise against routine prophylaxis in clean orthopedic soft tissue procedures, yet practice patterns vary. Evidence from retrospective studies and prospective trials consistently shows no significant reduction in SSI rates with prophylactic antibiotics in common soft tissue hand procedures, including carpal tunnel and trigger finger release, even when performed in procedure room settings. In implant-related hand surgery, theoretical infection risk is elevated because of potential biofilm formation. However, emerging evidence similarly indicates no significant difference in infection rates with or without prophylaxis in clean cases. Patient-related factors such as diabetes, immunosuppression, smoking, obesity, and prolonged operative duration are associated with increased SSI risk, but the extent to which prophylactic antibiotics mitigate this risk remains unclear. Notably, even in higher-risk populations, data supporting routine antibiotic use are inconsistent. Cefazolin is the preferred prophylactic agent because of its efficacy, safety profile, and low cross reactivity in patients with reported penicillin allergy. Substitution with alternative agents such as clindamycin or vancomycin is associated with higher complication and infection rates. Adverse events related to antibiotic use, including allergic reactions, Clostridioides difficile infection, and antimicrobial resistance, further emphasize the need for judicious use. Given the low baseline SSI rates in hand surgery, cost-effectiveness analyses support the selective rather than routine prophylactic strategy given high total numbers needed to treat to prevent infection. Overall, an individualized, risk-based approach aligned with antimicrobial stewardship principles represents the most evidence-based framework. Further high-quality randomized studies are required to define optimal prophylactic strategies in both soft tissue and implant-based hand surgery.Key words: Antibiotics, Evidence-based treatment, Guidelines, Hand surgery, Infection

Surgical site infections (SSIs) remain an important concern across all surgical disciplines, including hand surgery. Hand procedures, ranging from clean, soft tissue interventions to complex implant-based reconstructions, are generally performed in an environment of robust vascularity and minimal contamination risk. Despite these favorable conditions, the role of preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis remains a contentious subject of clinical debate. Historically, antibiotic prophylaxis has been advocated to reduce postoperative infections. However, for elective soft-tissue-only hand procedures, the benefits of routine prophylaxis are less clearly established. The American Society of Health-System Pharmacists therapeutic guidelines—a joint publication of the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists, the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), and the Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America—provide recommendations for orthopedic procedures based on current evidence and expert opinion.1 Their guidance for “clean soft tissue” procedures regardless of location recommends no antibiotic prophylaxis, while those with internal fixation should receive cefazolin or alternative agents. These recommendations are proposed broadly with a grade C strength of evidence.1 These guidelines have challenged the historical precedents that antibiotics are required for procedures, including the hand.

The foundational rationale (early mid-20th century science) for antibiotic prophylaxis was guided under the principle that early prophylaxis reduced perioperative microbial contamination and prevented SSIs. Importantly, however, these studies were methodologically flawed, limiting their generalizability.2,3 Despite this, concerns regarding infection risk, particularly in procedures with implants, led to the widespread adoption of antibiotic prophylaxis as standard practice for fear of bacterial biofilm on foreign implant-related surfaces, which may be resistant to standard host defenses and antibiotics.4,5 Extrapolated evidence from prosthetic joint surgeries supports prophylaxis, demonstrating reductions in infection rates by up to 50% in high-risk patient cohorts.6, 7, 8, 9 By contrast, procedural differences in hand surgery and the hand's rich vascular supply are thought to contribute to inherently lower infection rates and may warrant a more critical assessment of antibiotic prophylaxis.

A scoping review of the literature reveals that current evidence is heavily reliant on retrospective analyses and small cohort studies, with a paucity of large, prospective randomized controlled trials specific to hand surgery. Although landmark studies and guidelines (eg, IDSA) in hand surgery provide valuable parallels, the relative scarcity of hand-specific data and guidelines creates uncertainty regarding optimal prophylactic protocols.1

Advocates for restrictive antibiotic use argue that overuse in low-risk procedures contributes unnecessarily to global antimicrobial resistance, while proponents for broader prophylaxis underscore the devastating consequences of infection. As a result, practice patterns vary among surgeons, often influenced by anecdotal experience rather than high-level evidence.10, 11, 12 This article aims to provide an academic synthesis of current evidence and guidelines regarding antibiotic prophylaxis in hand surgery.

Indications: Time, Implants, Case Factors

Antibiotic prophylaxis in soft tissue procedures

In clean, soft tissue-only hand surgeries—such as carpal tunnel release, trigger finger release, nerve, and arthroscopic procedures—the necessity of routine prophylactic antibiotics has been critically evaluated. Growing evidence suggests that antibiotic prophylaxis does not reduce the rate of SSI following common hand procedures.13, 14, 15 Furthermore, the overuse of antibiotics in these cases may contribute to antimicrobial resistance and adverse drug reactions.16 This stance is echoed in current guidelines that advocate for a selective approach, reserving antibiotics for high-risk patients or procedures of extended surgical duration or involving contaminated wounds.

Using clean surgical techniques appears to be sufficient in maintaining low infection rates, without antibiotics, as demonstrated by studies of soft tissue procedures and fractures managed in the procedure room setting.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Furthermore, several prospective and randomized trials have demonstrated that prophylactic antibiotics do not reduce major or minor infections rates across clean hand procedures.24, 25, 26 Many of these trials have included both elective and nonelective soft tissue, arthroscopic, and implant-related cases.24,27

Antibiotic prophylaxis in implant-related procedures (eg, fractures)

In contrast to soft tissue procedures, implant-related hand surgeries present a theoretically higher risk of infection due to the propensity of foreign materials to harbor bacterial biofilms.28 Growing evidence suggests that the need for antibiotics in clean implant-based hand surgery may not mirror findings in other areas of the body. Two recent retrospective cohort studies both found no difference in infection incidence between patients receiving preoperative prophylactic antibiotics and those who did not during clean implant-based hand surgery.26,29 These findings corroborate findings from earlier work.15,24

Patient and surgical risk factors influencing prophylactic use

Established evidence identifies several high-risk conditions that necessitate careful evaluation when considering antibiotic prophylaxis; however, many of these factors remain understudied or have contradictory findings. Bykowski et al15 suggested that patients with diabetes, active smokers, and longer procedural lengths (>2 hours) were associated with SSI regardless of antibiotic use, which challenges conventional dogma.12,13,30 Procedure length has been associated with surgical site infection both within musculoskeletal and non-MSK related surgical settings. The existing threshold for increased infection risk is considered 2 hours, although newer evidence would be beneficial to re-examine these findings.31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37 In contrast, other studies have not demonstrated efficacy of antibiotics beyond this 2-hour window in hand surgery15,38 calling in to question whether this duration cutoff is applicable in hand surgery.

Patient-Adjusted Risk Factors

Immunocompromised status, which includes patients undergoing chronic corticosteroid therapy, chemotherapy, immunomodulation, or those with advanced human immunodeficiency virus infection, substantially increases the susceptibility to SSIs in both implant and nonimplant hand surgeries.39 The immunosuppressive state impairs innate immune mechanisms at the surgical site, thereby delaying wound healing and facilitating bacterial colonization. Data from orthopedic arthroplasty research parallels these findings, demonstrating nearly doubled SSI rates in immunocompromised individuals compared to immunocompetent cohorts (1.8% vs 0.9%).40, 41, 42, 43, 44

There is limited evidence to guide the management of immune modulators prior to hand surgery. Surgeon may defer to current American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons (AAHKS) guidelines regarding biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) and tofacitinib. Because of studies in nonsurgical patients showing that the durations of the immunosuppressive effects of bDMARDs do not correspond to their serum half-lives, the ACR/AAHKS guidelines instead use the dosing cycle to determine the optimal timing for surgery. For all bDMARDs, they recommend scheduling surgery in the first week of the first withheld dose (eg, week 5 for a drug dosed every 4 weeks) of the patient’s typical dosing regimen, as this point represents the expected nadir of the drug effect.45 However, routine soft tissue surgery of the hand may not require a pause in immunosuppression. In a study by Klifto et al,46 continuing or discontinuing immunosuppressant medications, including steroids, nonbiological DMARDs, and bDMARDs, within one dosing interval of their usual dose during surgery did not result in significant differences in postoperative complications. Additionally, studies by Bykowski et al and Li et al14 did not find a significant reduction in SSIs with antibiotic prophylaxis in the general population undergoing common soft tissue procedures of the hand, including those on immunosuppressive medications. Importantly, there may be specific cases where one may consider prophylactic antibiotics. Specifically, the guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and IDSA recommend antimicrobial prophylaxis for adult patients with cancer-related immunosuppression undergoing surgical procedures, emphasizing the importance of individualized risk assessment.47

In summary, immunomodulators should generally be continued in immunosuppressed patients undergoing clean, elective hand surgery, with specific adjustments for biologics as recommended by the ACR and AAHKS guidelines.

Diabetes mellitus, particularly in poorly controlled cases (HbA1c > 7.5%), consistently emerges as a critical risk factor for infection in surgery.48, 49, 50, 51 Meta-analytical evidence across various surgical disciplines indicates that hyperglycemia adversely affects neutrophil function and promotes biofilm development, both of which are integral to the pathogenesis of implant-associated infections.28,52,53 In diabetic patients, there is mixed data on the incidence of infection following soft tissue procedures; however, in those undergoing hand surgeries involving implants, infection rates approach 2% to 3%, markedly higher than the <1% observed in nondiabetic populations.13,15,54 Overall, however, the exact risk and relationship between glucose-control, insulin dependence, and A1c remains unclear.14,54,55 When evaluating the literature, insulin dependence appears to be the predominant risk factors. The statistical differences between patients with diabetes and those without disappeared when controlling for noninsulin dependent diabetics.56,57 Although overall there is mixed evidence on the efficacy of antibiotics to decrease the SSI risk in patients with diabetes, the literature does not clearly establish which aspects of DM are the most notable drivers of infection risk, nor whether antibiotic prophylaxis adequately mitigates this risk.13,15,58, 59, 60

Other moderate-to-high risk factors include smoking and vascular insufficiency. Smoking-induced vasoconstriction and compromised oxygen transport significantly hinder postoperative wound healing.61 Large-scale cohort studies have reported greater than 1.5-fold increase in SSI risk among smokers following hand surgery.15,39,62 Additional risk enhancers include sex, age, and obesity (body mass index [BMI] > 30 kg/m2).32 Obesity is associated with localized tissue hypoxia, whereas aging correlates with diminished skin elasticity and immunosenescence, both adversely affecting wound healing dynamics.32,63,64 Obesity has a complex relationship with infection as comorbidities confound findings as does the incidence of diabetes; however, studies in hand surgery and orthopedics more generally have demonstrated a positive relationship particularly as BMI increases.65, 66, 67 This finding was supported by the recent work by Dahmus et al26 demonstrating an increased risk in patients with high BMI after implant-related and soft-tissue-only hand surgery.39

One area that continues to push our understanding of surgical risk is wide-awake local anesthesia with no tourniquet (WALANT). Published infection rates in WALANT series are consistently low without routine antibiotic prophylaxis, supporting the broader observation that clean hand surgery with meticulous technique does not require prophylaxis in most patients.21, 22, 23,68, 69, 70

In summary, even in higher-risk populations such as those with diabetes, the routine use of prophylactic antibiotics may not be justified. This is supported in the larger reviews of WALANT surgery where the use of prophylactic antibiotics is rarely incorporated18,71 (Table 1).

Patients characterized by low-risk profiles—healthy, nonsmoking, immunocompetent without major metabolic or vascular comorbidities—demonstrate an exceedingly low incidence of postoperative SSIs (<0.5%) in clean, soft tissue-only hand surgeries devoid of implants. Interestingly, Young et al72 further elucidate that perioperative antibiotic administration in patients with prosthetic joints undergoing hand surgery does not correlate with reduced periprosthetic joint infection rates, suggesting that universal prophylactic application may be unwarranted in this population. This evidence supports a selective, rather than routine, approach to antibiotic prophylaxis in such cases.

Risk-based administration of prophylactic antibiotics in clean, elective hand surgeries is likely the most evidence-based approach. This aligns with the principles of antimicrobial stewardship, aiming to minimize unnecessary antibiotic use and reduce the risk of antibiotic resistance.73

Choice of Antibiotic: Allergies (True Allergy and Cross Reactivity), Indications for Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus

Cefazolin is the preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis of choice as it targets both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria that often colonize the skin and is both bioavailable and safe, as well as cost effective. When a patient has a listed allergy to penicillin, however, doctors are more likely to use alternative prophylaxis, such as vancomycin or clindamycin. Unfortunately, this appears to result in higher rates of SSI, likely secondary to a lack of gram-negative coverage, delays in receiving timely antibiotic administration, and higher potential for other complications such as acute kidney failure.74, 75, 76

The falsely high rate of reported penicillin allergy in the US population (8% to 12%) results from both inaccurate allergy reporting and the possibility for penicillin sensitivities to wane over time.77, 78, 79 In the patients with reported allergies, 99% have negative skin testing to a penicillin challenge and 95% do not have a history of immunoglobulin E mediated hypersensitivity.77 This has been borne out clinically, with low or even zero occurrences of reactions when patients with listed allergies are administered cefazolin.80, 81, 82 In 2022, multiple national allergy organizations recommended that cefazolin be given to patients with severe type 1 hypersensitivity reactions to penicillin, including those with anaphylaxis.83

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is another condition for which preoperative antibiotics are sometimes varied. Roughly 0.6% to 7% of the population is colonized with MRSA, whereas 30% of the population is colonized with Staphylococcus aureus overall.77 Reports vary as to whether decolonization regimens lower the rate of SSI. Vancomycin, however, is only recommended for preoperative prophylaxis in the setting of a history of MRSA infection, either as a standalone or as dual therapy.77 To complete the infusion 30−60 minutes prior to incision, administration should be initiated 90−120 minutes prior to incision given its longer infusion time. In hand surgery, this requires substantial advanced planning.

Complications of Antibiotic Use

As with any pharmacological intervention, the use of prophylactic antibiotics must be balanced against the potential for adverse effects that may affect individual patients and, in the case of antimicrobial agents, broader populations. Because antibiotics can alter the prevalence and diversity of bacteria in the environment, there is the potential to affect entire populations.

It is estimated that there are over 100,000 ED visits annually for adverse reactions to antibiotic therapy, the majority for allergic reactions.84 Although penicillin and cephalosporins are the offending agent in most of these visits, this frequency is largely a consequence of the widespread use of penicillins and cephalosporins compared to other antibiotics. When adverse reaction visits were normalized against the number of prescriptions written for each agent, the most important offenders were vancomycin, linezolid, moxifloxacin, clindamycin, and sulfonamides.84

Although some adverse reactions to antibiotic therapy are typical of a certain class of antibiotics, most antibiotic drug reactions are more associated with a particular antibiotic agent.85 Class-specific adverse reactions for antibiotics commonly used for surgical prophylaxis are shown in Table 2 (adapted from Cunha 2001). Several studies have looked at adverse reactions to perioperative antibiotics. In a retrospective review from the British National Health Service, the rate of adverse events was explored following a single prophylactic dose of 3 g oral amoxicillin or 600 mg oral clindamycin as prophylaxis for dental surgery finding no fatalities attributed to the drug over 30 years.86 The rate of nonfatal adverse reactions to oral amoxicillin was 23 reports per million prescriptions, of which 18 per million were thought to be allergic. In contrast, when a single dose of clindamycin was given before surgery, there were 13 fatalities per million prescriptions and 149 nonfatal adverse events. The fatalities were almost entirely attributable to Clostridioides difficile colitis. Nonfatal adverse events were dominated by gastrointestinal problems and allergic skin reactions.

In some instances, two different antibiotics have been used for surgical prophylaxis to further reduce the risk of SSI. As might be expected, prophylaxis using multiple agents increases the risk of adverse events while not necessarily decreasing the risk of SSI. In a retrospective study of over 70,000 procedures in the VA database, SSIs and adverse events were tabulated for patients who received antibiotic prophylaxis with either a β-lactam, vancomycin, or both agents combined. In the subset of patients undergoing joint replacement surgery, likely the group most akin to hand surgery patients, the risk of acute kidney injury was 11% greater for patients receiving vancomycin + β-lactam, than for either agent alone.87 There was no difference between groups with respect to C. difficile infection or for reduction of SSIs, a finding echoed by a companion study.87 The increased risk of C. difficile infection did not become significant until antibiotic prophylaxis was continued beyond 48 hours.

Among perioperative antibiotic complications, allergic reactions—particularly those attributed to penicillin—merit specific attention given their frequency and the clinical decisions they drive. Allergy to penicillin is the most widely reported drug allergy with a prevalence of 6% to 25%.88 Ninety percent of these individuals are found to be nonreactive on subsequent penicillin skin testing79,83,89; nevertheless, it is common practice, in the context of patient-reported penicillin allergy, for surgeons to use alternate medications such as vancomycin or clindamycin, to avoid a perceived increased risk of allergic reaction. Although cephalosporins and penicillins share a β-lactam ring, allergenic cross reactivity is primarily mediated by the R1 side chain, which is distinct between these agents and explains the low cross reactivity reported in clinical series (<1%).90,91 In a large meta-analysis exploring the frequency of dual allergies to both penicillin and cefazolin, the frequency of cefazolin allergy in patients with self-reported penicillin allergy was 0.7% but was 3% in patients with confirmed penicillin allergy.92 Similar hypersensitivity reactions have been reported at a rate of approximately 3% regardless of antibiotic prophylaxis agent and allergy status.93

Perioperative anaphylaxis occurs in approximately 1/10,000 musculoskeletal surgery cases in the US and the leading cause is antibiotics.94 A prospective study supported by a recent database review of over 100,000 surgical encounters in which patients were given prophylactic IV cefazolin found no difference in the rate of perioperative anaphylaxis between patients with documented penicillin allergy and those without.95,96 Interestingly, several studies show an increased risk of SSI when alternate antibiotics are used to avoid an allergic reaction (up to 1.5× risk).97

The nonselective use of antibiotics can give rise to antibiotic-resistant organisms, which can spread through a community challenging treatment algorithms. These broad negative impacts of antibiotic use have led to the concept of antimicrobial stewardship—the development of best practices in the selection, dose, duration, and route of administration of an antibiotic to maximize benefit and reduce harm to individuals, as well as minimize potential harm to the community. Little is known about how much a single dose of prophylactic antibiotic can affect the development of resistant organisms. In a study of patients undergoing hip or knee arthroplasty, prophylactic cloxacillin did not significantly change the bacterial communities present in swabs of the nares when comparing preoperative samples with those obtained some number of days after surgery.98 In contrast, similar pre- and postoperative swabs of selected skin sites did show significant changes in corynebacterium and staph species at the time of hospital discharge.98 Unlike the nares, however, the skin sites were also subjected to two preoperative showers with chlorhexidine soap and the operative prep with chlorhexidine/alcohol. Additional work remains to delineate normal temporal changes in the skin microbiome and how it is affected by prophylactic antibiotics.

Cost: IV Abx, Oral, Treating Complications

Cost analysis becomes particularly important when the clinical superiority of antibiotic prophylaxis is limited or equivalent to no treatment. Fundamentally, overall SSI rates across various conditions are low such as in clean elective hand surgery (approximately 0.3% to 0.5%), and clean traumatic cases (1% to 2%). Therefore, when small absolute infection risk reduction with antibiotics is present does such rarity adequately balance the cost/benefit of SSI prophylaxis.99 Conversely, in high-risk or traumatic cases the risks rise which may support the use of prophylaxis. The direct costs for an SSI can be substantial, whereas the costs for a single prophylactic antibiotic dose are typically low (tens of dollars).100,101 The utility of prophylactic antibiotic use and the cost-effectiveness thus hinges around SSI risk; prophylaxis saves money when baseline SSI risk and consequences are high.102Although a complex equation, the baseline SSI rates in hand surgery are low leading to large numbers needed to treat to prevent infection.15 Prophylaxis should be justified economically with thresholds set by infection rates and cost of infection management. Although cost benefit decisions are layered, a unifying principle can guide prophylaxis:

Local SSI Risk × Cost of Complication > Prophylaxis Cost

For example, rough modeling illustrates the trade-off. If an SSI costs $12,000 and prophylaxis costs $20, a 0.17% absolute risk reduction yields break-even (numbers needed to treat ≈ 600 to save $12,000). Thus, if the baseline SSI risk is 1% and prophylactic antibiotics can reduce this risk to 0.5%, then an expected savings of approximately $60 per patient is obtained (0.5% × $12,000) for each dose of antibiotics ($20 cost). This would suggest a small but important net benefit related to prophylactic antibiotics. In contrast, a baseline risk of 0.1%, would generate a $12 savings per patient, well below the cost of prophylaxis.10,100,103

Summary

The role of prophylactic antibiotics in hand surgery necessitates a nuanced, evidence-based approach. Although routine use is generally unwarranted in clean, soft tissue-only procedures, the role of prophylaxis in implant-based hand surgery remains less clearly defined. Emerging evidence suggests that the benefit is likely limited, and individualized risk assessment—accounting for patient comorbidities, surgical complexity, and local microbial patterns remains the most defensible approach. Continued research and adherence to evolving guidelines will further refine these practices, ensuring both efficacy in infection prevention and responsible antimicrobial stewardship. The lack of consensus underscores an urgent need for well-designed, high-powered randomized controlled trials to clarify the role of antibiotic with particular attention to patient-centered risk stratification.
