CLIC3 is upregulated across all subtypes of breast cancer and plays a key role in cell migration, invasion and growth in soft agar
Authors: Paul Mellor, Shari E Smith, Stephanie Kendall, Liliia Kyrylenko, Alissar Monzer, Anurag Saxena, Farah Goubran, Deborah H Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen with metastatic breast cancer have a disheartening 5-year survival rate of only 28%. CREB3L1 (cAMP responsive element binding protein 3 like 1) is a transcription factor and tumor suppressor which is downregulated in ~ 30% of human breast cancers, with higher frequencies in more advanced metastatic breast tumors.MethodsTo identify new targets contributing to metastatic properties, we carried out a differential gene expression analysis between highly metastatic breast cancer cells with low CREB3L1 and the corresponding lines expressing HA-CREB3L1. This analysis was carried out across three different subtypes of breast cancer cells (T47D, HCC1954 and HCC1806; all CREB3L1-low). Key signaling pathways and cell functions most impacted by CREB3L1 expression were identified using a bioinformatics analysis. Specific genes consistently upregulated in the metastatic cells were knocked down to assess their impact on cell migration, cell invasion, growth in soft agar across in multiple breast cancer lines. The effect of knocking down the top metastatic gene identified in this study was further tested using in vivo mouse model of primary breast tumor growth in the mammary fat pad, and metastatic colonization of the lung.ResultsBreast cancer cells with low CREB3L1 expression showed upregulation of metastasis and integrin signaling pathways and enhanced cell movement, migration, invasion functions, consistent with its known role as a tumor suppressor. CLIC3 (chloride intracellular channel 3), a protein with roles in integrin recycling, cell migration and invasion, was found to be consistently upregulated in CREB3L1-low cells and in all subtypes of breast tumors. Increased CLIC3 expression was associated with poor patient survival. Knockdown of CLIC3 in several cell lines reduced cell migration, invasion and anchorage-independent growth in soft agar, effects that could be rescued by co-transfection of an shRNA-insensitive CLIC3 plasmid. CLIC3 knockdown also decreased tumor growth and blocked metastases in a mouse xenograft model of breast cancer.ConclusionsThese results suggest that CLIC3 has a key role in promoting cell migration, invasion and growth in soft agar, and CLIC3 inhibitors may be a viable treatment option for breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02270-3.Keywords: CREB3L1, CLIC3, Metastasis suppression, Breast cancer, Differential gene expression, Knockdown and rescue, Mouse xenografts

Background

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women, representing 25% of the new cancer cases each year [1]. About 1 in 8 women will be diagnosed with breast cancer in their lifetime and 1 in 33 will die from it [1]. The 5-year survival rate for breast cancer is 99% for localized disease, 86% for regional disease that has spread outside the breast, and this drops to 28% for metastatic breast cancer [2]. Breast cancers are grouped into 4 major subtypes based on the expression of the estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER2) [3]. ER+ are the largest group of breast cancers (60–70%), express ER and/or PR, and are often treated with both endocrine and chemotherapy treatments [4]. ER+ breast cancers can be further subdivided into luminal A (40–60%), that lack expression of HER2, and luminal B (10–20%), which co-express amplified HER2. Both luminal B and HER2 + breast cancers (10–15%) overexpress HER2 and are treated with antibodies to HER2, such as trastuzumab [4]. Triple negative breast cancers (TNBCs) (15–20%) lack the three receptors that define the other subtypes and typically receive chemotherapy, since they lack the receptors present in the other subtypes.

We have been studying CREB3L1 (cAMP responsive element binding protein 3 like 1), a transcription factor that functions as a tumor suppressor in breast, bladder, ovarian and squamous cell lung cancer [5–10]. CREB3L1 is normally ubiquitously expressed [11, 12] and under low stress conditions resides in the endoplasmic reticulum as a ~84 kDa transmembrane precursor protein [13]. In response to cell stress, CREB3L1 traffics to the Golgi complex where it is cleaved by the site-specific proteases, S1P and S2P [13]. The soluble, mature and active CREB3L1 transcription factor (~57 kDa) translocates into the nucleus to regulate the expression of genes, some of which have known roles in tumor progression, angiogenesis and metastasis [5, 13].

CREB3L1-low rat and human breast cancer cell lines are highly metastatic as compared to breast cancer cells that retain expression of CREB3L1 [5, 8]. Ectopic expression of CREB3L1, significantly reduces cell migration, cell invasion, growth in soft agar and resistance to hypoxia-induced apoptosis, in vitro cell-based assays of cancer cell properties [5, 8]. Consistent with these results, poorly metastatic CREB3L1-expressing cells can be converted to a more metastatic phenotype (increased growth in soft agar, migration, invasion) by CREB3L1 knockdown [5]. Both a rat syngeneic model of breast cancer [5] and a mouse xenograft model of human breast cancer [8] demonstrated that ectopic expression of CREB3L1 significantly reduces the in vivo tumor growth and metastases observed in CREB3L1-low breast cancer cells. These results provide strong evidence that CREB3L1 is a tumor suppressor in breast cancer.

The expression of CREB3L1 is frequently lost in more advanced metastatic breast cancers, including most TNBCs. Analysis of CREB3L1 expression in breast tumors (> 200) and the TCGA database (> 400 tumors) found that CREB3L1 expression was frequently increased in early tumor development as compared to normal breast cells [6]. This could be in response to the stressful conditions that exist within the tumor environment including low nutrients and low oxygen levels, as observed for other stress response proteins [14–16]. Unique from other stress response proteins, CREB3L1 expression was frequently very low in most TNBCs and most high-grade metastatic breast cancers (51% of grade 8 and 73% of grade 9 tumors) [6, 8]. These findings support our in vitro data suggesting that the loss of CREB3L1 contributes to the metastatic properties of breast cancer cells and may be required to avoid apoptosis under prolonged stress conditions. Overall, CREB3L1 expression is absent from ~30% of breast cancers across all major subtypes, including up to 75% of TNBCs [8]. Moreover, patients with CREB3L1-low breast cancers have a significantly shorter relapse-free survival, particularly for the luminal A and TNBC subtypes [6, 8]. CREB3L1 is also typically absent from many metastatic cells, making it difficult to use this information to target them.

In this report, we set out to identify differentially expressed genes, specifically upregulated in CREB3L1-low breast cancer cells, and then to target key upregulated gene(s) that contribute to these metastatic properties. These genes could then form the basis for a new targeted therapy for metastatic breast cancers, many of which are CREB3L1-low [8].

Methods

Cell culture, transfection and shRNA knockdown

The human breast cancer cell lines, T47D (Luminal A; ER+), HCC1954 (HER2+), HCC1806 (TNBC), MDA-MB-231 (TNBC) and the human non-tumorigenic breast epithelial cell line, MCF10A, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were maintained according to ATCC recommendations for up to six months from the time of resuscitation. All cell lines were authenticated by the supplier.

Parental cells engineered to stably express three copies of a hemagglutinin (HA)-tag at the N-terminus of human CREB3L1 (HA-CREB3L1) have been previously described [8]. HA-CREB3L1 expression was verified after resolving cell lysates by SDS-PAGE, transferring to nitrocellulose and immunoblotting with an antibody for CREB3L1 (11235-2-AP from Protein Tech; rabbit, 1:500) and HA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; sc-7392; 1 µg/mL). Bound antibody was detected using anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IR680 or IR800 (LICOR Biosciences; 1:5000) and an LICOR Odyssey infrared scanner. Blots shown are representative of results from at least three independent experiments using distinct lysate samples to provide biological replicates.

Stable clones were generated with LCN2 or CLIC3 knockdown, as well as those with a non-targeting shRNA control. To produce knockdown cell lines, cells were transduced with a lentivirus containing shRNAs targeting LCN2 (shLCN2; also known as NGAL, sc-43969-V, Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA) or CLIC3 (shCLIC3; sc-62126-V; Santa Cruz), as well as a scrambled shRNA control (shControl; sc-108080; Santa Cruz). Twenty-four hours after transduction, culture media was replaced with fresh media containing puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, P8833, 5 µg/mL). Single cell clones were picked and expanded. Knockdown was verified by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR; see below) and by immunoblotting with an anti-LCN2 antibody (sc-57517, Santa Cruz, 1 µg/mL) or an anti-CLIC3 antibody (sc-81872, Santa Cruz, 1 µg/mL or 15971-1-AP, ProteinTech, 1:500) as appropriate. Bound antibodies were detected as detailed above.

To generate an shRNA-insensitive CLIC3 plasmid, the cDNA for CLIC3 was subcloned into a mammalian expression vector to generate a triple FLAG-tagged protein fused in-frame to the N-terminus of CLIC3 (FLAG-CLIC3). To generate the cDNA for CLIC3, RNA was extracted from MCF10A cells using an RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Toronto, ON, Canada) and cDNA was generated using oligo-dT primers with a Multi Scribe Reverse Transcriptase system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The cDNA encoding full-length CLIC3 was amplified by PCR, incorporating BglII and EcoRI sites immediately before and after the coding region respectively, using a KOD hot start DNA polymerase (Novagen, Madison, WI, USA). The PCR products were purified, digested and subcloned into the BglII and EcoRI sites of a previously described pFLAG3 vector [17]. The entire coding region of CLIC3 was verified by DNA sequencing. Three silent mutations were then introduced such that the shRNA would not bind and yet the amino acid sequence of CLIC3 would not be changed. Site-directed mutagenesis was carried out to introduce 3 single nucleotide changes with no resulting amino acid changes, using the QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. DNA sequencing of the entire coding region ensured that no additional mutations had been introduced.

Stable cell lines were generated by separately transfecting HCC1806 + shCLIC3 and HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 + shCLIC3 cells with the FLAG-CLIC3 plasmid using X-tremeGene 9 (Sigma-Aldrich, XTG9-RO), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were selected in blasticidin (Invitrogen 46-1120, 2 µg/mL) and the resulting clonal lines were evaluated for CLIC3 expression using quantitative real-time PCR and by immunoblotting with antibodies to CLIC3 as above, with β-actin (sc-517582, Santa Cruz, 1 µg/mL) serving as a loading control.

Quantitative real time PCR

RNA was extracted from cells using an RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Toronto, ON, Canada) and cDNA was generated using oligo-dT primers with a Multi Scribe Reverse Transcriptase system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). CREB3L1 expression (assay ID Hs00999642_ml, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and CLIC3 expression (assay ID Hs00362166_g1, Thermo Fisher) were measured by quantitative real-time PCR performed using TaqMan probes and TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), according to manufacturer’s protocols. Relative expression was calculated using glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; assay ID Hs02758991_g1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as a reference gene. All samples were loaded in triplicate wells, and fluorescent emission was detected using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). All results were normalized to the expression of GAPDH and are reported as expression relative to control samples. LCN2 expression was measured similarly by quantitative real-time PCR using TaqMan probes (assay ID Hs01008571_m1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as detailed above.

Microarray and differentially expressed gene (DEG) analyses

Microarray analysis comparing the parental human breast cancer cells (T47D, HCC1954, HCC1806) and their corresponding HA-CREB3L1 expressing cells was performed using the services of the Princess Margaret Genomics Centre, Toronto, Canada. Briefly, total RNA was isolated from the cell lines using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen). Two replicate RNA samples were analyzed for each cell line and a total of 47,323 probes were used, represented on the human whole genome HT-12 V4 BeadChip (Illumina). Only probes that were above the 20th percentile of the distribution of intensities were allowed to pass through a quality control filtering process. This final set contained 43,116 probes. Data was analyzed by a qualified bioinformatician at the Princess Margaret Genomics Centre, with only genes that had a 1.4-fold increase or decrease with a significance of p < 0.05 being included in the final results. Volcano plots were generated using Microsoft Excel (version 16.48) and imported into Microsoft PowerPoint (version 16.48) for formatting and highlighting. Venn diagrams were generated using InteractiVenn, free online software [18]. Canonical signaling pathways and cell functions most significantly altered in parental cells as compared to HA-CREB3L1-expressing cells were determined using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) software (QIAGEN Inc., https://www.qiagenbioinformatics.com/products/ingenuity-pathway-analysis). Z scores; significantly activated defined as Z ≥ + 2; significantly inhibited defined at Z ≤ -2. Note that the HT-12 V4 BeadChip microarray includes one probe specific for CREB3L1 mRNA located in the 3` untranslated region (Accession Number NM_052854.2; probe sequence: acccaaacagacacatcaacgcgaggggactcacagacacccca) and so it does not detect the transfected HA-CREB3L1 which only contains coding regions of the CREB3L1 gene.

Survival analysis

The Kaplan-Meier overall survival probability for breast cancer patients was determined using the online software KM plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=service#) [19, 20]. This database contains gene expression data and overall survival information from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), European Genome-phenome Archive (EGA) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). The database is handled by a PostgreSQL server, which integrates gene expression and clinical data simultaneously. The patient samples are split into two groups where all possible cut off values between the lower and upper quartiles are computed, and the best performing threshold is used as a cut off. The two patient cohorts are compared by a Kaplan-Meier survival plot, and the hazard ratio with 95% confidence intervals and log rank P value are calculated.

Gene expression in breast cancer tissues

The gene expression of LCN2, CFB, S100A8 and CLIC3 in normal breast (n = 92) and breast cancer (n = 4293) was obtained from the Gene Expression database of Normal and Tumor tissues (GENT2; data includes TCGA and GEO) [21] dataset GPL96. Box and whisker plots were generated using Microsoft Excel (version 16.48), with significance determined using a Mann Whitney U test using GraphPad PRISM software (version 9.1.0; GraphPad Software, Inc.). The gene expression of LCN2, CFB, S100A8 and CLIC3 across breast cancer subtypes was also obtained from the GENT2 database: luminal A (n = 379), luminal B (n = 244), HER2 (n = 230) and TNBC (n = 251). A One-Way ANOVA, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was carried out using GraphPad PRISM software (version 9.1.0; GraphPad Software, Inc.). In all instances, significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Cell-based assays

Cell proliferation was determined by cell counting over time. Briefly, cells (2 × 105) were seeded onto three 10 cm plates. One plate of each cell type was trypsinized and counted each day for 3 days. Cells (2 × 105) were assayed for their ability to migrate and invade through an 8 μm pore-size filter membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) toward medium containing 10% FBS or control low serum medium (0.5% FBS). Cells were serum starved for 16 h in RPMI 1640 medium containing 0.5% FBS prior to the migration or invasion assay. Invasion assays were performed similarly with the exception that the filters were coated with 2 mg/mL growth factor reduced Matrigel (Corning, Corning, NY, USA). Various time-points were used for the migration and invasion assays as indicated for each experiment. Filters were fixed with methanol, stained with hematoxylin, and migrated cells were counted using a bright field microscope. Where indicated, results were expressed as migration or invasion index defined as: the average number of cells per field for 10% FBS - the average number of cells per field for the 0.5% FBS control. To assess anchorage-independent growth, cells were grown in soft agar [8]. Briefly, cells (5 × 104) were suspended in soft agar in 6 cm dishes and allowed to grow for 21 days. An additional 1 ml of medium was layered on top every 7 days. Colonies were counted using a bright field microscope. Plates were fixed with methanol, stained with cresyl violet, and images were captured using a bright field microscope with a low magnification (5x) and are shown at the same magnification (scale bar = 300 μm). All assays were repeated in at least three independent experiments, unless otherwise indicated.

Hypoxia and apoptosis assays

Cells were plated in 10 cm plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2 (normoxic conditions). Plates were either kept under normoxic conditions or transferred to a hypoxia chamber and incubated at 37 °C under hypoxic conditions (0.5% oxygen) for 24 h. The level of hypoxia induced apoptosis was assessed with a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) annexin V/PI Apoptosis kit (Invitrogen V13242). Briefly, cells were resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cell/ml in annexin binding buffer. FITC-conjugated annexin V and propidium iodide were added and cells were incubated for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Cells were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Mouse tumor assays

All mice used in this study were handled according to the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care, and the regulations of the University of Saskatchewan Animal Care Committee (protocol number 20140030). Mice were obtained from our local breeding colony of NOD/SCID/γ mice at the University of Saskatchewan. Female NOD/SCID/γ mice were used to assess the effects of CLIC3 knockdown on tumorigenic and metastatic properties of HCC1806 cells. HCC1806 + shControl or HCC1806 + shCLIC3 cells (2 × 103, 8–10 mice per group) were washed, suspended in a 1:1 mixture of medium and Matrigel (Corning), and injected subcutaneously into the mammary fat pad. Mice were monitored 3 times per week for clinical signs of discomfort and weight loss). Tumor sizes were measured using calipers and tumor volumes were calculated (W2*L)/2. Mice were sacrificed when tumors reached 1.5 cm or for humane reasons or after 55 days. Tumors were harvested, formalin fixed, paraffin embedded and sections stains with hematoxylin and eosin as previously described [8]. Images shown are of the same magnification (scale bar = 2500 μm).

To assess the metastatic properties of CLIC3, female NOD/SCID/γ mice were injected in the tail vein with a suspension of HCC1806 + shControl or HCC1806 + shCLIC3 cells (2.5 × 105 in PBS, 8–10 mice per group) [22]. Mice were monitored as above and sacrificed at 54 days, or for humane reasons. The lungs and liver were harvested and assessed for metastases. No metastases were observed in the livers. Lung metastases were evaluated by a pathologist and quantified for the proportion (%) of the lung that was tumor bearing. Images were taken at 20x magnification using an Aperio virtual microscope (Leica) and are shown at the same magnification (scale bar = 1,500 μm).

Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as means ± standard error of mean (SEM), from at least three independent experiments, unless otherwise indicated. Unless otherwise indicated, statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2010 software. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used with significance set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Differential gene expression analysis upon CREB3L1-loss

To identify genes upregulated in CREB3L1-low breast cancers, we carried out an analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEG) in cells ± HA-CREB3L1 using microarrays. Three CREB3L1-low parental cell lines from different breast cancer subtypes were used: T47D (ER+, luminal A), HCC1954 (HER2+) and HCC1806 (TNBC), each compared to the corresponding line stably expressing HA-CREB3L1 (Additional file 1 - Fig. S1a-S1b). Genes that were differentially expressed in the parental CREB3L1-low cells were identified as compared to their corresponding HA-CREB3L1-expressing lines. The 5 most upregulated and 5 most downregulated DEGs for each cell line are listed in Table 1, with the full data available (Additional file 2 - Table S1). These genes have diverse functions with roles in stress responses, secretion, metabolism, cell proliferation, survival, adhesion and migration. A volcano plot shows some of the most significantly DEGs in CREB3L1-low for each cell line pair (Additional file 1 - Fig. S2 and Additional file 2 - Table S1). There are many changes in gene expression between the CREB3L1-low and HA-CREB3L1 expressing breast cancer cell lines.

Identification of the canonical signaling pathways and cell functions most impacted by low CREB3L1 expression, was assessed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) software. Each cell line pair was analyzed separately and the top signaling pathways and resulting cell functions in common were determined (Fig. 1a, b). Strikingly, having low expression of CREB3L1 resulted in the activation of many more cancer-related pathways and functions than were inhibited, supporting its role as a tumor suppressor. Cellular functions upregulated in CREB3L1-low breast cancer cells included: the transport of molecules, endocytosis, cell survival, cell movement, migration, invasion, the development of vasculature and metastasis. Since low CREB3L1 expression allows for their upregulation, these data suggest that CREB3L1 represses the expression of genes that promote cancer cell migration and metastasis.

Since we were specifically interested in genes upregulated in CREB3L1-low breast cancer cells as possible new targets for therapy, we further focused on the top four upregulated genes: LCN2 (lipocalin 2), CFB (complement factor B), S100A8 (S100 calcium binding protein A8) and CLIC3 (chloride intracellular channel 3) (also highlighted in red in Additional file 1 - Fig. S2). We reasoned that the best genes to target would have a higher expression in breast tumors associated with a shorter overall survival and as a result, a poorer prognosis. Therefore, we carried out a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis for each of these four genes (Fig. 2a–d). High expression of CFB correlated with better prognosis for breast cancer patients, suggesting that this gene was likely not an ideal target (Fig. 2b). In contrast, high expression of LCN2, S100A8 and CLIC3 all correlated with poorer prognosis due to a significantly shorter overall survival for breast cancer patients (Fig. 2a, c-d), suggesting they could be good targets to pursue.

To determine if expression of these genes was consistently found to be increased in breast tumor samples as compared to normal breast tissue, we analyzed the Gene Expression database of Normal and Tumor tissues (GENT2) [21]. We also compared gene expression across specific breast cancer subtypes (Fig. 2e–h). CFB expression was significantly increased in breast cancers across all subtypes (Fig. 2f). However, upregulation of CFB was associated with better outcomes for patients, as noted above, thus it was excluded from further study. S100A8 expression was significantly upregulated only in HER2+ breast cancers (Fig. 2g). Since we have previously reported that low CREB3L1 expression is primarily associated with the two HER2-negative subtypes of breast cancer, luminal A and TNBC [6], we decided not to pursue this gene target.

LCN2 expression was downregulated or relatively unchanged across breast cancer subtypes (Fig. 2e). This suggests that upregulation of LCN2, while associated with low levels of CREB3L1 in our model cell lines, and associated with poor prognosis, may not be a prevalent feature of breast cancers overall. Prior to carrying out this expression analysis, we carried out some LCN2 knockdown experiments which are discussed briefly in the next section. CLIC3 expression was consistently and significantly upregulated across all breast cancer subtypes (Fig. 2h), suggesting that it was the strongest gene candidate to pursue further.

Impact of LCN2 knockdown

Since low CREB3L1 levels are most prevalent in TNBCs we used HCC1806 ± HA-CREB3L1 cells to assess the impact of LCN2 knockdown on the properties of breast cancer cells. We knocked down expression of LCN2 in HCC1806 cells and in the corresponding HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 line to determine if there might be additional impacts (Additional file 1 - Fig. S3). Recalling that LCN2 was identified as a differentially expressed gene upregulated in HCC1806 as compared to HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 cells, it was not surprising that HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 showed a significant reduction in LCN2 expression as compared to HCC1806 cells. LCN2 was knocked down by 80% in the HCC1806 cells resulting in expression similar to that in HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 cells (Additional file 1 - Fig. S3a). Knockdown of LCN2 in HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 cells further reduced the already low levels of LCN2 expression.

LCN2 knockdown reduced the migration of HCC1806 cells to levels similar to that of HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 cells (Additional file 1 - Fig. S3b). The combination of HA-CREB3L1 expression and LCN2 knockdown did not show additional impacts, suggesting that increased CREB3L1 and decreased LCN2 impact cell migration similarly. In contrast, knockdown of LCN2 in HCC1806 or HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 cells, had little or no effect on anchorage-independent growth in soft agar (Additional file 1 - Fig. S3c). These results suggest that targeting LCN2 has the desired impact on some, but not all cancer cell properties. As noted above, targeting LCN2 was not pursued further.

Impact of CLIC3 knockdown

The remainder of our work focused on determining the impacts of CLIC3 knockdown on cancer cell properties using both in vitro cell-based assays and in vivo mouse xenograft experiments. To evaluate the impact of CLIC3 expression in HCC1806 and HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 cells we knocked down CLIC3 (Additional file 1 - Fig. S4a, Fig. 3a). Consistent with CLIC3 being identified as upregulated in HCC1806 cells as compared with HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 cells, CLIC3 was reduced upon HA-CREB3L1 expression by about 50% and knockdown of CLIC3 further reduced CLIC3 expression levels in both HCC1806 ± HA-CREB3L1 cells. CLIC3 knockdown and HA-CREB3L1 expression, as well as the combination of both, did not significantly impact cell proliferation rates (Additional file 1 - Fig. S4b), consistent with what has been reported previously when CREB3L1 was ectopically expressed [5, 8].

As a control for possible adverse effects of CLIC3 knockdown, we also characterized non-tumorigenic breast cells MCF10A, with CLIC3 knockdown (Additional file 1 - Fig. S4c). CLIC3 knockdown did not affect the viability of these cells or their proliferation rate (Additional file 1 - Fig. S4d). These results suggest that knockdown of CLIC3 is not toxic to cells, an important consideration if CLIC3 may be a target of future therapies for metastatic breast cancer.

Knockdown of CLIC3 caused a significant reduction in both cell migration and invasion for HCC1806 cells, providing benefits similar to those observed upon HA-CREB3L1 expression (Fig. 3b). Analogous results were seen for anchorage-independent growth, where CLIC3 knockdown reduced the number of colonies in soft agar compared to HCC1806 cells, to levels similar to those for HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 (Fig. 3c). The effects of HA-CREB3L1 expression and CLIC3 knockdown on these properties were not additive. These promising results suggested that targeting CLIC3 may be as effective as re-expression of CREB3L1 in reducing the cancer cell properties of migration, invasion and anchorage-independent growth.

CREB3L1 expression has been shown to cause a significant increase in hypoxia-induced apoptosis compared to CREB3L1-low cells in rat mammary adenocarcinoma cells [5]. Therefore, we assessed hypoxia-induced apoptosis and the impact of CREB3L1 expression and/or CLIC3 knockdown in the HCC1806 cells (Fig. 3d). Very low levels of apoptosis were detected under normoxic conditions for HCC1806 cells, and these did not change significantly upon HA-CREB3L1 expression and/or CLIC3 knockdown. In contrast, HA-CREB3L1 expression increased hypoxia-induced apoptosis in HCC1806 cells, and CLIC3 knockdown had similar effects (Fig. 3d). The combination of CREB3L1 expression and CLIC3 knockdown did not further increase the apoptosis induced by hypoxic conditions, consistent with the idea that CREB3L1 expression and CLIC3 knockdown have similar impacts on cell behavior.

The impact of CLIC3 knockdown was also evaluated in T47D and T47D + HA-CREB3L1 luminal A breast cancer cells (Fig. 3e). We have noted previously that T47D cells are too large to fit through the 8 μm pore size in Boyden chambers to carry out migration and invasion studies [8], thus we only carried out soft agar assays. Anchorage-independent growth was reduced by CLIC3 knockdown to a similar extent as HA-CREB3L1 expression, with a similar number of colonies formed in soft agar (Fig. 3f). As before, the combination of both HA-CREB3L1 expression and CLIC3 knockdown had similar effects as for each alone (Fig. 3f). We then also evaluated the impact of CLIC3 knockdown in HER2+ breast cancer cells, HCC1954 and in the TNBC line MDA-MB-231 (Figs. 4a, e). Cell migration, cell invasion and growth in soft agar were all significantly reduced upon CLIC3 knockdown in both cell lines (Fig. 4b–d, f–h). Thus, the impact of CLIC3 knockdown may be beneficial at reducing the cancer cell properties in multiple breast cancer cell lines of different subtypes.

CLIC3 rescue experiments

To ensure that the benefits we observed were specifically due to CLIC3 knockdown, and not possibly the result of off-target effects, we performed rescue experiments. We generated a triple FLAG-tagged CLIC3 plasmid that was insensitive to the shRNA used for the CLIC3 knockdown (FLAG-CLIC3; see methods) and transfected this into CLIC3 knockdown cells HCC1806 + shCLIC3 and HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 + shCLIC3 (Fig. 5a). Re-expression of FLAG-CLIC3 reversed the effects of CLIC3 knockdown on both cell migration (Fig. 5b) and anchorage-independent growth of colonies in soft agar in HCC1806 + shCLIC3 cells (Fig. 5c). This rescue experiment suggests that the properties we have been characterizing are the result of decreased CLIC3 and not from off-target effects. Re-expression of FLAG-CLIC3 did not significantly impact these properties in HCC1806 + HA-CREB3L1 ± shCLIC3 cells since expression of HA-CREB3L1 reduced these properties regardless of CLIC3 levels. These results indicate that re-expression of FLAG-CLIC3 reverses the phenotypes of CLIC3 knockdown in cells with low expression of CREB3L1 but cells expressing high CREB3L1 (i.e. HA-CREB3L1) are relatively insensitive to alterations in CLIC3 expression. This suggests that CLIC3 does not function downstream of CREB3L1.

Tumor xenograft and metastasis experiments

To assess the impact of CLIC3 knockdown on the tumorigenicity of breast cancer cells, female immune compromised (NOD/SCID/γ) mice were injected in the mammary fat pad with the various HCC1806-derived cell lines and tumor growth was monitored over time (Fig. 6a, b). HCC1806 + shControl cells grew large tumors whereas cells with CLIC3 knockdown showed a significant reduction in tumor growth over time (Fig. 6a). Stained sections of the resulting mammary tissue clearly showed more extensive breast tumor development in the mice injected with HCC1806 + shControl cells as compared to those injected with HCC1806 + shCLIC3 cells, indicating that CLIC3 knockdown reduces breast tumor development (Fig. 6b). To determine if CLIC3 knockdown impacts breast cancer cell metastasis, we carried out tail vein injections into female NOD/SCID/γ mice [22]. HCC1806 + shControl cells were highly metastatic, showing many lung metastases of considerable size (Fig. 6c, d). Remarkably, HCC1806 cells with knockdown of CLIC3 did not show any evidence of lung metastases (Fig. 6c, d). These results strongly suggest that CLIC3 contributes to metastasis and validates CLIC3 as a new clinical target to block breast cancer progression and metastasis.

Discussion

CREB3L1 is a transcription factor in the CREB3-family, and it contains a bZIP domain to mediate binding to DNA and dimerization [23]. In addition, CREB3L1 contains a unique transcriptional repression domain, not found in other CREB/ATF-family members [23], which suggests it could carry out novel gene repressor functions. To better understand the influence of CREB3L1 expression on gene expression, we identified genes that were differentially expressed in parental cells with low CREB3L1 expression, as compared to HA-CREB3L1 expressing cells across multiple subtypes of breast cancer. The overlapping genes differentially regulated in parental cells as compared to HA-CREB3L1-expressing cells were found to be involved in key processes including cell survival, cell migration, invasion, angiogenesis and metastasis. These results strongly support the previous reports that CREB3L1 functions as a tumor suppressor by blocking these key pathways when expressed [5–9, 24, 25]. As further support for this model, MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells knocked down to reduce CREB3L1 expression, exhibited significantly more lung metastases in mouse tail vein assays and were also resistant to doxorubicin and paclitaxel chemotherapy treatments [24]. These results suggest that CREB3L1 expression acts as gatekeeper to block the expression of genes promoting cancer progression and metastasis.

Of the 18 genes upregulated in all three CREB3L1-low human breast cancer cell lines, 3 of these were also upregulated in the CREB3L1-low ER-dependent rat breast adenocarcinoma characterized previously, including NDRG1, PCYOX1 and PTGES [5]. High expression of the PCYOX1 and PTGES genes did not correlate with poor overall survival for patients, so these genes were not prioritized for further analysis. High expression of NDRG1 did correlate with poor overall survival for breast cancer patients, however since NDRG1 is thought to function as a metastasis suppressor in breast cancer [26, 27], it was not prioritized for further analysis as a possible target in CREB3L1-low breast cancer. None of the 7 genes downregulated across all three CREB3L1-low parental human breast cancer cell lines were also downregulated in the CREB3L1-low rat breast cancer line [5]. This may be due to different genetic backgrounds in the breast cancer cell lines and/or species-specific gene expression.

We pursued knockdown experiments for two of the genes consistently upregulated in CREB3L1-low breast cancers, LCN2 and CLIC3. LCN2 (lipocalin 2; also known as NGAL, Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin) transports small hydrophobic molecules such as lipids, steroid hormones and retinoids. LCN2 is involved in multiple cellular processes, including maintenance of skin homeostasis, and suppression of invasiveness and metastasis. Overexpression of LCN2 promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cell migration and invasion in prostate [28] and breast cancer cells [29, 30]. In breast cancer, increased LCN2 expression was associated with decreased survival [31] and was also shown to stabilize MMP9, an extracellular matrix remodeling enzyme, during tumor growth and metastasis [32, 33]. Inhibition of LCN2 function with an inhibitory monoclonal antibody has shown promising results to block breast tumorigenesis and metastasis [34]. Consistent with some of these roles for LCN2, knockdown of LCN2 reduced cell migration properties, but did not significantly impact anchorage-independent growth in soft agar. Stronger phenotypes were seen for CLIC3 knockdown breast cancer cells, so these experiments were pursued preferentially.

CLIC3 (chloride intracellular channel 3) was originally thought to be a member of the chloride channel proteins, suggested to regulate cell membrane potential, transepithelial transport, maintenance of intracellular pH, and regulation of cell volume [35, 36]. There is however no strong evidence that CLIC3, or any of the six family members (CLIC1-6) have ion channel activity under physiological conditions [37], but instead appear to have dual functions which depend upon their oxidation state.

Oxidized CLIC-family proteins contain an intramolecular disulfide bond [38, 39] which exposes hydrophobic surfaces to foster the formation of non-covalent dimers. In the presence of membranes, these can form oligomers of 6–8 CLIC subunits, which can form redox and pH-sensitive ion channels [40–44]. Membrane-associated CLIC3 specifically plays an important role in protein trafficking by facilitating the recycling of pro-invasive proteins to help move them from the cell rear to the leading edge [45]. In pancreatic and ovarian cancer cells, CLIC3 promotes invasiveness by working with Rab25 to regulate α5β1 integrin recycling [46]. In breast cancer cells, CLIC3 is involved in recycling matrix metalloproteinases (e.g. MMP14) [45], required for cells to invade basement membranes and metastasize [47]. CLIC3 is overexpressed and associated with more invasive oncogenic phenotypes in a variety of cancers including those of the pancreas [46], ovary [46], bladder [48], salivary gland [49], gallbladder [50], lung [51], and breast [45].

Reduced CLIC proteins are soluble monomers with a glutathione S-transferase (GST) fold and glutathione-dependent oxidoreductase activity, adopting distinct conformations as compared to their oxidized forms [41]. CLIC oxidoreductase enzymatic activity has been suggested to play a role in maintaining an ideal redox environment within cells [41, 52–54]. CLICs 1, 4, 5 and 6 contain a monothiol active site motif (C-X-X-S), whereas CLIC2 and CLIC3 contain an active site dithiol motif (C-X-X-C) [41]. In the oxidized form of CLIC3 a disulfide bond is formed between these two active site Cys residues, blocking its oxidoreductase activity [39]. Thus, only the reduced soluble form of CLIC3 has oxidoreductase enzymatic activity.

A model has been proposed where increased expression of CLIC3 leads to CLIC3 secretion into the extracellular space, where its oxidoreductase activity activates extracellular transglutaminase 2 [53]. Activated transglutaminase 2 stiffens the extracellular matrix and activates α5β1 integrin signaling to induce tumor cell invasion and angiogenesis [55]. These findings suggest a mechanism for CLIC3 to contribute to the invasive properties of cancer cells. The consistent upregulation of CLIC3 expression in CREB3L1-low breast cancers and the beneficial effects of CLIC3 knockdown on breast cancer cell biology suggest that CLIC3 would make an excellent therapeutic target.

The high prevalence of low CREB3L1 expression in several cancer types suggests that its loss could play an important role in tumor progression and metastasis in a number of cancer types. These include: lung squamous cell carcinoma (p = 1.7 × 10− 19), clear cell kidney carcinoma (p = 1.2 × 10− 10), papillary kidney carcinoma (p = 1.2 × 10− 10), bladder urothelial carcinoma (p = 2.8 × 10− 6), colon adenocarcinoma (p = 0.00010), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (p = 0.0029), rectal adenocarcinoma (p = 0.0057), cutaneous melanoma (p = 0.043) and cervical squamous cell carcinoma (p = 0.046) [6]. These other CREB3L1-low cancer types may also benefit from the targeting of CLIC3, a key upregulated gene shown here to contribute to the migratory and metastatic properties of breast cancer cells.

A recent publication identified CLIC3 as a novel prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in TNBC [56]. Using an integrated multi-omics analysis validated with CLIC3 knockdown experiments, they showed that cell migration and invasion were significantly reduced in MDA-MB-231 CLIC3 knockdown cells suggesting that high expression of CLIC3 promotes TNBC progression. Further, using immunohistochemistry they demonstrated that high CLIC3 protein levels was preferentially associated with TNBC tumor tissues as compared to non-TNBC tumor tissues suggesting CLIC3 may be a new biomarker for TNBC patients [56]. These results are entirely consistent with our own and further support the suggestion that CLIC3 is a promising new therapeutic target for TNBC.
