Multidrug-Resistant Organisms: The Silent Plight of Burn Patients
Authors: Randa Elsheikh, Abdelrahman M Makram
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global increasing spread of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) is threatening the control of various infections in vulnerable populations and patient groups. One of the most affected groups is patients with burns, who are prone to hyperinfection as they suffer from a hypermetabolic state and weaken immune barriers. Those patients also share the infection risk of patients hospitalized for a long time, including ventilator-associated pneumonia and urinary tract infections. While some preventative and therapeutic management styles are still controversial, we discuss consensuses here. In this review, we aim to present the current knowledge on multidrug resistance with a special focus on patients with burns, discuss various causative organisms and their treatment options, and highlight the importance of antibiotic stewardship and teamwork in responding to an outbreak of MDROs.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, a significant global decrease in burn admissions and mortality has been witnessed.1 Nevertheless, despite the downward trend, infections continue to represent a major threat to patients with burns, being the leading cause of complications and making up 75% of mortality in patients suffering from thermal injuries.2–4 The increased susceptibility of patients with burns to infection can be attributed to the immune disruption secondary to the loss of the protective cutaneous layer, which acts as the first barrier against infection.2 Other factors like burn-induced bacterial translocation from the gut, immunosuppression caused by the activation of the pro-inflammatory cascade, as well as external factors like immobilization, catheterization, central venous lines, and prolonged hospital stay also contribute to making these patients prone to infection.2,5

The widespread use of over-the-counter antibiotics and the increased clinical administration of antibiotics has contributed to the emergence of antibiotic resistance.6 This was followed by the emergence of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs),7 defined as organisms resistant to three or more classes of antibiotics.8 The alarming death rates attributed to MDROs in patients with burns have prompted the search for alternative therapies, including nonantibiotic antimicrobials,9,10 or the combination of antibiotics with other therapies to obtain a synergistic effect.11

This review aims to review the current knowledge on multidrug resistance in patients with burns, evaluate the risk factors for acquiring MDROs, and understand the resistance patterns of such organisms. It also aims to highlight the importance of employing thorough prevention methods and antibiotic stewardship, as well as focusing on nonantibiotic therapy in tackling such infections.

COMMON INFECTIONS IN BURN PATIENTS

The most common infectious syndromes that occur following a burn are pneumonia, urinary tract infection (UTI), and burn wound infection.12 Starting with respiratory infections, it is mostly nonspecific to patients with burns as most cases suffer from ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). However, some authors believe that inhalation injury can be a risk factor for VAP or exacerbate the condition. This is due to the partial loss of the mucosa surface epithelium of the bronchi as well as the exudation of polymorphonuclear leukocytes in the alveoli.13

Secondly, UTI is the second most common infection encountered in patients with burns. Similarly, this infection is nonspecific to patients with burns as it is mostly associated with longer hospital stays and the use of Foley catheters.4

Lastly, burn wound infections are specific to burn wounds, and they may lead to bacteremia, septicemia, and septic shock.4,12 They are further classified into burn wound impetigo, burn wound colonization, burn wound cellulitis (the most common burn wound infection), and invasive burn wound infection, which is considered a surgical emergency.

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF MULTIRESISTANT BURN INFECTIONS

In recent years, drug-resistant infections, especially bacteria, have emerged as a public and global health crisis, threatening the lives of millions of people each year.7,14 There also exists an expanding gap between countries in terms of the level of antimicrobial resistance, with low- and middle-income countries suffering the most severe consequences.15,16 One of the patient groups that suffer the dire repercussions of MDROs is patients with burns, as they are often exposed to multiple, prolonged, and fruitful preventive and therapeutic antimicrobials.4,17–19 The infection in most patients (>90%) is mono-microbial,20 which may count as an advantage if the diagnosis and management of the case were done correctly and swiftly.

Moreover, an increased incidence of infections due to MDR Gram-negative bacteria (MDR GNB) has been observed.21 An analysis of MDR prevalence in an Indian burn center revealed that 15.2% of the admitted patients had MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR PA).21,22 Similar results were obtained in Brazil and Pakistan, where 23.1% and 19% of isolates showed MDR PA, respectively.23 Contrastingly, an Algerian study revealed a much higher incidence of the pathogen in their burn unit, with 64% of the isolated P. aeruginosa samples being MDR.24 Finally, a study conducted in a children’s hospital in Tanzania showed an incidence of MDR GNB as high as 64%,25 which was similar to the results obtained by an American tertiary-care burn unit (72%),26 highlighting the alarming increase in MDR observed in the last years.

TIMELINE OF BACTERIAL INFECTIONS

Despite burn injuries being initially sterile, they are soon populated with bacterial colonies, which are Gram-positive in nature at first and are later replaced with Gram-negative bacteria before an infection is established.27

The most common isolated pathogens from burn infections are commensal Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogens, which appear within 5 days of burn injury.28 This is followed by the shift to Gram-negative bacteria, predominant in the wound >5 days from burn injury.29 These include P. aeruginosa, which is the most common Gram-negative organism found in patients with burns and increases by 55% after 28 days of hospitalization,4Acinetobacter baumannii, the second most common Gram-negative organism,28 which has recently been displaying increased resistance patterns,21 and Enterobacteriaceae like Escherichia coli and Klebsiella, primarily responsible for UTIs and pneumonia.28 Most MDROs are isolated approximately 38 days following hospitalization; however, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is typically seen 11.5 days after burn injury.30 Generally, susceptibility to MDROs is positively associated with the increased length of hospital stay, with 19% of patients with burns being colonized at admission, 48% two weeks after admission, and 70% on their third week of hospital stay.6

Interestingly, the prevalence of microorganisms found in burn wounds has changed over time. Between 1997 and 2002, the most commonly isolated organism was P. aeruginosa,21 which was then replaced by S. aureus between 2002 and 2005, and coagulase-negative S. aureus between 2006 and 2009.31 Between 2010 and 2014, P. aeruginosa returned to be the predominant organism in burn injuries.21 Nevertheless, it was again replaced by S. aureus, which is currently the most commonly retrieved organism in swabs from patients with burns.28Figure 1 presents the timeline of bacterial infection.4

INFECTION CRITERIA IN BURN PATIENTS

Bacterial colonization of a wound surface is characterized by the presence of <105 colony-forming units. When the amount of bacteria exceeds this threshold, a localized infection is established. For an infection to be defined as invasive, in addition to the increased bacterial colonies, the wound should exhibit necrosis of surrounding tissue and signs of sepsis.32

The 2007 American Burn Association (ABA) consensus33 highlighted that the term “Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome” (SIRS) was obsolete when applied to patients with burns, as the term is used to indicate the occurrence of physiological imbalances, which normally occur in all patients with trauma and is therefore nonspecific. Moreover, SIRS criteria cannot be used to assess the severity of patients with burns and are only specific to adults. Consequently, the ABA developed criteria to diagnose infection in patients with burns (Box 1).

RESISTANCE PATTERNS

Understanding the pattern of antibiotic resistance of the different pathogens is crucial for searching for effective therapeutics and tracking the prevalence of the organism.

Despite carbapenems being the last available antibiotics effective against P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii, these pathogens developed carbapenem-resistance during recent years,21 leaving colistin as a last resort for treatment of several MDROs.34

The antibiotic resistance pattern of bacteria shows variation among different settings. S. aureus isolates exhibit up to 100% resistance to penicillin24 and oxacillin,2 and up to 80% resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, gentamicin, and ciprofloxacin.24 Similarly, P. aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumoniae display up to 100% resistance to cefotaxime, ceftazidime, piperacillin, imipenem, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole amikacin, tazobactam, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, piperacillin-tazobactam, and ticarcillin. Organisms that show a 100% resistance to tested antibiotics are known as extensively drug-resistant pathogens.35 Kabanangi et al., however, reported Pseudomonas resistance to imipenem and meropenem to be 23.1% and 18.1%, respectively. This difference in resistance patterns could be attributed to the higher prevalence of carbapenemase-producing Pseudomonas in certain settings more than others. A resistance ranging from 50% to 100% to most commonly used antibiotics is also seen in Enterobacteriaceae, owing to the increased frequency of extended-spectrum-beta-lactamase in most of the isolated samples.25

Furthermore, when evaluating the drug choice in infection of patients with burns, it is important to keep in mind the intrinsic resistance exhibited by pathogens to specific antibiotics. For instance, Acinetobacter is naturally resistant to ampicillin and glycopeptides, whereas E. coli shows intrinsic resistance to macrolides.36Table 1 summarizes the intrinsic resistance patterns of the most commonly isolated bacteria in patients with burns.

Lastly, an important point to consider when dealing with patients with burns is that resistance patterns of bacteria differ according to the geographic area, the unit the patient is being treated in, and the age group the patient belongs to. For instance, when comparing the antimicrobial resistance patterns of pathogens isolated from patients with burns in Mexico and the United States, a difference is seen in the cultured organisms as well as their resistance rates, with Mexican patients having a higher rate of resistant organisms than their American counterparts. Multidrug resistance Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter is most commonly isolated from Mexican patients, whereas methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis is most commonly seen in patients injured in the United States. Similarly, Mexican patients appear to have a higher overall prevalence of Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms than American patients.37 Understanding the cross-region variability of bacterial resistance patterns is of vital importance as it guides the choice of empiric antibiotic therapy. Similarly, when comparing resistance patterns of patients with burns admitted to the burn intensive care unit (BICU) to those treated in common wards, variability in the implicated pathogens and their resistance is noted. A study conducted in a large Chinese burn center revealed that the most commonly isolated pathogen in patients with burns treated in the BICU was A. baumannii, whereas S. aureus was the most commonly seen organism in ward patients. In addition, organisms isolated from BICU patients displayed higher antimicrobial resistance than those isolated from common wards.38 Finally, while variability in resistance patterns between adults and children might be expected, it is important to note that a discrepancy is also seen among the same population. For instance, in the pediatric population, children <2 years old display higher infection rates with the causative agent more likely to be a resistant pathogen than older children. This, in part, could be attributed to the fact that this age group is more active and more prone to be neglected by family members, thus more likely to develop burn injuries.20

PREDICTORS OF MDR IN BURN PATIENTS

Tackling multidrug resistance (MDR) requires a thorough understanding of the factors that predispose patients with burns to MDROs. Risk factors for burn wound infection in the hospital setting include mechanical ventilation and invasive procedures like central venous line, endotracheal intubation, and urinary catheter insertion, which are associated with the development of antibiotic-resistant colonies of S. aureus, Enterococcus faecalis, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli.5 Similarly, the presence of MDROs is directly proportional to the number of performed burn wound excisions.39,40 Moreover, the length of patient stay in burn centers was associated with colonization with Gram-negative bacteria like P. aeruginosa, whose resistance to meropenem, ciprofloxacin, ceftazidime, and piperacillin-tazobactam increases by 50% 28 days after hospitalization. Contrastingly, Hemophilus influenza decreases significantly one week after hospitalization, suggesting that prolonged hospital stay triggers the replacement of community-acquired bacteria with hospital-acquired ones.41

Furthermore, prolonged antibiotic therapy was found to predispose to imipenem-resistant A. baumannii.39 Nevertheless, antimicrobials were not associated with the increased incidence of some resistant strains like MRSA.40

Finally, some wound-related factors play a role in the colonization of the injury with MDROs, including increased burn total body surface area,28 flame burns,6 and burns involving the head region.40

MANAGEMENT

Common challenges

One of the difficulties when dealing with burn wound infection is distinguishing its different types. For example, differentiating noninfectious burn erythema from burn cellulitis and invasive burn wound infection may only be done by a tissue biopsy17 or more conveniently (but less accurately) by surface swab cultures93,94 or routine infection surveillance of the excised burns.17 Even the gold standard (quantitative tissue biopsy and culture) is believed by some authors to have shortcomings in the detection of all pathogens involved,65,66 emphasizing the need to biopsy wounds with clear physical changes.4 Not only that, but even discriminating a mere colonization of an MDRO from a full-blown picture of an infection can also represent a challenge for initiating the appropriate treatment.4 This issue is further deepened in the case of MDROs. However, a glimpse of hope is given by the advent of rapid diagnostic tests detecting MDROs and their antimicrobial sensitivity patterns, even with higher sensitivity and specificity when compared to standard cultures.95,96

While bronchoscopy can aid in the diagnosis of VAP caused by MDROs in patients with burns,62 especially those suffering from inhalational injuries,82,83 developing acute respiratory distress can complicate the diagnosis4 and increase the risk of complications.97

Another important consideration is the diagnosis of sepsis in patients with burns who already have multiple systemic physiological responses to burns that include increased metabolic rate, higher body temperature, proteolysis, lipolysis, insulin resistance, muscle wasting, and nonspecific inflammation.98–102 Some of these responses interfere directly with the ABA criteria for defining sepsis presented in Box 1, contributing to the difficulty encountered when reaching a definite sepsis diagnosis.103

Systemic antibiotic therapy for MDROs

The first line of management of MDRs is the identification and removal of the probable source of infection and the prompt initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotics,17 of which are later adjusted according to culture results. Logically, the choice of antibiotic therapy will differ according to the isolated organism and the antimicrobial sensitivity. Although colistin was initially a widely used antibacterial agent, it was abandoned through the years due to reports of developing nephrotoxicity following its intravenous administration.104 Nevertheless, the increased incidence of carbapenem-resistant GNB led to reconsidering its use in MDROs. Ganapathy et al. tested the use of colistin against MDR GNB in patients with burns. Of the 29 patients administered colistin, 20 showed a significant improvement, with no detected deterioration of the renal function. Combination therapy

The continuous abuse of antibiotics and the emergence of new resistant strains have prompted the search for methods of potentiating the effect of existing antibiotics through the combination with nonantibiotic therapies.

Liu et al. tested the effectiveness of combining gentamicin with antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT), which uses reactive oxygen species to control localized bacterial infections. Typically, the regrowth of bacteria occurs within a time-frame from antibiotic use. In this study, MDR S. aureus regrew four hours after gentamicin administration and ten hours after exposure of bacteria to aPDT. The combination of both therapies, however, could delay bacterial growth by 15 hours, highlighting the synergistic effect aPDT has on antibiotics.11

Similarly, the combination of nanomaterials with antibacterials has been found to reduce the dose of required antibiotics, increase their effectiveness, and decrease their toxicity, thus demonstrating effectiveness in tackling MDR.109 This synergistic effect can be attributed to the ability of nanomaterials to breach bacterial cell membranes and trigger lysis,110 acts as a carrier for antibiotics and produces a more lasting effect in the body.109

Topical antimicrobial agents

Burn wound eschar represents a favorable environment for bacterial proliferation and impairs skin healing by interfering with endothelial growth. For this reason, early excision and grafting of burn wounds are recommended.111 Nevertheless, when early excision is not possible, the use of prophylactic topical antimicrobial agents guards against the risk of infections.112 The most commonly used topical antimicrobial agents are silver-based agents like silver nitrate and silver sulfadiazine, which are toxic to a wide range of bacteria and fungi, including some of the most commonly isolated bacteria from burn infections like P. aeruginosa and S. aureus. Another commonly used topical agent is mafenide acetate, which is effective against numerous bacteria but not against fungi. It has the advantage of penetrating deep wounds, thus being preferred in full-thickness burns, burns with eschars, and burns over cartilage.113 Nevertheless, the 11% cream has been shown to favor neo-eschar formations and produced pain on application.114 To overcome this, a 2.5% aqueous solution was introduced and showed similar efficacy to the 11% concentration without the associated side effects.115 It is important to note, however, that the above-mentioned agents are cytotoxic to fibroblasts and keratinocytes, hence impairing wound healing. Consequently, clinicians should limit the use of topical antimicrobial agents to deeper wounds that will require surgical closure, facial burns, and when the depth of the burn is uncertain.116

An alternative to silver sulfadiazine and mafenide acetate is honey, which has been shown to promote early wound healing, reduce pain, and prevent wound infections.117 Interestingly, when compared to silver sulfadiazine, honey patients treated with honey exhibited shorter wound healing time and reported less pain.118

Nonantibiotic therapeutics

In addition to combination therapy, alternative therapies to antibiotics are being widely researched. This has witnessed the introduction of several natural options in wound management. These include cinnamon bark oil, which, when combined with a nano-structured lipid carrier, a colloidal drug delivery system, promotes rapid wound healing and skin regeneration and provides a strong antimicrobial effect.119

In addition, developing adequate wound dressings is essential in reducing wound infection. Khalil et al. tested the antimicrobial effect of acetone plant extracts like Glycyrrhiza glabra and Rosmarinus officinalis, which exert their antimicrobial effect through active compounds, including flavonoids and polyphenols. Interestingly, acetone plant extracts outperformed gentamicin in terms of antibacterial effect. Similarly, when tested against nystatin to see its efficacy against MDR fungal pathogens, licorice showed a more potent antifungal effect.120 Other plants used in burn management due to their antiinflammatory and antimicrobial effects are Ximenia coffra and Brachystegia boehmii. The former has antifungal, antigonococcal, and antimicrobial properties, particularly against Bacillus subtilis, Staphylococcus epidermidis, E. coli, and K. pneumoniae. Similarly, B. boehmii, when compared to silver nanoparticles, shows a significant antibacterial effect against S. epidermidis, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus. Nevertheless, no antimicrobial effect is exhibited against K. pneumoniae.121

Interestingly, some of these plants, like Aloe vera, have been known for their healing and antibacterial properties since ancient times, with some evidence suggesting their use by ancient Egyptians.122 Extracts are most commonly obtained from plants’ leaves and seeds, with powder being the most common formulation applied on burns, often in combination with honey and olive oil.123 Plant extracts contain a large amount of phytochemicals, classified into alkaloids, saponins, polyphenols, flavonoids, and other categories depending on their chemical structure. Phytochemicals are principally responsible for the healing and antibacterial properties of the plant. Flavonoids, for instance, modulate fibroblast activity, thus promoting wound healing. Similarly, saponins promote fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis and display antiseptic properties.124

Another viable wound dressing option is hydrogel-based dressings, which, in addition to promoting exudate absorption and wound regeneration, exhibit inherent antimicrobial properties. Particularly, the addition of chemical compounds to hydrogels, like in the epsilon-poly-L-lysin hydrogel, has shown a significant ability to reduce A. baumannii biofilms in burn injuries.125

DISCUSSION

Following the initial 72 h after suffering from a burn, infection becomes the leading cause of complications and mortality in patients with burns.4

Thermal injury begins with breaching the protective skin layer, followed by an attempt of the body to maintain its homeostasis via vasoconstriction and coagulation of blood vessels. The compromise of the external body barrier to infection, together with the initiation of a pro-inflammatory response, leads to a state of immunosuppression, significantly increasing the susceptibility of the patient to infection.17 This is further facilitated by the presence of protein-rich avascular necrotic tissue (eschar) in deep partial and full-thickness burns, which represents a favorable niche for microorganism proliferation.28 Commensal skin bacteria, which are predominantly Gram-positive, colonize the wound within 48 hours of infection and are joined by Gram-negative bacteria and yeasts 5-7 days after injury. The establishment of an invasive infection depends on the extent of the injury as well as the number of colonies.17

This poses a great challenge to the treating team as there can be no apparent distinction at the time of conversion from mere colonization to an infection, especially in critically ill patients and patients with MDROs.4 As highlighted in Table 2, an antimicrobial stewardship program should be promptly and directly involved in abstaining from using unnecessary antibiotics, including adjusting antibiotic doses to patients’ specific needs according to the recommendations of the International Society for Burn Injury, thus leading to the prevention of future individual and institutional infections with MDROs.42,51,53 The hyperdynamic state seen in patients with burns results in the increased renal clearance of administered antibiotics; thus, higher and more frequent antibiotic doses are required in this type of patient.17 Despite procalcitonin not being the best prognostic sepsis marker,28 some studies have highlighted its benefits in discontinuing antibiotics nearly five days earlier,76,77 avoiding the excessive use of antibiotics. Furthermore, burn centers should also focus on obtaining and using new technologies for rapid diagnosis of the causative organism to promptly start the targeted therapy and shorten the duration of antimicrobial intake.126,127

CONCLUSION

The emergence of multidrug-resistant organisms, which are continuously evolving to encompass new antibiotics in their resistance patterns, represents an increasing public health threat, especially against a vulnerable population like patients with burns. Despite the decreasing global trend in burn injuries through the years, significant mortality still occurs due to wound infections. The thorough and timely prevention of MDR infections requires a multidisciplinary approach that sees physicians, surgeons, nurses, clinical pharmacists, anesthetists, and infectious disease specialists at the forefront of the battle against multidrug-resistant organisms. In addition, in case of an MDRO outbreak, a hospital epidemiologist should be involved.

Moreover, it is necessary to redesign burn units to minimize mobilization of patients with burn by allowing intensive care procedures to be carried out within the burn center itself, thus reducing the risk of cross-infection.17 Special attention should also be paid to prevent patient-to-patient infection spread within burn units.

Understanding the histologic and clinical criteria for invasive infection, as well as the resistance pattern for each organism, is crucial for prompt diagnosis and directing therapy. The need to cut the prolonged use of antibiotics, in addition to the increased inefficiency of antibiotics against multiple pathogens, has redirected researchers to search for alternative therapies, opening the door for the use of nanomaterials and natural compounds in burn therapy.

Finally, it is paramount to address the issue of high antibiotic dose demand in patients with burns by tailoring antibiotic therapies to each patient’s needs, establishing hospital antimicrobial stewardship programs, and undergoing routine clinical and laboratory surveillance.17

In short, despite MDROs representing a substantial burden to the quality of life of patients with burns, the constant strive to find alternative solutions and develop early prevention systems highlights the unfolding of a new era in burn-patient care.
