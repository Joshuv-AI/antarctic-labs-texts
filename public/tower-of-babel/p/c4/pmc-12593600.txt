Educational Attainment as the Primary Socioeconomic Determinant of Heart Failure
Authors: Min Ye, Zhenbang Gu, Siyu Guo, He Xu, Yugang Dong, Wengen Zhu, Junyi Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocioeconomic status (SES), intelligence, and cognitive function have been associated with heart failure (HF) risk in observational studies, but their causal relationships remain unclear.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to determine whether SES components, intelligence, and cognitive function have independent causal effects on HF risk and to identify modifiable mediators underlying these associations.MethodsWe conducted 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses using summary statistics from large genome-wide association studies in populations of European ancestry. Genetic instruments for SES (education, income, Townsend Deprivation Index), intelligence, and cognitive function were identified. Multivariable MR was used to assess independent effects. A 2-step MR mediation analysis was conducted to identify and quantify lifestyle and clinical mediators linking educational attainment to HF.ResultsEducational attainment showed a robust inverse association with HF risk (OR per 1 SD [4.2 years] = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.55-0.83), independent of income and deprivation. Intelligence, cognitive function, income, and deprivation were not independently associated with HF after adjusting for education. Among 88 candidate mediators, 12 met criteria for causal mediation, including smoking, sleep duration, body mass index, body fat percentage, triglyceride level, hypertension, metabolic disorders, hypercholesterolemia, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, and atrial fibrillation. Myocardial infarction mediated the largest proportion of the education-HF association (61.4%), followed by body mass index and body fat (43.1% and 45.7%, respectively).ConclusionsEducational attainment is the primary causal component of SES affecting HF risk. These effects are mediated through modifiable lifestyle and cardiovascular factors, highlighting education as a key intervention target for HF prevention.

Heart failure (HF) is a multifaceted, life-threatening clinical syndrome where the heart struggles to pump blood efficiently as a major cause of hospitalization and mortality worldwide.1 It poses a significant public health burden, affecting an estimated 64.3 million individuals globally, particularly in middle- and low-income countries, and incurs an annual global economic cost exceeding $100 billion.2,3 Despite numerous studies and investigations, a comprehensive understanding of the etiology of HF remains elusive, which needs continuous exploration and investigation into the various factors that contribute to HF development and progression.

Socioeconomic status (SES), comprising education, income, and the Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), is a critical determinant of health outcomes.4, 5, 6 Several observational studies have demonstrated that health gradients aligned with SES such as lower educational levels, reduced income, or residence in deprived areas were correlated with an increased risk of developing HF.7,8 Nevertheless, discerning whether these associations are causative is challenging due to the potential confounding factors and reverse causation. In addition to SES, prior studies have indicated that higher intelligence, which is often associated with better education, income, and problem-solving abilities, may have healthier lifestyles, improved health care access, and effective chronic disease management.9,10 Moreover, cognitive function, encompassing abilities such as memory, attention, and language, has been linked to health behaviors and outcomes, including lower HF and cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related mortality.11, 12, 13 Yet, the causal direction and magnitude of the relationships between intelligence, cognitive function, and HF require further investigations.

A crucial question that needs to be addressed is understanding the mediating pathways that connect SES, intelligence, and cognitive function disparities in HF. These pathways play a critical role in the development of prevention and intervention strategies aimed at reducing health disparities in HF rates associated with low SES or cognitive function deficits.14 Epidemiological studies have established a connection between SES and various lifestyle factors or disease outcomes,14, 15, 16 some of which are partially modifiable, potentially influencing the risk of subsequent HF.17 Thus, we hypothesized that the impact of SES and cognitive functions on HF may be mediated by various lifestyle factors or diseases, which need further examination due to the limited data and methodological deficiencies in observational studies.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis offers a robust methodology to investigate causal associations of exposures with adverse outcomes using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs).18 In addition, the multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) approach allows for the examination of both total and direct causal relationships between exposures and outcomes, unraveling the underlying mediating effects in these associations. In this study, we aimed to conduct 2-sample MR analyses to determine the potential cause-effect relationships between SES, intelligence, cognitive function, and HF by analyzing the genetic data, and further identifying lifestyle factors and diseases as mediators in this relationship, providing insights into potential targets for interventions and strategies to reduce the risk of HF.

Methods

Study design

As illustrated in Figure 1, our study conducted a 2-step analysis. In step 1, we employed both univariable Mendelian randomization (UVMR) and MVMR to explore the causal relationships between SES (education, household income, and TDI), intelligence, cognitive function, and HF. Each exposure-outcome analysis was conducted using nonoverlapping genome-wide association study (GWAS) data sets. In step 2, we considered previously reported risk factors for HF as mediators and assessed their mediating effects, as well as the proportion of these effects on the relationship between education and HF. All GWAS data sets used in this study are listed in Table 1. Our manuscript was composed in adherence to the guidelines of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization statements.19 None of the data sources simultaneously examined HF along with SES, intelligence, or cognitive function. Data sources

Data pertinent to SES, intelligence, cognitive function, and HF were meticulously sourced from 4 distinct repositories (Table 1). A comprehensive breakdown of these data sets, complete with their origins, specific GWAS consortia, and sample sizes are detailed in Supplemental Table 1. Any ethical permissions for the original GWASs can be found in the publications that have been cited in this article.

Exposures

SES was characterized using genetic markers known to correlate with socioeconomic indicators. These markers were selected based on their association with elements such as the number of educational years completed, household income tiers, and scores on the TDI.

The GWAS data set for education was sourced from the SSGAC (Social Science Genetic Association Consortium) study, which conducted a longitudinal assessment of cohort participants aged 30 years or older (N = 766,345).21 Education level was quantified by the duration of schooling, harmonized across various studies in alignment with the International Standard Classification of Education categories.21 The sample-size-weighted average for years of education was 16.8 years, with a SD of 4.2 years.21 Genetic instruments associated with self-reported household income were obtained from the UK Biobank (N = 397,751), which reflects the average pretax household earnings between 2006 and 2010.5 Furthermore, the UK Biobank provided data for a GWAS on TDI, which includes factors such as unemployment, absence of car or home ownership, and household overcrowding (N = 462,464).5

Intelligence-related genetic variants were gleaned from a GWAS meta-analysis of 14 independent epidemiological cohorts associated with intelligence in European ancestry (N = 269,867),22 while the genetic variants were extracted from the GWAS meta-analysis of the Cognitive Genomics Consortium and UK Biobank conducted by Lee et al (N = 257,841).23

Outcomes

The HF outcome was derived from the HERMES (HEart failure Molecular Epidemiology for Therapeutic targetS) consortium GWAS meta-analysis, which included 47,309 cases and 930,014 controls of European ancestry across 26 cohorts. Diagnosis of HF was based on standard International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/ICD-10) diagnostic codes, determined through structured interviews, clinical records, or administrative data.20 These data sets were evaluated for their rigor and comprehensiveness, ensuring a reliable pool of genetic instruments to work with.

Potential mediators

Potential mediators were carefully selected based on previous observational studies and randomized controlled trials, potentially contributing to the pathway from education to HF.24 A total of 48 lifestyle factors and 40 diseases previously highlighted in previous studies were considered as potential mediators (Table 1).

For a factor to qualify as a mediator in the causal pathway from education to HF, it had to meet 3 criteria: 1) education should have a causal relationship with the mediator, not the other way around; 2) the mediator should have a causal and independent association with the HF outcome; 3) the relationship between education and the mediator, as well as the mediator's association with the HF outcome, should be in opposite directions.14,25

Genetic instruments

We employed linkage disequilibrium clumping, setting a threshold of r2 ≤ 0.001 within 10,000 kb windows for variants within the same locus, to isolate independent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to the exposure. Only SNPs reaching genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) were retained.26 For any SNP unavailable in the outcome data set, we substituted suitable proxies (with a minimum linkage disequilibrium of r2 = 0.8) when available.27 Finally, a total of 232, 36, 16, 132, and 101 SNPs were retained for education, income, TDI, intelligence, and cognitive function, respectively.

Statistical analysis

For the causal estimates derived from MR studies to hold validity, 3 core assumptions need to be satisfied: 1) relevance assumption: the genetic variant has an association with the risk factor; 2) independence assumption: the genetic variant is not correlated with any confounders; 3) exclusion restriction assumption: the genetic variant affects the outcome solely via its relationship with the risk factor.28 The analysis was conducted in a structured manner to ensure the validity and robustness of the findings.

UVMR and MVMR analysis

As our primary UVMR analysis, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method provided an average causal effect estimate of the exposures on HF using the ratio of coefficients.29 To assess the variability among individual genetic variant effect estimates, Cochran's Q statistic was calculated, since significant heterogeneity (P < 0.05) might indicate potential pleiotropy.30

The chosen exposures in our study, whether within the 3 pillars of SES (education, income, and TDI) or among SES, intelligence, and cognitive function, exhibit interconnected relationships. Given the existence of shared SNPs across multiple exposures, it was imperative to conduct an MVMR analysis, which could show the direct effect of each component on HF.31

Two-step mediation MR analysis

A two-step MR approach was applied to deconstruct the potential mediatory role of each risk factor in the association between education and HF.32 The first step involved correlating the IVs of education with potential mediators by UVMR (β1), while the following step assessed the relationship between each mediator and HF by MVMR (β2). The total effect of education on HF was derived from the initial UVMR analysis, while the indirect effect was calculated as β1 × β2. The proportion mediated was calculated as indirect effect/total effect. For the derivation of SES, we utilized the Delta method drawing upon effect estimates from our two-sample MR analyses.

Sensitivity analysis

An array of sensitivity analyses was undertaken to bolster confidence in the UVMR findings: 1) Weighted median method33; 2) MR Egger regression34; 3) Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier test (MR-PRESSO)35; 4) Contamination mixture method36; 5) MR-Mix37; 6) Bayesian-weighted Mendelian randomization38; and 7) Leave-one-out analysis.39

Only European ancestry was included in our analysis. All the statistical analyses conducted in this research were using TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.7), RadialMR (version 0.4), MR-PRESSO (version 1.0), and MendelianRandomization (version 0.9.0) packages of R language (version 4.2.2). Statistical significance was identified as P value <0.05. In MVMR, an adjusted P value after the Bonferroni correction was considered statistically significant. If the IVW estimates matched the direction and statistical significance of at least one sensitivity analysis and displayed no signs of pleiotropy (P for Egger intercept >0.05), they were interpreted as causal associations. Detailed methodological information is available in the Supplemental Appendix.

Results

The sensitivity analyses, encompassing the weighted median, MR-PRESSO, contamination mixture methods, and leave-one-out analysis, yielded consistent results as presented in Table 2 and Supplemental Figures 1 to 5. MR-Egger analysis revealed no apparent signs of horizontal pleiotropy, with all associated P values exceeding 0.05. The MR-Mix assessment identified education as the sole independent causal factor affecting HF, distinguishing it from income, TDI, intelligence, and cognitive function. Nevertheless, all 5 exposures were deemed statistically significant contributors to HF upon evaluation via Bayesian-weighted Mendelian randomization.

MVMR analysis of SES, intelligence, and cognition on HF

Given the 3 distinct components within SES, we initiated an MVMR to analyze the direct impact of each individual component. As depicted in Figure 2, the causal linkage between an additional 4.2 years of education and a reduced risk of HF persisted even after adjusting for income (OR: 0.67; 95% CI: 0.54-0.83), TDI (OR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.65-0.82), or both combined (OR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.55-0.83). Notably, the causal correlations of household income and TDI with HF became statistically insignificant after adjusting for education. The integrity of all estimates from the MV-IVW was further affirmed by the MVMR Egger method (Supplemental Table 2). We subsequently examined the direct effects of education (representing SES), intelligence, and cognitive function on HF. Figure 2 illustrates that education consistently exhibits an inverse association with HF even after adjusting for intelligence (OR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.62-0.86), cognitive function (OR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.65-0.93), or both (OR: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.63-0.90), as determined by MV-IVW. Effects of education on potential mediators

Out of 88 candidate lifestyle factors and diseases, 12 met all 3 mediator criteria (Figure 3). The UVMR analysis demonstrated that each 4.2-year increment of education was linked with various health-promoting lifestyles and reduced disease risks. Specifically, evaluated by the IVW method, education was correlated with fewer daily cigarettes (β −0.35; 95% CI: −0.43 to −0.28), extended sleep duration (β 0.05; 95% CI: 0.02-0.07), and lower body mass index (BMI) (β −0.27; 95% CI: −0.33 to −0.21). Furthermore, there was a decline in BF% (β −0.26; 95% CI: −0.30 to −0.23) and serum triglyceride (TG) level (β −0.22; 95% CI: −0.26 to −0.18). In addition, there was a decreased risk of diseases including hypertension (OR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.53-0.67), hypercholesterolemia (OR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.58-0.81), metabolic disorders (OR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.61-0.76), coronary artery disease (CAD) (OR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.60-0.73), myocardial infarction (MI) (OR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.49-0.60), AF (OR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.77-0.93), and stroke (OR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.59-0.70) (Figure 4, Supplemental Table 4). The genetic IVs for education consistently exhibited heterogeneity and displayed no pleiotropy with those of the mediators (Supplemental Table 5). Effects of potential mediators on HF

In the UVMR analysis, all 12 selected factors were significantly associated with the onset of HF (Supplemental Table 8). Discussion

Our present MR analyses demonstrated a clear causal relationship between genetically predicted educational attainment and HF risk and identified 12 modifiable risk factors and CVDs as significant mediators in the relationship between educational attainment and HF, as illustrated in the Central Illustration. Overall, these findings emphasized the need for targeted interventions that address education inequality by focusing on lifestyle modifications and improving access to quality health care for CVDs. Such interventions may have the potential to reduce the disparities in HF rates, ultimately leading to better health outcomes for all individuals, irrespective of their educational background.Central IllustrationEducational Attainment as the Primary Socioeconomic Determinant of Heart FailureEducation emerged as the primary factor causally linked to heart failure risk. Despite marked reductions in CVD-related morbidity and mortality, socioeconomic disparities persist in high-income countries.40 Prior studies have established that individuals in poor income categories suffered significantly higher risk of MI, stroke, hypertension, and HF compared to their affluent counterparts.41, 42, 43 Particularly, extensive observational studies have linked SES with incidence,43, 44, 45 health status,46 prognosis,47, 48, 49, 50, 51 and mortality52, 53, 54 of HF. Stewart et al included 15,402 individuals aged 45 to 64 years and revealed that the hospitalization rates for HF were significantly increased in the most deprived group compared to the most affluent group.43 In our present MR study, among the SES components, education was the most influential in reducing HF incidence. Although existing research emphasizes the impact of SES, especially income at the individual or country level, and cognitive function on HF risk.7,12 We found that income and TDI had no causal impact on HF risk after adjusting for educational levels, highlighting the overriding importance of educational attainment in this regard. Furthermore, we observed that an additional 4.2 years of schooling was causally linked to a 24% reduction in HF incidence independent of intelligence and cognitive function. Thus, the effects of SES, intelligence, and cognitive function on HF were predominantly influenced by educational attainment. Enhanced education not only equips individuals with the skills to make healthier lifestyle choices, comprehend medical advice, and manage cardiovascular risk factors effectively55 but also grants access to broader social resources. These include greater financial means to afford medical care and residence in areas with superior health care facilities,56 all potentially contributing to the reduced HF risk.

While socioeconomic deprivation is a well-established independent predictor of HF development and poor outcomes, the specific mechanisms of their causal relationship remain largely unexplored. A recent study from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, involving 55,081 adults aged 20 years and above, revealed that individuals with lower education levels exhibited poorer cardiometabolic health as well as poor levels of adiposity, blood pressure, glucose, lipids, and clinical CVDs, compared to those with higher education levels.57 A large community-based study encompassing 40,998 participants from 6 prospective cohorts highlighted a correlation between lower education and increased lifetime CVD risk, including CAD, HF, stroke, and CVD-related death.58 In addition, previous MR studies have uncovered causal links between educational attainment and factors such as cigarette smoking, BMI, and sedentary behavior, independent of intelligence.59,60 Based on these, our current MR study further provided additional insights into the causal relationships between educational level and various HF risk factors, encompassing a broad spectrum of lifestyle factors and disorders such as daily cigarette consumption, sleep duration, BMI, BF%, TG, hypertension, metabolic disorders, hypercholesterolemia, CAD, MI, stroke, and AF. Overall, daily cigarette consumption, BMI, BF%, hypertension, metabolic disorders, CAD, MI, stroke, and AF each mediated a substantial portion of education's total effect on HF risk, contributing to over 10%.

Among them, modifiable lifestyle factors such as daily cigarette consumption as mediating 11.31% of the education-HF link. Smoking has been recognized as a critical factor in HF in both observational and MR studies.61,62 Our findings consistently emphasized the need for smoking cessation and prevention initiatives, particularly targeting populations with lower educational levels. In addition, our analysis suggested that the impact of sleep duration accounts for 7.48% of the relationship between education and HF, consistent with prior research that highlighted the role of a healthy sleep pattern in reducing HF risk.63, 64, 65 Therefore, ensuring sufficient sleep is a crucial factor in the prevention of HF. This finding underscores the need for public health strategies that promote healthy sleep patterns, especially in educational contexts, as a means to reduce the overall burden of HF. With regard to the impact of body measurements on the relationship between education and HF, BMI and BF% emerged as significant modifiable mediators, accounting for 43.10% and 45.72% of the effect, respectively. These findings support the importance of maintaining a healthy weight and body composition in managing HF risk in public health guidelines.1,66 The link between education and these health metrics may be attributed to factors such as dietary habits, physical activity, and awareness of the health risks associated with excessive weight and body fat.

Several disorders or diseases that are unmodifiable also significantly mediate the causal relationship between education and HF. Previous MR studies have established a causal link between higher educational levels and reduced risk of CVDs, such as hypertension, CAD, AF, and stroke, all of which are well-recognized risk factors for HF.14,67, 68, 69, 70 This connection underpins our findings that these diseases significantly mediate the education-HF relationship. Notably, MI emerged as the most prominent mediator in our study, accounting for a substantial 61.40% of the educational impact on HF. This suggests that education may mitigate HF risk by reducing the occurrence or severity of MI events, possibly through enhanced health literacy, greater engagement in preventive health behaviors, or improved access to quality health care. Supporting this, prior observational studies have highlighted the profound impact of socioeconomic inequality on both the management and treatment of MI.71, 72, 73 CAD, hypertension, and stroke were also identified as notable mediators in our study, each accounting for over 20% of the effect of education on HF. This finding underscores the critical importance of coronary health and blood pressure control in HF prevention. Understanding these mediating effects is essential for both comprehending the underlying mechanisms and identifying effective intervention strategies.

Overall, our study substantiates the causal link between SES and HF, identifying education level as the predominant factor relative to other SES dimensions. While prior research on the SES-HF relationship primarily relied on correlative data from traditional observational studies (cross-sectional or longitudinal), our study's strength lies in its causal approach. This methodology effectively addresses issues of unmeasured confounding or reverse causation, offering pivotal insight that prioritizing education over wealth accumulation and intelligence is key to reduce HF incidence. Our findings suggest that policy interventions focused on enhancing educational opportunities could positively impact SES, subsequently lowering HF rates. In addition, our research illuminates crucial mediators in the education-HF link, providing a foundation for developing targeted prevention strategies to mitigate HF disparities caused by socioeconomic inequality.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although we selected SNPs strongly associated with SES, intelligence, and cognitive function, these genetic variants only explained a small portion of the total variance and cannot be considered precise proxies for these factors. In addition, the unknown biological mechanisms of these genetic instruments mean we cannot entirely exclude potential violations of the independence and exclusion restriction assumptions, especially concerning pleiotropy.74 However, we employed multiple methods to derive robust causal estimates. Second, our use of various genetic variants to assess the causal impact of SES, intelligence, and cognitive function on HF may not completely address biases from social or environmental factors, like assortative mating, genetic nurturing, and population stratification. Future within-family GWASs could help overcome these limitations.75 Third, the SNPs associated with education levels were mainly derived from the SSGAC study, focusing on individuals over 30 years of age. In contrast, the HF GWAS encompasses a more diverse age range. Education levels in younger demographics might be shaped by different variants with distinct associations with HF. However, GWAS data for this context are not currently available. Fourth, while we used education duration as a proxy for education level in this study, we acknowledge that overall educational attainment depends on both duration and quality. Future GWASs incorporating more detailed measures of education may help address this limitation. Fifth, the GWASs used in our study predominantly involved high-income European populations, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities and lower-income regions. Sixth, as the analysis was based on summary-level data, follow-up duration at the individual level is not available. Finally, while MR is a valuable tool for inferring effects, it reflects lifelong genetic exposures, contrasting with the transient nature of interventions. Thus, randomized controlled trials promoting education levels are necessary to conclusively establish causal relationships.

Conclusions

This MR study establishes education as a key independent SES factor in reducing HF risk, highlighting the potential benefits of educational enhancement in lessening HF incidence. Additionally, our findings indicate that lifestyle factors and certain health conditions mediate the relationship between educational attainment and HF risk.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: This study demonstrates that educational attainment has a direct, causal protective effect against HF, independent of income, deprivation, intelligence, and cognitive function.
