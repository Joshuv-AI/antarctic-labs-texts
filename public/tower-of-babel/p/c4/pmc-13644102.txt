Liver findings in newly diagnosed, treatment-naive patients with Crohn’s disease: a preliminary study of MRI proton density fat fraction and extracellular volume fraction
Authors: Luanxin Zhu, Zhiheng Zhang, Yuping Ma, Bota Cui, Faming Zhang, Melat Adugna Asnake, Jinfa Zhou, Jianguo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the applicability of MRI proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) and extracellular volume fraction (ECV) in detecting liver injury in patients with intestinal Crohn’s disease (CD). Additionally, it evaluated the relationships among multi-parametric imaging indicators, clinical scores, and serological markers. MethodsA total of 107 patients with CD were enrolled in this retrospective study conducted from December 2021 to October 2024. All subjects underwent MRI scans with Iterative decomposition of water and fat with echo asymmetry and least-squares estimation quantitation (IDEAL-IQ) sequence to generate the MRI-PDFF values. Moreover, pre- and post-contrast liver T1 mapping was performed at the level of the liver hilum. The ECV value was calculated by inputting the patient's hematocrit level (HCT). Based on the Harvey-Bradshaw index (HBI), the clinical score was assessed along with serological markers, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), fecal calprotectin (FC), and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the application value of ECV and MRI-PDFF value compared with ALP, ALT, AST, ESR, FC, and GGT. The correlation of the ECV and MRI-PDFF values with clinical score and serological markers was assessed using correlation coefficients.ResultsIn examining different levels of intestinal inflammation activity, significant statistical differences were observed in MRI-PDFF and ECV values (p < 0.001). ROC curve analysis indicated that MRI-PDFF and ECV exhibited superior discriminatory abilities, with AUCs of 0.773 and 0.843, respectively (ALP, ALT, AST, GGT, ESR, and FC of 0.512, 0.566, 0.530, 0.551, 0.693, 0.716). Both MRI-PDFF values (r = 0.520, r = 0.246, r = 0.339, all p < 0.001) and ECV (r = 0.534, r = 0.435, r = 0.433, all p < 0.001) were positively correlated with HBI, ESR, and FC values.ConclusionThis study suggests that chronic systemic inflammation may lead to liver injury in patients with CD. Liver MRI can help assess and monitor liver involvement that may not be visually detectable in these patients. Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic condition characterized by recurring granulomatous inflammation of the digestive tract, with its exact cause still unknown [1]. It is one of the most common forms of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [2]. The gastrointestinal tract and liver are physiologically interconnected through the portal circulation, which allows nutrients, toxins, and products from gut microbiota absorbed in the intestines to be metabolized by the liver. When pathological conditions arise, gut microbes and inflammatory factors can negatively impact liver function and structure through blood and lymphatic circulation, leading to liver injuries. This can result in several liver diseases, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease(MASLD) and acute liver failure [3]. Pathological findings indicate that liver injury is accompanied by hepatocyte edema and hepatic steatosis [4]. Accumulating studies have shown that these conditions, which manifest as MASLD, are common hepatic complications in patients with CD, and MASLD-related liver failure can exacerbate intestinal inflammation [5, 6]. The bidirectional relationship between the gastrointestinal tract and the liver, known as the gut-liver axis, plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of CD [7].

Evaluating liver function and managing liver-related issues can pose significant challenges for clinicians. While liver biopsy is considered the gold standard for assessment, its use is limited in clinical settings due to its invasive nature. Common serological markers used to evaluate liver function, such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), can be affected by factors such as age, sex, and various physiological and pathological conditions. This variability can lead to inadequate sensitivity or specificity in the results. Additionally, traditional imaging techniques often fail to detect liver involvement that may not be visually apparent [8].

Magnetic resonance imaging with proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) is a technique for separating water and fat signals based on chemical shifts, enabling the detection and quantification of liver fat and water content [9, 10]. However, its application in evaluating MASLD in CD patients remains underexplored.

T1 mapping techniques can provide T1 relaxation times before and after administration of extracellular gadolinium-based contrast agents, allowing estimation of tissue extracellular volume (ECV) [11]. Compared to other parameters of T1 mapping technology, ECV values exhibit high repeatability and consistency across different scanning hardware because they are independent of magnetic field strength and sequence selection [12]. ECV technology has demonstrated good diagnostic performance in assessing myocardial edema, fibrosis, and renal fibrosis [13–15].In studies related to liver disease, ECV has been employed for evaluating liver fibrosis [16]. However, to our knowledge, its application in assessing hepatocyte edema in CD patients has not been reported.

In this study, we evaluated the use of MRI-PDFF and ECV quantification in patients with CD. We aimed to explore the potential of MRI-PDFF and ECV as non-invasive biomarkers for early and accurate evaluation of liver involvement in CD. The objectives of this study were (1) to assess the reproducibility of MRI-PDFF and ECV measurements. (2) to compare MRI-PDFF and ECV values between CD patients with mild and moderate to severe inflammatory activity, and (3) to investigate the feasibility of using MRI-PDFF and ECV in CD patients with liver injury.

Patients and methods

The current study was approved by the institutional review board (approval number: 2021XJ-009-01), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived, given its retrospective design.

By searching our institutional electronic medical records from December 2021 to October 2024, Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) first-visit inpatients without any treatment history in our hospital, (2) adults (age ≥ 18 years), (3) CD diagnosis confirmed by colonoscopy performed after MRI scanning, and (4) patients with completed liver MRI data (including pre-and post-contrast T1 mapping and IDEAL-IQ) with linked clinical outcomes. Exclusion criteria included: (1) poor-quality liver MRI images, (2) a history of viral hepatitis or hepatitis virus carrier status, or other liver diseases, and (3) a diagnosis of alcoholism.

Liver MRI technique

All liver MRI examinations were performed using a 3.0T MRI scanner (SIGNA™ Premier, GE Healthcare) equipped with an adaptive radiofrequency coil for image reception. Patients were required to fast for 6 h before the MRI examination. Besides T1 and T2 weighted morphological sequences, the IDEAL-IQ sequence to calculate the PDFF and a T1 mapping pre- and 10 min post-contrast to calculate ECV were used. For IDEAL-IQ, a three-dimensional multi-echo Dixon method with six equally spaced echoes was employed in the axial plane. For T1 mapping, a modified Look-Locker inversion recovery (MOLLI) acquisition scheme was used in the axial plane. Three liver slices were acquired for the T1 mapping with the center at the level of the liver hilum to cover as much liver parenchyma as possible, as previously described [17]. Gadolinium diamine gluconate was used as the contrast agent, administered at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg and a rate of 1.5 ml/s, followed by a 20 ml saline flush at the same flow rate. The Hematocrit (HCT) of each patient, as well as clinical score (HBI) and serological markers, was collected on the same day as liver MRI.

MRI data analysis and processing

The T1 and MRI-PDFF values were measured by two radiologists with over 10 years of experience in abdominal imaging diagnosis, who were blinded to the patient’s clinical data, using the Advantage Workstation (AW4.7, GE Healthcare), the standard post-processing workstation for our MRI system. During delineating regions of interest (ROIs), T2-weighted images were selected to assist with positioning, while care was taken to avoid the marginal region, major blood vessels, bile ducts, focal lesions, and artifacts. For the measurement of the T1 value, three circular ROIs with an area of 1 cm2 were placed in the right and left lobes at the level of the portal vein bifurcation, and the mean value of the three ROIs was taken as the final value for the calculation of ECV. T1 values of the blood pool were obtained from the abdominal aorta on the same slice. The ECV was calculated using ROI-based T1 values with the following equation [18]: ECV = (1-HCT) × (1/T1liver-post − 1/T1liver-pre) / (1/T1blood-post − 1/T1blood-pre). For MRI-PDFF value measurement, the two radiologists copied ROIs to the software-generated fat fraction map and recorded MRI-PDFF values (Fig. 1). The average ECV and MRI-PDFF values measured by the two radiologists were used for the final statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (RStudio PBC, Boston, MA, USA). A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of this study, no formal correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni) was applied; therefore, all reported p-values should be interpreted as uncorrected, and the results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Categorical data were expressed as numbers and percentages. Continuous data were checked using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and expressed as the mean and standard deviation if normally distributed, or as the median and range if abnormally distributed.

According to the HBI scores [19], the 107 CD Patients were classified into a mild inflammatory activity group (HBI < 8) and a moderate to severe inflammatory activity group (HBI ≥ 8). Between-group comparison of age was conducted using an independent-samples t-test, and sex was compared using a Chi-square test.

The inter-observer reproducibility of ECV and MRI-PDFF measurement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and Bland-Altman plots showing the mean difference with 95% limits of agreement (LoAs).

Comparisons of the liver MR imaging quantitative parameters (ECV and MRI-PDFF) and serological markers (ALP, ALT, AST, ESR, FC, and GGT) between the mild and moderate to severe groups were performed using the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to calculate and compare the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of both liver MRI and serological parameters in both groups. If quantitative parameters were normally distributed, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was calculated. If the normality assumption was not met, Spearman’s correlation was used.

Results

187 patients suspected of CD were considered eligible for inclusion. Out of 187 patients, 80 patients were excluded because of non-liver MRI examination (n = 57), history of viral hepatitis or were hepatitis virus carriers (n = 3), incomplete inflammatory activity indicators [e.g., Harvey-Bradshaw index (HBI), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and fecal calprotectin (FC)] (n = 10), poor-quality liver MRI images (n = 8) and alcoholism (n = 2). In total, 107 patients were ultimately enrolled and analyzed (Fig. 2). Of the 60 CD patients in the mild group, 60.0% were males, with a mean age of 33.45 ± 13.40 years. Meanwhile, of the 47 CD patients in the moderate to severe group, 51.2% were males, with a mean age of 37.04 ± 17.31 years. (Table 2).

Discussion

Our hospital is one of the referral centers for CD in China. Some patients with newly-onset or refractory CD intend to seek microbiota transplantation therapy at the division of microbiota medicine. We routinely evaluate intestinal lesions and extraintestinal comorbidities of CD. To minimize patients’ radiation exposure, we prefer MRI over CT scans [20]. In the current study, we primarily investigated the usefulness of ECV and MRI-PDFF for detecting liver injury in patients with CD. Our main findings were: (1) ECV and MRI-PDFF demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity in differentiating the CD activity status compared to traditional liver function biomarkers (e.g., ALP, ALT, AST, and GGT). (2) Significant differences in liver ECV and MRI-PDFF values were observed between mild and moderate to severe CD groups, with robust correlations with clinical indicators, including HBI, ESR, and FC.

Although the present study lacked a healthy control group, the obtained MRI-PDFF and ECV data were compared with established normal reference values. In healthy populations, liver MRI-PDFF values are typically low, with an optimal cutoff of 5.7% for detecting hepatic steatosis in a recent meta-analysis [21], and a threshold above 5% defines hepatic steatosis. The median MRI-PDFF in our mild CD group already exceeded this threshold (6.83%), with a markedly higher level in the moderate to severe group (17.84%). Healthy liver ECV typically ranges from 25% to 30% [16], which is substantially lower than the ECV values of 38%–41% observed in our CD patients. These findings indicate that mild liver fat accumulation and extracellular matrix expansion occur even in patients with mild CD, and these hepatic abnormalities are further aggravated in moderate to severe CD.

MASLD is a multi-system disease characterized by liver steatosis, inflammation, injury, and fibrosis, which affects nearly one-quarter of the global adult population [22]. In the CD population, the prevalence of MASLD is higher compared with the overall prevalence [23]. The pathogenesis and relationship between CD and MASLD are more complex, with multiple factors potentially contributing to MASLD development, such as chronic inflammation, changes in intestinal permeability, use of hepatotoxic drugs, and dysbiosis of gut microbiota [24]. In a prospective study of 311 patients with CD, MRI-PDFF was used as a reliable tool to quantify liver steatosis severity [25]. This study demonstrated a positive association between increasing MRI-PDFF values and peak liver enzymes (ALT). In a prior study conducted by Hong Q et al. [26], MASLD in CD patients was defined using an MR-PDFF threshold of > 5.5%, and elevated serum liver function biomarkers were found to independently predict MASLD development. A similar method was used by McHenry S et al., who developed and assessed a clinical prediction tool for MASLD, where MASLD was diagnosed and determined by MRI-PDFF [27]. Unlike the above studies, we found that the liver MRI-PDFF values were significantly higher in the moderate to severe group compared to the mild inflammatory activity group. We also found that liver MRI-PDFF positively correlated with serum inflammatory markers (e.g., ESR, FC). These findings were the first to confirm the role of liver MRI-PDFF in evaluating CD activity. Notably, our findings appear to differ from those of McHenry et al. [28], who reported a higher prevalence of MASLD in patients with quiescent CD. This discrepancy may reflect differences in study populations. The patients in McHenry’s cohort had established CD with various treatment histories, including prolonged exposure to corticosteroids and immunomodulators, which are known risk factors for hepatic steatosis. In contrast, our cohort comprised exclusively newly diagnosed, treatment-naive patients, thereby minimizing the confounding effects of pharmacotherapy on liver fat accumulation. In our treatment-naive population, the severity of intestinal inflammation appears to directly correlate with the degree of liver involvement, as reflected by progressively elevated PDFF and ECV values across CD activity groups.

According to the pharmacokinetic studies, in the delayed MRI enhancement phase, most contrast agents were accumulated in the extracellular space, which led to the shortening of the T1 time of the tissues [29]. As a biomarker of the extracellular space, ECV can be calculated by using T1 relaxation times obtained before and after MR contrast enhancement [30]. Several published studies employed the T1 mapping-derived ECV technique to assess liver cirrhosis [31–34]. Based on the promising results, these studies identified that ECV had strong potential when measured in the liver. However, to the best of our knowledge, none of the previous studies focused on CD patients or the applicability of MRI-derived ECV to differentiate between different CD activity levels. Our research first highlighted the potential of ECV calculation for assessing liver injury in CD patients. The differences in ECV between mild and moderate to severe CD groups, and their correlations with the serum inflammatory markers, suggested that inflammation-induced hepatocyte edema was associated with an enlargement of extracellular space and, as a consequence, with increased ECV values [33, 35]. We hypothesize that an increased accumulation of contrast agents in the expanded extracellular space (increased ECV values) is mediated by intestinal inflammatory factors that increase cell permeability and alter the intracellular-extracellular microenvironment.

Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated an ECV measurement agreement of ± 7.5%, reflecting inter-observer variability related to manual ROI placement near the liver hilum. Nevertheless, the between-group ECV difference (38.79% vs. 41.24%, + 2.5%) was significant (p < 0.001), confirming that the biological signal exceeded measurement noise at the group level. With a high AUROC of 0.843, ECV showed favorable diagnostic performance. However, due to the small inter-group difference relative to measurement variability, individual ECV values should be interpreted cautiously. ECV provides the most value when incorporated into a multiparametric assessment with MRI-PDFF and clinical indicators. Future studies using automated segmentation and motion-robust sequences may further enhance measurement precision.

However, some limitations should be noted. First, this study was limited by its single-center design and small sample size. Second, the mismatch in case numbers between the two groups may lead to biased statistical estimates. Third, no liver biopsies were performed on the CD patients in this study. Fourth, the cohort only included newly diagnosed treatment-naive patients. Thus, the findings cannot be generalized to patients with established disease or ongoing medication, as treatment may independently affect hepatic fat and extracellular matrix alterations. Fifth, multiple statistical comparisons were not adjusted for multiplicity, which may increase type I error. The observed associations should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation in targeted studies. A multivariate predictive model integrating quantitative MRI parameters and inflammatory biomarkers may further enhance the precision of assessing liver involvement and stratifying CD activity. Future studies could improve the generalizability of the findings by enrolling a larger patient population across multiple centers, conducting longitudinal assessments, and incorporating liver biopsy as the reference standard.

In conclusion, our preliminary study demonstrates the clinical potential of ECV and MRI-PDFF in detecting and assessing liver injury in CD patients. These two techniques might provide additional diagnostic information over laboratory liver function markers. By providing novel insights into the early detection and management of liver injury in CD patients, these findings may help guide clinical assessment and inform future studies.
