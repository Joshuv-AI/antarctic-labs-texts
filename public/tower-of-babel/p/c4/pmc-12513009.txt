Selenium supplementation for management of gestational diabetes mellitus in pregnancy: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Juan Sun, Lei Liu, Juxiang Shen, Juan Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelenium is thought to improve glucose and lipid metabolism in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus. However, this finding is somewhat controversial. In this paper, we evaluated the effects of selenium supplementation on glucose and lipid metabolism in patients with GDM.MethodsSearches were carried out in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, EMBASE, CBM, Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wan Fang, and VIP from their inception until May 2025. Two reviewers independently extracted data. The Cochrane risk of bias tool was applied to assess the methodological quality of every study and Meta-analysis was carried out with a random effects model or a fixed effects model. Publication bias was evaluated by the Begg and Egger tests.ResultsFour randomized controlled trials, with 230 participants in total, were included. Participants were between 18 and 45 years old. The time span of these studies ranged from 2015 to 2022. The daily dose of selenium supplementation was from 100 µg/d to 200 µg/d and the duration of intervention was 6 weeks to 12 weeks. Compared with placebo group, the selenium group did not significantly reduce the level of homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance(P = 0.20, MD = -0.71, 95%CI: -1.80, 0.37). Selenium supplementation at 200 µg/d significantly reduced fasting plasma glucose(P = 0.0002, MD = -5.03, 95% CI: -7.70, -2.37) and the incidence of newborn’s hyperbilirubinemia((P = 0.0003, MD = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.03, 0.33). However, there was no obvious differences in improving total cholesterol, triglycerides, low density lipoprotein cholesterol and high density lipoprotein cholesterol.ConclusionsFor pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus, daily supplementation with 200 µg of selenium may help lower fasting blood glucose levels and the risk of hyperbilirubinemia in the newborn, but it does not significantly affect total cholesterol, triglycerides, low density lipoprotein cholesterol, or high density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. Given the limitations of this study, these conclusions require further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02045-5.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as abnormalities of glucose metabolism of varying degrees of severity that occur and are detected for the first time during pregnancy, including diabetes mellitus that existed prior to pregnancy but was missed, and abnormalities of glucose tolerance that occur during pregnancy. It is estimated that 23.34 million (19.7%) of Live births to women in 2024 had some form of hyperglycemia in pregnancy. Of these, 79.2% were due to GDM [1]. At present, the mechanism of GDM is not clear. Existing studies primarily indicate that the development of GDM is associated with factors such as insulin resistance, oxidative stress, islet cell function damage and deficiencies in micronutrients [2, 3]. GDM adversely affects both mother and infant. In the short-term, it increases the risk of hypertension, cesarean delivery [4], polyhydramnios, preterm birth, miscarriage, and stillbirth [5, 6]. Women with GDM face higher recurrence in future pregnancies (35.9–69.0%), a threefold increased risk of type 2 diabetes [7], and a 63% greater risk of cardiovascular disease due to lasting cardiovascular damage [8, 9]. For infants, GDM can lead to macrosomia, birth complications [10], neonatal hyperinsulinemia, and hypoglycemia, which may cause brain injury if untreated [11]. Offspring also face a doubled risk of childhood obesity [12] and long-term metabolic impairments, including higher susceptibility to type 2 diabetes [13].

Selenium (Se) is an essential trace element that plays important biological roles primarily through selenoproteins. Although once considered toxic, it is now recognized for its antioxidant functions, particularly in glutathione peroxidase activity, which helps mitigate oxidative stress [14–19]. During pregnancy, selenium levels may decrease due to physiological changes, potentially increasing the risk of GDM [19, 20]. Some studies suggest that selenium supplementation could improve glucose and lipid metabolism in GDM patients [21], while others report no significant benefits [22]. Different studies hold varying perspectives on the therapeutic efficacy of selenium for GDM. This meta-analysis evaluates the efficacy and safety of selenium supplementation for GDM patients, which provides a scientific foundation for future clinical applications.

Materials and methods

The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (Registration number CRD42024552724). Ethical approval and patient consent were not required for this study, given that this was a meta-analysis, which utilized published data.

Data sources and search strategy

This review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [23]. A Literature search of the electronic databases of PubMed, Web of science, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, CBM, CNKI, Wan Fang and VIP was carried out from their inception until May 2025. All randomized controlled trials evaluating selenium supplementation for GDM were included. The following search terms were used: gestational diabetes, diabetes mellitus gestational, gestational diabetes mellitus, pregnancy-induced diabetes, diabetes pregnancy-induced, gestational hyperglycemia; selenium, se, selenate. The reference Lists of full articles were also reviewed. No restrictions were placed on the treatment duration and the language of the results report. The detailed search strategy is given in Supplementary material 1.

Selection criteria and exclusion criteria

We included the randomized controlled trials (RCT) that met the following criteria: (i) Participants: trials for pregnant women diagnosed as GDM; (ii) Interventions: single therapy or combination therapy of selenium; (iii) Placebo: placebo (iv) Outcomes: at least one objective data of the efficacy and safety variables we need. (v) Study design: RCT design. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) non-randomized design; (ii) Participants had basic diseases such as type1 or type 2 diabetes, liver dysfunction, renal insufficiency prior to pregnancy; (iii) No control group; (iv) No outcomes for our study.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (LL and JX S) independently extracted data from eligible articles with a standard form. Any discrepancies between them were resolved by consensus, with the involvement of the third investigator if necessary. For each study, the study design, characteristics of the study populations, details on the interventions, duration of follow-up and relevant outcome data were extracted. We would contact the corresponding author if sufficient data of an eligible study could not be obtained from the full text.

Study quality assessment

The reviewers (LL and JX S) independently evaluated the methodological quality of the included studies according to the Cochrane risk of bias tool [24], including 7 domains: randomization sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, blinding of study personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias. For every study, the risk of bias was classified as “high,” “low,” or “unclear”.

Statistical analysis

Review Manager Version 5.4 software and Stata 15 MP was applied to calculate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and the mean difference (MD) for efficacy and safety outcomes. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the Cochran Q test and quantified using the I2 statistic [25]. I2 > 50% and P ≤ 0.05 showed a high heterogeneity and the random-effects model was utilized, otherwise the fixed effects model should be applied instead. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by using the stepwise exclusion method. The subgroup analysis was applied to explore the possible source of heterogeneity. The publication bias was assessed with the Begg and Egger test. All tests were 2 sided and P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Study selection

A total of 591 studies were initially searched in this study. A total of 10 articles were shortlisted for eligibility assessment. Out of the 10 articles reviewed, 6 studies were excluded. Of these 6 studies, 3 studies intervention did not meet the requirements, and 2 studies were excluded due to unrelated results. One study was excluded due to the unavailability of the full text. We contacted the corresponding author but did not respond. Finally, 4 eligible articles were included in this study. Meta-analysis

HOMA-IR

Three studies reported changes in HOMA-IR. Figure 4A shows the results. The selenium group intervention failed to significantly reduce insulin resistance in patients compared to the placebo group (P = 0.20, MD = −0.71, 95%CI: −1.80, 0.37), and there was 88% of heterogeneity between the two studies (I2 = 88%). Subgroup analyses were performed according to the study design. 2 studies did not control the diet and exercise of the patients, whereas only the study by Asemi Z [28] showed control over these factors among the study subjects. Figure 4B shows the results of the subgroup analysis of the 2 studies (without diet and exercise control). The results showed that selenium supplementation could not significantly reduce the patients’ insulin resistance index (P = 0.76, MD = −0.12, 95% CI: −0.93, 0.69) compared to the placebo group and heterogeneity was significantly reduced (I2 = 79%). The study by Karamali M [26] did not include HOMA-IR as an outcome indicator, so it was not included in the analysis.Fig. 4A Change of HOMA-IR: the result of meta-analysis. FPG

Three studies reported changes in FPG. Figure 5A shows the results. Compared to the placebo group, the intervention in the selenium group could not reduce fasting blood glucose in the patients (P = 0.95, MD = −0.30, 95%CI: −9.95, 9.34), and there was 95% of heterogeneity between the two studies (I2 = 95%). Subgroup analyses were performed based on the daily dose of selenium supplementation. Among the 3 studies, patients in 2 studies were administered a daily selenium intake of 200 µg, while those in 1 study received a daily intake of 100 µg. Figure 5B shows the results of the subgroup analysis of the 3 studies. There was a significant difference in reducing fasting blood glucose when patients’ daily selenium intake was 200 µg (P = 0.0002, MD = −5.03, 95% CI: −7.70, −2.37), with a significant decrease in heterogeneity (I2 = 0%).Fig. 5A Change of FPG: the result of meta-analysis. Adverse events

Adverse events were reported in four studies, and no significant adverse events were observed in any of these studies. Daily supplementation of 100–200 µg of selenium has been shown to be safe for patients with GDM.

Discussion

Selenium exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in the human body. In recent years, animal studies and epidemiological research have suggested that selenium is associated with the development of various diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [29]. Research has also revealed that selenium exhibits insulin-like properties, participating in regulating glucose metabolism and influencing insulin sensitivity [30]. Consequently, it is widely recognized that selenium may be closely Linked to type 2 diabetes mellitus [29, 30]. Given the potential shared pathophysiological mechanisms between T2DM and GDM [31, 32], maternal selenium nutritional status during pregnancy is considered a significant potential factor influencing the onset and progression of GDM [33]. Observational studies across multiple countries have found that pregnant women with GDM generally exhibit lower selenium levels compared to healthy pregnant women [34, 35], but the conclusions are inconsistent [36]. Furthermore, prospective studies in this field are relatively scarce, and their results show significant variation, making it difficult to provide reliable reference for clinical practice.

The major finding of this systematic review and meta-analysis was that daily supplementation with 200 µg of selenium had no significant effect on HOMA-IR, TC, TG, LDL-C, or HDL-C in GDM pregnant women. However, it significantly FPG levels and the incidence of newborn’s hyperbilirubinemia. A small-scale randomized controlled trial in Iran randomly assigned participants to a selenium group (200 µg/day), and a placebo group for a six-week intervention [27]. Results showed that compared with placebo, FPG was significantly reduced in the selenium group, which is consistent with our research findings. However, some scholars had not found this significance. In 2019, Sadat Najib F [22] conducted research on selenium supplementation for GDM. Participants were divided into a placebo group and a group receiving 100 µg/day of selenium. After a 12-week intervention, no significant effect of selenium was observed on HOMA-IR and FPG levels in GDM patients. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in drug dosage. Our study employed a selenium supplementation dose of 200 µg/d, whereas Sadat Najib F’s research utilized a dose of 100 µg/d. In 2022, Amirani E [28] also conducted a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effects of selenium and probiotic supplementation on glycemic status and serum lipoproteins in patients with GDM. The results indicated that selenium combined with probiotics significantly reduced HOMA-IR, FPG, TC, TG, and LDL-C levels, but had no significant effect on HDL-C levels. It should be noted that the intervention in this study involved the combined use of selenium and probiotics, making it impossible to distinguish whether selenium or probiotics played the primary role. Although the aforementioned studies achieved certain results, the short intervention periods, lack of baseline and post-intervention serum selenium level measurements, and unclear specifications of the selenium preparations used all reduced the reliability of the findings.

Previous scholars also have conducted systematic reviews and meta-analyses in this field. Xu W et al. [37] conducted a meta-analysis revealing that serum selenium levels were significantly lower in patients with GDM compared to healthy pregnant women. This finding was particularly evident in non-European populations and during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. The authors suggested that this discrepancy may be attributed to variations in dietary patterns across regions, as well as elevated insulin resistance and oxidative stress in GDM patients. However, some researchers hold differing views. Hamdan H et al. [38] suggested that although a decreasing trend in selenium levels was observed among GDM patients, the difference did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for publication bias. It is worth noting that most of the systematic reviews mentioned above are based on cross-sectional studies or case-control studies. Very few systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been conducted based on randomized controlled trials. Therefore, we cannot adequately compare our findings with those of previous studies.

In terms of sensitivity analysis, we used a stepwise exclusion method to test the reliability of the conclusions. For HOMA-IR and FPG, after excluding the study by Sadat Najib F, the pooled results of the remaining studies became statistically significant, inconsistent with the overall results, suggesting that the relevant conclusions lack robustness. Additionally, for TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and newborn’s hyperbilirubinemia, removing relevant studies one by one did not reveal any discrepancies between the pooled results and the overall results, suggesting that these conclusions have good robustness. Regarding publication bias, we conducted Egger tests for HOMA-IR and FPG to detect publication bias. The results of the Egger tests indicated no significant publication bias. However, for TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and newborn’s hyperbilirubinemia, due to the limited number of studies included in the analysis, Egger tests could not be performed.

The systematic evaluation also had some limitations. Firstly, the four included studies did not adequately report key factors such as baseline selenium status, serum selenium levels after supplementation, and the type of selenium used. We were unable to conduct more in-depth analyses based on these important confounding factors. Secondly, all of these studies were conducted in Iran, limiting their geographical applicability. Dietary habits and daily selenium intake vary significantly across regions, making the conclusions of this study difficult to generalize. Thirdly, this study included only four studies, and the results of sensitivity analysis and publication bias analysis may not be entirely accurate, potentially introducing greater bias. It is recommended that future studies expand the sample size for re-evaluation. Finally, although we conducted a literature quality assessment and optimized statistical methods, the risk of publication bias cannot be completely ruled out due to the limited sample size. Finally, due to language and search limitations, there may be omissions in literature retrieval and inclusion. Therefore, future studies require larger sample sizes and more comprehensive data to assess the efficacy and safety of treatment.

Conclusion

The study results indicate that for pregnant women with GDM, selenium supplementation at 200 µg/d may be beneficial for lowering FPG levels and reducing the incidence of newborn’s hyperbilirubinemia. However, due to the Limited available research data, the effect of 100 µg/d selenium supplementation on FPG levels remains unclear. No significant side effects were observed in this study. However, due to the limited number of studies included in the analysis, the sensitivity analysis and publication bias analysis had low statistical power. To draw more reliable conclusions, further studies are needed to validate these findings.
