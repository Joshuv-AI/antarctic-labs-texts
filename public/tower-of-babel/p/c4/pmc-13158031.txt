Comparative Clinical Analysis of DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 Inhibitors: A Real-World Switch Study
Authors: Hun Jee Choe, Mi Kyung Kwak, Ji Woo Lee, Yun Mi Choi, Eun-Gyoung Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are contemporary oral antidiabetic medications, each offering distinct advantages and challenges. Understanding their comparative effectiveness in real-world settings is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus who switched between DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors from December 1, 2014, to November 26, 2023, using a clinical data warehouse. All participants had baseline glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels between 6.5% and 10.0% and were receiving metformin. To reduce baseline imbalance in HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose (FPG), we excluded patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate <45 mL/min/1.73 m² and performed 1:1 propensity-score matching on baseline levels of HbA1c and FPG. The matched cohort included 168 patients (84 per group). The primary outcome was the between-group difference in change in HbA1c over 3 months. Secondary outcomes were changes in FPG, body mass index (BMI), liver enzymes, and renal function.ResultsSwitching to DPP-4 inhibitors was associated with a greater reduction in HbA1c (β, –0.26; 95% confidence interval [CI], –0.51 to –0.01; P<0.001). In contrast, switching to SGLT2 inhibitors led to significant improvements in FPG (β, 10.50; 95% CI, 0.22 to 20.78; P<0.001) and liver enzyme levels. Older patients and those with a lower BMI, higher baseline HbA1c, or chronic kidney disease derived greater benefit from switching to DPP-4 inhibitors.ConclusionTransitioning to DPP-4 inhibitors reduced HbA1c levels significantly, whereas switching to SGLT2 inhibitors improved FPG and liver-related metabolic parameters.Keywords: Dipeptidyl-peptidase IV inhibitors, Sodium-glucose transporter 2 inhibitors, Glycemic control, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Drug therapy

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus is a complex, chronic condition characterized predominantly by hyperglycemia.1 Effective management requires ongoing medical care and multifaceted strategies to achieve and maintain glycemic targets and minimize the risk of hypoglycemia. Due to the progressive nature of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), maintaining long-term glycemic control frequently requires adjustments to therapy, and often introducing an additional class of antidiabetic medications alongside metformin.2

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are two pharmacological classes of medications used to manage T2DM. Both offer glycemic control with minimal risk of hypoglycemia. DPP-4 inhibitors enhance β-cell sensitivity through incretin-based mechanisms.3 Research indicates that incretin therapies, which include DPP-4 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, may have superior glucose-lowering efficacy in Asian populations.4 This is reflected in the widespread use of DPP-4 inhibitors in Korea, where they are the second most commonly prescribed oral antidiabetic medications.5

SGLT2 inhibitors have recently attracted attention for their cardiovascular advantages and are now recommended for individuals with cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease, or heart failure.6 The initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors is increasingly considered for individuals with T2DM and CVD, regardless of baseline levels of glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) or specific glycemic targets.7 Nevertheless, the glucose-lowering effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors may diminish in patients with impaired renal function.8 Moreover, patients experiencing adverse effects from SGLT2 inhibitors or those preferring weight-neutral treatments may discontinue their use in favor of DPP-4 inhibitors.9

Despite the clinical significance of these medications, data on the metabolic consequences of switching between SGLT2 inhibitors and DPP-4 inhibitors remain sparse. This study aims to fill that knowledge gap by assessing the impact of such medication switches on a range of clinical parameters in Korean patients with T2DM. We hypothesized that switching from an SGLT2 inhibitor to a DPP-4 inhibitor would lead to greater improvements in glycemic control and related metabolic indices, such as body weight, blood pressure, and liver enzymes, over 3 months compared with switching in the reverse direction.

METHODS

Study design

We conducted a retrospective analysis using data from the clinical data warehouse at Hallym University Dongtan Sacred Heart Hospital in Gyeonggi-do, South Korea. The study compared the clinical characteristics of patients who switched from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors and vice versa from December 1, 2014, to November 26, 2023. In addition to data retrieved from the data warehouse, patient identifiers were used in a supplemental manual chart review of all 359 study participants to obtain missing baseline variables, including history of stroke, heart failure, and coronary artery disease (myocardial infarction, angina, or prior percutaneous coronary intervention), as well as any adverse events leading to a change in medication.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki as well as national and international guidelines. Due to the retrospective nature of our study, written informed consent was waived. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hallym University Dongtan Sacred Heart Hospital (approval number: 2023-07-001-001).

Study participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of T2DM who switched from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors or vice versa. Criteria for inclusion were a baseline HbA1c level of between 6.5% and 10.0% and concurrent use of metformin. Participants on dual therapy involving either sulfonylureas or thiazolidinediones, in addition to DPP-4 inhibitors or SGLT2 inhibitors, were included, aligning with insurance coverage.

Stringent exclusion criteria were chosen to minimize confounding variables: we excluded individuals on steroids or other medications that could significantly alter glycemic control, those on insulin, and patients treated with GLP-1 receptor agonists. We also excluded any patients who initiated or changed their antidiabetic treatment regimen during the study period, except for those who switched between DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors. Because adequate kidney function is essential for both drug classes and to enable subsequent matching, individuals with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <45 mL/min/1.73 m² were excluded.

Classification of the medications

SGLT2 inhibitors used in this study included dapagliflozin, ipragliflozin, ertugliflozin, and sotagliflozin. DPP-4 inhibitors included alogliptin, anagliptin, evogliptin, gemigliptin, saxagliptin, sitagliptin, teneligliptin, and vildagliptin. Thiazolidinediones included pioglitazone and lobeglitazone, while sulfonylureas covered glibenclamide, gliclazide, and glimepiride. Excluded medications were insulin of all kinds and the GLP-1 receptor agonists dulaglutide, lixisenatide, and liraglutide. Systemic steroids such as dexamethasone, hydrocortisone, prednisolone, and methylprednisolone were also excluded.

Hypertension medications included calcium channel blockers, angiotensin receptor blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, beta blockers, thiazides, alpha blockers, and other similar medications, such as hydralazine and indapamide. Dyslipidemia medications included statins and other antilipidemic medications, including ezetimibe, fenofibrate, and omega-3 acid. Antiplatelet medications included aspirin, cilostazol, clopidogrel, ginkgo biloba, and sarpogrelate.

Study outcome

The primary outcome was the differential impact on HbA1c levels in patients switching to DPP-4 inhibitors compared with those switching to SGLT2 inhibitors over 3 months, with an allowable measurement window of ±14 days. Secondary outcomes included changes in fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and metabolic parameters including body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, liver enzyme levels, eGFR, and hematocrit values following a medication switch.

To further understand the effects of anthropometric and clinical factors on HbA1c reduction, subgroup analyses were conducted based on age (below or above 60 years), sex, BMI (below or above 25 kg/m2), baseline HbA1c (below or above 8.0%), fasting glucose levels (below or above 140 mg/dL), and eGFR (below or above 60 mL/min/1.73 m2).

For explorative analysis, the differential impact on HbA1c levels and metabolic parameters was examined 6 months after a medication switch. Baseline HbA1c levels were not restricted to ensure a sample size sufficient for a robust, long-term analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean±standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Within the medication-switch group, the changes in variables were assessed by paired t-tests. The main between-group comparisons were based on 1:1 propensity-score matching for baseline HbA1c and FPG after excluding participants with an eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m². In the matched cohort (n=168; 84 per group), 3-month follow-up values were compared using linear regression models, with the follow-up value used as the dependent variable and the baseline value of the same parameter as a covariate when appropriate. For HbA1c and FPG, baseline adjustment was incorporated through matching and group assignment alone was used as the independent variable.

For transparency, the original unmatched 3- and 6-month regression analyses (performed before eGFR filtering and matching) are presented in the Supplementary Tables 1-4. Subgroup analyses were conducted and represented in forest plots, controlling for initial HbA1c levels. A P-value threshold <0.05 was set for statistical significance. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Participant characteristics and medication switch trends

Between December 1, 2014, and November 26, 2023, out of 15,599 patients diagnosed with T2DM, 2,679 who had switched medications to either SGLT2 inhibitors or DPP-4 inhibitors were initially considered. Following application of the main exclusion criteria, 269 patients who switched from DPP-4 inhibitors to SGLT2 inhibitors and 90 patients who switched from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors were eligible. An additional exclusion of participants with an eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m² was then applied, after which propensity-score matching for baseline HbA1c and FPG was performed in a 1:1 ratio, yielding a final matched cohort of 168 patients (84 in each group) for the primary analysis (Fig. 1).

The mean age of the matched participants was 54.0±12.7 years, and the mean BMI was 27.6±3.9 kg/m2, with no significant differences between the groups. The mean HbA1c at baseline was 7.3%±0.7% in those who switched to DPP-4 inhibitors and 7.3%±0.6% in those who switched to SGLT2 inhibitors (P=0.540) (Table 1). Baseline FPG levels were also similar and well matched (138.2±26.3 mg/dL vs. 138.8±26.7 mg/dL, DPP-4 inhibitors to SGLT2 inhibitors vs. SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors, respectively; P=0.866). The duration of diabetes did not differ significantly, averaging 6.2±5.9 years for those who switched to SGLT2 inhibitors and 5.6±5.8 years for those who switched to DPP-4 inhibitors.

The use of antihypertensive, dyslipidemia, and antiplatelet agents was comparable across both groups. The use of metformin, thiazolidinediones, and sulfonylurea was also similar between the groups. There was no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of coronary heart disease, stroke, or heart failure between the two groups.

There was a notable increase in switches in medication from 2014 to 2023, peaking in 2019 for switches from DPP-4 inhibitors to SGLT2 inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 1). Adverse events prompting medication changes were minimal, with two cases of vaginal itching in the SGLT2 inhibitor–to–DPP-4 inhibitor group and one case of vaginitis occurring after a switch from DPP-4 inhibitor to SGLT2 inhibitor. No serious adverse events, such as severe hypoglycemia, euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis, lower-limb amputation, Fournier’s gangrene, heart failure hospitalization, pancreatitis, severe hypersensitivity reactions, or bullous pemphigoid, were identified in either group as a reason for switching.

Comparative clinical outcomes after switching between SGLT2 inhibitors and DPP-4 inhibitors

The primary analysis was based on a propensity score-matched cohort of 168 patients, comprising 84 who switched from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors and 84 who switched in the opposite direction. These participants were followed for approximately 3 months after the medication switch, with a measurement window of up to 14 days before or after that point. Significant improvements in HbA1c levels were observed across both groups (Table 2), with a greater decrease in those switching from SGLT2 inhibitorsto DPP-4 inhibitors (β, –0.26; 95% confidence interval [CI], –0.51 to –0.01; P<0.001). Intriguingly, improvements in FPG were exclusive to the group switching from DPP-4 inhibitors to SGLT2 inhibitors, with these participants showing significantly greater reductions in FPG compared with the group switching from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors (β, 10.50; 95% CI, 0.22 to 20.78; P<0.001).

Additionally, the group that switched to SGLT2 inhibitors also experienced a significant reduction in BMI over the 3 months, an outcome that was not seen in those who switched to DPP-4 inhibitors (β, 1.76; 95% CI, 0.65 to 2.87; P=0.006), as well as a marked reduction in alanine aminotransferase (P=0.029). Increases in levels of blood urea nitrogen and hematocrit along with a decrease in uric acid levels were noted, aligning with the recognized pharmacological effects of SGLT2 inhibitors.

To assess the robustness of the main findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using the original unmatched cohort and linear regression models at both 3 and 6 months of follow-up (Supplementary Tables 1-4). Improved HbA1c levels were observed in the group that switched to DPP-4 inhibitors (β, –0.24; 95% CI, –0.46 to –0.02; P=0.030 at 3 months and β, –0.24; 95% CI, –0.42 to –0.06; P=0.009 at 6 months), while reductions in FPG, body weight, BMI, blood pressure, and uric acid levels, along with increased hematocrit, were evident in the group that switched to SGLT2 inhibitors.

Subgroup analysis of HbA1c improvement following switches between DPP-4 inhibitor and SGLT2 inhibitor

Subgroup analyses were performed in the full, unmatched cohort to preserve adequate sample size for interaction testing (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 1). Patients with HbA1c levels of 8.0% or higher demonstrated better glycemic outcomes after switching to DPP-4 inhibitors. However, changes in FPG levels did not vary consistently by baseline levels, indicating that improvements in HbA1c were not accompanied by similar patterns in FPG across the analyzed subgroups.

Additionally, the analysis revealed that participants aged 60 years or older, those with a BMI <25 kg/m2, and those with baseline eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 achieved significant improvements in glycemic control 3 months after switching to DPP-4 inhibitors.

DISCUSSION

This study provides important insights into the differential impacts of switching between DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors on glycemic control in Korean patients with T2DM. Our findings suggest that switching from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors significantly improves HbA1c levels at 3 months, particularly in older patients or those with a higher baseline HbA1c. Conversely, those transitioning from DPP-4 inhibitors to SGLT2 inhibitors experience substantial reductions in FPG. The findings emphasize the distinct and critical roles these therapeutic classes play in the personalized management of diabetes and indicate a need to select treatments according to their unique mechanisms of action and the specific metabolic requirements of the patients.

The surge in the frequency of medication switches to SGLT2 inhibitors during 2019 coincides with the release of landmark studies underscoring the substantial cardiovascular and renal benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors. Notably, publication of the results of the Dapagliflozin Effect on Cardiovascular Events–Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 58 (DECLARE-TIMI 58) trial in 2018 revealed secondary outcomes such as reductions in cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalizations and renal outcomes, which likely influenced prescribing trends.10 This trend continued with the Dapagliflozin and Prevention of Adverse Outcomes in Heart Failure (DAPA-HF) trial, which was released in 2019 and met its primary outcome of reducing worsening heart failure or death from cardiovascular causes in patients with existing heart failure.11 Subsequent influential studies, including Dapagliflozin and Prevention of Adverse Outcomes in Chronic Kidney Disease (DAPA-CKD), Empagliflozin Outcome Trial in Patients with Chronic Heart Failure and a Reduced Ejection Fraction (EMPEROR-Reduced), and EMPEROR-Preserved, all published in 2020, further emphasized the cardiometabolic and renal advantages of SGLT2 inhibitors and encouraged the shift toward this class of drugs in clinical practice.12-14 These results likely contributed to the growing uptake of SGLT2 inhibitors and their prioritization in the American Diabetes Association treatment algorithm as agents with intermediate-to-high glycemic efficacy and proven cardiorenal benefit.15 A proportional increase in switches from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors was also reported during the same period. This bidirectional switching pattern reflects diverse clinical considerations, such as tolerability, comorbidities, and glycemic priorities, and provided the opportunity to directly compare the metabolic effects of both transition directions within the same population.

The greater reduction in HbA1c levels observed after switching from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors, particularly among patients with a higher baseline HbA1c, may reflect ethnic and pathophysiological differences in treatment response. Although SGLT2 inhibitors are generally associated with greater reductions in glucose levels in individuals with higher glycemic burden,16 this relationship is also observed with DPP-4 inhibitors,16-18 indicating that baseline hyperglycemia can predict absolute HbA1c reduction regardless of drug class.19 In East Asians, preserved incretin responsiveness and a predominant defect in β-cell function rather than insulin resistance may enhance the efficacy of DPP-4 inhibitors.20

The superior glycemic efficacy of DPP-4 inhibitors in Asian populations is well documented.21 A small randomized clinical trial in Japan revealed that patients with T2DM who switched from DPP-4 inhibitor to the SGLT2 inhibitor tofogliflozin experienced reduced arterial stiffness, waist circumference, body weight, and body fat, yet paradoxically saw an increase in HbA1c at follow-up.22 Similar outcomes were observed in the LUNA study, in which increases in HbA1c followed a switch from DPP-4 inhibitors to luseogliflozin.23 A retrospective study in Korea noted improvements in HbA1c among those switching from DPP-4 inhibitors to SGLT2 inhibitors; however, the impact of a reverse switch was not assessed, leaving a gap in comparative efficacy data.24 In East Asians, including Koreans and Japanese, the incretin effect does not appear to be diminished compared with the effect in subjects with normal glucose tolerance, potentially enhancing the glucose-lowering impact of incretin-based therapies.25 Additionally, Korean dietary patterns— those characterized by a high carbohydrate intake—are associated with pronounced postprandial hyperglycemia,26 which is preferentially targeted by DPP-4 inhibitors through incretin-mediated enhancement of insulin secretion and suppression of glucagon, whereas SGLT2 inhibitors exert comparatively larger effects on fasting glycemia via glucosuria. Taken together, these pharmacologic, physiologic, and dietary factors may explain the observed glycemic benefit in our cohort when switching from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors, particularly among those with a high baseline HbA1c.

Additionally, our results align with findings from previous studies suggesting a significant correlation between baseline BMI and a reduction in HbA1c, particularly in studies in which the mean baseline BMI was under 30 kg/m2.21 This supports the notion of a potential BMI threshold below which incretin-based therapies may be more effective. In our study, individuals with a BMI below the Asian obesity cutoff of 25 kg/m2 particularly benefited from switching to DPP-4 inhibitors. The observed enhancements in the efficacy of DPP-4 inhibitor therapy among older individuals and those with higher baseline HbA1c levels further highlight the effectiveness of incretinbased therapies, particularly for leaner and higher-risk populations.

SGLT2 inhibitors are particularly effective at higher eGFR levels, taking advantage of the mechanisms of glucosuria and natriuresis.27 While they can help prevent the progression of chronic kidney disease in individuals with decreased renal function, their ability to lower glucose levels tends to diminish as eGFR decreases.8 Baseline eGFRs were higher in the group that switched from DPP-4 inhibitors to SGLT2 inhibitors, likely reflecting expectations of reducing the decline in eGFR. However, our findings suggest that, in terms of improving HbA1c, individuals with an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 experienced more significant benefits from switching from SGLT2 inhibitors to DPP-4 inhibitors. Despite this, the switch to SGLT2 inhibitors was accompanied by inherent benefits, including reductions in BMI and liver enzymes, along with an increase in hematocrit. Recent evidence also suggests that SGLT2 inhibitors may help ameliorate steatotic liver disease, demonstrating a broader therapeutic potential.28

This study has several limitations. Although we conducted a 6-month exploratory analysis, the study duration was insufficient to observe long-term outcomes such as renal protection. Additionally, being a retrospective, single-center study limits its generalizability across the broader Korean population with T2DM. When we applied propensity-score matching to minimize baseline imbalances in HbA1c and FPG, we confirmed that the main findings were robust in the matched cohort, with consistent directions of effect in sensitivity analyses using the full, unmatched population. Another limitation is the absence of a predefined minimum duration of baseline drug use before switching, which may affect the comparability of exposure between groups. In our clinical practice, follow-up intervals are generally 3 months, suggesting that most patients remain on their initial medication for at least this period. Although few adverse events were identified as prompting a switch, undocumented differences in drug tolerability or adherence cannot be excluded. Finally, the precise clinical rationale for switching, whether primarily to intensify glycemic control or to achieve cardiovascular or renal protection, cannot be determined in a retrospective design. A comprehensive baseline review showed no significant differences in atherosclerotic CVD, heart failure, or stroke between groups, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting our findings. Nevertheless, the strengths of this study include comprehensive capture of nearly a decade of real-world clinical data, rigorous propensity-score matching to minimize baseline imbalances, and detailed manual chart review to identify adverse events and cardiovascular history.

In conclusion, this pioneering study explored the glycemic and metabolic effects of interchanging DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors within a Korean cohort. Our findings suggest that, for SGLT2 inhibitor users not achieving HbA1c targets, switching to DPP-4 inhibitors can be safe and effective. Conversely, switching from DPP-4 inhibitors to SGLT2 inhibitors resulted in significant improvements in fasting glucose and liver enzyme levels, indicating that the benefits vary depending on the direction of the switch.
