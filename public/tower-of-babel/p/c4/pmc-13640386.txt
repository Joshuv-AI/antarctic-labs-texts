Metabolomic profiling in IgA nephropathy: urinary and salivary biomarker insights
Authors: İbrahim Eslem Demirbağ, Rümeyza Kazancıoğlu, Şahabettin Selek, Evrim Dalkılıç, Meltem Gürsu, Ömer Celal Elçioğlu, Özge Pasin, Metin Demirel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunoglobulin A Nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis, often leading to end-stage kidney disease in 20–40% of cases. Despite extensive research on urinary and serum metabolomics, salivary metabolomics remains unexplored. This study investigates metabolomic alterations in IgAN using both salivary and urinary analyses and examines potential correlations between these biofluids.MethodsMetabolomic profiling was performed using liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) on saliva and urine samples from 16 IgAN patients and 13 matched controls. Data were processed using TidyMass and MetaboAnalyst, with metabolite annotation via HMDB, MassBank and MoNA. Pathway analysis was conducted using the KEGG database, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05.ResultsSalivary analysis identified 42 metabolites, with four significantly altered in IgAN patients. Picric acid, Buphedrone and Deoxyadenosine were elevated, while N-Acetylneuraminic acid was reduced, implicating ABC transporters, purine metabolism and neurotransmitter pathways. Urinary analysis revealed 138 metabolites, with 14 significantly altered, primarily affecting the pentose phosphate pathway. No significant correlation was observed between urinary and salivary metabolomic profiles.ConclusionThough urinary and salivary metabolomes showed distinct alterations, our study only supported N-Acetylneuraminic acid as a potential IgAN biomarker.Keywords: Urine, Saliva, LC-MS/MS, Pathway analysis, N-Acetylneuraminic acid

Introduction

Immunoglobulin A Nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide, whose pathophysiology is based on multi-hit process, involving increased production of galactose-deficient IgA1, formation of immune complexes and subsequent mesangial deposition leading to renal inflammation and damage [1–3]. IgA is the typical product of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) such as the upper respiratory tract and for the defected IgA1 [4]. And also, IgA is predominant antibody in saliva which is a biofluid used in metabolomic studies for evaluating various diseases [5, 6]. Studies that have been done on IgAN patients’ tonsils which are nasopharynx-associated lymphoid tissue suggested that patients with IgAN have high levels of IgA-producing plasma cells in their tonsils which is one of the origins of excessive galactose-deficient IgA1 in the blood. Thus, the pathophysiology, the correlation between the clinical features like hematuria after a trigger like upper respiratory tract infection and episodic recurrency and progression of the disease is the key to IgAN [4]. Therefore, saliva was included in this study as a non-invasive biofluid alongside urine to evaluate mucosal IgA dysregulation associated with IgAN. The gold standard for IgAN diagnosis remains kidney biopsy [7]. Being an invasive procedure in a clinical practice with contraindications and complications are challenges with kidney biopsy. Also, clinical diagnosis has challenges with early diagnosis, misdiagnosis, complex presentation of the disease and prediction of the disease progression. So, there is an ongoing need for novel biomarker studies to personalize the diagnosis process, predict and monitor progression of the disease and evaluate the response to the treatment [8].

Most exploratory and validation metabolomic studies on IgAN which are used for providing precise insights into the physiological and pathophysiological status of an organism by assessing metabolic changes in biofluids, have focused on urinary and serum analyses, as well as fecal microbiota studies, to identify potential diagnostic biomarkers [9, 10]. Urine metabolomics give direct insight into tubular dysfunction and renal tissue alterations that occur due to IgA nephropathy [11]. On the other hand, saliva could give insight into defected IgA1 production and dysregulated mucosal immunity rather than the outcome [4, 12]. Similar pathway dysregulations that relates to the IgAN pathophysiology of both urine and saliva are expected. Zhang et al. (2022) conducted an exploratory serum metabolomics and proteomics study on IgAN patients, identifying palmitoleic acid as a unique diagnostic biomarker, along with disruptions in energy and amino acid metabolism [13]. Neprovasa et al. (2016) analyzed urinary metabolomics and proteomics in an exploratory study, identifying a panel of three metabolites that significantly differentiated IgAN patients from control groups [14]. Kalantari et al. (2016) examined metabolic pathway disruptions in IgAN severity in an exploratory study on IgAN patients, highlighting acetoacetate, hypotaurine, homocysteine and L-kynurenine as key differentiators between mild and severe cases, with phenylalanine metabolism as the most affected pathway [7]. Sample size and lack of standardization in study design which prevents reliable comparison and correlation of metabolomics data across independent studies are reported limitations of metabolomic studies [10, 13].

Despite numerous studies on urinary and serum metabolomics in IgAN, no research has yet explored salivary metabolomics in this disease. Given this gap, this study aimed to evaluate IgAN from a novel perspective by analyzing salivary and urinary metabolomic differences between healthy individuals and IgAN patients and assessing the correlation between urinary and salivary metabolomics in IgAN.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the salivary metabolomics in IgAN.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This cross-sectional, non-randomized controlled prospective study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration. The research was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Bezmialem Vakıf University (No: 100426). All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Participants provided consent for their anonymized data to be published in this study.

Study population

Among The IgAN patients of the Nephrology Outpatient clinic of Bezmialem Vakıf University 16 patients who were not receiving immunosuppressive treatment at least 3 months prior and who gave consent were included in the study. The control group consisted of 13 age and gender matched healthy individuals. Informed consent to participate in the study had been obtained from all participants. Consent to Publish declarations: not applicable.

Sample size were determined by a power analysis. At a 95% confidence level and 80% statistical power, with an effect size of 0.90 considered clinically significant, the required sample size was determined to be 16 participants per group. This calculation was based on the effect size estimates of metabolites reported by Pinto J et al. (2021) [15].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All participants with pregnancy, smoking, substance abuse, orthodontic treatment, unhealthy periodontal tissue, active tooth decay, use of removable dentures or dental splints, active urinary tract infection, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren’s disease, or antibiotic use within the past six months were excluded from both groups.

Recruitment for both groups took place between June 2023 and March 2024. During this period, general demographic information and laboratory parameters, including estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine creatinine, proteinuria, serum creatinine and serum urea levels, were obtained from the hospital’s electronic records.

For the IgAN group, demographic data (age, height, weight, body mass index and gender) and laboratory parameters from their most recent routine check-ups on the day of sample collection were recorded from the hospital’s electronic system. In contrast, demographic information for healthy participants (age, height, weight, body mass index and gender) was gathered based on their self-reported statements.

Additionally, for the control group, blood samples and an extra random spot urine sample were collected on the same day as saliva and urine samples for metabolomics analysis. These samples were used to assess eGFR, urine creatinine, proteinuria, serum creatinine and serum urea levels. The corresponding laboratory results from these samples were later retrieved from the hospital’s electronic records.

Saliva and urine sample collection and processing

Stimulated saliva samples were collected from all participants according to a study conducted by Khurshid et al. (2016) [16]. To standardize conditions, participants were restricted from eating and performing oral hygiene procedures for three hours prior to collection. Sample collection was conducted in the early hours of the day.

Participants were instructed to rinse their mouths with water for 30 s and rest for five minutes. They were then given sugar-free gum to chew. The saliva produced during the first minute of chewing was discarded. Participants continued chewing for another five minutes, during which they were instructed to spit accumulated saliva into a sterile specimen container without swallowing. Urine samples were collected on the same day as saliva samples. Spot urine samples were obtained from participants using sterile specimen containers. The samples were centrifuged at 11,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, sediment was observed in both saliva and urine samples. The supernatants were transferred into clean Eppendorf tubes and stored at -86 °C until further analysis.

According to previous studies, it is acknowledged that using sugar free gum for stimulation can alter the flow rate, pH and composition of the saliva and may expose it to exogenous compounds [17, 18]. To minimize this effect and preserve the relative differences between participants, all participants underwent the same standardized sampling procedure in the standardized conditions.

Metabolomic analysis

Before analysis, frozen saliva and urine samples were thawed at room temperature and subjected to methanol-based protein precipitation to reduce matrix related interference. Briefly, methanol was added to each sample at a 1:2 ratio, followed by centrifugation at 10.000 × g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was collected, concentrated by solvent evaporation, reconstituted in methanol, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and transferred into chromatography vials for LC-HRMS analysis.

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed at the Bezmialem Vakif University Drug Research Center (İLMER) using a Thermo Q Exactive high-resolution mass spectrometer coupled to a Troyasil C18 column (250 × 2.1 mm, 5 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (A) and methanol (B). The gradient program started at 5% B, increased to 95% B at 18 min and then returned to the initial conditions, giving a total run time of 20 min. The injection volume was 2 µL.

Mass spectrometric acquisition was performed in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. Data were acquired using a Full MS with data-dependent MS/MS (dd-MS²) strategy over an m/z range of 50–1050. Ion source parameters were set as follows: sheath gas, 45 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas, 10 arbitrary units; spray voltage, 3.80 kV; capillary temperature, 320 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 320 °C; and S-lens RF level, 50.

To monitor analytical performance and background contamination, pooled quality control (QC) samples and blank injections were incorporated throughout the analytical sequence. QC samples were prepared by pooling 10 µL aliquots from each study sample, while methanol was used as the blank. QC and blank samples were injected after every 10 study samples to assess analytical stability and potential background interference during the run. Study samples were analyzed in a randomized order to minimize potential run-order effects. Pooled QC samples were repeatedly injected throughout the analytical sequence, including across batches, to monitor instrument stability and analytical reproducibility over time.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Raw LC-HRMS data files were converted into mzXML and mgf formats using ProteoWizard. Data processing and statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.2) using the TidyMass 1.0 and MetaboAnalyst 5.0 packages. Prior to statistical evaluation, the dataset was normalized using probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN). Missing values were imputed using median imputation and the data were subsequently log-transformed before downstream analyses. In addition, for the urinary dataset, a creatinine-normalized sensitivity analysis was performed for the differential metabolites to further assess the robustness of the main findings.

Multivariate pattern recognition was performed using sparse partial least squares-discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA) to visualize clustering tendencies between groups. Score plots based on the first two components were used for visualization and the explained variance of each component was reported on the axes.

Spearman correlation analysis was used because of the limited sample size and the non-parametric structure of the data.

Metabolite annotation was performed by matching features against publicly available databases, including HMDB, MassBank and MoNA, based on accurate mass and MS/MS data. Annotation confidence was interpreted according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) and the metabolites reported in the present study were classified as putative annotations corresponding to MSI level 2.

Fold change (FC) values were calculated for group comparisons and p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Metabolites with p < 0.05 and/or FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Pathway analysis was performed in MetaboAnalyst 5.0 using the KEGG database.

Metabolomic profiling revealed differential metabolite patterns in both saliva and urine samples obtained from IgAN patients and healthy controls. Before downstream filtering and annotation, a total of 4,305 features were detected in the salivary dataset and 4,570 features in the urinary dataset. After annotation, 42 salivary metabolites and 138 urinary metabolites were retained for further evaluation.

In the salivary dataset, four annotated metabolites showed significant between-group differences (Table 2). Picric acid, deoxyadenosine and buphedrone were higher in the IgAN group, whereas N-acetylneuraminic acid was lower. Given the limited number of differential salivary metabolites and the putative nature of the annotations, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Exploratory pathway analysis of the salivary dataset suggested several potentially affected pathways, including ABC transporters, arginine biosynthesis, purine metabolism, histidine metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, glutamatergic synapse and GABAergic synapse (Fig. 1B).

To assess whether the observed salivary metabolite differences were independent of age, additional age-adjusted linear regression analyses were performed for the four metabolites identified in the primary comparison. After adjustment for age, deoxyadenosine, buphedrone and N-acetylneuraminic acid remained significantly associated with group status, whereas picric acid was no longer statistically significant (Table 2). The age-adjusted regression coefficients are also presented as effect size estimates in Table 2.

In the urinary dataset, 14 annotated metabolites differed between IgAN patients and controls (Table 3). Glucaric acid, N-acetylneuraminic acid, 4-imidazoleacrylic acid, D-gluconic acid, pseudouridine, pyruvic acid and DL-pyroglutamic acid were increased in IgAN, whereas 1-methylguanosine, uric acid, hexanoylglycine, L-glutamine, 3-indoleacrylic acid androsterone glucuronide(1) and 5.alpha.-androstan-17.beta.-ol-3-one sulfate were decreased. Exploratory pathway analysis suggested enrichment of the pentose phosphate pathway (Fig. 1A). Most urinary metabolites remained associated with group status after age adjustment, except for 1-methylguanosine and L-glutamine and age-adjusted regression coefficients were reported as effect size estimates (Table 3). In a creatinine-normalized sensitivity analysis, the overall pattern remained broadly consistent, with glucaric acid, N-acetylneuraminic acid, 4-imidazoleacrylic acid, D-gluconic acid, pseudouridine, pyruvic acid and DL-pyroglutamic acid remaining significantly associated with group status.

To further explore the clinical relevance of the identified metabolites, correlation analyses were performed between differential metabolites and renal function parameters within the IgAN group. Spearman correlation analysis showed that several urinary metabolites were associated with eGFR. Glucaric acid was negatively correlated with eGFR (rho = -0.640, p = 0.008), whereas L-glutamine (rho = 0.570, p = 0.021), 5.alpha.-androstan-17.beta.-ol-3-one sulfate (rho = 0.562, p = 0.023) and androsterone glucuronide(1) (rho = 0.534, p = 0.033) were positively correlated with eGFR. No statistically significant correlation was observed between the differential urinary metabolites and proteinuria. Likewise, no significant correlation was detected between salivary differential metabolites and either eGFR or proteinuria.

Multivariate analysis using sparse partial least squares-discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA) showed a tendency toward group-wise clustering in both saliva and urine datasets (Fig. 2). In the salivary dataset, component 1 and component 2 explained 7.5% and 7.7% of the variance, respectively, whereas the corresponding values in the urinary dataset were 9.0% and 7.3%. When samples were evaluated according to biofluid type, saliva and urine showed a clearer separation pattern, with component 1 explaining 29.7% and component 2 explaining 10.3% of the variance. In the combined sPLS-DA model including saliva control (SC), saliva IgAN (SP), urine control (UC) and urine IgAN (UP) groups, component 1 and component 2 explained 45.8% and 13.2% of the variance, respectively. Overall, the separation was more apparent according to biofluid type than according to disease status alone.

Discussion

Salivary and urinary metabolomic analyses enabled comprehensive metabolic profiling in IgAN, the most common primary glomerulonephritis [1, 19]. In this untargeted metabolomic study, salivary and urinary analyses identified differential metabolite patterns between IgAN patients and healthy controls. However, these findings should be interpreted within the exploratory framework of the study, given the limited sample size, the modest number of differential salivary metabolites and the putative nature of the metabolite annotations.

Exploratory pathway analysis of the salivary dataset suggested potential involvement of ABC transporters, arginine biosynthesis, purine metabolism, histidine metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, glutamatergic synapse and GABAergic synapse. Given the limited number of altered salivary metabolites and the putative nature of the annotations, these pathway-level findings should be interpreted cautiously.

In the salivary dataset, deoxyadenosine and N-acetylneuraminic acid remained associated with group status after age adjustment, whereas the salivary findings overall should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of differential metabolites and the uncertain biological plausibility of some putative annotations. In the urinary dataset, several metabolites related to energy metabolism, nucleotide metabolism and amino acid metabolism differed between IgAN patients and controls. Although both biofluids exhibited disease-related alterations, no direct correspondence between salivary and urinary metabolite patterns was demonstrated in the present study.

ABC transporters are involved in the transport of metabolites, xenobiotics and uremic solutes and altered transporter-related processes have been described in chronic kidney disease [20]. In the present study, the appearance of ABC transporters in the exploratory salivary pathway analysis may reflect altered metabolite handling in the context of renal dysfunction and medication exposure; however, this interpretation remains provisional and requires further validation.

Arginine biosynthesis is another impaired pathway in chronic kidney disease and IgAN, as suggested by Baylis et al. (2006) and Qu et al. (2024) which our findings indicate this impairment as well [21, 22]. Since a significant portion of arginine synthesis occurs in the kidney and liver, kidney damage due to IgAN most likely contributes to the dysregulation of this pathway.

Patients with chronic kidney disease exhibit altered amino acid levels in their circulation, including histidine, which functions as an anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agent. Similar to the role of arginine in nitric oxide metabolism, histidine is involved in nitrogen metabolism, making it critical in oxidative stress regulation [21, 23]. Persistent oxidative stress in IgAN and compensatory mechanisms may contribute to the observed metabolic pathway alterations. 4-Imidazoleacrylic acid (urocanic acid), a metabolite derived from histidine degradation and excreted in urine, was found to be upregulated in IgAN patients [24, 25]. A study by Peris-Fernández et al. on hemodialysis patients reported significantly reduced urocanic acid levels in blood samples during inflammation [26]. The upregulation of this metabolite in our study may indicate a systemic response to IgAN-associated chronic inflammation.

Glutamatergic and GABAergic synapse-related pathways emerged in the exploratory salivary pathway analysis. Both pathways are influenced by changes in glutamine levels, which were also found to be dysregulated in our study [27]. Previous research suggests that podocyte-driven glutamatergic signaling is crucial for maintaining the glomerular filtration barrier and impairment of this pathway has been associated with podocyte dysfunction and increased albuminuria [28]. Additionally, Natale et al. reported that serum from chronic kidney disease patients contains guanidino compounds, which act as uremic toxins and are associated with the inhibition of GABAergic transmission. Moreover, glutamatergic transmission was found to be elevated in dialyzed chronic kidney disease patients [29]. Taken together, these findings may suggest a possible relationship between renal injury, altered nitrogen-related metabolism and neurotransmitter-associated pathway signatures; however, this interpretation remains speculative in the absence of targeted validation.

Exploratory pathway analysis of the urinary dataset indicated possible enrichment of the pentose phosphate pathway in IgAN patients. D-Gluconic acid and pyruvic acid (pyruvate), both involved in energy metabolism, were among the upregulated urinary metabolites [30, 31]. Park et al. identified pyruvate as a significantly elevated urinary metabolite in chronic kidney disease, including IgAN [32]. Integrating these findings with our pathway analysis results, the disruption of the pentose phosphate pathway and the upregulation of D-gluconic acid and pyruvate suggest a metabolic alteration associated with IgAN. These findings may support a link between altered energy-related metabolism and IgAN pathophysiology, although this relationship should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory design of the study [2]. Glucaric acid was the most upregulated urinary metabolite identified in our study. As an end product of the D-gluconic acid pathway, it is primarily excreted in urine [31]. Given its role in energy metabolism and mitochondrial function, its dysregulation aligns with the pathophysiology of IgAN.

Several metabolites identified in our study are associated with chronic kidney diseases and chronic inflammatory processes. For instance, pseudouridine, which was found to be upregulated in IgAN patients, has been positively correlated with declining eGFR rates, chronic kidney disease incidence and the progression of type 2 diabetic kidney disease to end-stage renal disease [9, 33–35]. Given that IgAN is a chronic kidney disease, the upregulation of pseudouridine observed in our study is consistent with findings in other chronic kidney diseases.

Overall, several of the dysregulated metabolites identified in our study were broadly consistent with previous reports in chronic kidney disease and IgAN, particularly those related to inflammatory and energy-associated metabolic processes. The majority of these metabolites were linked to inflammatory processes or energy metabolism. Beyond these expected metabolite alterations, one notable finding was the dysregulation of N-acetylneuraminic acid. This metabolite, which plays a key role in mucosal immunity, was found to be dysregulated in both urine and saliva samples from IgAN patients, highlighting its potential significance in IgAN pathophysiology.

N-Acetylneuraminic acid was found to be downregulated in the saliva of IgAN patients compared to controls, while it was upregulated in their urine. Also known as sialic acid, this amino sugar derivative is widely present in human biofluids, including saliva and urine. It plays a crucial role in innate immunity by preventing infections of mucosal membranes, acting as a bait for pathogens and serving as a receptor for influenza. Additionally, it contributes to the slippery texture of saliva and may regulate glomerular basement membrane permeability.

Previous studies have reported increased levels of free N-acetylneuraminic acid in both serum and urine of patients with kidney failure [36–41]. Kidney failure can manifest acutely or chronically. Martin-Lorenza et al. (2017) identified N-acetylneuraminic acid as an upregulated metabolite in urinary metabolomics analysis of acute kidney injury patients, suggesting a potential link between its elevation and severe kidney vascular and tubular injury [42]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) can result from various underlying conditions, including diabetic kidney disease. Tomofuji et al. (2022) evaluated serum metabolites in diabetic patients with diabetic kidney disease and observed increased N-acetylneuraminic acid levels, suggesting its contribution to kidney damage via inflammatory pathways [43].

Additionally, Demir et al. (2020) conducted a metabolic study on IgA vasculitis with nephritis, an IgA-related immune complex disease that shares a similar pathogenesis with IgAN, characterized by galactose-deficient IgA1 accumulation in the renal glomeruli [44]. Their findings demonstrated elevated plasma N-acetylneuraminic acid levels in IgA vasculitis with nephritis, suggesting its involvement in IgA glycosylation [45]. Our results are aligned with previous studies on kidney failure and CKD, confirming N-acetylneuraminic acid upregulation in urine samples. Given that serum studies also report increased levels, a similar trend might be expected in saliva.

Studies on salivary metabolomics indicate that N-acetylneuraminic acid is elevated under conditions of excessive physiological stress, such as periodontal infections and apical abscesses and functions as an inflammatory marker due to its role in immune regulation, oxidative stress prevention and inflammatory response modulation [46–48]. Generally, metabolites that are elevated in serum tend to show similar elevations in saliva [49]. However, our findings contradict this trend, as we observed N-acetylneuraminic acid downregulation in saliva but upregulation in urine. Given that IgAN is a chronic kidney disease with mucosal immune involvement, the opposite direction of N-acetylneuraminic acid change in saliva and urine may reflect differences between local mucosal processes and systemic or renal excretory mechanisms. This observation should be considered preliminary and requires confirmation in larger cohorts and in additional biofluids.

Mucosal-kidney axis is a recognized concept of the IgAN. Secretory IgA is produced in MALT tissues like tonsils, main antibody isotype in mucosal secretions and is responsible for mucosal immunity. Defect in glycosylation of IgA due to IgAN causes aberrant mucosal immune responses to respiratory tract infections and aggravates the pre-existing IgAN with excessive production and accumulation of pathological IgA in glomeruli and promote clinical manifestations such as hematuria [5, 50]. Our salivary results suggest that N-acetylneuraminic acid, which is an important metabolite for mucosal immunity, pathogen interactions just like IgA and responsible for podocyte foot processes morphology is dysregulated in IgAN patients [5, 36–41, 51, 52]. Previous studies show elevated levels of N-acetylneuraminic acid in oral infections [46–48]. Although our salivary results could be altered by pre-existing local oral infections, setting several oral hygiene criterias as an exclusion criterion and the opposite trend in N-acetylneuraminic acid indicates that this downregulation in saliva likely represent systemic effects of IgAN on mucosal immunity. Because secretory IgA main specific immune defense mechanism in saliva and may play an important role in oral immunity [53]. On the other hand, N-acetylneuraminic acid is expected to increase in urine in IgAN patients as a chronic kidney disease which correlates with our results [43]. This opposite trend that N-acetylneuraminic acid shows between saliva and urine may be attributed to its direct involvement in mucosal immunity which is disrupted due to the pathophysiology of IgAN. This finding further suggests that it may represent a distinguishing feature, potentially enabling differentiation of this disease from other chronic kidney diseases. This observation should be considered preliminary and requires confirmation in larger cohorts and in additional biofluids.

Limitations

The present findings should be considered in the context of several limitations. The relatively small sample size and cross-sectional design restrict generalizability and do not allow assessment of longitudinal metabolic changes. Moreover, the identified metabolites were putatively annotated and should be confirmed in larger validation cohorts. Despite these limitations, the applied analytical workflow and quality control strategy support the robustness of this exploratory dataset.

Although saliva collection was fully standardized, the use of gum and stimulated saliva may represent a methodological limitation due to flow rate dilution and compositional changes including exogen compounds.

Conclusion

In conclusion, both urinary and salivary metabolomic datasets showed disease-related alterations in IgAN, although the patterns differed between the two biofluids. The urinary findings appeared more robust, whereas the salivary findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of differential metabolites and the putative nature of the annotations. N-acetylneuraminic acid emerged as a metabolite of interest because it was altered in both biofluids with opposite directional changes. Overall, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating and they warrant validation in larger, independent cohorts before any biomarker-related conclusions can be drawn.
