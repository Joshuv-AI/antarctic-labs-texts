A Comprehensive Evaluation of Rapid Diagnostic Methods in Neonatal Sepsis: Evaluating Their Accuracy, Turnaround Time, and Impact on Antibiotic Stewardship and Clinical Outcomes
Authors: Amar Devaguru, Kakara R. Kanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Neonatal sepsis is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among births in newborns in the global community. The current diagnostic approaches, especially blood culture, are insensitive and take a long time to give results, thus delaying treatment and increasing dependence on empirical broader spectrum antibiotics. Challenges posed by slow pathogen detection continue to be a norm in diagnostic platforms. Innovations like polymerase chain reaction (PCR), microfluidics, and procalcitonin (PCT) present probable solutions that can enhance pathogen detection.Methodology:This prospective observational study was conducted over 6 months from February 2024 to July 2024 at the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of Maheshwara Medical College, Hyderabad, India. We randomized the newborns to receive rapid diagnostic methods or standard blood cultures in 200 patients with suspected sepsis. This research assessed diagnostic concordance, time to result, antibiotic prescriptions, and clinical performance indicators such as mortality, NICU length of stay, and readmission.Results:Rapid diagnostic methods significantly outperformed conventional blood cultures in diagnostic accuracy, with PCR assays showing 92% sensitivity and 89% specificity. Turnaround times for PCR, microfluidics, and PCT tests were 6, 4, and 2 h, respectively, compared to 48–72 h for blood cultures. The implementation of rapid diagnostics led to a reduction in empirical antibiotic initiation from 95% to 70%, increased antibiotic de-escalation from 20% to 50%, and shortened antibiotic therapy duration from 8 to 6 days. Clinically, mortality rates decreased from 15% to 10%, NICU stay reduced from 14 to 11 days, and readmission rates dropped from 8% to 5%.Conclusion:This is because rapid diagnostic methods enhance the diagnosis of neonatal sepsis by increasing accuracy and also shortening the duration taken in its diagnosis. This helps in the efficient use of antibiotics, and hence good clinical results were observed. Of these, some can improve care, limit the use of antibiotics, and hence prevent the development of resistance and improve management of neonatal sepsis, especially in limited resource regions.

INTRODUCTION

Neonatal sepsis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in newborns worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where diagnostic and therapeutic resources are limited. Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for initiating targeted therapy, reducing unnecessary antibiotic exposure, and improving patient outcomes. However, traditional blood culture practices can take a few days to process and have low sensitivity, which would result in delayed management and increased use of empirical antibiotics.[12] This is because the development of rapid diagnostic methods including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), microfluidics-based assays, and point-of-care tests has in recent years proven to mitigate these factors. The former is screening or detection methods developed to deliver quicker and more precise results of the pathogen and/or resistance marker that enables the clinician to effect better antimicrobial management. For example, a systematic review by Mohan Pammi et al. (2011)[3] showed that molecular techniques such as PCR tests are more sensitive and specific than blood culture tests in the diagnosis of neonatal sepsis.

These methods are promising; however, the application of these methods in real-world clinical practice to address clinical outcomes, antibiotic stewardship, and health care resource utilization has been given inadequate focus. Recent literature by Iroh Tam et al.[4] revealed that, despite the clinical value of RDx for neonatal sepsis, more research is required to examine the impact of this test on the clinical consequence and direction of antibiotics in the management of neonatal sepsis.

The main rationale of this research can therefore be summarized as the following: To provide a systematic review of the available evidence of the performance of each rapid diagnostic method in terms of accuracy, turnaround time, and clinical consequences in neonates with suspected sepsis. Thus, we expect that, by comparing the various technologies described above and the results of their usage, we will be able to offer recommendations for the efficient implementation of these tools into daily practice for clinicians.

METHODS

This prospective, observational study was conducted over a 6-month period, from February 2024 to July 2024, in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of Maheshwara Medical College, Hyderabad. The study aimed to evaluate the accuracy, turnaround time, and clinical impact of rapid diagnostic methods for neonatal sepsis compared to conventional blood culture methods.

Neonates admitted to the NICU with suspected sepsis during the study period were enrolled. Neonates aged ≤28 days presenting with clinical signs of sepsis, such as fever, hypothermia, lethargy, feeding intolerance, or respiratory distress were included in the study. Neonates with congenital malformations or life-limiting conditions and those already receiving antibiotics for ≥48 h before admission were kept as exclusion criteria.

A total of 200 neonates were included in the study, and the calculation is based on the estimated incidence of neonatal sepsis (20%–25%) in similar settings. Ethical approval was taken from the hospital’s Institutional Ethics Committee, and informed consent was obtained from the parents/guardians of all participants.

The study compared the diagnostic performance of conventional blood cultures with rapid diagnostic methods, including PCR-based assays, microfluidics-based platforms, and procalcitonin (PCT) levels.

Blood samples were collected for all diagnostic methods, and the results of conventional blood cultures were processed using an automated system (e.g., BacT/ALERT, bioMérieux), with results reported within 48–72 h. Rapid diagnostic methods, including PCR assays, provided results within 6 h, microfluidics platforms within 4 h, and PCT testing within 2 h.[5]

The diagnostic measures we used are diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive values (PPVs), negative predictive values (NPVs), and immediate diagnostic test’s turnaround time starting from sample collection to report generation and its influence on the clinician. The interventional study to assess examined differences in antibiotic starting, altering, and length, in cooperation with other clinical results that include NICU length of stay, mortality rate, and readmission rates. Data analysis was done using IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0 software for descriptive data: continuous data was presented with mean ± standard deviation while categorical data were presented in percentage. Student’s t-test was used to compare the means of the two groups while the chi-square test was used to compare proportions, but both at P < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

Neonatal sepsis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, with significant challenges in diagnosis and timely management, especially in low-resource settings. Conventional blood cultures, while considered the gold standard for pathogen detection, have significant limitations in terms of diagnostic delay and sensitivity. This study evaluated the accuracy, turnaround time, and clinical impact of rapid diagnostic methods, including PCR, microfluidics-based assays, and PCT levels, in comparison to conventional blood cultures. The findings of this study align with and expand upon previous literature, highlighting the potential benefits of these rapid diagnostics in improving patient outcomes and antibiotic stewardship.

Accuracy of rapid diagnostic methods: The diagnostic performance of the rapid methods in this study compared favorably with that of traditional blood cultures as observed in some earlier research studies. The PCR assays analyzed in the study were found to have a sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 89%, which is consistent with the finding of Marre van den Brand et al. (2018),[1] where the sensitivity of the PCR in diagnosing neonatal sepsis was 90% and that of Mohan Pammi et al. (2011)[3] where the sensitivity was 93%. In addition, the high NPV of PCR in this study, which is 94%, is similar to the results of other similar studies. For instance, Mohan Pammi et al. (2011)[3] established that PCR testing had higher sensitivity when used in sepsis diagnosis contrary to other conventional tests.

Microfluidics-based assays, with sensitivity and specificity of 88% and 91%, respectively, also showed promising results, consistent with studies by U K Mishra et al. (2006)[6] and Pui-Ying Iroh Tam et al. (2017),[4] who demonstrated the utility of microfluidic platforms in providing rapid, high-sensitivity pathogen detection in neonatal sepsis. However, the specificity of microfluidics-based assays in our study was slightly lower than that reported in some literature, which may be attributed to the complexity of sample handling and pathogen diversity in clinical settings.

PCT levels, although useful as an adjunctive diagnostic tool, showed lower sensitivity (85%) and specificity (54%) in this study. This result is consistent with the work of Istemi Han Celik et al. (2022),[7] who highlighted that while PCT is a valuable biomarker for sepsis detection, it lacks sufficient sensitivity to be used as a primary diagnostic tool, particularly in cases of localized infection or nonbacterial causes of sepsis.

Turnaround time comparison: The rapid turnaround time of diagnostic methods was one of the most striking findings of this study. PCR assays, microfluidics-based assays, and PCT testing provided results within 6, 4, and 2 h, respectively, compared to the 48–72 h required for conventional blood cultures. These results are in line with previous studies that emphasized the importance of rapid diagnostics in neonatal sepsis management. For instance, a study by Marre van den Brand et al. (2018)[1] demonstrated that the use of PCR-based diagnostics reduced turnaround times from 48 h to under 6 h, leading to faster clinical decision-making.

The significant reduction in turnaround time observed in this study was similarly noted by Mohan Pammi et al. (2018),[3] who compared the diagnostic performance of PCR assays and blood cultures in neonatal sepsis and found that PCR reduced time to diagnosis by more than 48 h, facilitating earlier therapeutic interventions. This rapid diagnostic advantage is particularly important in the NICU setting, where delays in diagnosis can lead to increased morbidity and mortality.

Impact on antibiotic stewardship: This work identified the effects of rapid diagnostic methods on antibiotic stewardship. Before the use of rapid diagnostics, 95% of neonates were given empiric antibiotic administration. This reduced to 70% after the use of rapid diagnostic methods; this is in concordance with other similar studies that include Istemi Han Celik et al. (2021),[7] where empirical antibiotic initiation had reduced from 90% to 68% of their study population after PCR. This is especially important in the setting of neonatal care owing to concerns related to antibiotic resistance that are governed by empirical antibiotic use.

Another aspect that experienced improvement was antibiotic de-escalation, rising from only 20% in the previous study, to 50%. Such findings are in accordance with other similar studies such as the one conducted by U K Mishra et al. (2006)[6] in which the authors showed not only the ability of the fast diagnostic methods to avoid unnecessary antibacterial introduction but also to enhance the optimal therapy narrowing once the particular pathogens are isolated. Molecular diagnostics allow the clinician to focus the choice of antibiotic therapy to a particular pathogen reducing the use of broad-spectrum agents and promoting the appropriate/adequate use of antibiotics.

Additionally, the duration of antibiotic therapy decreased from 8 days to 6 days after the introduction of rapid diagnostics. This reduction in therapy duration is in line with findings from studies by Pui-Ying Iroh Tam et al. (2017),[4] which showed that rapid diagnostics led to a reduction in antibiotic treatment duration by an average of 2–3 days, directly improving patient safety and reducing the likelihood of adverse drug reactions.

Clinical Outcomes: The clinical outcomes in this study showed a significant improvement after the introduction of rapid diagnostics. The mortality rate decreased from 15% to 10% (P = 0.01), which is consistent with the results of previous studies that have demonstrated improved survival rates in neonates with sepsis when rapid diagnostics are used. For instance, the study by Mohan Pammi et al. (2021)[3] showed a 5% reduction in mortality rates following the adoption of rapid PCR-based diagnostics in a neonatal sepsis cohort. This reduction in mortality can be attributed to the faster and more accurate diagnosis of sepsis, allowing for more timely and targeted therapy.

Similarly, the length of NICU stay decreased from 14 to 11 days (P = 0.03), aligning with results from a study by U K Mishra et al. (2006),[6] which reported a reduction in NICU length of stay from 16 to 12 days in patients diagnosed with sepsis using rapid diagnostic technologies. The shorter length of stay is likely a result of more effective infection control and earlier resolution of sepsis due to timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment.

The readmission rate also decreased from 8% to 5% (P = 0.05), a finding consistent with the work of Pui-Ying Iroh Tam et al. (2017),[4] who noted a decrease in readmission rates in neonatal sepsis patients who received rapid diagnostic testing. This improvement is likely due to the more precise targeting of pathogens and the associated reduction in treatment failures and recurrent infections.

CONCLUSION

This study offers substantial support for the use of rapid diagnostic methods in neonatal sepsis owing to high accuracy, reduced turnaround time, and positive effects on antibiotic stewardship as well as clinical outcomes in neonates. The presented data correlates with the findings of other authors and indicates the effectiveness of the determined methods to enhance the management of neonatal sepsis. For suspected sepsis in neonates, rapid diagnostics can be considered a beneficial application for the enhancement of care since mortality, length of stay, and units of antibiotics have all decreased considerably.

Future directions should involve multisite studies, cost-utility analysis, and identification of specific pathogens that these techniques can detect to guide future practice.
