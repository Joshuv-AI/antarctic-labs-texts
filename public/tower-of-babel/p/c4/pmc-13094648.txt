The effects of ultrasound-assisted glycosylation modification on zein protein structure and resveratrol grafting
Authors: Na Guo, Qingao Duan, Wenjun Liu, Yu Wang, Xiaoshuang Wang, Ling Li, Guilan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zein possesses a unique amphiphilic structure and excellent biocompatibility, making it a natural carrier of bioactive substances with significant development potential in the food sector. However, its extremely poor water solubility has limited its scope of application. This study aims to optimize the structure of zein through ultrasound-assisted glycosylation and resveratrol grafting. The results indicated that mild ultrasonic treatment effectively exposed active amino acid groups in zein, promoted the glycosylation degree of zein, improved protein solubility, and enhances the grafting efficiency of resveratrol. In particular, 100 W ultrasonic treatment resulted in the highest glycosylation degree (40.0%) and protein solubility (18.36 mg/mL) for zein, while reducing the hydrophobic index by 46%. Following resveratrol grafting, the glycosylated nanoparticles treated with 100 W sonication exhibited the smallest particle size (15.56 nm), the highest grafting degree (90.32%) and the maximum solubility (28.47 mg/mL). This modification induced the loss of ordered secondary structures (β–sheets) and yielded significantly higher 2,2′-azobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS•+) scavenging activity and 2,2- diphenyl-1-pyridylhydrazine (DPPH) scavenging activity activities compared with zein–resveratrol nanoparticles. Both glycosylation and resveratrol-grafting modifications rendered the zein surface smooth, with a continuous and dense structure. This study aims to provide a theoretical basis for the preparation process of zein nanoparticles modified by multiple methods and to expand their applications.

Introduction

With the increasing health consciousness of consumers, there is a growing demand for functional foods that not only meet nutritional needs but also provide antioxidant, antibacterial, and other health benefits. Resveratrol is a natural, non-flavonoid polyphenol extracted from plants such as grapes and peanuts that possesses excellent antioxidant, antioxidant, antibacterial, and antiviral effects and cardiovascular protective and metabolic regulatory properties, thus offering broad application prospects in the fields of functional foods and nutritional supplements [1], [2], [3]. However, as a hydrophobic polyphenol, resveratrol has low solubility and is susceptible to environmental influences, resulting in low bioavailability and poor stability. For example, it undergoes isomerization under the influence of light and enzymes, oxidizes at high temperatures and in the presence of oxygen, and is highly unstable in acidic or alkaline environments [4], [5], [6]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for an efficient and stable delivery system to address these application bottlenecks and expand its utility in the food and pharmaceutical industries.

Nanocarriers fabricated using encapsulation techniques, such as proteins and polysaccharides, are frequently used to protect resveratrol, thereby overcoming its stability and water solubility limitations. Nanoparticles can significantly increase the solubility, stability, and bioavailability of food ingredients due to their unique size and surface effects, and therefore, they may potentially optimize the functionalization of resveratrol, curcumin, luteolin, and other bioactive compounds [6], [7], [8]. Zein, a plant protein with green economic potential, self-assembles through hydrophobic interactions into nanoscale spherical particles, enabling it to efficiently encapsulate hydrophobic bioactive compounds. Compared to other proteins, such as whey and soy protein, zein exhibits a level of resistance to gastric acid degradation. When used in encapsulation delivery systems, it was shown to significantly enhance the encapsulation efficiency and stability of bioactive compounds while also providing targeted delivery characteristics [9], [10], [11], [12]. However, the highly hydrophobic nature and poor water solubility of zein pose significant challenges in achieving a uniform dispersion in aqueous media during food production and processing. This issue affects product stability and limits its use in food processing.

To address these limitations, various modification strategies have been extensively investigated. Among them, glycosylation induces mild unfolding of the protein structure and introduces hydroxyl groups, thereby improving its aqueous solubility. As reported in the literature, glycosylated zein nanoparticles exhibit excellent physicochemical properties, including high solubility, redispersibility, environmental stability, and storage stability [11], [12]. In one study, polydisperse nanocomposites formed by glycosylated zein loaded with lutein had an average particle size of less than 200 nm, with a higher encapsulation efficiency than that of ordinary zein, and they enhanced the in vitro antioxidant activity of lutein [13].

Notably, single-modification approaches cannot comprehensively optimize protein function. Ultrasonication, as a green and efficient processing aid, utilizes acoustic cavitation, generating localized high shear stress, microstreaming, and shockwaves that disrupt chemical bonds between protein peptide chains and expose internal amino acid groups. These effects increase the number of chemical sites available for glycosylation, enhance the degree of glycosylation, and accelerate the grafting reaction efficiency [14], [15], [16].

This study combined ultrasonic technology with glycosylation reactions to fabricate glycosylated zein and resveratrol-grafted nanoparticles, and it thoroughly investigated the effects of ultrasonic treatment on zein glycosylation and resveratrol grafting. The study aims to provide a theoretical basis for the preparation process of zein nanoparticles modified by multiple methods and broaden their application scope in related fields.

Materials and methods

Materials

Zein (protein content, 92%) and glucose (purity > 99%) were obtained from Hefei Bomei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hefei, China), and resveratrol (purity > 98%) was acquired from Beijing Solarbio Life Science Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All other reagents were analytical grade.

Methods

Ultrasound-assisted treatment of glucose-glycosylated zein

Glucose was selected for glycosylation in this study due to its single glycosylation site, high selectivity, and high bioavailability. Briefly, 25 g of zein was dissolved in 500 mL of 70% ethanol, and the pH was adjusted to 13.0. The zein solution was stirred at 25°C for 5 h and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and mixed with glucose (mass ratio of zein:glucose = 1:2) using a magnetic stirrer at 25°C for 3 h. The resulting solution (100 mL) was ultrasonicated using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (model SM-650D; Nanjing Sunmax Instrument Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). The ultrasonic probe had a diameter of 6 mm and was positioned 1 cm above the bottom of the liquid. Ultrasonic treatment was performed at power densities of 100, 200, 300, and 400 W/100 mL for 30 min (1 s:1 s work/rest cycles). The treated material was stored at 4°C in the refrigerator before further analysis.

A sample of the solution was placed in a 90°C water bath and reacted for 60 min [9]. After the reaction completed, the solution was immediately placed in ice water to terminate the reaction. The resulting glycosylation reaction product of zein, a zein-glucose (zein-g) solution, was stored in a 4°C refrigerator for later use.

Preparation of a glycosylated zein-resveratrol solution mediated by tannic acid

Briefly, 50 mL of the zein-g solution (protein concentration, 22 mg/mL) was mixed with 0.11 g of tannic acid powder and magnetically stirred at 600 rpm and 25°C for 30 min. Then, 0.132 g of resveratrol powder was added to the mixture, which was again magnetically stirred at 600 rpm and 25°C for 30 min. The resulting solution was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected.

Preparation of nanoparticles

Dispersing zein in an aqueous solution at pH 4.0 imparts a net positive charge to the solution surface and produces small, uniform nanoparticles while preventing molecular aggregation [17], [18]. Here, 4 mL of the tannic acid-mediated glycosylated zein-resveratrol solution was rapidly injected using a syringe into 18.0 mL of pH 4.0 ultrapure water while stirring at 800 rpm. Next, the ethanol in the sample was removed by rotary evaporation, and the sample was replenished to 22 mL using pH 4.0 ultrapure water to complete the zein-g-res nanoparticle preparation. Similarly, zein-g and additional zein-g-res nanoparticles were prepared by the same method using different ultrasonic power levels: zein-g-100w, zein-g-200w, zein-g-300w, zein-g-400w, zein-g-100w-res, zein-g-200w-res, zein-g-300w-res, and zein-g-400w-res nanoparticles.

Analysis of the nanoparticle properties

Determination of turbidity

The glycosylated zein and its nanoparticle dispersions were analyzed using colorimetry by dispensing into 10-mL transparent glass vials and photographing under natural light. Additionally, the absorbance of each nanoparticle dispersion at 600 nm (UV1800, Shimadzu Spectrophotometer, Kyoto, Japan) was determined using a spectrophotometer with the solvent as a reference.

Determination of the degree of browning

The glycosylated zein samples were assessed using a visible spectrophotometer (UV1800, Shimadzu, Japan) to determine the absorbance at 290 and 420 nm.

Determination of the degree of grafting

The degree of grafting of the glycosylated zein was determined using the o-phthalaldehyde (OPA) method, with the OPA reagent prepared as previously described by Wen et al. [19]. In the analysis, 70% ethanol was used as a blank control. Each treatment sample was prepared by mixing 4 mL of OPA reagent with 200 μL of the sample solution. The samples were incubated in a water bath at 35°C for 3 min, and their absorbance was measured at 340 nm.

A 0.5 mg/mL lysine solution was used to construct a standard curve to obtain a standard equation. The mass concentration of the free amino group in the sample was calculated as follows:(1)Degreeofgrafting=C0-C1C0×100%

where C0 and C1 are the mass concentrations of free amino acids in the solution (mg/mL) before and after modification, respectively.

Determination of hydrophobicity

The hydrophobicity of the samples was determined by an 8-anilino-1-naphthalene sulfonate (ANS) fluorescent probe, using a method adapted from that of Hayakawa with modifications [20]. The glycosylated zein and its nanoparticle dispersions were diluted to 0.02, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL. Then, 2 mL of each diluted sample was mixed with 20 μL of 8 mmol/L ANS, and the mixture was incubated for 15 min at 25°C in the dark.

The fluorescence intensity of each resulting solution was measured at an excitation wavelength of 390 nm and an emission scan from 400 to 600 nm with a fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF5301, Shimadz, Kyoto, Japan). The fluorescence intensity was plotted with the protein concentration on the x-axis, and the slope of the regression curve was used to calculate the surface hydrophobicity (H0).

Uv–visible spectroscopy scanning

The glycosylated zein and its nanoparticle dispersions were appropriately diluted. Next, 3 mL of each diluted sample was analyzed to determine its UV absorption spectrum in the wavelength range of 200–500 nm.

Fluorescence spectral scanning

The glycosylated zein and its nanoparticle dispersions were diluted to 0.5 mg/mL. The fluorescence spectra of each sample were obtained by scanning at an excitation wavelength of 280 nm and an emission wavelength range of 290–450 nm.

Determination of protein solubility

The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method was used to determine protein solubility. Briefly, each nanoparticle dispersion was appropriately diluted with phosphate-buffered solution (PBS). Then, 200 μL of the diluted sample was mixed with 2 mL of BCA working solution. The solution was mixed thoroughly using a vortex mixer and heated for 20 min in a water bath at 37°C. The absorbance of the mixture was measured at 562 nm using a visible spectrophotometer. Protein solubility was calculated using a standard curve constructed from a bovine serum albumin concentration gradient.

Particle size and zeta potential determination

Each nanoparticle dispersion was diluted with distilled water (pH 4.0) to a protein concentration of 1 mg/mL, and the particle size and zeta potential were determined by a nano-laser particle sizer instrument (90Plus, Nanophox, Sympatec Co., Ltd.) at 25°C.

2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging rate

The DPPH radical scavenging rates of the nanoparticle dispersions were determined as previously described by Huang et al. with slight modifications [21]. Briefly, each sample was moderately diluted with distilled water (pH 4.0). Then, 2 mL of the diluted sample was mixed with 2 mL of 0.1 mol/L DPPH ethanol solution and reacted for 30 min at 25°C in the dark. The absorbance of the resulting solution was determined at 517 nm using a visible spectrophotometer, with distilled water (pH 4.0) as the control. The scavenging rate of the DPPH radical is calculated as follows:(2)DPPHscavengingrate(%)=Ac- (As-Ab)Ac×100%

where Ac is the absorbance of the control, As is the absorbance of the sample mixture, and Ab is the absorbance of the sample solution without the DPPH ethanol solution.

2,2′-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS•+) radical scavenging rate

The ABTS•+ radical scavenging rate of each nanoparticle dispersion was determined as previously described by Lin et al. with modifications [22]. Briefly, 7 mmol/L of ABTS•+ base solution and 4.9 mmol/L of potassium persulfate solution were prepared, and equal volumes of each were mixed. The mixture was incubated for 12 h in the dark to obtain the ABTS•+ stock solution. The ABTS•+ working solution was prepared by diluting the ABTS•+ stock solution with 10 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.0) until its absorbance at 734 nm reached 0.70 ± 0.02.

Each test sample was moderately diluted with distilled water (pH 4.0). Then, 0.5 mL of the diluted sample was mixed with 2 mL of ABTS•+ working solution, and the mixture reacted for 6 min at 25°C in the dark. The ABTS•+ working solution was used as the control. The absorbance of each sample was measured at 734 nm using a visible spectrophotometer. The ABTS•+ radical scavenging rate was calculated as follows:(3)ABTS∙+scavengingrate(%)=Ac- (As-Ab)Ac×100%

where Ac is the absorbance of the control, As is the absorbance of the sample mixture, and Ab is the absorbance of the sample solution without the ABTS•+ working solution.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Samples were freeze-dried using a freeze-dryer. The powdered samples were pressed and assessed by a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) in the wavelength range of 400–4000 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The morphology of the nanoparticles in the lyophilized samples was explored using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi High-tech Co., Ltd.) at an accelerating voltage of 10.0 kV. To overcome the poor electrical conductivity of the samples, the surface of each sample was sprayed with gold prior to observation.

Thermogravimetry (TGA)

The thermal properties of the samples were determined using TGA (SDT650, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA). Briefly, 7–9 mg of each sample was placed in a crucible and heated from 35°C to 500°C at a constant rate of 10°C/min under a controlled nitrogen flow (100 mL/min).

SDS–PAGE analysis

SDS–PAGE analysis was performed using a 15% separating gel and a 5% stacking gel system. First, 15 μL of the protein sample at a concentration of 1 mg/mL was denatured and loaded, followed by electrophoresis at a constant voltage of 120 V. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R250, then destained, and imaged.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Data processing and graphical visualization were performed mainly using SPSS、Peakfit v4.12 and Origin 2025 software. Analysis of variance was carried out using SPSS, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. ANOVA was performed to identify significant variations among the treatment groups, followed by post hoc tests for multiple comparisons of the treated group means.

Results and discussion

Effect of ultrasound on the properties of the zein-g solutions

SDS–PAGE analysis

SDS–PAGE was used to analyze the glycosylation effect of zein and the influence of ultrasonic treatment on the glycosylation process. As shown in Fig. 1, the bands of the glycosylated samples shifted slightly upward, indicating the successful grafting of glucose onto zein via glycosylation. Among these, the molecular weight of glycosylated zein treated at 100 W ultrasound increased most significantly, whereas further increasing the ultrasonic power did not result in a pronounced change in molecular weight. This result may be attributed to moderate ultrasonic treatment altering the spatial conformation of the protein, exposing more amino acid residues and thereby enhancing the binding interaction between the protein and polysaccharide molecules [23].Fig. 1SDS-PAGE of the glycosylation zein.

Turbidity of the glycosylated zein solution

The Maillard reaction is inherently accompanied by pigment formation, and the extent of this reaction positively correlates with the color intensity of the reaction system [24]. Therefore, the glycosylation efficiency of the samples was evaluated by visual colorimetry and turbidity measurements. As shown in Fig. 2A, the solutions that underwent ultrasonication exhibited a significantly deeper color than those processed using the traditional wet heat method. The solutions subjected to ultrasonication at 100 W exhibited the darkest color. As the ultrasonic power was further increased, however, the color intensity of the solutions gradually decreased. As the ultrasonic power increased, the turbidity initially rose and then fell, reaching a maximum at 100 W. This suggested that ultrasonication exposed more free amino groups, which increased the chances of collisions occurring between the zein and sugar molecules, promoting the formation of Maillard reaction products and generating a large amount of polymers. Excessively high ultrasonic power (400 W) caused protein molecules to re-aggregate, which decreased the content of free amino groups and reduced the extent of the Maillard reaction.

Analysis of the degrees of browning and grafting

The absorbance at 420 nm and 290 nm reflects the degree of non-enzymatic browning and the amount of early browning products formed [25]. As shown in Fig. 2B, the absorbance at the two wavelengths indicated that the ultrasonically treated samples had markedly enhanced non-enzymatic browning and more early browning products compared to untreated samples. The absorbance was the highest for the solution subjected to ultrasonication at 100 W, and the solution absorbance decreased as the ultrasonic power increased. An analysis of the degree of grafting (Fig. 2C) revealed that the grafting of the solutions increased after ultrasonication and reached a maximum of 39.82% with 100-W ultrasonication. This finding indicated that an appropriate ultrasonic power exposed more free amino groups due to the ultrasonic cavitation effect, which exposed more grafting active sites. In addition, ultrasonication enhanced the collision opportunities between zein and glucose molecules, promoting grafting reactions [26], [27]. When the power was < 200 W, intense cavitation effects and shear stresses disrupted the polypeptide chains of the maize protein, reducing the number of active glycosylation sites. This may have interfered with the initial stages of the Maillard reaction, thus inhibiting browning and limiting advanced glycation reactions. This reduced the turbidity, degree of browning, and degree of grafting [28].

Hydrophobicity analysis

The untreated zein had high surface hydrophobicity, as shown in Fig. 2D, which decreased after glycosylation and ultrasonication. As the ultrasonic power increased, the hydrophobicity index of zein first decreased and then increased. The solution after ultrasonication at 100 W had the lowest hydrophobicity index, which was ∼ 46% lower than that of the untreated zein. This finding was attributed to the glycosylation reaction, which introduced polar groups such as the hydroxyl (–OH) and aldehyde (–CHO) groups of glucose onto zein, forming a hydrophilic outer layer that reduced its hydrophobicity [28]. In addition, ultrasonic treatment increased the number of exposed free amino groups, such as the ε-amino group of the lysine, thereby exposing more grafting sites, which promoted the Maillard reaction and introduced more sugar chains that reduced the hydrophobicity [29].

Uv–visible spectral scanning analysis

The effects of ultrasonication on the conformational changes of glycosylated zein were investigated by UV absorption spectroscopy. Zein exhibited significant UV absorption at 278 nm, which was primarily attributed to the conjugated double bond structures of tryptophan and tyrosine [30]. As shown in Fig. 2E, at around 280 nm, the absorption intensity of glycosylated zein was higher than that of zein. Furthermore, the UV absorption of the samples gradually increased as the ultrasonic power increased, reaching a maximum at 100 W, and then gradually decreased. This finding was attributed to the combined effect of ultrasound and glycosylation on the conformation of zein, in which zein grafted to glucose molecules through covalent bonding. This changed the protein’s spatial structure, exposing aromatic amino acids (originally buried inside zein molecules) on the molecular surface, which enhanced UV absorption at 280 nm [31]. In addition, cavitation effects and mechanical forces of moderate ultrasound further contributed to changes in the protein structure and enhanced the protein UV absorption intensity. However, excessive ultrasonic power destroyed the protein structure, which caused the proteins to aggregate with reduced UV absorption [26].

Fluorescence spectral scanning analysis

In glycosylation reactions, the introduction of sugar chains may quench the fluorescence emitted by proteins. The fluorescence spectrum of the zein-g solutions clearly depicted the effect of the interactions between the protein and glucose molecules. As shown in Fig. 2F, zein and zein-g exhibited an intense fluorescence emission peak at approximately 310 nm, which originated from the tyrosine residues in zein [32]. The fluorescence intensities of the zein–g solutions were significantly lower than that of zein. This was attributed to the consumption of tyrosine during the Maillard reaction, which weakened the tyrosine residue-based fluorescence emission [33]. The fluorescence quenching effect caused by the ultrasonic treatment varied considerably with ultrasonic power, with the strongest effect observed at 100 W. Although the position of the emission peaks did not change significantly after zein glycosylation, their fluorescence intensity decreased significantly. This was attributed to zein-g forming through covalent bonding, which altered the polarity of the microenvironment surrounding the tryptophan and tyrosine residues in the protein. This change in polarity shielded the tryptophan and tyrosine residues, consequently reducing the fluorescence intensity. Our findings were consistent with the results of Li et al., who reported that Maillard reaction products induced fluorescence quenching of proteins [34]. Further, the ultrasound-induced quenching of zein fluorescence was most pronounced at 100 W; this could be attributed to the distribution of highly grafted glucose molecules around zein, thereby reducing the detectable level of its autofluorescence [35], [36].

Analysis of the zein-g and zein-g-res nanoparticles

Nanoparticle turbidity

Fig. 3 shows that the color of the zein-g nanoparticle dispersion was deeper than that of the zein nanoparticle dispersion, and its turbidity was also higher. As the ultrasonic power increased, the color intensity and turbidity of the zein-g nanoparticle dispersion first increased and then decreased, with a maximum at 100 W. In contrast, the color of the zein-g-res nanoparticle dispersion first lightened and then deepened as the ultrasonic power increased, and its turbidity first increased and then decreased. Furthermore, the nanoparticle dispersion was homogeneous with no precipitation. This finding was attributed to both the increased degree of glycosylation and molecular cross-linking induced by ultrasonication at low power and the glycosylation reaction. These changes increased the degree of glycosylation, which introduced more glucose chains on the molecule surfaces. Consequently, the particle size and turbidity increased. In contrast, high-power ultrasonication reduced the solution turbidity. This was due to the high shear forces breaking apart aggregates and homogenizing the solution [37]. The higher turbidity of the zein-g-res nanoparticle dispersions compared to the zein-g nanoparticle dispersions under the same treatment conditions was attributed to the hydrophobic interaction between resveratrol and zein, which restricted nanoparticle aggregation and thereby enhanced the light scattering effect [38].Fig. 3Colorimetric diagram and the A600 of the zein-g nanoparticles, and zein-g-res nanoparticles.

As shown in Fig. 4B, the hydrophobicity of the zein-g nanoparticles first decreased and then increased as the ultrasonic power increased. However, it consistently remained lower than that of the natural zein nanoparticles, with a minimum value of 235.62 at 100 W. The hydrophobicity of the zein-g-res nanoparticles was higher than that of the zein-g nanoparticles. The hydrophobicity of the zein-g-res nanoparticles also decreased and then increased as the ultrasonic power increased, with a minimum value at 200 W. The introduction of sugar chains increased the hydrophilicity of zein, and the glycosylation reaction was promoted by moderate ultrasonic power [28]. However, excessive ultrasonic power completely disrupted the protein conformation, which attenuated the glycosylation reaction and further increased the molecule hydrophobicity. These results were consistent with previous reports on the degree of glycosylation and solubility of zein. Resveratrol is strongly hydrophobic, which caused resveratrol to diffuse into the hydrophobic nuclei of the protein when the nanoparticles were prepared by the non-solvent method. However, the diffusion rate of resveratrol was slower than the protein precipitation rate, and resveratrol was therefore retained on the nanoparticle surface. These exposed hydrophobic regions increased the hydrophobicity of the nanoparticles [39].

Protein solubility analysis

Fig. 4C shows that the solubility of zein was 5.3 mg/mL. After glycosylation, the solubility of zein first increased and then decreased as the ultrasonic power increased, and it was consistently higher than the solubility of the pure protein. The maximum solubility was 18.4 mg/mL at 100 W, about four-fold higher than that of pure zein. The protein solubility of the zein-g-res nanoparticles exhibited a similar pattern. Ultrasonication exposed more free amino groups for the subsequent glycosylation reaction and increased contact between zein and glucose. However, excessively high power likely damaged the protein structure, which was unfavorable to the reaction [19]. The solubility of proteins in the zein-g-res nanoparticles was higher than that in the zein-g nanoparticles produced by the same treatment. This difference was attributed to a resveratrol-mediated increase in protein polarity and the formation of a hydrophilic shell–hydrophobic core structure, which indirectly increased the protein solubility. These findings agreed with the results of Chang et al., who reported that the water solubility of glycosylated zein protein nanocarriers increased after lutein loading [14].

Uv–visible spectral analysis

By comparing Fig. 1E and Fig. 4D, it is found that zein and zein-g exhibited strong absorption at 280 nm, with the UV absorption peak of the resveratrol–grafted zein dispersions appearing at ∼ 310 nm. This finding indicated that the addition of resveratrol red-shifted the absorption peak of the protein. This may be attributed to the effect of binding with resveratrol (maximum absorption peak at 306 nm) [40]. The UV absorption intensity of all the samples, except for the resveratrol-grafted glycosylated zein nanoparticles changed significantly compared with that of zein. However, the UV absorption intensity of the glycosylated zein gradually increased as the ultrasonic power increased, reaching a maximum at 100 W, and then decreased. These results suggested that the binding of resveratrol affected the zein structure by exposing previously buried hydrophobic groups or altering surface-exposed secondary structures. However, ultrasonication may also affect the resveratrol loading, as indicated by the altered UV absorption spectra.

Analysis of the fluorescence spectra

As shown in Fig. 4E and F, at an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, the zein-g and zein-g-res nanoparticles produced strong fluorescence emission peaks at ∼ 310 and ∼ 390 nm, respectively. A red shift in the maximum emission wavelength of the nanoparticles was observed after adding resveratrol. This finding was attributed to the increased polar groups in the protein microenvironment [8]. In addition, the zein-g nanoparticles subjected to ultrasonication coupled with glycosylation exhibited fluorescence quenching. As the ultrasonic power increased, the bursting effect first strengthened and then weakened, with a maximum at 100 W. This trend was consistent with the fluorescence bursting effect of the zein-g solutions.

The zein-g-res nanoparticle fluorescence scanning did not show a fluorescence emission peak at ∼ 310 nm but revealed a strong fluorescence emission peak at ∼ 390 nm. The fluorescence intensity of the zein-g-res nanoparticle emission peak first increased and then decreased as the ultrasonic power increased, with a maximum at 100 W. At powers greater than 100 W, the fluorescence intensity of the particles gradually decreased, with the fluorescence intensity at 400 W lower than that of the resveratrol-loaded zein nanoparticles. In addition, the maximum emission wavelength of the nanoparticles also exhibited a red shift at 100 and 200 W. The complete disappearance of the fluorescence intensity of the protein in the zein-g-res nanoparticles at 310 nm was caused by fluorescence quenching due to the presence of resveratrol [36]. At 390 nm, the fluorescence intensity first increased and then decreased as the ultrasonic power increased. This was because as the ultrasonic power increased, the loading of resveratrol first increased and then decreased. In addition, the hydrophobic zein core provided a hydrophobic environment for resveratrol. The resveratrol loading and more stable hydrophobic microenvironment of the nanoparticles may underlie the significant increase in their fluorescence intensity [41].

The zein-res nanoparticles exhibited an irregular morphology, an uneven surface, and significant agglomeration of large particles, while the zein-g-res nanoparticles had clear contours, a smooth surface, and a low degree of agglomeration. After ultrasonic treatment, the surface of the zein-g-100w-res nanoparticles was smoother, its structure was uniform and continuous, and it exhibited no apparent agglomeration. As the ultrasonic power increased, the surface roughness of the zein-g-400w-res nanoparticles increased, fragments were produced, and the degree of agglomeration increased. Samples containing resveratrol had a denser and smoother surface, with fewer folds and smaller pores. This finding suggested that glycosylation alone and moderate ultrasonic treatment strengthened intermolecular interactions and helped form a tight network structure. Moderate ultrasonic treatment may have improved the uniformity of contact between glucose and zein through mechanical shear forces and cavitation effects. This treatment may have also caused structures to form with a denser degree of cross-linking via glycosylation reactions. However, excessive ultrasonication disrupted the zein molecules, producing fragments of varying sizes. Excessive fragmentation may have induced agglomeration during the glycosylation reaction due to the exposed surface groups. A lower cross-linking efficiency than the degree of agglomeration may have introduced structural disorder in the protein [36].

FTIR analysis

Zein did not show a characteristic peak at 1050 cm−1, while all the glycosylated samples had a characteristic peak at this wavelength from Fig. 7. These peaks were attributed to the stretching vibration of the C–O–C structure in the glycosidic bond formed between zein and glucose at 1050 cm−1 [44]. The intensities of the peaks at specific wavenumbers in the infrared spectrum were mainly related to the content of the corresponding group and the extent of the dipole moment change during vibration. According to the magnified view of Fig. 7B showing the infrared spectra of the zein-g nanoparticles around 1050 cm−1, the peak intensity of the zein-g-100w nanoparticles was the highest. This indicated that the glycosylation product obtained through ultrasonication at 100 W combined with glycosylation treatment contained the most O-glycosidic bonds and underwent a greater dipole moment change. Therefore, the results showed that the combination of ultrasonication at 100 W and glycosylation treatment was the most effective treatment.Fig. 7Fourier transform infrared spectra of each nanoparticle: A and B zein-g nanoparticles, C zein-g-res nanoparticles, D Secondary structure analysis.

This result was consistent with the degree of glycosylation of zein (39.82%, as shown in Fig. 1C), its low hydrophobicity (Fig. 1D), and its high protein solubility (Fig. 4C), indicating that moderate ultrasonic treatment effectively enhanced zein glycosylation. Furthermore, moderate ultrasonic treatment promoted binding between zein and resveratrol, effectively improving the antioxidant properties of the protein.

In the secondary structure of proteins, the stretching vibration of the amide I band (1700–1600 cm-1 ) reflects changes in the secondary structure. Analysis of the secondary structure indicates that the proportion of α-helices in zein decreases and the proportion of β-sheets increases, which may be attributed to conformational changes induced by the glycosylation process. The effects of ultrasonic treatment on glycosylated zein were not significantly different. In contrast, the binding of zein with resveratrol led to a significant increase in the β-sheet proportion, likely resulting from structural rearrangement caused by polyphenol-induced structural changes, thereby rendering the structure more ordered. Under 100 W ultrasonic treatment, the proportions of α-helices and β-turns increased the most, and the proportion of β-sheets was the lowest. This clearly indicates that the treatment altered the structure of the zein protein.

Thermogravimetric analysis

As shown in Fig. 8, the thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal gravimetric analysis (DTGA) curves of zein and zein-res nanoparticles exhibit a two-stage mass loss pattern. In the initial stage (50°C–100°C), the mass significantly decreased, primarily due to the loss of moisture and volatile substances [35]. In the second stage (250°C–400°C), all samples underwent substantial mass loss, which was attributed to low-energy intermolecular bonds breaking during zein degradation. High-temperature mass loss is associated with protein thermal degradation. The DTGA profiles of the glycosylated zein-g and zein-g-res samples exhibited three distinct stages, with an additional substantial mass loss peak observed at 150°C–250°C, indicating the formation of glycosylation products. The mass loss associated with this peak stemmed from structural changes following the cleavage of glycosidic bonds between zein and glucose (Fig. 8).Fig. 8TGA and DTGA of each nanoparticles: A and B zein-g nanoparticles, C and D zein-g-res nanoparticles.

The TG curves indicated that zein had the highest weight loss rate, with a residue of 16.37% at 500°C. The maximum thermal degradation temperature of glycosylated zein was 305°C, which was lower than that of zein (320°C). Ultrasonic treatment slightly improved the thermal stability of glycosylated zein.

Among the nanoparticles grafted with resveratrol, the maximum degradation temperatures were all reduced, with zein-res exhibiting the lowest maximum degradation temperature of 293.5℃. This suggests that the addition of polyphenols causes protein chain denaturation and structural relaxation, and the hydroxyl groups introduced by resveratrol also lead to a reduction in the thermal degradation temperature. In this case, the maximum thermal degradation temperature of glycosylated zein increased significantly, and ultrasonic treatment slightly improved its thermal stability. The maximum thermal degradation temperature of glycosylated zein loaded with resveratrol was significantly increased, and ultrasonic treatment slightly improved its thermal stability.

Conclusion

In this study, ultrasound-assisted glycosylation was used to modify zein and prepare resveratrol-loaded nanoparticles. The results revealed that ultrasonic treatment was a key enhancer of both the structure and function of the protein. Ultrasonic treatment significantly increased the Maillard reaction degree of grafting, while the addition of resveratrol also contributed to forming smaller sized nanoparticles with improved dispersion. Consequently, ultrasonic treatment significantly optimized the glycosylation process and nanoparticle performance, providing a new strategy for generating high-performance bioactive delivery systems.
