Omega-3 fatty acids improve lipid metabolism by regulating miR-34a
Authors: Luxuan Li, Yale Tang, Xiaoyu Wang, Chao Wang, Guangyao Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipid metabolism disorders and hepatic lipid deposition are major contributors to the development of metabolic diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Omega-3 fatty acids have been shown to exert beneficial effects on lipid homeostasis and liver function, but their molecular regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we investigated the effects of omega-3 fatty acids on lipid metabolism and hepatic lipid deposition in addition to the modulatory role of miR-34a using an in vivo high-fat diet (HFD) model and an in vitro palmitic acid (PA)-induced HepG2 cell model. We assessed the effects of omega-3 fatty acid intervention and measured lipid levels in mouse serum, triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels in the mouse liver, and TG levels in HepG2 cells. Further, we evaluated the mRNA and protein levels of miR-34a, SIRT1, SREBP-1c, CPT-1 A and PGC-1α. We observed that miR-34a expression was upregulated in the HFD and PA groups. Omega-3 fatty acid intervention reduced the serum levels of TG, TC, and low-density lipoproteins(LDL-C), and decreased the levels of TG and TC in the liver tissue, alleviating hepatocyte steatosis. Further, omega-3 fatty acids intervention reduced miR-34a and SREBP-1c expression, and increased the expression of SIRT1,PGC-1α, CPT-1 A. Moreover, miR-34a overexpression inhibited the effects of omega-3 fatty acids on SIRT1, SREBP-1c, PGC-1α and CPT-1 A. Thus, omega-3 fatty acids promote lipid metabolism, thereby reducing hepatic lipid deposition by down-regulating miR-34a expression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43353-7.

Introduction

Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids consisting of α-Linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). Research has shown that dietary supplementation with omega-3 fatty acids, regardless of their content, experimental model, or dietary type, consistently decreased serum TG. Moreover, omega-3 fatty acids demonstrate significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, improving insulin resistance and reducing the risk of cardiovascular events. Recent studies have shown that omega-3 fatty acids can reduce hepatic lipid deposition in patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and in animal models of high-fat diet (HFD)-induced hepatic fat accumulation1,2. Although numerous studies have demonstrated the benefits of omega-3 fatty acids, their role in regulating liver-related genes remains controversial. Abnormal hepatic lipid deposition can lead to various diseases, including NAFLD, hyperlipidaemia, metabolic syndrome, and atherosclerosis3. Prompt detection and effective evaluation of hepatic lipid deposition using reliable biomarkers are essential for preventing disease progression.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenous non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression in eukaryotes by binding to target genes through base pairing and, depending on the degree of complementary base pairing, degrade target genes or inhibit their translation, thereby exerting post-transcriptional regulatory effects4. Several miRNAs have been shown to regulate the expression of genes related to lipid metabolism5, and a meta-analysis of multiple independent NAFLD studies found significantly increased miR-34a levels in the serum and liver of patients with NAFLD6.

Silencing information regulator sirtuin 1 (silent mating-type information regulation 2 homologue 1; SIRT1) is a multifunctional transcriptional regulator of NAD+-dependent histone deacetylase activity and is a notable miR-34a gene target7. SIRT1 regulates multiple metabolic pathways, including those that regulate lifespan and those involved in glucose metabolism and insulin secretion8–12. Increased SIRT1 expression downregulates fatty acid synthesis genes including SREBP-1c and FASN, and upregulates fatty acid oxidation genes including PPARα, CPT-1 A, and PGC-1α13,14 genes involved in the pathophysiology of hepatic fat deposition.

In this study, we investigated the effects of omega-3 fatty acids on lipid metabolism and the mechanisms underlying the regulation of related genes in the liver by establishing an HFD model and administering omega-3 fatty acids as an intervention. We measured the expression of miR-34a and lipid metabolism–related genes in the HFD model to further elucidate the mechanisms by which omega-3 fatty acids regulate lipid metabolism and hepatic lipid deposition, thereby identifying potential targets for improving lipid metabolism.

Materials and methods

Mouse HFD model

We purchased 8-week-old SPF male C57BL/6J mice from Shanghai Nanfang Model Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (licence number: SCXK (Shanghai) 2019-0002). It was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hebei Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval Research Ethics Review 2024-DW-004,Approval date: 22 February 2024). All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Animals were raised in a barrier system at the Clinical Medical Research Centre of Hebei Provincial People’s Hospital under a regulated room temperature (23–25 °C), with a relative humidity of ~ 60%,12 h light/12 h dark cycle, and ad libitum access to food and water. After 1 week of adaptive feeding, animals were randomly divided into two groups: a control group (CON, n = 9) and a high-fat diet group (HFD, n = 36). The CON group was given normal diet (caloric composition: protein 19.2%, carbohydrates 67.3%, fat 4.3%, caloric 3.85 kcal/g), and the HFD group was given a high-fat diet (caloric composition: protein 26.2%, carbohydrates 26.3%, fat 34.9%, calorics 5.24 kcal/g). The animals were then raised for 12 weeks, with weekly body weight measurements during the feeding period. After 12 weeks of feeding, the animals were fasted but not deprived of water for 12 h, and blood was collected from the tail vein.

Drug intervention

Omega-3 fatty acids were derived from Omacor® (Abbott Company, USA). Generic name: ω−3 fatty acid ethyl ester, 1 g per capsule, each capsule contains at least 900 mg of ω−3 fatty acid ethyl ester, among which EPA ethyl ester (EPA) is 465 mg and DHA ethyl ester (EPA) is 375 mg (drug approval number: HJ20220042; product batch number: 33690101).

The HFD group was further randomly sub-divided into four groups: the HFD + LDO group (HFD + low-dose Omacor, n = 9), the HFD + MDO group (HFD + medium-dose Omacor, n = 9), the HFD + HDO group (HFD + medium-dose Omacor, n = 9), and HFD group (HFD, n = 9). Carboxymethyl cellulose solution was used as the carrier to dissolve Omacor, and a 0.1 mL Omacor solution was prepared (the Omacor solution in the HFD + LDO group contained 300 mg/kg Omacor per 0.1 mL, the Omacor solution in the HFD + MDO group contained 600 mg/kg Omacor per 0.1 mL, and the Omacor solution in the HFD + HDO group contained 1200 mg/kg Omacor per 0.1 mL), and was administered intragastrically daily. The CON and HFD groups were administered 0.1 mL of carboxymethyl cellulose solution daily for 8 weeks.

Sample collection

The mice were fasted, but not deprived of water for 12 h overnight, followed by body weights measurements. Pentobarbital sodium (2%) was injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 450 mg/kg to euthanasia of animals. The average weight of mice is 40.72 ± 5.45 g.The eyeballs were removed, and blood was collected in collection tubes. The mice were fixed and disinfected. The abdomen was opened along the midline of the mouse body, and the liver was quickly removed. The liver was rinsed with a pre-cooled 0.9% sodium chloride solution, and a small piece was placed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The remaining tissues were divided into cryotubes and rapidly frozen with liquid nitrogen, then transferred to a −80 °C refrigerator for storage. After sample collection, the tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm/min for 15 min. The upper serum was collected using a pipette and aliquoted into microcentrifuge tubes, and stored at −80 °C until use.

Determination of serum biochemical parameters

Serum levels of TG, TC, LDL-C, and High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were determined using their respective assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Institute). Blood glucose levels were measured using a blood glucose assay kit (Yeasen). Insulin levels were determined using a mouse ultrasensitive insulin ELISA kit (ALPCO Company) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Insulin sensitivity was determined using the insulin sensitivity index (quantitative insulin sensitivity check index, QUICKI) using the following formula: QUICK value = 1/(lgFINS + lgFPG).

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT)

The IPGTT was conducted at weeks 12 and 20 (after 8 weeks of omega-3 fatty acid intervention). After a 12 h overnight fast without water restriction, the bedding was changed to avoid interference from faeces and other factors in subsequent experiments. The body weights of mice were measured, and 2 g/kg glucose saline (50% glucose solution: 0.9% sodium chloride solution, 1:1 ratio) was intravenously injected into via intraperitoneal at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min. Blood was collected from the tail vein at these time points, and the corresponding blood glucose levels were measured using a Johnson and Johnson blood glucose meter. The area under the glucose curve (AUC) of the IPGTT was calculated.

Measurement of TG and TC in liver tissue

The liver tissue was mechanically homogenized in an ice-water bath using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as the homogenization medium. Protein content of the homogenates was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Institute). TG and TC levels in the homogenates were determined using TG and TC kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Institute), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Liver histopathological examination

The liver tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Oil Red O, followed by image acquisition using a microscope.

Western Blotting

Protein samples were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein detection kit (Subkon Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was then blocked with QuickBlock Western blocking solution (Shanghai Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) at 4 °C and incubated with the following antibodies: mouse β-actin monoclonal (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, (CST), Danvers, MA, USA), rabbit SIRT1 monoclonal (1:1000, CST), rabbit CPT-1 A monoclonal (1:1000, Abcam, UK), rabbit SREBP-1c monoclonal (1:1000, Abcam), and rabbit PGC-1α monoclonal (1:1000, Abcam) overnight. The membrane was then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10000, Beijing Kangwei Century Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) for 60 min. Immunodetection was performed using an ECL developing solution (Chengdu Zhengeng Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and protein bands were detected using an imaging system (NIKON DS-U3, Nikon, Japan). β-actin was used as the internal reference.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) detection

Total RNA and miRNAs from liver tissues were extracted using the miRcute miRNA Extraction Kit (Beijing Tiangen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Total RNA and miRNAs were reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the FastKing cDNA First-Chain Synthesis Kit and the miRcute Enhanced miRNA cDNA First-Chain Synthesis Kit (Beijing Tiangen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Subsequently, real-time PCR was performed using the SuperReal Fluorescence Quantitative Premix Reagent Enhancement Kit and the miRcute Enhanced miRNA Fluorescence Quantitative Detection Kit (Beijing Tiangen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and the results were detected using an ABI7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). β-actin was used as the internal reference for mRNA quantification and U6 was used as the internal reference for miRNA quantification. The mRNA and miRNA sequences are shown in Tables 1 and 2, and 3. The relative expression levels of miRNAs and mRNAs were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method.

Cell culture and treatment

HepG2 cells were purchased from the National Cell Resource Centre (China). Cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% foetal bovine serum, 1% non-essential amino acids, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. To establish the high-fat model, confluent cells were treated with the culture medium (without serum) containing 300 µM palmitic acid (PA). Cells treated with the culture medium (without serum) alone were used as the CON group.

Cell proliferation and toxicity evaluation

A cell proliferation toxicity detection kit (CCK8) was used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of EPA. EPA was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to form an EPA stock solution of 100 mM. Working solutions were prepared by diluting the stock solution in serum-free medium at different ratios. HepG2 cells were treated with 10, 50, 100, 200, and 500 µM EPA. When the cells reached 80% confluence, the absorbance was measured determine cell viability.

Cell transfection

PA-induced HepG2 cells at 60% confluency were transfected with miR-34a and NC mimics using Lipofectamine 3000, followed by culture in 6-well plates at 37 °C for 24 h. EPA was then added to the cells, followed by subsequent culture for 24 h. The groups included CON, PA, PA + EPA, PA + EPA + NC mimic, and PA + EPA + miR-34a mimic. The PA + EPA + NC mimic group was used as a control for the PA + EPA + miR-34a mimic group to exclude false-positives due to transfection-induced stress.

Statistical analysis

All data were processed and statistically analysed using SPSS software (version 26.0). The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Two-sample comparisons that met the normal distribution were analysed using an independent sample t-test (Student’s t-test), and multi-sample comparisons were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pairwise comparisons were performed using LSD (homogeneity of variance) or Tamhane’s test (non-homogeneity of variance). Otherwise, nonparametric tests were performed. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Result

Establishment of the HFD model

We established an HFD model to investigate the effects of omega-3 fatty acids on lipid metabolism and hepatic lipid deposition. Serum TG and TC measurements after 12 weeks revealed significantly elevated levels in the HFD group than those in the CON group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A–B), thus validating the establishment of a HFD model. IPGTT after 12 weeks revealed significantly higher blood glucose levels in the HFD group than that in the CON group at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min (P < 0.001). The blood glucose level peaked at 15 min in the CON group and at 30 min in the HFD group (Fig. 1C). The glucose AUC in the HFD group was significantly higher than that in the CON group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). Simultaneously, the insulin level in the HFD group was significantly higher than that in the CON group (P < 0.001), and QUICKI was significantly lower (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1E–G). These results suggest that an HFD can lead to decreased insulin sensitivity and the development of insulin resistance. Moreover, the weight of the mice in the HFD group was significantly higher than that of the mice in the CON group in the second week after being fed an HFD (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1H).

Omega-3 fatty acid supplementation improves glucose tolerance and insulin resistance, but does not affect body weight

After 8 weeks of omega-3 fatty acids intervention, IPGTT was conducted on mice in each group. The blood glucose levels in the CON, HFD + LDO, HFD + MDO, and HFD + HDO groups peaked at 15 min, while those in the HFD group peaked at 30 min. Compared to the mice in the CON group, the blood glucose levels of mice in the HFD group increased significantly at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min (P < 0.05), suggesting the development of insulin resistance in the HFD group. Compared to mice in the HFD group, the blood glucose levels of mice in the HFD + LDO, HFD + MDO, and HFD + HDO groups decreased to varying degrees at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min. Moreover, the glucose AUC decreased significantly with increasing drug dose (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A–B; Table 4). The fasting blood glucose in the omega-3 fatty acids intervention group did not change significantly compared to that in the HFD group, but the insulin levels in the HFD + MDO and HFD + HDO groups were significantly lower than those in the HFD group, with a significantly increased QUICKI value (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1C–E), indicating that omega-3 fatty acids can improve insulin resistance and increase insulin sensitivity. There was no statistically significant difference in the weight gain rate between the omega-3 fatty acids intervention group and the other groups (Fig. 3 F).

Omega-3 fatty acids improve lipid levels and hepatic lipid deposition

The serum levels of TG, TC, and LDL-C were significantly increased in the HFD group compared to the CON group, while they were decreased to varying degrees in a dose-dependent manner in the HFD + LDO, HFD + MDO, and HFD + HDO groups compared to the HFD group (P < 0.05). HDL-C levels were not statistically different among the groups (Fig. 3A–D).

TG and TC levels in the liver were significantly higher in the HFD group than those in the CON group (P < 0.05). After drug intervention, hepatic TG and TC levels were significantly decreased, especially in the HFD + MDO and HFD + HDO groups, compared with the HFD group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3E–F).

HE staining revealed that the liver lobules of mice in the CON group had clear and intact structures, with uniform liver cell nuclei, no obvious atypia, uniform red cytoplasmic staining, few lipid droplet vacuoles, and no obvious fatty degeneration. By contrast, mice in the HFD group had unclear liver lobules, disordered liver cells, displaced liver cell nuclei, mild heterogeneity, size-variable cytoplasmic lipid droplet vacuoles, and obvious fatty degeneration. Compared with the HFD group, hepatic fat degeneration and lipid droplet vacuoles were slightly reduced in the HFD + LDO group, and were significantly reduced in the HFD + MDO and HFD + MDO groups (Fig. 3G).

Oil Red O staining of liver tissue revealed blue cell nuclei and almost no orange-red lipid droplets in the CON group and significant orange-red lipid droplet deposition in the HFD group. Compared with the HFD group, the lipid droplets in the HFD + LDO group were slightly reduced and were significantly reduced in the HFD + MDO and HFD + HDO groups (Fig. 3G).

Omega-3 fatty acids regulate lipid metabolism factors

The hepatic protein levels of SIRT1, CPT-1 A, and PGC-1α were significantly decreased in the HFD group compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Hepatic CPT-1 A and PGC-1α protein levels increased in a dose-dependent manner in the HFD + MDO and HFD + HDO groups compared with the HFD group (P < 0.05). Further, hepatic SIRT1 levels were significantly increased in the HFD + HDO group (P < 0.05). Hepatic SREBP-1c levels were significantly elevated in the HFD group compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Omega-3 fatty acid intervention decreased hepatic SREBP-1c levels in a dose-dependent manner (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A–E).

Hepatic mRNA levels of SIRT1, CPT-1 A, and PGC-1α were significantly decreased in the HFD group compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Omega-3 fatty acid intervention increased the hepatic mRNA levels of CPT-1 A, PGC-1α, and SIRT1 in a dose-dependent manner, with significant increases observed in the HFD + MDO and HFD + HDO groups (P < 0.05). Hepatic mRNA levels of miR-34a and SREBP-1c were significantly increased in the HFD group compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Omega-3 fatty acid intervention decreased the hepatic mRNA levels of miR-34a and SREBP-1c in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4F–J).

Omega-3 fatty acids alleviate PA-induced lipid disorders

We evaluated the effect of EPA on lipid metabolism in vitro using the CCK-8 assay, which showed that EPA (≤ 100 µM) did not significantly inhibit HepG2 cell viability within 24 h (Fig. 5A). Therefore, we selected 100 µM EPA for subsequent experiments.

Intracellular TG levels in the PA group were significantly higher than those in the CON group (P < 0.05), and TG levels in the PA + EPA group were significantly lower than those in the PA group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). Oil Red O staining showed that lipid accumulation significantly increased after PA treatment, whereas EPA intervention reduced lipid accumulation (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, western Blotting and RT-qPCR assays revealed increased SREBP-1c and decreased CPT1A, PGC-1α, and SIRT1 levels in HepG2 cells after PA treatment. EPA significantly reversed the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes in HepG2 cells after PA treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D–G).

Omega-3 fatty acids reduce hepatic lipid accumulation by regulating miR-34a

The expression of miR-34a was significantly increased by transfection with miR-34a mimics in HepG2 cells compared to controls, validating the transfection efficacy (Fig. 6A). SREBP-1c expression was increased and CPT-1 A, PGC-1α, and SIRT1 levels were decreased in the PA + EPA+miR-34a mimics group compared with the PA + EPA group. Additionally, no significant differences were observed in SREBP-1c, CPT-1 A, PGC-1α, and SIRT1 levels between the PA + EPA+miR-34a mimics and PA groups (Fig. 6B–K). These results indicate that miR-34a mimics inhibited the regulatory effect of EPA on lipid metabolism. Thus, omega-3 fatty acids regulate lipid metabolism by downregulating miR-34a expression.

Discussion

In this study, we established an HFD model to evaluate the effects of omega-3 fatty acids on lipid metabolism and hepatic lipid deposition. The body weight of mice in the HFD group rapidly increased over 12 weeks. Moreover, serum TC and TG levels were significantly higher in the HFD group than those in the CON group, validating the successful establishment of the HFD model. Furthermore, blood glucose levels were significantly increased in the HFD group than those in the CON group, showing a delayed peak, increased glucose AUC, and decreased QUICKI value. These findings indicate that an HFD impairs insulin function, decreases insulin sensitivity, and causes insulin resistance.

Inconsistent effects of omega-3 fatty acids have been observed in numerous studies, therefore we administered different concentrations of omega-3 fatty acids to mice to minimise the possibility of false-negative results caused by insufficient drug dosage. Owing to the significant differences in mouse body weight in the HFD group, we used weight growth rate as an indicator. Omega-3 fatty acid intervention did not affect body weight, consistent with the results of Zhi-Hong Yang15. However, Hao et al. showed that omega-3 fatty acid intervention significantly reduced the weight of obese mice16. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in research design: Hao et al. maintained the same total caloric intake in mice between groups by reducing the intake of other types of oils in the omega-3 fatty acid group. The calorics provided by the administered drug were minimal relative to the daily diet, thus deliberately lowering other dietary fats could distort the assessment of omega-3 fatty acid efficacy. Moreover, omega-3 fatty acid intervention did not decrease fasting blood glucose levels. This is consistent with the results of C-Y Wong et al.17, that daily supplementation of 4 g fish oil had no significant effect on fasting blood glucose levels in patients with type 2 diabetes without cardiovascular disease. This study found that although blood glucose levels did not decrease after omega-3 fatty acid intervention, the delayed glucose peak was improved. Moreover, the reduction in serum insulin and the increase in QUICKI values suggest that omega-3 fatty acids may possess insulin-sensitizing properties, consistent with previous studies18,19. Omega-3 fatty acids supplementation decreased the serum and hepatic TC and TG levels in a dose-dependent manner compared to those in the HFD group, which is consistent with the results of Yang et al.15. Capanni et al. reported that liver steatosis, evaluated by ultrasound, improved by more than 60% after consuming fish oil20. These studies highlight the potential of omega-3 fatty acids for improving hepatic lipid deposition.

Studies evaluating the effects of omega-3 fatty acids on LDL-C levels have yielded conflicting results, with some studies showing that omega-3 fatty acids increased LDL-C levels21, possibly by promoting VLDL-C-to-LDL-C conversion. A marked increase in triglycerides promotes hepatic clearance and metabolism of triglyceride-rich VLDL, part of which is converted into LDL particles, resulting in a transient elevation of LDL-C levels. Other studies have shown no significant effects of omega-3 fatty acids on LDL-C levels17,22. However, in this study, LDL-C levels significantly decreased following omega-3 fatty acid intervention, which may be attributed to the upregulation of hepatic LDL receptor activity by omega-3 fatty acids, thereby enhancing LDL-C clearance23. Omega-3 fatty acids may also inhibit SREBP-1c, reducing hepatic TG and TC accumulation, consequently decreasing VLDL-C secretion, thereby decreasing LDL-C levels. There were no significant differences in HDL-C levels among the groups, which is consistent with previous studies17,22.

Impaired fat generation and breakdown homeostasis significantly affects hepatic lipid deposition due to abnormal TG accumulation. Therefore, inhibiting fat genesis and promoting fat breakdown may be an effective strategy for preventing and improving hepatic lipid deposition.

Omega-3 fatty acids affect lipid metabolism by regulating the expression of miRNAs including miR-34a, −25, −17, −26a, −29c, −31, −200a, −206, −140, and-32324,25. Among these, miR-34a is closely associated with lipid metabolism. Several studies have shown increased miR-34a expression in patients with NAFLD6,26, with miR-34a abundance correlating with the degree of fat degeneration27. Our results showed that miR-34a is highly expressed in the livers of HFD model mice and in PA-induced HepG2 cells. Omega-3 fatty acid intervention significantly decreased miR-34a expression and enhanced lipid metabolism, suggesting that omega-3 fatty acids improve lipid metabolism by downregulating the expression of miR-34a. SIRT1—closely associated with lipid and energy homeostasis—is an important gene target of miR-34a and is considered a potential therapeutic target for several diseases, including cancer, diabetes, and arteriosclerosis7. miR-34a can directly bind to SIRT1 mRNA and cause its degradation, thereby inhibiting its downstream pathways28,29. Animal experiments revealed that liver-specific SIRT1 knockout mice are prone to fatty liver development, whereas SIRT1 overexpression can alleviate HFD-induced fatty liver development30. Similarly, in vitro experiments have shown that the absence of SIRT1 significant increases triglyceride accumulation in liver cells, whereas SIRT1 overexpression significantly reduces triglyceride synthesis31,32. We showed that SIRT1 expression was decreased in the liver tissues of HFD model mice and in PA-induced HepG2 cells. In induced pluripotent stem cell differentiated liver cells, SIRT1 deficiency activated SREBP-1c and inhibited PGC-1α, thus increasing fatty acid biosynthesis and exacerbating fat accumulation33. SREBP-1c is a major regulator of cholesterol and lipid synthesis and controls the expression of multiple genes involved in fatty acid synthesis. Studies have shown that hepatic SREBP-1c expression is increased in HFD mice, leading to increased TG and TC accumulation34. This finding is consistent with the results of the present study. Omega-3 fatty acid intervention decreased SREBP-1 expression, which is consistent with the results of previous studies35,36. We found that HFD decreased PGC-1α levels, consistent with previous studies34, and that omega-3 fatty acid intervention reversed this effect. PGC-1α promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative capacity by increasing the expression of various transcription factors (such as PPARs), activating them, and regulating energy metabolism. Additionally, SIRT1 regulates CPT-1 A, a rate-limiting enzyme that transports long-chain fatty acids to the mitochondria for β-oxidation. SIRT1 enhances the stability of CPT-1 A by deacetylating it and inhibiting its ubiquitin-dependent degradation, thereby accelerating fatty acid oxidation and alleviating liver steatosis. We found that HFD and PA decreased CPT-1 A levels, and that omega-3 fatty acid intervention increased CPT-1 A expression, improving hepatic lipid accumulation, which is consistent with previous research37.

Further investigation using miR-34a mimics showed that SREBP-1c expression was increased in the PA + EPA + miR-34a mimics group, while the levels of CPT-1 A, PGC-1α, and SIRT1 were decreased. Additionally, the levels of SREBP-1c, CPT-1 A, PGC-1α, and SIRT1 were not different between the PA + EPA + miR-34a mimics and the PA groups. These results indicate that miR-34a overexpression blocks the regulatory effect of EPA on lipid metabolism, suggesting that omega-3 fatty acids regulate lipid metabolism and improve hepatic lipid deposition by downregulating miR-34a expression.

The limitations of this study include its focus solely on the effects and partial mechanisms of omega-3 fatty acids on lipid metabolism and hepatic lipid deposition, without evaluating parameters such as liver inflammation or fibrosis. Further studies are needed to address these aspects.

In summary, in HFD mice and PA-induced HepG2 cells, miR-34a and SREBP-1c expression levels were elevated, whereas SIRT1, CPT1A, and PGC-1α expression was decreased. Omega-3 fatty acid supplementation significantly reversed these changes, reducing miR-34a expression and improving lipid metabolism and hepatic lipid deposition. Furthermore, transfection with miR-34a mimics attenuated the effects of omega-3 fatty acids, confirming that the beneficial regulation of lipid metabolism by omega-3 fatty acids is mediated through the downregulation of miR-34a.
