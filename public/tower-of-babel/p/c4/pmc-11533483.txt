Leveraging mixed-effects location scale models to assess the ERP mismatch negativity’s psychometric properties and trial-by-trial neural variability in toddler-mother dyads
Authors: Serena K Mon, Brittany L Manning, Lauren S Wakschlag, Elizabeth S Norton
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrial-by-trial neural variability, a measure of neural response stability, has been examined in relation to behavioral indicators using summary measures, but these methods do not characterize meaningful processes underlying variability. Mixed-effects location scale models (MELSMs) overcome these limitations by accounting for predictors and covariates of variability but have been rarely used in developmental studies. Here, we applied MELSMs to the ERP auditory mismatch negativity (MMN), a neural measure often related to language and psychopathology. 84 toddlers and 76 mothers completed a speech-syllable MMN paradigm. We extracted early and late MMN mean amplitudes from trial-level waveforms. We first characterized our sample’s psychometric properties using MELSMs and found a wide range of subject-level internal consistency. Next, we examined the relation between toddler MMNs with theoretically relevant child behavioral and maternal variables. MELSMs offered better model fit than analyses that assumed constant variability. We found significant individual differences in trial-by-trial variability but no significant associations between toddler variability and their language, irritability, or mother variability indices. Overall, we illustrate how MELSMs can characterize psychometric properties and answer questions about individual differences in variability. We provide recommendations and resources as well as example code for analyzing trial-by-trial neural variability in future studies.Keywords: Event-related potentials (ERPs), Individual differences, Neural variability, Psychometric reliability, Mismatch negativity, Mixed-effects location scale models

Introduction

Event-related potentials (ERPs) are widely used to measure neural activity that is time-locked to an event of interest. Given that neural responses vary across trials, ERPs are traditionally averaged over multiple trials to isolate the signal from the background “noise” (Luck, 2014). However, this approach assumes that the “signal” is constant across trials and removes potentially meaningful information about the variability of the neural response. Trial-by-trial neural variability is related to learning and perception and has developmental and clinical implications (Magnuson et al., 2020, Thomas et al., 1997). This study leveraged mixed-effects location scale models (MELSMs), which have the advantage of examining predictors and covariates of variability, as a promising approach to consider trial-by-trial neural variability as a signal of interest. We applied this approach in an analysis of ERP auditory mismatch negativity (MMN) in both toddlers and adults (their mothers).

The role of trial-by-trial neural variability

Trial-by-trial variability (also referred to as within-person variance or intra-individual variability, Trenado et al., 2019) measures the extent to which a participant’s ERP values of interest (e.g., mean amplitude at a given electrode for each artifact-free trial) fluctuate across trials. This fluctuation arises from multiple neurophysiological mechanisms, including synaptic plasticity and changes in the excitation and inhibition of neural networks (Dinstein et al., 2015). Trial-by-trial fluctuations are thought to index neural response stability, learning, and adaptability (Garrett et al., 2013, Lam et al., 2017, Pinneo, 1966). For example, as infants mature, neural variability to tones decreases (Thomas et al., 1997), suggesting developmental tuning. Excessive neural variability may affect neural processing and contribute to maladaptive outcomes in autism (Magnuson et al., 2020, Milne, 2011), ADHD (Saville et al., 2015), and schizophrenia (Haigh et al., 2023, Roth et al., 2007).

Trial-by-trial neural variability is distinct from other trial-level processes like systematic modulations and artifactual noise. Systematic modulations describe the average change in magnitude of trial-level values according to a defined pattern (e.g., linear). For example, auditory ERP components reduce in amplitude, or habituate, according to linear and quadratic patterns with repeated stimulus presentations (Cortesa et al., 2019, Hudac et al., 2018). Artifactual noise consists of non-neural influences on trial-level values (e.g., body movements that did not meet artifact detection thresholds). Appropriate analysis approaches are needed to separate artifactual noise from theoretically meaningful neural variability.

Estimating trial-by-trial neural variability with mixed-effects location scale models

Mixed-effects models and summary measures of variability are two trial-level analysis methods that cannot isolate trial-by-trial variability from other processes. Mixed-effects models (also referred to as multilevel models or hierarchical linear models) have been used to examine systematic modulations in trial-level ERP data (e.g., Volpert-Esmond et al., 2018) but assume that trial-by-trial variability and artifactual noise are constant across participants and groups. As a result, mixed-effects models cannot examine theoretically meaningful variability differences (e.g., due to age or diagnosis). In addition, mixed-effects models have decreased statistical power and increased type-1 error if the assumption of constant variability is violated (i.e., heteroscedasticity, DeShon and Alexander, 1996). In comparison, summary measures of variability have been used to quantify variability differences in trial-level ERP data (e.g., standard deviation, Magnuson et al., 2020; Saville et al., 2015; mean absolute deviation, Milne, 2011; Morett et al., 2020). However, these measures collapse across all trial-level processes and thus, variability estimates may be confounded by factors such as artifactual noise. These measures also have low reliability and are sensitive to low trial counts (Estabrook et al., 2012, Wang and Grimm, 2012), which may especially impact developmental data.

Mixed-effects location scale modeling (MELSM), on the other hand, is a comprehensive analytic framework that overcomes many of these limitations. MELSMs are an extension of mixed-effects models that relax the assumption of constant variability and enable examination of predictors and individual differences in trial-by-trial variability (Hedeker et al., 2008). MELSMs consist of two sub-models: a location sub-model and a scale sub-model. The location sub-model estimates the effect of predictors on the mean of the outcome (e.g., mean of trial-level ERP amplitudes), which is similar to a mixed-effects model. The scale sub-model estimates the effect of predictors on the variability of the outcome (e.g., trial-by-trial ERP variability), which is not possible with a mixed-effects model. Each sub-model can include fixed effects (i.e., effects that are consistent across samples) and random effects (i.e., effects that differ across samples) (for further MELSM introductory texts, see Hoffman and Walters, 2022; Williams et al., 2021).

We illustrate an example MELSM applied to trial-level ERP data in Fig. 1. This example model has two location effects: a fixed effect of trial presentation number level to account for linear habituation of amplitude (e.g., a linear decrease in amplitude from Trial 1 to 3, Fig. 1 A) and a by-participant random intercept to account for individual differences in amplitude (e.g., Participant 1 has greater amplitude data than Participant 2, Fig. 1 C). The same example model has two scale effects: a fixed effect of data quality to account for variability differences (e.g., low data quality is associated with more variable data compared to high data quality, Fig. 1 B) and a by-participant random intercept to account for individual differences in variability (e.g., Participant 1 has less variable data than Participant 2, Fig. 1 D). This example illustrates how MELSMs can isolate trial-by-trial variability from systematic modulations (e.g., amplitude habituation) and artifactual noise (e.g., variability differences due to data quality) within the same statistical model. Given that MELSMs estimate both sub-models together, they can account for correlations between location and scale effects (e.g., Participant 2 has both smaller amplitude data and greater trial-by-trial variability, Fig. 1 C,D). This comprehensive approach is beneficial for examining trial-by-trial variability because it accounts for covariates, individual differences in participants’ variability levels, and other potential confounds (e.g., data quality). Controlling for these confounds is especially important because they may make it more difficult to detect often small brain-behavior relation effect sizes. These estimates of trial-by-trial variability are also useful for calculating subject-level psychometric properties (Clayson, Brush, et al., 2021). These properties, such as internal consistency, are important for describing a sample and interpreting statistical results, as well as for estimating statistical power for future studies, which has been notoriously difficult in developmental ERP studies.

MELSMs have additional properties that make them well-suited to analyzing developmental and clinical ERP data. First, MELSMs account for unbalanced data by estimating parameters that are weighted based on each participant’s included trial counts (i.e., partial pooling, which is beneficial if participants in one group have fewer trials than another group). Second, MELSMs return unbiased estimates under common missing data conditions by including covariates related to missingness (e.g., a covariate of age if younger children have more missing data). Third, MELSMs can be estimated using a Bayesian framework to incorporate expected effect sizes using priors and account for uncertainty of model estimates (Williams et al., 2021). These properties are especially beneficial for analyzing small effects and sample sizes, such as those found in developmental studies (van de Schoot et al., 2014). Despite these many benefits for analyzing trial-by-trial neural variability, MELSMs have been applied to only one adult ERP study (Clayson et al., 2022) and no developmental ERP studies to our knowledge.

Current study: Applying mixed-effects location scale models to the mismatch negativity (MMN)

This study applied MELSMs to examine trial-by-trial variability of the auditory MMN in a sample of toddlers and their mothers. The MMN is an index of the brain’s response to a frequently repeated “standard” stimulus compared to an infrequent “deviant” stimulus. It is widely used and can be assessed across the lifespan (Alho et al., 1990, Bishop et al., 2011). To create trial-level MMNs, we calculated the difference between each deviant trial minus the standard trial immediately preceding it (An et al., 2021, Bishop and Hardiman, 2010) and extracted the MMN mean amplitude for each trial using literature-defined time windows. This trial-level procedure was similar to creating traditional averaged MMNs (average of deviant trials minus average of standard trials) but may result in greater data loss for participants with few artifact-free consecutive standard/deviant trials. Trial-level MMNs are beneficial because they can be used to examine similar questions as averaged MMNs (e.g., brain-behavior relations) as well as questions about trial-level processes. While averaged MMN amplitudes have been examined in relation to language/reading and psychopathology (Aoi et al., 2021, Bar-Haim et al., 2003, Norton et al., 2021), trial-by-trial MMN variability and its relation to behavior have not been previously reported to our knowledge. MELSMs fit to trial-level MMNs can answer questions about how trial-by-trial MMN variability differs meaningfully across participants, changes over the course of an experiment, and relates to language and psychopathology. Note that MELSMs do not simulate the activity of underlying neural populations and cannot directly test theories of MMN generation (e.g., prediction error versus neural adaptation; see Lieder et al., 2013; Poublan-Couzardot et al., 2023). However, they can characterize processes that impact trial-level MMNs, such as habituation of amplitude and variability, which may inform other modeling approaches that compare MMN theories.

We first used MELSMs to characterize the MMN’s psychometric properties in our sample. Prior studies have primarily examined the MMN’s test-retest reliability as an indicator of its psychometric properties (Pekkonen et al., 1995, Wang et al., 2021); however, this measure does not fully capture the variability of the MMN within an experiment session, which may be related to theoretically relevant underlying processes and represent neural stability of change detection and processing. We focused on subject-level internal consistency, or the extent to which each participant’s data varies during an experiment session (Clayson, Brush, et al., 2021). This differs from group-level internal consistency, which calculates one value per sample. No studies, to our knowledge, have reported subject-level internal consistency of the MMN, which is important for describing MMN variability and informing effect sizes for future studies. Given that we expected participants to have different levels of trial-by-trial variability, we expected a range of internal consistency values in our samples of toddlers and adults.

We next used MELSMs to examine predictors of toddler trial-by-trial variability, which may be potential biomarkers of disorders. Though mean-averaged MMN amplitudes correlate to language (Guttorm et al., 2005, Leppänen et al., 2010) and psychopathology (Aoi et al., 2021, Bar-Haim et al., 2003), no prior studies have examined whether trial-by-trial MMN variability is related to behavior. Based on the hypothesis that excessive noise is maladaptive (e.g., for developmental dyslexia, Hancock et al., 2017), we predicted that toddlers with greater trial-by-trial MMN variability would have significantly lower language and higher irritability scores.

Finally, given interest for early identification of clinical language and psychological disorders with MMN (Norton, MacNeill, et al., 2021), we examined potential intergenerational similarity of toddler and mother trial-by-trial variability using MELSMs. Given that the MMN has a heritable basis (Bhat et al., 2021, Hall et al., 2006, Hall et al., 2009), we predicted that toddler-mother dyads would have greater similarity of trial-by-trial MMN variability than randomly shuffled non-related dyads.

Methods

Participants

Toddlers and parents were enrolled in the broader When to Worry (W2W) Study, which includes two integrated cohorts followed longitudinally (n=410). The When to Worry about Irritability (W2W-I; Wakschlag, 2016, R01MH107652) study explores infant irritability as an early indicator of risk for developmental psychopathology and enrolled a cohort of children at age 1 who were oversampled for high irritability. The When to Worry about Language (W2W-L; Norton & Wakschlag, R01DC016273) extended the W2W-I study to explore various predictors of preschool language impairment and enrolled a cohort of late-talking toddlers at age 2 (see Norton et al., 2024 for full recruitment and sample details and other papers using this sample). Participants were recruited from pediatric clinics in the greater Chicago area, community groups, and social media. The Northwestern University Institutional Review Board approved this study and parents provided written informed consent. Families were compensated for participation and transportation.

Toddlers were eligible for the current study if they were: born >=34 weeks gestation, native English speaker, no known hearing loss or other sensory/perceptual diagnosis, and not diagnosed with any neurological or developmental conditions. The biological parent of the toddler participated (because the study measured other constructs including pregnancy factors).

184 toddler-parent dyads completed an age 2 visit which included EEG along with other behavioral measures (see Norton et al., 2022). Toddlers and parents from this sample were included in the analytic sample if they had usable MMN data (see Supplementary Figure S1 for details). The final analytic sample consisted of 84 toddlers and 76 mothers; 58 were dyads with usable MMN data from both toddler and their mother. Demographic information for this sample is reported in Table 1. Supplementary Table S1 compares child demographics from the analytic sample to the broader W2W sample without MMN data for generalization across papers.

MMN paradigm

Toddlers and mothers completed an auditory oddball MMN paradigm consisting of natural speech syllables (/ba/ and /da/) recorded from an adult female native English speaker. Stimuli were ∼200 ms in duration and were equated for root mean square loudness (see Norton, Beach, et al., 2021). A total of 1300 trials (85 % standard /ba/ and 15 % deviant /da/) were presented with Presentation software (Neurobehavioral Systems, CA). The interstimulus interval was jittered, ranging from 350–400 ms. The paradigm began with 6 standard stimuli for familiarization, which were excluded from analyses. Each deviant was preceded by ≥3 consecutive standards. Total MMN recording time was 13 min.

EEG recording

EEG recordings were collected from the toddler and mother simultaneously while they were seated next to each other at a table. Toddler-mother dyads watched a quiet movie of their choice during stimulus presentation. Due to the challenges of using earphones with EEG caps in toddlers, sounds were presented via speakers behind the toddler. The sound level of the stimuli was kept at ∼70 dB (verified by periodic checks with a sound level meter) and the decibel level of the movie was kept substantially lower, such that the syllable stimuli were clearly audible.

Toddlers and mothers were each capped with an elastic fabric cap containing active Ag-AgCl electrodes (Electro-Cap Inc., Eaton, OH). Both caps contained 32 electrodes arranged in 10–20 system positioning (electrodes at locations Fp 1/2; AF 3/4; F 7/3/z/4/8, FC 5/1/2/6, C 3/z/4, T 7/8, CP 5/1/2/6, P 7/3/z/4/8, PO 3/4, O 1/z/2). EEG was recorded using two linked Biosemi ActiveTwo Systems (Biosemi B.V., Amsterdam). As reported in prior studies using this setup (Norton et al., 2022), recordings were made in single-ended mode that amplifies the difference between each electrode site and a common mode sensor electrode. No online reference is applied and impedance did not need to be lowered with this system due to the combination of pre-amplifiers at each electrode, a driven right leg circuit, and high electrical isolation (Kappenman and Luck, 2010). Offset values for each electrode were <40 mV. Data were recorded with a low-pass hardware filter with a half-power cutoff at 104 Hz and digitized at 512 Hz with 24 bits of resolution.

EEG/ERP data processing

Data were processed offline in MATLAB (v2021b; The Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA) with the following toolboxes: EEGLAB (v2022.0; Delorme and Makeig, 2004), ERPLAB (v9.00; Lopez-Calderon and Luck, 2014), and the HAPPILEE/HAPPE+ER pipelines optimized for low-density developmental data (v3.3; Lopez et al., 2022; Monachino et al., 2022). Custom MATLAB scripts used for processing are available in our project repository (https://osf.io/4dwmg). Toddler and mother data were processed separately.

First, data were referenced to Cz, DC offset was removed, and files were imported into the HAPPILEE/HAPPE+ER pipeline. Within the pipeline, CleanLine (Mullen, 2012) was used to filter 60 Hz line noise. Bad electrodes were identified and removed if any electrode: did not have ≥5 seconds of data, contained residual line noise, had low correlation with other electrodes, and/or contained outlier power spectrum values. Time and frequency-localized artifacts were corrected using the pipeline’s “hard” wavelet threshold setting. Data were then bandpass filtered (Butterworth filter, half-amplitude cutoffs at 0.1 and 40 Hz, 12 dB/octave roll-off). Afterwards, data were segmented from −100–600 ms relative to stimulus onset and baseline-corrected to the −100–0 ms period. Trials were removed if they contained an electrode exceeding a simple voltage threshold (±150 μV for toddlers, ±80 μV for mothers). Following trial rejection, previously identified bad electrodes were interpolated using spherical spline interpolation. In the last step of the pipeline, data were re-referenced to infinity using the REST method (Dong et al., 2017, Yao, 2001). This referencing scheme has lower relative error compared to the average reference and performs well for low and high electrode densities (Lei and Liao, 2017; for additional discussion, see Lopez et al., 2022).

Data were then processed in ERPLAB to extract (artifact-free) deviant trials and standard trials immediately preceding each deviant. Trial-level MMN waveforms were calculated as the waveform for each deviant trial minus the standard trial immediately preceding it (An et al., 2021, Bishop and Hardiman, 2010). Table 1 provides the mean number of artifact-free MMN trials and other EEG data quality metrics for the toddler and mother sample. No minimum trial thresholds were set for participant exclusion, in line with recommendations for models that use partial pooling (Heise et al., 2022). Toddlers and mothers were included in analysis if they had usable MMN data consisting of at least one artifact-free MMN trial and at most eight (of 32) interpolated electrodes (see Supplementary Figure S1 for participant inclusion/exclusion details).

Mismatch negativity measurements and analysis

The early and late MMN components were identified using literature-defined spatial regions and time windows of interest and via visualizations of the grand-average MMN waveforms (see Fig. 2). The early MMN has a peak at approximately 150–250 ms post-stimulus with a negative central distribution. It is associated with automatic change detection, auditory memory, and stimulus tracking (for a review, see Bishop, 2007). In contrast, the late MMN (also referred to as the “late discriminative negativity” or LDN) peaks at approximately 300–500 ms post-stimulus with a negative frontocentral distribution. It is associated with reorienting of attention (Choudhury et al., 2015, Yang et al., 2015) and fine-grained processing of stimulus feature differences (Bishop et al., 2011). Time windows for analysis differed for the toddler and mother samples due to developmental differences; the early MMN was assessed in central-parietal clusters (left: CP5, P7; right: CP6, P8) from 100–200 ms for toddlers and 125–225 ms for mothers. The late MMN corresponded to two clusters (left: FC1, F3; right: FC2, F4) from 300–600 ms for both the toddler and mother sample. These parameters are in line with prior MMN studies for these age ranges (Bishop et al., 2011, Cantiani et al., 2013, Putkinen et al., 2012). For each MMN component, the mean amplitude was extracted from participants’ trial-level MMN waveforms.

Behavioral assessment

We focused on language and irritability measures because of their established associations with language/reading and mental health, as irritability is an indicator of later internalizing and externalizing disorders (Finlay-Jones et al., 2024). Toddler expressive and receptive language were assessed using the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (Mullen, 1995). Data were missing for the receptive language subscale for 8 participants. For the 76 toddlers in analysis, raw receptive language scores ranged from 14–35 (M=27.6, SD=3.6). There were no missing data for the expressive language subscale. The raw expressive language scores ranged from 14–35 (M=24.8, SD=4.6). We used raw scores for analysis given that age was included as a covariate in our analysis models.

Parent-report of toddler irritability were assessed using the Multidimensional Assessment Profiles-Temper Loss Scale (MAPS-TL), Infant-Toddler version (Wakschlag et al., 2014). Parents answered 30 items (e.g., “Have a temper tantrum”) using a 6-point objective frequency scale: 0 = “Never”, 1 = “Rarely (Less than once per week)”, 2 = “Some (1−3) days of the week”, 3 = “Most (4−6) days of the week”, 4 = “Every day of the week”, 5 = “Many times each day”. Irritability scores were calculated using item response theory to scale items based on their frequency and qualitative intensity within this sample and timepoint (for additional details, see Krogh-Jespersen et al., 2022). There were no missing data for the MAPS-TL scale. Irritability scores ranged from −1.2–2.3 (M=0.8, SD=0.7).

Data analysis plan

Analyses were pre-registered on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/bds2n) and conducted separately for the early and late MMN time windows. Analysis code is available in our project repository (https://osf.io/4dwmg).

Psychometric properties

We first characterized the internal consistency of the toddler and mother samples using the ERP Reliability Analysis Toolbox (v0.5.2; Clayson and Miller, 2017a) run in MATLAB (v2021b; The Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA). The toolbox used CmdStan (v2.21.0; Stan Development Team., 2019) to implement the analyses in Stan (Carpenter et al., 2017). Custom code was added to extract subject-level internal consistency for difference waves/scores, which accounts for the reliability of the raw waveforms (Clayson, Baldwin, et al., 2021). We extracted two internal consistency measures—dependability and intraclass correlation—from generalizability theory (a framework that is more suitable for ERP data than classical test theory, e.g., Cronbach’s alpha; Clayson, Brush, et al., 2021). Dependability is the ratio of between-person variance (how much participants differ from each other) and person-specific between-trial variance (how much each participant’s trials differ from each other due to trial-level processes) after accounting for the number of included trials. Intraclass correlation is calculated using a similar equation but does not weigh based on trial count. Equations are included in Supplementary Materials. Simplified MELSMs without covariates were used to calculate variance. Dependability and intraclass correlation values range from 0 (a participant’s trials are highly dissimilar) to 1 (a participant’s trials are highly similar), and a minimum threshold of 0.7 has been recommended for ERP data (Clayson and Miller, 2017b). Lower internal consistency scores indicate greater between-trial variance (e.g. due to greater trial-by-trial variability or artifactual noise). In addition, intraclass correlation values are generally lower than dependability values because they do not account for participants’ trial counts, which can impact between-trial variance.

Toddler MMN-behavior relations

Next, we analyzed toddler trial-level MMNs using models fit in R (v4.2.3; R Core Team, 2023) with the brms package (v2.20.4; Bürkner, 2017). Each model was run with 4 chains, 10,000 iterations, and weakly informative priors (see Supplementary Materials for information about prior distributions). Our model building procedure began with the simplest model and built up to more complex models. Leave-one-out cross validation was used to compare model fit using the loo R package (v2.6.0; Vehtari et al., 2023). Greater model fit was indicated by a positive expected log predictive density relative to the comparison model (elpdˆ.diff). Model 1 was a mixed-effects model examining trial-level amplitude as the outcome. Covariates were biological sex, age, maternal education, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, and EEG data quality, which were selected due to their relation to the MMN and brain development (e.g., age-related MMN changes, Bishop et al., 2011; Morales et al., 2023; Werwach et al., 2022). We included MMN trial presentation number to examine linear patterns of habituation (McGee et al., 2001). See Supplementary Materials for information about variable coding for categorical variables and centering for continuous variables. A by-toddler random intercept and slope of presentation number was included. Model 2 was a MELSM examining both trial-level amplitude and trial-by-trial variability as the outcomes. Trial-level amplitude was predicted in the location sub-model by covariates of biological sex, age, maternal education, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, and EEG data quality and a by-toddler random location intercept and slope of presentation number. Trial-by-trial variability was predicted in the scale sub-model by the same six covariates (biological sex, age, maternal education, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, EEG data quality) and a by-toddler random scale intercept and slope of presentation number. Model 2 was compared with Model 1 to examine if model fit improved by accounting for differences in variability. Based on the best-fitting model, we constructed three additional models; each of these models was identical to the best-fitting model with the addition of one behavioral variable (receptive language, expressive language, or irritability score). We examined receptive and expressive language in separate models because they were moderately correlated in our sample and both have been shown to be related to the MMN previously (Guttorm et al., 2005). Each model was compared with the best-fitting model to examine if model fit improved by accounting for the behavioral variable.

Toddler-mother MMN similarity

We used dyadic models to examine trial-level MMNs collected from toddler-mother dyads with complete data (i.e., data from both members). Rather than fitting a separate toddler-only model and mother-only model, we used a single model that accounted for the relatedness of toddler and mother data by nesting data within dyads (Rast and Ferrer, 2018). This type of dyadic model can account for covariates that uniquely capture toddler-related and mother-related effects independently (e.g., estimate a toddler habituation effect and a mother habituation effect). In addition, it can estimate the variance and covariance between random effects in both the location and scale sub-models, which is beneficial for examining toddler-mother MMN similarity. For example, we can examine the correlation between by-toddler and by-mother random location intercepts to determine whether toddlers with greater MMN amplitudes have mothers with greater MMN amplitudes. Similarly, we can examine the correlation between by-toddler and by-mother random scale intercepts to determine whether toddlers with more variable MMNs have mothers with more variable MMNs. Other correlations can be examined if they are fit as random effects (e.g., correlation between by-toddler and by-mother random location slopes of presentation number can be examined to determine whether toddlers with greater amplitude habituation have mothers with greater amplitude habituation). Any remaining correlation that is not explained are estimated via residual correlations. Importantly, all correlations are estimated at the same time as other fixed and random effects within a dyadic model, which improves model estimates and accounts for potential confounding factors.

Toddler-mother trial-level MMN similarity was analyzed using dyadic models fit with the brms package. Each model was run with 4 chains and 10,000 iterations. We used the same weakly informative priors as the toddler-only models (Section 2.7.2). We conducted a model building procedure and used leave-one-out cross validation to examine model fit; greater model fit was indicated by a positive elpdˆ.diff. Model 3 was a dyadic mixed-effects model examining toddler and mother trial-level amplitudes as the outcomes. Toddler covariates were biological sex, age, maternal education, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, and EEG data quality. A by-toddler random intercept and slope of presentation number were included. Mother covariates were their education level, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, and EEG data quality. A by-mother random intercept and slope of presentation number were included. Model 4 was a dyadic MELSM examining toddler and mother trial-level amplitudes and trial-by-trial variability as the outcomes. Toddler trial-level amplitude was predicted in the location sub-model by six covariates (biological sex, age, maternal education, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, EEG data quality) and a by-toddler random location intercept and slope of presentation number; toddler trial-by-trial variability was predicted in the scale sub-model by the same six covariates (biological sex, age, maternal education, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, EEG data quality) and a by-toddler random scale intercept and slope of presentation number. Mother trial-level amplitude was predicted in the location sub-model by four covariates (education level, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, EEG data quality) and a by-mother random location intercept and slope of presentation number; mother trial-by-trial variability was predicted in the scale sub-model by the same four covariates (education level, MMN trial presentation number, electrode hemisphere, EEG data quality) and a by-mother random scale intercept and slope of presentation number. Model 4 was compared with Model 3 to examine if model fit improved by accounting for differences in variability. For the best-fitting model, we extracted the point estimate and 95 % credible intervals of the correlation between by-toddler and by-mother random location intercepts and (if applicable based on best-fitting model) by-toddler and by-mother random scale intercepts. If there were significant toddler-mother correlations, we examined the familial specificity of the relationship by creating randomly assigned dyads of mothers and toddlers from our sample and fitting each dataset to the best-fitting model. This process was repeated 10,000 times for each MMN time window and the correlations between randomly assigned dyads were compared with the original dataset.

Toddler-mother MMN similarity

Early MMN

Next, we examined if toddlers and mothers shared similar trial-by-trial early MMN variability. We compared two models: a dyadic mixed-effects model with covariates of trial-level amplitude only (Model 3) and a dyadic MELSM with covariates of both trial-level amplitude and trial-by-trial variability (Model 4). Our pre-registered Model 4 had low model convergence as indicated by Rˆ values that exceeded 1.05 and low bulk and tail effective sample sizes. To address this, we removed by-toddler and by-mother random slopes of presentation number from the location and scale sub-models. The final Model 4 had adequate convergence based on Rˆ and effective sample size values and improved model fit compared to Model 3 (elpdˆ.diff = 433.1, SE(elpdˆ.diff) = 34.8). Model 4 coefficients are presented in Supplementary Tables S6 and S7. The toddler fixed intercepts have the same interpretation as Model 2’s fixed intercepts described above. The mother fixed intercepts correspond to the early MMN at trial presentation 98 recorded from the left hemisphere electrode cluster for a reference mother with an EEG data quality value of 0.5 and a bachelor’s degree.

There were significant individual differences in early MMN amplitude and variability for toddlers and mothers. The by-toddler random location and scale intercepts were similar in magnitude to the random intercepts from Model 2 with the full toddler sample. The by-mother random location intercept was 0.18 μV (95 % Crl: 0.01–0.49) and the by-mother random scale intercept was 0.18 log μV (95 % Crl: 0.14–0.22). These results suggest that there was significant variation in the sample in terms of individuals’ mean early MMN amplitudes and their trial-by-trial variability. Toddlers and mothers also exhibited significantly greater trial-by-trial variability for the right hemisphere compared to the left hemisphere, exhibiting a similar pattern to the toddler-only Model 2.

There was no significant toddler-mother similarity in trial-by-trial early MMN variability as indicated by the correlation between by-toddler and by-mother random scale intercepts (−0.09, 95 % Crl: −0.38–0.22). There was also no significant toddler-mother similarity in trial-level early MMN amplitudes as indicated by the correlation between by-toddler and by-mother random location intercepts (0.11, 95 % Crl: −0.63–0.75). These results suggest that the effect of intergenerational similarity on the early MMN was not significant in our sample. In addition, there was no significant residual correlation (0, 95 % Crl: −0.01–0.02), which suggests that there was no unexplained toddler-mother similarity in our model. Given the statistically insignificant toddler-mother correlations, we did not additionally examine correlations for randomly shuffled toddler-mother dyads.

Late MMN

We then examined if toddlers and mothers shared similar trial-by-trial late MMN variability. Similar to the early MMN analysis, our pre-registered dyadic MELSM (Model 4) had low model convergence as indicated by high Rˆ values and low bulk and tail effective sample sizes. We removed the by-toddler and by-mother random slopes of presentation number from the location and scale sub-models. The final Model 4 had adequate convergence and improved model fit compared to the mixed-effects model (Model 3) (elpdˆ.diff = 328.6, SE(elpdˆ.diff) = 30.9). Model coefficients are presented in Supplementary Tables S8 and S9. Both toddlers and mothers exhibited significant individual differences in late MMN amplitude and variability. The by-toddler random location intercept was 2.10 μV (95 % Crl: 1.34–2.97) and the by-toddler random scale intercept was 0.10 log μV (95 % Crl: 0.08–0.14). The by-mother random location intercept was 0.38 μV (95 % Crl: 0.05–0.71) and the by-mother random scale intercept was 0.17 log μV (95 % Crl: 0.14–0.21). Overall, these results suggest significant variation in individuals’ mean late MMN amplitudes and trial-by-trial variability in the sample.

There was no significant toddler-mother similarity in trial-by-trial late MMN variability as indicated by the correlation between by-toddler and by-mother random scale intercepts (−0.12, 95 % Crl: −0.42–0.20). There was also no significant toddler-mother similarity in trial-level late MMN amplitudes as indicated by the correlation between by-toddler and by-mother random location intercepts (−0.12, 95 % Crl: −0.66–0.50) and no significant residual correlation (0, 95 % Crl: −0.02–0.02). These results suggest that there was no significant effect of intergenerational similarity on the late MMN in our sample.

Summary of toddler-mother MMN similarity

Dyadic MELSMs revealed that both toddlers and mothers had significant individual differences in amplitudes and trial-by-trial variability. Toddlers’ MMN individual differences were not significantly related to mothers’ MMN individual differences in our sample.

Discussion

Whereas the MMN has been widely used to study language, reading, and mental health in developmental samples, nearly all existing studies have collapsed across individual trial variability and even across groups defined by these topics of interest. This study was the first to our knowledge to characterize neural variability of the MMN amplitude and examine its relation to dimensional child behavior and maternal variables. These behavioral measures fall along a continuum and dichotomization of continuous variables decreases statistical power (MacCallum et al., 2002). In our first set of analyses, we described the psychometric properties of the toddler and mother MMN using MELSMs and found a wide range of subject-level internal consistency (dependability and ICC) values. Though internal consistency values of >0.7 are considered good (Clayson and Miller, 2017b), this threshold was not reached for any toddler or mother in our sample. Dependability had a greater range of values across participants compared to ICC, because it accounts for participants’ included trial counts. Dependability values may be a more appropriate measure for reporting internal consistency for samples with a wide range of trial counts across participants. Although low internal consistency decreases statistical power to detect between-person differences (Clayson and Miller, 2017b), a wide range of internal consistency values may be beneficial for variability analysis, because it indicates that participants differ in their between-trial variance. Given that subject-level internal consistency measures for ERP data are a relatively recent area of interest (Clayson, Brush, et al., 2021), our findings contribute to a growing body of research characterizing this psychometric property of ERP components.

In our second and third analyses examining toddler MMN-behavior and toddler-mother MMN relations, we found that MELSMs were a significantly better fit to the data than other models and identified significant individual differences in toddler and mother trial-by-trial variability. That is, some participants had more variable MMN amplitudes, whereas others had less variable MMN amplitudes. The trial-by-trial variability of the MMN has not been previously characterized with either summary measures or MELSMs, to our knowledge. Our estimates of participants’ variability with MELSM were more comprehensive than typical summary measures and were significant for both the early and late MMN. The early MMN is associated with automatic change detection, auditory memory, and stimulus tracking (Bishop, 2007), so greater trial-by-trial variability may be associated with greater sensitivity to the environment. In comparison, the late MMN is associated with reorienting and conscious processing of stimulus feature differences (Bishop et al., 2011, Choudhury et al., 2015), so greater trial-by-trial variability of the late MMN may be associated with noisier/less stable internal representations of stimulus features. Moderate levels of response variability have been suggested to be beneficial for learning and adaptability, but excessive variability may be maladaptive (e.g., Dinstein et al., 2015). There has been no prior characterization of trial-by-trial MMN variability, so there is no framework to determine if our sample included a normative range of variability or if some participants may have had excessive variability levels. This is an important consideration given that our study oversampled for late talking and irritability, which are risk factors for language and mental health conditions that may reflect more inconsistent neural processing. Finally, these results have implications for common analysis models of ERP data (e.g., ANOVA, regression), which assume constant trial-by-trial variability across all participants and are negatively impacted when this assumption is violated (DeShon and Alexander, 1996). As shown in our data, this assumption may not be accurate for the MMN and alternative analysis methods such as MELSMs should be used.

In our analysis of MMN in relation to child behavior and child-maternal relations, we did not find significant associations between toddler trial-by-trial variability with language or irritability scores, nor with mother trial-by-trial variability. Prior studies using traditional mean-averaging approaches to extract the MMN have found significant associations with language (Guttorm et al., 2005, Maurer et al., 2009), psychopathology (Aoi et al., 2021, Bar-Haim et al., 2003), and heritability (Hall et al., 2006, Hall et al., 2009) but some studies had mixed or non-significant findings (e.g., Reeb-Sutherland et al., 2009). These findings may have been impacted by the magnitude of the mean-averaged MMN, which is relatively small and may be obscured by neural variability or artifactual noise (Picton et al., 2000), and/or by small effect sizes of brain-behavior relations (Marek et al., 2022). Our study offers a new angle to examine MMN-behavior relations using a trial-level approach to extract the MMN and characterize trial-by-trial variability, which has not been used in prior MMN studies.

Our MELSM approach examined MMN amplitude and variability within the same model, controlled for covariates and confounding variables, weighted participants’ data based on included trial counts, and used a Bayesian estimation framework to estimate the uncertainty of each predictor. We did not find significant associations for either trial-level MMN amplitudes or trial-by-trial variability, which may be due to small true effect sizes of brain-behavior relations that require larger sample sizes and greater behavioral variability to detect. This factor may have been compounded by the process used to create trial-level MMN waveforms (An et al., 2021, Bishop and Hardiman, 2010) which results in data loss if both a deviant and the standard trial immediately preceding it are not available in the final dataset. Statistical power and model uncertainty may also have been impacted by residual artifactual noise that was not accounted for by our EEG data quality covariate indexing the amount of data cleaning performed in the HAPPILEE/HAPPE+ER automated pipeline. For example, it may be beneficial to include a data quality covariate that measures the degree of child’s head movement during data collection. Lastly, the toddler age range of our sample has been very rarely studied with MMN or ERP measures in general, and so may represent a period in development when brain-behavior relations are different from those seen longitudinally from the first year of life to later ability or concurrent ability in other age ranges. For example, using mean-averaged MMNs, newborn responses related to later language and reading (Guttorm et al., 2005) and MMNs were concurrently related with psychopathology (e.g., school-age children, Aoi et al., 2021; Bar-Haim et al., 2003) and MMN heritability (e.g., adults, Hall et al., 2006). Prior studies have also not examined whether there are age-related changes in trial-by-trial MMN variability. It is possible that trial-by-trial MMN variability may undergo maturational changes and there may be stronger MMN-behavioral relations in older children that we were not able to detect in this sample.

Our last finding was that toddler trial-by-trial variability was significantly associated with the electrode hemisphere, but not with biological sex, age, maternal education, MMN trial presentation number, or EEG data quality. Trial-by-trial variability was greater for the early MMN in the right hemisphere compared to the left hemisphere and this pattern was also present in the mother sample. Hemispheric differences for the early MMN amplitude have been reported (Leppänen et al., 1999, Thiede et al., 2020) but no prior study has examined hemisphere differences in trial-by-trial variability. Additional studies are needed to confirm this pattern found in our sample. In terms of habituation, traditional mean-averaged MMN amplitudes have been found to be smaller in school-age children and adult participants (McGee et al., 2001) although there have also been mixed findings (for a summary, see Bishop, 2007). No prior studies have examined habituation trends in trial-level MMN amplitudes or trial-by-trial MMN variability. Our models provided relatively strong evidence that there was no linear habituation of trial-by-trial variability as indicated by the fixed scale effect of trial presentation number of 0 and 95 % credible interval of [0,0] for both the toddler and mother samples. These results suggest that later trials do not have increased variability compared to earlier trials in a session. We also did not find significant linear habituation of trial-level amplitudes as indicated by the fixed location effect of trial presentation number of 0 and narrow 95 % credible intervals. This finding may be due to the short MMN recording session (13 min) compared to McGee and colleagues (2001) who reported habituation occurring at 15 min, and the fact that we calculated trial-level MMN difference wave amplitudes, rather than calculating mean-averaged MMNs across the whole experiment. Though we focused on linear habituation effects of the MMN, prior studies have reported linear and quadratic habituation of auditory ERP components that overlap with the MMN time window (e.g., N1/M1, Hudac et al., 2018; Muenssinger et al., 2013). Trial-level analyses are beneficial for differentiating between these habituation patterns, which may exhibit different developmental patterns and behavioral relations.

Limitations and future directions

Three main limitations should be considered in relation to the generalizability of our findings. First, our quantification of the MMN’s internal consistency and trial-by-trial variability was conducted in a sample of predominantly white, non-Hispanic/Latino toddlers and their biological mothers with moderate to high socioeconomic status from one metropolitan area. This impacts the generalizability of our findings such that the specific range of internal consistency and variability values may not be reflective of participants from more diverse backgrounds. Quantifying the MMN’s internal consistency and variability in a broader sample would better characterize normative MMN individual differences in these populations. Although the specific range of MMN values may differ, we expect that the pattern of significant individual differences of MMN variability will hold in a broader sample. These MMN individual differences are hypothesized to index differences in automatic change detection and stimulus representation that are present starting at birth and are not expected to relate to social constructs, such as race. Additional studies are needed to examine how MMN variability impacts statistical learning and other skills. In addition, MMN variability may also represent a stable marker of individual differences. Prior studies have reported good test-retest reliability of the MMN amplitude (Hall et al., 2006, Wang et al., 2021) but no study has examined test-retest reliability of trial-by-trial MMN variability. In other domains, high reliability of neural variability of visual processing has been reported in adults and suggested to index a stable neural characteristic (Arazi et al., 2017). Longitudinal studies could examine whether trial-by-trial MMN variability is a consistent marker over time.

Second, the null relation observed here between toddlers’ MMN and behavioral variables was limited to our assessments of language and irritability. For language, we measured receptive and expressive language skills using the Mullen Scales of Early Learning but did not examine other skills that are related to the MMN amplitude, including phonological awareness and rapid naming (Norton, Beach, et al., 2021). As a result, we lack evidence that there are no relations between MMN variability and other skills and note that established behavioral relations were observed with averaged amplitude or latency measures. Further studies are needed to examine the relationship between toddler MMN variability and a broader range of concurrent and subsequent language and reading skills. For irritability, we used a validated parent-report measure, but did not include direct assessment tasks (e.g., Disruptive Behavior Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Wakschlag et al., 2007). Likewise, future studies are needed to examine the relation between toddler MMN variability and a broader set of irritability measures and other assessments of early markers of psychopathology.

Third, the observed non-significant relation between a toddler and their mother’s MMN may have been limited by our sample size. Our dyadic MELSM approach required data from both members of a dyad and resulted in some data loss. This factor, combined with the data quality limitations we set within our sample, may have reduced statistical power and increased model uncertainty. Thus, we lack evidence that there is no relation between toddler and mother MMNs in a sample with more participants or less excluded data. Using the parameter estimates from our analysis, future studies could conduct power simulation studies to examine the number of dyads needed to detect different effect size magnitudes of intergenerational MMN similarity. Our findings were also limited to toddler-mother dyads. Prior studies of parent-child neural similarity have found biological sex-specific similarity patterns in the corticolimbic circuit (Yamagata et al., 2016). Future studies are needed to characterize the relation in toddler-father samples.

We do not expect that our choice of MMN stimuli or paradigm impacts our results. Our MMN paradigm used /ba/ and /da/ stimuli that reflect a phonemic contrast that is relevant and discriminable for the English speakers in our sample. While MMN paradigms using speech stimuli are relevant for clinical questions (e.g., neural processing differences in individuals with or without dyslexia, Gu and Bi, 2020), we expect to find MMN individual differences for other relevant stimuli contrasts that elicit the MMN in participants (e.g., duration and frequency differences). Similarly, other MMN paradigm specifications (e.g., number of stimuli) are not expected to impact our finding that the MMN varies across participants. We have no other indications that these results are specific to other characteristics of the participants, materials, or context.

Conclusion: Recommendations and further resources

Given our findings and consolidation of the literature, we provide the following recommendations for ERP researchers interested in estimating trial-by-trial variability in their data. Heise and colleagues (2021) provide guidelines to ensure that EEG data collection and processing can support trial-level ERP analyses. We include example code for processing and calculating trial-level MMNs in our project repository (https://osf.io/4dwmg).

Recommendation 1: When measuring ERPs, examine whether neural variability differs across participants or groups in the sample. Neural variability differences may be a common but underexamined feature of ERPs, as demonstrated by our MMN analysis. If neural variability is a signal of interest, select an analysis approach that is suitable for the research question (also see Recommendation 2). Even if neural variability is not a signal of interest, it is important to verify whether variability may differ in a sample, especially if the analysis model assumes constant variability (e.g., ANOVA, regression, mixed-effects models). One preliminary method is to calculate subject-level internal consistency scores for a sample, because a wide range of internal consistency values suggests that participants may differ in their trial-by-trial variability. There are existing toolboxes to extract subject-level internal consistency (e.g., ERP Reliability Analysis Toolbox, Clayson and Miller, 2017a) and guidelines to report internal consistency for ERP studies (Clayson and Miller, 2017b, Lopez et al., 2023). Another method is to plot participant trial-level waveforms (see e.g., Fig. 3) or extracted data (e.g., via a scatterplot) to examine whether participants have different data distributions. If trial-by-trial variability is a signal of interest to study, we recommend conducting these checks after pre-registering (see Recommendation 4) to reduce potential bias from examining trends in the data.

Recommendation 2: Use research questions to guide selection of appropriate trial-by-trial variability analysis approaches. MELSMs are suitable for isolating trial-by-trial variability from other trial-level processes and confounds, but this approach requires modeling the ERP data as the outcome. This approach may also require more computational time and resources than typical analysis models (e.g., toddler-only models from Section 3.2 were run on 4 cores of a computing cluster and required ∼20 hours of compute time). In contrast, summary measures of variability, such as standard deviations, can be included as either the predictor or outcome variable in an analysis, but offer a less comprehensive estimate of variability.

Recommendation 3: Determine relevant EEG data quality measure(s) for inclusion in analysis. Given that trial-by-trial neural variability may be impacted by artifactual noise, we recommend including a covariate for EEG data analysis. In these analyses, we included a covariate of the amount of data cleaning performed in the HAPPILEE/HAPPE+ER automated pipeline. Alternate measures include data quality indicators recorded at the time of data collection (e.g., motion data collected with an accelerometer) or data quality measures of the final ERP dataset. We do not recommend data quality covariates that are calculated based on the variability of trial-level waveforms (e.g., standardized measurement error, Luck et al., 2021) as these measures assume that participants with more variable trials have lower data quality and may correlate with meaningful variability due to neural processes.

Recommendation 4: Pre-register analytic plans, including detailing how trial-by-trial variability will be extracted. If a MELSM approach is used, the pre-registration may include information such as the prior distribution for model predictors, model comparisons, and inference criteria. Priors can be selected based on prior research or based on domain knowledge; stronger priors may be helpful in cases of small effect sizes (for an example walkthrough, see Depaoli and van de Schoot, 2017). We recommend including a comparison of a MELSM and a mixed-effects model to determine if modeling trial-by-trial variability with the MELSM improves model fit (as demonstrated in our MMN analysis here). We also recommend including information about how model convergence will be assessed and what steps will be taken if the model is too complex for the data. In general, a Bayesian framework may enable fitting more complex models. However, low model convergence may still occur, as shown in Section 3.3 (for more information about convergence with Bayesian MELSMs, see Rast et al., 2012). Overall, pre-registering the analysis ensures consideration of researcher degrees of freedom prior to data analysis. The Open Science Framework (https://osf.io) offers templates and resources. For further guidelines for pre-registering ERP analyses, see Paul et al., (2021).

To facilitate fitting and interpreting MELSMs, we suggest the following resources. MELSMs are an extension of mixed-effects models and share a similar structure (e.g., fixed and random effects). Thus, we recommend the following introductory resources that describe how ERP data can be analyzed in this framework: (Heise et al., 2022, Volpert-Esmond et al., 2021). To learn more about MELSMs specifically, we recommend the following resources: Hoffman and Walters, (2022); Rast and Ferrer, (2018); Williams et al., (2021). Given the benefits of applying a Bayesian approach to MELSMs to estimate priors and interpret model uncertainty, we also recommend the following introductory Bayesian resources: Depaoli and van de Schoot, (2017); Gabry et al., (2019); McElreath, (2020); Schad et al., (2021); van de Schoot et al., (2014). Our MELSM analysis used the brms R package (Bürkner, 2017), which uses similar syntax to packages used for building regression and mixed-effect models. We provide our brms MELSM scripts as an example (https://osf.io/4dwmg).

In summary, MELSMs are a promising approach to examine trial-by-trial variability as a signal of interest. We demonstrated how to apply this analysis framework to examine the MMN’s psychometric properties, trial-by-trial variability differences across participants, and relations to theoretically relevant behavior and covariates. We encourage researchers to take advantage of MELSMs and to leverage the provided analysis scripts to make this approach more accessible.
