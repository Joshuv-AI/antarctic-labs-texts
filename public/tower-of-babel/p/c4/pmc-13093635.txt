To the Editor:

We read with great interest the article by Sundaralingam et al. [1], titled “Investigation and outcomes in patients with non-specific pleuritis: results from the International Collaborative Effusion Database” published in ERJ Open Research. We would like to congratulate the authors for presenting a comprehensive, multi-centre study on a clinically relevant and still ambiguous topic in pleural disease. The data and conclusions offered by the authors have been informative and valuable for our clinical practice.

One of the persistent challenges in pleural medicine is the management of patients whose pleural biopsies yield a diagnosis of non-specific pleuritis (NSP). Despite multiple efforts, there is currently no consensus on the optimal duration of follow-up required to rule out false-negative results, particularly in cases of underlying malignancy. We would like to contribute to this discussion by sharing a representative case from our practice.

In patients with exudative pleural effusion where the diagnosis remains unclear despite comprehensive clinical, radiologic, laboratory and cytologic evaluation, pleural biopsy remains an essential diagnostic tool. Histopathologic evaluation typically yields one of three outcomes: malignant infiltration, granulomatous inflammation, or NSP. NSP refers to histologic findings that do not support a specific aetiology and is used as a default diagnosis when malignancy and granulomatous disease are excluded [2]. However, NSP is a heterogeneous entity, encompassing various benign aetiologies, and its pathophysiology remains poorly understood. It is often referred to as idiopathic, non-specific, or fibrinous pleuritis in the literature [2].

NSP is frequently reported in biopsy-based case series, particularly when benign diseases other than tuberculosis are considered [3–6]. In a review including 2434 medical thoracoscopies, 469 patients (19.2%) were diagnosed with NSP, and of these, 66 patients (14.2%) were later found to have malignancy [2]. Other studies have reported false-negative rates ranging from 15% to 18.8% [3, 4, 7].

While follow-up durations in previous NSP cohorts have varied between 12 and 60 months, no standardised follow-up period has been established [3–6]. However, most authors suggest a minimum of 12 months, with extended surveillance in cases where malignant pleural disease is suspected [2]. Diffuse pleural mesothelioma (DPM) is sometimes characterised by its fibrotic and heterogeneous growth pattern, which may reduce diagnostic yield. Tumour invasion may not be present in sampled areas due to focal low cellularity or sampling error, especially when pleural thickening is extensive or subpleural fibrosis predominates [8]. Consequently, false-negative rates for DPM are higher compared to other pleural malignancies. Several studies have shown that DPM accounts for a considerable portion of false-negative NSP cases [4–6].

In the study by Sundaralingam et al. [1], the false-negative rate was reported as 6%, with diagnoses made beyond 3 months in more than half of these cases. Notably, the longest interval to diagnosis was 32 months, and this case involved DPM.

We would like to present a case that further emphasises the need for long-term follow-up in patients with NSP and asbestos exposure:

A 74-year-old male presented to our clinic 22 months ago with pleuritic chest pain in the left hemithorax. He had a history of environmental asbestos exposure during childhood (ages 0–12) in a village located in our rural area, a 52 pack-year smoking history, and comorbidities including type 2 diabetes mellitus and benign prostatic hyperplasia. He was a retired primary school teacher. On physical examination, no abnormalities were found. Thoracic computed tomography imaging revealed a minimal smooth pleural thickening on the posterior costal surface of the left hemithorax, without invasion, along with scattered calcified pleural plaques (figure 1a). The patient was advised to follow up, but did not return.

Serial thoracic computed tomography (CT) images of the same patient. a) Initial thoracic CT showing local and minimal smooth pleural thickening with scattered calcified pleural plaques, without evidence of invasion. b) Follow-up CT at 22 months demonstrating massive left-sided pleural effusion with associated minimal thickening of the mediastinal and fissural pleura.

Approximately 22 months later, he presented with progressive dyspnoea again. Computed tomography imaging revealed massive left-sided pleural effusion and minimal pleural thickening involving the mediastinal pleura and fissure (figure 1b). Thoracentesis yielded a serosanguinous exudative fluid. Cytological examination revealed an abundance of atypical mesothelial cells and lymphocytes; however, two separate samples were negative for malignancy. The patient underwent medical thoracoscopy, which revealed diffuse nodular thickening of the parietal pleura with calcified plaques and nodules on the visceral pleura. Histopathology confirmed an epithelioid-type of DPM. He was staged as T2N0M0 (Stage II) and started on neoadjuvant cisplatin-pemetrexed chemotherapy.

This case highlights the need for prolonged follow-up in patients diagnosed with NSP, particularly in those with known risk factors for DPM. Patient education is crucial, and we suggest that a follow-up duration of at least 24 months may be warranted when clinical or radiological suspicion of DPM persists, even in the absence of initial diagnostic confirmation.
