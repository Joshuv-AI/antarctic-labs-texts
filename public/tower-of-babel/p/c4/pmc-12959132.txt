The Impact of COVID-19 on Alcohol-related Morbidity and Mortality: A Multicenter Study
Authors: Abhinav K. Rao, Erica Paulos, Nancy Crossley, Don C. Rockey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a rise in alcohol (ethanol) use nationwide. In this study, we investigated the effect of COVID-19 on alcohol-related hospital outcomes.MethodsDeidentified claims data spanning 2018 through 2022 from HCA’s Enterprise Data Warehouse (South Atlantic Division) were examined. The population included adults aged >18 years who had an encounter at an HCA facility and a diagnosis of alcohol use disorder or alcohol-related illnesses. Two time frames were evaluated: 2018–2019 (pre- COVID-19) and 2021–2022 (COVID-19). Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate LOS, home discharge, and mortality.ResultsOf the 72,575 patients (26,678 during the COVID-19 time period and 35,897 from 2018 to 19) meeting inclusion criteria for alcohol use and alcohol-related illness, the majority were male, had an average age of 50-years-old, and were predominantly White. Among the 26,678 COVID-19 patient admissions (2021–2022), 12,735 (35 %) were diagnosed with COVID-19 (p < 0.001). During the COVID-19 period, there was a 36 % increase in mortality, 2 % fewer discharges home, and a half day increase in average LOS (vs pre-COVID-19 period; all p < 0.001). In multivariable regression, patients who tested positive for COVID-19 had a 3-day longer length of stay, a 60 % lower likelihood of discharge to home, and a 284 % higher inpatient mortality rate compared to COVID-negative patients (all p < 0.001).ConclusionsPatients diagnosed with COVID-19 and alcohol-related morbidity had longer LOS, fewer discharges home, and higher mortality. Together, the data suggest that COVID-19 is associated with increased morbidity in patients with an alcohol-related illness.

Introduction

The impact of COVID-19 on morbidity and mortality has been studied in multiple large studies.1–12 However, for a variety of diseases, there are limited data on cofactors that may be important in determining outcome. One of the most remarkable aspects of the COVID-19 pandemic was a striking increase in alcohol (ethanol) consumption. Indeed, it has been reported that early in the pandemic, there was a 54 % increase nationwide in alcohol sales, including an increase of 262 % in online sales alone.1

Given the important potential impact of COVID-19 on alcohol-related disease, we hypothesized that alcohol-related comorbidities and mortality rates during the COVID-19 period increased. In this large multicenter study, we utilized alcohol-related ICD codes to review inpatient admissions during the pre-COVID-19 and COVID-19 time periods. The primary goal of this study was to compare inpatient mortality and length of hospital stay between the COVID-19 and pre-COVID-19 time periods.

Methods

2.1. Patients

This was a retrospective study comprising 72,575 subjects from 11 different hospitals in the south-eastern United States. The study population included all patients aged greater than 18 who had a diagnosis of alcohol use disorder or alcohol-related illnesses. Patients who were admitted to the hospital were included, while those aged less than 18 were excluded. Since the study utilized only deidentified data, IRB approval was not sought.

Alcohol use disorder and alcohol-related illnesses were defined as diagnoses indicating alcohol use resulting in organ damage or functional impairment requiring inpatient admission. A comprehensive list of ICD-10 codes was used to capture a wide range of alcohol-related conditions requiring hospitalization, including hepatic, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and neuropsychiatric disorders (Supplemental Appendix (https://scholarlycommons.gbmc.org/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1533&window=additional_files&context=jchimp)). This list was developed in collaboration with clinical experts to ensure high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. Using this extensive list, patients with alcohol-related diseases who tested positive for COVID-19 were also identified through the corresponding ICD-10 code for a COVID-19 diagnosis.

2.2. Data abstraction

Deidentified claims data spanning 2018 through 2022 (excluding 2020) from HCA’s Enterprise Data Warehouse (“EDW”) were obtained. These included medical claims for all HCA patients in all HCA facilities in the South Atlantic Division, including Florida, Georgia and South Carolina. The above data was selected based on the 10th revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) codes, which are listed in the supplemental material.

2.3. Statistical analysis

The pooled data was characterized by categorical variables of sex (male vs female), race (White vs non-White), discharge disposition (home vs not-home), COVID-19 diagnosis and mortality; and by continuous variables including age, length of stay (LOS) and markers of alcohol-related liver disease including total bilirubin, creatinine, sodium, and INR.

Observations were grouped into two categories: 2018–2019 (pre-COVID-19 pandemic) and 2021–2022 (COVID-19 pandemic). The year 2020 was excluded to establish a clear distinction between the pre-COVID-19 era and the COVID-19 era, allowing for more reliable attribution of differences in study outcomes to the impact of the pandemic. Chi-square and t-tests were performed on these two decompositions of the data. Additionally, regression (logistic and linear) were performed on the three outcome variables: home discharge, mortality, and length of stay (LOS). Statistics were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0; SPSS, Armonk, NY). P-values less than 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Among the 72,575 patients who met inclusion criteria for alcohol use and related illness from 2018 to 2022, there were 35,897 patients in the pre-COVID-19 time period (2018–2019) and 26,678 patients during the COVID-19 time period (2021–2022) (Supplementary Figure 1 (https://scholarlycommons.gbmc.org/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1533&window=additional_files&context=jchimp)). The majority of patients were male, had an average age of 50-years, and were predominantly White (Table 1). Average creatinine, bilirubin, and INR levels were similar in both groups. Among the 26,678 admissions during the COVID-19 time period, 12,735 (35 %) of these patients were diagnosed with COVID-19 (p < 0.001). During the COVID-19 period, there were 2 % fewer discharges home, a 0.50 day increase in average LOS and a 36 % increase in mortality (3.0 % vs. 2.2 %) (all p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Linear regression analyses were performed to examine length of stay for patients with alcohol-related diseases during the COVID-19 period (Table 3). Binary categories were employed to evaluate differences in outcomes. Length of stay was, on average, 0.57 days longer during the COVID-19 period compared to the pre-COVID-19 period (p < 0.001). COVID-19 positive patients had significantly longer hospitalizations, averaging 3 days longer than patients without COVID-19 (p-value <0.001). There was an increase of 0.05 days in length of stay for each additional year of age (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences in length of stay were found based on race or gender.

Regression analyses also evaluated home discharge rates pre- and post-COVID-19 (Table 4). The study found a 29 % increase in the likelihood of being discharged home during the COVID-19 period (OR = 1.29, p < 0.001). However, among patients diagnosed with COVID-19, there was a 60 % decrease in the likelihood of being discharged home (OR = 0.40). Younger patients and non-White patients were more likely to be discharged home (both p < 0.001). Gender did not significantly impact the likelihood of being discharged home (p = 0.569).

Overall, of the 36,678 alcohol-related hospital admissions during the COVID-19 period, 1094 (3.0 %) patients died (vs. 2.2 % in the pre- COVID-19 period, p < 0.001). After adjusting for age, race, gender, and COVID-19 status in multivariable regression, patients with alcohol-related diseases admitted during the COVID-19 period had lower in-hospital all-cause mortality compared to those admitted in the pre-COVID-19 period (OR = 0.78, p < 0.001). However, COVID-19 -positive individuals experienced a 284 % increase in mortality during hospitalization (OR = 2.84, p < 0.001 vs. COVID-19 negative patients) (Table 5). Race did not have a significant impact on mortality rates. For every 1-year increase in age, the likelihood of in-hospital mortality increased by 5 % (OR = 1.05, p < 0.001). Additionally, men were 13 % more likely to die during hospitalization compared to women (OR = 1.13, p < 0.05).

Discussion

In this large multicenter study of patients with alcohol-related disorders, there was a notable rise in the number of hospital admissions for alcohol-related disease during the COVID-19 pandemic. COVID-19 positive patients with alcohol-related disease had longer lengths of stay and were less likely to be discharged home. Compared to the pre- COVID-19 period, overall mortality increased by 36 % during the COVID-19 period (3.0 % vs. 2.2 %). In addition, patients diagnosed with both COVID-19 and alcohol-related disease experienced an even more pronounced increase in mortality, rising by 284 %.

The increased mortality rate in COVID-19 positive patients with alcohol-related disorders raises the possibility that COVID-19 somehow exacerbates the severity of alcohol-related disorders. This is particularly notable given that alcohol-related comorbidities were of heightened severity during the pandemic.13 Existing studies have indicated that multiorgan failure is the most common cause of death in COVID-19 patients, particularly among the elderly with significant comorbidities.14–16 Elderly patients with alcohol-related comorbidities and COVID-19 experienced higher mortality than their younger counterparts.13 This suggests that the combination of COVID-19 and alcohol-related comorbidities contributed to a more severe clinical phenotype, particularly in older individuals.8,13,17,18 Future studies that focus on specific alcohol-related diagnoses affected by current and future pandemics may help identify targeted, practice-changing interventions.

The pre- and post-COVID-19 time periods revealed a significant difference in LOS. During the COVID-19 period, the average hospital stay was 0.57 days shorter than in the pre-COVID-19 period. The reason for this is not entirely clear, though we suspect that during this time, patients without COVID-19 may have been discharged more rapidly to offset demand for beds required by COVID-19 patients, who required longer hospitalization. This is consistent with the finding that patients diagnosed with both COVID-19 and alcohol-related ICD codes experienced an extended average hospital stay, which was three days longer than those without COVID-19. The reasons for increased LOS for COVID-19 patients is likely multifactorial, ranging from delay in discharge caused by regulations implemented by rehab and skilled nursing facilities to prevent the spread of the virus, to a greater severity of illness in COVID-19 patients.

During the COVID-19 era, there was a significant shift in discharge patterns. Compared to the pre- COVID-19 period, patients in the COVID-19 period without a diagnosis of COVID-19 were more frequently discharged home, while COVID-19 positive patients were sent to nursing care facilities, rehabilitation centers, and other hospitals for more advanced care. This change may have been driven by the pandemic’s health demands, which exceeded the capacity of many hospitals, resulting in a shortage of hospital beds, ICU beds, and ventilators. COVID-19 positive patients with alcohol-related comorbidities experienced a more severe clinical phenotype, requiring higher levels of care upon discharge, or died in the hospital, reducing the number of home discharges.

We recognize potential limitations in our study. First, our study was limited to patients in the southeastern United States, and our findings may not be generalizable to other regions. However, with 11 hospitals spread throughout the southeast region, we suspect that the population examined is likely very typical of a US cohort of patients that went through the COVID-19 pandemic. Second, it is possible that not every alcohol-related disease was captured through data retrieval using available ICD codes. However, we took great measures to ensure a comprehensive list of all ICD codes pertaining to alcohol-related diseases (see Appendix). This approach mitigated the issue of selection bias; however, it may allow for potential misclassification of diagnoses dependent on the accuracy of ICD coding. While we have no evidence to suggest systematic misclassification, we included a comprehensive list of ICD codes—many of which have been validated in prior studies—to minimize this risk. Additionally, residual confounding may be present due to variables such as liver disease severity (e.g., MELD), critical care admission status, and other clinical or sociodemographic factors. However, the large sample size likely mitigated some of this variability, and baseline MELD components were comparable between groups (Table 1).

In conclusion, this study highlights the significant impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on patients with alcohol-related disorders. We found that COVID-19 positive patients with alcohol-related disorders had longer hospitalizations and fewer discharges home compared to patients without COVID-19. Furthermore, these patients had a dramatic increase in mortality (284 %). Although the COVID-19 pandemic has concluded, these data have implications for future pandemics, and since COVID-19 continues to be an important illness for many patients.
