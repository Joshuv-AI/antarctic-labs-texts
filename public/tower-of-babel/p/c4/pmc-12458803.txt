Prevalence of and factors influencing elevated blood pressure among Chinese adolescents: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lijuan Zheng, Weiwei Xiang, Rui Zheng, Xinwen Min, Li Dai, Jun Chen, Juanjuan Lu, Yujue Wang, Xiang Liu, Wenwen Wu, Hao Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to examine the prevalence of elevated blood pressure (BP) among adolescents aged 13–17 years in Shiyan city, Hubei province, China. Additionally, it analyses the distribution characteristics and potential factors influencing elevated BP, providing a scientific foundation for the prevention and management of hypertension in adolescents.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingA total of 11 schools in central China.ParticipantsFrom October 2023 to January 2024, a cross-sectional study involving 8534 students aged 13–17 years from 11 schools in central China was conducted. Convenience sampling was used to select participants, and data were collected through questionnaires and medical physical examinations.Primary outcome measuresThis study used physiological measurement to assess BP levels, while questionnaire surveys and anthropometric measurements were employed to identify associated factors.ResultsThe overall detection rate of elevated BP was 22.7% (20.2% for boys and 25.6% for girls). Increased body mass index (BMI) (OR: 1.026, 95% CI: 1.003 to 1.048), neck circumference (OR: 1.099, 95% CI: 1.073 to 1.126) and chest circumference (OR: 1.015, 95% CI: 1.007 to 1.023) were associated with an increased risk of elevated BP. Conversely, longer screen time was linked to a lower risk of elevated BP (OR: 0.961, 95% CI: 0.942 to 0.98). Girls (OR: 1.592, 95% CI: 1.419 to 1.787) were at higher risk than boys. Additionally, not consuming meat or poultry (OR: 2.029, 95% CI: 1.171 to 3.514) was identified as a risk factor for elevated BP.ConclusionThe distribution of elevated BP among adolescents exhibited population heterogeneity. Elevated BP was found to be associated with higher BMI, neck circumference and chest circumference, as well as the absence of meat consumption. Additionally, targeted attention to girls is essential for preventing elevated BP.Keywords: adolescents, blood pressure, prevalence, risk factors, observational study

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is a global public health issue and one of the leading causes of premature death.1 2 The Global HTN Report 2023, published by the WHO, indicated that the number of people affected by HTN has increased globally from 650 million in 1990 to 1.3 billion in 2019.3 In China, the prevalence of HTN rose from 5.1% in 1959 to 27.5% in 2018.4 Research has established a direct correlation between blood pressure (BP) levels and the prevalence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. For instance, a study conducted in an Asian population suggested that each 10 mm Hg increase in systolic BP (SBP) may be associated with a 53% and 31% higher risk of stroke and fatal myocardial infarction, respectively,5 although the small sample size warrants cautious interpretation. The Global Burden of Disease Study estimates that HTN was responsible for approximately 10.85 million deaths worldwide in 2019, accounting for 31% of all deaths.6 From 1990 to 2019, the absolute number of deaths attributable to HTN in China increased by 112.72% (from 1.22 to 2.60 million), while the mortality rate rose by 77.04% (from 103.25 to 182.79 per 100 000 population). Compared with 1990, the increases observed in 2019 in China were 1.89 times and 7.36 times higher than the corresponding global percentage increases in HTN-attributable deaths and mortality rate, respectively.7

While HTN has traditionally been considered a condition of adulthood, recent studies have revealed an alarming rise in its prevalence among children and adolescents, posing new challenges for global public health.8 This shift represents a considerable challenge to global adolescent health, necessitating urgent intervention. In India, according to data from the Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey (2016–2018) reanalysed in a 2022 study, the prevalence of HTN among children aged 13 years and older was reported to be 25.1%.9 In Tunisia, a cross-sectional study conducted between 2017 and 2018 reported an HTN prevalence of 15.4% among adolescents aged 14–19 years.10 Similarly, in Turkey, a 2019 cross-sectional study found a prevalence of 14.8% in school-aged adolescents within the same age range.11 In China, data from the China Nutrition and Health Surveillance (2015–2017) revealed a higher prevalence of 24.9% among children and adolescents aged 6–17 years.12 A national survey conducted between 2017 and 2018 among 12 543 Chinese adolescents aged 12–17 years reported notable sex differences in prevalence, with rates of 22.3% in boys and 20.0% in girls.13 Additionally, a 2021 survey conducted in Zhengzhou showed a prevalence of 26%.14 In contrast, substantially lower rates have been reported in Italy (3.6% based on 2020 data)15 and the USA (3.7% according to National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 1999–2018).16 A 2021 meta-analysis that synthesised data from Southeast Asian studies across various periods estimated an overall prevalence of 32.45% among school-aged adolescents.17 These findings reflect a complex and heterogeneous global landscape, in which China exhibits a particularly high and regionally variable burden of adolescent HTN.

The diagnosis of HTN in adolescents is often delayed. This may be attributed, on one hand, to its asymptomatic nature,18 and on the other, to systemic gaps in clinical practice—particularly the fact that BP measurement is not routinely performed during paediatric consultations, unlike in adult care.19 Such delays in diagnosis may pose immediate and long-term risks to their physical health. It is a major risk factor for the early onset of type 2 diabetes, regardless of the adolescent’s body mass index (BMI).20 Diabetes can lead to secondary complications, including retinal damage.21 HTN causes gradual, painless deterioration in visual function due to impaired retinal microcirculation and damage to the retinal nerve fibre layers.22 Other studies have also linked adolescent-onset HTN to an increased risk of stroke in adolescence.23 Moreover, HTN in late adolescence has been identified as a modifiable risk factor for renal damage.24 In addition to elevating the risk of cardiovascular events in adulthood,25 HTN in adolescence predicts the future development of HTN in later life.26 Given these risks, early detection and treatment of elevated BP in adolescents could be an effective strategy for preventing and managing HTN in this age group.

Previous studies conducted in China primarily diagnosed HTN in adolescents using a single cross-sectional BP measurement.27 28 However, BP is dynamic and can fluctuate due to various external factors. Even at rest, BP can vary, and the ‘mean reversion’ effect may cause BP values to decrease gradually as more measurements are taken.29 According to the 2018 Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of HTN in China,30 adolescents should undergo at least three BP measurements on different days, with each measurement exceeding the elevated BP threshold (P95). The intervals between measurements should be one to 2 weeks, and a consistent rise in BP over time is used to confirm a diagnosis of HTN. In studies based on a single time point and cross-sectional design, ‘HTN’ is more accurately referred to as ‘elevated BP’. Research has shown that elevated BP in adolescence is strongly associated with increased pulse wave velocity,31 greater carotid intima–media thickness32 and left ventricular hypertrophy in adulthood.33 These changes are linked to a higher risk of cardiovascular diseases and mortality later in life.34 Given the evidence that elevated BP in childhood and adolescence predicts adult cardiovascular abnormalities and long-term risks,32 studying elevated BP in adolescents serves as a critical early indicator for identifying potential HTN—aligning with preventive strategies that target the childhood origins of adult cardiovascular disease.

Given China’s vast geographical diversity and regional disparities in economic development and lifestyle patterns, the prevalence and associated factors of elevated BP among adolescents may vary significantly across different regions. Shiyan city, located at the geographical centre of China and near the provinces of Henan, Shanxi and Chongqing city, offers a representative sample of the younger population in the central region. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of elevated BP among adolescents in Shiyan city, central China, and to explore its associated sociodemographic, behavioural and anthropometric factors. The findings will inform the development of regionally tailored strategies for the early prevention and control of adolescent HTN. Measuring instruments must be calibrated in accordance with strict operational guidelines.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted using a convenience sampling method to recruit students aged 13–17 years from 11 schools in Shiyan city, Hubei province, China. The study population included both junior high school students (typically aged 13–15 years) and first-year and second-year senior high school students (typically aged 16–17 years). Third-year senior high school students were excluded due to their intensive academic preparation for the National College Entrance Examination. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians after providing a clear explanation of the study’s purpose. Participants must possess sufficient cognitive ability to accurately answer questions, independently complete questionnaires and cooperate with BP and anthropometric measurements. Individuals with a history of diseases that might affect BP, such as cardiovascular, renal or endocrine diseases, were excluded from the survey. Additionally, individuals who declined to participate were not included in the study sample.

Throughout the survey process, all participants were encouraged to ask questions. The following formula was used to calculate the sample size: n = Z2PQ/d2, where Z=1.96 at 95% CI, p=prevalence of elevated BP, Q=1−P and d=absolute allowable error.35 In our study, the prevalence of elevated BP was presumed to be 13.48% based on a previous study8 and d=0.01348. The sample size yielded was at least 2466 in our study. A total of 8537 adolescents were invited to participate. After excluding three individuals with missing data of key variables (eg, gender), the final analysis included 8534 participants.

Measurements

All investigators underwent standardised training and assessment before the study began. On successful completion, they were authorised to participate in the data collection process. Before formal data collection, the researchers explained the study’s purpose and relevance to the participants, who then completed the survey in the classroom.

BP measurement

BP, including SBP and diastolic BP (DBP) measurements, was assessed by trained medical personnel using a calibrated Omron HEM-1020 automated electronic sphygmomanometer. Participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise for at least 1 hour before BP measurement and to remain still for at least 5 min prior to the assessment; the sphygmomanometer was positioned at the level of the brachial artery to ensure accurate measurement; BP readings were taken twice, with a 1–2 min interval between each measurement; the average of the two readings was recorded; if the SBP or DBP differed by more than 5 mm Hg, a third reading was conducted, and the average of all three measurements was used.30

Operational definitions of elevated BP

The BP standards used in this study were based on China’s ‘Reference for Screening Elevated BP Among Children and Adolescents Aged 7 to 18 Years’ (WS/T610-2018): according to this standard, elevated BP in adolescents aged 7–18 is defined as systolic and/or diastolic BP at or above the 95th percentile (P95) for their respective gender, age and height percentiles.36

Other anthropometric measurements

Other anthropometric data, including height and weight (which were used to calculate BMI), as well as neck circumference, chest circumference, waist circumference and hip circumference, were collected by trained medical personnel. In accordance with China’s ‘Technical Standards for Physical Examination of Students’ (GB/T26343-2010),37 height and weight were measured using an ultrasonic device (Meilen MSG003SC) with participants in light clothing and bare feet. BMI was determined by dividing weight (kg) by height squared (m2), as these measurements served to derive this key anthropometric indicator. A flexible measuring tape (ER301) was used to measure neck, chest, waist and hip circumferences.

Sociodemographic and lifestyle data

The following sociodemographic data were collected using self-administered questionnaires completed by the adolescents: age (years), gender, ethnicity (Han, others), academic performance (good, moderate) and personality (introverted, neutral, extroverted).

In this study, lifestyle and dietary habit data were used as explanatory variables. Specifically, these variables included screen time (hours, average daily hours in the past month), sitting time (hours, average daily hours in the past month), mode of commuting to school (on-campus living, walking, riding or other, current school term), active smoking (yes/no, smoked ≥1 cigarette in past month), passive smoking (yes/no, exposed to secondhand smoke ≥15 min/day in past month), alcohol consumption (never, occasionally (1–3 times/month), often (1–2 times/week) in the past month), daily physical exercise (<30 min, 30–60 min, or ≥60 min, average daily duration in past week), hearing device use (every day, occasionally, never in past month) and breakfast consumption (every day, occasionally, never, frequency in past week). In addition, the frequency of consuming eggs, dairy products, vegetables, fish or shrimp, meat or poultry, fruit, sweets, fried foods and coffee (past week) was categorised as every day, occasionally or never.

To ensure data accuracy and reliability, at least two researchers were present in each classroom to provide support and address any questions from the students. Additionally, a random sampling review of the completed questionnaires was conducted, followed by dual data entry and logical verification, to enhance data accuracy and completeness, with any discrepancies resolved by reviewing original materials.

Sleep quality

Sleeping conditions were assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), developed by Buysse et al in 1989.38 The Chinese version of the PSQI demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.842. The scale consists of 19 self-assessment items and five additional assessment items. High scores on the 0–3 point scale indicate poor sleep quality. In this study, a PSQI total score of 5 or higher indicated poor sleep quality, while a score below 5 indicated good sleep quality.39

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and plotting were performed using SPSS V.26.0 and R V.4.2.2. Given the large sample size (n=8537), continuous variables were reported as mean±SD, with central tendency robustly estimated according to the Central Limit Theorem. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using independent-samples t-tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and the χ² test was used to compare groups. Univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore potential factors associated with elevated BP. Variables identified as statistically significant (p<0.05) in the univariate analyses were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. A backward stepwise method was applied to retain variables with p<0.05, and multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factors (VIFs), ensuring that all VIFs were below 10. A two-sided test was used, with a significance level of α=0.05.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the study design, conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

The study population consisted of 8534 adolescents, with a mean age of 14.28 years (SD=1.20). The majority of participants were boys (54.0%), aged 13–15 years (80.5%) and enrolled in junior high school (73.1%). The mean SBP and DBP were 114.5±12.5 and 72.1±8.6 mm Hg, respectively. Significant differences in SBP and DBP were observed among adolescents in different age groups (p<0.001). Examination of factors influencing the detection rate of elevated BP in adolescents

Univariate analysis of elevated BP in adolescents

The results of the univariate analysis showed statistically significant differences in the detection rate of elevated BP across various factors, including BMI, waist circumference, hip circumference, neck circumference, chest circumference, screen time, gender, personality, physical exercise and frequency of consuming meat or poultry (p<0.05) (online supplemental table 1).

Multivariate analysis of elevated BP in adolescents

The dependent variable was the BP group (0=normal BP, 1=elevated BP), and the statistically significant variables from the univariate analysis were included as independent variables in a multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results showed that increased BMI (OR: 1.026, 95% CI: 1.003 to 1.048), neck circumference (OR: 1.099, 95% CI: 1.073 to 1.126) and chest circumference (OR: 1.015, 95% CI: 1.007 to 1.023) were associated with an increased risk of elevated BP. By contrast, the risk of elevated BP decreased with increased screen time (OR: 0.961, 95% CI: 0.942 to 0.980). Girls (OR: 1.592, 95% CI: 1.419 to 1.787) exhibited a higher risk of elevated BP than boys. Not consuming meat or poultry was identified as a risk factor for elevated BP (OR: 2.029, 95% CI: 1.171 to 3.514) (figure 1).

Discussion

HTN and elevated BP in children and adolescents have been widely studied in China and globally. Previous investigations have reported varying prevalence rates across different regions and age groups. For example, a study in Jiangsu province found a detection rate of 16.2%,40 another study in Jinan city reported 12.5%,41 and a large survey across seven provinces in mainland China in 2013 showed a prevalence of 13.2%.42 Additionally, Ma et al43 reported that the national detection rate of HTN among children and adolescents in 2015, according to the 2018 Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of HTN in Children, was approximately 20.5%.

In this context, our study is the first to estimate the prevalence of elevated BP and its associated factors among adolescents in Shiyan city, a representative region in central China. The results revealed a detection rate of 22.7% for elevated BP, which is notably higher than most previous regional and national estimates. We also identified several independent predictors of elevated BP, including BMI, neck circumference, chest circumference, screen time, gender and poultry meat consumption.

Several factors may contribute to these findings. First, the single-point cross-sectional measurement used in this study is considered an inadequate approach for diagnosing HTN. Paediatric HTN guidelines in various countries recommend that adolescent HTN diagnosis should be based on at least three separate BP measurements taken on different days.44 Hu et al found that the crude prevalence of newly diagnosed HTN during initial screening was remarkably higher than the prevalence after confirming diagnoses with multiple measurements.28 Liu et al evaluated the BP of 16 220 adolescents aged 12–17 in China using the ‘2018 Chinese HTN Prevention and Treatment Guidelines’. The prevalence of newly diagnosed HTN was 24.1%, but after multiple measurements, it decreased to 12.5% after the second measurement and to only 8.4% after the third.45 Second, the ‘white coat HTN’ effect may have contributed to the high detection rate. Adolescents may experience anxiety when BP is measured by someone in a white coat, leading to temporary increases in BP. Third, inadequate preparation by the subjects may have played a role. Despite the research team’s efforts to explain the study’s purpose thoroughly and advise participants to avoid intense exercise, tea or coffee before measurements, compliance could not be guaranteed. However, when compared globally, the detection rate in this study is lower than that reported in a study of adolescent patients at a hospital in Colombia (30.6%).46 This discrepancy could be attributed to a more pronounced ‘white coat effect’ in the hospital setting and the potential impact of treatment medications on BP readings.47

The results of this study indicated that the likelihood of elevated BP increased with higher BMI, neck circumference and chest circumference, all of which are indicators of obesity. Previous studies have shown that obesity can lead to dysfunction of the autonomic nervous system in adolescents, which stimulates the sympathetic nervous system and raises BP.48 Additionally, adipose tissue in obese individuals secretes leptin, interleukins and other substances, which can contribute to atherosclerosis and further increase the risk of elevated BP.49 Moreover, given that adipocytes are the primary source of leptin production, obesity has been linked to elevated leptin levels in adolescents, which in turn induces increased sympathetic nervous activity,50 ultimately resulting in increased BP.51

The relationship between screen time and HTN among adolescents remains inconsistent in domestic and international studies. A study conducted by Farhangi et al52 found that high levels of screen time were correlated with an increased risk of HTN by about 15%, while Carson and Janssen53 reported no significant correlation between screen behaviour and the risk of HTN. The findings of this study, however, indicated that the probability of identifying elevated BP in adolescents was inversely associated with increased screen time. In this study, electronic screens were frequently used for teaching in schools, resulting in extended exposure. Additionally, the pervasive use of mobile phones among adolescents has contributed to a tendency to use these devices for entertainment or socialisation during leisure time, particularly at night. This entertainment or socialisation may help alleviate stress and reduce depressive moods. Research has shown remarkable improvements in depressive moods among individuals who used their phones on more than three nights per week compared with those who did not use their phones at night.54 Negative emotions, such as depression, in adolescents are well established to be closely associated with elevated BP,55 potentially due to disruptions in the autonomic nervous system.56

This study found that female adolescents have a higher risk of elevated BP compared with their male counterparts. Some scholars suggest that these gender differences in BP elevation may be related to variations in BMI. During puberty, girls mature earlier than boys, resulting in earlier increases in growth indicators, such as weight, height and body fat. Consequently, the period of rapid BP increase occurs later in boys than in girls.57 Previous studies have shown that the incidence of HTN tends to increase with high BMI levels.58 In our study, female adolescents (20.61 kg/m²) had a remarkably higher BMI compared with male adolescents (19.87 kg/m²), which may explain the higher risk of elevated BP in females, consistent with findings from other research.59 Moreover, female adolescents exhibit higher levels of neuroticism, anxiety and irritability than their male counterparts.60 In addition to daily life and academic pressures, chronic stress can impact multiple physiological systems. Activation of the sympathetic nervous system leads to adrenal cortex dominance, resulting in elevated release of glucocorticoids, such as cortisol. These glucocorticoids considerably influence the stress response through the autonomic nervous system, leading to vasoconstriction and increased BP.61

Furthermore, this study identified the lack of poultry meat consumption as a risk factor for elevated BP compared with daily poultry meat intake. This association may be attributed to poultry meat’s richness in high-quality proteins, ω−3 fatty acids, B vitamins, iron, zinc and nutrients known to help reduce BP.62

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample was drawn using a convenience sampling method from a limited number of junior and senior high schools in central China. While this approach ensured the feasibility of data collection, it resulted in a relatively homogeneous sample and may have introduced selection bias, thereby limiting the generalisability of the findings to the broader adolescent population. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference due to the lack of temporal sequence, as it cannot determine whether the identified predictors (eg, BMI, screen time) precede the onset of elevated BP. Third, some data (eg, academic performance, personality) were collected through self-reported questionnaires, using measures that may lack standardised or validated instruments. This could introduce information bias and affect the accuracy of the data collected.

Conclusion

This study identified a relatively high prevalence of elevated BP (22.7%) among adolescents in Shiyan city. BMI, neck circumference, chest circumference, screen time, gender and poultry meat consumption emerged as potential independent predictors of elevated BP in this population. These findings provide critical baseline data for adolescent BP epidemiology. To address the causal ambiguity inherent in cross-sectional design, future prospective multicentre cohort studies are recommended to: (1) determine the temporal precedence of changes in BMI, neck/chest circumference and screen time relative to elevated BP onset; (2) investigate the dose-response relationship between poultry meat consumption and BP elevation over time; and (3) clarify gender-specific longitudinal BP trajectories in adolescents.
