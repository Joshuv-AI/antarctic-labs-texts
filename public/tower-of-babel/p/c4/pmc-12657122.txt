Treatment‐related amyloid clearance (TRAC): a framework to characterize patients in the era of anti‐amyloid therapies
Authors: Renaud La Joie, Jeffrey L Cummings, Jeffrey L Dage, Douglas Galasko, Milos D Ikonomovic, Thomas K Karikari, Susan M Landau, Jorge J Llibre‐Guerra, Catherine J Mummery, Rik Ossenkoppele, Julie C Price, Shannon L Risacher, Ruben Smith, Christopher H van Dyck, Maria C Carrillo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing regulatory approval of anti‐amyloid beta (Aβ) therapies, a better characterization of patients receiving these treatments is needed to guide clinical management and inclusion in future trials. This Alzheimer's Association‐convened workgroup proposes a terminology, treatment‐related amyloid clearance (TRAC), to reflect alterations in disease pathophysiology based on biomarker evidence for clearance of Aβ deposits. TRAC designates biomarker‐defined pharmacodynamic changes, rather than direct neuropathological evidence, and applies to individuals with (1) pretreatment biomarker confirmation of cerebral Aβ deposition, (2) treatment with an Aβ‐targeting therapy, and (3) a follow‐up biomarker test indicative of partial or full clearance of Aβ deposits. The workgroup currently recommends defining TRAC using amyloid‐positron emission tomography (PET) and emphasizes the role of quantitative measurements for defining the degree of clearance. This framework is expected to be adapted over time in response to rapidly evolving biomarker and clinical advances and with the accumulation of real‐world data on patients receiving anti‐Aβ therapies.Highlights TRAC identifies patients with biomarker evidence for clearance of amyloid deposits.TRAC is currently defined using amyloid‐PET.Full TRAC means that PET levels dropped below predetermined positivity threshold.Partial TRAC means that PET levels dropped significantly but remain above threshold.This framework is meant to guide future research on patients receiving treatment. INTRODUCTION

The recent regulatory approval of three anti‐amyloid beta (Aβ) monoclonal antibodies (MABs) constitutes a major breakthrough for the treatment of patients in the early symptomatic stages of Alzheimer's disease (AD). The development of these drugs and the design of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have relied on the development of biomarkers for patient screening and pharmacodynamic monitoring. Amyloid‐β positron emission tomography (amyloid‐PET), a validated imaging biomarker to detect and quantify the burden of fibrillar Aβ, has shown clearance of Aβ deposits in patients treated with MABs. Meta‐analyses have identified that, across RCTs, anti‐Aβ MABs that most effectively reduced amyloid‐PET signal were associated with the most clinical benefit. 1 , 2 The effect of anti‐Aβ MABs on amyloid‐PET was a key argument in the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)'s decision to grant aducanumab and lecanemab accelerated approval before lecanemab and donanemab later received full approval based on clinical outcomes.

Beyond group‐level trends in biomarker changes, some RCTs have explored amyloid‐PET changes at the individual patient level, documenting the proportion of participants becoming amyloid‐PET negative 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 (Table 1). Prospective tracking of treatment‐related changes in amyloid‐PET was an integral part of the donanemab trials, as participants were switched to placebo when their amyloid‐PET levels dropped below predetermined thresholds. 3 , 4

As patients are now receiving anti‐Aβ MABs in clinical settings in multiple countries, there is a need to better characterize patients with biomarker evidence for Aβ clearance to guide clinical management and care. To this end, the Alzheimer's Association gathered a group of academic clinicians and scientists with expertise in AD trials and biomarkers to address clinical and terminology issues related to Aβ clearance. This group aimed to summarize the current literature, identify gaps in knowledge, make recommendations on nomenclature, and discuss potential operationalization and implementation‐related issues.

CONCEPT AND NOMENCLATURE

The workgroup proposes the terminology treatment‐related amyloid clearance (TRAC) to indicate biomarker evidence for clearance of cerebral Aβ deposits. This term highlights that natural disease mechanisms have been altered and would apply to individuals who fulfill three criteria:

Had biomarker evidence for cerebral Aβ deposition before treatment initiation.

Had biomarker evidence for cerebral Aβ deposition before treatment initiation.

Received treatment with a drug that targets cerebral Aβ neuropathology. While current data primarily come from anti‐Aβ MABs, this framework could theoretically be applied to other therapeutic modalities targeting Aβ (e.g., vaccines, small molecules).

Received treatment with a drug that targets cerebral Aβ neuropathology. While current data primarily come from anti‐Aβ MABs, this framework could theoretically be applied to other therapeutic modalities targeting Aβ (e.g., vaccines, small molecules).

Have a follow‐up biomarker test indicative of Aβ deposit clearance, that is, full or partial normalization of biomarker compared to pretreatment levels. As detailed below (Section 4), the workgroup currently only endorses the use of amyloid‐PET as the post‐treatment biomarker, based on the data available at the time of publication.

Have a follow‐up biomarker test indicative of Aβ deposit clearance, that is, full or partial normalization of biomarker compared to pretreatment levels. As detailed below (Section 4), the workgroup currently only endorses the use of amyloid‐PET as the post‐treatment biomarker, based on the data available at the time of publication.

TRAC as a dynamic biomarker status

TRAC is a biomarker classification reflecting biomarker evidence for Aβ clearance, rather than direct neuropathological evidence. We acknowledge the paucity of currently available autopsy data from patients treated with MABs (Section 3.2), which will be necessary to characterize the neuropathological underpinnings of biomarker changes.

TRAC is defined by biomarkers and, although it may have clinical implications, is not intended to identify a clinical syndrome. Note that all data reviewed in this manuscript refer to trials at symptomatic stages of AD, but TRAC could be identified at any disease stage, including in cognitively unimpaired participants in the preclinical stage of AD. 11

Like other biomarker categorization schemes, TRAC reflects a state at a given time point, rather than a permanent trait. Data from RCTs have shown that biomarker trajectories, including amyloid‐PET outcomes, are dynamic during and after treatment with anti‐Aβ MABs. Therefore, partial/full TRAC status can be transient and potentially reversible over time. In TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ, individuals treated with donanemab whose amyloid‐PET levels dropped below positivity thresholds (i.e., full TRAC) were switched to placebo; amyloid‐PET signal then reaccumulated at a rate of ∼3.4 Centiloids [CL])/year, 12 comparable to estimates of reliable CL accumulation from studies of natural disease history. 13

Articulation with revised criteria for diagnosis and staging

By definition, the TRAC terminology implies that natural disease processes have been altered by anti‐Aβ therapies. This classification is meant to complement the revised criteria for diagnosis and staging of AD, 14 which focuses on the biomarker‐based characterization of the disease's natural history. According to these criteria, the diagnosis of AD requires a positive result for one of the core diagnostic biomarkers (Box 1).

Both T1 biomarkers and hybrid ratios are highly predictive of amyloid‐PET positivity in patients who have not been treated with anti‐Aβ MABs. 15 , 16

Although anti‐Aβ MABs can decrease amyloid‐PET levels (Table 1), a “negative” amyloid‐PET in a patient treated with anti‐Aβ MABs (i.e., full TRAC) should not be interpreted as the absence of underlying AD as it would be in a treatment‐naive patient. TRAC should not be interpreted as AD being cured; instead, it reflects a modified state of AD that differs from the natural disease pathophysiology.

Importantly, anti‐Aβ therapies likely alter relationships among biomarkers; in this modified disease state, the associations among biomarkers, clinical measures, and underlying neuropathology might differ from those occurring in observational studies (Section 3.1).

Correspondence with existing terms

Previous publications reported patients who would fulfill our criteria for full TRAC (Table 1) using a variety of terms: “participants in the donanemab group who had amyloid‐negative status (defined as an amyloid plaque level of <24.10 CL),” 3 “participants reaching amyloid clearance (<24.1 CL),” 4 “participants reached a complete amyloid clearance threshold of 24.1 CL,” 10 “ amyloid‐negative status (≤24 CL) in … participants receiving gantenerumab,” 5 and “subjects … converted from amyloid positive to amyloid negative ”. 7

Because of this heterogeneity, we aimed to harmonize the language used to describe these individuals. The creation of a specific term and abbreviation, i.e., TRAC, aimed at avoiding the use of broad terms (e.g., “clearance”) that could also describe more general biological processes; as described earlier, TRAC is a biomarker‐based classification with specific criteria. In addition, our framework recommends identifying patients who see a reduction of amyloid‐PET signal while remaining above positivity thresholds (partial TRAC), which has not been systematically reported elsewhere.

CHARACTERIZATION OF TRAC: CURRENT (GAPS IN) KNOWLEDGE

Trajectories of “core 1” (A/T1) biomarkers

Aducanumab

Amyloid‐PET was collected at the beginning of the aducanumab EMERGE and ENGAGE studies to confirm the diagnosis of AD and at weeks 26 and 78. In the high‐dose arm, 48% of patients in EMERGE and 31% of patients in ENGAGE had a negative amyloid‐PET based on a standard uptake value ratio (SUVR) < 1.1 (∼ 20.2 CL) at week 78. 6 When analyzed as a continuous variable, amyloid‐PET levels showed decreases of 71% (EMERGE) and 59% (ENGAGE) compared to baseline levels in the high‐dose arms.

Markers for phosphorylated and secreted AD tau (T1 biomarkers) showed smaller changes than amyloid‐PET. Plasma phosphorylated tau 181 (p‐tau181) decreased by 13% (EMERGE) and 16% (ENGAGE) over 78 weeks in the high‐dose arm. Changes in plasma p‐tau181 were correlated with reductions in amyloid‐PET (r = 0.38 in EMERGE, r = 0.42 in ENGAGE) when assessed across all groups, including placebo, low‐dose arms, and high‐dose arms. 6 The trials also included a small cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) substudy (n <20 in the high‐dose arms); increases in CSF Aβ42 were observed in both ENGAGE and EMERGE, while CSF p‐tau181 and total tau significantly decreased in EMERGE only.

Lecanemab

In the PET sub‐study of the lecanemab phase 2 trial, 81% of treated patients had a visually negative amyloid‐PET after 18 months of treatment with the dose recommended for clinical use. 8 Across the n = 5 treatment arms, a decrease in amyloid‐PET was correlated with an increase in plasma Aβ42/40 (r = 0.79; p = 0.11) and a decrease in plasma p‐tau181 (r = 0.85; p = 0.07). In the phase 2 study, participants were followed after treatment termination for ∼24 months before open‐label treatment was initiated. During this treatment gap, there was a slight amyloid‐PET re‐accumulation (∼6 CL), a 47% decrease in plasma Aβ42/40, and a 24% increase in plasma p‐tau181. 7 In the phase 3 PET substudy, amyloid‐PET levels decreased by 55.6 CL (from baseline 77.9 CL) over 18 months in the treated group, while the placebo group increased by 3.6 CL. 9 By the end of the trial, 68% of treated patients had a negative amyloid‐PET scan based on a predetermined threshold of 30 CL.

Donanemab

Treatment with donanemab over 76 weeks reduced amyloid‐PET levels by 84.1 CL in the phase 2 trial (from baseline levels: 107.6 CL 3 , 10 ) and 87.0 CL in the phase 3 study (baseline: 103.5 4 ). Amyloid‐PET levels dropped below the predetermined 24.1 CL threshold in 68% of patients in the phase 2 study 3 , 10 and 78% in the phase 3 trial. 4 Long‐term data on post‐treatment amyloid‐PET re‐accumulation are not yet available, but modeling studies using donanemab observations indicate that several years are need for PET signal to re‐accumulate beyond the 24.1 CL threshold (median: 3.9 years, 95% prediction interval 1.9 to 8.3). 10

In the donanemab phase 2 trial, plasma p‐tau217 decreased by 23% over 72 weeks of treatment. When combining longitudinal data from both placebo and treatment arms, changes in plasma p‐tau217 and changes in amyloid‐PET levels were correlated (r = 0.48; p < 0.001). 17 Aβ42/40 plasma levels were also measured in the phase 2 study and showed no significant drug–placebo difference. 17

Summary: PET‐fluid biomarker associations and differences in treatment

Altogether, amyloid‐PET data from anti‐Aβ MAB trials indicate that, after 18 months of treatment with the recommended dose of FDA‐approved anti‐Aβ MABs, around two‐thirds of patients fulfilled study‐specific criteria for full TRAC (Table 1). These patients had a concomitant increase in plasma Aβ42/40 and decreases in p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 (see recent comprehensive review 18 ). Plasma changes correlated with decreases in amyloid‐PET, although the magnitude of treatment‐related fluid biomarker changes was smaller than with amyloid‐PET. In addition, unlike amyloid‐PET, fluid biomarker levels (Aβ42/40, p‐tau181, or p‐tau217, in the blood or CSF) do not seem to reach normal (i.e., “negative”) levels: plasma p‐tau217 is increased two‐ to four‐fold in amyloid‐positive individuals at the early symptomatic stages of AD but is reduced by less than 40% following treatment with anti‐Aβ MABs. 18 The lack of full normalization for ptau‐217 is consistent with data showing that (1) circulating p‐tau217 levels correlates with both Aβ and tau neuropathologies 19 and (2) anti‐Aβ MABs do not lower tau burden, as measured by tau‐PET. 4

Moreover, comparison across RCTs with different anti‐Aβ MABs shows a dissociation between observations from PET versus fluid biomarkers. While gantenerumab showed a modest effect on amyloid‐PET compared to FDA‐approved MABs and did not show a significant effect on clinical outcomes (Table 1), the treatment effect on Aβ42/40, p‐tau181, or p‐tau217 was comparable between gantenerumab and the FDA‐approved molecules (see Hu et al. 16 for discussion).

These results are consistent with the growing understanding that imaging and fluid biomarkers are not directly equivalent 14 : none of them on their own can capture the full pathophysiological complexity of AD, just as the assessment of a single neuropathological feature of AD (e.g., neuritic plaques) does not depict the whole spectrum of AD pathophysiology. Amyloid‐PET is typically conceptualized as a measure of the lifetime accumulation of insoluble Aβ aggregates in the brain parenchyma and vasculature. In contrast, fluid biomarkers measure soluble elements of disease processes (e.g., Aβ and tau) that dynamically reflect net rates of production versus clearance of pathological proteins at a given point in time. As a result, the extent to which biomarkers are interchangeable varies according to context of use. 20 In studies of AD natural disease history, amyloid‐PET and “core 1” fluid biomarkers (Box 1) are highly concordant in establishing the presence of AD pathophysiology. 15 , 16 Yet little is known about the relationship between these biomarkers during and after anti‐Aβ treatment, as the initial data from RCTs suggest that treatment with anti‐Aβ MABs may alter the relationship and concordance between PET and fluid markers. 20

Neuropathological changes associated with TRAC

Studies combining ante mortem PET and neuropathological assessments have been crucial for the FDA approval of amyloid‐binding radiopharmaceuticals. 21 , 22 , 23 PET‐to‐autopsy studies have established the limitations of PET, highlighting low sensitivity to detect the earliest neuropathological stages of Aβ deposition. 24 , 25 These investigations have also refined our understanding of radiotracer affinity to different types of Aβ deposits (neuritic, cored, diffuse, and vascular). 25 , 26 Similarly, PET‐to‐autopsy studies in patients who received anti‐Aβ MABs are needed to characterize the effect of these new treatments on the underlying neuropathology and allow a better interpretation of the observed PET changes.

In this section, we aimed to summarize existing knowledge on the impact of anti‐Aβ MABs on AD neuropathology and relate it to established treatment effects on amyloid‐PET. Evidence is limited to date, as very few participants from RCTs have come to autopsy.

Data from FDA‐approved therapies

In an 84‐year‐old female AD patient treated with 32 monthly doses of low‐dose aducanumab (titrated up to 6 mg/kg, cumulative dose: 186 mg/kg), 27 amyloid‐PET showed a major decrease over time, with the cortical SUVR dropping from 1.5 before treatment to 1.1 after 56 months of treatment (manuscript did not report quantification in Centiloids). Yet the last PET scan showed residual focal positivity in the occipital cortex (the region with the highest signal at baseline in this individual), which would be interpreted as partial TRAC in our framework. The patient died 4 months after the last dose; brain autopsy revealed sparse Aβ plaques involved with reactive ameboid microglia and neuritic plaque‐associated tau pathology appeared less dense than in a reference cohort of untreated patients. Overall, the patient had low levels of AD neuropathological changes (Thal 2, Braak V, Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease sparse neuritic plaques); the area of highest Aβ plaque density was the occipital cortex, consistent with the ante mortem PET. The patient also showed mild cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) but no microhemorrhages in brain parenchyma and no recent or remote infarcts, consistent with the absence of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) on ante mortem magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

VandeVrede et al. 28 reported data on two participants who received low cumulative doses of aducanumab through the ENGAGE trial. Participant 1 was originally randomized to placebo and then received 14 doses of low‐dose (up to 6 mg/kg) aducanumab in the open‐label extension. After 16 months off treatment, this participant received three titration doses (up to 3 mg/kg) through the EMBARK trial before dying of unrelated causes; overall, this participant received a cumulative dose of 67 mg/kg. Participant 2 received a cumulative dose of 68 mg/kg of aducanumab in ENGAGE and passed away 2.5 years later. For both participants, amyloid‐PET mildly decreased over time, but both were still clearly amyloid‐PET positive on their last scan (50.7 and 51.5 CL, compared to 61 CL at baseline for both). For both participants, amyloid plaque abundance was considered typical by neuropathologists, with no noticeable change in plaque morphology.

Boon et al. 29 recently published neuropathology findings in five participants from clinical trials at the Mayo Clinic, including two presenilin‐1 (PSEN1) mutation carriers, who had received treatment with aducanumab. Participants had various levels of drug exposure (5 to 241 mg/kg, cumulatively) and time between final treatment dose and death (5 to 41 months), and all of them clinically declined over the course of treatment. All aducanumab‐treated participants had serial amyloid‐PET scans showing minimal (115 to 108 CL, −6%) to strong (124 to 24 CL, −81%) reduction in cortical signal; for all patients, the last available amyloid‐PET scan was visually positive (personal communication, 2025). Two participants showed evidence of ARIA (both edema and hemorrhagic types) on MRI. All participants had high AD neuropathological changes at autopsy (A3, B3, and C3) and had CAA in both leptomeningeal and cortical vessels. Histopathological analyses compared the treated participants to 12 untreated cases matched for genetics (PSEN1 and APOE), sex, and age of onset. No Aβ clearance was detected in participants treated with the lowest cumulative dose of aducanumab (5 mg/kg). In the remaining four aducanumab‐treated cases, authors noted disproportionate clearance of Aβ deposits in cortical layer I compared to deeper layers, as well as clearance in the putamen. The morphology of Aβ deposits was also altered; plaques appeared more dot‐like and less fibrillar in treated participants. Although no statistical analyses could be conducted due to sample size, patients with lower Centiloid values at the last PET scan tended to show lower Aβ immunoreactivity in the cortex.

A neuropathological description was available in two participants treated with lecanemab in the CLARITY‐AD trial, but none of the relevant publications included information on follow‐up amyloid‐PET, so it is unclear if these patients would have fulfilled criteria for TRAC. The first participant was a 79‐year‐old who entered the trial as amyloid‐PET and tau‐PET positive (A+T+). After treatment with lecanemab, MRI scan showed evidence of cerebral edema with increased numbers of cortical microhemorrhages (i.e., ARIA). At autopsy, a significant proportion of Aβ plaques had no amyloid core but showed preserved tau‐positive dystrophic neurites, suggesting that neuritic plaques were cleared of Aβ, as well as intense microglia involvement. 30 Neuropathology findings included severe CAA and neuroinflammatory changes, consistent with the diagnosis of severe ARIA. Similar findings of perivascular inflammatory changes and microvascular abnormalities were observed in a 65‐year‐old participant of CLARITY‐AD who was enrolled in a subsequent open‐label extension study. The participant received three lecanemab infusions before suffering a stroke, with edema and multiple microhemorrhages on MRI scan, and died from hemorrhagic complications after fibrinolytic treatment by tissue plasminogen activator. 31 Brain autopsy showed multiple subarachnoid and intraparenchymal hemorrhages along with severe CAA with vasculitis and fibrinoid necrosis of vessel walls. Neuropathology findings supported the conclusion that Aβ deposits were undergoing phagocytosis and indicated greater Aβ clearance in the temporal cortex relative to the frontal cortex. 32

Data from non‐FDA–approved therapies

Solanezumab and bapineuzumab are two anti‐Aβ MABs that did not result in clinical benefit in RCTs and showed no to mild effects on amyloid‐PET. 33 , 34 A few participants treated with these drugs through RCTs have come to autopsy; all showed high density of parenchymal plaques and CAA, similar to non‐treated participants. 35 , 36 , 37 However, biochemical analyses of tissues revealed that treated participants had substantially greater levels of soluble and insoluble Aβ40, but not Aβ42, concentrations when compared to non‐treated AD cases for both solanezumab and bapineuzumab.

Neuropathological data were also available from participants treated as part of the earlier AN‐1792 vaccine trial, an active immunotherapy approach with purified Aβ peptide 1‐42. The trial was abandoned due to occurrence of meningoencephalitis in some participants. Case reports from these early trials demonstrated successful reduction of amyloid burden, with focal clearance of compact and diffuse Aβ plaques. However, cases also showed persistent CAA, blood–brain barrier leakage, and neuroinflammatory reaction. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 Like the findings from bapineuzumab and solanezumab, participants treated with AN‐1792 showed altered concentrations of Aβ in brain tissues, with a relative reduction of Aβ42 and increase in Aβ40 concentration. Such shifts in biochemical pools of Aβ could be in part responsible for detrimental effects of immunotherapies on cerebral vasculature that might worsen CAA and impair the blood–brain barrier in patients with ARIA.

Summary and gaps in knowledge

While autopsy analyses of cases from active and passive immunotherapy trials support an effect of some of these treatments on underlying neuropathology, the results have been inconsistent in terms of the magnitude and nature of Aβ pathology changes. None of the treated cases who came to autopsy showed post mortem evidence of complete amyloid deposit clearance. Moreover, none of the autopsy cases published to date showed ante mortem PET evidence for full TRAC, only partial TRAC; note that additional cases with TRAC and autopsy have been presented at conferences but have not yet been published in peer‐reviewed journals. 44 Overall, major changes in the density of amyloid deposits have been noted in participants treated with high doses of aducanumab and lecanemab, which are both known to be associated with a high likelihood of TRAC (Table 1). This contrasts with data from solanezumab and bapineuzumab treatments, which are associated with no‐to‐mild decrease in amyloid‐PET levels 33 , 34 and were not associated with clear changes in the density of Aβ deposits at autopsy, showing only subtle biochemical alterations. Altogether, these limited data suggest that substantial decrease in amyloid‐PET and, by extension, TRAC likely reflects a major decrease in the density of Aβ deposits.

To further characterize the neuropathological underpinnings of TRAC, future PET‐to‐autopsy studies in patients with ante mortem amyloid‐PET will not only need to include a comprehensive quantitative and qualitative analysis of Aβ deposits but also consider associated microvascular and neuroinflammatory changes. Additionally, biochemical analyses will be needed to provide greater insight into effects of immunotherapy on concentrations of soluble and insoluble Aβ species, both in the brain parenchyma and in cerebral vasculature.

Predictors of TRAC

As described earlier, not all patients treated with anti‐Aβ MABs reach full TRAC, and secondary analyses from RCTs have identified factors predictive of full TRAC.

First, treatment‐related factors can be highlighted, with some anti‐Aβ MABs showing higher rates of full TRAC than others (Table 1). Unpublished data from TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ 4, a RCT that directly compared donanemab and aducanumab, showed that 70% patients treated with donanemab reached full TRAC after 12 months, compared to 22% for patients treated with aducanumab. 45 Within a given trial, higher doses and longer treatment duration are associated with higher rates of full TRAC (Figure 2).

Second, participant‐related factors could predict the likelihood of TRAC. Data from TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ showed that baseline amyloid‐PET levels were inversely associated with TRAC, 10 such that patients who reached full TRAC within 24 weeks had lower baseline amyloid‐PET signal (92.8 ± 28.7 CL) than patients who did not reach full TRAC (117.4 ± 33.9 CL). Future investigations should determine how baseline PET levels, along with other potential variables (e.g., apolipoprotein E genotype, incidence of ARIA, demographics, clinical status, comorbid health conditions) might help predict when an individual is likely to reach full TRAC.

Relevance to patient management and future trials

Impact on clinical management

When comparing across RCTs, those achieving high rates of participants with full TRAC were those most associated with a significant slowing of clinical decline. In contrast, trials with low full TRAC rates (e.g., gantenerumab or ENGAGE trial for aducanumab) were not associated with significant clinical benefit (Table 1). However, the relationship between amyloid‐PET changes and clinical benefit has not been established at the individual patient level; examining these relationships is a key goal of developing the present TRAC framework.

Current clinical guidelines do not mandate monitoring TRAC, although the FDA prescribing information for donanemab mentions that clinicians could “consider stopping dosing with KISUNLA based on reduction of amyloid plaques to minimal levels on amyloid‐PET imaging,” similar to the TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ trial design. 4 Similarly, the 2025 update on appropriate use criteria for amyloid‐PET now consider amyloid‐PET to be appropriate for monitoring response in patients receiving approved amyloid‐targeting therapy. 46

However, monitoring fibrillar Aβ removal with PET is not mentioned in the prescribing information for aducanumab or lecanemab, nor in the appropriate use recommendations for these drugs. In addition, the FDA approved a maintenance dosing for treatment with lecanemab (one injection every 4 weeks instead of every 2 weeks) in January 2025; the transition to this maintenance regimen is to be implemented after the initial 18 months of treatment, not based on any biomarker monitoring.

Beyond differences in the design of phase 3 trials, anti‐Aβ MABs differ in their affinity for soluble aggregated Aβ species 2 : lecanemab targets protofibrils, 47 while donanemab targets pyroglutamate Aβ plaques. 48 These differences across antibodies might influence the relationship between Aβ plaque clearance and clinical benefit, thereby impacting the relevance of identifying TRAC and subsequently titrating treatment.

Overall, future studies with biomarker monitoring and assessment of clinical outcomes should specifically investigate optimal treatment regimens after TRAC. Such studies will help clarify the relevance of measuring TRAC from a personalized medicine perspective and establish if TRAC should trigger a change in patient management, for example, treatment termination or transition to maintenance dosing. Guidelines on patient management after TRAC will need to be based on more formal clinical studies and might be antibody‐specific.

Future clinical trials and observational studies

TRAC status has significant implications for the design of future trials in patients with early AD. Participants with TRAC could be included in trials assessing the potential clinical benefits of preventing fibrillar Aβ re‐accumulation after full TRAC and maintaining Aβ burden below PET detection levels. However, as anti‐Aβ MABs are now approved therapies for early clinical stages of AD and prescribed as part of some patients’ clinical care, all RCTs recruiting patients with early AD must consider whether to include individuals who have already received anti‐Aβ treatment when testing new candidate therapies. This approach presents new challenges, as trials would need to account for participants’ prior treatment history and changes to the disease pathophysiological mechanisms and biomarkers. Parameters to be considered include partial versus full TRAC, time since evidence for TRAC (considering rates of amyloid re‐accumulation), possible drug–drug interactions, mechanism of action of the candidate therapy, and possible adverse event interactions. In addition, inclusion of patients with TRAC could complicate efficacy evaluations and impact on the anticipated effect size of the candidate therapy, with implications for sample sizes of the treatment arms. Analyses of treatment outcomes in those with and without TRAC will require prespecification. Discussion of the trial choices prior to study initiation with the FDA or other relevant regulatory agencies is important.

Overall, the availability of anti‐Aβ MABs in the clinic and the presence of patients with TRAC may require the development of new control group strategies or the implementation of adaptive trial designs to better assess treatment effects in this unique population.

CHALLENGES IN THE IMPLEMENTATION OF TRAC

The implementation of this framework to identify patients with TRAC outside of clinical trials, in both observational research studies and clinical settings, will face challenges, including access to repeated PET measurements and analytical considerations.

Access to repeated PET scans

The current TRAC framework relies on repeated PET measurements, prior to and after treatment onset. This raises issues in terms of resources, patient burden, and timing of follow‐up scanning. In research settings, observational studies that incorporate longitudinal amyloid‐PET and clinical monitoring are now enrolling patients treated with anti‐Aβ MABs in countries where anti‐Aβ MABs have received regulatory approval. These studies will generate data on TRAC that will complement data available from RCTs to document the effect of anti‐Aβ MABs on clinical and biomarker outcomes.

However, in clinical practice, PET imaging is limited by factors such as access to tomography, tracer availability, cost, and coverage issues. Moreover, as described in Section 3.4.1, no clinical guideline currently mandates treatment monitoring with amyloid‐PET.

Time between treatment onset and follow‐up PET scan

The workgroup does not recommend specific timing for repeating PET scanning, considering that TRAC has been observed in as few as 6 months in some patients, while others were still amyloid‐PET positive after 18 months of anti‐Aβ therapy (Figure 2). As described in Section 3.3, the likelihood of reaching full TRAC depends on both treatment and patient characteristics. Analysis of data from RCTs, as well as data being collected in observational studies or through registries collecting real‐world data, 49 will be needed to estimate treatment duration needed to reach full TRAC at the individual patient level. These individualized approaches would make it possible to repeat PET scanning when patients are likely to have reached full TRAC rather than use a one‐size‐fits‐all approach that may result in unnecessary PET scans.

Note that newer‐generation MABs or a combination of MABs with novel approaches to deliver antibodies to the brain may shorten the time needed to reach full TRAC. 50

Situations when only fluid biomarkers are available prior to treatment

While the workgroup highlighted that fluid biomarkers are not interchangeable with amyloid‐PET when it comes to establishing treatment‐related clearance of Aβ deposits, we acknowledge that the latest fluid biomarkers (T1 markers and hybrid ratios, see Box 1) predict amyloid‐PET positivity with excellent accuracy in individuals who have not received anti‐Aβ therapies. For this reason, we consider that patients with pretreatment confirmation of amyloid positivity based on one of the diagnostic fluid biomarkers described in Box 1 and a “negative” amyloid‐PET after treatment can be considered likely to have full TRAC.

Given current evidence, estimating the extent of partial TRAC – if it occurs at all – is challenging without a pretreatment amyloid‐PET for comparison. Thus, the term “partial TRAC” should be applied only to individuals with both pre‐ and post‐treatment PET scans, although this recommendation may evolve as emerging fluid‐biomarker methods could soon enable more accurate estimation of Centiloid levels (beyond simple amyloid positivity) in treatment‐naive individuals. 51 , 52

PET measurement considerations

Methodological factors impacting PET signal

The workgroup recommends maximizing the comparability of PET images acquired pre‐ and post‐treatment (similar tomograph, tracer, acquisition time window, and reconstruction parameters) to optimize longitudinal amyloid‐PET measurement and establish TRAC as reliably as possible. 53 , 54 While this level of standardization should be feasible in research studies that typically follow strict PET protocols, it might prove more difficult in clinical settings. Moreover, quantification should be performed with consistent pipeline/software: While head‐to‐head comparisons have shown very robust correlations across methods (R 2 > 0.94), differences might exceed 10 CL in some individuals. 55 , 56 , 57 , 58

In addition, the workgroup identified methodological factors related to amyloid‐PET measurement in the context of anti‐Aβ therapies that have not yet been thoroughly investigated, including:

‐To what extent is PET quantification impacted by ARIA or by the brain volume loss documented in RCTs? 3 , 4 , 6 , 8

To what extent is PET quantification impacted by ARIA or by the brain volume loss documented in RCTs? 3 , 4 , 6 , 8

‐Are PET tracer kinetics, and all metrics derived from PET quantification (SUVR, CL), affected by treatment?

Are PET tracer kinetics, and all metrics derived from PET quantification (SUVR, CL), affected by treatment?

PET cut‐offs and criteria to establish full versus partial TRAC

Similar to the revised criteria for diagnosis and staging of AD, 14 the current framework does not intend to mandate a specific thresholding approach, but we aimed to provide guidance on how to operationalize TRAC.

Full TRAC relies on the identification of a “negative” (non‐elevated) amyloid‐PET scan after treatment. Whenever possible, the workgroup recommends combining visual read and PET quantification to establish negativity, with cut‐off values in the range of 15 to 25 CL. Note that 25 CL corresponds to the amyloid burden detected by visual reads 59 , 60 and to intermediate to high levels of AD neuropathological changes at autopsy. 23 This suggestion will be easily implementable in research studies where Centiloid quantification is now usually available. In clinical settings, scans are typically interpreted based on a binary visual read, but quantification software has recently received regulatory approval in various countries and is becoming more common as an adjunct to visual read. 59

The workgroup insists that full TRAC should ideally correspond to the clearance of Aβ deposits below levels detectable by PET, which means that all gray matter areas should have non‐elevated PET signals. Such a comprehensive review of the whole brain is a slight deviation from current FDA‐approved methods to interpret scans, which were developed more than a decade before the approval of anti‐Aβ therapies and vary slightly across tracers. 46 For instance, the approved criteria to read [18F]Flutemetamol and [18F]Florbetaben exclude the occipital cortex, while [18F]Florbetapir criteria consider all cortical regions. Note that most approaches to quantifying amyloid‐PET as a single value (e.g., Centiloid method) typically exclude the occipital lobe, which could lead to an underestimation of amyloid burden, potentially leading to low Centiloid values (i.e., values in the “negative range”) in the context of rare occipital‐predominant binding, 59 as reported in a case treated with aducanumab. 26 Similarly, striatal signal is specifically mentioned in [18F]Flutemetamol guidelines but not for the other two approved tracers, and is included in some, but not all quantification approaches. For these reasons, we recommend using a combination of thorough visual interpretation combined with image quantification (e.g., Centiloid method, regional SUVR with reference values) to confirm the absence of elevated PET signal in all gray matter areas.

Partial TRAC refers to cases where treatment has reduced Aβ burden significantly, but not below PET detection thresholds. Classification as partial TRAC therefore involves a reliable decrease in amyloid‐PET signal, that is, decline beyond the variability related to test–retest and measurement error. Rather than provide a predetermined absolute threshold to define reliable amyloid‐PET decline, we encourage investigators to establish these thresholds based on their own datasets and PET processing methods, by quantifying the variability in amyloid‐PET slopes in a placebo or untreated group. Importantly, we discourage the sole reliance on visual read to establish partial TRAC, as visual methods have not been validated to detect changes in amyloid‐PET signal beyond a negative/positive distinction.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

This research framework is based on currently available data in a fast‐paced field, considering both theoretical and pragmatic factors. It will necessarily be adapted over time in response to rapidly evolving biomarker and clinical advances, as well as with the accumulation of data on patients receiving anti‐Aβ MABs. Future research, including secondary analyses from RCTs, observational studies enrolling patients receiving anti‐Aβ therapies, and registries collecting data from real‐world settings, will be crucial in characterizing TRAC and its significance for patient management.
