Adaptive resource aware and privacy preserving federated edge learning framework for real time internet of medical things applications
Authors: Khalid Mahmood, Sonia Khan, Maha Abdelhaq, Mahmood Ul Hassan, Mueen Uddin, Raed Alsaqour, Kamran Ahmad Awan, Muaadh A Alsoufi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Internet of Medical Things requires frameworks that ensure secure processing, computational efficiency, and scalability for continuous healthcare data streams. Existing solutions remain limited in their ability to support real-time anomaly detection, resource-constrained optimization, and multi-tier aggregation in large-scale deployments. This work proposes an Adaptive Federated Edge Learning framework specifically designed for healthcare systems enabled by the Internet of Medical Things. The framework incorporates Adaptive Modular Learning Units that allocate computations according to device-specific resource budgets and update models through federated gradient descent. Dynamic Data Encoding transforms heterogeneous medical data into a unified and decorrelated feature representation with alignment and quality weighting to preserve consistency across distributed sources. A Hierarchical Federated Aggregation mechanism performs device, regional, and global parameter updates using data-size weighting and delay-aware factors to maintain training stability and scalability in heterogeneous network environments. Privacy-Preserving Secure Enclaves provide encrypted model training and aggregation with differential privacy noise injection, ensuring that sensitive clinical data remain protected throughout the learning cycle. Real-time anomaly detection is achieved through a streaming pipeline based on sliding windows, dimensionality reduction, covariance analysis, and adaptive thresholding with context-aware clustering. Experimental evaluation using clinical datasets and simulated Internet of Medical Things environments demonstrated that the framework achieved 96.3% accuracy in controlled conditions and sustained 110 ms latency in streaming anomaly detection.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The Internet of Medical Things integrates networked medical devices, clinical applications, and middleware to acquire, transport, and process physiological signals and clinical records with strict constraints on latency, privacy, and reliability1–3. Federated learning provides a distributed training paradigm in which local models update shared parameters without centralizing raw clinical data, thereby aligning with regulatory requirements and reducing exposure of sensitive information4,5. Edge, fog, and cloud computing form a multi-tier execution substrate that assigns sensing, inference, aggregation, and archival functions to appropriate hardware tiers according to bandwidth, compute capacity, and availability profiles6. Contemporary deployments must support continuous anomaly detection, adaptive resource allocation on heterogeneous devices, and scalable coordination across geographically dispersed sites7,8. Heterogeneity in sensing modalities, sampling rates, and data quality introduces feature misalignment and covariate shift, which degrade model convergence and increase false alarms9. Intermittent connectivity and variable propagation delay complicate global optimization and increase staleness of model updates. Privacy risks arise from gradient inversion, linkage to auxiliary datasets, and enclave or transport compromises. These factors motivate architectures that combine resource-aware local training, universal feature representations for heterogeneous streams, hierarchical aggregation with delay sensitivity, and privacy-preserving computation10,11. The objective is to achieve stable convergence, bounded end-to-end latency, and defensible privacy guarantees while operating under device-level power and memory limits and network-level throughput constraints2,6.

Motivation and problem statement

Healthcare delivery supported by the Internet of Medical Things requires analytical frameworks that can process heterogeneous clinical data streams under strict latency and privacy constraints12. Current approaches face limitations when applied to distributed deployments with thousands of interconnected medical devices producing data of varying modalities and sampling frequencies13. Centralized machine learning solutions cannot satisfy the dual requirements of scalability and compliance with privacy regulations because they rely on raw data aggregation in cloud infrastructures, introducing communication overhead, latency, and risk of disclosure14. Conventional federated learning has addressed part of this issue by keeping data local; however, it does not natively incorporate resource awareness, and the training process often exceeds the memory and computational budgets of edge devices. Additionally, heterogeneity in feature distributions across patients and devices produces alignment inconsistencies, limiting global model convergence and increasing false detection rates in anomaly monitoring tasks15. Communication efficiency is another critical challenge because repeated parameter exchanges between devices and the central server saturate bandwidth and degrade system responsiveness16. These technical barriers indicate that conventional learning paradigms remain insufficient for large-scale IoMT healthcare deployments where real-time detection and continuous adaptation are essential.

The problem is further compounded by architectural and security considerations in multi-tier deployments17. Edge and fog devices exhibit non-uniform availability, variable communication delays, and heterogeneous reliability, producing staleness in global updates and lowering predictive accuracy when standard aggregation rules are applied. Existing anomaly detection schemes integrated into IoMT platforms typically rely on static thresholds or shallow models that cannot adapt to the dynamics of real-time patient monitoring, limiting their applicability in emergency-critical scenarios18. Privacy mechanisms such as homomorphic encryption or differential privacy, when applied in isolation, increase computational and communication overhead and are often infeasible on low-power devices19. Moreover, adversarial inference attacks on gradients and potential vulnerabilities in execution environments expose sensitive health information to reconstruction risks21. The absence of unified frameworks that simultaneously address resource-aware learning, universal feature encoding, hierarchical aggregation, and privacy-preserving execution creates a critical gap in the design of IoMT-enabled healthcare analytics22. Addressing this gap requires an integrated system that sustains model accuracy, ensures low-latency anomaly detection, and guarantees protection of sensitive clinical data across distributed healthcare networks.

Research objectives and practical implications

The primary objective of this research is to design and validate an adaptive federated edge learning framework that integrates resource-aware local training, dynamic data encoding, hierarchical aggregation, and privacy-preserving computation for Internet of Medical Things healthcare systems23. The framework aims to establish a unified approach that enables real-time anomaly detection while operating within the constraints of computational budgets, memory capacities, and bandwidth limitations inherent to distributed devices. A central research goal is to construct Adaptive Modular Learning Units that allocate model complexity according to device-specific resource profiles while maintaining convergence across heterogeneous environments24. Another objective is to formulate Dynamic Data Encoding techniques that align heterogeneous feature distributions and reduce inter-device variability to improve global model stability. At the aggregation layer, the research targets the development of Hierarchical Federated Aggregation mechanisms that incorporate delay-aware weighting to mitigate the effects of asynchronous updates while sustaining scalability25. Finally, the research seeks to implement Privacy-Preserving Secure Enclaves that allow encrypted computations and integrate differential privacy noise injection to ensure that sensitive clinical information is protected throughout the training and inference pipeline26. These objectives are directed toward advancing the technical state of federated learning in healthcare by addressing challenges of heterogeneity, latency, and security in a unified manner27.

The practical implications of this research extend to multiple facets of healthcare delivery and system deployment. In clinical monitoring scenarios, the framework enables continuous assessment of patient data streams with real-time anomaly detection, thereby supporting prompt decision-making in intensive care units, remote patient management, and emergency response28. From a deployment perspective, the proposed system reduces dependency on centralized infrastructures by distributing computation across edge and fog devices, which enhances scalability and resilience in large hospital networks and telemedicine platforms. The integration of delay-aware hierarchical aggregation provides robust model convergence even under heterogeneous network conditions, ensuring that predictive accuracy is maintained in geographically distributed healthcare facilities29. Privacy-preserving computation directly supports compliance with data protection regulations, minimizing risks of data leakage and ensuring patient confidentiality during collaborative model training30. Efficient resource allocation reduces operational costs associated with high-bandwidth transmissions and centralized processing while enabling low-power devices to contribute to federated learning without exceeding energy budgets.

Overview of the proposed approach

The proposed framework introduces an integrated architecture for adaptive federated edge learning tailored to the operational requirements of Internet of Medical Things healthcare systems. At the device level, Adaptive Modular Learning Units allocate model complexity in proportion to the available computational and memory resources, enabling lightweight execution of inference and training tasks without exceeding device-level budgets. These modules utilize resource-aware objectives to maintain convergence while ensuring that local updates remain computationally feasible. Dynamic Data Encoding is employed to transform heterogeneous sensor streams, electronic health records, and imaging features into a unified representation. This transformation incorporates normalization, feature alignment, and decorrelation procedures to reduce inter-device variability and mitigate covariate shift, thereby stabilizing global training across distributed participants. At the intermediate layer, Hierarchical Federated Aggregation performs multi-tier parameter updates from edge devices to fog nodes and subsequently to cloud servers. The aggregation process incorporates data-size weighting and delay-aware factors to adjust the contribution of updates from regions with variable connectivity and propagation delays, which preserves stability in asynchronous environments. Privacy-Preserving Secure Enclaves form the protective layer of the architecture, ensuring encrypted execution of computations and augmenting privacy protection with differential privacy noise addition during aggregation. Collectively, these components operate as a unified system to address the limitations of conventional federated learning by balancing accuracy, efficiency, and security in real-time healthcare analytics.

The approach also incorporates a real-time anomaly detection pipeline designed to operate in parallel with the federated learning cycle. This module applies sliding windows to continuous patient data streams, followed by dimensionality reduction and covariance-based scoring to identify anomalies. Adaptive thresholding ensures that detection remains sensitive to variations in patient-specific baselines, while clustering-based contextual analysis distinguishes true anomalies from statistical outliers. Integration with the federated learning process ensures that local anomaly detection benefits from continuously updated global models, which improves precision in detecting clinically significant events. By embedding this anomaly detection mechanism within the broader framework, the system supports immediate identification of abnormal physiological patterns while simultaneously contributing to global learning. The hierarchical design allows anomaly information to propagate through local and regional levels without overloading network links, maintaining low end-to-end latency even in large-scale deployments.

Related work analysis

Research on Internet of Medical Things has progressed through multiple dimensions including federated learning for decentralized training, edge and fog computing for latency-sensitive processing, and privacy-preserving techniques for secure clinical data management20. Existing literature provides valuable insights into distributed anomaly detection, resource-aware scheduling, and multi-tier aggregation strategies; however, most studies address these aspects in isolation rather than within a unified architecture. A comprehensive analysis of related work is therefore required to identify existing contributions, highlight technical gaps, and establish the foundation for the proposed adaptive federated edge learning framework. This section presents an overview of prior research followed by focused subsections on specific approaches and limitations.

Existing literature on IoMT security, sensing, and distributed analytics

Research on Internet of Medical Things security identifies authentication, authorization, key management, confidentiality, integrity, availability, and real-time assurance as primary requirements for clinical deployments21. AI-driven security models provide network intrusion and malware detection through supervised classification and ensemble methods, offering improved detection rates for protocol- and flow-level threats34. Explainable temporal architectures using simple recurrent units extend this direction by assigning contribution scores to features while modeling sequential traffic, improving interpretability for operational use37. Cloud–IoMT big data systems enable elastic storage and compute for population-scale analytics and pandemic response pipelines35, whereas fog-computing frameworks reduce end-to-end delay by relocating inference and preprocessing closer to sources42. However, these strands frequently assume centralized data aggregation or device-agnostic models, and they do not provide end-to-end mechanisms for resource-aware training under device constraints, alignment of heterogeneous feature spaces, or hierarchical aggregation with delay sensitivity. In addition, prior security-focused work often omits formal integration with privacy-preserving training, leaving exposure to gradient inversion and linkage risks when learning across distributed datasets. Proposed framework: adaptive federated edge learning framework

The proposed Adaptive Federated Edge Learning (AFEL) framework is designed to address fundamental challenges in Internet of Medical Things deployments, including heterogeneous data distributions, constrained computational resources, asynchronous connectivity, and strict privacy requirements. As illustrated in Figure 1, the framework integrates Adaptive Modular Learning Units (AMLUs), Dynamic Data Encoding (DDE), Hierarchical Federated Aggregation (HFA), Privacy-Preserving Secure Enclaves (PPSE), and Real-Time Anomaly Detection (RTAD) into a layered workflow. Each component targets a distinct limitation while contributing to a cohesive architecture that supports end-to-end healthcare analytics. AMLUs implement resource-aware training by adjusting model depth and complexity according to device-specific budgets, which prevents overload on low-capacity nodes while preserving local contribution to global optimization. DDE transforms heterogeneous input streams from wearable sensors, imaging systems, and electronic health records into a universal, decorrelated feature space, thereby minimizing covariate shift across devices. This transformation stabilizes convergence during aggregation and improves anomaly detection accuracy. The workflow, as shown in Figure 1, establishes an end-to-end system where heterogeneous inputs are standardized, processed under resource-aware conditions, aggregated hierarchically, secured at the computational level, and analyzed in real time for anomaly detection, enabling reliable and privacy-preserving healthcare analytics in Internet of Medical Things systems.

The upper layers of the framework coordinate secure aggregation and anomaly detection in real time. HFA performs multi-tier aggregation at edge, fog, and cloud levels using data-size weighting and delay-aware coefficients, which mitigates staleness of model updates while maintaining scalability across large deployments. PPSE enforces privacy at the computation layer by executing training and aggregation within hardware-secured enclaves and injecting calibrated differential privacy noise, preventing gradient inversion and inference attacks on sensitive medical data. RTAD operates as a continuous monitoring pipeline, applying sliding windows, dimensionality reduction, covariance-based scoring, and adaptive thresholds to detect anomalies in patient data streams with low latency. This module supports immediate detection of clinically critical events while leveraging continuously updated global models from the federated process.

Experimental simulation & results

The experimental evaluation of the proposed framework was carried out using a controlled testbed configured to replicate IoMT deployment scenarios. Edge-layer emulation was performed on Raspberry Pi 4 Model B units, each equipped with a quad-core ARM Cortex-A72 processor and 4 GB RAM, while the fog layer was implemented on a Dell Precision 5820 Tower workstation incorporating an Intel Xeon W-2245 CPU, 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 4000 GPU. The iFogSim simulation platform was employed to model IoMT traffic flows, latency behavior, and computational workload distribution across hierarchical layers. The evaluation compared the proposed framework with state-of-the-art baselines including IoMT-CVA36, AI-SecIoMT34, XSRU-IoMT37, TL-IoMT38, and Neuro-IoMT39.

The MIMIC-III database43, which contains de-identified intensive care unit (ICU) patient records with multi-modal physiological signals, served as the input dataset. Preprocessing involved several stages: (i) missing value imputation using forward-fill and interpolation to ensure temporal continuity; (ii) outlier filtering through z-score normalization and median absolute deviation thresholds; (iii) temporal resampling to achieve uniform sampling rates across heterogeneous devices; (iv) feature normalization using min–max and z-score scaling for numerical stability in downstream models; and (v) encoding of categorical attributes through one-hot and ordinal schemes where applicable. Processed data streams were partitioned into training, validation, and testing sets with stratification to preserve class distribution.

Accuracy analysis

In controlled conditions with static data and minimal noise, the proposed approach achieved the highest accuracy of 96.3%, outperforming IoMT-CVA (92.1%) and AI-SecIoMT (91.8%), as illustrated by Figure 2. XSRU-IoMT and TL-IoMT recorded slightly better performances than other baselines, with 93.5% and 94.2%, respectively, while Neuro-IoMT trailed at 89.6%. Under noisy and dynamic conditions, the proposed approach remained robust, achieving 94.7%, compared to IoMT-CVA (88.3%), AI-SecIoMT (86.9%), TL-IoMT (91.6%), XSRU-IoMT (90.4%), and Neuro-IoMT (85.1%).

For real-time anomaly detection, the proposed system achieved 95.8%, surpassing IoMT-CVA (89.4%), AI-SecIoMT (88.7%), TL-IoMT (92.5%), and XSRU-IoMT (93.1%). Neuro-IoMT lagged at 84.7% due to limitations in streaming scenarios. Hierarchical federated aggregation further showcased the proposed system’s efficiency, recording 97.2%, outperforming IoMT-CVA (91.5%), AI-SecIoMT (90.8%), TL-IoMT (94.1%), XSRU-IoMT (93.4%), and Neuro-IoMT (88.6%).

Latency analysis

The latency of the proposed system was evaluated across four distinct scenarios, each designed to assess its responsiveness under varying IoMT conditions. In scenario 1 (Static Data with Minimal Processing), the proposed system recorded an average latency of 120 ms, significantly lower than IoMT-CVA (145 ms) and AI-SecIoMT (150 ms), as represented by Figure 3. XSRU-IoMT and TL-IoMT demonstrated moderate delays of 135 ms and 130 ms, respectively, while Neuro-IoMT lagged at 165 ms, hindered by less efficient data handling mechanisms. Scenario 2 emulated real-world noisy conditions in which proposed approach achieved a latency of 150 ms, outperforming IoMT-CVA (190 ms) and AI-SecIoMT (200 ms). TL-IoMT and XSRU-IoMT recorded 165 ms and 170 ms, respectively, while Neuro-IoMT experienced the highest delay at 220 ms, highlighting its limitations in noise-resilient processing.

Under real-time streaming conditions, the proposed system demonstrated exceptional performance with an average latency of 110 ms, compared to IoMT-CVA (140 ms) and AI-SecIoMT (145 ms). TL-IoMT (125 ms) and XSRU-IoMT (130 ms) exhibited moderate latency, while Neuro-IoMT, focused on wearable devices, recorded 160 ms due to processing delays. For hierarchical aggregation across regions, the proposed approach maintained an average latency of 180 ms, outperforming IoMT-CVA (220 ms) and AI-SecIoMT (230 ms). XSRU-IoMT (195 ms) and TL-IoMT (190 ms) displayed competitive performance, whereas Neuro-IoMT lagged at 240 ms, constrained by its aggregation framework.

Resource utilization analysis

The resource utilization analysis was conducted over four scenarios to evaluate CPU and memory efficiency. In the first scenario, involving static data with minimal processing, the proposed system demonstrated remarkable efficiency, using 38% of CPU resources and 45% memory on average, as shown in Figure 4. By contrast, IoMT-CVA and AI-SecIoMT consumed 55% and 58% CPU, respectively, while XSRU-IoMT and TL-IoMT utilized 50% and 47%. Neuro-IoMT exhibited the highest resource usage at 63%, hindered by its suboptimal handling of low-complexity data.

In the second scenario, which involved dynamic data with high noise levels, the proposed system maintained an average CPU usage of 48% and memory utilization of 52%, showcasing its robust adaptability. IoMT-CVA and AI-SecIoMT, however, recorded higher CPU utilization at 62% and 65%, respectively. TL-IoMT and XSRU-IoMT exhibited moderate efficiency, consuming 55% and 58% CPU, while Neuro-IoMT again struggled, reaching a peak utilization of 72%.

Scalability analysis

In the first scenario, involving a small-scale network with 50 devices, the proposed system demonstrated exceptional performance, maintaining an average throughput of 95 Mbps and latency of 120 ms, as shown in Figure 5. IoMT-CVA and AI-SecIoMT achieved throughputs of 85 Mbps and 80 Mbps, respectively, while XSRU-IoMT and TL-IoMT exhibited moderate results at 88 Mbps and 90 Mbps. Neuro-IoMT, constrained by its architecture, reached only 75 Mbps. The second scenario expanded to a medium-sized network of 200 devices, where the proposed approach maintained 92 Mbps throughput, outperforming IoMT-CVA at 82 Mbps and AI-SecIoMT at 78 Mbps. TL-IoMT and XSRU-IoMT recorded 85 Mbps and 87 Mbps, while Neuro-IoMT lagged at 70 Mbps.

In the third scenario, a large network of 500 devices was simulated, pushing system limits. The proposed system retained superior scalability with throughput of 88 Mbps, compared to IoMT-CVA (75 Mbps) and AI-SecIoMT (70 Mbps). TL-IoMT and XSRU-IoMT showed moderate scalability at 80 Mbps and 83 Mbps, while Neuro-IoMT struggled, managing only 65 Mbps. Discussion

The experimental outcomes confirm that the proposed Adaptive Federated Edge Learning (AFEL) framework achieves substantial improvements in accuracy, latency, and scalability when compared with state-of-the-art baselines. The integration of Adaptive Modular Learning Units (AMLUs) allowed heterogeneous edge devices to contribute updates without exceeding their computational budgets, ensuring that even constrained nodes maintained active participation in the training cycle. This design directly reduced model divergence caused by resource imbalance, which is a persistent challenge in conventional federated learning deployments. The Dynamic Data Encoding (DDE) module further stabilized global convergence by normalizing, aligning, and decorrelating heterogeneous features into a unified representation. This preprocessing step was essential in mitigating covariate shift across distributed data sources, thereby enhancing robustness against false anomaly detections. Hierarchical Federated Aggregation (HFA) improved system scalability by reducing update staleness through delay-aware weighting while simultaneously lowering communication overhead. The ability of the framework to sustain 96.3% accuracy under controlled conditions and 95.8% in real-time anomaly detection illustrates its suitability for critical healthcare tasks that demand stable performance despite noisy environments and asynchronous connectivity.

Beyond performance gains, the Privacy-Preserving Secure Enclaves (PPSE) ensured that sensitive clinical data remained protected throughout training and aggregation. By confining computations to enclave environments and applying calibrated differential privacy noise, the framework achieved strong resilience against gradient inversion, linkage attacks, and inference threats that compromise patient confidentiality. This dual-layer privacy design directly addresses regulatory and ethical requirements in clinical data handling, making AFEL highly suitable for real-world adoption. Resource utilization analysis further demonstrated that the framework significantly reduced CPU and memory consumption compared with baselines, thereby extending operational viability for low-power IoMT devices. Scalability tests confirmed consistent throughput across deployments ranging from 50 to 1,000 devices, highlighting the framework’s capacity to adapt to hospital-scale and telemedicine-scale environments.

Conclusion

Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) deployments require architectures that achieve efficiency, scalability, and reliability under heterogeneous devices and stringent privacy constraints. This research presents an Adaptive Federated Edge Learning framework whose core advances are a hierarchical federated aggregation mechanism with delay-aware weighting and resource-aware modular learning units. Resource utilization analysis indicated 38% CPU usage under minimal processing, outperforming XSRU-IoMT (50%) and Neuro-IoMT (63%). Throughput remained consistent across scale, sustaining 95 Mbps in small networks and 85 Mbps in large networks, exceeding IoMT-CVA and AI-SecIoMT by 10–20%. End-to-end latency averaged 110 ms for real-time anomaly detection, while controlled evaluations maintained 96.3% detection accuracy. Future work will extend cross-device communication efficiency through adaptive scheduling and congestion-aware update policies, incorporate multi-modal medical data streams (physiological waveforms, imaging, and electronic records) with modality-specific encoders within the DDE layer, and strengthen enclave operations via remote attestation and formal privacy accounting.
