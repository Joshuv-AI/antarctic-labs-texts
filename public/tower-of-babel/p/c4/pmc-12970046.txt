ACHTSAM study protocol: outreach diagnostics and assessment of tolerability in severe ME/CFS—a pilot study
Authors: Carmen Fricke, Peter Deibert, Philipp Maier, Winfried Kern, Oliver Krumnau, Friedrich Barsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMyalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a severe, multisystem condition that often emerges after viral infections and affects physical and cognitive function. Severely affected patients are underrepresented in research due to immobility and exertional intolerance.MethodsThis is a prospective, non-interventional observational study investigating the feasibility and tolerability of home-based diagnostics in patients with severe and very severe ME/CFS. Phase 1 includes remote identification using validated questionnaires. Phase 2 involves home visits for physiological, cognitive and biological assessments. The primary outcome is feasibility; secondary outcomes include tolerability and methodological barriers.Ethics/disseminationThe study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Freiburg (No. 25-1031-S1). Written informed consent is obtained from all participants. Results will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications and patient support groups.Trial registration numberDRKS00035231; FRKS005506.

Introduction

Background and rationale

The clinical picture of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is defined as a severe, chronic, multisystem disease. Depending on its severity, it can lead to substantial physical and cognitive impairments—potentially resulting in incapacity to work, the need for long-term care, artificial nutrition or, in extreme cases, death.1 2 Among the systems affected are the central and autonomic nervous systems, the immune system, the cardiovascular system (particularly endothelial cells), and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Common symptoms include orthostatic intolerance with dizziness, tachycardia or syncope on standing, as well as disturbances in blood circulation, the gut microbiome and nerve fibres. Reactivations of persistent viruses, such as herpesviruses, may also contribute to further physiological damage.25

In approximately 80% of cases, ME/CFS develops following an infectious illness, commonly triggered by viruses such as Epstein-Barr virus, influenza virus or SARS-CoV-2.2 6 7 Surgical procedures, resuscitations and head or neck trauma are also recognised as potential triggers; in some cases, however, the underlying cause remains unknown.4

The onset of the disease is often insidious or episodic.2 The hallmark symptom is post-exertional malaise (PEM)—a profound worsening of overall health following minimal exertion, accompanied by muscle pain, fatigue and flu-like symptoms.8 Other common symptoms include dizziness, sleep disturbances, cognitive impairments, pain syndromes, temperature dysregulation and marked hypersensitivity to stimuli and food.9 Approximately 25% of affected individuals are largely housebound, and over 60% are unable to work.10

Prior to the pandemic, the global prevalence was estimated at 0.3%–0.9%. In Germany, according to the IQWiG, there were between 140 000 and 310 000 cases, including 70 000 to 90 000 children and adolescents.11 12 According to studies, it is assumed that the number of affected individuals has at least doubled since the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic.4 13 According to a statement by the National Association of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (Kassenärztliche Bundesvereinigung), the overall prevalence of ME/CFS in Germany in 2021 was approximately 500 000 cases.14

The disease pattern can be categorised into different groups based on the severity of the disease:

Mild ME/CFS: despite burdensome symptoms (eg, pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances), employment remains possible.

Moderate ME/CFS: work capacity is no longer preserved; daily activities are limited and require frequent rest periods.

Severe ME/CFS: only minimal activities, such as eating or personal hygiene, remain possible. Many affected individuals require a wheelchair and recover only slowly from exertion.

The prognosis for individuals with severe and very severe ME/CFS is significantly poorer. To date, no specific or effective treatment is available.16 Although specialised ME/CFS services within the NHS report improvements in individual symptoms such as fatigue, anxiety or depression through cognitive behavioural therapy and graded exercise therapy, no distinctions were made regarding disease severity.17

A central limitation of the current body of research is the insufficient inclusion of patients with severe and very severe ME/CFS into clinical studies. This patient group is often excluded from studies—primarily due to immobility, lack of caregiver support and pronounced exertional intolerance, with the risk of symptom exacerbation caused by PEM. Cox and Findley15 reported that >60% of severe ME/CFS patients were unable to complete inpatient diagnostic protocols due to PEM, underscoring the need for adaptive, home-based approaches.1 15

While several recent studies have employed low-burden diagnostic approaches—such as the analysis of blood-based parameters or the use of various standardised questionnaires18 19—there remains a lack of diagnostic methods that are both minimally taxing and capable of accurately assessing key systems affected in ME/CFS. These include, in particular, the central and autonomic nervous systems, as well as the cardiovascular system, especially endothelial function.20

While blood biomarkers (eg, cytokine panels) and questionnaires (eg, Questionnaires for symptom assessment as18 19) show promise, they often fail to capture autonomic dysfunction (eg, heart rate variability (HRV), pupillography) or endothelial impairment (eg, non-invasive perfusion assessments as20) in severe ME/CFS due to the burden of clinic visits. On the other hand, autonomic abnormalities18 and endothelial dysfunction21 are increasingly recognised in ME/CFS, their assessment in severe patients is hampered by the risk of PEM8 15 and logistical barriers.15 Furthermore, when assessing cognitive impairments, diagnostic equipment is more suitable than assessment using questionnaires. Home-based alternatives for these diagnostic tools are urgently needed for several reasons: Identifying tolerable diagnostic tools could accelerate enrolment in clinical trials (eg, for antivirals or immunoadsorption) by reducing dropout rates. Home-based assessments may reduce noise from clinic-induced stress, providing more ecologically valid data. Severely affected patients are often excluded from research due to logistical barriers and because of the potential risk to harm them, especially with the risk of PEM. This study aims to determine the tolerability of various test procedures and thus contribute to patient safety in future studies.

It is crucial that such diagnostic procedures are tolerable for severely affected ME/CFS patients. Therefore, a systematic investigation of this patient subgroup is urgently needed to develop more targeted diagnostic, therapeutic and care strategies. Once therapeutic options are available, repeated examinations of different parameters are necessary to objectify a response to treatment.

Methods

Study design

The study is a prospective, non-interventional observational study with a single-group design and an exploratory framework. Participant recruitment began in September 2025, and the study is expected to be completed by the end of April 2026.

Study setting

The study is based at the Institute for Exercise and Occupational Medicine at the University Medical Centre Freiburg and is conducted as an outreach study. Data collection takes place in the patients’ home environment through home visits and questionnaires.

Participants

Eligible participants include male and female patients aged 18 and older with severe to very severe ME/CFS (Bell score ≤30). Additionally, it is required that patients find the planned diagnostic procedures acceptable. Only patients living within a maximum distance of 50 km from Freiburg will be included. Exclusion criteria include absolute arrhythmia, blindness or amelia. All patients must provide written informed consent for participation and pseudonymised data use before the study begins.

Variables

The primary variables of interest were feasibility and tolerability of the planned home-based assessments. Feasibility was defined as the proportion of assessments completed with the receipt of valid measured values, while tolerability was assessed based on premature termination, need for additional rest periods and patient-reported PEM following assessments. Outcomes are evaluated per individual assessment and documented at predefined follow-up time points after each study visit. Secondary variables included autonomic, cognitive, vascular, activity-related and biological parameters collected during home visits. Disease-related variables such as disease severity (Bell Score), disease duration and symptom burden were recorded as contextual variables. Potential confounders included age, sex, medication use and testing conditions.

Data collection

The selection of the assessment methods and measurement devices used in Phase 2 was based on the prerequisite that all devices hold valid certification as medical devices. This regulatory classification implies standardised testing with regard to safety, functionality and measurement accuracy, allowing for the assumption of high validity and reliability of the collected data. A methodological exception to this requirement is made for the procedures used to assess HRV and endothelial function.

The HRV measurement is a non-invasive and painless measurement method that has been previously employed in studies involving conditions associated with dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system and elevated stress levels (eg, musculoskeletal pain).24 Given that similar autonomic dysfunctions have been described in ME/CFS, the use of HRV measurement in this study is deemed appropriate for detecting potential pathophysiological changes.

Endothelial function is assessed using the EndoPAT device, which enables non-invasive peripheral arterial tonometry at the fingertip. Evidence of vascular dysfunction and impaired vascular regulation in patients with ME/CFS suggests that this method is suitable for investigation in this context. Accordingly, it has already been applied in previous studies involving individuals with ME/CFS.21 25

Bias

Potential sources of bias include selection bias, as participation is limited to patients with severe or very severe ME/CFS who are able to tolerate home-based assessments and provide informed consent. Consequently, individuals with extremely severe disease or insufficient caregiver support may be underrepresented.

Attrition bias may occur due to premature termination of individual assessments as a result of symptom exacerbation. To address this, all incomplete assessments and the reasons for their termination are systematically documented.

Information and measurement bias may arise from symptom fluctuations and fatigue-related performance variability. To minimise this risk, assessments are conducted in a staged approach and may be paused or discontinued if necessary.

Sample size justification

A sample of 25 is sufficient to estimate feasibility with 95% CIs ±20% (assuming 50% completion rate). This aligns with pilot study guidelines.26 27 Subgroup analyses (eg, by Bell score tertiles) will be exploratory due to limited power.

Quantitative variables

Disease severity will be analysed both as a continuous variable and categorised according to predefined Bell score thresholds for exploratory analyses.

Statistical methods

The descriptive analysis of the feasibility of assessments in patients with severe ME/CFS will initially be conducted for each type of examination separately. This will involve calculating the proportion of assessments that could be completed in full and yielded valid results. Specifically, the number of successfully completed tests will be expressed as a proportion of the total number of planned tests. In addition, feasibility will be compared across subgroups based on age, sex, disease severity and functional capacity.

Furthermore, the feasibility of study-related procedures will be analysed in clusters (eg, questionnaires, diagnostic measures performed in supine/sitting/standing positions, neurocognitive tests, perfusion assessments, etc). This approach will help identify which types of assessments can reasonably be administered to this patient population and which may be too burdensome.

Subgroup comparisons (eg, Bell score ≤20 vs 21–30) will use Fisher’s exact tests for categorical outcomes.

The identification and evaluation of challenges in conducting clinical assessments will be performed using descriptive statistics. Reasons for test failure or limitations in test administration will be recorded, and the frequencies of various causes of limitations will be analysed.

Tolerability of individual assessments and clusters will also be evaluated. This includes determining whether additional rest periods were required after specific tests, whether assessments had to be terminated prematurely, or whether symptoms occurred subsequently.

The study is not powered to estimate prevalence, validate biomarkers or establish normative reference values in this specific group of patients. All biological outcomes will be analysed descriptively and interpreted exclusively in an exploratory context.

Data management

The data collected in the context of this study will be stored both electronically and in paper form at the Universitätsklinikum Freiburg and processed in accordance with the general data protection regulation. All data will be pseudonymised, meaning that personal identifiers such as names or addresses will be replaced by a code number. A securely stored re-identification list will be accessible only to authorised personnel. Online and sensor data will likewise be pseudonymised and securely stored. Biological samples will be encrypted and stored for 3 years in the central biobank. Any publications will be based solely on anonymised data. Contact with treating physicians will occur only with written informed consent. The study management team is responsible for data protection.

Harms

Participation in this study is intended for the scientific investigation of the physical and cognitive capacity of individuals with ME/CFS and aims to contribute to the long-term improvement of diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. No direct medical benefit for participants is expected. Potential risks are associated with participation, including local reactions to blood sampling as well as possible cognitive, emotional or physical overexertion. In particular, there is a risk of PEM, a characteristic worsening of symptoms following exertion, which may be prolonged or, in rare cases, irreversible. To minimise these risks, an individualised assessment of exertion tolerance is performed in advance, all assessments are carried out in a staged and symptom-oriented manner, and participants are continuously monitored. Examinations may be paused or discontinued at any time. Rest periods between examinations are given as needed. Symptom tracking (ie, questions regarding comfort and well-being) is done during the evaluation steps. In addition, participants will be contacted one week after the assessments to determine whether they experienced PEM symptoms. Participation is also possible in a reduced form. The study procedures can be interrupted or terminated at any time, and informed consent may be withdrawn without any disadvantage to the participant.

Patient and public involvement

The study protocol was developed in collaboration with the ME/CFS Freiburg Initiative, two individuals affected by ME/CFS, and one caregiver.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Freiburg (No. 25-1031-S1). Written informed consent is obtained from all participants prior to study inclusion. Given the vulnerability of the study population, assessments are conducted in a staged approach, with the option to pause or terminate individual procedures at any time in order to minimise the risk of PEM.

Study results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and shared with patient support groups. Pseudonymised data will be made available on reasonable request to the corresponding author in accordance with data protection regulations.
