Social experiences of people living with Parkinson’s disease in Ireland: A qualitative study
Authors: Francesca Gaiera, Emma O’Shea, Gerard O’Keeffe, Suzanne Timmons
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionParkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor and non-motor symptoms, compromising people with PD’s (PwP) quality of life and psychological well-being, which may be compounded by stigma. We sought to explore the social experiences of PwP in Ireland, including the role of stigma.MethodsTwenty-one people with PD were recruited through purposeful and snowball sampling. Data were collected through 30–50 min face-to-face or virtual semi-structured interviews. The interview explored PwP’s symptoms, psychosocial experiences, and support needed in the context of stigma. A thematic analysis was performed on transcripts.ResultsParticipants reported diverse social experiences, however stigma was frequently experienced in social situations where PwP felt pitied, misjudged, or closely observed by others. While most were generally open about their diagnosis and did not routinely avoid social settings or encounters, some expressed reluctance in unfamiliar contexts, often due to concerns about visible symptoms, such as poor dexterity when eating, a quieter voice making conversation difficult, or fear of falling. Most reported having a strong and reliable social network offering emotional and practical support. However, many felt uncomfortable sharing their inner emotional responses to Parkinson’s with close others and would welcome access to informal or professional outlets for emotional expression.ConclusionThis study provides a snapshot of the social experiences of people with PD, including the role of stigma, and its effects on well-being, identity, and social connectedness. The findings highlight the importance of greater public education to reduce stigma and promote understanding of PD symptoms. They also underscore the value of peer support groups in fostering emotional connection and resilience. In addition, many participants expressed a need for external emotional support, pointing to a potential role for psychotherapeutic interventions in helping PwP process their experiences and maintain psychological well-being.

Introduction

This study was conducted in the Republic of Ireland and focuses on the social experiences of people with Parkinson’s (PwP) in this cultural context. Understanding where the research took place is important because experiences of Parkinson’s, and particularly stigma, are shaped by social norms, healthcare systems, and cultural expectations, which can vary between countries [1].

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive, neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor symptoms (MS [1]) and non-motor symptoms (NMS [2]). PwP frequently describe changes in social connectedness, enduring misconceptions about their symptoms, and experiences of awkwardness or misunderstanding in everyday interactions, which often lead to feelings of embarrassment, withdrawal, and isolation [3]. The experience of stigma has been reported to drive social isolation in people with PwP and to exacerbate NMS [3]. Stigma is caused by negative preconceptions of people due to a perceived deviation from social norms [4]. In PD, stigma is concerned with devaluing and discriminating attitudes, which affects how PwP are viewed and treated, and how they view themselves. Stigma arises from the complex interplay between the illness, personal factors, and environmental influences, which together shape how PwP experience and respond to stigma [4].

‘Felt stigma’, sometimes called ‘internalized stigma’, is the process whereby the stigmatized person starts to believe in their ‘abnormality’, a term reflecting how societal norms define what is considered normal or deviant [5]. It can result in diminished self-esteem and emotional well-being, reduced social engagement and an increased vulnerability to the illness [[6], [7], [8]]. Another form of stigma, ‘enacted stigma’, refers to the negative attitudes of others in response to the PwP’s visible symptoms. Such attitudes can take the form of staring, avoiding, and critical questioning [9]. Felt and enacted stigma are usually correlated; however, felt stigma is more prominent than enacted stigma in research with people living with health conditions and/or disability (e.g.,[10,11]).

Although social experiences and stigma have been investigated in chronic conditions, such as epilepsy [12] and HIV [13], there is a need to better understand stigma in PD [3,14]. Previous studies have highlighted that a wide range of PD symptoms, including visible motor impairments (e.g., tremors, rigidity), non-motor symptoms (e.g., cognitive changes, emotional blunting), and communication difficulties (e.g., dysarthria, facial masking), can contribute to the experience of stigma among people with PD. For example, a Canadian study exploring social symptoms of PD across a broad patient population found that visible and non-visible symptoms alike could trigger negative social reactions [5]. A U.S. study involving healthcare practitioners reported that facial masking, which limits emotional expressiveness, may be particularly stigmatizing for women, who are often socially expected to display warmth and agreeableness [15,16]; as such, women with pronounced facial masking may be more likely to experience stigma than men [17]. Similarly, research in the U.S. and Canada has shown that dysarthria can result in social exclusion, such as PwP being discouraged from participating in group reading activities due to their speech difficulties [18]. These findings, drawn largely from North American contexts, point to the complex and multifaceted nature of stigma in PD and highlight the need to explore how such dynamics manifest in other cultural settings, such as Ireland.

Felt stigma can mean PwP avoid social interactions for fear of embarrassment and social rejection [19,20], resulting in isolation and loneliness. Loneliness is a risk factor for morbidity and mortality in PwP, and paradoxically, clinicians reported to give more care to PwP who seemed more sociable and less lonely [21].

In the context of PD, it is important to understand how PwP adjust, not only to the most visible symptoms, often referred to as ‘social symptoms’ (e.g., facial masking, dysarthria, and disordered movements), but also to a broader range of symptoms that may elicit stigma in different social contexts. These include drooling, urinary urgency, gait disturbances, and rigidity, which can lead to misperceptions, such as being viewed as intoxicated or unwell [1]. Many PwP report efforts to hide their diagnosis in public settings or avoid certain activities, such as eating in public, out of fear of being pitied, judged, or reduced solely to their condition. Post-diagnosis psychological adjustment, therefore, involves adapting not only to the physical symptomatology of PD but also to the evolving sense of identity and the anticipation of how others might perceive them [22].

Previous studies examining stigma in PD (e.g., [23,24,17]) have highlighted how visible motor symptoms, facial masking, and gendered social expectations shape the way PwP are perceived and treated by others. However, many such investigations have been conducted outside of Ireland and have primarily used quantitative methods (e.g., [19,25]). As stigma is a culturally-bound phenomenon, shaped by prevailing social norms, values, and health system contexts, it can manifest differently across societies and influence how individuals perceive and respond to illness [26,27]. It is therefore important to examine stigma in PwP within specific local contexts to capture culturally embedded experiences that may not emerge in research conducted elsewhere. To date, no such study has been carried out in the Republic of Ireland. This study adopts a qualitative social gerontology approach to examine the social experiences of people with Parkinson’s (PwP) in Ireland, including stigma.

Methodology

Design

A qualitative approach was employed, in which semi-structured interviews were conducted and analyzed within a phenomenological framework. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research checklist (COREQ [28]) was used to promote reporting transparency.

Participants

Participants were recruited through purposeful and snowball sampling using social media and Ireland-based PD advocacy and support groups. The participant information leaflet informed potential participants that the survey was about stigma. Eligibility criteria included a formal PD diagnosis, fluent in English, and being community-dwelling, resident in Ireland and >18 years of age. There were no limits on sex/gender. Materials

The interviews comprised open-ended questions and probes to encourage elaboration on topics relating to the research topic. The schedule was not constructed on an ‘a priori’ coding scheme. Non-directive probes were offered to encourage the elaboration of answers. This was done by paraphrasing what the participants said to prompt confirmation or clarification, giving space and time for further information. This paraphrasing also demonstrated active listening and empathy. The questions prompted interviewees to reflect on their life with PD, their social engagement and their perceptions of social situations in relation to their PD symptoms. An emphasis was placed on exploring experiences of stigma.

Procedure

The Social Research Ethics Committee of University College Cork granted approval for this study. Participation in the study was voluntary, and no participation incentives were offered. Interview data were collected over a 5-month period in 2023. Interviews were conducted face-to-face at participants’ homes (n = 3) or on the university campus (n = 3), via Microsoft Teams (n = 12), or by telephone (n = 3). Interviews lasted an average of 42 (range: 30–50) minutes. Family carers could take part in the interview, to facilitate communication for PwP in more advanced stages. Before the interview, participants provided informed consent, after being given study information and told about the study purpose and procedures, and issues relating to data confidentiality and the right to withdraw their data for up to two weeks post-interview. They were also reassured that any published data reflecting their experiences would be anonymous. Answers were recorded through a dictaphone and subsequently transcribed verbatim by the researcher (FG). Thus, after prompting participants’ sociodemographic information (i.e., age, sex − and gender, if different-, province and time since diagnosis), the interview schedule (Appendix A) followed. Data sufficiency was considered to be reached when no new themes or insights emerged from subsequent interviews, indicating that the data collection process had adequately captured the range of experiences and perspectives relevant to the research question. The term “data sufficiency” has been suggested as a more nuanced alternative to “data saturation,” recognising that the aim is to gather enough rich and relevant data rather than exhaust every possible perspective [29].

Data analysis

Following Braun and Clarke’s [29] guidelines, data were electronically transcribed. To uphold confidentiality, pseudonyms were applied to participants. Participant information that could potentially be linked to them (e.g., people/place names), was redacted during transcription.

Braun and Clarke’s [29] six phases of analysis entailed several cycles of coding, generating, refining, and reviewing themes and subthemes. Phase one involved thoroughly reading the transcripts to boost familiarity. An initial coding list, based on all transcripts, was produced until theoretical saturation was reached (i.e., no additional insights were identified from the latter data). The transcripts and initial codes were then transferred to Nvivo 12 software to facilitate data organisation for constant comparison. After examining the interrelationships and implications between candidate themes, the generation of subthemes and a more cohesive hierarchically-structured framework was developed. Positionality statement

As a researcher with no prior experience or qualifications in PD or other neurodegenerative conditions, my personal interest in the topic guided my approach to the research. Although I did not have a relationship with the participants or any ties to relevant national associations, I remained conscious of my tendency to focus on stigma in PD (as I believe highlighting negative aspects is crucial for raising awareness and promoting change). Thus, I regularly checked in with my supervisors, and kept reflective memos, to ensure that this bias did not unduly influence the research process. During interviews, I avoided leading questions and allowed participants to take the lead, giving them the space to focus on what was most significant to them. Throughout the research, I felt empathy for the participants, which likely influenced my engagement. Despite these emotional responses, I made a conscious effort to remain objective by staying focused on the participants' perspectives and letting their voices shape the findings.

Themes and subthemes.

‘Experiencing and Sharing the Diagnosis’ describes participants’ personal responses to learning they had PD, from the lack of empathy by healthcare professionals to the shock participants felt, to the challenges they faced when sharing the news with their family. ‘Adjusting Identity and Relationships’ examines the acceptance process and difficulties with sharing feelings, illustrating how internalized stigma and fears about disease progression affect identity and coping. ‘Navigating Everyday Social Life with PD’ explores participants’ engagement with their social sphere, encompassing disclosing their diagnosis and maintaining social engagement, highlighting how symptoms and felt stigma influence social interactions. ‘Extrinsic and intrinsic attitudes’ captures experiences of stigma through healthcare professionals’ attitudes, public scrutiny and self-consciousness, detailing how enacted stigma and felt stigma reinforce each other. The interviews explored how participants reacted from a practical and psychological point of view during the period around the diagnosis, with experiences grouped into three sub-themes.

Early reactions to receiving a PD diagnosis

Participants’ first encounters with PD as a personal reality were often shaped by how the diagnosis was delivered and the emotions it triggered. For many, the moment of disclosure felt abrupt, with professionals perceived as showing limited empathy or sensitivity. Such early interactions influenced not only their immediate emotional adjustment but also how they anticipated others might view or respond to them in the future. “Tony [pseudonym] came in with me then, and he was as shocked, I think, as I was, by how it was delivered. No softening anything or anything. So that was that”. Helen (66, F, 8 years post-diagnosis). Similarly, Max remembered the way he felt at the doctor’s office:He said, do this for me, and do that, and he said, stand up and walk around the chair […] And he said, yeah, so, let's start on this medication or whatever. And I said, “So what is it?” And he said, “It's Parkinsonism of course.“ […]It left me more upset than angry. And then anger… The lack of empathy was huge. and the abruptness. (65, M, 17 years post-diagnosis)

Immediate emotional response

Many remembered the shock they felt at hearing their diagnosis. This reaction was particularly strong among participants with young-onset PD, where the unexpected nature of the diagnosis contributed to the overwhelming emotional impact at the moment of revelation. For these participants, the moment of diagnosis not only marked the beginning of living with PD’s physical symptoms but also introduced a new social identity that they feared could lead to judgement, pity, or exclusion. Matt described his reaction, saying, “I wasn't ready for a diagnosis of Parkinson's disease at age 44, and it completely blew me out of the water.” (58, M, 14 years post-diagnosis). Trevor expressed his disbelief, stating, “I was gutted. I was looking for all the answers as to how it could have happened to me, what was the cause of it.” (72, M, 13 years post-diagnosis). Joe reflected on the emotional toll, saying, “I was devastated for two weeks… But eventually it wore off and I just got on with life from the 65 mark onwards.” (76, M, 13 years post-diagnosis).

Breaking it to family members

Participants remembered telling the news of their PD diagnosis to their loved ones. The emotional weight of sharing the diagnosis was a significant moment for participants, with reactions varying between emotional distress and calm acceptance. Many family members reacted in a composed manner while trying to rationalise the shock. Conversely, others’ family reacted with feelings of despair and hopelessness. For some, family reactions of distress or despair reinforced feelings of being seen primarily through the lens of the illness, heightening concerns about being pitied or treated differently. This awareness of altered perceptions marked an early point at which stigma, felt or anticipated, began to shape their relationships. Donald tried to keep it hidden as long as he could until his symptoms became too evident, explaining, “I couldn't tell her. Then eventually […] my leg was starting to drag […] and the shaking a bit more. So […] I told them eventually, so they were quite shocked. They were a bit hurt too.” (64, M, 16 years post-diagnosis). His reluctance to disclose reflects a fear of being seen differently or reduced to the label of his illness, a common element of anticipated stigma. By delaying disclosure, he sought to maintain a sense of normalcy and avoid the potential pity, altered treatment, or emotional distress that might follow, yet this concealment also risked eroding trust, as seen in his family’s feelings of hurt. Alex recalled:Told her (sister) and she said, 'you don't seem to be very bad, so it's okay.' And then I was scared to tell my partner. And I told him. He said, 'It's okay. It doesn't impact on us at all now.” (58, M, 14 years post-diagnosis)

Participants were asked how they viewed themselves as a result of being diagnosed with PD, with views grouped into 3 sub-themes.

Self-acceptance

Many PwP reported having accepted their condition at last: “Acceptance is not surrender. It's just you accept what you can do at that moment, that day […] and that's it. No point in beating yourself about.” (Donald, 64, M, 16 years post-diagnosis). This feeling of acceptance came with an awareness of their helplessness in completing certain tasks, “I said, I have to learn to accept that I can't do everything the way I used to do it, and that's okay.” (Alex, 76, M, 9 years post-diagnosis). On the other hand, some PwP admitted not having processed their condition completely and still felt like they had not accepted it, like David:The one statement I know is Michael J. Fox. It's all about acceptance. And if you don't mind me using the derogatory term, Michael J. Fox [Foundation] can take its acceptance and shove it where the sun don't shine […] Have I fully accepted it? No. (62, M, 11 years post-diagnosis)

The one statement I know is Michael J. Fox. It's all about acceptance. And if you don't mind me using the derogatory term, Michael J. Fox [Foundation] can take its acceptance and shove it where the sun don't shine […] Have I fully accepted it? No. (62, M, 11 years post-diagnosis)

Rejecting acceptance narratives may reflect frustration with societal expectations and a struggle to integrate the diagnosis into one’s identity.

Fear of the future

Many interviewees said they would actively avoid places and people that would make them think about their future as PwP, like Patrick: “Where we all gather together […] there's somebody who was ten years [with PD] and they're very badly … so I’d be pretty conscious of that.” (59, M, 4 years post-diagnosis). On the contrary, words of acceptance and living in the moment arose from a few lone voices:See people who are down the line in Parkinson's and it can be devastating. I'll accept that I'm living today. I am what I am today. And when I get to that stage, I'll be what I am at that stage. (Sean, 78, M, 7 years post-diagnosis)

Difficulties with sharing feelings

Participants were asked if they felt comfortable sharing their feelings with their loved ones. Most did not. Ava attributed this to her husband’s inability to deal with emotional issues: “My husband is very much a head-in-the-sand kind of man, and he wouldn't be good at emotional stuff […] he doesn't know how to deal with me.” (70, F, 1 year post-diagnosis). Others attributed their unwillingness to share their feelings around PD to the fact that people would not understand anyway, such in the case of David: “I don't confide in any. I'd love to, but I don't think they'd understand […].” (62, M, 11 years post-diagnosis). Many participants expressed that when asked how they are doing, they do not feel like opening up, as questions like “How are you?” sound like a mere display of passive interest where others do not expect a true answer. From their accounts, most participants admitted, almost with resignation, how no one in their close circle of people would volunteer to genuinely ask them how they were feeling about their illness. This was reflected in Mary’s account: “I would have expected to offload everything with them, but for some reason, maybe I'm protecting them. They don't ask as much because I don't think they're able to cope, really.” (58, F, 4 years post-diagnosis).Theme 3: Navigating Everyday social life with PD

Others admitted some reticence in telling people around them for fear of rumors and bad reactions, like Linda: “I would say it has been like the wave in a lake. I have spread the news slowly, yeah. To my closest friends first, then more people around.” (58, F, 14 years post-diagnosis).

Maintaining social engagement

A few participants reported having a wide social circle of family and friends. At the same time, some interviewees were comfortable with engaging less in social activities. “I'm very happy to be alone and be quiet. So, I tend to be less involved in social activities, and that's okay.” (Alex, 76, M, 9 years post-diagnosis). Sean reflected a similar balance: “And now I have a reasonably good social life, my family with the people around me, with people I play golf with, but I would be hesitant to automatically mix in unknown situations.” (Sean, 78, M, 7 years post-diagnosis).

However, the majority of participants’ social lives were affected by worries about PD symptoms in social situations. Claire expressed this concern vividly: “When I'm walking the street, I'm saying to myself, is my walk different? Does it attract attention? I say things like that.” (Claire, 64, F, 4 years post-diagnosis). Physical symptoms, such as difficulties with dexterity, also posed barriers: “I have to ask for a paper cup because I actually cannot hold a cup with a handle correctly.” (Ava, 70, F, 1 year post-diagnosis). Voice changes were another common social barrier, limiting participants' ability to engage in conversations. “When you get a diagnosis of Parkinson’s, your voice begins to fade. And that's really a problem because if you can't be heard, you're going to be excluded naturally from the conversation.” (Matt, 58, M, 15 years post-diagnosis).Theme 4: Extrinsic and intrinsic attitudes

Participants were also directly asked about episodes in which they felt stigmatized or treated in an unfair manner, which are described across 3 sub-themes.

Healthcare professionals’ attitudes

The interview touched on participants’ views of how they are treated within the healthcare system. In addition to a non-empathetic disclosure experience, the general feeling that transpired through the accounts of post-diagnostic care is one of disillusionment, feeling overlooked and undervalued as a person: “We were three years going to [hospital] and never seen a consultant. You felt a little undervalued, dismissed, like a number.” Joe (76, M, 13 years post-diagnosis). Such is also the case of Anne, who reported that she was overlooked and dismissed at the hospital:The attitude of the doctors and nurses […] was deplorable. They looked at me as a person of 71 at the time, a person with Parkinson's … she’ll be all right. She's old. The doctor, after looking at me for five minutes … uhm … he didn't even examine my hip. Public scrutiny

Some participants shared episodes of stigmatization when others made incorrect assumptions about the nature of their tremors. For instance, Ava recalled an encounter in which a person insinuated her symptoms were as a result of alcohol abuse, leaving her dismayed: “I've had funny remarks made to me. “Were we on the soup last night?”, that sort of remark. I've had that said a couple of times, and you could give a sharp answer and say, “I have bloody Parkinson's.” (70, F, 1-year post-diagnosis). Donald shared how using a walking stick early on affected how others perceived him:“When I was diagnosed, first thing I was told, or was advised by somebody was to use a walking stick from the very start because I was a bit of… weird. I felt…I thought I was still young, like I was turning 48, and people might think that I was on drugs or that I would be drunk during the day, walking around the − walking on the street. I used a cane as a walking stick, earlier on, and people will see the cane, so they'll kind of accept that, you know, they'll give me − they'll walk around you.” (Donald, 64, M, 16 years post-diagnosis).

They'll get out of your way, or they'll make room for you […] Most people are sympathetic.” (64, M, 16 years post-diagnosis).

Self-consciousness

A recurrent social situation where PwP experience felt stigma is at the supermarket.

“Sometimes if I'm a bit slow, I'm conscious of people behind me, but they're not saying anything to me. It's me that's being conscious.” (Alex, 76, M, 9 years post-diagnosis). “You're trying to […] keep up with the pace of everybody else […] if you put yourself under that pressure, then you stall even more.” (Max, 65, M, 17 years post-diagnosis). Others admitted to feeling bad about their PD symptoms in social situations, like Joanna during a gathering with friends: “Nobody's made me feel bad, but I just feel bad myself about it.” (71, F, 3 years post-diagnosis). Donald (64, M, 16 years post-diagnosis) reflected on disclosing his diagnosis to family: “Because I was − there was like a stigma. Kind of like a failure, or something. When I was diagnosed that day, I couldn't believe it because I thought Parkinson's was for older people… I didn't think younger people got it at all.” This highlights how internalised stigma and misconceptions about PD, especially regarding age, can intensify feelings of isolation and reluctance to disclose the diagnosis.

Discussion

This study investigated the perspectives and experiences of people living with PD in Ireland in relation to stigma. Reactions to the diagnosis were mixed, with some in shock or denial and others relieved. Family and friends had similar responses, ranging from rationalization to fear. most participants were disappointed with the healthcare system, describing the delivery of their diagnosis as abrupt and impersonal, leaving them in shock and distress, and feeling undervalued in their care afterwards. The mixed reactions to diagnosis and disclosure observed here echo findings by Bos and colleagues [6] and Chaudoir and colleagues [7]. Both studies highlight how stigma processes, including felt and enacted stigma, contribute to emotional distress and affect disclosure decisions, which aligns with our findings on how the diagnosis experience shapes feelings of vulnerability and social withdrawal among PwP.

While many participants struggled to share their emotions about PD, some expressed frustration over the lack of emotional support from loved ones. In our study, PwP sometimes shielded loved ones from their diagnosis and didn’t share their feelings, and also downplayed the PD to friends and others. This has been reported by Schrag and colleagues [30], who found that PwP often protected their family members or caregivers from the full extent of their symptoms, emotional struggles, and diagnosis. This indicates a role for peer support, wherein a PwP can honestly discuss worries and feelings with people outside their family who understand what they are experiencing. The acceptance process was split; some struggled with disbelief or denial, while others moved through grief stages towards acceptance, which often included feelings of powerlessness. These findings support Moss-Morris [22], illustrating that psychological adjustment in PwP involves not only adapting to physical symptoms but also navigating shifts in identity, with participants describing a process marked by denial, grief, and eventual acceptance. Most participants feared the future due to the unpredictability of symptoms and avoided social situations with those in more advanced stages. This fear of what lies ahead, though not a direct manifestation of stigma, is an important emotional barrier that may prevent newly diagnosed individuals from attending support groups or exercise classes, potentially increasing their isolation and limiting access to valuable peer support.

Most participants did not let their PD diagnosis negatively affect their social life, though many were hesitant to interact with unfamiliar groups due to concerns about symptoms like tremors, gait impairment, and voice changes, fearing unwanted stares and comments. Notably, no participants mentioned facial masking as a concern, suggesting possible low awareness or no perceived connection to stigma, or a low incidence in this particular cohort. A few were motivated to engage more socially post-diagnosis, while others did not notice any change post-diagnosis. These findings partially confirm existing literature, supporting the link between stigma-related concerns and social hesitation [19,20], though the overall limited impact on participants’ social lives and absence of concern about facial masking suggest a more nuanced or cohort-specific experience. Most participants were comfortable sharing their diagnosis, though some preferred to control who they told to avoid rumors or negative reactions.

Experiences within the healthcare system reflected a sense of being devalued and dismissed. Participants described non-empathetic interactions, feeling like a number, and being overlooked due to age or diagnosis, contributing to a broader sense of disillusionment with post-diagnostic care. Experiences of stigma included overt discrimination, particularly being mistaken for inebriated or incapable of simple tasks. Felt stigma was more common, with participants expressing self-consciousness in social settings and reluctance to disclose their diagnosis or share feelings. The findings from this study align with previous research on stigma in PD, particularly regarding its impact on social interactions and self-perception. Some participants in this study, like those in Ma and colleagues [3] and Hou and colleagues [19], expressed reluctance to socialize due to concerns about visible symptoms linked to stigma. As found in studies by Lin and colleagues [20] and Cacioppo and colleagues [21], such stigma can lead to social isolation, negatively impacting emotional well-being. Experiences of enacted stigma, such as being mistaken for intoxicated, align with Rao and colleagues [9] and Miller and Thomas [10]. Overall, the results highlight the complex relationship between stigma, social engagement, and psychological adjustment in PD [18,22].

Implications

PD comes with an array of symptomatologic expressions, and at a costly social price. The findings from this research can inform the implementation of social and healthcare strategies to improve PwP’s experiences with the illness. Firstly, healthcare professionals should be aware of how a person might react when receiving a diagnosis of PD. Clinicians should monitor PwP’s adaptation to the illness while identifying and addressing potential barriers to their optimal adjustment. For instance, a study by Gilbert and colleagues [31] found a correlation between PwP’s self-alienation and deep-brain stimulation post-operative estrangement. To fully understand PwP’s adaptation process, it is important to adopt a hermeneutic approach to the illness, in a way that takes into account any influencing external variables that might have an impact on their acceptance and adjustment to the diagnosis.

In addition, greater attention should be given to how people are supported immediately after receiving a diagnosis of PD. Providing access to clear, empathetic communication and follow-up educational materials could help PwP and their families better understand what to expect and where to find support. Resources signposting local and national peer-support networks, counselling options, and community-based groups could ease the emotional impact of the diagnosis and promote early engagement with coping supports. Structured follow-up sessions or facilitated introductions to support groups might further normalise emotional responses and reduce the sense of isolation that often accompanies diagnosis.

Secondly, the unpredictable nature of this neurodegenerative disorder makes understanding patients’ experiences and psychosocial well-being even more crucial. Indeed, stigmatizing attitudes, discrimination, shame, embarrassment and social isolation are silent and hidden phenomena that need to be included in the holistic management of PD. As stigma has a negative effect on the psychosocial adjustment of PwP, it may have direct consequences on illness management, such as driving PwP to stop or avoid treatment (e.g., [32]). These authors showed that stigma not only impacts emotional well-being but may also influence how individuals manage their illness, underscoring the importance of addressing stigma in clinical care and support interventions. In this regard, psychotherapeutic approaches to stigma might be a potential avenue to strengthen skills to deal with felt stigma (e.g., [33,34]). These might include cognitive-behavioral therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, or group counseling, which have been shown to promote self-esteem, empowerment, and adaptive social behaviors [35]. More recently, compassion focused therapy (CFT) group interventions have also been explored with PwP, showing that such approaches are acceptable, can foster social connection, and have the potential to alleviate distress [36]. Thirdly, there is an urgency to inform and educate those who have poor PD awareness and literacy, to discourage stigmatizing views. By disseminating information on PD to the general public, starting with informal caregivers, it may be possible to reduce disability which originates from the interplay of an illness with personal and environmental factors. Finally, a holistic and multidisciplinary approach to PD is paramount to ensure the effective management and treatment of the illness. The necessity to focus on PwP’s NMS is a core requirement for patient-centered practice, as is healthcare professionals’ understanding of the patient as a person instead of a clinical presentation. While this was not directly explored in our data, prior research strongly supports the importance of this approach (e.g., [1,2]).

Indeed, NMS often underpin reduced social participation, heightened self-consciousness, and withdrawal, reinforcing feelings of stigma and isolation. Addressing NMS within the broader psychosocial care framework would, therefore, not only improve QoL but also enhance the effectiveness of social and peer-support interventions. Integrating NMS assessment and management into routine multidisciplinary care could help clinicians better understand the emotional and interpersonal dimensions of PwP’s experience, ensuring that support for social connectedness and stigma reduction is truly holistic.

Strengths and limitations

This study has some limitations. As all participants self-selected to participate, the research might have suffered from self-selection bias. Also, participants who chose to take part in the interview showed a certain level of proactiveness, which may have led to the inclusion of individuals who experience lower levels of shame, embarrassment, and social avoidance or anxiety, thus skewing results. Future research should look at the experiences of PwP from different backgrounds and not associated with any support/advocacy groups, to get a more comprehensive narrative of how it is to live with PD. The study sample comprised PwP mostly in a mild and moderate stage of the illness, which limited the exploration of experiences of PwP in the more advanced stage, or manifesting more visible signs of disability. Also, the sample involved participants aged 49 or higher, which excluded the consideration of younger age onset on felt stigma in PD. The findings from the present study warrant additional investigation into the stigma experienced by other subcategories of PwP.

This study has some strengths. The qualitative approach allowed the researcher to delve into participants’ inner perspectives and capture raw feelings and experiences of their journey with PD. The sample comprised an almost equal proportion of males and females, offering a gender perspective on how it is to live with and adapt to PD. This is important, as PD is commonly thought of as a male disorder, which might influence the views and behaviors of the general population towards PwP. Furthermore, as women are usually considered more emotionally competent than men [15], the impact of social symptoms could provoke more stigmatizing attitudes toward women with PD. Finally, this study captured a type of felt stigma that was not yet explored in the literature, where participants’ stated reluctance to talk about their feelings around PD with their loved ones could have been due to the shame and embarrassment they felt about sharing a piece of themselves that was marked by the illness. This avoidance of in-depth conversations may reflect broader cultural factors, such as the traditionally reserved nature of Irish society, where discussing personal difficulties and emotional struggles is often limited or stigmatized. Given this cultural context, it is perhaps unsurprising that PwP might hesitate to disclose the full extent of their challenges, highlighting the need for sensitive approaches to emotional support within families and peer networks. Future studies should focus on understanding the mechanism of inhibited sharing in PwP. This would allow families of PwP to comprehend the psychological underlying mechanisms that prevent PwP from confiding in them and, hopefully, help them to broach the issue with their families or peer supporters.

Conclusion

Social attitudes towards PD are critical to PwP’s adaptation to the illness, as they determine the environment in which they must interact with others and live. Stigma refers to not only a sense of shame and embarrassment emerging from a heightened self-perception from the loss of autonomy and agency and visible symptoms of PD, but also the manifested attitudes and beliefs of the public and healthcare workers towards PwP. Despite experiences of being misjudged or treated differently, many PwP continue to benefit from positive social engagement and openness about their diagnosis with family and friends, which supports their wellbeing and acceptance of the illness. However, some PwP have significant unmet emotional needs and feel uncomfortable discussing the full impact of PD with loved ones, indicating a clear role for accessible formal psychological support. In the healthcare context, better consideration of PwP’s experience of living with PD and their psychological needs may allow healthcare workers to care for PwP in a more holistic, empathetic and tailored way. Finally, these findings highlight the importance of public education and emotional support options to reduce stigma and improve the quality of life for people living with PD.

Clinical impact

The social experiences of people with Parkinson’s disease (PwP) in Ireland include frequent encounters with stigma, challenges in unfamiliar social contexts due to visible symptoms, reliance on strong but sometimes emotionally limited support networks, and a recognised need for additional outlets for emotional expression. Stigma is commonly experienced by PwP, encompassing feelings of being misjudged, treated unfairly, or devalued. While many PwP remain socially engaged and share their diagnosis with friends and family, this openness does not necessarily signal an absence of stigma; rather, it reflects resilience and the value of supportive networks in counteracting its effects. Social engagement can promote emotional well-being and acceptance, but reluctance to discuss the personal impact of PD points to ongoing internalized stigma and unmet emotional support needs. These findings highlight the importance of healthcare, community, and research initiatives that address both the social and psychological dimensions of living with PD.

Plain Language Summary

Parkinson’s disease is a long-term condition that affects movement, mood, and many other parts of daily life. Many of the symptoms of Parkinson’s are visible to others, and impact upon social experiences. Stigma i.e., the feeling of being devalued, judged, or treated differently because of their condition is a common social experience for PwP. Stigma can lead to feelings of shame, isolation, or being misunderstood, adding emotional and social challenges to an already difficult illness.

This study looked at how people with Parkinson’s in Ireland experience stigma and how it affects their lives. Twenty-one people with Parkinson’s took part in interviews where they talked about their symptoms, their social and emotional experiences, and the kind of support they need.

We found that while many people were open about their diagnosis, they often felt self-conscious in public or at events with people they did not know well. Some had experienced being stared at, pitied, or even misjudged—for example, someone thought a participant who was shaking was drunk. Even when their family and friends were supportive, most people didn’t feel comfortable talking about how Parkinson’s made them feel emotionally. Some said they would like more support outside of their families, such as informal meetups or talking with a counsellor.

Despite the challenges, most participants had strong support networks that helped them manage their condition and stay socially active. Still, stigma—both from others and from their own feelings of embarrassment—affected how they saw themselves and how they interacted with the world.

This research helps us understand what it is really like to live with Parkinson’s disease and how stigma can impact emotional well-being.
