Connectivity mediates the spatial ecological impacts of a glyphosate-based herbicide in experimental metaecosystems
Authors: Jorge Octavio Negrín Dastis, Brendon McGuinness, Christina P. Tadiri, Viviane Yargeau, Andrew Gonzalez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Metacommunity ecology has shown that connectivity is important for the persistence of a species locally and across connected ecosystems, however we do not know if ecological effects in freshwater ecosystems exposed to biocides leaking from agriculture depend on metaecosystem connectivity. We experimentally replicated metaecosystems in the laboratory using gradostats as a model system. We tested the effects of connectivity, in terms of node distance from the pollutant-source, flow rate, and a glyphosate-based herbicide, on phytoplankton productivity, diversity and stability. Gradostats were composed of interconnected equally spaced nodes where resources and phytoplankton move directionally along a gradient of increasing distance from the source of the polluting herbicide. We hypothesised that ecological effects would be stronger in the node situated closer to the point of herbicide input, but that flow would suppress phytoplankton populations in distant nodes. Overall, RoundUp impacted phytoplankton productivity and stability by reducing algal biomass and abundances. This occurred especially in the node closest to the diluted herbicide point-source and under high flow, where species abundances were heavily suppressed by the effects of the rapidly flowing herbicide. At low flow on the other hand, distant nodes where buffered from the effects of the slow-moving herbicide. No differences in beta and gamma diversity among replicate metaecosystems was found; however, a significant loss of alpha diversity in all metaecosystems occurred through time until the end of the experiment. Together, these results point to the importance of considering aquatic connectivity in management plans for monitoring and mitigating unintended ecological consequences of agrochemical runoff.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00442-024-05601-3.

Introduction

Glyphosate is a potent herbicide and has been shown to affect non-target natural areas neighbouring sites with intensive agricultural practices subjected to periodic herbicide drifts (Marshall 2001; Lucadamo et al. 2018). Glyphosate can impact a variety of organisms (algae, microbes and animals) in both terrestrial and aquatic environments (Helander et al. 2012). In Canada, most glyphosate-based herbicides are not approved for use in freshwater aquatic environments, with specific Canadian water quality guidelines established for the protection of aquatic life set between 0.8 and 1.32 mg l−1 (Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment 2012). In the U.S. on the other hand, standards of glyphosate concentrations are set to reach acute toxicity in non-vascular plants at approximately 12.1 mg l−1 (Smedbol et al. 2017). These two countries share some major waterways so, in a connectivity context, what happens in the U.S. can affect Canadian ecosystems. Research suggests that glyphosate-based herbicides entering aquatic system via agricultural runoff (Bashir et al. 2020) and groundwater seepage may contaminate and thus alter the physiology of aquatic organisms (Annett et al. 2014; Hébert et al. 2019). In agricultural settings, connectivity can directly affect the dynamics of contaminants by dispersing species and resources, coupling contaminant stocks in spatially separated ecosystems (Schiesari et al. 2017), especially when uncontaminated aquatic habitats are linked to land contaminated sites. Under these conditions, individual species can also frequently influence local contaminant fate by sequestering, amplifying and temporarily storing contaminants in their biomass, and/or transforming a contaminant's physical and chemical structure via stabilization, activation and degradation (Moore et al. 2011).

There is strong experimental evidence to suggest that glyphosate-based herbicides (e.g., RoundUp) can affect the structure of phytoplankton assemblages (Pérez et al. 2007, 2011; Vera et al. 2010; Annett et al. 2014; Fugère et al. 2020; Brovini et al. 2021; Berman et al. 2022). Overall, there are several empirical studies suggesting that glyphosate can change phytoplankton production and community composition (Rumschlag et al. 2020). For example, Vera et al. (2010), conducted a 42-day experiment and found that diatoms were far more sensitive to RoundUp in periphyton communities than cyanobacteria, resulting in a change in diversity after only 8 days. Saxton et al. (2011), using microcosms containing phytoplankton and water from Lake Erie, found that glyphosate suppressed the growth of the cyanobacterium Microcystis but facilitated the growth of another potentially toxic cyanobacterium, Planktothrix. More recently, Berman et al. (2022) found that glyphosate was associated with higher abundance of small-celled phytoplankton better adapted to poor light conditions arising from turbidity. In many cases, sensitive species are unlikely to be effective biotransporters since they are negatively affected by the contaminant and it follows that the more dissimilar the biotransporter is relative to the local community in terms of the sensitivity to the contaminant, the higher the likelihood that biological transport will be relevant (Schiesari et al. 2017). All these experimental studies point to the physical connectedness of terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems from the spatial flow rates of materials and organisms across ecosystem boundaries, with the potential for land cover changes to further impact aquatic biodiversity and ecosystem functions at local scales and that glyphosate influences different components of the planktonic food web by favoring some species over others.

Knowledge of how habitat connectivity and spatial processes interact is essential to understand the magnitude and timing of diversity change and its consequences for ecosystem functioning (Staddon et al. 2010). In agricultural landscapes, human modifications to land cover and habitat have altered how pesticides spread and flow across land and through water (Elser and Bennett 2011; Nyström et al. 2019). One way by which pesticides are expected to affect the productivity and stability of linked aquatic ecosystems, is by disrupting flows of energy, materials and organisms in metaecosystems (Loreau et al. 2003; Donohue et al. 2016; McCann et al. 2021). This process can ultimately lead to highly modified landscape flows and stability (Scheffer et al. 2001; Zaller and Brühl 2019; McCann et al. 2021). Strong connectivity driven by directional transport networks (Raymond et al. 2008), can accelerate the flow of agrochemical compounds such as biocides, spreading through a network of ecosystems, and ultimately destabilise biological processes far from the source (Diaz and Rosenberg 2008). Therefore, what happens in one location can have ecological consequences downstream from the source of pollution and throughout the network of ecosystems, ultimately altering local and regional community structure and ecosystem functioning (Rumschlag et al. 2020; Harvey et al. 2023). In a meteacosystem context, we use distance of nodes (Node) in reference to the location from the pollutant-source (proximal, central vs. distal) as a proxy for structural connectivity. Additionally, we use rate of dispersal and algal movement between nodes (Flow rate), which accelerates or reduces the movement of organisms between nodes, as a proxy for functional connectivity.

Metaecosystem theory predicts that external additions such as nutrients can drive the loss of ecological stability and function. In particular, slowing down spatial nutrient pathways can be a potent means of stabilising degraded ecosystems (McCann et al. 2021). In this framework, the coupling among ecosystems creates feedbacks that can propagate instabilities (Gounand et al. 2018), suggesting that biocides also could potentially propagate instability across entire ecosystems (Gounand et al. 2014). However, the effects of a biocide are different than nutrient enrichment as analyzed by McCann et al. (2021), because in the case of an herbicide, it suppresses plankton populations whereas nutrients stimulate population growth. Thus, it is theoretically possible that increasing the availability of a biocide to spatially connected ecosystems, may also introduce instability due to decreasing densities of species by reducing species fluctuations, and thus lead to loss of diversity and species turnover.

Herbicides often move between different water bodies by surface runoff and by percolation through the soil and into the groundwater, and have been detected in high concentrations in surface waters worldwide (de Souza et al. 2020). Much of the theory on the effects of connectivity on the impacts of runoff on aquatic ecosystems remains untested by experiments (but see Gülzow et al. 2019; Harvey et al. 2020; and Tadiri et al. 2024 for some recent examples). Gradostats, a term coined by Lovitt and Wimpenny (1979), are excellent laboratory model systems designed to mimic the spatial and temporal heterogeneity in connected ecosystems (Lovitt and Wimpenny 1979; Codeço and Grover 2001). In a gradostat, medium is pumped from a reservoir into connected culture sequence vessels, and collected in a common waste culture receiver at the end of the system (Lovitt and Wimpenny 1981). A multichannel peristaltic pump ensures the transfer of media between the vessels connected by tubing. Gradostats are particularly useful for manipulating movement of solute concentrations present in the environment. Gradostats are especially suited to testing ecological theories of systems that are typically spatially heterogeneous and temporally dynamic (Codeço and Grover 2001). A gradostat in its simplest form is a dynamical system in which multiple chemostats are interconnected by directed flows and diffusion (Taylor and Rapaport 2021). This type of multistage continuous culture system has an advantage over single-stage homogenous chemostats in that they produce dynamic solute gradients that can be transferred between interconnected nodes. In a recent experimental study, Tadiri et al. (2024) found that connectivity can impact stability of distant ecosystems, particularly in linear systems. Few other studies (but see Bork et al. 2021 for an example), have assessed the ecological outcomes of gradients in biocides in connected systems.

Herbicides can sometimes flow from agricultural land into freshwater ecosystems, spreading between connected water bodies, and transforming their diversity, functioning, and stability. However, we do not know whether herbicide effects depend on (i) how far an ecosystem is from the source of pollution (Node 1 ‘proximal’ vs. Node 2 ‘central’ vs. Node 3 ‘distal’ from the herbicide) and (ii) the rate at which the herbicide and organisms move between ecosystems (flow rate; rate of dispersal and algal movement between nodes). We conducted a 10-day fully replicated controlled gradostat experiment to assess how connectivity mediates the ecological effects of an herbicide (RoundUp) on the productivity, diversity and stability of an aquatic phytoplankton community. We characterise instability in our study as mean-driven instability, which occurs when one species at equilibrium is pushed to low densities inflating the coefficient of variation (CV). The experimental gradostat metaecosystems, each composed of 3 interconnected nodes, were arranged unidirectionally on a gradient of increasing distance from the source of agrochemical pollution. We predicted that the herbicide would cause an overall decrease in algal biomass at the node level, and would also have a negative impact in community composition due to different sensitivity of phytoplankton groups, increasing instability. We hypothesized that these effects would be mediated by connectivity. Specifically, we predicted that node distance from the source would buffer the ecological effects of herbicide, where simple diffusion would produce stronger effects in the node situated closer to the point of input, but that high flow rate would act to bring more herbicide in faster throughout the metaecosystem.

Methods

Experimental design

We used gradostats as a multistage continuous culture model system with series of flasks connected by tubing linked by a controlled flow of medium that is pumped in, through and out producing an herbicide solute gradient. This 10-day experiment featured a 2 × 2 factorial design to test the effects of flow rate (two levels: low flow at 10% volume displacement/day vs. high flow at 33% volume displacement/day) and herbicide RoundUp® exposure [two levels: no herbicide (NH) vs. herbicide (H)] on the productivity, stability and diversity of phytoplankton communities. To justify these different flow conditions prior to conducting the 10-day experiment, a preliminary 14-day pilot study was conducted in the laboratory to assert flow parameter rates. More information can be found in the Electronic Supplemental Material (ESM1). One gradostat unit consisting of three interconnected nodes (500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks) was allocated to each treatment combination. The full experimental design (4 metaecosystems) was replicated over three time blocks spanning an interval of 10 days between blocks (total of 12 gradostats). Gradostats were maintained in a closed 36 ft2 growth chamber in the Phytotron experimental facility at McGill University under 12 h light:12 h dark light intensity photocycle, constant ambient temperature of 20 °C and a light intensity of approximately 50 μE m−2 s−1. Percentage humidity in the Phytotron chamber was kept constant for the entire duration of the experiment at 50%.

In spatial terms, the gradostat was composed of three nodes connected in a simple linear chain (Node 1 → Node 2 → Node 3). The first node of the series (Node 1) was connected to an inflow Erlenmeyer flask of 1000 mL (Inflow Reservoir → Node 1) that contained the solution of diluted herbicide. Glyphosate was therefore a point source contaminant from the Inflow reservoir, that flowed into Node 1, then Node 2 and Node 3, and finally ended its course in the outflow reservoir. Therefore, Node 1 was the first to receive glyphosate followed by Node 2 and then Node 3. The last node of the series (Node 3) was connected to an outflow Erlenmeyer flask of 1000 mL (Node 3 → Outflow Reservoir) (Fig. 1). For every metaecosystem, each corresponding node was interconnected to each other through masterflex two-stop pump Tygon tubing connected to a 4-channel head peristaltic pump (Dynamax RP-1). Communities were exposed to glyphosate contained in RoundUp. The treatment we applied reflected North American guidelines, since the two countries share some major waterways. We applied diluted concentrations above maximum thresholds for Canadian standards for the protection of aquatic life set at 1.32 mg l−1 and U.S standards of glyphosate concentrations which are set to reach acute toxicity in non-vascular plants at approximately 12.1 mg l−1 (Smedbol et al. 2017). To approximate these concentrations in the laboratory, a first dose of RoundUp was set to 5 mg l−1 (Dose 1, introduced on day 2 of the experiment), < 4 mg l−1 above Canadian standards, and a second dose of RoundUp was set to 10 mg l−1 (Dose 2, introduced on day 5 of the experiment), slightly below U.S standards. Based on the ecology and demographic parameters of the phytoplankton species, this second higher dose was applied in all metaecosystems in order to avoid decontamination, since various studies suggest that various species of algae, such as Chlorella vulgaris (Reno et al. 2016) and other Chlorella sp. (Pachouri et al. 2024), have the potential to reduce glyphosate toxicity.

The herbicide formulation consisted of 293 mg of RoundUp®/L/RSD = 1.5% subsequently diluted in ultrapure type-I milliQ water. Because glyphosate degrades rapidly when exposed to UV radiation, we covered the inflow and all our flasks with aluminium foil to minimise UV reactivity. We used polyethylene flasks to reduce glyphosate adsorption to the flask walls.

Phytoplankton particle analysis and classification

Algal monocultures were composed of the following eight species: 1. Ankistrodesmus falcatus (Chlorophyceae), 2. Tetradesmus obliquus (Chlorophyceae), 3. Scenedesmus obliquus (Chlorophyceae), 4. Raphidocelis subcapitata (Chlorophyceae), 5. Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Chlorophyceae), 6. Parachlorella kessleri (Chlorophyceae), 7. Chlorella vulgaris (Chlorophyceae), and 8. Romeria leopoliensis (Cyanophyceae). We used a highly enriched modified Bold’s basal medium (BBM) (Stein-Taylor 1973) to culture algal species in the laboratory. As a precaution, and before inoculating in Bold’s basal medium, we acclimated Romeria leopoliensis (Cyanophyceae) using a BG-11 medium (Rippka et al. 1979), specifically used as a successful growth medium for most cyanobacteria. Upon preparation and mixing of 8 algal cultures, and since cyanobacteria rely heavily on NaNO3 for efficient growth, a BBM solution was prepared containing 1.5 mL/L of NaNO3 stock solution instead of the standard 1 mL/L and maintained at an approximate pH of 7.1. Please refer to additional laboratory methods concerning algal strains and culturing procedures in the Electronic Supplemental Material (ESM1).

We used the images captured by FlowCAM™ from the monocultures to generate a labeled dataset where the species of each image is known. FlowCAM™ automates particle analysis and extracts key morphological features such as particle diameter, circularity, aspect ratio, intensity and elongation. A sample volume of 1 mL was pipetted from each from one of the duplicate monoculture subcultures for FlowCAM™ sample imaging processing with cell densities that were in the range of 1–5 million cells/mL. We did this analysis for the eight phytoplankton monocultures prior to mixing and inoculating them in the experiment. This approach allows quantification of monoculture particle characteristics with FlowCAM™.

We used the morphological features from our monoculture data to train a K-nearest neighbours’ algorithm (KNN) to classify the different species in our experiment. This machine learning method, which has been previously used on FlowCAM™ data, classifies a phytoplankton particle based on what the K-nearest neighbours are in the training dataset (Blasko et al. 2005). This allowed us to estimate community composition of our samples which we then used to calculate community metrics such as community stability and alpha diversity. We first ran a Principal Components Analysis (PCA) on our monocultures to reduce the dimensionality of our dataset by constructing compact feature representations of their geometric and size-related mean properties (Fig. 2). By default, the PCA was centered, and we used the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) algorithm. The first two principal components captured 98.4% of the total variance. Finally, we conducted a tenfold cross validation using the crossval function in MATLAB (2021), to estimate error and decide on the best hyperparameter K (number of nearest neighbours for classification) to use in our model (Hastie et al. 2009). The testing data for the cross validation was a combination of mixtures of the labelled monoculture data kept apart from the training data. Here, labelled data refers to images of particles in which we know the strain, i.e. our monoculture data. This is what allowed us to evaluate the accuracy of our model which we then used to estimate the community composition of unlabelled data (our mixed samples). The total accuracy of our model averaged among species is 92.3% when K = 3. The highest and lowest species-level accuracy was 98.8% and 76.1% for Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and Tetradesmus obliquus, respectively. The species-level breakdown of classification accuracy is reported in the Electronic Supplemental Material (ESM1). Since we trained our model on monoculture data without the addition of glyphosate, we assumed that presence of other species as well as addition of glyphosate did not affect the morphology of the cells in our images.

Laboratory methods and sampling

Samples were taken daily, approximately 6.25% of the entire node volume, for algal biomass estimation and at two time points for estimating initial (day 2) and final phytoplankton (all species) community composition (day 10). The peristaltic pump was stopped at each sampling period to avoid disrupting the 400 mL volume within each node. After sampling, the amount of solution sampled from the gradostats was then gradually refilled to previous water volumes with new media from the inflow reservoir and/or neighbouring nodes until hydrostatic equilibrium. We measured algal biomass in each node, referred throughout this paper as ‘node algal biomass’, over the entire duration of the experiment. Node algal biomass measurements were taken daily (N = 3 replicates, 10 time points; 360 samples). We monitored total chlorophyll-a (in the range of 0–200 µg chl-a/L) in samples collected in amber centrifuge tubes to stop photosynthetic activity. We measured chlorophyll-a via particle fluorescence using an in vivo analysis by introducing the sample into a 25 mL glass cuvette and placed in a FluoroProbe (bbe Moldaenke GmbH, Schwentinental, Germany).

Changes in phytoplankton community composition were also quantified at the node level for two time points, days 2 and 10, and for all nodes for all metaecosystems by preserving a 7 mL sample in a 10 mL falcon tube and topped with 70% ETOH. Although 8% of the entire node volume was sampled on day 2 for this added analysis, this unique sampling event could have not lowered the glyphosate concentration in our experiment, since another dose was added on day 5 (10 mg/L) that was 2 times the first dose. Since one replicate sample from block 3 was lost for this analysis, reducing the variance among blocks, we only present results for N = 2 metaecosystem replicates for community composition. Each replicate, however, consisted of a large number of species-specific observations involving 10,000 images/minute scanned using a Flow Cytometer coupled with and Microscope [(FlowCAM)™ 8000®, Maine, USA)]. In total therefore, we collected a total of 48 samples which were sequentially scanned for image particle analysis (48 × 8 species = 384 individual species-level measurements) for this analysis, including both no herbicide (NH) and herbicide (H) treated metaecosystems. Images and particles as small as 300 nm were analysed to identify and differentiate algae species-specific particle morphologies through particle size verification screening. In terms of phytoplankton community composition, samples were taken on day 2 (before herbicide addition) and day 10 of the experiment to document impact between initial and final conditions for both no-herbicide (NH) and herbicide (H) treated metaecosystems.

To confirm that the effects we observed on the algae community were due to the persistence and accumulation of glyphosate in the nodes, measurements of glyphosate concentrations were taken from the gradostats exposed to the herbicide solution on the last day of the experiment (Day 10). In total 18 analytical samples were used for analysis (N = 3 replicates, 3 nodes, 2 flow rates, 1 time point). To analyse glyphosate levels, 50 mL water samples were collected from each node, acidified in the laboratory to low pH (pH 2–3) with sulfuric acid (2 M), preserved in amber centrifuge tubes to stop the bacterial activity and stored frozen at − 20 °C until posterior chromatography and liquid-mass spectrometry could be done (GC–MS, IC/LC–MS analysis). Water samples were taken on the last day of the experiment to assess differences in final glyphosate concentration levels in herbicide-treated replicated nodes.

Statistical analysis

We first assessed differences in Log10-transformed glyphosate concentrations for all replicate sets in a single ANOVA, to confirm the efficacy of the herbicide treatment. Since the experiment was run in three time-blocks, ‘block’ was used as a random effect in the model. We fitted a linear mixed-effects model via the REML likelihood function to analyze differences in node algal biomass concentrations in space and time. We used the default REML method estimation used in the lmer package (Bates et al. 2009) in R as it compares models with random effects and the same fixed effect structure. To compute a robust model involving fixed and random effects, we used lme4 to perform single term deletions using Satterthwaite's method. We additionally used the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al. 2017) to approximate p values to a lmer model, and the multi-model inference MuMIn package (Bartoń 2009) which contains an r.squared GLMM function that calculates an equivalent of the R2 for mixed models. We used the following fixed factors in the model; Flow rate (2 levels; low flow vs. high flow), Node (3 levels; Node 1 vs. Node 2 vs. Node 3) and Glyphosate [2 levels; No Herbicide (NH) vs. Herbicide (H)]. In addition, time was coded as fixed, and treated as continuous explanatory variable, even though it was sampled at discrete times. To test the normality of the ANOVA model residuals, we used a Cramer-von Mises normality test. Significant within-level differences for each explanatory fixed factor were analysed using Tukey HSD 95% confidence level tests, in order to detect pairwise differences at a significance level of p < 0.05 (p adjusted differences). In order to measure the proportion of variance associated with each main effect and interaction effect in the mixed model, we calculated effect sizes (η2). Last, a repeated-measures within-subject ANOVA was used at a lower temporal resolution to test the glyphosate effects on node algal biomass to evaluate whether there were any differences between both 2 dosage points (Dose 1 and Dose 2).

To detect changes in the stability of the algae communities between initial and final conditions, we used the coefficient of variation (CV). We estimated species-specific abundance counts and calculated the coefficient of variation (CV) as the standard deviation divided by the mean of raw untransformed species-specific counts detected by FlowCAM™ particle analysis. A full factorial ANOVA was then parametrised at the species-level to analyse community stability as changes in species CV (dependent variable) between initial and final conditions.

Finally, we calculated alpha, beta and gamma diversity to monitor changes in these diversity metrics between initial and final conditions. Alpha diversity was calculated as the average number of species, and gamma as the total number of species. Beta diversity was then calculated as [gamma/alpha – 1]. Local changes were estimated at the node level in the alpha parameter of Fisher's logarithmic series (alpha diversity) between these two time periods using identified species counts through FlowCAM™ particle analysis. We used alpha diversity as it has the advantage that it summarizes the structure of an ecological community with respect to its richness (number of taxonomic groups), evenness (distribution of abundances of the groups), or both (Willis 2019).

Linear mixed models were run using lme4 package (Bates et al. 2009) and effect sizes of significant model terms were calculated using the effectsize package (Ben-Shachar et al. 2020) in R (2022). To statistically quantify abundance counts differences (multiple Y responses) and alpha diversity differences between initial and final conditions, PERMANOVA tests were run using vegan 2.6-4 package (Oksanen et al. 2022) using ade4() and fisher.alpha () diversity functions in R to analyse community data permuted using a Bray–Curtis dissimilarity coefficient matrix. Normality tests were not conducted since PERMANOVA model residuals have the advantage of being distribution-free (Anderson et al. 2014). Finally, we used the pairwise.adonis() function in R version 4.2.1 to test for specific pairwise differences. These tests were conducted at a significance level of p < 0.05 and were subsequently implemented applying the Bonferroni type adjustment to control for family-wise error rates.

Results

Glyphosate concentrations

We found an interaction between Node and Flow rate (Fig. 3; Table 1; Node × Flow rate; F = 27.48; p = 3.31e−05). A gradient of impact was detected between opposite nodes (high concentrations in Node 1 vs. low concentrations in Node 3; Tukey HSD post hoc test; p adj = 0.012; Fig. 3), however this effect was much more pronounced in high flow metaecosystems. Additionally, under low flow, glyphosate concentrations were all lower in all nodes in comparison to high flow (concentrations in all nodes by the end of the experiment where < 7 mg l−1 of glyphosate). Node 2 and Node 3 in the high flow treated nodes also showed high concentrations in the range of 12–16 mg l−1 and 6–11 mg l−1, respectively (Fig. 3). Between-level contrasts showed that Node 1 under high flow reached peak concentrations of just below 40 mg l−1 on Day 10 compared to Node 1 under low flow which reached concentrations of 5 mg l−1 (Tukey HSD post hoc tests conducted at p < 0.05; p adj = 0.002; Fig. 3). Our initial ANOVA (Fig. 3) showed strong statistical differences among nodes in glyphosate concentrations by the end of the experiment that were due to spatial position of the node (Node main effect; Fig. 3; Table 1; F = 35.09; p = 9.69e−06) and flow rate (Fig. 3; Table 1; F = 134.82; p = 6.97e−08). Changes in node algal biomass

Overall we found that the herbicide had a negative impact on final productivity (Table 2; Herbicide (H) main effect; t value 2.89; p = 0.003). We found statistical evidence that both node and flow rate interacted with herbicide input to influence biomass because, downstream, a three-way interaction demonstrated that under low flow, herbicide input negatively impacted algal biomass in central Node 2 (Table 2; Days × Flow rate (low flow) × Node (Node 2); t value 2.33; p = 0.02). Discussion

We used gradostats with mixed algal monotrophic communities to investigate the role of node spatial position and flow rate in hindering or enhancing the ecological effects downstream from the source of herbicide input on productivity, diversity and stability. In our experiment, algal species and resources were being dispersed throughout the nodes of the metaecosystem at the same time as the herbicide via flow. Both phytoplankton biomass and species abundances decreased in response to the herbicide treatment and overall, the magnitude of this effect on stability depended on node (Effect size = 39%) and flow rate (Effect size = 22%), which were connectivity features that we controlled. Our results also suggest that the herbicide suppressed phytoplankton population abundances. Under high flow, when the herbicide moved rapidly through the first node, we found that this suppression was amplified in proximate ecosystems (lower biomass, lower species abundances) locally; however, it was buffered under low flow in more distant ecosystems (higher biomass, higher species abundances).

Under these circumstances we demonstrate that because the concentration of the herbicide was higher in the node closer to the point of input, a buffering effect of connectivity was observed only in more distant nodes at low flow. This buffering effect emerged for biomass, individual species counts, diversity and stability. Additionally, we found that flow rate interacted with the herbicide to directly affect biomass and diversity differences through time. This finding concurs with other studies conducted in the field, that have found that in rivers for example, flow of water downstream (Pollux et al. 2009) also mediates pollution impacts (Vörösmarty et al. 2010) and underpins key ecological mechanisms that operate to mediate species persistence and ecosystem resilience (Beger et al. 2022). While we only used three nodes at the metaecosystem level, our result shows that spatially connected ecosystems are important in determining ecological responses at the node and wider metaecosystem levels. Overall however, more research is required to better understand the propagation of instability driven by these complex compounds through connected ecosystems.

While it is generally acknowledged that diversity and spatial growth processes are affected by the configuration of the spatial (patch) network (Gilarranz and Bascompte 2012; Gilarranz et al. 2017; Thompson and Gonzalez 2017; Blanchard and Munoz 2023), a better understanding of how network metrics and structure relate to flows and functional ecological outcomes could help interpret the effects of biocides in agroecosystems (Bohan et al. 2013; Jeanneret et al. 2021; Windsor et al. 2022). Although we used a simple linear spatial configuration, future research could use gradostats to assess how more complex branching network and non-linear topologies such as in the dendritic networks (Tadiri et al. 2024) or streams and rivers, mediate the effects of glyphosate-based biocides in real-world systems. Since we did not use different trophic levels in this experiment, species biomass and decreases in species abundances were solely the effect of competition and species’ responses (i.e. mortality rates) to the herbicide.

In our study, we found that all species responded similarly to glyphosate addition, with individual species abundances declining significantly when the herbicide was applied. This result potentially implies no species-specific variation in terms of physiological adaptation or plasticity. This suggests that it is unlikely that biological transport is relevant here or that the species acted to remove contaminants from the system. However, small differences in species-specific variation suggest that it is possible that some of the species that we used for the experiment such as Chlorella sp., may have been capable of storing the herbicide in their biomass (Pachouri et al. 2024). This process would have likely acted against the simple diffusion effect of the herbicide, potentially explaining the amplification of glyphosate concentrations in the node closest to the herbicide point source of pollution.

Our study aligns with recent theory proposed by McCann et al. (2021) that points to the important relationship between connectivity and stability, mediated by flow; however, it also differs from it because in our experimental metaecosystem, overall, instability effects were not propagated downstream by connectivity. Perhaps this also has to do with the fact glyphosate is a toxic compound which is supposed to photodegrade in water, so the effects are most acute at the source, whereas with nutrients they may accumulate. We tried as best as possible to avoid photodegradation in our experiment by covering the flask with foil; however, it is still possible that a minimal level of photodegradation did occur, eventually degrading the full efficacy of the herbicide. Also, the effects of an herbicide versus nutrient enrichment are different in that the herbicide suppresses plankton populations whereas nutrients encourage population growth, leading to unstable species-specific abundance fluctuations.

We believe that our study has interesting applications for applied ecology and recommend that further quantification and mapping of aquatic flow rates of nutrients and biocides in nature will improve our understanding of the fate of populations that inhabit interconnected systems linked, for example, by lateral movement processes such as dispersal and gene flow (Stevens et al. 2006; Whittaker et al. 2017; Mestre and Höfer 2021). Future experiments could be scaled at the macroscopic level to include more complex node interaction networks and spatial heterogeneity in flow rates, to better understand how these processes interact to define ecological dynamics in more complex metaecosystems.

Conclusion

We used gradostat metaecosystems to assess the role of node and flow rate on the productivity and diversity of phytoplankton communities exposed to RoundUp, a common herbicide used in agriculture. In our experiment, the spatial position of the node mediated the spatial ecological effects of the herbicide, with stronger effects in the node closest to the source of the herbicide. Our results show that prolonged exposure to RoundUp, reduced productivity and community stability in space and time. Node position and flow rate were important determinants of observed productivity and diversity changes, but much more research is needed to assess a greater range of flow rate values and spatial configurations with this experimental model system. We conclude that aquatic connectivity should be considered in management plans for monitoring and mitigating unintended ecological consequences of agrochemical flows, especially in aquatic habitats situated in-close proximity to point sources of agrochemical runoff.
