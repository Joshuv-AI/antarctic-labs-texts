Antibacterial and Antibiofilm Properties of Kulim Bark (Scorodocarpus borneensis Becc.) Hydrogel on Polymicrobial Biofilms
Authors: Rafika Sari, Sylvia Utami Tunjung Pratiwi, Yosi Bayu Murti, Ema Damayanti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe study aimed to analyze the antibacterial and antibiofilm activity of hydrogels from Kulim (Scorodocarpus borneensis Becc.) stem bark extract and fraction against polymicrobial biofilms.MethodsThe stem bark extract and fraction of Kulim (Scorodocarpus borneensis Becc.) were formulated into a hydrogel (tween 80glycerin). Antibacterial and antibiofilm activities were assessed using a microdilution test against polymicrobial growth and polymicrobial biofilms, while acute toxicity was evaluated using wader pari (Rasbora lateristriata) embryos.ResultsKulim extract hydrogel (4 μg/µL) showed 69.16% antibacterial activity and 61.25% biofilm inhibition, with 72.58% and 82.86% biofilm degradation at 24 and 48 hours, respectively. The fraction-based hydrogel (0.22 μg/µL) had 58.10% antibacterial and biofilm inhibition activity, with 81.14% and 79.64% degradation at 24 and 48 hours. MIC50 and MBIC50 values were 2.42 μg/µL (extract) and 0.15 μg/µL (fraction) as well as 2.83 μg/µL and 0.19 μg/µL, respectively. Based on observation, MBEC50 values were 1.09 μg/µL and 0.57 μg/µL (24 h), as well as 0.70 μg/µL and 0.03 μg/µL (48 h), respectively, while the extract’s LC50 was 0.143 mg/mL.ConclusionThe hydrogel made from Kulim stem bark extract exhibited stronger antibacterial and antibiofilm activities than the ethyl acetate fraction against polymicrobial.Keywords: antibacterial, antibiofilm, hydrogel, Kulim bark, polymicrobial, Rasbora lateristriata

INTRODUCTION

Microbial biofilm is responsible for the development of an infection in approximately 80% of cases [1]. In these structures, bacteria adhere not only to the surface but also to each other through quorum sensing (QS), forming complex microbial consortia [2]. Biofilms typically comprise 10% microbes and 90% water, with polysaccharides making up 50%-90% of the organic matrix. Common biofilm-forming bacteria include Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus, which are often associated with the presence of medical devices and persistent infections.

Polymicrobial species, such as P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and E. coli, frequently coexist in chronic wound infections [3]. In diabetic foot infections, S. aureus commonly coexists with Gram-negative bacteria, namely, P. aeruginosa and E. coli. These bacteria are also prevalent in chronic burn wound infections [4]. Additionally, polymicrobial biofilms are associated with otitis media, oral diseases, sinusitis, urinary tract infections, wound infections, and cystic fibrosis [5].

Treating polymicrobial biofilm infections is a major challenge due to their antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, new antimicrobial candidates are needed to manage biofilm-related infections [6]. For this purpose, plant-based antibiofilm agents are favored due to their effectiveness and fewer side effects [7]. Kulim (Scorodocarpus borneensis Becc.), commonly referred to and native to Borneo, Indonesia, is a promising medicinal plant [8]. Traditionally, Kulim is used both as a spice and a medicine with antiplatelet, anticancer, and antimicrobial properties [9]. The n-hexane extract from this plant contains saponins, alkaloids, flavonoids, and tannins [10]. The ethyl acetate extract of Kulim stem bark can inhibit S. aureus and E. coli [11]. Additionally, the leaf extract can effectively inhibit the biofilm of cariogenic Streptococcus mutans ATCC 25175 [10].

Despite these promising results, incorporating Kulim extract into a hydrogel formulation has not been reported. Hydrogels are three-dimensional polymeric networks with high water retention capacity. They offer several advantages such as ease of topical application, cooling effects due to slow evaporation, minimal disruption of skin functions, convenient removal, and controlled drug release [12, 13].

This study developed a hydrogel using a combination of tween 80 and glycerin as the base. Tween 80, a non-ionic surfactant with an optimal hydrophilic-lipophilic balance, facilitates the dispersion of both semi-polar and non-polar bioactive compounds while featuring non-toxic and non-irritant properties [14]. These characteristics are crucial for integrating the Kulim bark extract effectively into a water-based hydrogel matrix, enabling potential therapeutic applications. Furthermore, the acute toxicity of the hydrogel from the Kulim bark extract is evaluated using wader pari (Rasbora lateristriata) embryos to ensure safety for potential biomedical usage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Kulim stem bark extraction and fractionation

The Kulim (Scorodocarpus borneensis Becc.) stem bark was collected from Tanjung Bunga Village, Sanggau, West Borneo, Indonesia. A voucher specimen (22.19.1/UN1/FFA.2/BF/PT/2022) was stored at the herbarium of the Department of Pharmaceutical Biology, Universitas Gadjah Mada, Yogyakarta, Indonesia. Approximately 10 g of ground Kulim (stem parts) was macerated with 120 mL of methanol (Merck, Germany) at room temperature for 72 hours. Then, the mixture was filtered through a cellulose filter paper with a pore size of 0.45 μm. Methanol was evaporated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator, generating a brownish-red, gummy dry extract. Furthermore, fractionation was performed using Vacuum Liquid Chromatography. Afterward, the fractions were evaporated using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph, Germany) set to 100 rpm, with a cooler at 17℃ (D-lab, China) and a temperature of 50℃, to obtain a dry product [15].

2. Kulim hydrogel formulation and evaluation

The modified base formula extract (4 mg/100 µL) was dissolved in methanol. The fraction (0.22 mg/100 µL) was treated using the same process. Then, methanol was evaporated at 80℃. Tween 80 was added to the mixture at a 3:1 ratio until achieving homogeneity. A glycerin solution, mixed with distilled water, was gradually added while stirring continuously until the mixture became homogeneous and cooled [16]. Afterward, the resulting preparation was subjected to physical characterization, including organoleptic evaluation, dispersion ability, adhesive strength, pH measurement, viscosity, and zeta potential analysis.

The Total Phenolic Content (TPC) of the formulation was determined. Specifically, a mL of the gel was dissolved in 10 mL of water, followed by filtering. The resulting filtrate was combined with 1 mL of the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 1 mL of NaOH. The presence of phenolic compounds was signified by a color change to reddish-purple, blackish-blue, black, or green. TPC quantification was performed using UV-Vis spectrophotometry with gallic acid as the standard. To achieve this, 100 mg of the hydrogel was accurately weighed and dissolved in methanol. A mL of the solution was added to 5 mL of 7.5% Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, mixed, and left to stand for 1 hour. Subsequently, 4 mL of 1% NaOH solution was added, and the absorbance was measured at 730 nm. The TPC was calculated using the linear regression equation of y = ax + b [17].

3. Inhibitory activity against polymicrobial bacterial growth

Bacterial strains S. aureus ATCC 25923, P. aeruginosa ATCC 10145, and E. coli ATCC 25922 were obtained from the Microbiology and Cell Biology Laboratory at Universitas Gadjah Mada. Inhibition testing of polymicrobial growth was conducted by adding 100 µL of a media mixture containing various concentrations of hydrogel extract (0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mg/mL) and fractions (0.03, 0.055, 0.11, and 0.22 mg/mL) to each well of a 96-well microtiter plate. Furthermore, 1 µL of a 24-hour polymicrobial culture with a density of 105 CFU/mL was added to each well. Negative controls consisted of the media and hydrogel base, while the positive control contained 1% chloramphenicol in hydrogel. The 96-well microtiter plates were incubated at 37℃ for 24 hours. Then, optical density (OD) was measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 595 nm [18].

4. Inhibition and degradation of polymicrobial biofilm

A total of 100 µL of mixed media containing varying concentrations of hydrogel extract (0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mg/mL) and fraction (0.03, 0.055, 0.11, and 0.22 mg/mL) was added to each well of a 96-well microtiter plate to assess biofilm formation inhibition. Furthermore, 1 µL of a 24-hour polymicrobial culture with a density of 105 CFU/mL was introduced. Negative controls contained media and hydrogel bases, while the positive control consisted of 1% chloramphenicol in hydrogel. The 96-well microtiter plates were incubated at 37℃ for 24 hours. After the incubation, each plate was washed using sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stained with 125 µL of 0.1% crystal violet solution. The plates were left at room temperature for 15 minutes, washed three times with PBS, and treated using 200 µL of 96% ethanol for 20 minutes at room temperature. OD was measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 595 nm. For the mid-phase biofilm, the plates were incubated at 37℃ for 24 hours before adding the extract and fraction. For the maturation-phase biofilm, incubation lasted 48 hours. Subsequently, the procedures for assessing the inhibition, 24-hour degradation, and 48-hour degradation adhered to an identical protocol [1].

5. Observation of biofilm structure

A total of 500 µL of polymicrobial culture and 500 µL of each hydrogel concentration were treated as described in the biofilm inhibition assay. Following incubation at 37℃ for 24 hours, the coverslip was carefully washed three times with sterile PBS and fixed in stages using 70%, 95%, and 98% alcohol. After 98% alcohol was discarded, the coverslip was left to dry overnight at room temperature. Then, it was adhered to double-sided carbon tape and coated twice with platinum-vanadium using an ion sputter (Bal-Tec SCD 005) for 11 seconds. The sample was prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at a voltage of 10 kV with magnifications ranging from 500 to 2,000 times [19].

6. Acute toxicity to wader pari fish embryos (Rasbora lateristriata)

The toxicity test was conducted using 200 wader pari (Rasbora lateristriata) fish embryos, divided into five treatment groups containing 20 embryos with two repetitions each, according to the OECD guidelines number 236 for fish embryo acute toxicity (FET) test. The embryos were at 7 hpf (hours post fertilization; early development period), corresponding to the 70 '5 epiboly phase. Daily feeding was administered at 3% of the fish’s body weight. In this study, the Kulim bark extract was prepared at concentrations of 1, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.125 mg/mL. Subsequently, a toxicity test of the extract was conducted on the Rasbora lateristriata embryos to obtain the LC50 value as the basis for determining the dose. Exposure to the extract lasted 48 hours, and embryos were observed by comparing between groups at 24, 48, 72, and 96 hpf through direct observation using a microscope with a magnification of 40× and 100×. Finally, the parameters observed were hatching, heartbeat, and survival rates [20].

7. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) normality test. The process followed the Shapiro-Wilk method, and the normality level for the test was p < 0.05. Normality and homogeneity tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 for Windows version 10.4.1 (627) January 2024 Apache license, California, and IBM SPSS 26 for Windows version 20, USA. Probit analysis was performed to determine the MIC50, MBIC50, and MBEC50.

RESULTS

1. Characteristics of a hydrogel containing Kulim extract and fraction

Hydrogels from the methanol extract and ethyl acetate fraction of the Kulim stem bark were successfully formulated using Tween 80 and glycerin. Both formulations exhibited semi-solid consistency with spreadability and pH suitable for topical application, measuring 5.48 for the extract hydrogel and 5.64 for the fraction hydrogel. Particle size and zeta potential analyses revealed that the methanol extract had a smaller average particle size (31.06 nm) compared to the ethyl acetate fraction (38.29 nm), with polydispersity indices of 0.43 and 0.49, respectively, indicating moderate particle distribution homogeneity. Both samples exhibited good stability, as indicated by their negative zeta potential values. The ethyl acetate fraction (–36.38 mV) showed a slightly higher stability than the methanol extract (–34.51 mV). 2. Inhibition of polymicrobial bacterial growth activity

The antibacterial activity of Kulim (Scorodocarpus borneensis) bark hydrogel formulations was evaluated against polymicrobial growth (Fig. 1). Both the extract and fraction-based hydrogels exhibited antibacterial effects. The extract hydrogel demonstrated the highest inhibition at 4 μg/µL, while the fraction hydrogel showed its greatest inhibition at 0.22 μg/µL. Additionally, both hydrogels showed lower percentages of inhibition at 58.10% ± 0.73% compared to the positive control (chloramphenicol). Statistical tests (Bonferroni post hoc) confirmed that all formulas differed significantly from negative controls (p = 0.00). This signified the considerable impact of each hydrogel concentration on polymicrobial antibacterial activity.

3. Inhibition and degradation of polymicrobial biofilm

Fig. 2 illustrates the inhibitory and degradative effects of Kulim bark hydrogel formulations on polymicrobial biofilms at both early and mature stages. The extract-based hydrogel at a 4 μg/µL concentration demonstrated the highest inhibition of biofilm formation at 61.25% ± 5.49% and achieved biofilm degradation of 72.58% ± 2.71% after 24 hours and 82.86% ± 2.04% after 48 hours. Meanwhile, the fraction-based hydrogel at a 0.22 μg/µL concentration showed 58.10% ± 4.48% inhibition, with degradation levels of 81.14% ± 3.02% after 24 hours and 79.64% ± 3.59% after 48 hours. Chloramphenicol exhibited a slightly greater efficacy, demonstrating inhibition and degradation rates of 89.78% ± 3.49% and 90.55% ± 3.53%, respectively.

Fig. 2 also shows that the MBIC50 values, reflecting the minimum concentration required to inhibit 50% of polymicrobial biofilm formation, were 2.83 μg/µL for the extract-based hydrogel and 0.19 μg/µL for the fraction-based hydrogel. For biofilm degradation, the MBEC50 at 24 hours (MBEC24) was 1.09 μg/µL for the extract and 0.57 μg/µL for the fraction. At 48 hours (MBEC48), the values decreased to 0.70 μg/µL and 0.03 μg/µL, respectively. Therefore, both the extract and fraction become more effective over time, while higher concentrations result in greater biofilm inhibition and degradation.

Statistical analysis using the Kruskal-Wallis post hoc test confirmed significant differences among the treatment groups for biofilm inhibition and 48-hour degradation (p < 0.05). The most significant differences from the positive control in the inhibition test were observed in the 0.5 μg/100 µL extract and 0.027 μg/100 µL fraction. For 48-hour degradation, the most distinct results were found in the 0.5 μg/100 µL extract and 1 μg/100 µL fraction formulations.

5. Acute toxicity of the Kulim stem bark extract against Rasbora lateristriata embryos

1) Lethal concentration

The toxicity profile of the Kulim stem bark extract was evaluated using probit analysis to determine its lethal concentration. The analysis demonstrated a clear dose-dependent response, with the increased concentrations of the extract leading to higher mortality in the tested population.

Fig. 4 shows that the extract exhibited an LC50 (lethal concentration for 50% of the population) of 0.143 mg/mL, indicating moderate toxicity. At the lower end of the concentration range, 0.084 mg/mL (LC₁₀) was estimated to cause 10% mortality, while intermediate concentrations (0.202 mg/mL; LC₈₀) were associated with 80% mortality. The highest recorded concentration of 0.375 mg/mL (LC₉₉) was predicted to result in nearly complete lethality (99%).

Probit analysis of the Kulim stem bark extract. The concentration gradient steadily increases from LC10 (probit 0.1) to LC90 (probit 0.9). The LC50 value is determined at a concentration of 0.143 mg/mL.

2) Morphological abnormality

Fig. 5 shows the presence of abnormalities such as an impaired swim bladder, blood clots, and spinal abnormalities in the treatment group with the Kulim stem bark extract at a concentration of 0.125 mg/mL. In contrast, abnormalities at a concentration of 0.0625 mg/mL included only impaired swim bladder and spinal abnormality. All embryos in the treatment groups with bark extract concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL did not survive beyond 96 hpf.

3) Heartbeat rate

Based on the normality test, the data obtained did not follow a normal distribution; hence, a non-parametric test was used to compare the means between the groups. Dunn’s multiple comparison test was applied, revealing a significant difference in the heartbeat rate of embryos between control and treatment groups exposed to the Kulim stem bark extract. The administration of the Kulim stem bark extract affected the heartbeat rate of R. lateristriata embryos (Fig. 6). Specifically, the heartbeat rate of 96 hpf embryos decreased following the treatment, with higher extract concentrations leading to a progressively lower heartbeat rate. Embryos exposed to the Kulim stem bark extract at concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL did not survive until 96 hpf.

4) Hatching rate

The hatching rate of R. lateristriata embryos decreased following Kulim stem bark extract treatment (Fig. 7). R. lateristriata embryos typically hatched at 24 hpf. The administration of 0.0625, 0.125, and 0.25 mg/mL bark extract did not significantly influence hatching. Naturally, embryos can hatch within 24 hours under normal conditions. Therefore, adding the extract did not affect the embryo’s hatching ability. Furthermore, the added extract was non-toxic, as demonstrated by a hatching rate exceeding 90%, with no abnormalities observed in embryos after hatching into larvae.

Hatching rate graph of Rasbora lateristriata embryos treated with the bark extract over the observation period.

5) Survival rate

The survival rate of R. lateristriata embryos decreased with increasing concentrations of the Kulim stem bark extract (Fig. 8). A clear dose-dependent trend was observed, where higher extract concentrations corresponded to lower embryo survival rates compared to the control group. At concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mg/mL, none of the embryos survived beyond 96 hpf. Although the survival rate of the control group appeared slightly lower than in some treatment groups at earlier time points, the overall results remained valid according to the OECD Test Guideline 236, as mortality in the control group did not exceed the 10% threshold.

DISCUSSION

Hydrogels formulated from the Kulim (Scorodocarpus borneensis) stem bark extract and its ethyl acetate fraction were successfully developed, exhibiting suitable organoleptic properties and spreadability according to the Indonesian National Standard (SNI). Both formulations demonstrated adequate adhesive strength (≥ 4 seconds), appropriate pH (5.48 and 5.64) within the skin’s physiological range (4.5-6.5), and viscosity that met the SNI requirement (3,000-50,000 cps). Thus, the hydrogels are suitable for topical application.

The organoleptic properties of both hydrogels showed similar characteristics, meeting the dispersion criteria in SNI no. 06-2588 (5-7 cm). The adhesive strength of the hydrogel was evaluated to determine its capacity to maintain sufficient contact with the skin for effective drug delivery. An acceptable adhesion time was defined as a minimum of 4 seconds [21]. This showed that both formulas had good adhesion, as their values met the requirements. The suitability of hydrogel pH relative to the physiological pH of the skin was assessed, which should range from 4.5 to 6.5 to prevent irritation [22]. The pH of the hydrogel extract and fraction was 5.48 and 5.64, respectively. A previous study reported that a natural polyphenol hydrogel showed effective results in inhibiting bacteria in antibacterial experiments concerning pH and temperature [23]. These SEM findings confirm that both the extract and fraction hydrogels derived from the Kulim stem bark exhibited antibiofilm activity, with inhibitory effects comparable to the control. Viscosity refers to a measure of the resistance of a liquid to flow and influences its spreadability and interaction with the skin surface. Gel viscosity should be between 3,000 and 50,000 cps [24]. The PI shows the stability and uniformity of the particle size in the extract and fractions, with an average range of 20 and 500 nm. The achieved size of 30-40 nm meets the specified requirements. According to previous research [25], smaller particle size enhances antibiofilm effects since reduced size contributes to a stronger interaction with biofilms. Zeta potential analysis showed a value smaller than –30 mV, displaying good stability. Studies showed that higher values contribute to enhanced antibacterial growth. Based on previous research [26], hydrogels promote wound healing biofilms of methicillin-resistant S. aureus or carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa.

The TPC test demonstrated that hydrogels formulated from both the extract and its ethyl acetate fraction of the Kulim stem bark contained phenolic compounds, as signified by the appearance of blue and greenish colors. The hydrogel derived from the crude extract had a higher TPC compared to that derived from the fraction. This variation could be explained by the differences in the secondary metabolite composition between the bulk extract and the fraction. Additionally, the solvent used played a crucial role because methanol, being highly polar, more effectively solubilized phenolic compounds, resulting in higher concentrations [27]. Hence, phenolic and organosulfur compounds altered bacterial cell wall rigidity, leading to compromised structural integrity [28]. Phenolic structures with OH groups enhance the inhibition of biofilm formation, as well as the disruption of preformed biofilms [29].

Both hydrogels derived from the Kulim stem bark extract and ethyl acetate fraction showed antibacterial activity against polymicrobial biofilms. This observed activity is possibly associated with the presence of bioactive secondary metabolites with known antimicrobial properties. Previous research showed that the ethyl acetate fraction of the Kulim stem bark contains alkaloid, phenolic, and triterpenoid compounds [30]. These compounds effectively inhibit polymicrobial biofilm formation; for example, alkaloids interfere with peptidoglycan in bacterial cells. Furthermore, triterpenoids contribute to damaging membranes due to their lipophilic nature, whereas polyphenols can harm protein structures through bonding. Tannins and flavonoids can stop biofilm growth because of their ability to inhibit certain genes, such as icaA and icaD. Alkaloids also affect the formation of the peptidoglycan in bacteria, preventing its complete formation and causing cell death. The breakdown of polymicrobial biofilms occurred after 48 hours, particularly during the phase when the biofilm layer thickens. In this 48-hour phase, reducing growth is more difficult because biofilm has already formed a structured, three-dimensional matrix that is harder to destroy [31].

This study showed that hydrogel extracts had stronger antibacterial and antibiofilm activity than the fraction, except for the first 24 hours. Inhibition was evaluated using SEM, providing insight into the structural changes in polymicrobial biofilms upon treatment. Biofilms are complex, multicellular communities encased in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, which contributes to the resistance against antimicrobial agents by impeding their diffusion and activity. SEM visualization showed significant morphological alterations, including cell shrinkage, lysis, and disruption of the EPS matrix, accompanied by a visibly reduced cell density. In contrast, the untreated control presented a well-structured, dense polymicrobial biofilm with a thick EPS layer. Chloramphenicol treatment also led to the significant degradation of the biofilm structure.

Previous literature [32] suggested that polymicrobial biofilms exhibit enhanced structural integrity due to synergistic interactions among different bacterial species, facilitated by QS and two-component regulatory systems, particularly during EPS production. The observed damage in the biofilm structure can be attributed to the increased membrane permeability and degradation of mature biofilms, leading to cell death and EPS disruption. The results of antibacterial and antibiofilm activity tests demonstrated that topical preparations of the hydrogel from the Kulim bark extract were more effective than the ethyl acetate fraction. Therefore, the acute toxicity test was conducted using R. lateristriata embryos to assess the safety of these hydrogel preparations for topical application. Its wide distribution throughout Southeast Asia and the ability to produce 1,000 eggs [33] make this fish a good model animal in research. Based on the phylogenetic relationship between zebrafish, R. laetistriata, the Rasborini tribe, has similar characteristics; thus, it can be used as a model animal, as well as an alternative in toxicity testing. Additionally, it is characterized by genetically faster embryonic development and a higher fecundity rate than zebrafish [34, 35]. Research using R. lateristriata embryos has 100 times more sensitivity than that using other conventional animal models, making it an alternative in biomedical, genetic, and pharmaceutical research [36]. Although the toxicity value is within the toxic range, the results showed that the hydrogel did not pose a risk when applied to the skin. The concentration obtained from the LC50 value could be used as a benchmark of the upper limit of the extract concentration for testing, as well as to determine the dose to be used over a longer duration [37].

Observable abnormalities included swim bladder and spinal abnormalities, which can also be caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in the body, thus affecting swimming power, a factor in endurance [38]. The abnormalities found did not represent the main cause of larval mortality, which states that there is a difference in sensitivity to larvae when compared to embryos and adult fish [39]. This difference was seen in the larvae’s swimming behavior, which was inhibited by toxic components after exposure for more than 2 days. The normal heart rate of fish larvae ranges from 150 to 180 beats per minute (bpm), increasing to 165 bpm when they reach 48 hpf [40]. Therefore, adding the extract did not affect the heart rate, as it remained normal. The heartbeat rate of 96 hpf in R. lateristriata embryos after the extract treatment also decreased, possibly due to ROS accumulation as a trigger for oxidative stress, resulting in damage and death [41]. The higher the concentration given, the lower the embryo’s heartbeat rate. The solvent control group showed no significant difference compared to the control group, indicating that the solvent used did not affect the heartbeat rate of R. lateristriata embryos.

Hatching is influenced by the presence of choriolysin and the movement of the embryo within the egg [42]. Additionally, the blockage of the chorionic pores can disrupt the transport between the embryo and the environment, affecting the hatching ability [43]. However, treatment with higher concentrations of 0.5 and 1 mg/mL led to a marked delay in hatching time. The results also showed that larvae began to experience significant mortality at 72 hours, as the concentration of the added extract increased. Considering the wader’s hatching time, which is 24 hours after fertilization, the time required after exposure is approximately 48 hours (24-72 hours). Thus, the embryo is protected by the chorion before hatching. Furthermore, the extract cannot penetrate the chorion membrane, as the hatching rate was still high, and no abnormalities were found upon hatching. The larval mortality that occurred was likely due to the crude extract used, which is suspected to contain various active compounds that can kill larvae. Susceptibility to larval death may also stem from the rapid development of larvae in the range of 48-72 hpf, making them highly susceptible to toxicants [39]. This effect can be minimized in the future by using similar fractions and isolates. There are components other than curcuminoids in the crude extract of Curcuma zedoaria Rosc. that can cause toxicity to embryos and larvae [44]. Exposure to high concentrations of extracts significantly affected egg hatchability, an indicator of normal fish development and the presence of external factors. Some substances are suspected of altering the chorion structure, causing its shrinkage, damaging the protective layer, and causing egg death [45]. Overall, the ethyl acetate fractions tend to have a slightly different profile of phenolic compounds, similar to terpenoids, as a major compound in plants combating bacterial infections. We can examine which compounds are present in the fractions and determine which ones play a role as antibacterial and antibiofilm agents in future studies. Therefore, further investigation is required to evaluate the transdermal migration of the Kulim stem bark hydrogel, its potential for skin irritation, and its overall safety and efficacy as an antibiofilm agent.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, hydrogels made from the Kulim stem bark extract and its fraction showed significant antibacterial and antibiofilm activities against polymicrobial biofilms. Furthermore, the extract-based hydrogel demonstrated toxicity, suggesting its potential for further development as an antimicrobial agent with topical application and appropriate safety.
