Increasing the ecological footprint significantly reduces life expectancy and increases infant mortality rates in Ethiopia
Authors: Melaku Adinew Ayitehgiza, Abebaw Shimeles, Ketema Bekele, Million Sileshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to determine the effects of environmental degradation, proxied by the ecological footprint, on human health outcomes, particularly life expectancy and infant mortality, in Ethiopia. Using annual data for the period between 1980 and 2023, the study utilized long-run cointegration techniques: fully modified ordinary least squares (FMOLS), dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS), canonical cointegration regression (CCR), and Toda‒Yamamoto Granger causality. The results show a statistical association where a 1% increase in the ecological footprint is associated with a 0.162% increase in the infant mortality rate, and a 0.0089% reduction in life expectancy, suggesting a potential correlation between environmental pressure and health outcomes. Conversely, economic growth (0.43% increase in life expectancy per 1% increase in GDP) and public health expenditures (0.799% increase in life expectancy per 1% increase in spending) are positive long-term determinants. Bidirectional causality exists between health outcomes, economic growth, and environmental degradation. This study suggests that, pending further verification of causal mechanisms, policymakers may consider integrated strategies that simultaneously address environmental sustainability and public health investments.

Introduction

Environmental degradation, which is driven primarily by human activities, is the fundamental cause of global warming and subsequent climate change phenomena and is now considered one of the most serious threats to human health1. Human-induced activities, including deforestation, intensive pesticide usage, and rapid industrialization, not only compromise biological ecosystems but also diminish the Earth’s ability to sustain social and environmental demands2. Furthermore, the persistent threat posed by these environmental stressors, particularly climate change, significantly impacts human well-being3. Consequently, the pursuit of uninterrupted economic expansion remains a fundamental problem for environmental sustainability4. This tension is particularly evident in developing countries where accelerated economic growth often necessitates the rapid depletion of natural resources.

Climate change is a direct consequence of human activity5,6. This degradation stems from a convergence of factors, including deforestation7, rapid urbanization8, unsustainable consumption patterns, emissions from transportation, burning from fossil fuels and biomass3, water pollution9, and air pollution10. Importantly, the health sector plays a dual role, while vulnerable to these changes, it also contributes approximately 4.4% of greenhouse gas emissions11,12. Furthermore, the escalation of extreme weather and rising temperatures profoundly negatively influences economic activity13. These environmental shifts jeopardize investment, compromise public health, and disrupt the fundamental functioning of global ecosystems14,15, rendering climate change a preeminent threat to worldwide biodiversity16.

Ethiopia has a substantial population, diverse topography, and an economy that relies on agriculture, which is heavily reliant on rainfall17, and the nation is uniquely vulnerable to environmental deterioration18. The country faces critical ecological challenges, including rapid population growth, deforestation, and land degradation19. These persistent environmental problems exacerbate various health hazards, specifically increasing the prevalence of infectious diseases and the proliferation of respiratory ailments20. Furthermore, exposure to elevated concentrations of both indoor and outdoor air pollution has been identified as a primary driver of contemporary public health crises in the region21.

Despite these challenges, Ethiopia has demonstrated better outcomes than many African nations do, particularly with respect to life expectancy and infant mortality. Between 2000 and 2023, the nation’s life expectancy increased by a remarkable 17 years, rising from 50.8 to 67.3 years, thereby outpacing the continental average increase of 10.6 years. Concurrently, the infant mortality rate (IMR) declined significantly, from 92.2 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2000 to 35.7 in 2023. Despite these substantial gains, Ethiopia’s health indicators remain below global benchmarks; its 2023 life expectancy of 67.3 years trails the global average of 72.3 years. Furthermore, while Ethiopia’s IMR is lower than the sub-Saharan African average of 43.13, it remains higher than the global average of approximately 27 deaths per 1,000 live births22.. These health shifts are analyzed within this study to better understand the correlation between environmental pressures and public health outcomes.

Given Ethiopia’s status as a developing country, understanding how environmental degradation affects human health is important for the formulation of robust public health programs. While this relationship has been extensively documented in the international literature23–26, longitudinal and empirical evidence within the Ethiopian context remains limited. Existing studies in Ethiopia primarily utilize qualitative or cross-sectional designs focused on isolated health outcomes. For example, Teku and Eshetu27 provided a detailed assessment of the impacts of climate change on national health and livelihoods. Their analysis was descriptive rather than quantitative. Similarly, contemporary studies have explored various determinants of child mortality28 and the specific repercussions of pollution29. While previous studies have explored various determinants of health in Ethiopia, there remains a need for quantitative, longitudinal analyses using comprehensive environmental metrics like the ecological footprint. In essence, the study aims to address how environmental degradation (proxy by the ecological footprint) quantitatively affects human health outcomes (specifically life expectancy and infant mortality) in Ethiopia using longitudinal data.

This study provides three contributions to the literature. First, it addresses the empirical gap in Ethiopia by providing a rigorous, 44-year quantitative analysis of health and environmental data. Second, this work introduces methodological novelty over the prevalent reliance on environmental degradation measures such as air pollution. Environmental degradation is measured by the ecological footprint because it provides a holistic view of human impacts beyond what a single pollutant, such as CO2 or PM2.5, can depict. Indeed, such an approach lacks the multifaceted nature of environmental degradation, which is typified by land use changes, water pollution, and biodiversity loss, among other factors30. In this context, the present study explores the ecological footprint as a more holistic indicator of human pressure on the environment. The ecological footprint measures the area of biologically productive space that is needed to maintain a population, including the waste assimilation capacity31,32. Third, this study moves beyond simple correlation by applying advanced long-term cointegration techniques (such as fully modified ordinary least squares (FMOLS) and dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS) and canonical cointegrating regression (CCR)) within the Grossman health production framework, providing rigorous empirical evidence that environmental sustainability is not merely an ecological goal but a fundamental requirement for improving public health in low-income countries. Furthermore, the application of three distinct cointegration estimators (FMOLS, DOLS, and CCR) ensures the robustness of the empirical evidence, providing a reliable foundation for policymakers to develop integrated health and environmental sustainability strategies.

Literature review

The relationship between environmental degradation and human health is frequently analyzed through the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC), a theoretical framework postulating an inverse U-shaped relationship where economic expansion initially triggers resource depletion and industrial pollution33,34. This model suggests that while early development stages prioritize industrial output at the expense of the ecosystem, subsequent growth eventually facilitates a transition toward cleaner technologies, service-oriented industries, and more stringent regulatory environments. Consequently, as environmental quality begins to recover after this economic turning point, human health outcomes, which often decline during initial industrialization, are expected to improve in a parallel trajectory known as the Health Kuznets curve35.

The theoretical framework of child mortality developed by Mosley and Chen36stresses a more comprehensive approach that takes into account the biological, proximal, and socioeconomic elements that influence children’s health. According to the theory, disease, infection, and malnutrition are examples of proximate determinants that have a direct impact on mortality, whereas socioeconomic determinants do not directly explain mortality. Additionally, proximate determinants reflect environmental conditions such as pollution, poor sanitation, and unhealthy dietary intake that directly worsen health and cause death37.These effects can manifest as respiratory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, cancers, infectious diseases, and developmental disorders. The theory emphasizes that a healthy environment is a prerequisite for public health; in contrast, environmental degradation poses a serious threat to human well-being.

The effects of environmental change and human disturbance have caused ecosystems to undergo degradation processes similar to those associated with human health problems38. Pollutants that harm human populations and market-based and consumption-oriented economies39. More precisely, on a variety of spatiotemporal scales, resource-exploitative land uses, including mining, transportation, built-up land expansion, and industrialized or intensive agriculture, can worsen environmental health39,40. However, many industrial processes produce materials and substances that are harmful to human health and are not found in the natural environment, such as plastics and nanomaterials41,42.

Environmental degradation, including air pollution and water pollution, is known to play an important role in labor productivity, economic growth, and individual health43. It is well established that environmental pollution plays a dangerous role in various health outcomes in humans, such as mental health, respiratory diseases, chronic diseases, mortality, sleep quality, and self-esteem44. This results in environmental health inequities where some populations bear a heavy burden of environmental diseases. Therefore, solving environmental quality problems is not only a public health issue but also a social justice issue. Investing in environmental protection and implementing effective environmental regulations are important strategies for promoting public health and achieving sustainable development goals.

The relationship between health and economic development is bidirectional45. While better health leads to economic growth, economic growth facilitates improvements in health by increasing health care spending and improving infrastructure46. Although the reform of Ethiopia’s health sector, the expansion of primary health services, and health insurance programs have contributed to better health outcomes, the gap between urban and rural areas is still a challenge47. Addressing these disparities is critical to achieving equitable economic growth and increasing the country’s demographic dividend.

Ansarinasab and Saghaian48 utilized ridge regression analysis on a dataset encompassing 30 European countries to examine the impact of greenhouse gas emissions, including CO2, CH4, and N2O, on the prevalence and severity of the COVID-19 pandemic and reported that higher concentrations of these environmental pollutants significantly increased active coronavirus cases and mortality rates. Similarly, Zhang et al49. conducted an empirical investigation using annual data from low-income countries (1996–2020) and a comprehensive literature review, concluding that poor environmental quality and ineffective governance quality are the primary drivers of deteriorating health status; their results suggests a significant link between environmental degradation and the prevalence of respiratory and cardiovascular issues, although the complexity of these pathways requires further granular study.

Studies examining the intersection of environmental quality and human health reveal that both localized pollution and global environmental stressors significantly influence health outcomes. In Nigeria, Okogor50 utilized ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation techniques to analyze secondary time series data and reported that CO2 emissions significantly reduce life expectancy, whereas improved access to water and sanitation facilities acts as a vital countermeasure to enhance health status. Conversely, Sundas et al.51 conducted a scoping review using the PRISMA-ScR framework to synthesize a diverse range of qualitative and quantitative data from the global literature, concluding that the interplay between air pollution, climate change, and meteorological conditions creates a multifaceted burden on respiratory, cardiovascular, and metabolic health.

Recent studies on sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) emphasize that while economic growth and health expenditures are vital for improving population health, their effectiveness is often nonlinear and contingent on broader socioeconomic factors. Adjotor and Nketiah-Amponsah52 utilized panel data from 1996 to 2021 and an instrumental variable two-stage least squares (IV-2SLS) estimator to identify specific economic thresholds, such as a GDP per capita of $1,033.61 for HIV and $1,501.81 for malaria, beyond which the positive impact of development begins to diminish. Complementing these empirical findings, a systematic literature review by Yeboah et al53. synthesized 18 studies published between 2000 and 2022, revealing that while 11 studies confirmed that economic growth significantly reduces maternal and child mortality, others reported inconsistent results.

Studies on life expectancy (LEX) identify diverse drivers that vary on the basis of a nation’s development stage and specific socioeconomic context. One study focused on China from 2000 to 2023 utilized autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL), fully modified ordinary least squares (FMOLS), and dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS) estimators to analyze time series data and reported that health expenditure, education, and birth rates positively influence LEX, whereas inflation and mortality rates have negative impacts54. Comparatively, a broader cross-country analysis of 20 nations from 2004 to 2016 employed Pearson correlation and multiple regression models to examine nine economic and environmental indicators55. This comparative study revealed that while urbanization and GDP per capita generally promote longevity, factors such as healthcare expenditure and education spending can have divergent effects depending on whether a country is developed or developing; specifically, education spending was found to negatively impact LEX in developed countries but positively impact it in developing countries.

Despite the existing body of literature exploring the relationship between environmental quality and health, there appears to be a gap in research specifically focusing on Ethiopia and the nuanced impact of environmental degradation on health outcomes, especially within the framework of achieving sustainable development goals related to health, the environment, and economic well-being. The results obtained via different methodologies and in different geographical contexts suggest a need for a focused investigation within the East African setting to understand the specific pathways and magnitudes of these impacts, potentially utilizing more comprehensive measures of environmental degradation and health that go beyond single indicators and consider the interconnectedness within a region highly vulnerable to climate change and environmental shifts.

Methodology

Model specification

The conceptual framework of this study is grounded in the health production function framework developed by Grossman56, which posits that health outcomes are determined by a combination of specific inputs. This study categorizes these inputs into three distinct factors—environmental, social, and economic—to analyze their collective impact on human capital. The environmental factors are primarily represented by the ecological footprint, a comprehensive metric that captures human pressure on ecosystems, including land use changes and pollution, which directly influences health through environmental stressors. The economic factors include gross domestic product and public health expenditure, which represent the financial resources available for healthcare infrastructure and improved living standards. Finally, social factors are proxied by immunization levels (DPTs), reflecting the effectiveness of preventive medical interventions. Health outcomes are the primary indicators and include life expectancy and the infant mortality rate. By integrating these variables into a long-term cointegration model, the framework maps how environmental degradation competes with economic and medical advancements to shape the general health status of the Ethiopian population. According to Grossman56, medical facilities, preexisting health conditions, and personal habits are important inputs. The combination of these inputs that produces health outcomes is effectively modeled by the health production function.

This study used long-term cointegration techniques to examine the interrelationships among the variables studied. Fully modified ordinary least squares (FMOLS) and dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS) estimation techniques were applied. For time series analysis, Phillips and Perron64 introduced a regression method known as FMOLS. Its objective is to estimate parameters in cointegrated systems effectively. Hamit-Haggar65 stated that it is particularly well known for its reliability in small sample sizes and its ability to manage serial correlation and indigeneity. The DOLS estimator developed by Stock and Watson66 was shown by Kao and Chiang67 to outperform FMOLS by considering regressor correlations. The long-term integration results are confirmed by both FMOLS and DOLS analyses68,69. To confirm the robustness of these findings, the study employed the canonical cointegrating regression (CCR) estimation approach70. In the single equation method, the canonical cointegrating regression (CCR) methodology is essential for predicting long-term coefficients. In a cointegrating regression, the statistical issue that arises is meant to be fixed. It is a powerful alternative to other versions of the OLS technique, such as fully modified OLS (FMOLS) and dynamic OLS (DOLS), both of which aim to generate a trustworthy inference in cointegrated systems.

Data presentation

For this study, annual time series data from 1980 to 2023 were used. The selection of variables in this study is grounded in the multidimensional relationships among economic growth, environmental quality, and public health. Life expectancy (LE) and infant mortality (IMR) are utilized as the dependent health outcomes, as they represent the most sensitive indicators of a nation’s overall well-being and the efficiency of its healthcare system. The ecological footprint (EF) is a proxy of environmental degradation. In existing studies, measuring environmental quality is often based on narrow proxies such as CO2 emissions59–61,71. The measurements of environmental quality via the CO2 approach do not adequately capture the complex nature of environmental degradation, which includes land use changes, water pollution, and biodiversity loss, among other factors30. To address this limitation, this study employs the EF as a more comprehensive indicator of human pressure on the environment. The EF quantifies the biologically productive area required to sustain a population, including the capacity to absorb waste31,32. To isolate the environmental impact, we control for GDP per capita, which captures the wealth–health effect where higher income levels facilitate better nutrition and infrastructure. Furthermore, public health expenditure (PHE) and DPT immunization are included to represent the supply side of health outcomes; PHE measures the government’s fiscal commitment to health, whereas DPT coverage serves as a critical proxy for the accessibility and functional reach of the primary healthcare infrastructure in a developing context such as Ethiopia. The data were collected from the World Bank development indicators (WDI)22, and the environmental degradation data were measured as the ecological footprint collected from the Global Footprint Network (GFN)72. All variables were transformed into their natural logarithms (ln) to reduce potential heteroscedasticity and to allow the estimated parameters to be interpreted as elasticities. While variables such as lnPHE and lnDPT are expressed in percentages, their log-transformation is a standard procedure in log-log specifications to maintain functional consistency across the model (Table 1).

Results

Descriptive statistics of the variables

The descriptive statistics are provided in Table 2 for the variables life expectancy (lnLE), infant mortality rate (lnIMR), ecological footprint (lnEF), economic growth (lnGDP), public health expenditure (lnPHE), and immunization rate lnDPT. It provides the mean, median, maximum, minimum, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, Jarque–Bera statistic, and probability of each variable. The distribution shape, normality, dispersion, and central tendency of each variable are described by these statistics, which provide an initial understanding of how the variables behave in the dataset.

The results indicate that the distributions of lnLE, lnIMR, lnGDP, lnPHE, and lnDPT were symmetrical because of their relatively low standard deviations and relatively similar means and medians. In other words, the data measure the variables that fall within a small range of the mean. However, lnEF stands out from the others because it has a very high standard deviation, a very high mean and median difference, and a maximum value at an exceptionally high point. Since these extreme values indicate outliers or deviations from the rest of the variables that fit within the typical symmetrical distribution patterns, such features may be suggestive of a highly skewed distribution for lnEF. Further analysis of skewness and kurtosis reveals that lnEF and lnGDP have positive skewed kurtosis, indicating that their tails are longer on the right side of the distribution. Conversely, lnDPT has the highest kurtosis and the greatest negative skewness, suggesting that the distribution may have a longer tail on the left and a heavier tail overall. The Jarque-Bera test results in Table 2 confirm that the residuals are normally distributed, supporting the reliability of the FMOLS and DOLS estimates. The results of the JB test indicate that the null hypothesis of normality could not be rejected at the 5% level for most variables after logarithmic transformation if they are nonnormal but rather addressed via the robustness of FMOLS. Owing to their low probability values, lnEF, lnGDP, and lnDPT appear to be further from normalcy according to the Jarque–Bera test, which gauges how normal the distributions are. In conclusion, the other variables seem to be regularly distributed, but lnEF and lnDPT have unique distributional characteristics.

Correlation matrix of the variables

The correlation matrix shows that health outcomes are closely linked to economic growth and environmental factors, as presented in Table 3. Life expectancy (lnLE) is negatively correlated with the ecological footprint (lnEF) and positively correlated with economic growth (lnGDP), whereas the infant mortality rate (lnIMR) is similar to that of vaccinations (lnDPT) and lnGDP but increases with increasing environmental factors. Healthcare expenditures (lnPHEs) and vaccinations (lnDPTs) make crucial contributions to reducing longevity and mortality. Ultimately, economic and medical interventions such as GDP and vaccines are very strong predictors of improved life expectancy and reduced mortality, whereas environmental stresses act as a constant countervailing force that negatively offsets these benefits.

Unit root test

Unit root and cointegration tests are typically used to assess the reliability of estimation findings and the selection of a regression technique. An application of the unit root test is used to confirm that the data are stationary. First difference I(1) or level I(0) data must be stationary. Since every test contains errors, two unit root tests were conducted to produce more precise and acceptable results. The augmented Dickey‒Fuller (ADF)73 and Phillips‒Perron (PP)64 unit root tests were used to confirm the stationary nature of the variables. The ADF and PP unit root tests, however, may yield erroneous results if there is a structural break in the data because they fail to account for the possibility of structural breaks. To account for one structural break, this study uses the Zivot and Andrews74 unit root test. The Zivot–Andrews unit root test aims to determine if a time series (Xt) is stationary after accounting for a single structural break, contrasting the null hypothesis of a unit root without a break against trend-stationarity with a break in either the level (intercept), the trend, or both. The test iteratively evaluates all potential break dates (TBs) within the sample, identifying the date that yields the smallest t statistic for the coefficient (β) of the lagged level variable; this TB is the estimated break date. The significance of the coefficients δ and θ reveals whether the break is significant at the level or trend level, respectively. In contrast, the primary focus on β determines if the series becomes stationary after considering the identified break.

Cointegration test

The data must now be stationary to proceed to the next stage of cointegration. The cointegration test is used to investigate the long-term relationships among several variables. The Johansen cointegration test, which was first created by Johansen75 and Johansen and Juselius76, was used in this work. The Johansen cointegration test yields dependable results and is the most consistent. Shahbaz77 added that the Johansen test is better suited for short datasets. This approach is therefore more suitable for the results of this investigation. The two statistics that are produced by the Johansen cointegration test are trace statistics and eigenvalues.

The null hypothesis for the Johansen cointegration test is that Ho: the variables do not cointegrate. In contrast, the alternative hypothesis is that Ha: the variables are cointegrated. The Johansen cointegration test results are summarized in Table 5. The findings indicate that at least two cointegration connections reject the null hypothesis that there is no cointegration. Thus, it can be concluded from the results that there is a long-term association between the variables used in this study.

Discussion

The study revealed that environmental degradation is positively related to infant mortality and negatively related to life expectancy, a finding that directly aligns with the early-stage dynamics of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC). This relationship implies that unsustainable depletion activities, such as deforestation and increased greenhouse gas emissions, worsen pollution and climate change, creating an environmental poverty trap78. In developing regions such as Ethiopia, the scale of economic activity initially increases the ecological footprint at the expense of public health, as the most vulnerable segments of the population lack the infrastructure to mitigate environmental hazards. These findings offer support for the ‘Health-EKC’ hypothesis within the Ethiopian context, suggesting that environmental pressures may influence health outcomes differently across stages of economic development79. Consequently, higher levels of environmental degradation systematically decrease life expectancy and increase infant mortality, reinforcing the need for policy interventions that move the nation toward the “turning point” of the EKC through green technology and improved healthcare spending80.

Economic growth acts as a fundamental way to improve public health outcomes, primarily by facilitating higher living standards and stronger health care systems. In the context of developing nations, recent evidence has demonstrated that a rising GDP is negatively correlated with neonatal and infant mortality while being a primary driver of increased life expectancy81. This advancement is justified by the “income effect,” where increased national wealth expands the fiscal space for public revenue, enabling critical investments in healthcare infrastructure, expanded food supplies, and subsidized medical services82. These results align with the findings of Miladinov83 and Wang et al.79, who highlighted that in low- and middle-income countries, economic prosperity triggers a demand for high-quality care and better sanitation, which are essential for bypassing the “poverty-illness trap.” Furthermore, the role of institutional frameworks remains a crucial determinant; the effectiveness of environmental regulations and fiscal decentralization ensures that economic gains are equitably translated into tangible health improvements rather than being offset by the negative externalities of industrialization79.

The results of this study provide significant statistical evidence of the positive influence of public health spending on health outcomes in Ethiopia, reinforcing the premise that targeted fiscal allocation is a necessary precondition for improving longevity and reducing systemic health risks. Increased health expenditure enhances longevity by expanding access to essential medical services, financing preventive care programs, and scaling healthcare infrastructure, most notably through Ethiopia’s Health Extension Programme and Community-Based Health Insurance schemes84. These investments are particularly vital for maternal and child health, as they fund immunizations, nutrition programs, and prenatal care, which directly lower infant mortality rates. This aligns with Gillani et al.85, who emphasize that the structural quality of the healthcare system determines the utilization of such services. Furthermore, these findings support those of Anwar et al.86 and Sultana et al.87, who argue that public spending not only improves health performance but also elevates general living standards. In the context of the health-induced environmental Kuznets curve (EKC), recent evidence from Ambel et al.88 suggests that equitable public spending acts as a stabilizer, ensuring that the health benefits of economic growth are not offset by the negative externalities of environmental degradation. Thus, sustained government investment in healthcare remains the most critical instrument for shielding vulnerable populations from the “poverty trap” and fostering a resilient, healthy society.

The level of immunization is positively correlated with life expectancy and negatively correlated with infant mortality, as increased vaccine coverage directly translates into longer life expectancies and a significant reduction in pediatric deaths. These findings corroborate the studies of Alanazi et al89. and Akinlo and Sulola90, who demonstrated that expanding vaccine programs for preventable diseases such as pneumonia, polio, and measles is essential for protecting vulnerable populations, particularly in developing nations where healthcare access may be limited. In addition to individual protection, vaccines provide a systemic benefit by reducing the volume of acutely ill patients, thereby alleviating the burden on clinics and hospitals. This public health success is increasingly reinforced by modern infrastructure; for instance, Shattock et al.91 recently estimated that global immunization efforts have saved approximately 154 million lives over the last 50 years, with the majority being infants. Furthermore, as the healthcare landscape evolves, the synthesis of medical science and digital literacy becomes paramount. Ngo and Hastie92 argue that literacy in emerging technologies, such as artificial intelligence, is now a vital component of professional adaptation. In a public health context, such literacy enables the deployment of AI-driven predictive analytics to optimize vaccine distribution and combat misinformation, ultimately securing long-term gains in human well-being.

Conclusion

This study utilized annual time-series data from 1980 to 2023 to investigate the long-term relationship between environmental deterioration and public health outcomes in Ethiopia. By employing robust econometric techniques, including FMOLS, DOLS, and CCR, the analysis provides empirical evidence that environmental stressors, represented by the ecological footprint, are significantly associated with diminished life expectancy and elevated infant mortality rates. The results show a statistical association where a 1% increase in the ecological footprint is associated with a 0.162% increase in the infant mortality rate, and a 0.0089% reduction in life expectancy, suggesting a potential correlation between environmental pressure and health outcomes. Conversely, the results highlight that economic growth, expanded public health expenditures, and improved DPT immunization rates serve as critical countervailing forces that enhance longevity and reduce pediatric deaths.

Policy implications

The findings of this study offer useful information to policymakers who want to enhance health outcomes through strategies and policies related to economic and environmental development. These findings have policy implications, especially in light of the relationship between environmental deterioration and human health effects. The infant mortality rate is negatively impacted by current environmental deterioration, but life expectancy is positively impacted. This suggests that policymakers should take action immediately to prioritize regulations that mitigate environmental pressures, such as air and water pollution, deforestation, and climate change, to improve public health and accomplish Sustainable Development Goal 3, which aims to ensure that everyone has a good quality of life.

The results show that economic growth is positively associated with human health outcomes. The study indicates that economic growth that promotes sustainable and all-encompassing growth will improve health outcomes. To maximize the health benefits of economic growth in society, policymakers should prioritize investment in public health care infrastructure, particularly in low-income communities.

The role of public health expenditure is crucial to improving the health of individuals. These findings indicate that financing the health care system has a favorable effect on health outcomes in terms of public health spending. By prioritizing funds to support public health infrastructure, policymakers can reduce the burden on public health by focusing on sustainable development initiatives.

The level of immunization shows a positive effect on health outcomes. Public awareness initiatives and community-based health care programs can help eliminate obstacles to immunization and vaccination. Policymakers should increase vaccination coverage for vaccine-preventable diseases, especially for underprivileged populations. By addressing these problems, promoting sustainable development, and achieving SDG-3, which ensures the health and well-being of its population, significant progress can be accomplished.

Limitations

While this study provides a robust macro-level analysis, its focused scope offers clear pathways for subsequent research. The utilization of aggregate national data establishes a critical baseline; however, future investigations could employ spatial or panel data methodologies to unpack subnational dynamics and identify specific regional environmental-health hotspots. Furthermore, while the ecological footprint serves as a comprehensive environmental proxy, future research might integrate granular indicators, such as localized healthcare quality metrics and behavioral lifestyle variables, to further refine targeted interventions at the intersection of environmental sustainability and public health.
