A prospective observational pilot study assessing childhood asthma control in areas with increased urban environmental greenery
Authors: Scott Bickel, Aruni Bhatnagar, Ted Smith, Ray Yeager, Pawel Lorkiewicz, Zhengzhi Xie, Nemr Eid, Adrian O’Hagan, Janice E. Sullivan, Sara Watson, Ronald Morton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purpose of this study is to assess the feasibility of measuring differences in asthma symptoms, lung function, asthma biomarkers, and allergic sensitization in children with asthma living in areas undergoing environmental greening compared to control areas.MethodsThis was a single-site prospective observational pilot study enrolling children living in either areas undergoing systematic environmental greening or in a designated control area. Eligible children were ages 6–18 with an asthma diagnosis. Children were followed for one year. The pre-specified primary endpoint was the change in Standardized Pediatric Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire (PAQLQ(S)) scores between the initial and final visit. Secondary outcomes included changes in spirometry values, impulse oscillometry results, and asthma biomarkers (exhaled nitric oxide, IgE, blood eosinophils). Observational endpoints included healthcare utilization and self-reported rescue inhaler use. Urine samples were collected to measure plant metabolites. The trial was designed to provide key data to inform future larger studies in this domain.ResultsTen children in the control area (mean age 12.1 years) and nine children in the intervention area (mean age 11.9 years) with similar baseline PAQLQ(S) scores and lung function were enrolled. From initial to final measurement, mean PAQLQ(S) asthma symptom domain scores improved in children living in the intervention areas (baseline 5.4, final 6.1, p = 0.048) while scores in the control areas showed no change (baseline 5.6, final 5.2, p = 0.66). There were statistically significant declines in FEV1 and FEF25−75% from initial to final measurement in the control group. There were no significant changes in any lung function parameter in the intervention group. There were no statistically significant differences in exhaled nitric oxide, blood eosinophils, and IgE in either group. Urine plant metabolite data suggested higher plant exposure in the intervention group. Subjects attended 96.8% of expected visits and were able to complete nearly all study-related tasks.ConclusionIn this small pilot study, children with asthma living in areas undergoing systematic environmental greening had improvements in asthma quality of life scores. Study interventions were feasible for subjects to complete. Larger studies with appropriate power are needed to study the direct impact of greening interventions on pediatric asthma.

Introduction

Asthma is an inflammatory disease of the airways that affects over 25 million children in the United States [1]. Disparities in social determinants of health, including indoor and outdoor environmental exposures, have substantial impacts on pediatric asthma prevalence and control [2, 3]. It is well established that increased exposure to outdoor air pollution increases the risk for asthma symptoms in children. Trenga, et al. demonstrated a detrimental effect on FEF25−75% with even low levels of exposure to particulate matter in children with asthma in an urban area and noted that further investigation with more sensitive measures of lung function and airway inflammation are needed [4]. Early childhood exposure to traffic-related air pollution has been associated with increased incidence of asthma in children up to age 12-yers of age [5]. Cromar et al. estimated that poor air quality resulted in excess all-cause annual mortality of 6,270 deaths (95% CI 2,180 to 11,200) in the United States with about 12,700,000 excess “impacted days” where a child or adult’s activity, including school and work attendance, were adversely affected [6].

While the detrimental impact of outdoor air pollution on respiratory health is well described, interventions that can be implemented at a local, neighborhood level to improve air quality and health outcomes are lacking. Reduction of air pollution emissions from sources such as power generation have been demonstrated to reduce asthma morbidity but may not be feasible in all communities [7]. Increasing greenspace in neighborhoods has been suggested as a potentially impactful intervention [8]. In one cross-sectional study, higher urban tree density was associated with decreased asthma hospitalization rates in areas with higher levels of outdoor air pollution [9]. Most studies examining greenspace on health outcomes are observational and cross sectional in nature. There are often significant confounders, such as higher socioeconomic status in areas with higher rates of greenspace, which makes drawing conclusions on the usefulness of greening as an intervention challenging. Furthermore, while the deleterious impact of air pollution is clear, there is mixed data on the relationship between environmental greenness and childhood asthma/allergy outcomes. Systematic reviews looking at the effect of greenness on asthma and allergies in children found that inconstancies between trial designs substantially limited the ability to draw conclusions [10, 11]. Increasing greenspace could increase exposure to allergens thus having a negative impact asthma on control, as suggested by one case control study conducted in Lithuania [12]. To our knowledge, no prior study has prospectively introduced mature greenspace in a neighborhood while monitoring a designated control area of similar baseline characteristics that is not undergoing greenspace enhancement to assess differences in health outcomes between the two areas.

In Louisville, KY, a unique and first of its kind study entitled the Green Heart Project, conducted by the University of Louisville Christina Lee Brown Envirome Institute, aimed to prospectively study the effects of greening interventions in an urban residential area on the health of residents, with a primary focus on cardiovascular outcomes in adults [13]. In addition, extensive assessments of traffic-related air pollution have been undertaken in the study area which are being used to estimate residential-level air pollution exposures of Green Heart study participants [14]. The Green Heart Project study area is a collection of contiguous urban residential neighborhoods, encompassing 10 square miles, in Louisville, Kentucky. It is characterized by moderate to high levels of vehicle traffic, low-to-moderate resident socioeconomic status (SES), and lower existing green cover than most Louisville neighborhoods. Approximately 8,000 mature trees were planted between 2019 and 2022 within a geographically defined intervention area that was environmentally (green cover, air pollution) and demographically (SES, population density) matched with the adjoined control area to facilitate comparative analysis. Tree species, other vegetation, their planting location, and layout (i.e. vegetative roadside buffers) were selected for their ability to filter air pollutants. Trees were also selected specifically to minimize the likelihood of increased pollen and other potential allergens. Primary planting was completed in June 2022.

There were no children enrolled in the primary Green Heart study. However, members of the Green Heart Project study team partnered with other stakeholders to facilitate ancillary projects, to be conducted to take advantage of the greening intervention, across multiple health and environmental domains, including pediatric asthma. An R01 grant was proposed to the National Institutes of Health for a full-scale study of children with asthma in this area. The proposed grant was not funded, with the primary feedback being that additional pilot data was necessary to ensure study feasibility. Therefore, this ancillary pilot study enrolled a small cohort of children with asthma living in both the intervention areas undergoing environmental greening and the control area to prospectively follow them over an approximately 12-month period. The study aimed to assess feasibility and inform trial design for a larger study while gathering preliminary data on the impact of the greening intervention on subject’s asthma control and atopic status.

Methods

This was a single-site prospective observational pilot study with two groups: children living in the intervention or control areas of the Green Heart Project. The boundaries between intervention and control areas were established by the Green Heart Project [13]. Eligible children were 6–18 years old at the time of enrollment with a physician diagnosis of asthma who resided in the study area for at least 75% of the time. They were required to be prescribed bronchodilator therapy for acute symptoms. Exclusion criteria included primary use of tobacco, e-cigarettes, marijuana or other inhaled substances of abuse, significant medical co-morbidities such as cystic fibrosis or cyanotic congenital heart disease, and/or inability to perform pulmonary function testing. Sample size was determined by available funding for this pilot study. Subject demographics are in Table 1. Children were followed quarterly for one year. The pre-specified primary endpoint was changes in the Standardized Pediatric Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire (PAQLQ(S)) scores between enrollment and final visit. The PAQLQ(S) is a validated instrument used in multiple prior asthma studies and results in a total score as well as sub-scores in the domains on asthma symptom control, activity limitations, and emotional function [15, 16]. Appropriate permissions were obtained from the copyright holder to use the PAQLQ(S) in this study. Total and sub-domain scores of the PAQLQ(S) have a minimal clinically important difference of 0.5 points.

Secondary outcomes included changes in spirometry measurements (FEV1, FEF25−75), impulse oscillometry (IOS) measurements (R5, R20, X5, Fres, D20-5), and asthma/allergy biomarkers (exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), IgE, blood eosinophils, and an environmental radioallergosorbent allergy panel). PAQLQ(S) and pulmonary function testing (spirometry, IOS, FeNO) were completed at each quarterly visit. Spirometry (CPFS/D Spirometer, MGC Diagnostics, St. Paul, MN) and IOS (Impulse Oscillometry System, Vyaire Medical, Mettawa, IL) were performed both pre- and post- bronchodilator with testing conforming to American Thoracic Society / European Respiratory Society guidelines [17, 18]. All spirometry and IOS tests were reviewed by the primary investigator (SB) to ensure they met criteria for acceptability. Blood samples were obtained at the first and last visit. Subject’s guardians were notified of all clinical test results once available and reviewed by the primary investigator. They were referred to their pediatrician if there were any clinical concerns. One subject in the intervention arm was lost to follow up and did not provide an end of study blood sample.

Urine samples were collected at each visit to store for analysis of biogenic volatile organic compound (BVOC) metabolites using a validated panel developed by the Christina Lee Brown Envirome Institute Bioanalytics Core for the Green Heart Project investigators to measure exposure to greenspace. This panel measures two metabolites of limonene and three metabolites of pinene, all demonstrated previously to correlate with greenspace exposure [19, 20]. Limonene is emitted by a large array of plants while pinene is emitted primarily by coniferous and deciduous trees. The elimination rates for these urinary metabolites are comparable for both, with half-lives of approximately 0.5–1.5 h for pinene metabolites and 0.7–2.7 h for limonene metabolites [19, 20].

Health care utilization (reports of systemic steroid use, emergency room visits, and hospital admissions) was tracked for both groups. Use of acute bronchodilator therapy and associated asthma symptoms were collected using written asthma journals. Data collection occurred between September 2021 and September 2023. All testing was done at the Norton Children’s Research Institute within the Novak Center for Children’s Health in downtown Louisville, approximately 15 min from the neighborhoods undergoing the greening intervention.

Analyses for this pilot study followed an intention to treat principle with a primary focus on assessing feasibility for a larger, appropriately powered study. Per the study’s protocol, the pre-specified primary outcome for this pilot study was the result of a two-tailed, paired t-test to compare the change to health-related quality of life scores in children with asthma at their initial visit and at their final visit as measured by the PAQLQ(S) and its sub-domains. Paired t-tests were also used to compare changes in lung function parameters and asthma/allergy biomarkers from the first to final visit. Unpaired t-tests were used for between-group comparisons (control versus intervention) at each testing interval for PAQLQ(S) scores, lung function assessments, and asthma/allergy biomarkers. Mann-Whitney U tests were used to assess urinary BVOC plant metabolites between groups which were not normally distributed. This project was approved by the University of Louisville Institutional Review Board (#20.0029). This study conforms with the Declaration of Helsinki and US Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects. Informed consent from the participant’s parent or legal guardian was obtained prior to inclusion in the study; children ages 7 and up provided written assent. This study was funded by grants from the National Institutes of Health (UG1 OD024954) and the American Lung Association. Urine plant metabolite analysis was supported in part by two NIH S10 shared instrumentation grants: S10OD032361 and S10OD026840.

Results

Ten children in the control areas (mean age 12.1 years) and nine children in the intervention areas (mean age 11.9 years) with similar baseline PAQLQ(S) scores and lung function were enrolled. Asthma severity may have been worse in the control group based on asthma classification and baseline use of maintenance medications. Both groups had a majority female and African American makeup. There were similar numbers of secondhand smoke and pet exposure in each group. The mean length of time per subject between initial and final visits was 359 days ensuring similar seasonality for comparisons. Complete demographics are in Table 1.

In total, subjects attended 92 of the 95 expected visits (96.8%) though one subject from the intervention arm was lost to follow up prior to the final blood draw collection. From enrollment to the final visit, mean PAQLQ(S) asthma symptom domain scores improved in children living in the intervention areas (baseline 5.4, final 6.1, p = 0.048) while scores in the control areas showed no change (baseline 5.6, final 5.2, p = 0.66). Mean total PAQLQ(S) improved, albeit non-significantly, in children living in the intervention areas (baseline 5.7, final 6.3, p = 0.067) while there was no improvement in scores in the control area (baseline 5.7, final 5.6, p = 0.93). Activity and emotional domain scores also improved in the intervention area by greater than 0.5 points. There were no statistically significant differences between the control and intervention group at any time interval. Complete results are listed in Table 2.

Spirometry was similar in both groups with an initial mean percent predicted FEV1 of 103.7% in the control group and 101.2% in the intervention group. Each group had a slight decline in FEV1 by the final visit which was statistically significant in the control arm (control: 97%, p = 0.003; intervention: 96.7%, p = 0.11). Similar findings were seen with FEF25−75% percent predicted. Impulse oscillometry was performed in addition to spirometry due to its ability to detect small airway impairment more readily than spirometry [21]. IOS parameters (R5, R20, X5% predicted) were normal at baseline in each group and showed no significant change at the end of the study. There were no statistically significant differences between the control and intervention group at any time interval with respect to lung function testing. All subjects were able to provide repeatable and acceptable results with both spirometry and IOS. Spirometry and IOS results are summarized in Table 2.

Mean exhaled nitric oxide was normal at baseline in the control group (23.4 ppb) but elevated in the intervention group (41.8 ppb). A downward trend was observed in the intervention group (final mean FeNO 20.7 ppb, p = 0.21) which was not observed in the control group (final mean FeNO 28.7ppb). All subjects were able to complete FeNO testing. Similarly, mean blood eosinophils were somewhat more elevated in the intervention area at baseline but declined by the end of the study (intervention group: 311 cells/mcL baseline, 242 cells/mcL at final visit, p = 0.28; control group: 274 cells/mcL baseline, 386 cells/mcL at final visit, p = 0.26). Mean serum total IgE was higher in the intervention area compared to control and demonstrated a modest, non-significant improvement (intervention group: 406 IU/mL baseline, 345 IU/mL at final visit, p = 0.50; control group: 136 IU/mL baseline, 134 IU/mL at final visit, p = 0.27). A radioallergosorbent panel including multiple common regional tree and grass species was conducted and neither group exhibited changes in allergic sensitization to trees over the course of the study (Table 2). There were no statistically significant differences between the control and intervention group with respect to asthma biomarkers at either time interval.

There were four instances of subjects being administered oral corticosteroids during the study period, all in the control group. Two of these were administered in the emergency department and two prescribed by the subject’s pediatrician. There were no hospital admissions in either group during the study.

There were more self-reported episodes of albuterol use in the intervention group than in the control group. The intervention group reported using albuterol 122 times during the study period while the control group reported using albuterol 37 times during the study period. Albuterol use varied considerably between subjects, particularly in the intervention group, and may have been underreported in the control group. Two subjects in the intervention group accounted for 53% of the reported albuterol use. There were seven instances of failure to complete the asthma journal as instructed at a quarterly visit in the control group compared to two instances in the intervention group. In the intervention group, subjects reported no albuterol use in that prior quarter at 20 of 33 (61%) of quarterly visits compared to at 22 of the 40 (55%) quarterly follow-up visits in the control group.

Urine samples were analyzed to measure BVOCs of limonene and pinene to determine exposure to plants. Two of the five metabolites measured (LM6b and aPM4d) were statistically higher in the intervention group while none were statistically higher in the control group (Table 3).

Discussion

This pilot study is the first of its kind to prospectively assess the possible impact of intentional environmental greening compared to a control group in pediatric asthma. The study proved feasible with a high completion rate of visits despite testing being conducted in part during the COVID-19 pandemic and at a location outside of the neighborhood where the parent study was being performed. Subjects in this age range were able to reliably perform all testing maneuvers. Obtaining bloodwork at the initial and final visits was not a barrier to enrollment. Consistent use of a paper journal for asthma symptoms and albuterol use proved challenging for some subjects. In an expanded trial, this could be ameliorated using electronic diaries with reminders and incentives as well as digital inhaler tracking [22–24].

The study observed improvements in asthma quality of life scores in areas undergoing environmental greening between the initial and final visit that were not observed in the control group though direct comparisons between the two groups were not statistically significant. Furthermore, decreases in spirometric measures of lung function (FEV1 and FEF25−75%) were seen in the control group but not the intervention group. Direct group comparisons were again not significant. The reason for the observed declines in the control group is uncertain and could relate to differences in asthma severity, medication adherence, or differing seasonal effects. Members of the intervention group had higher exposure to plant-emitted monoterpenes as measured by urinary BVOC metabolites suggesting higher levels of plant exposure during the study period. No worsening of asthma or allergy biomarkers were seen in either group. There was no increase in sensitization to common regional trees and grasses in the intervention group.

Exposure to greenspace may be protective against cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases in adults [25, 26]. Exposure to greenness in the Green Heart study area has been linked to a less pro-inflammatory innate immune profile as well as decreases in high-sensitivity C-reactive protein in adults [27, 28]. The impact of greenspace on childhood health remains under investigation. A large meta-analysis of over 35,000 children in Europe looked at the impact of greenspace on respiratory, cardiometabolic, and neurodevelopmental outcomes [29]. They found higher levels of childhood greenspace exposure was associated with higher lung function, however there were no consistent associations with any other studied outcome, including clinical asthma control. The authors also noted that the effect on lung function was stronger in children from higher socioeconomic backgrounds. In a cohort of children in the United States hospitalized with bronchiolitis, exposure to residential greenspace between birth and bronchiolitis admission was associated with a lower risk of asthma and atopy by age 6 [30]. Zeng, et al. found that increased levels of greenness around schools was associated with fewer asthma symptoms [31]. Similarly, data from four cohorts of children and teenagers in Finland and Estonia suggested that early-life exposure to greenspace near the home was associated with decreased atopic sensitization, in support of the biodiversity hypothesis that increased environmental exposures attenuates atopic response [32, 33]. In contrast, however, Lovasi, et al. found that in New York City, higher levels of tree canopy coverage near living environments were associated with an increased risk for the development of allergies and asthma by age 7 [34]. These examples of discordant results in the literature underscore the need for large controlled, prospective studies of the impact of planned greenspace enhancement on childhood respiratory health.

This study has several limitations. It is a small pilot study designed to assess feasibility, gather preliminary data with exploratory statistical analysis, and refine methods for a future larger trial. While some trends in data were observed, this study is not adequately powered to detect differences in key outcomes between groups nor assess causality. The post-hoc power for the primary outcome of the total PAQLQ(S) score was 27.7%. Subjects in the control group may have had more severe asthma based on their asthma classification and use of maintenance medications which may have influenced study results, although both groups had similar lung function and asthma quality of life scores at enrollment. Adherence with self-reported albuterol use via paper journals varied between the two groups, making interpretation of those results challenging. We also did not directly measure indoor air quality, though there were similar numbers exposed to secondhand smoke and pets in the home environment. There may also have been unrecognized socioeconomic differences between our groups, though the overall geographic groupings of the Green Heart areas controlled for this. This data was gathered from areas where greenspace was enhanced in a very deliberate and selective manner to improve air quality while minimizing increased pollen exposure. As such, these findings may not be generalizable to other greening interventions. Given the exploratory nature of this study, multiple tests were conducted without multiplicity corrections (such as Bonferroni corrections), which increases the risk of Type 1 error. Furthermore, this study was observational in nature and subjects were unable to be randomized or blinded to the intervention which may induce a placebo effect in subjective measures.

This pilot study provides the framework for future studies. Subjects were largely adherent with the quarterly visit frequency, which may be important to detect seasonal differences in symptoms in a larger study. Subjects were able to complete all visit assessments including multiple modalities of lung function testing without issue. The PAQLQ(S) provided important information on asthma control across multiple domains. Daily symptom and inhaler use tracking may benefit from electronic approaches instead of paper journals. We found no evidence of harm caused by the study greening intervention. Appropriately powered, larger cohorts of children with asthma encompassing all classes of severity exposed to controlled greening interventions are needed to determine their impact and provide additional information on underlying mechanisms while controlling for other confounding measures such as socioeconomic status, medication adherence, indoor air quality, and individual activity levels. An appropriately powered study to detect a 0.5-point difference in PAQLQ(S) scores with a power of 80% and alpha of 0.05 would need to enroll approximately 106 subjects in each arm. Future larger prospective studies as well as retrospective studies within the Green Heart area can provide valuable insight into the impact of greening interventions on healthcare utilization such as changes in emergency department visits, hospital admissions, and need for corticosteroids.

Conclusion

In this small pilot study, children with asthma living in areas undergoing environmental greening were observed to have improvements in asthma quality of life scores between the start of the study and the final study visit though direct causality cannot be established. There were no significant differences in asthma quality of life scores between the intervention group and the control group at any point during the study. There were no trends to suggest that the environmental greening intervention worsened any allergy or asthma parameters. Larger studies are needed to assess these observed trends, establish causality, and explore underlying mechanisms.
