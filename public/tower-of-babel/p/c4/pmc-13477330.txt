HIF1α Attenuates Doxorubicin‐Induced Cardiotoxicity by Activating TEX264‐Associated ER‐phagy
Authors: Xinying Wang, Ge Zhan, Jiatian Li, Xinmiao Yang, Yuhang Wen, Yuqi Tang, Puyu Zhang, Yunlong Xia, Xiaolei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical utility of doxorubicin, a potent chemotherapeutic agent, is severely limited by its dose‐dependent cardiotoxicity. Hypoxia‐inducible factor 1α (HIF1α) is a key regulator of cardiovascular adaptation, but its role and mechanism in doxorubicin‐induced cardiotoxicity (DIC) remain unclear.MethodsUsing in vitro (AC16 cells) and in vivo (mouse) models of DIC, we used genetic (knockout, knockdown) and pharmacological (FG4592) approaches to modulate HIF1α. Cardiac function, apoptosis, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) morphology, and ER‐phagy flux were assessed. Molecular mechanisms were investigated using chromatin immunoprecipitation and promoter activity assays.ResultsHIF1α exhibited a dynamic, biphasic expression pattern during DIC progression. Stabilization of HIF1α by FG4592 alleviated doxorubicin‐induced cardiac dysfunction, atrophy, fibrosis, and apoptosis, whereas HIF1α knockout exacerbated these injuries. The protective effects of FG4592 were strictly dependent on HIF1α. Mechanistically, HIF1α transcriptionally activated the ER‐phagy receptor gene testis‐expressed protein 264 (TEX264) by directly binding to its promoter. This activation enhanced ER‐phagy, and suppressed ER stress–mediated apoptosis. Crucially, ablation of TEX264 abolished the cardioprotective effects of both HIF1α and FG4592.ConclusionsThis study identifies a novel HIF1α/TEX264/ER‐phagy axis that is suppressed in DIC and is central to cardiomyocyte survival. Targeting this pathway, particularly with the clinically available HIF1α stabilizer FG4592, represents a promising therapeutic strategy against DIC.Keywords: cardioprotection, doxorubicin cardiotoxicity, ER‐phagy, FG4592, HIF1α, TEX264Subject Categories: Cardio-Oncology

Doxorubicin is a chemotherapeutic agent widely used to treat various malignant tumors, but its clinical use is a double‐edged sword: dose‐dependent cardiotoxicity, known as DIC, often culminates in heart failure, severely compromising patient outcomes. 1 , 2 , 3 Beyond classical mechanisms such as oxidative stress, dysregulation of cellular organelle homeostasis, 4 particularly within the ER, has emerged as an important contributor to DIC. 5

The ER is a multifunctional cellular organelle with vital roles in protein synthesis, modification, and secretion; lipid and steroid synthesis; and regulation of Ca2+ signaling. 6 , 7 ER dysfunction is a hallmark of various cardiovascular diseases, 8 and substantial ER damage has been conclusively linked to doxorubicin‐induced myocardial injury. 5 , 9 Thus, maintaining ER homeostasis represents a compelling therapeutic target for DIC.

Hypoxia‐inducible factor 1α (HIF1α), which is widely expressed in mammalian cells, is a master transcriptional regulator of cellular adaptation to hypoxia. Under normoxic conditions, HIF1α stability is negatively regulated by prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs), leading to rapid degradation. 10 , 11 Under hypoxia or upon PHD inhibition, stabilized HIF1α translocates to the nucleus, dimerizes with HIF1β, and activates gene expression by binding to hypoxia‐response elements (HREs), thereby orchestrating adaptive responses. 12 Previous research has demonstrated that HIF1α activation is a central mediator of cardioprotection. 13 For example, intermittent hypoxia protects the heart against ischemia–reperfusion injury in a manner critically dependent on HIF1a gene dosage, highlighting it as a fundamental adaptive mechanism. 14

Given this pivotal role in orchestrating endogenous cardioprotection, the pharmacological activation of HIF1α has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy to combat cardiac injury. Pharmacological stabilization of HIF1α through PHD inhibition is a promising therapeutic strategy. 15 , 16 Early research tools such as cobalt chloride 17 and dimethyloxalylglycine 18 have shown cardioprotective efficacy in preclinical models. Among newer‐generation compounds, FG4592 (Roxadustat) is a potent and reversible 19 PHD inhibitor clinically approved for the treatment of anemia. 20 It offers superior selectivity, oral bioavailability, and a well‐established safety profile, with pharmacokinetic properties (half‐life ~12–19 hours, >99% protein binding) that support convenient dosing. 21 , 22 FG4592 has demonstrated therapeutic effects in hypertension, 23 ischemia–reperfusion injury, 24 and diabetic myocardial injury. 25 Notably, FG4592 alleviates DIC without interfering with the antitumor efficacy of doxorubicin. 26 However, although its protective effects have been linked to HIF1α target genes such as BCL2 and SOD2, the precise cellular mechanisms, particularly those related to organelle homeostasis, remain unclear.

Previous studies have shown that HIF1α is closely associated with ER function and homeostasis, 27 leading us to hypothesize that HIF1α protects against DIC by preserving ER homeostasis. A critical process in ER quality control is ER‐phagy, 28 a selective autophagy that removes excess or damaged ER. Here, we tested the hypothesis that HIF1α confers cardioprotection in DIC by activating ER‐phagy. Using integrated genetic and pharmacological approaches, we demonstrate that HIF1α mitigates cardiac injury by directly transactivating the gene encoding the key ER‐phagy receptor TEX264.

METHODS

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its online Supplemental Material. We used the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments reporting guidelines when reporting our animal research. 29 Detailed materials and methods are given in Data S1. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Ethical Statement

All animal procedures were approved by Animal Experimental Ethics Committees of Dalian Medical University and complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health. All mice were kept at room temperature (22–25 °C) under 12‐hour light/dark cycles, with free access to food and water. Mice were euthanized in a stable state of deep anesthesia (3% tribromoethanol) before euthanasia to minimize suffering.

Experimental Animal Models

Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) weighing 21 to 25 g were obtained from Changsheng Bio‐technology Company. Cardiomyocyte‐specific Hif1α knockout (Hif1α‐cKO) mice (mHif1α flox/flox , αMyHC‐Cre) and their littermate controls (Hif1α fl/fl ), purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (China), were generated by crossing female Hif1α‐cKO mice with male Hif1α fl/fl mice. Female Hif1α‐cKO mice were crossed with male Hif1α fl/fl mice to generate Hif1α‐cKO mice. Adeno‐associated virus 9 (AAV9) carrying short hairpin RNA (shRNA) against Tex264 (AAV‐shTex264) and empty vector (AAV‐shNC) were conducted by Hanbio Biotechnology Company. AAV9 virus (1 × 1012 viral particles in 100 μL) was administered via tail vein injection. Two weeks after AAV9 injection, mice were subjected to doxorubicin treatment to establish the DIC model. Doxorubicin, obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China; Catalog #D107159), was administered intraperitoneally at 5 mg/kg 30 , 31 once a week for 4 weeks. FG4592 (Roxadustat), purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, Shanghai, China; Catalog #HY‐13426), was injected intraperitoneally at 10 mg/kg 25 , 26 48 hours before each doxorubicin injection. The control group of mice received an equal volume of DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) dissolved in saline. Mice were weighed before each doxorubicin injection and before euthanized.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean±SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between 2 groups were analyzed using 2‐tailed unpaired Student t‐test. For comparisons involving more than 2 groups, 1‐way or 2‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Repeated measures were analyzed using 2‐way repeated measures ANOVA, with appropriate post hoc tests as indicated. Survival data were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log‐rank test. For Western blot and immunohistochemical quantifications, at least 3 independent experiments were performed. In vivo studies used a minimum of 5 to 8 mice per group. All experiments were performed with the investigator masked to group allocation during data acquisition and analysis. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures by P values directly.

RESULTS

HIF1α Is Dynamically Regulated during DIC

To investigate the role of HIF1α in DIC, we first examined its expression in cellular and animal models. In vitro, HIF1α protein levels in AC16 cells decreased in a dose‐dependent manner with increasing doxorubicin concentrations (Figure 1A and 1B). When AC16 cells were stimulated with 0.5 μM doxorubicin, HIF1α expression exhibited a biphasic response, with a significant upregulation peaking at 12 hours (P<0.05 versus 0 hour) followed by a significant decline by 48 to 72 hours (Figure 1C and 1D). In vivo, we systematically evaluated HIF1α protein levels with whole‐heart tissue after doxorubicin injection (Days 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14). HIF1α expression increased during the first 3 days following doxorubicin administration and subsequently declined, falling below baseline levels by Day 14 (Figure 1E and 1F). These results demonstrate that HIF1α is dynamically regulated during DIC progression, suggesting its involvement in the pathophysiology of DIC.

HIF1α Reduces Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis in DIC Mice by Maintaining ER Homeostasis

To explore the biological function of HIF1α in DIC, we used a mouse model involving 4 weekly administrations of doxorubicin (5 mg/kg) 32 with or without co‐administration of the HIF1α stabilizer FG4592 (10 mg/kg) (Figure 2A). Doxorubicin markedly impaired overall survival and reduced body weight; both effects were significantly attenuated in doxorubicin+FG4592 mice (Figure S1A and S1B). Additionally, FG4592 attenuated doxorubicin‐induced cardiac dysfunction, as evidenced by significant improvements in left ventricular ejection fraction percentage and fractional shortening percentage (Figure 2B through 2D). Structural remodeling was evaluated by hematoxylin and eosin, Masson' trichrome, and wheat germ agglutinin staining. Doxorubicin‐treated mice exhibited significantly reduced ventricular wall thickness (Figure 2E), a lower heart weight/tibia length ratio (Figure 2F), and decreased cardiomyocyte cross‐sectional area (Figure 2G and 2H), indicating cardiac atrophy. These changes were significantly alleviated in doxorubicin+FG4592 mice. Masson staining showed that FG4592 reduced doxorubicin‐induced myocardial fibrosis (Figure S1E and S1F). Additionally, the number of TUNEL (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling)‐positive cells was lower in doxorubicin+FG4592 mice than in doxorubicin mice (Figure 2I and 2J). To assess potential angiogenic effects, we performed CD31 staining and observed a trend toward increased capillary density in FG4592‐treated hearts, although this did not reach statistical significance (Figure S1G and S1H). To definitively establish that FG4592 exerts its cardioprotective effects specifically through HIF1α, we administered FG4592 to doxorubicin‐treated Hif1α‐cKO mice. In the absence of HIF1α, FG4592 failed to improve cardiac function (left ventricular ejection fraction percentage, fractional shortening percentage) (Figure S1K through Figure S1M) or reduce cardiomyocyte apoptosis (TUNEL‐positive cells) (Figure S1N and S1O).

We next examined ultrastructural changes in myocardial tissue using transmission electron microscopy. As shown in the transmission electron microscopy images (Figure 2K), the control group displayed normal myocardial architecture with compact structure, well‐ordered myofibrils, closely arranged myofilaments, and distinct Z‐discs. Numerous mitochondria with regular morphology and clear cristae were observed, along with well‐organized ER studded with abundant ribosomes (black arrows). In contrast, doxorubicin administration resulted in loosened myocardial structure, focal myofibril disruption, disorganized myofilaments, and indistinct Z‐discs. Mitochondrial number was reduced, with some showing mild atrophy. Notably, doxorubicin‐treated hearts exhibited marked ER dilation (black arrows), with visibly swollen cisternae forming vacuole‐like structures and reduced ribosome density—consistent with previous findings by Fu et al. 9 Importantly, FG4592+doxorubicin co‐treatment markedly alleviated doxorubicin‐induced injury, as evidenced by preserved mitochondrial morphology and significantly attenuated ER dilation (black arrows). Additionally, ER‐phagy characterized by double‐membrane structures engulfing ER fragments was observed (yellow arrows), suggesting that HIF1α may induce ER‐phagy to maintain ER homeostasis. We further investigated the effects of doxorubicin on HIF1α expression and ER stress‐mediated apoptosis (Figure 2L, 2M through 2P). Western blots analysis revealed that doxorubicin decreased HIF1α expression while activating ER stress–associated apoptotic signaling, including CHOP upregulation, caspase‐12 cleavage, and JNK phosphorylation. FG4592 significantly increased HIF1α expression and attenuated activation of these apoptotic pathways. Based on the transmission electron microscopy findings, we quantified levels of the autophagy marker LC3II in heart tissue and found significantly higher LC3II levels in doxorubicin+FG4592 mice than in doxorubicin mice (Figure 2L and Figure 2Q). Collectively, these data indicate that HIF1α attenuates doxorubicin‐induced cardiac injury by maintaining ER homeostasis, and that the cardioprotective effect of FG4592 is strictly HIF1α‐dependent.

HIF1α Reverses Doxorubicin‐Induced Apoptosis in AC16 Cells by Enhancing ER‐Phagy

We next performed in vitro experiments using AC16 cells (Figure 3A). Compared with vehicle‐treated controls, doxorubicin incubation significantly increased the number of TUNEL‐positive cells, which was attenuated by FG4592 (Figure 3B and 3C). Doxorubicin suppressed HIF1α expression, and this suppression was also attenuated by FG4592 (Figure 3D and 3E). Additionally, FG4592 significantly attenuated doxorubicin‐induced upregulation of p‐JNK/JNK and CHOP, while increasing pro‐caspase‐12 and LC3II expression (Figure 3D, 3F through 3I). To confirm HIF1α transcriptional activation, we measured mRNA levels of its canonical targets VEGFA and HMOX1 in AC16 cells and found that FG4592 significantly upregulated both (Figure S2A and S2B). To test whether the effects of FG4592 are mediated specifically through HIF1α, we performed a rescue experiment in which knockdown of HIF1α (siHIF1α) completely abolished the protective effects of FG4592 against doxorubicin‐induced apoptosis (Figure S2C through S2L), demonstrating that the cardioprotective action of FG4592 is critically dependent on HIF1α. FG4592 also decreased the ER area in AC16 cells exposed to doxorubicin (Figure 3J and 3K). Using the ER autophagy tandem reporter assay, 33 we analyzed ER‐phagy flux in cardiomyocytes. ER‐phagy flux was suppressed after 48 hours of doxorubicin incubation, but this effect was attenuated by FG4592 (Figure 3L and 3M). Taken together, HIF1α enhances ER‐phagy and mitigates doxorubicin‐induced ER expansion, thereby reducing doxorubicin‐induced apoptosis.

HIF1α Disruption Promotes Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis and ER Dilation in DIC Mice

To further define the role of HIF1α in DIC, we used Hif1α fl/fl and myocardial‐specific Hif1α knockout (Hif1α‐cKO) mice (Figure 4A). Immunoblot analysis confirmed that HIF1α expression was specifically blocked in the heart (Figure S1I and S1J). Doxorubicin‐treated Hif1α‐cKO mice exhibited lower overall survival rate and body weight compared with doxorubicin‐treated Hif1α fl/fl mice (Figure S3A and S3B). Compared with Hif1α fl/fl + doxorubicin mice, Hif1α‐cKO + doxorubicin mice showed further reductions in left ventricular ejection fraction percentage and fractional shortening percentage (Figure 4B through 4D), greater ventricular wall thinning (Figure 4E), lower heart weight/tibia length ratios (Figure 4F), reduced myocyte cross‐sectional area (Figure 4G and 4H), increased numbers of TUNEL‐positive cardiomyocytes (Figure 4I and 4J) and myocardial fibrosis (Figure S3E and S3F). Thus, Hif1α deletion in cardiomyocytes exacerbated the deterioration of cardiac function and myocardial remodeling induced by doxorubicin. Cardiomyocytes from Hif1α‐cKO+doxorubicin mice showed more pronounced ER dilation compared with Hif1α fl/fl +doxorubicin mice, characterized by loosened tissue architecture, decreased matrix density, and discontinuous ER profiles (red arrows) (Figure 4K). Additionally, HIF1α knockout further increased p‐JNK/JNK, CHOP, and caspase‐12 cleavage levels, while decreasing LC3II levels, compared with the Hif1α fl/fl +doxorubicin group (Figure 4L through 4Q). These findings confirm that HIF1α is essential for activating ER‐phagy and maintaining ER homeostasis during DIC progression.

HIF1α Disruption Suppresses ER‐Phagy and Exacerbates Doxorubicin‐Induced Apoptosis in AC16 Cells

Consistent with the in vivo results, deletion of HIF1α significantly increased doxorubicin‐induced TUNEL‐positive cardiomyocytes (Figure 5A and 5B), further enhanced activation of apoptotic signaling pathways, and reduced LC3II expression (Figure 5C through 5H). In si‐NC+doxorubicin cells, ER area was significantly increased and ER‐phagy flux was reduced compared with si‐NC cells. Disruption of HIF1α further exacerbated doxorubicin‐induced ER expansion (Figure 5I and 5J) and further suppressed ER‐phagy flux (Figure 5K and 5L). These findings indicate that HIF1α is essential for inhibiting ER dilation induced by doxorubicin.

HIF1α Transcriptionally Regulates the ER‐Phagy Receptor TEX264

The preceding results suggested that HIF1α mitigates DIC by enhancing ER‐phagy. We therefore examined the mRNA of the ER‐phagy receptor. TEX264 expression significantly increased following HIF1α upregulation (Figure 6A). We then observed a marked decrease of TEX264 expression in doxorubicin‐treated mice and AC16 cells. HIF1α overexpression (via FG4592) restored TEX264 expression, whereas HIF1α disruption reduced it (Figure 6B through 6E; Figure S4A through S4D). Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated that HIF1α binds to the TEX264 promoter (Figure 6F). Using JASPAR, we predicted potential HIF1α binding sites within the human TEX264 promoter (Figure S4F). Four high‐scoring transcription factor binding sites and their corresponding mutants (mut) were constructed and transfected into HEK293T cells (Figure 6G). Upon HIF1α overexpression, mutation of HRE1, HRE2, or HRE3 significantly reduced promoter activity compared with the wild‐type promoter. In contrast, mutation of HRE4 did not impair the promoter's responsiveness to HIF1α, with activity remaining comparable to the wild‐type promoter (Figure 6H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HRE1, HRE2, and HRE3 are essential motif for HIF1α‐mediated transactivation of the TEX264 promoter, whereas HRE4 is dispensable for this regulation.

Disruption of TEX264 Abolishes the Protective Role of HIF1α in DIC

Given that HIF1α directly upregulated TEX264 transcription, we next examined whether HIF1α protects against DIC through TEX264‐mediated ER‐phagy. Mice were injected with AAV‐shTex264 or control AAV‐shNC (Figure S5A and S5B) and treated with FG4592 or vehicle (Figure 7A). Doxorubicin treatment suppressed TEX264 expression, which was restored by FG4592; however, this restoration was abolished in TEX264 knockdown mice (Figure S5C and S5D). Compared with the AAV‐shNC group, Tex264 disruption further reduced survival rates and body weight (Figure S5E and S5F) and resulted in cardiac dysfunction (Figure 7B through 7D), myocardial fibrosis (Figure S5I and S5J), severe cardiac atrophy (Figure 7E through 7H) and apoptosis (Figure 7I, 7J, 7L through 7Q), in DIC mice. These effects were not attenuated by FG4592.

DISCUSSION

Despite extensive research on DIC, 5 , 9 its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, using a combination of gain‐ and loss‐of‐function approaches in vivo and in vitro, we demonstrate that HIF1α plays a critical cardioprotective role in DIC. HIF1α expression was transiently upregulated but subsequently suppressed in doxorubicin‐treated hearts and cardiomyocytes. Stabilization of HIF1α, either pharmacologically or genetically, attenuated doxorubicin‐induced cardiac dysfunction and apoptosis. Mechanistically, HIF1α directly bound the TEX264 promoter to upregulate ER‐phagy, thereby limiting ER expansion and slowing DIC progression. These findings identify HIF1α as a key regulator of ER homeostasis and suggest that strategies to enhance its expression may hold therapeutic potential for DIC.

Although HIF1α is a well‐established master regulator of hypoxia adaptation 34 and cardiovascular protection, 16 , 35 , 36 its role in DIC was previously unclear. A prior study indicated that HIF1α stabilization may confer protection in a DIC model. 26 Here, we extend those observations by defining the dynamic expression profile of HIF1α during DIC progression, characterized by an early transient increase followed by sustained suppression (Figure 1), consistent with the recognized biphasic pathogenesis of DIC. 37 This pattern suggests that the initial HIF1α upregulation may represent a compensatory stress response, the failure of which likely contributes to chronic maladaptive remodeling.

To establish a causal role for HIF1α, we used genetic and pharmacological gain‐ and loss‐of‐function approaches. FG4592, a PHD inhibitor approved for the treatment of anemia, 38 , 39 acts as a potent stabilizer of HIF1α and has demonstrated emerging cardiovascular benefits. 24 Although HIF1α is known to promote tumor proliferation and metastasis, prior studies have shown that FG4592 alleviates doxorubicin cardiotoxicity while enhancing doxorubicin's anticancer efficacy. 26 In our models, stabilization of HIF1α by FG4592 markedly improved cardiac function, attenuated atrophy, and reduced apoptosis (Figures 2 and 3). Conversely, cardiomyocyte‐specific deletion of HIF1α exacerbated doxorubicin‐induced injury (Figures 4 and 5) and abolished the protective effects of FG4592, indicating that FG4592‐mediated cardioprotection is HIF1α‐dependent (Figures S1 and S2). The observed trend toward increased capillary density, although not statistically significant, raises the possibility that early FG4592 intervention exerts direct cytoprotective effects, while prolonged administration may further promote vascular repair.

We next investigated how HIF1α confers protection. In terminally differentiated cardiomyocytes, maintaining ER homeostasis 7 is essential for survival. 40 , 41 Consistent with previous studies, 9 , 42 , 43 we confirmed that doxorubicin disrupts this homeostasis, causing marked ER dilation and activating ER stress–associated apoptotic signaling (Figure 2). Clearance of damaged ER is primarily mediated by ER‐phagy, wherein dedicated receptors target ER fragments for degradation. 28 Recent research has underscored the complexity of autophagic regulation in DIC, revealing a biphasic response where general autophagy is activated early but suppressed in the later phase. 31 Notably, impaired autophagic flux itself is recognized as a driver of DIC progression. 32 Our findings extend this framework to ER‐phagy specifically. While compensatory ER‐phagy occurs during early stress (e.g., via CCPG1 at 24 hours), 43 our long‐term model demonstrated that sustained doxorubicin exposure ultimately suppresses ER‐phagy flux. This suppression suggests a vicious cycle in DIC: doxorubicin impairs ER‐phagy flux, which compromises this critical clearance mechanism, exacerbating ER dysfunction and pro‐apoptotic signaling.

Our study elucidates the mechanism by which HIF1α regulates TEX264, a major ER‐phagy receptor, 44 , 45 as a direct transcriptional target of HIF1α (Figure 6). TEX264 is considered a major receptor for reticulophagy in mammals and mediates more than half of ER‐phagy flux under starvation conditions. Although TEX264‐mediated ER‐phagy is known to protect against acetaminophen‐induced liver damage 46 and Alzheimer disease, 47 its role in the heart and in DIC was previously undefined. Our work establishes TEX264 as the critical downstream effector that links HIF1α activation to the restoration of ER‐phagy flux and cardioprotection in DIC. This finding provides a novel and precise target within the broader therapeutic strategy of autophagy modulation in DIC. Previous studies have shown that pre‐administering drugs to increase autophagy levels can mitigate doxorubicin‐induced cardiac damage. 48 , 49 , 50 Our study reveals that specifically augmenting the HIF1α/TEX264 branch of ER‐phagy represents a potent and targeted approach to counteract late‐phase autophagic dysfunction in chronic DIC.

Our study has several limitations. First, although our data support the notion that FG4592 acts primarily through HIF1α, effects via other substrates (e.g., HIF‐2α) cannot be completely ruled out. Second, although we observed significant changes in HIF1α levels and activity within whole‐heart tissue lysates in vivo, our mechanistic studies focused on cardiomyocytes in vitro. The potential involvement of other cardiac cell types, such as endothelial cells and fibroblasts, remains to be clarified. Third, this study focused specifically on the role of ER‐phagy in the DIC model. The potential effects of ER‐phagy on cardiac electrical activity and contractile function warrant further investigation.

CONCLUSIONS

Using integrated genetic and pharmacological approaches, we demonstrate that HIF1α mitigates cardiac injury by directly transactivating the gene encoding the key ER‐phagy receptor TEX264, thereby identifying a previously unrecognized role for the HIF1α‐TEX264 axis in regulating ER‐phagy during DIC progression and providing a potential therapeutic strategy to prevent the development of DIC in clinical practice.
