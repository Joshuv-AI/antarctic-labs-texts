Antibiotics in the first week of life are not associated with functional gastrointestinal disorders at 9–12 years of age
Authors: Nora C. Carpay, Kim Kamphorst, Ruurd M. van Elburg, Arine M. Vlieger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDysbiosis due to early‐life antibiotics may contribute to the development of functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs). This follow‐up study of a birth cohort primarily investigates the association between antibiotic treatment in the first week of life and the presence of FGIDs at 9–12 years. Secondarily, it examines whether a history of infantile colic or current food allergy is associated with FGIDs.MethodsA prospective observational birth cohort of 436 term‐born infants was followed up at the age of 9–12 years; 151 received intravenous antibiotics in the first week of life due to suspected early onset sepsis (AB+) and 285 did not (AB−). Participants filled out questionnaires on the presence of FGIDs (Rome IV questionnaire) and food allergies, and FGID diagnoses were reported by general practitioners. Statistical analyses included chi‐squared tests and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsThree hundred and six of 388 eligible participants (79.5%) participated in the follow‐up study: 109 (35.6%) AB+ and 197 (64.4%) AB−. FGID prevalence at 9–12 years was similar in AB+ and AB− (any FGID: odds ratio [OR] 1.083, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.608–1.932). Infantile colic was not significantly associated with FAPDs after adjusting for confounders (adjusted OR 2.007, 95% CI 0.978–4.003, p = 0.051). Children with a food allergy were more likely to have a functional abdominal pain disorder (FAPD) (adjusted OR 4.028, 95% CI 1.532–10.286).ConclusionsNo statistically significant association was observed between first‐week antibiotics or infantile colic and FGIDs at 9–12 years of age, but FAPDs were significantly more prevalent in children with food allergies.

INTRODUCTION

Functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) are characterised by disturbances in the interaction between the gut, including its microbiota, and the brain. 1 These disorders are highly common in children, 2 although their prevalence varies per geographical region and the applied assessment tool. 1 , 3 The pathophysiology of FGIDs is not yet fully understood. 4 Many factors contribute to their development and persistence including genetics, neuroimmune interactions, gut motility and inflammation, nutrition, psychosocial factors and visceral hypersensitivity. 1 , 4 , 5

The gastrointestinal microbiome composition has been hypothesised to influence the development of FGIDs, 4 but studies on the link between dysbiosis and FGIDs are limited, particularly in paediatric populations and in FGIDs other than irritable bowel syndrome and functional constipation. 1 , 3 Additionally, infantile colic has been associated with an increased risk of FGIDs later in life. 6 , 7 Lastly, food allergies have been linked to FGIDs, possibly due to a shared pathophysiological mechanism in which the immune system, psychological processes and microbiome composition interact. 1 , 4 , 5

To evaluate the association between early‐life dysbiosis due to antibiotic treatment in the first week of life and clinical outcomes, the INCA‐study (INtestinal microbiota Composition after Antibiotic treatment in early life) was set up. 8 This prospective cohort study included infants who received antibiotic treatment in the first week of life due to suspected early‐onset sepsis (AB+), and healthy infants not exposed to antibiotics (AB−). 8 Previously we reported that antibiotic treatment in the first week of life was associated with dysbiosis, 9 infantile colic and wheezing in the first year of life. 10 At 4–6 years of age, there was an association with (food) allergies 11 and functional abdominal pain. 12 Other studies investigating the association between dysbiosis due to early life antibiotics and paediatric FGIDs found conflicting results, preventing general conclusions. 13 In the 4–6 year follow‐up study, infantile colic was not associated with FGIDs, but the prevalence of FGIDs was higher in children with food allergies than those without. 12

The current study is a second follow‐up study at 9–12 years investigating whether previously observed associations persist. It also examines any new associations, especially for FGIDs that are more common in children over 8 years of age such as functional nausea/vomiting, functional dyspepsia, irritable bowel syndrome, abdominal migraine and functional abdominal pain—not otherwise specified. 14

METHODS

Ethics statement

Parents provided consent to participate in the follow‐up study, as well as the children aged 12. Additional consent to obtain the general practitioner's diagnoses was optional. The medical ethical committee determined that the follow‐up study did not fall within the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act. The follow‐up study was approved by the local assessment committee of the St. Antonius Hospital (R&D/Z24.030).

Study design and data collection in the original study

The methods of the original INCA study have been described previously. 8 The INCA study started in 2012 in four Dutch hospitals. Infants were included if they were born at 36–42 weeks gestation. Both infants with suspected neonatal infection, receiving broad‐spectrum antibiotics intravenously in their first week of life (AB+), and infants born in the same hospitals but not exposed to antibiotics (AB−) were included. AB+ infants received an antibiotic regimen consisting of gentamicin combined with either penicillin, amoxicillin or amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid, depending on local hospital protocols. Before antibiotic treatment was started, a blood culture was taken. In case of a negative blood culture, combined with a low clinical suspicion of infection and low C‐reactive protein, antibiotics were discontinued after 2–3 days. Otherwise, antibiotics were continued for 5–7 days. 10

Throughout the first year of life, parents filled out daily questionnaires about symptoms including fever, coughing, wheezing and excessive crying. The questionnaires were used to determine which infants fulfilled the criteria of infantile colic, defined as parent‐reported excessive crying of ≥3 h per day, ≥3 days a week, within the first 3 months of life. Parents also completed monthly questionnaires about potential confounders such as feeding practices, daycare attendance, pets, and siblings at home. At 1 year of age, children were examined at the outpatient clinic.

Follow‐up study

When the INCA children reached the age of 9–12 years, their parents were asked to participate in the current follow‐up study. This study consisted of a questionnaire about general health and FGIDs (the Rome IV questionnaire 15 ). Rome IV criteria divide paediatric FGIDs into several groups: functional dyspepsia, irritable bowel syndrome, abdominal migraine, functional abdominal pain—not otherwise specified, functional constipation, non‐retentive faecal incontinence, functional nausea, functional vomiting, cyclic vomiting syndrome, adolescent rumination syndrome and aerophagia. 15

The questionnaire also included questions about atopic diseases (ISAAC questionnaires 16 ) and separate questions about allergies: first, parents were asked whether they knew or thought their child had an allergy. They were then asked which type of allergy (food, inhalant and/or contact allergy).

Additional informed consent was asked to approach the participants’ general practitioners (GPs) to determine whether the conditions that were mentioned by parents in the questionnaire had also been diagnosed by a doctor. The following International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC) 17 codes related to gastrointestinal disorders were obtained: D93 (irritable bowel syndrome), D01 (generalised abdominal pain/cramps), and D12 (obstipation).

The questionnaires were filled out between May and September 2024, and general practitioner's diagnoses were collected between July and November 2024.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R Studio version 4.3.2. Baseline differences between the AB+ and AB− groups were assessed by chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, and unpaired t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests when appropriate.

The associations between three different independent variables (first‐week antibiotics exposure, a history of infantile colic and current food allergy) and the prevalence of FGIDs at ages 9–12 were analysed. Unadjusted associations between these independent variables and FGIDs were explored using univariate logistic regression analyses. Associations with a p‐value < 0.1 were subsequently evaluated in multivariable logistic regression models. Covariates were selected based on the literature and availability of the data (<5% not available within the dataset). All covariates listed in the baseline characteristics table were considered potential confounders, and the covariates with <5% missing data were added one at a time to the multivariable models assessing the association between antibiotic exposure, infantile colic, food allergies and FGIDs. Covariates were retained in the final model if their inclusion resulted in a >10% change in the β‐coefficient of the independent variable (antibiotic exposure, infantile colic or food allergy) (change‐in‐estimate criterion). To reduce the risk of overfitting, the number of covariates included in the final model was limited based on a minimum of 10 events per covariate. Complete case analysis was performed. If there were too many covariates for too few events, an adjusted odds ratio could not be obtained. p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The original study included 436 infants (151 AB+, 285 AB−). At ages 9–12, 385 (88.3%) were available for follow‐up. The FGID questionnaire was completed by 306 children (70.2% of the original cohort and 79.5% of the still available participants). GP's diagnoses were obtained from 244/306 children (79.7%, 56.0% of the original cohort) (Figure 1).

Exposure to antibiotics in the first week of life and FGIDs

At 9–12 years, the number of children with at least one FGID was not significantly different between AB+ and AB− (odds ratio [OR] 1.083, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.608–1.932) (Table 2). Regarding the subgroup of functional abdominal pain disorders (FAPDs), no difference between AB+ and AB− was found (OR 1.089, 95% CI 0.584–2.030). FAPDs consist of functional dyspepsia, irritable bowel syndrome, abdominal migraine and functional abdominal pain—not otherwise specified. None of the FGIDs in the Rome IV questionnaire showed significant differences in prevalence between the AB+ and AB− groups (Table 2).

General practitioner's diagnoses were available in 244 of 306 children (79.7%). The numbers of FGID diagnoses made within the last 9–12 years were not significantly different between the AB+ and AB− groups (Table S2).

Infantile colic and FGIDs

Children with a history of infantile colic were more likely to have an FAPD at age 9–12 in the unadjusted analysis (Table S3, OR 2.229, 95% CI 1.099–4.520). However, after adjusting for sex, this association did not reach statistical significance (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.007, 95% CI 0.978–4.003, p = 0.051). The separate FAPDs were not significantly associated with infantile colic.

Food allergy and FGIDs

Significantly more children with a food allergy also had an FGID (Table 3, OR 3.159, 95% CI 1.330–7.507), also after adjustment for relevant covariates (atopy parents): aOR 2.793, 95% CI 1.132–6.679. Additional analyses showed that this difference was only significant for FAPDs (OR 3.429, 95% CI 1.409–8.341), and the association remained after adjustment for sex (aOR 4.028, 95% CI 1.532–10.286). For the individual FGIDs, food allergies were only significantly associated with post‐prandial distress syndrome (OR 3.754, 95% CI 1.357–10.385).

DISCUSSION

In this follow‐up study of the INCA‐cohort, we found no statistically significant association between antibiotic treatment in the first week of life and FGIDs at 9–12 years of age. The second independent variable that was investigated, infantile colic, was associated with FAPDs at ages 9–12 in the unadjusted analysis, but this association was just above the conventional threshold for statistical significance after adjusting for confounders (p = 0.051). Finally, children with a food allergy were significantly more likely to also have an FAPD.

In contrast to the current follow‐up study, the previous INCA follow‐up study at 4–6 years did identify a significant association between first‐week antibiotics and functional abdominal pain. 12 This discrepancy could be attributed to the younger age of the children, which could make the diagnosis of functional abdominal pain less reliable. The capacity to vocalise feelings and location of pain is still developing between 4 and 6 years of age. 18 , 19 Possible differences in the pathogenesis of functional abdominal pain at younger versus later age or spontaneous remission later in life may also be factors. Additionally, comparability of the studies may be hampered by the use of Rome III criteria in the previous study, and Rome IV criteria in the current study. 20

Few studies have explored the relationship between gut dysbiosis and FGIDs, but existing research indicates differences in the microbiota composition between children with irritable bowel syndrome 4 , 21 or functional constipation 3 and healthy controls. These studies are highly heterogenous, and since these studies were cross‐sectional, it is impossible to determine whether dysbiosis is a cause or consequence of these FGIDs. 3 , 21 Some studies have linked microbiome disruptions, for example by caesarean section or antibiotic treatment, with the development of FGIDs, although findings are inconsistent. 3 , 12 , 13 , 22 Lastly, attempts to influence the microbiome composition using prebiotics, probiotics or synbiotics in children with FGIDs have had mixed or no effects in children with constipation 3 and some positive results in paediatric functional abdominal pain and irritable bowel syndrome. 23 In conclusion, the current evidence does not allow for a definitive answer whether microbiome composition and FGIDs are causally related.

Infantile colic has previously been associated with the development of FGIDs at a later age. 6 , 7 It has been hypothesised that this is due to common etiopathogenetic factors such as psychosocial interactions, immaturity and/or dysmotility of the gastrointestinal tract, or intestinal dysbiosis. 6 , 7 Analysis of the microbiota composition throughout the first year of life of 184 INCA participants (141 of whom participated in this follow‐up study) showed that aberrant microbiota development preceded infantile colic. 24 This seemed to be partially mediated by exposure to antibiotics in the first week of life, which was previously shown to be associated with infantile colic. 10 However, in the 4–6 year follow‐up study, infantile colic and FGIDs were no longer associated. 12 In the current follow‐up study, the association between infantile colic and the development of FAPDs just failed to reach statistical significance in the adjusted analysis. More prospective research in larger patient populations is needed to determine the true relationship between early dysbiosis, infantile colic and FGIDs at a later age.

Previous studies have explored the association between allergies and FGIDs. 1 , 5 Many children with an FGID report worsening of symptoms after ingestion of one or more specific food products, 1 , 5 , 21 but this is not always associated with an allergy. The possible link between atopic disease and FGIDs is supported by several studies showing that anti‐allergy medications may alleviate FGID symptoms in both children 5 and adults. 25 Observations of mast cell and eosinophil infiltration in the stomach, duodenum and colon of patients with FGIDs add further evidence to a role of the immune system in FGIDs. 5 , 26 Importantly, by definition, FGIDs can only be diagnosed when the symptoms cannot be fully explained by another medical condition. 1 When children experience gastrointestinal symptoms due to food or inhalant allergies, they cannot fulfil the criteria for an FGID.

This leads to a limitation of our follow‐up study: it is difficult to assess whether allergies cause the gastrointestinal symptoms in children with a (parent‐reported) food allergy and FGID, or whether gastrointestinal symptoms lead patients to believe they have an allergy. This is further complicated by the fact that food allergies in this study were parent‐reported, which can lead to overestimation of the prevalence of food allergies within the cohort. 27 Another limitation is that an online questionnaire was sent to the parents of the participating children. Parents were advised to fill out the questionnaire together with their child, as it contained some specific questions that their child may not have disclosed independently. However, we did not confirm whether parents and children completed the questionnaire together. Further, the overall prevalence of FGIDs in this INCA cohort was 20.3%, which is similar to the global average of 21.8%. 2 However, the prevalence of FAPDs in our study was 16.7%, which is higher than that of the general population (9.0%, 95% CI 6.7–12.0). 20 This could be due to selection bias; parents of children with abdominal pain could have been more likely to participate in this follow‐up study. This could affect the generalisability of our conclusions regarding FAPDs.

Strengths of this study are the large, prospective cohort of children followed for 9–12 years, with a high response rate (almost 80% of available participants; 70.2% of the original INCA cohort). Risk of bias from confounding by indication or reverse causation is reduced in our study because antibiotics were administered before the onset of any allergic or abdominal symptoms. Finally, a validated questionnaire was used to assess the prevalence of FGIDs, and children were included in four hospitals in different areas of the Netherlands. This improves the generalisability of the results.

Recommended directions for future research are to investigate the relationship between microbiome composition or dysbiosis, infantile colic, food allergy and the development of FGIDs prospectively and in larger cohorts. This may identify targets for early modification of the gut microbiome, with practical implications for preventative or therapeutic interventions. Moreover, additional studies are needed to identify which children with atopic diseases and FAPDs may benefit from anti‐allergy treatment and to explore the underlying mechanisms involved. 5

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this prospective cohort study showed no statistically significant association between antibiotic exposure in the first week of life and FGIDs at ages 9–12. Infantile colic was no longer associated with FAPDs at ages 9–12 after adjusting for confounders. FAPDs, and specifically post‐prandial distress syndrome, were associated with parent‐reported food allergies.
