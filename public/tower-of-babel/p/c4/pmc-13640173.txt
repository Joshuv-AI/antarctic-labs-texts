Chelerythrine: a novel candidate for targeting miR-21/PTEN/PI3K/AKT in nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Wenqing Zhang, Jie Liu, Huimei Chen, Lan He, Xianwen Wang, Yingchun He, Jingying Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is characterized by high metastatic potential and therapeutic resistance, necessitating the development of novel and safe therapeutic strategies. Hyperactivation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, often driven by the oncogenic microRNA-21 (miR-21) and the subsequent suppression of PTEN, plays a key role in NPC progression. Chelerythrine (CHE), a natural plant-derived alkaloid, exhibits potent anti-tumor activities; however, its precise regulatory mechanisms and translational potential in NPC remain elusive.MethodsNetwork pharmacology was employed to predict the core downstream cascades of CHE against NPC. In vitro, CCK-8, real-time cellular analysis (RTCA), flow cytometry, wound healing, and Transwell assays were utilized to evaluate the specific cytotoxicity and anti-malignant effects of CHE in normal nasopharyngeal epithelial cells (NP69) and NPC cells (5–8 F and 6-10B). RT-qPCR was used to quantify the expression of primary (pri-miR-21), precursor (pre-miR-21), and mature miR-21. In vivo, a BALB/c nude mouse xenograft model was established to systematically assess the tumor-suppressive efficacy and macroscopic/histological biosafety of CHE. Furthermore, definitive rescue experiments were conducted using a specific PI3K activator and lentivirus-mediated miR-21 modification cell lines.ResultsNetwork pharmacology highlighted the PI3K/AKT pathway as the key effector cascade of CHE. Functionally, CHE exhibited a highly favorable safety profile in NP69 cells and in vivo major organs, while significantly inhibiting NPC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and inducing apoptosis. Mechanistically, CHE synchronously suppressed the expression of pri-miR-21 and pre-miR-21, indicating a transcriptional downregulation of miR-21. This upstream suppression led to the restoration of the tumor suppressor PTEN, subsequent inactivation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, and reversal of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) phenotype. Importantly, co-treatment with a PI3K activator or lentiviral overexpression of miR-21 significantly counteracted the tumor-suppressive effects of CHE, whereas miR-21 knockdown synergistically potentiated its efficacy.ConclusionCollectively, our findings indicate that CHE is a safe and effective therapeutic candidate for NPC. Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignancy of the nasopharyngeal epithelium that imposes a substantial health burden in Southern China and Southeast Asia, where its incidence is 20–30 times the global average [1]. The anatomical complexity of the nasopharynx-particularly its proximity to critical neurovascular structures-complicates surgical resection, while nonspecific early symptoms (e.g., recurrent epistaxis, tinnitus, and nasal obstruction) often delay diagnosis until advanced stages [2, 3]. Although radiotherapy remains the cornerstone of NPC treatment, locoregional recurrence, distant metastasis, and therapeutic resistance remain major clinical challenges [4, 5]. Established risk factors for NPC include Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, genetic predisposition, and environmental factors, yet molecular mechanisms underlying its aggressive phenotype are still not fully elucidated [1, 6].

While EBV infection is a primary etiological factor initiating NPC, its oncogenic effects are heavily reliant on the downstream hyperactivation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis, a key cascade that governs cell survival, proliferation, and metastasis [7, 8]. Notably, the EBV-encoded latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) acts as a classical viral oncogene that frequently triggers this aberrant PI3K/AKT activation. Recent studies demonstrate that disregulated PI3K/AKT signaling in NPC not only drives tumor growth and EMT but also confers radioresistance by inhibiting apoptosis and enhancing DNA repair [9–12]. In addition, elevated levels of phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) correlate with advanced tumor stage and poorer prognosis in NPC patients [13].These studies underscore the PI3K/AKT pathway as a therapeutic target for NPC. Emerging evidence identifies microRNA-21 (miR-21) as a key oncogenic driver in NPC, with its overexpression correlating with advanced clinical stages and poor prognosis. Mechanistically, miR-21 facilitates tumor progression predominantly by targeting and silencing the tumor suppressor PTEN, thereby relieving the negative regulation on the PI3K/AKT pathway and unleashing its hyperactivation. This oncogenic axis is known to be further amplified by EBV/LMP1 signaling [14, 15]. Therefore, regardless of the initial viral triggers, targeting the universal downstream miR-21/PTEN/PI3K/AKT axis represents a promising strategy for the treatment of NPC metastasis.

Natural compounds, particularly plant-derived alkaloids, have attracted considerable attention due to their multitarget anticancer effects and favorable safety profiles. For instance, curcuminoids and berberine exhibit antitumor effects by modulating PI3K/AKT, NF-κB, and apoptosis-related pathways, or by modulating microRNA (miR) expression [16, 17]. These compounds not only inhibit tumor growth but also enhance sensitivity to conventional therapies. Such findings have promoted investigations into other bioactive alkaloids, including chelerythrine (CHE), for applications in precision oncology. CHE is a benzophenanthridine isolated from plants such as Chelidonium majus, exhibiting antiviral, anti-inflammatory and antitumor activities in multiple cancer types, including colorectal and gastric cancer [18, 19]. Its mechanisms include induction of mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis, suppression of metastatic phenotypes, and inhibition of pro-survival pathways such as PI3K/AKT [20]. However, its specific efficacy against NPC, its in vivo biosafety profile, and whether it can fundamentally disrupt the upstream miR-21/PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling cascade have yet to be fully elucidated. Given the dependence of NPC on PI3K/AKT signaling and the promise of natural alkaloids in cancer therapy, elucidating CHE’s impact in this context is of particular importance.

Therefore, this study combines bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation to systematically investigate the therapeutic effects of CHE on NPC cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and migration via the miR-21/PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway. Furthermore, it seeks to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects, thereby providing an experimental foundation for further research and offering theoretical support for the development of novel anti-NPC therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Cells and animals

Human NPC cell lines 5–8 F (BNCC341932) and 6-10B (BNCC359308) were purchased from Beina Chuanglian Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). To specifically investigate the metastatic cascade and the EMT process in NPC (these paired isogenic cell lines were selected: 5–8 F is characterized by high tumorigenicity and high metastatic potential, whereas 6-10B exhibits tumorigenicity but low metastatic potential). The human nasopharyngeal epithelial immortalized cell line NP69 (CC4036) was purchased from Guangzhou Saiku Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) and sub-cultured in our laboratory.

BALB/c-nu nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were purchased from slack Jingda Experimental Animal Co. Ltd., Hunan Province, China with the certificate number [430727231100426454], and were raised by the Experimental Animal Center of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine, China.

Reagents

All reagents were of analytical grade. CHE (≥ 98%, lot J16HB185135) was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. CHE was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution. For in vitro experiments, the stock solution was diluted with culture medium, ensuring the final concentration of DMSO was strictly maintained below 0.1% to avoid any solvent-induced cytotoxicity. Cisplatin (Cis; lot MKCM2435) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The PI3K activator 740 Y-P (lot HY-P0175) was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). CCK-8 reagent (lot KA259598) and the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (lot VBD53MZL3) were supplied by TargetMOI (Beijing, P.R. China) and Elabscience (Shanghai, P.R. China), respectively. Primary antibodies were sourced as follows: N-Cadherin (mouse mAb, lot GR3245174-40) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); AKT (rabbit mAb, lot AF1777) from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China); PTEN (mouse mAb, lot 9559), PI3K (mouse mAb, lot 4249), E-Cadherin (mouse mAb, lot 15), Vimentin (mouse mAb, lot 8), PCNA (mouse mAb, lot 7), and XIAP (rabbit mAb, lot 04) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA); Snail (rabbit pAb, lot 00191001), GAPDH (mouse mAb, lot 10028230), p-AKT (rabbit mAb, lot 230003107), and Bcl-2 (rabbit mAb, lot 00152084) from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA); and p-PI3K (rabbit mAb, lot #51U8853) from Affinity Biosciences (Cincinnati, OH, USA).

Network pharmacology

Targets of CHE were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database (TCMSP; https://old.tcmsp-e.com/index.php) and SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), while NPC–related targets were compiled from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org), DrugBank (https://www.drugbank.com/datasets), OMIM (https://omim.org), PharmGKB (https://www.pharmgkb.org), and the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD; https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). The overlapping targets were identified via the Venn diagram (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed with the ClusterProfiler package in R (v4.1.3).

Clinical Database and Bioinformatics Analysis

To validate the clinical relevance of miR-21 in NPC, the miRNA expression microarray dataset GSE32960 was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The dataset encompasses clinical tissue samples, including primary NPC tissues and normal nasopharyngeal epithelial tissues. Differential expression genes (DEGs) were identified using the criteria | log2 FC | >1 and P < 0.05, using the limma package. The normalized expression data specifically for hsa-miR-21 was extracted for targeted comparison. To visually present the overall differential expression landscape and highlight the significant upregulation of miR-21, a volcano plot was generated using the “ggplot2” package. The statistical thresholds for significance were predefined as an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2(Fold Change)| > 1. Furthermore, a heatmap visualizing the expression profiles of differentially expressed miRNAs, including miR-21, across individual clinical samples was constructed using the “pheatmap” package.

RT-qPCR

Following conventional digestion, the resulting single-cell suspension was centrifuged, the supernatant discarded, and total RNA extracted. A 800 ng of total RNA was used for reverse transcription. RT-qPCR amplification was performed with miR-21 as the target gene and GAPDH as the reference gene. Relative miR-21 expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, where ΔCt = Ct(target) – Ct(reference), ΔΔCt = ΔCt(experimental) – ΔCt(control), and relative expression = 2⁻ΔΔCT. The amplification procedure was as follows: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 50 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and ending with the melt curve procedure. The specific primers for U6, primary miR-21 (pri-miR-21), and precursor miR-21 (pre-miR-21) were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST and Primer3 software based on sequences obtained from the NCBI and miRBase databases. The forward primer for mature miR-21 was designed to exactly match the mature hsa-miR-21-5p sequence, followed by amplification with a universal reverse primer. miR-21 lentivirus transfection

The miR-21 lentivirus was designed and synthesized by Shanghai Genechem Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, P.R.C.). 5–8 F cells were prepared as a single-cell suspension and adjusted to a concentration of to 3 × 104 cells/mL. The multiplicity of infection (MOI) was determined through preliminary experiments in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, 1 mL of the cell suspension was seeded into a 6-well plate, and lentivirus infection was performed after cell adhesion. The volume of lentivirus was calculated according to the viral titer, and the culture medium was replaced after 24 h. RT-qPCR verified the infection efficiency.

Cell Counting Kit-8(CCK8) assay

Cells in a good growth state were collected and resuspended as a single cell suspension at 5 × 104 cells/mL. Then, 100 µL of cell suspension was added per well. After cell adherence, the drug-containing medium was added for 24 h, 36 h and 48 h. At the end of each treatment interval, the culture medium was removed and 100 µL of CCK-8 working solution was added to each well. After incubation for 2 h at 37 ℃, the absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme maker. The relative proliferation rate was calculated as follows: (absorbance of the experimental group – absorbance of the blank group)/ (absorbance of the control group – absorbance of the blank group).

Real-time cellular analysis (RTCA) technology

A total of 50 µL of complete culture medium was added to the special culture plate for RTCA to measure the baseline. Cells in the logarithmic growth period were gathered and adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 104 cells/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of the cell suspension is seeded into each well. After setting the parameters, the plate was allowed to stand for 30 min before initiating real-time monitoring. Drug treatment was applied before the cell growth reached, and monitoring continued for 48 h. Cell proliferation was evaluated on the cell index (CI), and the relative proliferation rate was calculated after normalization. Proliferation rate (fixed base ratio) was defined as: (Normalized CI at x h/ Normalized CI value at 0 h) – 1. And the relative proliferation rate was calculated as: (proliferation rate of the experimental group/proliferation rate of the control group) × 100%.

Annexin V-FITC/PI staining

Cells in the logarithmic growth period were transferred into a dish. Following 24 h of drug intervention, cells were digested, collected, and washed twice with PBS, then centrifuged to achieve cell precipitation. The pellet was resuspended in 50 µL of binding buffer, and 5 µL each of annexin V-FITC and PI were added to each EP tube. After mixing, the samples were reacted in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Finally, 50 µL of binding buffer was added to terminate the staining reaction and the apoptosis level of each group was assessed using a dual fluorescence cell analyzer.

Wound healing

After conventional digestion, the cell concentration was adjusted to 1 × 105 cells/mL. When the cells had grown fully, a rectangular blank area was created using a negative-pressure suction device. The floating cells were washed with PBS, and 1 mL of serum-free medium containing varying concentrations of CH was added to each well. Then, plates were placed in the CytotionTm 5 Cell Imaging Multifunctional Detection System. Cell migration was monitored for 36 h and the relative mobility was calculated as follows: migration area of drug group/migration area of control group × 100%.

Transwell assay

50 µL of matrigel diluted with serum-free medium was evenly spread in the upper chamber of a Transwell insert and allowed to solidify for 2 h. After routine digestion, cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and adjusted to a concentration of 2 × 104 cells/mL. Then, 100 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while 800 µL of complete culture medium was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. Once the cells adhered to the wall, different concentrations of drugs were applied and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 1% crystal violet for 10 min, and imaged using the Cytation™ 5 Cell Imaging Multi- Functional Detection System.

Establishment of xenograft model in nude mice

Four-week-old SPF BALB/c nude mice were randomly assigned to each group. The mice were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. After one week of adaptive feeding, 100 µL of treated NPC cells (approximately 5 × 106 cells) were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla. When the tumor reached a diameter of 0.5 cm, the mice were randomly allocated into diverse treatment groups (n = 6 mice per group) using a random number generator to ensure unbiased assignment. After successful modeling, mice in the control group received daily intraperitoneal injections of 0.1 mL saline. The CHE group received daily 0.1 mL of CHE (25 mg kg− 1) intraperitoneally and the cisplatin group received 0.1 mL of cisplatin (10 µg kg− 1) every two days. To minimize observer bias, the measurement of tumor volumes and the subsequent histological evaluations were performed by an investigator who was blinded to the treatment group assignments. Tumor volume (V) was calculated as: a × b2/2 (where a and b represents the longest and shortest diameter of the tumor, respectively). Drug administration was lasted for three weeks. At study endpoint, mice were euthanized by under isoflurane anesthesia, and the tumors were removed for further analysis.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining

After dewaxing and hydrating with xylene and gradient ethanol (anhydrous ethanol, 95%, 85%, 75%), the samples were stained with hematoxylin for 3 min, differentiated with differentiation solution for 30 s, and counterstained with lithium carbonate for 2 min followed by eosin for 5 min. The sections were then dehydrated through descending concentrations of ethanol (75%, 85%, 95%, 100%), cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

Immunohistochemical staining

The paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were sectioned (a thickness of 4 μm). After dewaxing and hydrating with xylene and gradient ethanol (anhydrous ethanol, 95%, 85%, 75%), endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 10 min. For antigen retrieval, the sections were immersed in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) and heated under high temperature (121℃) and pressure for 3 min, followed by PBS rinsing. Sections were then blocked with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4 ℃, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 min. Immunoreactivity was visualized by the dropwise addition of 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic solution. Subsequently, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated through a reverse gradient ethanol series (75%, 85%, 95%, anhydrous ethanol). cleared in xylene, and sealed with neutral gum for imaging. Optical density was obtained by Image-pro Plus 6.0 software analysis, and the relative protein was calculated as: optical density of experimental histone/optical density of the control group.

Western blotting

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into culture dishes, treated with the drug, and lysed for protein extraction. Protein was quantified using a BCA assay. Samples containing 80 µg protein in 25 µL were denatured at 100 °C for 10 min and subjected to SDS-PAGE, followed by membrane transfer. Membranes were blocked with skimmed milk, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C (p-PI3K, p-AKT, PI3K, AKT, PTEN, PCNA, XIAP, Bcl2, Snail, E-cadherin, N-cadherin and vimentin diluted at 1:1000, GAPDH diluted at 1:5000), and with secondary antibodies in the dark for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized and analyzed using the Odyssey dual-color infrared imaging system.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 24.0 software. All in vitro experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates. For in vivo studies, each group consisted of 6 mice. Data are presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied when data were normally distributed with equal variances; Welch’s t-test was used when variances were unequal; and the Mann–Whitney U test was used when normality was violated. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied when assumptions of normality and variance homogeneity were met; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All statistical tests were two-sided. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and Adobe Illustrator 2022. Detailed sample sizes and replicate numbers are specified in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

CHE inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis of NPC cells in vitro

To initially evaluate the anti-tumor potential of CHE against NPC in vitro, we first assessed its specific cytotoxicity and effects on cell viability. Importantly, prior to examining its anti-tumor efficacy, a CCK-8 assay was performed on the normal human nasopharyngeal epithelial immortalized cell line NP69 to determine the safety profile of CHE. The results demonstrated that varying concentrations of CHE exhibited minimal cytotoxicity to NP69 cells after 24 h of intervention (Fig. 1A), thereby establishing a acceptable therapeutic window. Subsequently, CCK8 assay and real-time cellular analysis (RTCA) revealed that CHE inhibited the proliferation of NPC (5–8 F and 6-10B) cells at 24, 36, and 48 h post-treatment (Fig. 1B-C). Furthermore, the relative proliferation rate in CHE-treated groups was significantly lower than that in the control group (Fig. 1D-G).

(B-G) CHE suppresses the proliferative activity of NPC cells (5–8 F and 6-10B), as monitored by CCK-8 at 24, 36, and 48 h and RTCA assays at 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. (H-K) Flow cytometric analysis using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining reveals that CHE treatment induces NPC cell apoptosis. Given that the decline in cell viability is often accompanied by cell death, we subsequently evaluated the apoptosis of NPC cells. Annexin V-FITC/PI staining demonstrated that CHE treatment for 24 h significantly induced NPC cell apoptosis. Specifically, at a concentration of 2.5 µmol/L, the apoptosis rates of 6-10B and 5–8 F cells were 7.55 ± 0.045% and 12.63 ± 2.21%, respectively ; at 5.0 µmol/L, these rates increased to 18.84 ± 0.70% and 14.77 ± 0.70% (Fig. 1H-K). Mechanistically, western blot analysis revealed that CHE at 2.5 and 5.0 µmol/L reduced the protein expression of XIAP, PCNA, and Bcl2 in both cell lines (Fig. 1L-P).

CHE significantly attenuates the migration and invasion capacities of NPC cells in vitro

Because high invasiveness and early metastasis are major clinical challenges in NPC, we further investigated whether CHE could attenuate the metastatic potential of 5–8 F and 6-10B cells. As shown in the wound healing assay, CHE treatment for 24 and 36 h suppressed the migration of both NPC cell lines compared with the control group (Fig. 2A-D). Additionally, Transwell assays demonstrated that CHE significantly reduced the number of invading cells and the invasion rate in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2E-F).

CHE attenuates the migration and invasion capacities of NPC cells in vitro. (A-D) Wound healing assays show that CHE intervention for 24 and 36 h attenuates the migratory capacity of 5–8 F and 6-10B cells. (E-F) Transwell assays reveal a reduction in the number of invading NPC cells following CHE treatment. (G-L) Western blot analyses show that CHE suppresses the EMT process by upregulating the epithelial marker E-cadherin while suppressing the mesenchymal markers Snail, N-cadherin, and vimentin. To further explore the underlying molecular changes, western blot analysis was performed to detect the expression of EMT markers. The results revealed that CHE treatment for 24 h downregulated the expression of mesenchymal markers, including Snail, N-cadherin and vimentin, while upregulating the epithelial marker E-cadherin (Fig. 2G-L). Collectively, these findings suggest that CHE exerts its anti-metastatic effects by inhibiting the EMT process in NPC cells.

CHE suppresses the growth and metastatic potential of NPC xenografts in vivo

To further validate the therapeutic efficacy of CHE against NPC in vivo, we established a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model in BALB/c nude mice using the 5–8 F NPC cell line. Following 16 days of drug intervention, macroscopic observation and measurements revealed that both the tumor volume and the excised tumor weight in the CHE-treated group were significantly suppressed compared with the control group (Fig. 3A-B). Consistent with these macroscopic findings, histological evaluation via Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 3F) confirmed the anti-tumor activity of CHE. In the control group, tumor cells were densely arranged with large, hyperchromatic nuclei, exhibiting typical malignant atypia and vigorous mitotic figures. In contrast, the tumor tissue in the CHE-treated group exhibited a looser structure with extensive areas of cellular necrosis, accompanied by typical morphological hallmarks of apoptosis, such as distinct nuclear pyknosis and karyorrhexis.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which CHE intervenes in tumor biological behaviors in vivo, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining to detect key executioner proteins in situ within the tumor sections. Highly consistent with the in vitro findings, IHC results demonstrated that CHE treatment significantly downregulated the nuclear expression of the pro-proliferative protein PCNA, as well as the cytoplasmic expression of anti-apoptotic proteins (XIAP and Bcl-2) (Fig. 4A-C, H) in tumor tissues. Furthermore, regarding metastasis-associated proteins, CHE intervention suppressed the nuclear accumulation of Snail, the membranous localization of N-cadherin, and the cytoplasmic distribution of vimentin, while simultaneously upregulating the membranous expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin (Fig. 4D-G, I).

Crucially, alongside this anti-tumor efficacy, CHE exhibited a favorable in vivo biosafety profile. Throughout the intervention period, continuous monitoring revealed no significant body weight loss in the CHE-treated mice compared to the control group (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the evaluation of vital organ indices demonstrated no significant differences in the liver index across all groups. The kidney index in the CHE-treated group was essentially identical to that of the control group; notably, the cisplatin-treated positive control group exhibited a slight, albeit non-significant, increase in the kidney index, hinting at the well-documented renal burden associated with conventional platinum-based chemotherapy (Fig. 3D-E). Importantly, this systemic safety was corroborated at the histological level via H&E staining of major organs. Consistent with the macroscopic organ index data, the liver and kidney tissues from the CHE-treated mice displayed normal morphological architecture without any evident signs of hepatocellular degeneration, inflammatory cell infiltration, or renal tubular necrosis (Fig. 3G-H).

Taken together, these in vivo data indicate that CHE not only effectively inhibits the growth of NPC xenografts by inducing tissue necrosis and apoptosis, but also hinders the EMT process within the living tumor microenvironment. Notably, these potent therapeutic effects are achieved with favorable systemic safety, highlighting CHE’s translational potential.

CHE exerts anti-tumor effects in NPC by suppressing the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

To systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-NPC effects of CHE, network pharmacology analysis was initially conducted. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses based on the predicted targets highlighted the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 5A-C), suggesting it as a key cascade mediating CHE’s efficacy.

PI3K/AKT pathway is a downstream target of CHE. (D-J) Both in vitro Western blot (NPC cells) and in vivo IHC staining (xenograft tissues) show reduced phosphorylation levels of PI3K (p‑PI3K) and AKT (p‑AKT) following CHE treatment. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent biological replicates (n = 3). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 compared with the Control group

Guided by this prediction, we experimentally evaluated the activation status of the PI3K/AKT cascade. In vitro Western blot analyses demonstrated that CHE treatment significantly inhibited the protein levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT in NPC cells. Consistently, in vivo IHC staining further confirmed the suppression of the cytoplasmic expression of p-PI3K and p-AKT within the tumor tissues (Fig. 5D-J). To further verify that the PI3K/AKT axis is the functional target of CHE, a rescue experiment was performed using a specific PI3K activator. Notably, co-treatment with the PI3K activator restored the p-PI3K and p-AKT levels that were originally suppressed by CHE (Fig. 4K-L). These data support the PI3K/AKT pathway as a key downstream effector of CHE in NPC.

CHE relieves PTEN suppression by transcriptionally downregulating miR-21

Given that the tumor suppressor PTEN is a classical negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway, we next investigated its response to CHE. Western blot analysis revealed that CHE treatment significantly upregulated PTEN expression in NPC cells (Fig. 6A-B). This effect was recapitulated in vivo, as CHE administration significantly elevated PTEN expression in xenograft tumors derived from nude mice (Fig. 6C, E). Because PTEN is a well-documented direct target of the oncogenic microRNA miR-21, we hypothesized that CHE modulates the miR-21/PTEN axis.

To test this, we first analyzed public GEO datasets, which revealed that miR-21 is aberrantly upregulated in NPC clinical samples (Fig. 6D, F). Consistent with this, our in vitro qRT-PCR data showed higher miR-21 baseline expression in NPC cell lines (5–8 F and 6-10B) compared to the normal epithelial cell line NP69 (Fig. 6G). Notably, CHE intervention led to a downregulation of mature miR-21 levels in vivo (Fig. 6H). To precisely map the stage at which CHE disrupts miR-21 biogenesis, we quantified its primary and precursor transcripts. CHE synchronously suppressed the expression of pri-miR-21 and pre-miR-21 (Fig. 6I-J). Collectively, these findings provide evidence that rather than interfering with RNA processing, CHE exerts its inhibitory effect at the transcriptional level, thereby suppressing miR-21 to restore PTEN expression and subsequently inactivate the PI3K/AKT cascade.

CHE inhibits NPC progression by targeting the miR-21/PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling axis

To definitively establish whether miR-21 mediates the anti-NPC efficacy of CHE, we constructed miR-21-overexpressing (OE-miR-21) and knockdown (sh-miR-21) cell lines. The transfection efficiency was verified via RT-qPCR (Fig. 7A-C). Functionally, compared with the OE-NC group, miR-21 overexpression significantly enhanced NPC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while suppressing apoptosis. Conversely, miR-21 knockdown exerted the opposite inhibitory effects. Notably, while CHE treatment alone restricted these malignant phenotypes and promoted apoptosis, these therapeutic effects were significantly attenuated by miR-21 overexpression (OE-miR-21 + CHE vs. OE-NC + CHE). In contrast, miR-21 silencing further enhanced the anti-tumor capacity of CHE (sh-miR-21 + CHE vs. sh-NC + CHE) (Fig. 7D-Q).

We subsequently evaluated the molecular alterations corresponding to these phenotypic rescues. At the protein level, miR-21 overexpression upregulated pro-proliferative/anti-apoptotic markers (XIAP, PCNA, Bcl2) and mesenchymal markers (Snail, N-cadherin, vimentin), while concomitantly decreasing the epithelial marker E-cadherin. As expected, miR-21 knockdown reversed these expression patterns. Consistent with our aforementioned findings, CHE intervention suppressed the expression of XIAP, PCNA, Bcl2, Snail, N-cadherin, and vimentin, but upregulated E-cadherin. Notably, the CHE-induced modulation of these functional proteins was effectively counteracted by miR-21 overexpression and further augmented by miR-21 knockdown. Importantly, these in vitro expression profiles were faithfully recapitulated in the xenograft tumor models.

Finally, we examined the PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling axis to complete the mechanistic loop. As a well-documented tumor suppressor and direct target of miR-21, PTEN expression was suppressed by miR-21 overexpression and elevated by its knockdown. Consequently, miR-21 overexpression indirectly facilitated the activation of the downstream cascade, elevating p-PI3K and p-AKT levels, whereas miR-21 knockdown suppressed their activation. In the context of drug intervention, the characteristic upregulation of PTEN and the concomitant suppression of p-PI3K/p-AKT induced by CHE were reversed upon miR-21 overexpression in NPC cells (OE-miR-21 + CHE vs. OE-NC + CHE) (Fig. 8).

Collectively, these data provide evidence that CHE suppresses the malignant progression of NPC by negatively regulating the miR-21/PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling axis.

Discussion

Previous studies have identified various agents targeting the PI3K/AKT pathway in NPC. For instance, the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 inhibits NPC cell proliferation and migration by blocking PI3K/AKT signaling activation [21]. Likewise, natural compounds such as curcumin and curcumol have also been shown to impede NPC progression by downregulating miR-21 expression [22, 23].However, compared with synthetic single-target inhibitors, plant-derived bioactive alkaloids often exhibit favorable safety profiles and multi-target synergistic effects. The antitumor efficacy of CHE, the focus of this study, has been demonstrated across multiple cancer types—including hepatocellular carcinoma [20], non-small cell lung cancer [24], esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [25], cervical cancer [26], and prostate cancer [27]-via suppression of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and other mechanisms [28]. However, its precise efficacy and underlying mechanisms in NPC remain unclear.

To address this, we first employed network pharmacology to predict CHE’s potential targets in NPC. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses implicated the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as a key mechanism of CHE’s antitumor effects. We then validated these predictions through in vitro and in vivo experiments: CHE inhibited NPC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion; induced apoptosis; and suppressed tumor growth in xenograft models. Importantly, our findings demonstrate that CHE significantly modulates a panel of key functional proteins, which directly correlates with the primary clinical challenges in NPC management. Specifically, the downregulation of XIAP and Bcl-2, critical anti-apoptotic factors often associated with therapeutic escape [29], suggests that CHE may serve as a potent sensitizer to overcome the intrinsic radioresistance frequently encountered in clinical practice. Furthermore, the observed reversal of the EMT phenotype, characterized by the upregulation of E-cadherin and the suppression of N-cadherin and vimentin [30], provides a molecular explanation for CHE’s ability to inhibit the aggressive invasive behavior. To establish the dependency of these effects on the PI3K/AKT axis, we introduced a specific PI3K activator in our rescue experiments. The results showed that pharmacological reactivation of PI3K attenuated the anti-tumor effects of CHE, confirming that the PI3K/AKT cascade is indeed the key downstream effector of CHE. By targeting these key executioners of survival and metastasis, CHE demonstrates potential for clinical translation as an adjunctive therapy to standard NPC treatments [31].

While the downstream suppression of PI3K/AKT by CHE was confirmed, we further investigated the upstream oncogenic drivers. As a critical oncogenic regulator, miR-21 drives tumor progression predominantly by targeting and silencing the tumor suppressor PTEN, thereby relieving the negative regulation on the PI3K/AKT pathway (Fig. 9) [32, 33]. Studies have demonstrated that EBV-encoded LMP1 upregulates miR-21, driving an oncogenic cascade that confers therapeutic resistance in NPC [10, 34]. Consistent with this, our in vitro models demonstrated that miR-21 overexpression suppressed PTEN and upregulated p-PI3K and p-AKT, whereas miR-21 knockdown produced the opposite effects. This confirms the miR-21/PTEN/PI3K/AKT axis as a pivotal driver of NPC progression.

Overview for effects of miR-21/PTEN/PI3K/AKT on nasopharyngeal carcinoma

Given the essential role of this axis, we investigated CHE’s specific regulatory effects. Notably, our study provides novel insights into the upstream molecular intervention by CHE. Our qRT-PCR data revealed that CHE synchronously and profoundly suppressed the expression of primary (pri-miR-21), precursor (pre-miR-21), and mature miR-21. This indicates that rather than interfering with RNA processing or Dicer/Drosha enzymatic cleavage, CHE fundamentally exerts its inhibitory effect at the transcriptional level. Consequently, this transcriptional silencing of miR-21 restores PTEN expression, ultimately leading to the inactivation of the downstream PI3K/AKT cascade. Through bidirectional rescue experiments, we demonstrated that miR-21 overexpression reversed CHE’s suppression of malignant behaviors and PI3K/AKT activation, validating that CHE’s efficacy is dependent on miR-21 downregulation.

While the inhibitory role of CHE in NPC malignancy as a monotherapy is well-supported by our findings, exploring its combinatorial potential represents a key avenue for future research. The clinical management of NPC heavily relies on radiotherapy and platinum-based chemotherapy (e.g., cisplatin), which frequently face challenges such as dose-limiting toxicities and acquired resistance [35]. To contextualize its translational potential, we comparatively evaluated CHE alongside cisplatin. Both CHE and cisplatin exhibited tumor-suppressive efficacy in the xenograft models. While the clinical application of cisplatin is limited by its systemic toxicities, such as nephrotoxicity and significant body weight loss. Indeed, our in vivo evaluations confirmed that cisplatin intervention led to weight loss and renal histological alterations in the nude mice. In contrast, CHE exhibited a more favorable in vivo safety profile without inducing such adverse effects. Because our data demonstrate that CHE significantly downregulates key anti-apoptotic proteins and reverses the EMT phenotype with an favorable in vivo safety profile, we hypothesize that CHE could serve as a chemosensitizer or radiosensitizer. Furthermore, given that hyperactivation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a known driver of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [36], it is reasonable to hypothesize that CHE-mediated suppression of this axis may remodel the TME—for instance, by reducing the infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) or promoting the recruitment and activation of cytotoxic T cells. Such immunomodulatory effects could significantly synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1 antibodies) to overcome acquired resistance in NPC [37–39]. Therefore, comprehensive in vivo combination studies are warranted.

Despite these translational perspectives, several limitations in the current study must be acknowledged. First, regarding the NPC-specific viral etiology, our mechanistic investigations of CHE were conducted exclusively in EBV-negative NPC cell models (5–8 F and 6-10B). Given that EBV-encoded LMP1 is a known upstream activator of the miR-21 axis [40] ,whether CHE directly interferes with LMP1-driven oncogenic signaling or specific viral-miRNA interactions remains unexplored. Future studies employing EBV-positive models (e.g., C666-1) or LMP1-overexpressing systems are warranted to map the NPC-specific anti-tumor profile of CHE. Second, it is important to note that we evaluated the in vivo efficacy of CHE using a subcutaneous xenograft model in immunodeficient nude mice. While this model effectively confirmed the suppression of tumor growth and the alteration of EMT markers by CHE, it lacks an intact immune system and does not fully represent the complex process of distant metastasis. Therefore, future studies utilizing orthotopic NPC models, experimental metastasis models (e.g., tail vein injection), and immunocompetent syngeneic mouse models are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the anti-metastatic and immunomodulatory potentials of CHE in a physiological tumor microenvironment. Third, the direct correlation between CHE sensitivity and patient clinical parameters remains to be verified using patient-derived xenografts (PDX) or primary NPC tissue microarrays. Finally, while our preliminary in vivo evaluations—including continuous body weight monitoring and detailed histological examinations of major organs—indicate a highly favorable morphological biosafety profile, the precise quantitative assessment of hepatic and renal functions via specific serum biochemical markers (such as ALT, AST, and creatinine) was not performed in the current cohort. Therefore, comprehensive systemic toxicity profiling, incorporating detailed hematological and biochemical panels, is required in future IND-enabling studies.

In summary, this study delineates the mechanism by which CHE suppresses NPC proliferation, migration, and invasion through the transcriptional downregulation of miR-21, thereby restoring PTEN and inactivating the PI3K/AKT axis. The integrated strategies proposed herein will be important for translating CHE from a promising preclinical candidate into a clinically viable therapy, ultimately offering additional treatment options for NPC patients.
