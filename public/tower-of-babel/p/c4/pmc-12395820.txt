Significant correlation between ferroptosis-associated genes and ulcerative colitis: implications for diagnosis and treatment
Authors: Kai Zhang, Kailing Wang, Chengzhao Weng, Dalong Sun, Miao Ouyang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUlcerative colitis (UC) is a significant type of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Ferroptosis is a type of procedural death that is associated with different types of diseases to various degrees. This study aimed to explore the key ferroptosis-associated genes in UC and assess their potential implications for biological therapy.MethodsBioinformatic prediction combined with experimental verification were applied to this study for the correlation between ferroptosis and UC. The Gene Expression Omnibus database was used to obtain disease-related gene expression data and then the identified genes expression were validated using Dextran Sulfate Sodium Salt (DSS) induced mouse colitis model. Next, the infiltration of immune cells was compared between UC and normal groups, and the correlation between different immune cells and the identified genes was analyzed.ResultsIn this study, 91 ferroptosis-associated genes associated with UC were identified. Four genes (TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44) were captured with diagnostic values among them, whose expression in DSS-induced mouse colitis was higher than control tissues. Besides, the expression levels of these four genes in the biological agents responsive group showed a distinction from those in active UC patients. Immune infiltration analysis revealed that there were positive or negative correlations between four genes with neutrophils, M0 and M1 macrophages, B cells, and NK cells.ConclusionsThis study clearly indicates that immune cell infiltration in UC patients is associated with ferroptosis. The key genes associated with ferroptosis may have diagnostic value and predict therapeutic effects, which also can be helpful for discovering the molecular mechanism of UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03014-3.

Background

Ulcerative colitis (UC) occurs in the colon and rectum that is an idiopathic, chronic, and recurrent inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [1, 2]. Over recent decades, UC has emerged as a global health concern, with its incidence increasing worldwide [3]. Despite extensive research, the exact pathogenesis of UC remains incompletely understood. Therefore, it is important to further explore its etiology and pathogenesis for better diagnosis and treatment.

Ferroptosis, a type of iron-dependent, non-apoptotic regulated cell death, was first described in 2012 [4]. It is primarily driven by iron imbalance and lipid peroxidation, leading to oxidative stress within cells, which impacts the metabolic pathways of proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and lipids, ultimately resulting in cell death [5]. An increasing number of research suggests that ferroptosis also plays a role in the etiology of UC and may contribute to UC progression by modulating immune cell infiltration [6–8]. Recent evidence has found that blocking ferroptosis significantly relieves UC symptoms and promotes intestinal repair, which may be the potential biomarkers as pharmacological therapeutic targets or predict response to biologic therapy [9]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which ferroptosis influences UC are not yet fully elucidated and there is currently a lack of systematic analysis of the relationship between ferroptosis and UC, particularly regarding its association with clinical treatment responses.

In this study, we integrated UC datasets from the GEO database with ferroptosis-related genes from FerrDB for bioinformatics analysis, aiming to identify potential biomarkers. We examined the relationship between ferroptosis and the therapeutic efficacy of commonly used UC biologic drugs, which seeks to elucidate the pharmacological mechanisms and optimize personalized therapy for active UC.

Methods

Data acquisition

Gene expression data and associated clinical information were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). To mitigate batch effects across different datasets, the “sva” package in R was employed.

Ferroptosis-related genes were obtained from FerrDb, the first database dedicated to ferroptosis regulators and their associations with diseases, compiled from peer-reviewed publications [10].

Analysis of differential gene expression

The limma package facilitated the analysis of differential gene expression. Genes that satisfied criteria of p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)|> 1 were classified as differentially expressed genes. The "pheatmap" package in R was utilized to create heat maps illustrating these genes.

Ferroptosis correlation and Venn analysis

Venn diagrams were generated to identify genes common to both differentially expressed genes and ferroptosis-associated genes. Heat maps for these intersecting genes were constructed using R software.

Functional enrichment analysis

The "clusterProfiler" package was used to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses on genes linked with ferroptosis, aiming to explore the pathomechanisms underlying active Ulcerative Colitis (UC).

Machine learning approaches for identifying Ferroptosis biomarkers in UC

To pinpoint ferroptosis-related biomarkers in UC, machine learning techniques including the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) were applied. Genes identified by both methods were preliminarily recognized as potential ferroptosis-related biomarkers.

Immune-related factors and immune cell infiltration

The “CIBERSORT” algorithm was utilized to estimate the proportions of immune cells infiltrating UC and healthy control (HC) samples [11]. Additionally, a Spearman's correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationships between ferroptosis-related biomarkers and the levels of immune cells.

Experimental animals

Eight-week-old wild-type C57BL/6 mice were procured from Hunan Slake Jingda Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. and housed at the Central South University's experimental animal facility. All protocols involving animal experiments and euthanasia were conducted following the ethical guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at Central South University. (Approve No. 2023030148).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (AG, China), adhering to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop™ instrument. For complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, 0.2 μg of RNA was used with the Evo M-MLV RT Kit (AG, China). PCR amplification was conducted using SYBR Green PCR Mastermix Kit (AG, China) in a QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR system. Quantitative analysis was based on the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Primers were synthesized by Sangon (Shanghai, China). Details of the primer sequences used are provided in Table 1. Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (NCM, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors (NCM, China) in a 100:1 ratio, following methodologies described in earlier studies [12]. The primary antibodies used were TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44. Proteins were separated by electrophoresis on precast gels (ACE Biotechnology, China) and subsequently transferred onto Immobilon-P membranes (Ireland). The membranes were blocked using 5% non-fat milk powder and incubated with primary antibodies including CD44 (AF01783, AiFang Biological, 1:1000), TIMP1 (YE3853116, Invitrogen, 1:1000), LPIN1 (13A230818, Abiowell, 1:1000), SOCS1 (AF03521, AiFang Biological, 1:1000), and β-actin (ab8227, Abcam, 1:5000) used as a loading control. Detection was achieved using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abiowell, China) following the manufacturer's protocol.

Hematoxylin–Eosin staining (HE) and immunohistochemical staining (IHC)

Immunohistochemistry for TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44 was conducted according to the provided manufacturers' guidelines. Colonic tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Abiowell, China), and embedded in paraffin. Sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol (100%, 95%, and 75%). HE staining was staining with hematoxylin for 4 min, differentiate with hydrochloric acid ethanol for 3 s, and finally stain with eosin for 1 min. Antigen retrieval of IHC was performed by heating the sections in EDTA at 95 °C for 10 min. After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibodies, followed by application of horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Visualization was achieved with diaminobenzidine and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Statistical analysis

All data of bioinformatics were analyzed and visualized using R software, version 4.1.3. The t-test was employed to determine the differences in expression levels of hub genes between UC and HC samples. Statistical analyses were conducted in triplicate or more, and results were expressed as means and standard deviations. Correlation analysis were calculated using Spearman analysis. Statistical comparisons of animal experiments data were performed using t-test in Graphpad Pism 9.5. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 denoting varying levels of significance.

KEGG and GO enrichment analysis of overlapping genes

The KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the ferroptosis-related DEGs predominantly participate in several pathways including the HIF-1 signaling pathway, the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, the PPAR signaling pathway, ferroptosis, and fatty acid metabolism (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). GO enrichment analysis showed that in the category of biological processes, these DEGs were associated with processes such as temperature homeostasis, response to oxidative stress, response to peptide hormones, cellular response to chemical stress, response to nutrient levels, and fatty acid metabolic process. Concerning cellular components, enrichment was observed in structures like the organelle outer membrane, microbody, peroxisome, apical plasma membrane, NADPH oxidase complex, and the apical part of the cell. In terms of molecular functions, the identified genes were enriched in functions including nuclear receptor activity, heme binding, pentosyltransferase activity, and oxidoreductase activity, which involves acting on paired donors with incorporation or reduction of molecular oxygen, as well as NAD + ADP-ribosyltransferase activity and protein ADP-ribosylase activity (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2E-F).

Identification of Ferroptosis-associated biomarkers in UC using machine learning

The expression data from 91 ferroptosis-associated DEGs were utilized to develop an optimal diagnostic model employing both LASSO regression and the SVM-RFE algorithms, aiming to pinpoint potential diagnostic biomarkers for UC. The LASSO regression approach refined the list of ferroptosis-associated DEGs, identifying 11 candidates as potential biomarkers for UC (Fig. 3A-B). Concurrently, the SVM-RFE algorithm highlighted 19 signature genes (Fig. 3C-D). The hub ferroptosis genes effectively modulated the immune micro-environment and inflammatory infiltration in colon during UC

Our research included immune infiltration analysis of 81 UC cases and 19 healthy controls. This analysis was performed using the CIBERSORT algorithm to examine 22 types of immune cells. We identified significant differences in the distribution of 11 cell types among the groups (Fig. 3F), including memory B cells, activated memory CD4 + T cells, resting NK cells, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, resting and activated dendritic cells, resting and activated mast cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils. Furthermore, we explored potential correlations among all 22 cell types. The correlation analysis revealed that TIMP1 positively correlates with monocytes, neutrophils, resting NK cells, and resting memory CD4 + T cells, but negatively correlates with M1 macrophages. SOCS1 shows positive correlations with activated dendritic cells, M1 macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils, resting NK cells, and activated memory CD4 + T cells, and a negative correlation with plasma cells. LPIN1 negatively correlates with M2 macrophages. CD44 is positively correlated with activated dendritic cells, neutrophils, and activated memory CD4 + T cells, yet shows negative correlations with M2 macrophages, activated NK cells, and plasma cells (Fig. 3G).

The hub correlated strongly with disease activity and drug efficacy in UC

We assessed the expression levels of four central genes in an additional dataset sourced from GEO to confirm their roles as biomarkers in UC. In dataset GSE73661, which examines the impact of infliximab (IFX) and vedolizumab (VDZ) therapies on colonic mucosal gene expression, the expression levels of these genes were elevated in active UC patients compared to healthy controls. Following IFX therapy, the expression levels of TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44 in the IFX-responsive group showed a distinction from those in active UC patients, yet remained above those observed in the healthy controls (Fig. 4A-D). Experimental validation in a mouse model of UC

A schematic of the UC mouse model is depicted, with the mice being sacrificed on day 7 (Fig. 5A). Significant differences were observed in the body weight of each group. Notably, there was a marked reduction in body weight following treatment with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) (p < 0.001 compared to the control group) (Fig. 5B). Colon length, a crucial marker of colon inflammation, was considerably shorter in the DSS-treated mice compared to controls (Fig. 5C). HE staining revealed that while the colonic mucosa of control mice maintained intact epithelial cells and abundant goblet cells without any signs of atrophy, deformation, necrosis, or inflammatory infiltration, the DSS-treated mice exhibited a disrupted normal pathological structure, destroyed crypt architecture, a significant reduction or absence of goblet cells, and infiltration by inflammatory cells in the mucosa and submucosa (Fig. 5E). Discussion

Ulcerative colitis (UC), a chronic inflammatory bowel disease, is distinguished by symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, blood in the stool, and weight loss. Although there have been significant advancements in UC treatment, the quest for novel therapeutic approaches remains urgent. Ferroptosis, a recently recognized form of regulated cell death (RCD) marked by iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and escalated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, has been implicated in both the onset and progression of UC [9, 13]. In our research, we pinpointed four genes shown potential associations with Ferroptosis regulatory pathways, which have shown strong positive correlations with active UC. Notably, TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44 emerge as potential new regulators of UC, presenting promising targets for developing treatments for the active phase of this disease. Moreover, the credibility of bioinformatic analyses was reinforced by data from external datasets and experimental validations, offering fresh insights into UC's pathogenesis and therapeutic strategies.

This study revealed that the group with active ulcerative colitis (UC) demonstrated heightened activation of various immune cells compared to the normal control group. Analysis of KEGG and GO terms associated with DEGs indicated that these genes predominantly enrich pathways related to fatty acid metabolism, response to oxidative stress, cellular reaction to chemical stress, and oxidoreductase activity. Notably, fatty acid metabolism (FAM) is essential for the proliferation and function of immune cells [14]. Research on the connection between FAM and inflammation in colon tissue found significant enrichment of immune-associated DEGs in processes such as neutrophil migration and T cell activation [15]. Furthermore, CIBERSORT analysis revealed enhanced infiltration of neutrophils, M0 and M1 macrophages, B cells, and NK cells. Recent findings have highlighted an increased presence of M0 macrophages and neutrophils in UC tissue samples [16]. Macrophages are the key immune cells to maintain intestinal immune homeostasis and mucosal stability [17]. The imbalance in macrophage polarization is a key factor in the onset and advancement of UC [18]. Neutrophils contribute to the disease by breaching the epithelial barrier, which results in microbial disequilibrium, exacerbated intestinal structural damage, and inflammation [19]. In this context, our findings show that TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44 are linked with neutrophils, macrophages, and other immune cells which proved that the level of hub genes may influence the activity of immune cells, supporting the hypothesis that ferroptosis may play a role in UC pathogenesis. Thus, targeting ferroptosis to preserve immune balance could represent a viable therapeutic approach for UC patients.

TIMP1, a key component of the TIMP family, while traditionally associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, has been shown in some contexts to be involved in redox homeostasis, suggesting a potential connection to Ferroptosis susceptibility [20] and has been consistently upregulated in the intestinal mucosa of UC patients, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for this condition [21–23]. Research by Pan et al. suggests that TIMP1 may serve as a therapeutic target for UC and as an indicator of immune cell infiltration in colon cancer [24]. SOCS1, part of the CIS/suppressors of cytokine signaling family, has been implicated in regulating oxidative stress and inflammation, processes intricately linked to Ferroptosis [25, 26]. Han’s study indicates that TUG1 mitigates TNF-α-induced cell injury and inflammation in UC by modulating the SOCS1 pathway [27]. CD44, encoding a cell surface glycoprotein, plays a role in cell–cell interactions and adhesion, with an increase in expression during inflammation that enhances the adhesion and migration of endothelial and white blood cells to inflamed tissues [28]. CD44 has been reported to promote Ferroptosis resistance through stabilizing SLC7A11, a key anti-Ferroptosis gene, by interacting with cystine/glutamate transporters [29]. LPIN1, belonging to the lipin protein family, facilitates the conversion of phosphatidic acid to diacylglycerol and influences fatty acid oxidation genes through its interaction with nuclear peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) and PPARα co-stimulator factor 1α (PGC1α) [30]. Moreover, LPIN1 is implicated in lipid peroxidation, which may lead to reduced GSH and GAPDH levels, contributing to ferroptosis in UC.

Infliximab, an anti-tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) monoclonal antibody, works by inducing apoptosis in cells that express TNF-α, thus reducing inflammatory responses [31]. Vedolizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody, selectively targets α4β7 integrin on T lymphocytes, specifically within the intestinal tract [32]. These treatments not only prompt clinical improvements and mucosal healing but also enhance the quality of life for patients suffering from active ulcerative colitis (UC) and have been staples in its clinical management for several years.In this research, post-treatment measurements showed that the levels of TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44 were altered in comparison to their levels prior to Infliximab administration, however, they still remained elevated compared to those in healthy controls. A similar variation in expression levels was observed following Vedolizumab treatment. These targeted biomolecular drugs might influence inflammation management through a process known as iron death. Evaluating the efficacy of these drugs could potentially involve measuring levels of TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44. Therefore, these genes may serve as predictive biomarkers or therapeutic targets, that can be used to develop models for predicting drug response or to guide personalized therapeutic strategies.

There are some limitations in this study. The conclusions drawn from the GEO dataset based on bioinformatics analysis and machine learning have not been fully validated by experiments. We conducted animal experiments to prove the reliability of our results which still need more molecular experiments to confirm. There is still a need for a large number of clinical samples to validate heterogeneity. In the future, we intend to conduct in vitro and in vivo studies focusing on these genes, to explore their regulatory role in ferroptosis during intestinal inflammation, which will include loss- and gain-of-function assays, measurements of lipid peroxidation and the use of ferroptosis modulators to clarify the genes’ involvement in ferroptotic pathways.

Conclusions

Overall, employing a range of bioinformatics tools, we identified four central genes (TIMP1, LPIN1, SOCS1, and CD44), associated with UC, and we confirmed their elevated expression in both human UC patients and DSS-induced mouse models. These findings enhance our comprehension of UC pathogenesis and may lead to the identification of new biomarkers for diagnosing and treating the disease.
