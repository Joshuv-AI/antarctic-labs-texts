A serious video game for teaching basic point-of-care echocardiography skills: a non-inferiority trial
Authors: Josef Michael Lintschinger, Lena Reischmann, Daniel Alexander Klaus, Wolfgang Schaubmayr, Lorenz Kapral, Marc Dunst, Florian Kahlfuss, Alexandra Kaider, Gernot Gerger, Eva Schaden, Christina Hafner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe use of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is expanding, but providing effective training remains challenging. Serious video games offer a promising, accessible, and cost-effective alternative. This study compares the non-inferiority of the serious game Underwater for learning basic echocardiography skills with that of traditional simulator-based training.MethodsThis prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled non-inferiority trial was conducted at the Medical University of Vienna. The participants were medical students, all of whom had a basic knowledge of anatomy but no prior experience in cardiac POCUS. Their performance in echocardiography examinations was evaluated using the modified Objective Structured Assessment of Ultrasound Skills (OSAUS) score (4 to 20 points) and its subcategories (1 to 5 points). Following a two-hour workshop and a baseline assessment, the students participated in four training sessions over a two-week period. A final assessment was conducted afterward. A non-inferiority two-sample t-test with a one-sided significance level of 5% was applied to compare the primary outcome – the change in the total modified OSAUS score from baseline to final assessment – between the two groups.ResultsFrom October 3rd to May 5th, 2024, 97 out of 103 participants were analyzed, resulting in a dropout rate of 5.8%. The mean difference in OSAUS scores was − 1.24 ± 3.72 for the simulator and − 0.57 ± 3.51 for the video game group, demonstrating non-inferiority as hypothesized (p < 0.001). No significant differences in image optimization due to hand-eye coordination were observed between the two groups (-0.16 ± 1.40 for simulator vs. -0.23 ± 1.18 for video game, p = 0.780). 63.8% of the video game group and 78.0% of the simulator group found the randomized learning method to be helpful or very helpful.ConclusionSerious video game-based training was statistically non-inferior to mannequin-based simulation for basic cardiac POCUS hand-eye coordination skills. However, neither modality produced measurable improvement beyond the initial workshop, and both groups showed a small decline in performance over time. These findings indicate equivalence between modalities rather than effectiveness in this reinforcement setting and underscore the importance of longitudinal integration, distributed practice, and deliberate reinforcement when implementing innovative training approaches.Trial registrationThe trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06106178; 04/03/2023).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08995-1.

Background

The use of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is steadily increasing in various medical specialties [1–3], alongside the rise of mobile ultrasound devices. This non-invasive, cost-effective diagnostic tool is ideal for answering focused clinical questions [4], and can be performed bedside or at emergency scenes without the need for radiologists. However, its diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility depend strongly on operator experience [5]. Structured training is therefore essential for the safe integration of POCUS into clinical practice, particularly with regard to probe handling, image optimization, and interpretation of findings. Among these skills, hand-eye coordination during probe manipulation is especially challenging for novice learners and appears to be more demanding in transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) than in other ultrasound applications [6–8]. Accordingly, this study focuses specifically on hand-eye coordination in cardiac POCUS.

Despite the growing use of POCUS, standardized training methods are lacking [9–12]. While high-income countries often rely on mannequin-based ultrasound simulators to teach basic skills [13–15], their high cost and limited availability restrict their widespread use. Newer, more affordable training options like educational apps [16], serious games [17, 18], and virtual reality [19, 20] are gaining popularity, offering accessible and engaging ways to teach POCUS skills. However, it remains unclear whether these digital tools are equally effective for learners of different genders and age groups [21].

Beyond the choice of the digital training tool itself, learners’ subjective perceptions of the training method may also influence training outcomes, motivation and engagement in learning activities. These include perceived learning progress, levels of enjoyment or frustration, desire for greater integration of digital tools in medical education, and cognitive load measured using the gold-standard National Aeronautics and Space Administration Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) [22, 23]. Together, these factors may contribute to the effectiveness of novel digital learning methods [16, 24–27].

Another challenge is the lack of standardized and validated tools to assess POCUS competency. While often the number of completed POCUS exams is used as a measure of skill, this approach doesn’t account for the complexity of tasks or individual learning rates [6, 28–31]. The Objective Structured Assessment of Ultrasound Skills (OSAUS) is a more detailed tool to assess general ultrasound skills in different settings [32–34].

The primary aim of this non-inferiority study was to evaluate the serious video game Underwater as a training tool for basic ultrasound skills, with a focus on hand-eye coordination in cardiac POCUS. Secondary aims included the assessment of participants’ subjective impressions and cognitive load with the randomized training methods.

Methods

Study design

This prospective, single-center, single-blind, randomized controlled non-inferiority trial compared the serious video game Underwater with traditional ultrasound simulator training for basic cardiac POCUS hand-eye coordination skills. Ethical approval was provided by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (MedUni Vienna; approval number: 1289/2023), and the trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06106178; 04/03/2023).

Participants

Medical students, aged 18 years or older, with basic knowledge of anatomy but no previous experience in cardiac POCUS were recruited via bulletin board postings, email lists, and social media channels of the MedUni Vienna and its associated student body. Additional recruitment occurred during clinical clerkships and internships at the Department of Anesthesia, Intensive Care Medicine and Pain Medicine. Participants were required to refrain from any additional hands-on ultrasound training outside of the assigned group and were also prohibited from playing video games while participating in the study.

Sample size

An internal pilot study was conducted to determine the final required sample size of 92 participants. The internal pilot was performed exclusively to estimate the variability (standard deviation) of the outcome measure - by using data of the first 55 participants - to finally calculate the required sample size. The effect size was not investigated and, to avoid type one error inflation, no interim analyses - comparing the intervention and the control group with respect to the primary outcome measure - were performed. Based on the pilot study, a dropout rate of approximately 7% was anticipated.

With this sample size, the study had 80% power to reject the null hypothesis that the intervention group is inferior to the standard group (i.e., a difference in means of 2 or more), in favor of the alternative hypothesis that the two groups are non-inferior, assuming an expected difference in means of 0. The calculations were based on a one-sided t-test with a significance level of 0.05.

Study protocol

The study comprised four phases. To assess the feasibility of the study design, including time requirements, teaching materials, staffing, and workflow, two members of the study team per group completed all study phases prior to participant enrollment. This pilot testing informed organizational and educational planning but did not generate data for statistical analysis.

Phase 1

All participants attended a standardized two-hour workshop (Supplementary file 1) covering basic ultrasound principles and cardiac POCUS views. Given the participants’ limited prior experience, training was restricted to basic cardiac views of the focus-assessed transthoracic echocardiography (FATE) protocol, including the apical four-chamber, parasternal long-axis, parasternal short-axis, and subcostal four-chamber views, which constitute the foundation of perioperative cardiac POCUS at the study site [35]. Consequently, views that are not part of the basic protocol, such as the subcostal inferior vena cava view included only in the extended FATE protocol, were not included. Immediately after the workshop, participants completed a baseline assessment by performing a cardiac POCUS examination on a young, healthy male ultrasound model. They were asked to identify predefined sono-anatomical structures (Supplementary Table 1) and to demonstrate predefined views using the freeze mode (Fig. 1a) on the Philips Lumify™ mobile ultrasound device (Philips Ultrasound, Inc., Bothell-Everett Hwy Bothell, WA, USA, equipped with the S4-1 (4 − 1 mHz) transducer). This assessment was videorecorded (Fig. 1b). Participants were then randomized to the control or intervention group, balanced by gender, using the web-based randomization software Randomizer from MedUni Vienna [36].

Phase 2

Over the next two weeks, participants completed four 20-minute training sessions. The control group practiced hand-eye coordination using the surgicalscience ultrasound simulator [37], while the intervention group used the serious video game Underwater [17, 38] to practice the same skills (Fig. 2). In addition, during the workshop, participants were provided with written materials covering the workshop’s topics such as ultrasound images and sono-anatomical structures. This document was designed to help participants retain their knowledge during phase 2 for the final assessment.

Phase 3

A final assessment, identical to the baseline assessment, was performed on the exact same ultrasound model and again videorecorded.

Phase 4

Blinded experts evaluated the video-recorded assessments using the modified OSAUS score. The pseudonymized videos displayed only the ultrasound screen and participants’ hands (Fig. 1b), with relevant sono-anatomical structures labeled and no verbal commentary provided (Fig. 1a). Each expert assessed half of the videos. Video labels were re-randomized to ensure blinding to both participant identity and assessment time point (baseline or final). Randomization was performed such that each expert evaluated both the baseline and final assessment of a given participant while remaining blinded.

Prior to study initiation, four assessment videos (two easy and two difficult to score) were independently rated by both experts. The results were subsequently compared and discussed with the study team to establish a consistent scoring strategy and reduce inter-rater variability.

Ultrasound simulator and serious video game Underwater

The surgicalscience ultrasound simulator (Surgical Science Sweden AB, Gothenburg, Sweden), which was used in this study, has a sonography basic skills module [37]. This module provides tasks aimed at improving basic sonography skills, with an emphasis on hand-eye coordination. Participants locate hidden objects from a predefined view, such as a cylinder, within a torso manikin to develop proficiency in maneuvering the ultrasound probe and understanding the relationship between probe movement and the resulting image of a 3D shape. Similarly, the serious game Underwater (Sfinx Games, Groningen, Netherlands) offers playful training opportunities by using 3D-printed ultrasound probes to find objects in a playful underwater environment [17, 38].

Modified OSAUS score

The OSAUS score [32] was used in a modified form (Supplementary Table 2) to evaluate performance outcomes in the present study, consistent with approaches previously reported by Todsen et al. and Andersen et al. [33, 39]. As only the image optimization subcategory of this score was directly influenced by the interventions, conclusions regarding the training effect were derived from both the total score and the subanalysis of individual subcategories.

The categories indication for the examination, documentation of the examination, and medical decision making from the original scoring system were excluded due to their limited relevance in this educational context. This modification resulted in a total possible score ranging from 4 to 20 points, with individual objectives (applied knowledge of ultrasound equipment, image optimization, systematic examination, and image interpretation) scored on a scale of 1 to 5 points.

In category 1 (applied knowledge of ultrasound equipment), the positioning of the test subjects was also assessed. Category 2 (image optimization) focused on hand-eye coordination in combination with image adjustments (gain, depth, and focus), as these factors together represent the fundamental prerequisites for optimal ultrasound image acquisition. Scoring was expert-based and conducted according to the relevant items of each OSAUS subcategory (Supplementary Table 2). For category 3 (systematic examination), scoring was defined as follows: a score of 5 was awarded only if all 6 required views were adequately visualized; 4 points for at least 4 views; 3 points for at least 3 views; 2 points for at least 2 views; and 1 point if only 1 or no views were adequately visualized. Additionally, for category 4, the assessments were standardized so that 1 point corresponded to less than 10%, 3 points to 50%, and 5 points to more than 90% of the sono-anatomical structures listed in the Supplementary Table 1.

Data collection

A questionnaire collected basic demographic and background data, including age, gender, current medical school semester, video game experience, openness to experience (assessed using the 100-item HEXACO PI-R subscale ranging from 1 to 5 [40], and technology readiness (measured using the Technology Readiness Score [41] ranging from 12 to 60).

To assess the primary outcome, two blinded physicians certified by the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging, scored the video-recorded assessments (Fig. 1).

The time required to complete the baseline and final assessments was recorded, with a maximum duration of 10 min per participant for each assessment.

After the assessments, participants’ cognitive load was measured using the NASA TLX, ranging from 0 to 100 on a visual scale (Supplementary file 2) [22, 23].

Additionally, their experiences of the randomized learning method (in terms of subjective levels of helpfulness, enjoyment and frustration) and their desire for greater digitalization in medical education were evaluated using 5-point Likert scales following the final assessment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS version 29 and SAS version 9.4. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and categorical data are reported as counts and percentages.

A non-inferiority two-sample t-test with a one-sided significance level of 5% was applied to compare the primary outcome – the change in the total modified OSAUS score from baseline to final assessment – between the two groups.

A two-factor ANCOVA model, including an interaction term, was used to examine the influence of gender on the primary outcome and its potential modifying effect on the training effect (i.e., group effect). To account for potential imbalances between the groups, the HEXACO subscale and Technology Readiness Score were included as covariates in the ANCOVA model.

Differences in each objective of the modified OSAUS scores, as well as differences in assessment time and NASA TLX scores from baseline to final assessment, were compared between the randomized groups using Student’s two-sample t-test.

No correction for multiple testing was made due to the singular primary objective. All secondary objectives were exploratory. Missing data were excluded from all analyses. Statistical significance was defined as a one-sided p-value < 0.05 for the primary objective and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 for secondary objectives.

Results

From October 3rd to May 5th, 2024, 97 out of 103 recruited participants were analyzed, resulting in a dropout rate of 5.8% (n = 6). Among the excluded cases, 2 participants did not show up, 1 participant got sick, and 3 participants could not attend all training sessions due to unknown reasons. The majority of participants were female (66.0%; n = 64), between 21 and 23 years (44.3%; n = 43) old and in their first five semesters of their medical education (93.8%; n = 91; Table 1). The two groups showed comparability in their gaming experience (Fig. 3), in openness to experience, and their technology readiness (Table 1).

In the performance subscale of the NASA-TLX, the change from baseline to final assessment differed significantly between groups (p = 0.025). The control group showed a reduction in perceived performance-related workload (-12.4, 95% CI: -20.0 to -4.8), whereas no relevant change was observed in the intervention group (0.3, 95% CI: -7.9 to 8.6).

For the remaining NASA-TLX subscales, no significant between-group differences in change from baseline to final assessment were observed. Mean changes (final minus baseline) in the control vs. intervention groups were − 3.6 (95% CI: -10.8 to 3.6) vs. 1.6 (95% CI: -5.0 to 8.2) for effort (p = 0.289), -0.2 (95% CI: -10.4 to 10.0) vs. 1.5 (95% CI: -7.3 to 10.3) for frustration (p = 0.803), -5.1 (95% CI: -12.3 to 2.1) vs. 0.9 (95% CI: -6.9 to 8.6) for mental demand (p = 0.262), 4.5 (95% CI -1.3 to 10.3) vs. 2.3 (95% CI: -1.1 to 5.6) for physical demand (p = 0.508), and − 1.1 (95% CI: -10.3 to 8.1) vs. -3.6 (95% CI: -12.8 to 5.7) for temporal demand (p = 0.703).

Questionnaire

After completing the final assessment, participants shared their impressions of the randomized training tool through a questionnaire. 78.0% (n = 39) of the control group and 63.8% (n = 30) of the intervention group found the training tool to be very helpful or rather helpful. Additionally, 88.0% (n = 44) of the control group and 83.0% (n = 39) of the intervention group found the training to be very enjoyable or rather enjoyable. Moreover, 66.0% (n = 33) of the control group and 72.3% (n = 34) of the intervention group did not find the training frustrating or rather frustrating. Finally, 76.3% (n = 74) of all participants expressed a desire to see more digitalization in medical education.

Discussion

This study evaluated whether the serious video game Underwater was non-inferior to a traditional mannequin-based ultrasound simulator for teaching basic cardiac POCUS hand-eye coordination skills. While non-inferiority between the two modalities was demonstrated, neither group showed improvement in modified OSAUS scores from baseline to final assessment. Instead, both groups exhibited a small but comparable decline in performance over time.

Importantly, participants had already achieved a moderate performance level at baseline following the initial two-hour workshop, suggesting that this introductory training was effective in enabling ultrasound novices to acquire basic skills. However, the subsequent phase of training – regardless of the training modality – did not lead to measurable additional improvement. Within the confines of this study design, phase-two training therefore appears insufficient to further enhance or consolidate skills, independent of whether a serious game or simulator was used.

The non-inferiority result should not be interpreted as evidence of effectiveness of either modality in this context, but rather as evidence that both approaches yielded comparable outcomes under the given conditions. It remains possible that the timing, duration, structure, or integration of phase-two training limited its educational impact.

The decrease in modified OSAUS scores from baseline to final assessment may reflect limited skill retention between the workshop and the final assessment. Ultrasound competence is experience-dependent and requires repeated, distributed practice [5, 42, 43]. Skill decay in the absence of reinforcement has been described in several ultrasound education studies [44–46]. From the perspective of the spacing effect and distributed practice theory, consolidation of psychomotor skills requires repeated exposure over time rather than isolated reinforcement sessions. It is therefore plausible that the amount or spacing of practice in phase two was insufficient to counteract natural performance decline over time.

The observed decline in performance was not anticipated during study planning. However, the final analysis demonstrated that the magnitude of this change was comparable in both groups, indicating that it was unlikely attributable to one specific intervention. Rather, it may reflect contextual factors such as limited reinforcement, time delay between sessions, or competing academic workload. Several additional factors may explain the absence of measurable improvement. First, a potential ceiling effect cannot be excluded, as baseline scores were already moderate following the workshop. Second, the primary outcome focused on cardiac POCUS image acquisition and hand-eye coordination, which may not fully capture subtle improvements in probe handling efficiency or spatial orientation. Third, the structured video-based assessment may have lacked sensitivity to detect small incremental gains. Fourth, participant engagement with supplementary materials may have varied. Finally, the integration of training modalities at this specific stage of learning may not have been optimal.

Furthermore, differences in realism and contextual fidelity between mannequin-based simulation and serious game-based training may nevertheless influence perceived performance and confidence differently [13–17, 47]. Importantly, recent evidence suggests that fidelity requirements depend on the targeted learning outcome: while high fidelity is critical for clinical integration and decision-making, lower-fidelity digital tools may be sufficient for isolated psychomotor skills such as hand-eye coordination [13–15, 48]. Incorporating more realistic environments into the video game software could help to mitigate this limitation.

With respect to cognitive load, no significant between-group differences were observed in overall NASA-TLX change scores. Among subscales, only perceived performance differed between groups, without corresponding objective differences. This dissociation between subjective perception and measured performance has been described in simulation-based research [33, 49–52]. Notably, secondary outcomes suggested that learners perceived subjective benefit despite the absence of measurable skill improvement, indicating that acceptance and perceived usefulness do not necessarily translate into objective performance gains within short training intervals [53]. From a cognitive load theory perspective, it is possible that the instructional demands during phase two neither sufficiently increased germane load to stimulate further schema construction nor provided enough variability to strengthen retention.

The results also raise important considerations for the implementation of innovative digital tools. Although digital tools, such as serious games, simulators, educational apps and virtual or augmented reality solutions are often promoted as effective training modalities [47, 54–57], their educational value may depend strongly on timing, integration into a longitudinal curriculum, and opportunities for repeated application. In this study, neither modality alone was sufficient to produce measurable improvement beyond the initial workshop. This does not invalidate the potential role of serious games in ultrasound education; rather, it suggests that such tools may function better as preparatory, adjunctive, or distributed practice components within a structured curriculum, rather than as short, isolated reinforcement sessions [17, 19, 47, 54]. Moreover, the learning preferences of digitally experienced medical students may favor such tools, potentially enhancing engagement and motivation [47, 54–57]. This is reflected in the high proportion of participants who expressed a desire for more digital learning tools in medical education.

It is also notable that both groups demonstrated comparable changes, suggesting that the observed decline was unlikely attributable to a specific intervention. External factors such as time delay, competing academic workload, or limited repetition may have contributed. Therefore, the findings primarily inform the educational design of phase-two reinforcement rather than the intrinsic value of serious games versus simulators.

Strengths and limitations

Although the study demonstrates statistical non-inferiority with respect to the primary outcome, the absence of measurable improvement in either group limits conclusions regarding the effectiveness of phase-two training itself. The study was specifically designed to assess short-term reinforcement of hand-eye coordination rather than long-term skill retention or progressive skill acquisition. The observed decline in performance may therefore reflect limited reinforcement and practice over time, underscoring the importance of longitudinal and blended learning approaches.

This study focused on basic cardiac POCUS skills, with particular emphasis on hand-eye coordination. Consequently, the findings cannot be directly extrapolated to more advanced clinical scenarios, higher-level cognitive tasks, or other ultrasound protocols. At present, the serious video game evaluated does not integrate learners into a comprehensive virtual clinical environment, which limits its applicability for training complex clinical decision-making or workflow-related skills.

The study was conducted at a single center with a specific participant population. It is possible that students with a higher intrinsic interest in technology, ultrasound, or video games were more likely to participate. To address this potential selection bias, baseline measures of personality structure and technology readiness were collected and were comparable between groups. Nevertheless, the results may not fully generalize to learners with lower affinity for digital technologies. Future studies including more diverse participant populations, and levels of prior exposure to digital media and video games and settings with longer training intervals are needed to better assess the broader applicability of serious game-based ultrasound training.

In addition, no cost analysis was performed. At the time of the study, the software and hardware required for the serious video game did not have an official purchase price, and local cost data were incomplete, precluding a meaningful economic evaluation.

Conclusion

Serious video game-based training was statistically non-inferior to mannequin-based simulation for teaching basic hand-eye coordination skills in cardiac POCUS. However, neither modality resulted in measurable improvement beyond the initial workshop, and both groups demonstrated a small decline in performance over time. The findings therefore support equivalence between modalities rather than effectiveness of either approach in this specific reinforcement setting.

These results emphasize that instructional design, timing, distributed practice, and deliberate reinforcement are critical determinants of educational impact. Innovative tools such as serious games or simulators may be most effective when embedded within structured, longitudinal training frameworks that incorporate repeated practice, feedback, and progressive complexity.
