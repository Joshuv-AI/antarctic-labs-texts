Novice nurses’ experience with an ‘Attending Rounds’ clinical case mentoring model: a phenomenological study
Authors: Haixiang Zhu, Yuan Wu, Ruiting Wang, Lili Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the lived experiences of novice nurses during their transition from participation in the ‘Attending Rounds’ clinical case mentoring training model to independent clinical practice, and to understand its influence on their development of clinical competence, role identity and team collaboration.DesignA descriptive phenomenological study using semistructured, face-to-face interviews, guided by Colaizzi’s seven-step method of data analysis.SettingTwo large teaching hospitals in eastern China, representing secondary and tertiary levels of care.Participants12 novice nurses (nine female, three male; aged 22–29 years; bachelor’s or master’s degree holders) who had completed the ‘Attending Rounds’ training at least four times and subsequently entered clinical practice. Participants were recruited purposively to ensure variation in demographic and educational backgrounds.InterventionsNot applicablePrimary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were novice nurses’ lived experiences and perceptions of the ‘Attending Rounds’ model, captured through semistructured interviews and analysed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological method.Secondary outcome measuresExperiential domains included: (1) skill improvement—enhanced clinical judgement, patient management and ward management; (2) learner anxiety and growth—initial discomfort and stress evolving into confidence and resilience; (3) role modelling—professional inspiration and learning through observation of senior nurses and (4) team cohesion—improved communication, collaboration and patient-centred care.ResultsFour overarching themes with 12 subthemes were identified: (1) skill improvement (clinical judgement, patient and ward management); (2) stress and growth (initial anxiety and adaptation leading to confidence); (3) role modelling (learning from senior nurses, leadership inspiration) and (4) team cohesion (enhanced collaboration, strengthened patient-centred care). Nurses described initial discomfort and anxiety, but over time developed greater competence, confidence and awareness of professional responsibilities.ConclusionsThe ‘Attending Rounds’ model facilitated novice nurses’ transition into clinical practice by integrating structured case-based learning, role modelling and team collaboration. While stress and anxiety were common at the outset, these experiences were ultimately perceived as catalysts for growth. Findings suggest the model may be further refined to support novice nurses’ professional identity formation and clinical competence.Keywords: Caregivers, Clinical Competence, Clinical Decision-Making, Nurses, Observational Study, EDUCATION & TRAINING (see Medical Education & Training)

Introduction

Novice nurses often face considerable challenges when transitioning from academic learning to clinical practice. Rapid adaptation to the practical demands of nursing, particularly in comprehensive public hospitals, is essential to ensure both patient safety and quality of care.1 This transition requires the acquisition of clinical decision-making skills, technical competencies and effective patient management.2 In China, the clinical environment of tertiary hospitals is highly dynamic, where novice nurses are expected not only to quickly adapt to these changes but also to guarantee high-quality patient care. To address these challenges, structured clinical mentoring models, such as ‘Attending Rounds’, have been introduced as effective approaches to support novice nurses in rapidly acquiring the clinical competencies required for independent practice.3 ‘Attending Rounds’ in medicine refers to the process in which the attending physician leads a team (including residents, interns and medical students) to conduct a systematic evaluation and discussion of patients. The primary aims are to assess patient conditions, adjust treatment plans and optimise care management, while simultaneously serving as a clinical teaching opportunity to cultivate clinical reasoning and communication skills among trainees. The ‘Rounds’ model for nursing education originated from this medical practice and is underpinned by constructivist learning theory, which emphasises the active role of learners in developing knowledge through engagement, interaction and reflection.4 Its distinctive strength lies in case-based reflection, enabling novice nurses to critically analyse patient cases, identify gaps in practice and incorporate feedback into subsequent care decisions. This reflective process not only enhances clinical reasoning but also fosters professional identity formation during the early stages of practice. In this model, novice nurses are immersed in real clinical scenarios, thereby bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application. Experienced nurses lead case discussions, guiding novices to develop critical thinking, clinical reasoning and patient management skills. The ‘Rounds’ training model provides novice nurses with opportunities to engage with complex patient cases, receive immediate feedback from senior nurses and refine their clinical decision-making and problem-solving abilities. Moreover, this model encourages teamwork and knowledge-sharing, thereby promoting a culture of patient safety and evidence-based practice.5 This structured yet realistic process accelerates the development of clinical competence while simultaneously building confidence in novice nurses as they transition to independent practice.6

In China, the transition period for novice nurses is typically longer than in other countries,7 and they are often required to manage patients independently during training. To accelerate this adaptation, it is essential to integrate the ‘Rounds’ clinical case mentoring model into novice nurse education. However, evidence regarding the effectiveness of clinical mentoring models such as nursing rounds remains limited. Therefore, this study aims to explore the experiences of novice nurses participating in the ‘Rounds’ clinical case mentoring model in cardiology departments. Using a phenomenological approach, we seek to understand how this training influences novice nurses’ confidence, development of clinical skills and overall ability to transition successfully into professional practice. Therefore, the ‘Rounds’ model in nursing education represents a structured mentoring approach, rather than a coaching process, and is explicitly grounded in constructivist principles.

Background

Globally, the transition from nursing education to clinical practice presents significant challenges for novice nurses. In China, most new nurses hold a bachelor’s degree, and only a small proportion pursue advanced training at the master’s level.8 The full-time Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) programmes in China focus on fundamental nursing theories, knowledge and skills, emphasising nursing education, management and research.9 Most newly graduated nurses face difficulties in role transition when entering clinical practice.10 Consequently, developing clinical competency, particularly in patient assessment, clinical decision-making and teamwork, is critical for their successful integration into real-world healthcare settings. To bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical application, various educational models have been implemented.10 11

At our institution, the nursing education department updated the traditional ‘Attending Rounds’ model to provide novice nurses with immersive bedside mentoring under the guidance of senior staff. Unlike conventional ward rounds, the improved model emphasises structured case discussions and interactive participation.6 Typically, rounds focus on critically ill patients and are led by the head nurse or senior nurses. Participants include the head nurse, clinical educators, charge nurses, primary nurses, nursing students and occasionally family members. In this collaborative process, the head nurse leads the discussion, the primary nurse reports patient conditions and the charge nurse supplements key information. Other participants raise questions for clarification, and family members may also contribute. This team-based approach is central to developing novice nurses’ clinical reasoning, decision-making and communication skills. Building on this foundation, our team further developed a ‘Rounds-based head nurse bedside mentoring model’, in which head nurses synthesise information from patient examinations, laboratory results and treatment plans to guide novice nurses in identifying overlooked issues, engaging in reflection, stimulating independent thinking and enhancing professional competence.6 The ‘Rounds’ model is underpinned by constructivist learning theory, which emphasises learners’ active role in constructing knowledge through engagement, interaction and reflection.12 Its distinctive strength lies in case-based reflection, enabling novice nurses to critically analyse patient cases, identify practice gaps and incorporate feedback into clinical decision-making. This reflective process enhances clinical reasoning and fosters professional identity formation during the early career stage. Furthermore, outcomes-based education (OBE) advocates a paradigm shift from teacher-centred to learner-centred education.13 Integrating OBE principles with the ‘Rounds’ mentoring model aligns nursing education with clinical practice needs, promoting critical thinking, strengthening decision-making and preparing practice-ready novice nurses.8 10

Despite these efforts, evidence on the effectiveness of clinical mentoring models such as nursing rounds remains limited. This gap highlights the need for further exploration of how structured rounds models, informed by constructivist and OBE principles, contribute to the professional development of novice nurses.

To standardise practice, we developed the ‘Rounds’ framework, which provides the structural foundation for the Rounds mentoring model and ensures consistency in implementation and theoretical alignment. Explicitly grounded in constructivist learning theory, this framework operationalises mentoring into six key steps: Room, Observation, Understanding, Notice, Summary and Decision—to guide clinical case discussions and enhance nursing practice (see figure 1). R (Room)—entering the room and setting a professional example; O (Observation)—observing the overall ward environment; U (Understanding)—ensuring patient and family comprehension and clarifying the purpose and duration of the activity; N (Notice)—capturing critical information from bedside examination and nurse reports, particularly regarding potential risks; D (Decision)—formulating daily care decisions; and S (Summary)—providing feedback and summarising key points. This structured approach strengthens novice nurses’ ability to anticipate complications, manage complex cases and address individual learning needs. The Observation and Notice steps also reflect Vygotsky’s concept of scaffolding, whereby experienced nurses provide guidance as novices progressively develop skills. The Understanding step highlights collaborative communication and knowledge-sharing, reinforcing the constructivist principle that learning is a social process within a community of practice. Rounds are conducted weekly, with novice nurses rotating as the primary nurse. However, the effectiveness of this model, particularly in improving clinical skills and patient outcomes, remains understudied. Therefore, this study explores the lived experiences of novice nurses engaged in the ‘Attending Rounds’ mentoring model, assessing its impact on their transition to clinical practice and management of critically ill patients. Findings from this study will provide insights into optimising structured clinical training programmes to better support novice nurses during this crucial stage of professional development.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

None.

Design

The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist was used to guide the reporting of this qualitative study14 (see online supplemental file 1). We employed a descriptive phenomenological design to explore the experiences and emotions of novice nurses during their clinical training period, as this approach enables a deeper exploration of participants’ lived experiences, reflections and emotions. Phenomenology is particularly well suited to uncovering the meanings behind lived experiences, aligning with the aim of this study to understand the emotional and reflective aspects of nurses’ transition to practice. As both a philosophical approach and a research method, descriptive phenomenology seeks to capture the essence of lived experiences,15 enhance understanding of individuals and elucidate the nature of their actions. Key concepts guiding this research included intentionality and phenomenological reduction (bracketing). Intentionality refers to the idea that all human thoughts, feelings and actions are directed toward experiences in the world.15 Thus, creating an environment that allows participants to express themselves freely was considered essential to reflect their authentic experiences. Phenomenological reduction, or bracketing, required the researcher to consciously set aside prior knowledge and personal biases in order to describe the lived experience in its ‘pure, universal sense’.16 Phenomenological analysis, rather than content analysis, was deliberately chosen to capture the deeper meanings underlying participants’ personal experiences, emotions and reflections. While content analysis can identify themes and patterns, it does not allow the same depth of interpretation of emotional and reflective dimensions, which were central to the objectives of this study.

Setting

This study was conducted at an affiliated teaching hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, which has more than 500 novice nurses. The choice of this site was based on two considerations: (1) it has a comprehensive ‘Rounds’ clinical training programme that is well established and has demonstrated positive outcomes in developing clinical nurses; and (2) the first author works at this hospital as a department nurse manager, overseeing clinical training in the ‘Rounds’ programme, which facilitated both access to participants and close familiarity with the training context.

Sampling and participants

A purposive sampling strategy was adopted because it allows for the deliberate selection of participants who are most likely to provide rich, relevant and diverse insights into the phenomenon under study.17 Diversity in nursing school background, gender and nursing degree (bachelor or science in nursing ormaster of science in nursing) was considered to ensure variation. The inclusion criteria were: (1) novice nurses who had completed their theoretical studies and were in the clinical practice training phase; (2) those who had participated in the ‘Rounds’ mentoring training at least four times; (3) individuals willing to participate and provide informed consent and (4) participants who agreed to contribute to the final validation stage of data analysis. Exclusion criteria included new graduates who had taken leave for more than 1 month during training due to illness, maternity leave or other reasons. Withdrawal criteria applied to participants who chose to discontinue for various reasons, such as inability or unwillingness to continue participation.

The researcher contacted the coordinators of the novice nurse transition programme in each department to obtain permission and access to the ‘Rounds’ clinical training schedules. Based on novice nurses’ availability and the inclusion criteria, the executive director recommended approximately 50 eligible nurses in each department. In line with the principle of maximum variation sampling, 15 candidates were initially selected from this list and contacted by phone. During the calls, the researcher explained the purpose of the study, data collection procedures and emphasised that participation was entirely voluntary, with no consequences for declining or withdrawing at any stage. Nurses were also assured that refusal would not affect their current or future employment. Those who consented were then contacted via WeChat to arrange interview times and locations. Ultimately, two candidates declined participation, and one was excluded once data saturation had been reached. A total of 12 novice nurses completed interviews, representing four different nursing schools.

Data collection

Data were collected between November 2023 and January 2024 using semistructured, open-ended interviews. To adhere to the principle of bracketing, only broad, open-ended questions were posed to allow participants to freely share their experiences.18 The primary guiding questions included: (1) ‘Could you share your experience of participating in the ‘Rounds’ training practice?’ (2) ‘What were your feelings during the participation in the ‘Rounds’ training practice?’ and (3) ‘How did this experience affect your ability to assess patient conditions, manage patients and manage the ward?’ Probing and follow-up questions such as ‘Could you elaborate on this?’ or ‘Would you please explain further?’ were used to encourage deeper reflection and storytelling (see online supplemental Material). The interviews were conducted by WR, an experienced registered nurse with a master’s degree, who has extensive experience in qualitative research methods. WR had also attended training in phenomenological research techniques to ensure consistency and rigour throughout the interview process. To maintain the integrity of the data, WR remained neutral, refraining from influencing participants’ responses and instead using probing questions to elicit clarification and elaboration. All interviews took place in a quiet, private room within the hospital, free from interruptions, with no additional persons present. Written informed consent was obtained before each interview. All sessions were audio-recorded, and field notes were simultaneously taken to capture non-verbal cues (eg, tone of voice, facial expressions and gestures). These notes were later incorporated with transcripts to provide contextual depth during analysis.

The recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 hours by members of the research team to ensure accurate reflection of participants’ accounts. Each interview lasted between 20 and 42 min. To minimise bias, the interviewer maintained neutrality throughout, using probing only to deepen understanding of participants’ emotions and experiences. Transcripts were subsequently cross-checked for accuracy by ZH, a chief nurse in the same hospital.

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. NVivo software was used to manage and code the data. After no new themes emerged at interview N10, two additional interviews (N11 and N12) were carried out to confirm that only previously identified codes were repeated, signifying that data saturation had been achieved.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Colaizzi’s seven-step method, which is widely used in phenomenological research to extract and interpret the essential meanings of lived experiences.19 We clarified the use of phenomenological methods and their appropriateness for this research, especially in comparison to content analysis. Phenomenology allows for a deeper exploration of participants’ lived experiences, emotions and reflections, which is central to the aim of this study. Unlike content analysis, which focuses primarily on identifying themes or patterns, phenomenology involves deep engagement with the text to uncover the meaning behind these lived experiences.20 This approach is critical for capturing the emotional and reflective aspects of the participants’ experiences that are central to the research question. The process of analysis included iterative readings to identify both explicit and implicit meanings in the data. Colaizzi’s method emphasises extracting phrases or sentences directly related to the phenomenon under investigation, while also integrating the researchers’ perspectives through reflexive engagement with their preunderstanding. We followed a dual-cycle process in which significant statements were first selected explicitly, and then we interrogated contextual and metaphorical language to reveal deeper meanings. In addition to transcript analysis, field notes documenting non-verbal cues (eg, tone of voice, facial expressions and body language) were incorporated into the analytic process to provide contextual depth and enhance interpretive accuracy. To ensure the credibility of the findings, we engaged in reflexive analysis. Colaizzi’s method acknowledges that researchers inevitably bring their own perspectives to the analysis. To address this, we maintained reflective journals and conducted peer-debriefing sessions, which allowed us to triangulate our interpretations and ensure that the analysis remained grounded in the participants’ experiences. We also used member checking as part of our validation process. Two participants (N3 and N5) were randomly selected to review the findings for accuracy. During member checking, they contextualised themes (eg, clarifying that ‘loss of autonomy’ specifically referred to restricted family visitation) and confirmed the accuracy of the interpretations. All participants agreed with the researchers’ interpretations and raised no objections. Thematic development was supported by conceptual mapping to visualise the relationships between codes. The meanings were formulated through consensus coding, which achieved a 90% intercoder agreement (Cohen’s κ=0.87), with discrepancies resolved iteratively. We also employed negative case analysis, such as considering one participant’s atypical response to certain aspects of the Rounds process, which allowed us to test and refine thematic boundaries. This approach helped us understand how different perspectives might either challenge or strengthen the emerging themes. To further deepen the analysis, we applied thick description throughout the coding process, which captured the complexity and depth of the participants’ experiences. For example, raw statements such as ‘I felt unsure’ were transformed into more nuanced conceptual themes like ‘uncertainty as a catalyst for learning’.

Additionally, we maintained an audit trail, which was reviewed by an independent qualitative researcher to ensure consistency and rigour across all phases of coding, theming and interpretation. Reflexivity and bracketing

During the study, RT, as a master’s nurse, played the role of the interviewer and had no prior knowledge or interaction with the novice nurses before data collection. To minimise bias, bracketing was applied throughout the interview process as well as during the description of the data. However, rather than removing subjectivity, bracketing was used as a means to critically examine and reflect on the researchers’ preconceptions, assumptions and perspectives, ensuring that these influences were made explicit. RT’s dual role as both a master’s nurse and a researcher was particularly important in shaping her understanding of the data. Her professional background in nursing provided an insider perspective that helped her interpret participants’ experiences more deeply. Throughout the analysis process, RT maintained a reflexive journal to document how her clinical experiences, particularly in the areas of patient care and treatment adherence, informed her interpretations. This reflection was crucial in ensuring that her insights were integrated into the analysis in a way that enhanced, rather than distorted, the interpretive process. The research team reached an agreement on the findings, ensuring that the analysis was conducted in a way that acknowledged and critically engaged with the researchers’ own positions and preunderstandings.

The interviews were conducted in Chinese, as the participants were native Chinese speakers. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and translated into English by a bilingual researcher to ensure accuracy in meaning and terminology. The translation process was checked by an independent researcher to ensure fidelity and consistency.

Findings

12 novice nurses from five departments (n=12) participated in the study and were coded N1–N12. Participants ranged in age from 22 to 29 years (mean age=23.9 years); nine were female and three were male. Nine held bachelor’s degrees and three held master’s degrees. Eight nurses were unmarried, and four were married. The demographic characteristics are summarised in table 2. Data analysis yielded 16 initial codes, which were organised into four overarching themes and 12 subthemes, reflecting participants’ key experiences and perceptions of the ‘Rounds’ mentoring model. The four themes were: (1) skill improvement, (2) learner anxiety and growth, (3) role modelling and (4) team cohesion (table 1).

Skill improvement

Interviews indicated that participants achieved notable progress in clinical judgement, patient care and ward management. Nurses reported that participation in the ‘Rounds’ enabled them to recognise personal limitations and pursue more targeted skill development. As one participant noted, ‘Through the rounds, I realized the details I had previously overlooked, and now the key aspects of patient care are much clearer’ (N1). Another reflected, ‘Thanks to the guidance from senior nurses, I identified my shortcomings in patient judgment and now have a clear path for improvement’ (N6). Similarly, a nurse stated, ‘Participating in the rounds allowed me to see practical examples of how to prioritize and organize patient care. I learned to focus on critical details that were previously missed, and this has significantly improved my clinical judgment and decision-making’ (N11). Collectively, these narratives underscore the value of structured feedback and role modelling in enhancing clinical competence.

The second subtheme concerned the development of management skills. Nurses emphasised that their awareness of ward management and the broader care environment improved substantially. One nurse explained, ‘The rounds made me realize that the ward environment is just as important as patient care, and I can now manage safety issues in the ward more effectively’ (N3). Another added, ‘After the rounds, I felt I had more structure in ward management and could better identify risks’ (N10). Similarly, a participant observed, ‘During the rounds, I learned how to coordinate multiple tasks while maintaining patient safety. Observing senior nurses managing the ward helped me understand how to identify potential risks and prioritize issues effectively, which has made me more confident in handling complex situations’ (N9). Together, these findings illustrate the holistic orientation of the ‘Rounds’ model, in which both patient care and environmental safety are afforded equal importance.

Stress and growth

In this study, stress was primarily expressed as learner anxiety. Participants reported experiencing varying levels of stress, particularly when receiving feedback. Nurses frequently reported concerns about overlooking critical details or failing to provide accurate responses during the early stages of the ‘Rounds’. One participant described, ‘I felt quite anxious at the beginning of the rounds, fearing I might overlook essential nursing details’ (N2). Another shared, ‘I experienced significant pressure, constantly thinking that I wasn’t adequately prepared to answer questions’ (N5). These accounts suggest that psychological stress among novice nurses is largely manifested as anxiety during their transition to clinical practice.

The second subtheme reflected gradual adaptation and professional growth. As participants became more familiar with the rounds, their stress levels diminished while their confidence increased. One nurse explained, ‘Although I was initially nervous, as I gained experience, I started to feel more confident in my skills’ (N8). Another noted, ‘Now, after going through the rounds, I feel that I’ve grown a lot, and I’m more capable of handling clinical challenges’ (N12). These reflections indicate that while stress was initially perceived as anxiety, it ultimately acted as a catalyst for growth, reinforcing the developmental value of the ‘Rounds’ model.

Role modelling

The first subtheme highlighted the acquisition of clinical skills through observing senior nurses. Participants consistently emphasised the value of watching experienced nurses’ approaches during the ‘Rounds’, which provided them with insights into conducting comprehensive patient assessments. One nurse reflected, ‘I learned a lot from the senior nurses during the rounds. Their perspective and thought process benefited me greatly’ (N7). Such experiences emphasise the importance of role models in shaping novice nurses’ clinical competence. The second subtheme centred on leadership and professional inspiration. Senior nurses were described not only as providers of clinical guidance but also as sources of motivation and confidence. As one participant explained, ‘The senior nurses’ rounds showed me the complexity and intricacy of ward management’ (N4). Another noted, ‘I learned a lot from the team leader. Their leadership gave me more confidence in handling clinical work’ (N8). These accounts highlight the dual role of senior nurses as both clinical mentors and organisational leaders, fostering the professional growth of novice nurses through skill demonstration and leadership modelling.

Team cohesion

Alongside skill development and role modelling, participants also emphasised the collective dimension of the ‘Rounds’, namely team cohesion. Nurses described how participation in the rounds strengthened collaboration and improved patient care delivery. The first subtheme emphasised improved teamwork, with nurses noting that the support of colleagues during rounds facilitated smoother workflows and improved care quality. One nurse remarked, ‘During the rounds, the cooperation and communication among us made me feel the power of the team, and the nursing work became smoother’ (N6). Another added, ‘Through the rounds, we can support each other and provide better care for our patients’ (N9). These narratives underscore the positive impact of collaborative teamwork on clinical practice.

The second subtheme reflected an increased awareness of patient-centred service. Nurses described a heightened focus on patients’ needs and the provision of more individualised care. As one participant explained, ‘We pay more attention to patients’ needs during the rounds and can provide more personalized nursing services’ (N1). Another stated, ‘This sense of team cohesion makes me feel that serving patients is a very important task’ (N3). These reflections illustrate how the ‘Rounds’ fostered a shared sense of responsibility for patient care, reinforcing the importance of team cohesion in complex clinical environments.

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of novice nurses participating in the ‘Rounds’ practice model and identified four overarching themes: skill improvement, stress and growth, role modelling and team cohesion. These themes are consistent with prior research while also highlighting the distinctive contributions of the ‘Rounds’ model to nurse development.

The interviews revealed notable advancements in both clinical and managerial competencies. Novice nurses reported enhanced clinical judgement, patient care and ward management, findings that align with previous studies on the value of structured clinical teachings.11 Recognising personal limitations and subsequently improving in these areas illustrates the constructive role that the ‘Rounds’ model plays in professional growth. Feedback from senior nurses was particularly influential in bridging knowledge gaps and strengthening decision-making abilities, consistent with observations from Mofokeng et al,21 where novice nurses demonstrated growth through structured mentorship and practice. Furthermore, the development of ward management skills observed in this study echoes the importance of comprehensive clinical competence.6 Notably, the emphasis on ward management was more pronounced in this study, reflecting the holistic and case-based reflective approach of the ‘Rounds’ model, which distinguishes it from traditional clinical teaching models. This underscores how the comprehensive and structured nature of the ‘Rounds’ practice enhances both clinical and managerial skill development, reinforcing the importance of holistic training programmes in nursing education.

Nurses reported experiencing learner anxiety and discomfort, particularly at the beginning of their participation in the ‘Rounds’. In this study, the psychological stress reported by novice nurses primarily manifested as learner anxiety, characterised by uncertainty in mastering clinical skills and concerns for patient safety. This unease stemmed from concerns about making errors or failing to meet expectations, a phenomenon also noted in previous studies on novice nurses’ anxiety during clinical transitions.11 Over time, participants adapted to the structure of the rounds, which reduced their anxiety and enhanced their self-confidence. This progression is consistent with the learning curve described in prior research, where initial anxiety often functions as a temporary but formative challenge in professional development.22 In this study, early discomfort served as a catalyst for growth, underscoring the value of exposure to challenging environments in clinical education. The sense of accomplishment that developed over time highlighted the contribution of practice rounds to resilience-building and confidence, aligning with the General Practice Foundation’s (2015) framework of skill acquisition.23 Compared with other studies, our findings suggest that learner anxiety, though prevalent in the early stages of training, can be transformative, fostering growth rather than hindrance. This interpretation resonates with the concept of ‘productive stress’ in nursing education literature,24 which posits that temporary discomfort is an integral component of professional maturation.

The importance of role modelling by senior nurses was another central finding. Novice nurses emphasised that observing senior nurses during the ‘Rounds’ not only enhanced their clinical competencies but also provided critical leadership inspiration. This finding parallels prior research in which mentorship and role models played pivotal roles in nurse development.25 However, the ‘Rounds’ model appeared to provide a more integrated form of mentorship by combining clinical teaching with leadership demonstration. Senior nurses served simultaneously as clinical experts and organisational leaders, influencing novice nurses beyond the scope of patient care. This observation complements research highlighting the role of nurse leaders in shaping clinical competence,26 but it also suggests that the ‘Rounds’ model uniquely reinforces leadership development through case-based reflective practice.

Team cohesion also emerged as a significant theme. Participants reported that the ‘Rounds’ practice promoted stronger teamwork, which in turn enhanced patient services. This is consistent with previous literature on the critical role of collaboration in healthcare.27 The structured nature of the ‘Rounds’ facilitated open communication and collective problem-solving, thereby reinforcing the notion that cohesive teams are essential for effective patient care. As novice nurses gained confidence, they were better able to collaborate with peers and contribute to coordinated care delivery. Our findings extend prior research by demonstrating that team cohesion not only improves patient outcomes but also supports the personal and professional development of novice nurses.

With regard to sampling, purposive sampling was employed to ensure participants had relevant experiences with the ‘Rounds’ training. While marital status and other demographic variables were not directly related to the training itself, these factors may influence nurses’ emotional support systems and coping strategies during adaptation.28 Although no direct relationship between marital status and training outcomes was identified, this remains a relevant area for future research. Considering such personal factors may help to further contextualise how novice nurses adapt to structured training programmes.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the ‘Rounds’ practice model provides novice nurses with opportunities for skill development, role modelling and team collaboration, while also transforming early learner anxiety into confidence and resilience. These findings suggest that structured, reflective practice models like ‘Rounds’ can substantially facilitate the transition from novice to competent nurse by integrating clinical training, leadership development and collaborative practice.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the in-depth exploration of novice nurses’ lived experiences using phenomenological analysis, which offered a rich, contextualised understanding of participants’ emotions, skills and reflections. This approach ensured credibility by capturing the depth and complexity of lived experiences central to the research question. Reflexivity was further strengthened by the involvement of a researcher with direct clinical experience (RT), whose reflective journaling provided a contextualised perspective. Member checking, in which participants (N3 and N5) reviewed the themes, confirmed the accuracy of interpretations, while an audit trail, reviewed by an independent qualitative researcher, enhanced dependability and confirmability, ensuring that results were traceable to the data rather than researcher bias.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted at a single institution using purposive sampling, which may limit the transferability of findings. The sample was predominantly female, potentially under-representing male perspectives. In addition, factors such as personality traits or educational background (eg, MSN versus BSN) were not considered, although they may influence how novice nurses experience the ‘Rounds’. Future studies should account for these dimensions to enhance transferability. Moreover, the study focused exclusively on novice nurses, and the perspectives of more experienced nurses or those from other departments were not explored. Finally, as the interviews were conducted in Chinese and translated into English, subtle nuances may have been lost in translation despite independent review for consistency.

Conclusions

The transition from novice to competent nurses is a crucial phase in their professional growth. Our study identified both the challenges and the development experienced during ‘Rounds’ practice. While stress was common initially, nurses showed significant improvements in clinical judgement, patient management and teamwork as they progressed. Early support from senior nurses and structured training programmes are essential to building novice nurses’ confidence and competence. These findings provide valuable insights for refining training programmes to better meet the needs of novice nurses, ultimately enhancing patient care and team dynamics.
