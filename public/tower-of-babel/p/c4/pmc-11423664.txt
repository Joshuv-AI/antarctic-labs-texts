Association of hepatitis B core antibody level and hepatitis B surface antigen clearance in HBeAg-negative patients with chronic hepatitis B
Authors: Jian Wang, Zhiyi Zhang, Li Zhu, Qing Zhang, Shaoqiu Zhang, Yifan Pan, Jiacheng Liu, Fei Cao, Tao Fan, Ye Xiong, Shengxia Yin, Xiaomin Yan, Yuxin Chen, Chuanwu Zhu, Jie Li, Xingxiang Liu, Chao Wu, Rui Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPredicting hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) clearance is important for chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients receiving pegylated interferon-alfa (Peg-IFN) therapy. We aimed to determine the predictive value of serum hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc) for HBsAg clearance. A total of 189 HBeAg-negative CHB patients who received Peg-IFN based therapy were retrospectively included and classified into two groups: nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) add-on Peg-IFN group (add-on group, n = 94) and Peg-IFN combined with NAs or Peg-IFN monotherapy group (combination or monotherapy group, n = 95). After 48 weeks of treatment, 27.5% (52/189) and 15.9% (30/189) of patients achieved HBsAg clearance and seroconversion, respectively. Patients in the combination or monotherapy group tended to achieve relatively higher HBsAg clearance (31.6% vs. 23.4%, p = 0.208) and seroconversion (21.1% vs. 10.6%, p = 0.050) rates than those in the add-on group. In combination or monotherapy group, anti-HBc levels at week 12 were lower in patients with HBsAg clearance (9.0 S/CO vs. 9.9 S/CO, p < 0.001) and seroconversion (8.8 S/CO vs. 9.8 S/CO, p < 0.001) than those without. Anti-HBc level at week 12 was an independent predictor of HBsAg clearance and seroconversion. Patients with lower anti-HBc levels at week 12 showed a more significant decline in HBsAg levels during treatment. Combination of anti-HBc at week 12 and baseline HBsAg could identify over 70% of patients who achieved HBsAg clearance after 48 weeks of treatment. In addition to HBsAg, anti-HBc level could be used as a promising marker for selecting HBeAg-negative CHB patients who are more likely to respond to Peg-IFN-based therapy.KEYWORDS: Hepatitis B surface antigen, level of anti-HBc antibody, pegylated interferon, antiviral, functional cure

Introduction

Approximately 296 million individuals suffer from chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, and approximately 0.5 to 1 million deaths result from HBV-related complications per year worldwide, including liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. Functional cure, which is characterized by hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) clearance with or without seroconversion (development of antibody against HBsAg (anti-HBs)), is associated with a reduced risk of cirrhosis, HCC, and liver-related mortality in patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) [2,3]. Pegylated interferon alfa (Peg-IFN) is a potential drug for achieving HBsAg clearance in patients with CHB [2,3]. Sustained immune control is one of the main mechanisms of HBV clearance by Peg-IFN [2,3]. However, the use of Peg-IFN is limited due to its injection, significant toxicity, high cost, and narrow eligibility for treatment options [2–4]. Moreover, only a minority of individuals achieved a functional cure after Peg-IFN treatment. Thus, a valuable biomarker to identify candidates who are most likely to achieve a better treatment response with Peg-IFN treatment is needed.

Hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc) is a classical biomarker of HBV infection and can be used to identify the immune status of HBV infection [5]. Our previous study demonstrated that CHB patients in the immune-active phase had significantly higher serum anti-HBc levels than those in the immune-tolerant and inactive phases defined by the AASLD 2018 hepatitis B guidance [3,6]. Serum anti-HBc levels are positively correlated with alanine transaminase (ALT) levels and liver inflammation activity, suggesting that serum anti-HBc may be a surrogate indicator of HBV-specific immune activation [7,8]. A growing body of evidence suggests that serum anti-HBc detected by the double-sandwich immunoassay is a promising predictor of Peg-IFN treatment response in patients with CHB [9–11]. However, the detection method for serum anti-HBc has not been commercialized, which limits its wide application in clinical practice. The ARCHITECT assay (Abbott GmbH) has been widely used to detect serum anti-HBc levels in clinical practice with satisfactory accuracy and value [6]. Our previous study found that serum anti-HBc detected by the ARCHITECT assay was a strong predictor of serum HBeAg seroconversion in HBeAg-positive CHB patients treated with nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) [12].

The association of serum anti-HBc measured by ARCHITECT assay with treatment response of Peg-IFN-based therapy requires further assessment. Therefore, the purpose of this real-world multicenter study was to investigate the predictive value of serum anti-HBc levels for HBsAg clearance in HBeAg-negative CHB patients with Peg-IFN-based therapy.

Methods

Study population

One hundred and eighty-nine HBeAg-negative CHB patients treated with Peg-IFN-based regimens between January 2018 and April 2022 in three medical centres were included. All patients received Peg-IFN-based therapy, including 94 patients with NAs add-on Peg-IFN treatment, 67 patients with Peg-IFN monotherapy, and 28 patients with Peg-IFN and NAs combination treatment. Notably, patients in the NAs add-on Peg-IFN group had received NAs treatment for ≥1 year prior to Peg-IFN treatment (Figure S1). All patients were treated with Peg-IFN alfa-2a (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) or Peg-IFN alfa-2b (Amoytop, Xiamen, China) (180 μg/week) for 48 weeks. Serum HBsAg positivity for more than 6 months was defined as chronic HBV infection. The exclusion criteria were concurrent with other acute or chronic liver diseases, including hepatitis A, hepatitis E, hepatitis C, decompensated cirrhosis, and HCC. Patients with autoimmune diseases, thyroid diseases, or malignancies were also excluded. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (No. 2008022) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03097952), and the process followed the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. A waiver of informed consent was granted by the ethics committees due to a retrospective design.

Data acquisition and laboratory measurements

Clinical data were collected from the electronic medical record system, including demographic characteristics, routine blood examination, liver function tests, and serological markers of HBV. The quantitative HBV serological markers were detected by ARCHITECT assay (Abbott GmbH, Wiesbaden, Germany), including HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and anti-HBc. Positive HBsAg was defined as serum levels over the threshold of 0.05 IU/ml, and was further tested with a stepwise dilution of 1:20 to 1:1,000 if >250 IU/ml. Positive anti-HBs was defined as serum levels above the threshold of 10 mIU/ml. Positive anti-HBc was defined as a sample/cut-off (S/CO) ratio ≥1.0. HBV DNA was detected using the Cobas TaqMan HBV test (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) or TaqMan polymerase chain reaction assay (Daan Gene, Guangzhou, China), and the lower limit of normal was 20 IU/ml or 500 IU/ml, respectively.

Follow-up and outcomes

Patients were followed up and tested routinely at time points of weeks 12, 24, and 48 after the initiation of Peg-IFN therapy. Serum HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion were regarded as primary outcomes and secondary outcomes during follow-up, respectively. Serum HBsAg clearance was defined as HBsAg <0.05 IU/ml regardless of anti-HBs status, and HBsAg <0.05 IU/ml together with anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/ml was defined as HBsAg seroconversion.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians (interquartile range [IQR]), which were compared using the independent sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data are shown as counts (proportion), which were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test between groups. The ANOVA analysis of repeated measurements was conducted for interclass comparisons of repeated sample data, and the p value was corrected using the conservative Greenhouse–Geisser procedure. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess the value of the parameters for predicting HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion. Factors associated with HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion were identified using logistic regression analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.2.0 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) were used for the statistical analysis.

Results

Baseline features of subjects

The baseline characteristics of the patients were shown in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 42.0 years and 83.1% of patients were male. The median levels of ALT and AST were 25.5 U/L and 22.0 U/L, respectively. In aspect of HBV serological markers, the median levels of serum HBsAg, anti-HBc, and HBV DNA were 2.6 log10 IU/ml, 9.2 S/CO, and 1.8 log10 IU/ml, respectively. About 91.5% of patients were anti-HBe-positive, and 50.8% of patients had undetectable serum HBV DNA. During Peg-IFN-based therapy, serum anti-HBc levels were 9.1 S/CO, 9.3 S/CO, and 9.1 S/CO at week 12, week 24, and week 48 of treatment, respectively. At week 48, 52 (27.5%) and 30 (15.9%) patients achieved HBsAg clearance and seroconversion, respectively.

Patients were divided into two groups based on Peg-IFN regimens: the NAs add-on Peg-IFN group (add-on group) and the Peg-IFN combined with NAs or Peg-IFN monotherapy group (combination or monotherapy group). Patients in the add-on group had higher serum HBsAg (2.8 log10 IU/ml vs. 2.3 log10 IU/ml, p = 0.008) levels and lower ALT (23.8 U/L vs. 29.7 U/L, p = 0.012), AST (21.2 U/L vs. 22.4 U/L, p = 0.023), anti-HBc (9.0 S/CO vs. 9.6 S/CO, p = 0.003), and HBV DNA (1.3 log10 IU/ml vs. 2.8 log10 IU/ml, p < 0.001) levels than those in combination or monotherapy group (Table 1). Similarly, serum anti-HBc levels in add-on group were also lower at week 12 (8.7 S/CO vs. 9.5 S/CO, p < 0.001) and week 24 (8.9 S/CO vs. 9.6 S/CO, p = 0.003) than those in combination or monotherapy group. Patients in the combination or monotherapy group achieved relatively high rates of HBsAg clearance (31.6% vs. 23.4%, p = 0.208) and HBsAg seroconversion (21.1% vs. 10.6%, p = 0.050). The detailed HBsAg clearance rates in different NAs regimens in both the add-on group and the combination or monotherapy group were shown in Table S1, which reveals comparable HBsAg clearance rates in different regimens in these two groups.

Comparison of baseline features between patients with and without HBsAg clearance and seroconversion

We compared the clinical features of patients with and without HBsAg clearance under different Peg-IFN regimens (Table 2). Patients with HBsAg clearance had lower baseline HBsAg levels than those without HBsAg clearance (1.9 log10 IU/ml vs. 2.7 log10 IU/ml, p < 0.001). Similar results were observed in the add-on group and in the combination or monotherapy group. Of note, in combination or monotherapy group, serum anti-HBc levels at baseline (9.1 S/CO vs. 9.7 S/CO, p = 0.026), week 12 (9.0 S/CO vs. 9.9 S/CO, p < 0.001) and week 24 (9.0 S/CO vs. 9.8 S/CO, p = 0.021) of treatment were significantly lower in patients with HBsAg clearance compared to patients without HBsAg clearance.

Further comparisons of clinical features between patients with and without HBsAg seroconversion were conducted (Table S2). Overall, patients with HBsAg seroconversion had lower baseline HBsAg level (1.7 log10 IU/ml vs. 2.7 log10 IU/ml, p < 0.001), anti-HBc levels at baseline (8.9 S/CO vs. 9.3 S/CO, p = 0.036) and week 12 (8.2 S/CO vs. 9.2 S/CO, p = 0.001) than patients without HBsAg seroconversion. Similar results were also observed in both the add-on group and the combination or monotherapy group.

Factors associated with HBsAg clearance and seroconversion

We analysed the factors associated with HBsAg clearance and seroconversion in all patients, add-on and combination or monotherapy subgroups, respectively. The multivariate analysis showed that serum anti-HBc levels at week 12 were independently associated with HBsAg clearance in all patients (OR 0.739, 95% CI 0.576–0.949, p = 0.018) and combination or monotherapy subgroup (OR 0.455, 95% CI 0.256–0.809, p = 0.007). Baseline serum HBsAg level was also associated with HBsAg clearance in all patients (OR 0.444, 95% CI 0.307–0.643, p < 0.001) and combination or monotherapy subgroup (OR 0.417, 95% CI 0.234–0.743, p = 0.003), respectively (Table 3). Similarly, serum anti-HBc levels at week 12 remained independently associated with HBsAg seroconversion in all patients (OR 0.570, 95% CI 0.377–0.864, p = 0.008) and combination or monotherapy subgroup (OR 0.491, 95% CI 0.292–0.827, p = 0.008) (Table 4).

Serum HBsAg and anti-HBc levels for predicting HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion

According to the multivariate logistic regression analysis, baseline serum HBsAg and anti-HBc levels at week 12 were independent predictors of HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion. Thus, ROC curves were used to evaluate the predictive value of HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion (Table S3, Figure S2). Baseline HBsAg could predict HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion in all patients, as well as in add-on and combination or monotherapy subgroups. Serum anti-HBc level at week 12 could also predict HBsAg clearance with an area under ROC (AUROC) of 0.608 (95% CI: 0.516–0.701, p = 0.022) and HBsAg seroconversion with an AUROC of 0.697 (95% CI: 0.591–0.804, p < 0.001) in all patients. In the combination or monotherapy subgroup, serum anti-HBc at week 12 also showed relatively high predictive performance, with AUROCs of 0.740 (95% CI: 0.639–0.841, p < 0.001) for HBsAg clearance and 0.777 (95% CI: 0.673–0.882, p < 0.001) for HBsAg seroconversion. In the add-on group, anti-HBc level at week 12 could also predict HBsAg seroconversion (AUROC: 0.715, 95% CI: 0.498–0.933, p = 0.026).

Dynamic changes of HBsAg according to different week 12 anti-HBc levels during follow-up

The dynamic change in HBsAg was analysed based on serum anti-HBc levels at week 12 (Figure 1). The selection of optimal cut-off of serum anti-HBc value based on the highest AUROCs of serum anti-HBc predicts HBsAg clearance, with a cut-off value of 8.3 S/CO in all patients, 7.3 S/CO in add-on group, and 9.8 S/CO in combination or monotherapy group, respectively. All patients with low serum anti-HBc levels presented lower serum HBsAg levels compared to patients with high serum anti-HBc levels at each follow-up point (Figure 1a). However, the decline in serum HBsAg level was not statistically different at each follow-up point (Figure 1d). In the add-on group, patients with low serum anti-HBc levels also had lower serum HBsAg levels (Figure 1b) and a more significant decline in serum HBsAg levels (Figure 1e), especially at weeks 24 and 48. Of note, patients with low serum anti-HBc levels had lower serum HBsAg levels than those with high serum anti-HBc at week 12, week 24 (p < 0.05), and week 48 (p < 0.05) (Figure 1c) in the combination or monotherapy group. In addition, patients with low serum anti-HBc presented a more significant decline in serum HBsAg levels than patients with high serum anti-HBc levels at week 24 (p < 0.05), and week 48 (p < 0.05) in this group (Figure 1f).

Rates of HBsAg clearance and seroconversion stratified by serum HBsAg and anti-HBc

We further analysed the rates of HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion by combining the optimal cut-off values of baseline serum HBsAg and anti-HBc at week 12. Among all patients, 46.3% (19/41) and 36.6% (15/41) of patients with low serum HBsAg and anti-HBc levels achieved HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion, respectively (Figure 2a,b). However, only 7.1% (3/42) and 2.4% (1/42) of patients with high serum HBsAg and high anti-HBc achieved HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion, respectively. The predictive value of serum anti-HBc was further analysed in the add-on group (Figure 2c,d) and combination or monotherapy group (Figure 2e,f). The results revealed that patients with low anti-HBc levels had higher HBsAg clearance and HBsAg seroconversion rates in both the add-on group and combination or monotherapy group, and combined with low serum HBsAg would further help to identify patients with a higher possibility of functional cure.

Discussion

In the present study, lower serum anti-HBc levels at week 12 were significantly associated with higher HBsAg loss in HBeAg-negative CHB patients treated with Peg-IFN. Patients with low serum anti-HBc levels showed a significant decline in HBsAg levels during treatment. Combining serum anti-HBc at week 12 and baseline HBsAg could identify over half of the patients who achieved HBsAg clearance after 48 weeks of treatment, especially in the combination or monotherapy group.

Serum HBsAg clearance is an ideal endpoint for treatment and is associated with a decreased risk of adverse outcomes in patients with CHB [2,3]. Although Peg-IFN is a potential drug for achieving HBsAg clearance, several dilemmas limit its wide application in clinical practice, including side effects, high cost, and heterogeneity of treatment response. Therefore, identifying or establishing accurate indices for predicting treatment responses is important. Low baseline serum HBsAg levels have been demonstrated to be a favourable predictor for obtaining HBsAg clearance under Peg-IFN-related schemes, and baseline HBsAg < 1500 IU/ml was identified as the optimal cut-off value to predict HBsAg clearance [13–16]. Thus, in this real-world cohort of CHB patients, baseline HBsAg levels were lower than 1500 IU/ml in the majority of patients. Our results confirmed that low baseline HBsAg level is a strong predictor of both HBsAg clearance and seroconversion. Numerous studies have reported that the rate of HBsAg clearance varies from 20% to 40% in patients with HBsAg < 1500 IU/ml at week 48 of Peg-IFN treatment [16–19]. Our study also found that 27.5% and 15.9% of the patients achieved HBsAg clearance and seroconversion, respectively. Thus, identifying a novel index in addition to HBsAg for more accurate prediction of the antiviral response of Peg-IFN-based therapy is necessary.

Previous studies have demonstrated that serum anti-HBc levels can be a promising biomarker for monitoring the treatment response to both NAs and Peg-IFN [11,20,21]. Hou et al. reported that patients with higher baseline anti-HBc levels had higher rates of HBV DNA suppression and HBeAg seroconversion after Peg-IFN therapy [11]. High baseline serum anti-HBc levels were also found to be a strong predictor of HBeAg seroconversion in CHB patients under NAs therapy [20,21]. In contrast, low levels of serum anti-HBc have been reported to be associated with HBsAg loss in patients with CHB [9,10]. Hu et al. reported that untreated HBeAg-negative patients with anti-HBc levels <3 log10 IU/ml (53.0%) presented a higher spontaneous HBsAg clearance rate than those with higher anti-HBc levels (19.8%) after a 12-year follow-up period [9]. Another study also found that the HBsAg clearance rate was significantly higher in patients with low serum anti-HBc levels after Peg-IFN treatment, which was consistent with our findings [10]. However, of note, serum anti-HBc was measured using double-sandwich anti-HBc immunoassay in these studies [9–11]. Although the method showed better performance in terms of both sensitivity and specificity than the ARCHITECT assay, it has not been commercialized and is limited to wide application in clinical settings. The ARCHITECT anti-HBc assay is widely used for anti-HBc detection in clinical practice with satisfactory accuracy and high clinical applicability [22]. Our previous study demonstrated that baseline serum anti-HBc detected by the ARCHITECT assay can be a promising predictor of HBeAg seroconversion [12]. The association between HBsAg clearance and serum anti-HBc levels detected using the ARCHITECT assay is unclear. Our study showed that patients with HBsAg clearance had lower serum anti-HBc levels at each observation time point, especially at week 12, which was a strong predictor of HBsAg clearance. More importantly, serum anti-HBc at week 12 combined with baseline serum HBsAg could further stratify the possibility of HBsAg clearance, ranging from 7.1% to 78.9%. The results suggested that serum anti-HBc remains a valuable predictor of Peg-IFN-based treatment response.

Of note, previous studies confirmed that higher serum anti-HBc levels were associated with higher HBeAg clearance rates in HBeAg-positive CHB patients, whereas the present study found that higher serum anti-HBc levels were not a favourable factor for HBsAg clearance in HBeAg-negative patients [11,20,21]. The different roles of serum anti-HBc in predicting antiviral response in HBeAg-positive and HBeAg-negative CHB patients have not yet been fully elucidated. The appearance of serum anti-HBc is associated with the core antigen of HBV and often lasts for a lifetime [23]. Anti-HBc is a valuable marker for reflecting host-adaptive anti-HBV immune activation and intrahepatic viral load [9]. In HBeAg-positive CHB patients, serum anti-HBc levels were consistent with ALT levels and liver inflammation activity, rather than HBV DNA levels. The active host immunity response accelerates virus clearance, which may be an important cause of the high HBeAg clearance rate in HBeAg-positive CHB patients with elevated serum anti-HBc levels [8,21]. However, in HBeAg-negative patients, anti-HBc levels were determined by intrahepatic core antigen and positively associated with covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) load but were not associated with liver inflammation activity [9]. Therefore, HBeAg-negative patients with low serum anti-HBc levels may have lower intrahepatic cccDNA levels and a higher probability of achieving a functional cure. However, further studies are needed to explore these specific mechanisms.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study with limited sample size and follow-up time period. Thus, prospective studies with large sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are required to validate our findings. Second, the impact of HBV genotype on HBsAg clearance was not analysed in this study due to the lack of HBV genotype data. However, HBV genotype B and genotype C have been reported as the predominant genotypes in China [24]. Third, the baseline features were different, which may have caused inconsistent association of anti-HBc levels with HBsAg clearance in the combination or monotherapy group and add-on group. Lastly, we did not analyse separately for patients receiving Peg-IFN monotherapy and Peg-IFN combined with NAs, as in the previous study [25], due to the limited sample size of patients with Peg-IFN monotherapy, which might introduce bias into the results.

In conclusion, serum anti-HBc level detected by ARCHITECT assay was a strong predictor of HBsAg clearance and seroconversion in HBeAg-negative CHB patients receiving Peg-IFN-based therapy. Our findings suggest that, in addition to HBsAg, serum anti-HBc levels are useful for selecting patients who are more likely to respond to Peg-IFN-based therapy.
