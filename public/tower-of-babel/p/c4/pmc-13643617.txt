Under‐ and overdosing insulin in the hospital: A retrospective cohort study of dose deviation and patient outcomes
Authors: Hussein Zaitoon, Sher Matsri, Shira Nagler, Matteo M Lalovich, Ralph A DeFronzo, Eugene Feigin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsGlycaemic abnormalities during hospitalization are common and linked to morbidity and mortality, yet little is known about how deviations from guideline‐recommended insulin dosing affect outcomes. The aim of this study is to examine whether deviation from an expected insulin dose in non‐critically ill hospitalized adults with type 2 diabetes, compared with guideline‐concordant dosing, is associated with mortality, length of stay or dysglycaemias.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adults with type 2 diabetes hospitalized at a tertiary academic medical centre (2010–2025) who received ≥ 24 h of subcutaneous insulin. The expected total daily insulin dose (TDID) was calculated based on guideline‐concordant dosing using the ADA‐recommended range (0.3–0.6 units/kg/day), and the observed TDID was derived from medication records. Patients were classified as undertreated (< 0.3 units/kg/day), guideline‐concordant (0.3–0.6 units/kg/day) or overtreated (> 0.6 units/kg/day). Outcomes included length of stay, and 30‐day mortality, hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia.ResultsAmong 3430 hospitalized adults with type 2 diabetes receiving subcutaneous insulin, 37.6% received guideline‐concordant dosing, 43.7% were undertreated, and 18.7% were overtreated. Undertreatment was independently associated with increased 30‐day mortality (aHR 1.273, 95% CI 1.024–1.583; p = 0.03), while overtreatment was not. Undertreated patients had more hyperglycaemia and longer hospital stays (p < 0.001), whereas hypoglycaemia rates were similar between groups.ConclusionDeviation from guideline‐based insulin dosing is common in hospitalized adults with type 2 diabetes. Undertreatment was associated with increased 30‐day mortality and prolonged hospitalization. Monitoring insulin dose deviation may help identify patients at risk for adverse outcomes.Keywords: hospital mortality, hyperglycaemia, inpatient diabetes management, insulin dosing, length of stay

What's new?

What is already known?

Inpatient dysglycaemia (hyper‐ and hypoglycaemia) is common and linked to worse outcomes, including mortality and longer hospital stay. Guidelines recommend weight‐based scheduled insulin regimens, yet real‐world dosing often deviates due to dynamic clinical factors

What this study has found?

Among hospitalized adults with type 2 diabetes receiving subcutaneous insulin, deviation from guideline‐based insulin dosing was more common than guideline‐concordant treatment. Undertreatment occurred in nearly half of patients and was independently associated with increased 30‐day mortality, longer hospital stays and higher rates of hyperglycaemia, while overtreatment was not associated with increased 30‐day mortality or hypoglycaemia.

What are the implications of the study?

Deviation from expected insulin dosing, especially undertreatment, is associated with adverse outcomes. Standardizing and monitoring insulin dosing relative to guideline‐based expectations may improve inpatient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Glycaemic abnormalities during hospitalization—both hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia—are common and are independently associated with adverse outcomes including longer length of stay, ICU transfer, higher costs, readmission and mortality. 1 , 2 , 3 Insulin remains the cornerstone medication for inpatient glycaemic management; however, administering the appropriate insulin dose in the complex and dynamically changing inpatient environment is challenging. Patient caloric intake, stress response, renal function, steroid exposure, and level of care frequently change, and insulin orders are often modified or missed, leading to dosing deviations from guideline‐based expectations or a patient's home regimen. 4 , 5 , 6

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care 7 and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology (AACE) 6 guidelines both recommend scheduled insulin regimens, rather than sliding‐scale insulin alone, for most non‐critically ill hospitalized patients with persistent hyperglycaemia. Both suggest initiating insulin when blood glucose is consistently above 180 mg/dL and emphasize adjusting doses for age, renal function, nutritional status and hypoglycaemia risk. The ADA provides a broader recommended total daily dose (TDID) range of ~0.3–0.6 units/kg/day, allowing higher doses for patients with significant insulin resistance such as those with obesity, infection, or steroid use. In contrast, the AACE recommends a lower starting basal insulin dose of 0.2–0.4 units/kg/day, escalating as needed if hyperglycaemia persists. Additionally, the AACE offers more structured guidance on when to move from correctional insulin alone to full basal‐bolus therapy, while the ADA is less prescriptive but advises reducing high home basal doses (≥0.6 units/kg/day) to prevent hypoglycaemia. Overall, both guidelines prioritize safe, individualized dosing, but the AACE favours a more conservative initial approach with clearer escalation criteria.

While a recent study has examined the relationship between insulin administration errors and with glycaemic excursions in hospitalized patients, 8 no studies compared the insulin dose that a patient actually receives during hospital admission to an expected or guideline‐recommended dose and then examines whether the magnitude and direction of that deviation with clinical outcomes. Quantifying the insulin dose deviation could help clarify whether systematic underdosing or overdosing contributes to hypoglycaemic events, hyperglycaemic events, prolonged length of stay, mortality and readmission. 9

The aim of this study is to systematically evaluate insulin dosing practices in hospitalized adults by calculating the expected insulin dose for each patient based on weight‐based, guideline‐recommended formulas and comparing the dose with the actual insulin administered during the hospital stay. By quantifying the dose deviation, we seek to determine the effect of underdosing or overdosing on the patients' clinical outcomes.

METHODS

This was a retrospective cohort study of adult patients admitted to Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center (TASMC), a large tertiary care academic medical centre, between 2010 and 2025. The hospital has approximately 1500 inpatient beds and utilizes a fully integrated electronic health record system. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board, which granted a waiver of informed consent given the retrospective design and the use of deidentified data.

The study population included adults aged 18 years or older with diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) who received at least 24 h of subcutaneous insulin and had at least one documented blood glucose measurement during the hospitalization. At least 24 h of insulin treatment was chosen to exclude patients who received insulin as part of treatment for hyperkalaemia or isolated hyperglycaemia. We excluded admissions that were primarily for diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) because these conditions are managed using distinct intravenous insulin protocols that differ substantially from standard inpatient care. Pregnant patients were excluded due to unique insulin dosing requirements. Admissions were also excluded if patients received continuous intravenous insulin throughout hospitalization or had incomplete data on body weight, medication administration records or nutrition status that prevented accurate estimation of expected insulin dosing. Furthermore, we focused on medical units only, excluding patients admitted to the intensive care unit, surgical, psychiatric and OBGYN patients. Patients who were nothing by mouth (NPO) during admission and those who died within 72 h of admission were also excluded. For patients with multiple admissions during the study period, only the first qualifying hospitalization was included in the primary analysis to avoid clustering effects.

The primary exposure variable was the deviation between the calculated insulin dose and the actual daily insulin dose based on a standardized weight‐based formula. The expected total daily insulin dose (Expected‐TDID) was calculated using a widely accepted basal‐bolus insulin initiation approach of between 0.3 and 0.6 units/kg/day, consistent with the previously established inpatient diabetes management guidelines. 6 , 7 Adjustments for patients with significant renal dysfunction, advanced age, or markedly increased insulin resistance were not applied in this primary formula to maintain a standardized and reproducible calculation across the cohort, though these factors were accounted for in subsequent multivariable models. Expected‐TDID was recalculated based on the patient's most recent documented weight, recognizing that changes in clinical status could influence expected dosing over time.

The observed total daily insulin dose (Observed‐TDID) was calculated for each 24‐h period by summing all subcutaneous insulin units administered as recorded in the electronic health medication administration record. When intermittent intravenous insulin infusions were used, the amount delivered was converted to daily units and included in the Observed‐TDID.

Based on ADA inpatient diabetes management recommendations, insulin dosing was categorized using a weight‐adjusted total daily insulin dose range of 0.3–0.6 units/kg/day. Patients receiving less than 0.3 units/kg/day were classified as undertreated, patients receiving 0.3–0.6 units/kg/day were classified as guideline‐concordant, and patients receiving greater than 0.6 units/kg/day were classified as overtreated.

Because insulin requirements and prescribing patterns may change dynamically during hospitalization, we additionally evaluated within‐hospitalization variability in insulin dosing classification. Patients whose daily Observed‐TDID crossed between predefined dosing categories (undertreated, guideline‐concordant, or overtreated) during admission were considered exposure‐unstable. Approximately 28% of otherwise eligible patients demonstrated category crossover during hospitalization and were excluded from the revised primary analysis to minimize exposure misclassification and better reflect sustained insulin dosing patterns. Most crossover occurred between undertreatment and guideline‐concordant dosing, consistent with ongoing inpatient insulin titration during clinical stabilization.

The primary outcome of interest was hospital length of stay, measured in calendar days from admission to discharge. Secondary outcomes included the occurrence of inpatient hypoglycaemia, inpatient hyperglycaemia and 30‐day mortality. Hypoglycaemia was defined as any blood glucose value below 70 mg/dL, with severe hypoglycaemia defined as less than or equal to 54 mg/dL. Hyperglycaemia was defined as any glucose measurement greater than 180 mg/dL, and severe hyperglycaemia as greater than 300 mg/dL; hyperosmolar‐range hyperglycaemia was defined as any measurement greater than 500 mg/dL. Outcomes were extracted directly from the electronic records using standardized institutional definitions and validated algorithms.

The following parameters were collected from the electronic health records: patient demographics including age and sex; body mass index (BMI); baseline kidney function measured by the most recent creatinine test; type of diabetes was also available; most recent haemoglobin A1c; glucose measurements during admission; corticosteroid use during the hospital stay; concomitant use of other glucose‐lowering agents; and comorbidities. We also recorded nutrition status during hospitalization, including whether the patient was on an oral diet, NPO, or receiving enteral or parenteral nutrition, as these factors can significantly affect insulin requirements and glucose control.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics and distributions of both Expected‐TDID and Observed‐TDID. Continuous variables were presented using mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile ranges, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests, and categorical variables were compared using Chi‐square. Survival was compared using COX regression with and without adjustment for age, sex, HbA1c and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI). Secondary outcomes were compared using logistical regression adjusted for age, sex and CCI.

The primary analysis examined the association between three groups of insulin treatment appropriateness [guideline‐concordant patients, undertreatment, overtreatment] and 30‐day mortality. Secondary outcomes included length of stay, hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia.

Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value of ≤ 0.05. Results were reported in accordance with STROBE guidelines for observational studies (Figure 1). All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 29.0.0.0 (241).

RESULTS

Study population and overall outcomes

A total of 4764 hospitalized adults with type 2 diabetes receiving subcutaneous insulin therapy initially met eligibility criteria. Of these, 1334 patients (28.0%) crossed between insulin dosing categories during hospitalization and were excluded from the revised primary analysis to reduce exposure misclassification related to dynamic insulin titration. The final analytical cohort therefore included 3430 patients (Figure 1). The mean age of the cohort was 70.8 ± 13.4 years, and 1992 patients (58.8%) were male. Mean body weight was 79.6 ± 19.7 kg, and mean BMI was 30.2 ± 34.9 kg/m2. The median Charlson Comorbidity Index was 5 [4, 7].

Overall, 30‐day mortality occurred in 402 patients (11.7%). Hyperglycaemia between 181 and 300 mg/dL occurred in 871 patients (25.4%), severe hyperglycaemia > 300 mg/dL occurred in 2481 patients (72.3%), and hyperosmolar‐range hyperglycaemia > 500 mg/dL occurred in 598 patients (17.4%). Hypoglycaemia between 55 and 69 mg/dL occurred in 433 patients (12.6%), and severe hypoglycaemia < 55 mg/dL occurred in 365 patients (10.6%). Median hospital length of stay was 6.8 [4,12.3] days.

Classification by insulin treatment appropriateness

Patients were categorized according to concordance between observed and expected total daily insulin dose. Overall, 1290 patients (37.6%) received guideline‐concordant insulin dosing, 1500 (43.7%) were undertreated, and 640 (18.7%) were overtreated.

Association between insulin treatment appropriateness and mortality

For 30‐day mortality, undertreatment was associated with increased mortality risk in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. The unadjusted HR was 1.4 (95% CI 1.13–1.74; p = 0.002), and the adjusted HR was 1.273 (95% CI 1.024–1.583; p = 0.03). Overtreatment was not associated with increased 30‐day mortality in either unadjusted (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.55–1.05; p = 0.094) or adjusted analyses (aHR 0.981, 95% CI 0.719–1.38; p = 0.981). Figure 3.

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated similar findings. Using alternative insulin dose thresholds (< 0.225 and > 0.75 units/kg/day), undertreatment remained associated with increased 30‐day mortality (aHR 1.278, 95% CI 1.039–1.572; p = 0.02), whereas overtreatment was not associated with mortality after adjustment. Similarly, when patients who died within the first 72 h of admission were included, undertreatment remained independently associated with increased 30‐day mortality (aHR 1.29, 95% CI 1.025–1.624; p = 0.03). DISCUSSION

In this large cohort of non–critically ill, non‐surgical hospitalized adults receiving subcutaneous insulin, we observed substantial variation between actual insulin dosing and a standardized, weight‐based expected total daily insulin dose (Expected‐TDID). Approximately one‐third of patients received insulin doses within the ADA‐recommended weight‐adjusted dosing range, whereas 43.7% were undertreated and 18.7% were overtreated. These deviations were clinically meaningful: undertreatment was independently associated with significantly higher 30‐day mortality. Furthermore, undertreated patients demonstrated higher rates of moderate hyperglycaemia and longer hospital stays. Overtreatment was associated with high rates of severe hyperglycaemia and hyperosmolar‐range hyperglycaemia, without a corresponding increase in hypoglycaemia or 30‐day mortality.

Inpatient dysglycaemia is consistently associated with adverse outcomes and increased mortality. 10 Hyperglycaemia, irrespective of diabetes status, independently predicts healthcare‐associated infections, acute kidney injury, and stroke. 11 In critically ill patients, both hyper‐ and hypoglycaemia, as well as high glycaemic variability, are linked to increased in‐hospital mortality, need for renal replacement therapy, mechanical ventilation, and shock. 2 , 12 , 13 New‐onset hyperglycaemia without prior diabetes confers markedly higher mortality than either known diabetes or normoglycemia. 14 , 15 Dysglycaemia at admission and during hospitalization also predicts longer lengths of stay and increased likelihood of discharge to transitional care or nursing facilities, 14 , 16 while in patients with diabetes, it is associated with higher one‐year mortality, particularly among those with comorbidities. 10 , 17 In patients with sepsis and pneumonia, both excessive hyper and hypoglycaemia independently elevate in‐hospital mortality, 17 , 18 a finding reinforced by reviews linking uncontrolled inpatient glycemia to mortality, infections, and renal complications. 12 Our findings extend prior work linking inpatient dysglycaemia and adverse outcomes by focusing on insulin dose adequacy, not just glucose metrics. Although inpatient insulin therapy must be individualized for age, comorbidity, nutritional intake, renal function and evolving clinical status, our analysis suggests that substantial deviations from guideline‐based expectations may leave patients exposed to sustained hyperglycaemia and its associated consequences.

Undertreatment of glycemia likely represents a state of sustained metabolic stress. In the present study, patients who received substantially less insulin than recommended by guidelines were more likely to experience prolonged or recurrent hyperglycaemia. Persistent hyperglycaemia can impair neutrophil and macrophage function, worsen endothelial and microvascular dysfunction, disrupt fluid and electrolyte balance, and amplify inflammatory and oxidative pathways. 19 , 20 , 21 In acutely ill hospitalized adults, these abnormalities can accumulate rapidly and interfere with the normal physiologic response needed for recovery. 22 This mechanistic framework is consistent with our findings: undertreatment of glycemia was independently associated with increased 30‐day mortality after multivariable adjustment. Undertreated patients also experienced the longest hospital stays, potentially reflecting greater illness burden, delayed metabolic stabilization, or persistent hyperglycaemia during hospitalization that was inadequately addressed. Importantly, these findings remained consistent in sensitivity analyses using alternative insulin‐dose thresholds and when including patients who died within the first 72 h of admission.

Interestingly, although overtreated patients demonstrated high rates of severe hyperglycaemia (> 300 mg/dL) and hyperosmolar‐range hyperglycaemia (> 500 mg/dL), overtreatment was not associated with increased 30‐day mortality after multivariable adjustment. This pattern suggests that higher insulin doses may have been reactive rather than excessive in absolute terms, potentially reflecting severe insulin resistance, acute physiologic stress, corticosteroid exposure, or clinician response to marked hyperglycaemia. Notably, overtreatment was not associated with increased hypoglycaemia, suggesting that the higher administered insulin doses may have been appropriate for the patients' metabolic demands and closely monitored clinical settings. 23 , 24 In this context, higher insulin requirements may represent a marker of illness complexity and intensified glycaemic management rather than inappropriate prescribing alone.

In our cohort, average length of stay was significantly longer than the average length of hospitalization in internal medicine wards in Israel. 25 This suggests that our cohort was comprised of patients with more severe conditions requiring longer treatments than the usual internal medicine patients. Further strengthening this notion is the median CCI scores of 5, which reflects a substantial comorbidity burden and elevated long‐term mortality risk. 26

Our observations highlight an opportunity to improve inpatient glucose management. Standardized order sets, protocol‐driven pharmacist or nurse titration, and electronic decision‐support tools may reduce unwarranted dose deviations and facilitate earlier correction of hyperglycaemia. 27 , 28 , 29 Future studies should evaluate whether structured insulin titration protocols, the selective use of continuous glucose monitoring in hospitalized settings, and multi‐centre validation studies can determine whether aligning observed insulin doses more closely with guideline‐based expectations leads to better outcomes without increasing the risk of hypoglycaemia.

LIMITATIONS

This retrospective single‐centre observational study is subject to residual confounding, temporal practice variation and potential misclassification of both expected and observed insulin dosing. During the 2010–2025 study period, inpatient diabetes management evolved substantially, including implementation of digitized insulin‐adjustment protocols and transition from sliding‐scale to guideline‐based weight‐adjusted insulin regimens, which may have influenced prescribing practices. Expected insulin dose was estimated using a simplified weight‐based model that could not fully account for dynamic clinical factors such as illness severity, nutritional intake, renal dysfunction, palliative care status or individualized clinician decision‐making. Glycaemic events were derived from routine clinical glucose measurements with variable timing and frequency, potentially leading to under detection of hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia. In addition, patients who crossed insulin dosing categories during hospitalization were excluded to reduce exposure misclassification, which may have introduced selection bias toward patients with more stable insulin requirements.

Finally, because this was an observational study, the identified associations should not be interpreted as causal. Insulin dose deviation may partially reflect underlying illness severity, clinical complexity, or reactive treatment strategies rather than inappropriate prescribing alone.

We believe that these limitations do not diminish the clinical relevance of the observed associations, which reinforce the relationship between inpatient dysglycaemia, insulin dosing variability and adverse clinical outcomes in hospitalized patients.

CONCLUSIONS

Substantial deviations between actual and guideline‐based weight‐adjusted insulin dosing are common among hospitalized adults. Undertreatment was independently associated with increased 30‐day mortality, longer hospitalization and higher rates of hyperglycaemia. In contrast, overtreatment was not associated with increased 30‐day mortality or hypoglycaemia. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring insulin dosing relative to guideline‐suggested targets and recognizing insulin dose deviation as a potential marker of adverse clinical outcomes and illness complexity. Implementation of standardized insulin order sets, pharmacist‐driven titration, and electronic decision‐support tools may improve dosing consistency and patient outcomes.
