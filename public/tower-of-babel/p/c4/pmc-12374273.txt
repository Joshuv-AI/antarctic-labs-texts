Integrated analysis of O/K-antigen biosynthesis diversity and molecular serotyping scheme development of Vibrio alginolyticus
Authors: Keyi Yu, Zhenzhou Huang, Yanjun Li, Lizhi Ma, Xuemei Bai, Xiuping Fu, Biao Kan, He Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vibrio alginolyticus is a foodborne pathogen that poses considerable threats to human health and leads to substantial economic losses in the aquaculture industry. O and K antigen are bacterial surface structures with strong plasticity and environmental adaptability, making O antigen biosynthesis gene clusters (O-AGCs) and K antigen biosynthesis gene clusters (K-AGCs) good materials for studying bacterial genetic evolution. However, no studies have carried out in-depth analyses of the O and K antigen-related gene clusters of V. alginolyticus. In the present study, we conducted the first genome-based serogroup study of V. alginolyticus and performed a comprehensive analysis of the O- and K-AGCs. We elucidated their molecular evolutionary mechanism by comparing them at the whole gene cluster structural and molecular levels. There were 14 serotypes of O-AGCs between dgkA and gmhD and 48 serotypes of K-AGCs between gmhD and rjg among V. alginolyticus chromosomes. Serogroups O1, O4, and O11 were most prevalent in our sample set, accounting for approximately 60% of all V. alginolyticus strains. The K serogroups had more extensive diversity than the O serogroups. We further identified the K serogroup-specific gene, wzy, corresponding to the serogroup in a one-to-one parallel relationship. The variation in K antigen, distinguished by the unique sequence of the gene wzy, makes an essential contribution to the diagnostic investigations of V. alginolyticus. Our results represent the first insight into the O and K antigen diversity of V. alginolyticus and underline the importance of the structural versatility and functional adaptability of O- and K-AGCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04216-9.

Introduction

V. alginolyticus is a Gram-negative, halophilic, mesophilic, and facultative anaerobic Vibrio bacterium ubiquitously found in aquatic and marine environments, such as seawater [1] and river estuaries [2]. It is recognized as one of the 12 common opportunistic pathogens closely associated with human Vibrio-related diseases [3]. V. alginolyticus is an emerging human foodborne pathogen capable of causing numerous diseases with ecosystem-wide effects on humans and marine animals. It is also commonly found in human food poisoning and sporadic diarrhea in coastal areas [4], and can cause clinical diseases, including gastroenteritis[5], wound infection [6] and otitis externa [7]. It is the etiological agent of diseased fish [8], causing huge economic losses to the aquaculture industry.

The O antigen (also called O-specific polysaccharide), consisting of a polymer of repeating oligosaccharide units [9], is one of the most variable components of the cell surface structure of Gram-negative bacteria [10]. The fact that the O antigen forms a very complex diversity can be mainly attributed to differences in monosaccharide composition, arrangement, and linkage between monosaccharides [11, 12]. Diverse covalent modifications of O antigen occur, which are important for niche adaptation and play a large role in O antigen diversity and serotyping [13]. The O antigen is the primary target of the host immune system and bacteriophages [14, 15], and the specificity of O antigen plays an important role in bacterial virulence and fitness [16]. The genes required for O antigen biosynthesis are usually located in a contiguous gene cluster between two housekeeping genes in the bacterial chromosome, which is named the O antigen biosynthesis gene cluster (O-AGC). These genes can be divided into three categories according to their biological functions: oligosaccharide unit processing, nucleotide sugar synthesis, and glycosyltransferase genes [17]. Previous studies have shown that they are commonly involved in three major biosynthetic pathways: the Wzx/Wzy-dependent, ABC transport-dependent, and synthetase-dependent pathway [17–19]. Generally, the former is common in Gram-negative bacteria, whereas the latter two pathways are relatively rare. Most O-AGCs are organized between two conserved genes in many bacterial species, such as galF and gnd in Escherichia coli [18], gmhD and rjg in Vibrio cholerae [20], and hemH and gsk in Yersinia pseudotuberculosis [21].

At the same time, the K antigen (also called the capsular polysaccharide, CPS), an extracellular structure composed of long polysaccharide chains, has a typical negative charge and therefore produces a highly hydrophobic capsular layer [22, 23]. The K antigen is a critical virulence factor that enables immune evasion, protecting encapsulated bacterial cells from host immune defenses [24]. A single microbial species can form a range of structurally distinct K antigens, and K serotyping is often based on the genetic structure of the CPS. Previous studies have confirmed that V. alginolyticus possesses both LPS surface O antigen and K antigenic CPS [25]. The K antigen biosynthesis gene cluster (K-AGC) contains three major classes of genes: monosaccharide synthase, glycosyltransferase, and oligosaccharide unit processing enzyme genes. The K antigen biosynthetic pathway is often Wzy-dependent and corresponds to the O antigen.

Gram-negative bacteria exhibit remarkable biodiversity across diverse ecological niches. The O and K antigens, as bacterial surface structures characterized by high plasticity and robust environmental adaptability, render O- and K-AGCs ideal models for investigating bacterial genetic evolution. Moreover, O and K antigens are responsible for the serological diversity of bacterial species, and have been widely applied in clinical and epidemiological diagnosis to differentiate between any two serotypes [26, 27]. To date, an increasing number of bacterial serotypes have been determined, with their serotyping systems being increasingly improved; for example, V. parahaemolyticus is classified into 13 O serotypes and 71 K serotypes [28, 29]. V. alginolyticus has an evolutionary relationship with V. parahaemolyticus, both of which belong to the Harveyi clade in the genus Vibrio [30, 31]. Therefore, we presumed that V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus share similar O- and K-antigen gene cluster structures. In V. parahaemolyticus, the putative O- and K-AGCs have been identified in the available genome sequence of the O3:K6 strain RIMD2210633, and it has been found that O-AGC was flanked by dgkA (also called coaD) and gmhD, while K-AGC was flanked by gmhD and rjg [12].

The genetic diversity of O- and K-AGCs provides theoretical support for the application of serotyping schemes to classify Gram-negative bacteria [32]. The O-AGC structure corresponding to any serogroup is unique among Vibrio species [33]. Analysis of the O- and K-AGCs has strong potential to provide a clearer picture of the population structure of V. alginolyticus. As an opportunistic pathogen, V. alginolyticus has not received sufficient attention with the current studies on V. alginolyticus mainly focus on virulence factors. A complete genomic dataset for this bacterial species has not yet been established to explore the diversity of gene clusters encoding O-antigen and K-antigen, which would greatly limit the development of serogroup-based molecular typing methods and lead to a delay in establishing a serotyping scheme for V. alginolyticus. Meanwhile, no commercial diagnostic antiserum is currently available.

In the present study, O- and K-AGCs of V. alginolyticus were used as models to understand the genetic characteristics of O and K antigens among V. alginolyticus strains, representing the first genome-based serogroup study of this species. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of these putative O- and K-AGCs and elucidated their molecular evolutionary mechanisms by comparing them at the whole-gene cluster structural and molecular levels. We analyzed 14 types of O-AGCs between dgkA and gmhD and 48 types of K-AGCs between gmhD and rjg among V. alginolyticus chromosomes in depth. Furthermore, we identified K serogroup-specific genes that corresponded to the respective K serogroups. Our molecular serotyping analysis provides new insights into the genetic diversity of O- and K-AGCs in V. alginolyticus and provides theoretical support for the development of molecular diagnostic methods to identify this important pathogen.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

From the 615 V. alginolyticus genomes listed in the NCBI GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/, accessed on June 10, 2023), those with low sequencing quality were removed. Another 133 previously described isolates [34] collected from several Chinese seas were also included in the study. Due to draft genomes, O:K gene clusters may be interrupted, resulting in the dispersion of different contigs. Overall, 155 strains with complete gene clusters were selected for further analyses. The detailed information is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Whole-genome sequencing

V. alginolyticus strains were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at the BGI Genomics Institute (Shenzhen, China) with a depth of 200 × coverage. FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) was used to evaluate the raw data and low-quality reads were filtered. Reads containing adapters, those with a proportion of N bases greater than 10%, contaminated and duplicated reads, as well as low-quality reads (Phred score ≤ 20 bases exceeding 50% of the read length) were removed. Then, the clean data were assembled into contigs using SPAdes v3.8.2 [35].

O- and K-AGC genetic region extraction and gene prediction

Each O-AGC between dgkA and gmhD and between gmhD and rjg of V. alginolyticus genomes were extracted from the draft genome sequence for further analysis. Open reading frames (ORFs) prediction and annotation were performed using Prodigal v2.6.3 [36] and Prokka v1.13.3 [37]. Homology searches were performed against several databases, including web-BALST, Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG, www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/COG/), and the PFAM (pfam.sanger.ac.uk) protein motif databases. The TMHMM v.2.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/) program was used to predict transmembrane domains within the protein sequences.

Further network analysis of the K-AGC genetic region

A relationship network of the K-AGC genetic region was constructed by combining multiple bioinformatic tools. Briefly, the number of homologous genes shared by any two serogroups were calculated. The homologous genes were determined by using BLAST + [38]. The R igraph package was used to display the relationship network. The width of the links was related to the number of shared genes. Then the non-redundant homologous gene dataset using and CD-HIT [39] tools and converted the gene dataset into a matrix in row × column format as K-AGC × gene.

Phylogenetic analysis of K serogroup-specific gene

Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the wzy gene protein sequences of the 155 V. alginolyticus strains. The genetic distance and sequence similarity of paired wzy genes were calculated in MEGA v7.0 [40] using the maximum-likelihood algorithm and Kimura’s 2-parameter model. Bootstrap resampling was set to 1000.

Identification of the putative O and K antigen gene clusters of V. alginolyticus

BLAST sequence homology comparison between the whole genome sequence of the V. alginolyticus type strain ATCC 17749T and the three known O:K gene clusters of V. parahaemolyticus corresponding to O3:K6, O4:68, and O4:K12 serotypes revealed a contiguous O:K gene cluster sequence of V. alginolyticus with 36.2 kbp in length (Fig. 1). General feature of the O- and K- AGCs genetic region

A total of 155 putative O- and K-AGCs from V. alginolyticus genomes were obtained in this study (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). For O-AGCs, these gene clusters consisted of 9–19 ORFs, with an average of 16 ORFs per O-AGC. The size of O-AGCs ranged from 8.1 kbp (strain 40B, including 9 ORFs) to 18.9 kbp (strain VN-7501, including 19 ORFs), and the GC content ranged from 39.82 (strain Va154) to 42.83% (strain Va68). Among V. alginolyticus genomes, each K-AGC contained 16–35 ORFs. The size of K-AGCs ranged from 18.6 kbp (strain Va152, including 16 ORFs) to 35.3 kbp (strain Va70 = Va94 = Va98 = Va99, including 32 ORFs), and the G+C content ranged from 38.60 (strain Va194 and Va195) to 41.55% (strain UCD-32C).

Pan analysis of O and K antigen among the V. alginolyticus strains

According to the results of gene annotation and homologous comparison analysis, a putative gene pool, including 114 homologous genes, was found for O antigen biosynthesis in V. alginolyticus (Fig. 2A). Similarly, 605 homologous genes were identified in all K-AGC genetic regions (Fig. 2B). The two-dimensional hierarchical clustering heatmap revealed compositional discrepancies in genetic structures among K-AGC-related homologous genes, segregating these K-AGC sequences into two primary subdivisions designated as group 1 and group 2, respectively (Fig. 2B). Such differences were not observed in the genetic structures of O-AGC. Comparing the O-AGCs between V. alginolyticus and other species, it was found that most O-antigen encoding regions (OERs, which are functionally defined genomic regions responsible for encoding genes related to O-antigen synthesis) had the highest homology with V. parahaemolyticus, and the identity parameter can generally reach more than 95%. Therefore, it was speculated that these similar genes have the same biosynthetic functions. Specifically, kdkA, kdtA, waaF, and msbB were present in all 14 O-AGCs and were involved in encoding 3-deoxy-D-manno-octulosonic acid kinase, 3-deoxy-D-manno-octulosonic acid transferase, ADP-heptose-lipooligosaccharide heptosyltransferase, and lipid A biosynthesis myristoyltransferase, respectively (Supplementary Table 4). The rmlB (encoding dTDP-glucose 4,6-dehydratase) and rmlA (encoding glucose-1-phosphate thymidylyltransferase) genes were also found in most O-AGCs.

Organization and distribution of K-AGCs

V. alginolyticus strains were divided into 48 different K serogroups based on the rich complexity of their K-AGC homologous gene composition, which were named K1–K48 (Fig. 4). Statistically, K43 (n=15) was the most prevalent among V. alginolyticus strains, followed by K45 (n=14), K48 (n=12), K47 (n=11), K37 (n=11), K34 (n=11), and K6 (n=9). The serogroups for the remaining K-AGCs are listed in Table 2. The representative V. alginolyticus K-AGCs of groups 1 and 2 are listed in Fig. 2A and 2B, respectively, suggesting high genetic diversity. After BLAST alignment of these K-AGCs sequences, the results showed that the two ends were conserved regions with high similarity, while the source of variation was mainly concentrated in the middle sequence region. Each category of distinct K-AGCs sequences indicated a unique K serogroup in V. alginolyticus bacterial population. Because K antigens have a more complex diversification than O antigens, we further analyzed the network relationships among the various K antigens. After a de-redundant comparison, we found that more than half of the homologous genes (n=359, 59.3%) were unique to the 48 K serogroups. Next, we calculated the paired homologous genes that constitute the K-AGCs sequence, resulting in the formation of an intricate relationship between the K-AGCs and a spherical network (Fig. 5A). Overall, there was a relatively low correlation between a single K-AGC and any other K-AGCs. Certain K-AGCs that shared a large number of homologous genes were more closely related, for example, the highly close correlation between the K36 and K37 serogroups. The organization of homologous genes shared by any number of paired K-AGCs revealed a typical power-law distribution, suggesting that there were very few shared gene fragments between these K antigens (Fig. 5B). The relationship network for the 48 different K serogroups and their homologous genes agreed with the two major subdivisions of K-AGCs (Fig. 5C). Phylogenetic analysis of wzy gene in the K antigen

We found that all K-AGCs among 155 V. alginolyticus strains possessed both wzx and wzy genes, whose biological functions involved encoding K antigen flippase and K antigen polymerase, respectively (Supplementary Table 5). This finding reveals that the K antigen biosynthetic pathway uses the Wzx/Wzy-dependent pathway. Sequence alignment showed that the wzy gene had > 99.5% DNA similarity across the same serogroup, whereas the sequence similarity was less than 10% in different serogroups. The DNA sequence length of wzy ranged from 735 to 1,869 bp, while the amino acid sequence length ranged from 245 to 623 aa. The G+C content of gene wzy varied from 27.09% to 40.05%. The number of transmembrane structural domains in each wzy gene was predicted to be 5–14.

A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the amino acid sequences of wzy (Fig. 6). The tree showed that 155 strains of V. alginolyticus formed 48 separate and independent evolutionary branches with high bootstrap support according to the concatenated alignment sites. Notably, each monophyletic branch corresponded to the 48 different K serogroups identified in this study, with a one-to-one parallel relationship. This evidence demonstrates that wzy is a K serogroup-specific gene in V. alginolyticus, which was also consistent with the findings in Enterobacteriaceae species. The variation in wzy gene sequences can therefore be considered the basis for the diversity in the system of molecular typing for K antigen serogroups.Fig. 6The phylogenetic tree based on the amino acid sequences of wzy gene among 48 V. alginolyticus K serogroups. Discussion

Subtle variations in O and K antigen genetic structures provide the basis for the development of intraspecific serological classification schemes for Gram-negative bacteria. The first serological method based on the structures of the O antigen was proposed by Kauffmann and White for the genus Salmonella, which clearly demonstrated that the serotypes of Salmonella genus members could be identified by the presence of saccharide substitution of mannose or galactose [41]. From 1945 to 1950, a specific serotype of E. coli was found to be associated with outbreaks of enteritis in newborns in nurseries, and serotyping was subsequently used for epidemiological investigation of organisms [42, 43]. The serotyping method became the "gold standard" for strain identification and epidemiological investigation, and it was used as a basic tool for outbreak research and surveillance [44]. Currently, clinical and aquaculture infections caused by opportunistic pathogenic V. alginolyticus are major public health problems. However, the genetic structure of its O antigen and K antigen has not been well characterized so far. In this study, we established the most complete genome dataset of V. alginolyticus to date. Through non-redundant homologous genes analysis, we explored the genomic polymorphism of coding gene clusters for O antigen and K antigen in the V. alginolyticus population.

In this study, 14 O serogroups were identified, with the O1 serogroup accounting for 38.83% of isolates, suggesting it is the dominant prevalent type. The O-AGCs of V. alginolyticus exhibit > 95% homology with those of the close relative V. parahaemolyticus, implying they may share O antigen synthesis mechanisms through horizontal gene transfer or possess common ancestral gene modules in evolutionary history. Phage predation, environmental stresses (e.g., nutrient competition, antimicrobial exposure), and horizontal gene transfer are critical additional forces shaping antigen evolution, particularly for O antigens. This high conservation provides insights for cross-species antigenic cross-reactivity studies. Core gene analysis revealed that kdkA, kdtA, waaF, and msbB are stably present in all 14 O-AGCs, indicating their critical roles in basal O antigen synthesis and bacterial viability. These genes are involved in the biosynthesis of LPS core regions that directly influence membrane integrity and pathogenicity. For example, msbB-encoded myristoyltransferase modifies lipid A to enhance bacterial resistance to host innate immune responses [45]. Additionally, the presence of rmlA and rmlB in most O-AGCs suggests that V. alginolyticus primarily synthesizes deoxy sugars via the Rml pathway [46], consistent with O antigen synthesis pathways in other Vibrio spp.

The remarkable structural diversity of K antigens depends almost entirely on the genetic variation in K-AGCs. The chemical composition of the capsule is mainly composed of polysaccharides, polypeptides, and proteins [47, 48]. Polysaccharides are the capsular components of most bacteria, whose molecular composition and configuration are usually diverse and complex [47], whereas polypeptides are components of a few bacterial capsules, such as Bacillus anthracis [49]and Yersinia pestis [50]. In recent decades, with the continuous development of sequencing technology, hundreds of bacterial K-AGCs have been deciphered. For example, Streptococcus pneumoniae can be divided into 98 currently recognized K serotypes [51]. Various CPS structures of E. coli have determined the structure of more than 80 K antigens in this species [52]. The diversity of K serotypes may be the result of adaptation to the host or acceptance of external selection pressure [53].

This study classified Vibrio alginolyticus into 48 independent K serogroups (K1-K48) based on the complex composition of K antigen gene clusters (K-AGCs), revealing a highly diversified genetic landscape. Serogroups such as K45 and K48 showed significantly higher prevalence (n ≥ 9), suggesting these serotypes may possess stronger environmental adaptability or transmission advantages. As the main component of capsular polysaccharides, K antigens directly participate in critical pathogenic processes such as immune evasion and adhesive colonization, thus facing stronger host immune selection pressure that drives high-frequency variation to avoid recognition [54]. Statistical results showed that over half of the homologous genes (59.3%) were specific to single serogroups, and gene fragments shared between serogroups were extremely rare, indicating that the evolution of V. alginolyticus K antigens primarily rely on lineage-specific differentiation rather than frequent horizontal gene transfer. This may be related to the limited host diversity and restricted gene exchange in its marine ecological environment.

Network analysis further revealed that although most serogroups showed weak genetic relatedness. A few serogroups such as K36 and K37 shared close homologous genes, suggesting they may originate from recent common ancestors or local gene recombination events. This coexistence of "closely related clusters" and "independently differentiated clusters" reflects the dual evolutionary mechanisms of V. alginolyticus K-AGCs. The local gene recombination can generate closely related serogroups, while overall genetic isolation maintains serogroup independence. Notably, the two major groups (group 1 and group 2) shared almost no homologous genes (except for the conserved region genes gmhD and rjg), implying their evolutionary paths may have diverged early, which possibly be related to adaptations to different ecological niches (such as host species differences and salinity tolerance ranges).

This study for the first time demonstrates that the biosynthesis of the K antigen in V. alginolyticus is mediated by the Wzx/Wzy-dependent pathway. A previous study has demonstrated that the Wzy-dependent and ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter-dependent pathways are two common pathways with different polymer biosynthesis strategies in the assembly of most K antigens [55]. These synthetic and transporter-related gene clusters have been applied to the study of genetic evolution in a variety of bacteria, such as E. coli and S. pneumoniae, where there is a high degree of diversity in K antigens, which can be attributed to DNA recombination between strains or the uptake of external DNA [56]. This finding aligns with the established K antigen synthesis mechanism in Enterobacteriaceae [57–59], highlighting the conservation of this pathway across Gram-negative bacteria. Notably, phylogenetic analysis reveals that the 48 distinct K serogroups each correspond to independent monophyletic branches, exhibiting a strict one-to-one correspondence. This observation establishes the wzy gene as a serogroup-specific molecular determinant for K antigens in V. alginolyticus. The wzy gene encodes a glycosyltransferase, where point mutations or domain rearrangements in its nucleotide sequence may alter substrate specificity, leading to the biosynthesis of K antigens with distinct glycan architectures [60]. Its sequence variations directly dictate the structural diversity of K antigens, thereby driving serotype differentiation. Thus, the evolutionary pressures on the wzy gene represent a central evolutionary force shaping serotype diversity. This "one gene-one serotype" paradigm offers a robust molecular typing framework for V. alginolyticus. The targeted analysis of wzy gene variants enables rapid serotyping of clinical isolates, circumventing the limitations of traditional serological method. Notably, the strict correspondence between wzy genes and serogroups observed in this study differs from the one-to-many phenomenon of serotypes and gene clusters in some Enterobacteriaceae. This discrepancy may reflect differences in selective pressures (e.g., host immune pressure, niche competition) faced by different bacterial genera during evolutionary history. Future studies should expand strain sample sizes and integrate genomic-level recombination analysis to decipher the evolutionary dynamics of wzy gene, thereby fully elucidating the mechanisms underlying K antigen diversity in V. alginolyticus.

In this study, the genetic diversity of putative O- and K-AGCs in V. alginolyticus was determined. To our knowledge, this study is the first to catalogue both the O and K serogroups within the V. alginolyticus population. Given the structural versatility and functional adaptability of O and K surface antigens, our work helps facilitate the development of V. alginolyticus vaccines based on surface polysaccharide antigens, the establishment of clinical serotyping systems, and the epidemiological surveillance of V. alginolyticus infections.
