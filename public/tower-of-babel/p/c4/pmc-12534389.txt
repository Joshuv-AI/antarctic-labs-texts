Generating detectors from anomaly samples via negative selection for network intrusion detection
Authors: Zhiyong Li, Xiang Wei, Chunyan Li, Jianhong Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Negative selection algorithms (NSAs), which simulate the human immune mechanism for network anomaly detection, represent a promising approach in this field. Traditional NSAs randomly generate candidate detectors in high-dimensional feature subspaces. However, network anomaly detection datasets such as NSL-KDD typically exhibit sample distributions concentrated in low-dimensional subspaces. This dimensional mismatch causes traditional NSAs to underperform due to insufficient mature detector generation. To address this limitation, we leverage underutilized anomaly samples from the training set as candidate detector centers. Since these samples encode critical information about feature space distribution, they enable effective generation of mature detectors within the relevant low-dimensional subspaces. Furthermore, to mitigate misclassification of anomalies within coverage holes, we implement secondary classification based on the class attributes of nearest neighbor samples. Experiments on the NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15 datasets showed our method outperforming eight other algorithms.

Introduction

With accelerating informatization and digitalization, cybersecurity threats in cyberspace have intensified significantly. According to the Kaspersky Security Bulletin 2024, global malware attacks reached 302 million incidents-averaging approximately 820,000 detections daily1. In this context, Network Anomaly Detection Systems (NADS), also termed Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS), monitor malicious activities and policy violations within networks to provide robust protection against cyberattacks2. As a critical cybersecurity infrastructure component, network anomaly detection constitutes an indispensable element of network behavior analysis and represents an active research domain in both academia and industry3. Contemporary methodologies addressing this challenge include Artificial Neural Networks4, Support Vector Machines (SVM)5, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)6, clustering techniques7, deep learning architectures8, Bayesian networks9, and negative selection algorithms10, among others.

This study focuses on the application of the Negative Selection Algorithm (NSA) in network anomaly detection. Inspired by the human immune system, which protects the body by distinguishing self from non-self to identify and eliminate foreign pathogens11, network anomaly detection operates on a strikingly similar principle. Both systems maintain stability within dynamic environments by differentiating between normal (self) and anomalous (non-self) entities. Consequently, the NSA, derived from this biological paradigm, has been considered particularly well-suited for network anomaly detection12.Within the human immune system, immature T cells undergo a process of negative selection (self-tolerance) in the thymus to achieve maturity. T cells that react strongly with self-molecules are eliminated, thereby preventing mature T cells from mistakenly attacking the body’s own tissues. Mature T cells exiting the thymus are thus tolerant to self but capable of recognizing non-self threats.In 1994, Professor Stephanie Forrest at the University of New Mexico proposed the Negative Selection Algorithm13. This algorithm simulates the T-cell maturation mechanism to generate detectors capable of identifying network anomalies. Figure 1 illustrates the core concepts of the NSA14.Fig. 1Core Concepts of the Negative Selection Algorithm.

Core Concepts of the Negative Selection Algorithm.

As illustrated in Figure 1, the NSA comprises two fundamental phases: training and detection.During the training phase, randomly generated candidate detectors undergo self-tolerance exposure, eliminating those detectors that recognize any self-antigens; only candidates demonstrating no self-reactivity mature into functional detectors and join the detector set for subsequent non-self identification. In the detection phase, test samples matching (i.e., being covered by) any mature detector are classified as non-self, while samples failing to match any detector in the set are classified as self. Crucially, the NSA training phase requires only self-samples yet enables the detection of non-self anomalies. This distinctive feature underpins NSA’s widespread application in network anomaly detection10, network intrusion detection15, fault detection16, civil engineering structural health monitoring17, and related domains. Fundamentally, these applications constitute binary classification problems distinguishing self (normal) from non-self (abnormal)18.

Although the NSA has gained widespread adoption in network anomaly detection, its application faces two fundamental limitations in high-dimensional feature spaces: detector generation difficulty and the hole problem. Traditional NSA employs random candidate detector generation, which performs adequately in low-dimensional spaces with uniform sample distribution. However, in high-dimensional spaces like the NSL-KDD dataset, sample distribution becomes sparse and complex, with most samples concentrated in low-dimensional subspaces. The randomness of detector generation proves inefficient here, as candidate detectors rarely land in the relevant feature subspaces where actual samples reside. Consequently, most randomly generated detectors are eliminated during training, resulting in insufficient mature detectors and algorithm failure.

To address the detector generation challenge, we propose using abnormal samples from the training set as detector centers. Since these samples encapsulate authentic feature space distribution characteristics, they enable targeted detector generation within the actual sample subspaces. Notably, while traditional NSA utilizes only normal samples for training, our approach incorporates both normal and abnormal samples.

Regarding the hole problem-uncovered regions where samples are misclassified as normal-we implement a nearest-neighbor reclassification strategy. Samples within holes are reclassified based on the class attributes of their nearest neighbors, thereby reducing misclassification of abnormal samples in these uncovered regions.

To summarize, the principal contributions of this work are: Abnormal sample-guided detector generation: Departing from traditional NSA’s random detector generation, we propose using abnormal training samples as detector centers. Section 5 concludes the study and suggests future research directions.

Related work

The historical evolution of negative selection algorithms commenced in 1994 when Professor Stephanie Forrest pioneered the negative selection algorithm, generating detectors that simulate T-cell maturation mechanisms to identify network abnormalities13. Subsequent development occurred in 2000 with Hofmeyr and Forrest’s Artificial Immune System (ARTIS) for network intrusion detection, utilizing binary representations of antibodies/antigens and r-continuous bit matching for similarity measurement19. A significant advancement emerged in 2003 through GonzÃ¡lez et al.’s Real-Valued Negative Selection Algorithm (RNSA), which normalized antigens and antibodies to unit hypercube space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,1]^n$$\end{document} and employed Minkowski distance metrics, thereby extending NSA to real-valued applications20. This progression culminated in 2004 with Ji and Dasgupta’s V-Detector algorithm featuring variable-radius detectors, where dynamically adjusted radii enable large detectors to cover extensive non-self regions while compact detectors address coverage gaps (holes)21. Figure 2 visually contrasts non-self space coverage between fixed-radius and variable-radius detectors in 2D feature space: green circles denote self-regions and blue circles represent detectors, demonstrating how variable-radius detectors achieve superior non-self coverage with greater efficiency.Fig. 2Coverage Performance Comparison Between Fixed-Radius and Variable-Radius Detectors.

Coverage Performance Comparison Between Fixed-Radius and Variable-Radius Detectors.

Following pivotal transitions from binary to real-valued space and fixed to variable-radius detectors, NSAs have gained substantial adoption in network anomaly detection due to their capability to identify unknown attacks without prior knowledge. Subsequent innovations include Gong et al. (2012) introducing self-region covering detectors to reduce training time while improving detection rates through Euclidean distance-based hole classification22; Chen et al. (2013) developing hierarchical clustering-based CB-RNSA23; Idris (2014) optimizing detectors via particle swarm optimization24; Lin et al. (2015) proposing bidirectional inhibition-based BIORV-NSA for enhanced coverage efficiency25; Saurabh and Verma (2016) creating EPAADPS with self-tuning evolutionary detectors26; Tao et al. (2017) designing antigen density-driven ASD-RNSA with antibody suppression termination criteria27; Ziwen et al. (2018) enhancing V-Detector with phased generation (Prophase large-radius/Anaphase small-radius detectors) for wireless sensor networks28; Weikai et al. (2019) developing IDIA combining shift-mutation NSA with dynamic clonal selection for industrial cloud security29; Chao et al. (2020) creating ADC-NSA using non-self cluster centers/outliers for detector generation30; Liang et al. (2021) proposing multisource detectors via chaotic/DNA-GA methods31; Kim et al. (2022) implementing hybrid NSA/clone selection for industrial multi-class detection32; and Hanyuan et al. (2023) establishing hierarchical differential evolution-based intrusion detection33. XiaoWen et al. (2024) addressed the hole problem through self-antigen-based candidate detectors with complementary space representation34, while Lu Peng et al. (2025) employed multi-layer immune tolerance to reduce false positives35.

While numerous scholars have successfully applied NSA to network anomaly detection with significant results, two fundamental challenges persist: detector generation difficulties in high-dimensional feature spaces and the unresolved hole problem. Previous approaches to dimensionality challenges primarily employed reduction techniques-including principal component analysis26,28–30, feature extraction31, and feature selection36–though earlier studies23–25 lacked validation on standard datasets like KDD CUP99 and NSL-KDD. Our methodology addresses the detector generation problem by utilizing anomaly samples from training data as detector centers. Since these anomalies naturally cluster in low-dimensional subspaces, this approach enables efficient detector generation within their actual distribution regions. Crucially, unlike traditional NSA that exclusively uses normal samples during training, our algorithm effectively leverages abnormal samples. Regarding the hole problem, where prior solutions focused on coverage reduction24,27,28,30,31, we implement nearest-neighbor reclassification based on adjacent sample attributes to mitigate abnormal sample misclassification in uncovered regions.

The proposed algorithm

In this section, we first analyze limitations in traditional negative selection algorithms, then present our proposed solution.

Limitations of the traditional negative selection algorithm

As established, the dual challenges of detector generation in high-dimensional feature spaces and the hole problem persist in current NSAs. We address these fundamental limitations separately in the following subsections.

High-dimensional detector generation

We first define the feature subspaces, then analyze the distribution patterns of samples in the NSL-KDD dataset, and finally demonstrate that randomly generated candidate detectors exhibit low probability of occupying the low-dimensional subspaces where samples predominantly reside. This empirically confirms that samples concentrate in low-dimensional feature subspaces while candidate detectors distribute sparsely across high-dimensional space, fundamentally impeding effective detector generation in high-dimensional feature spaces.

Proved Corollary 1 demonstrates the difficulty for randomly generated candidate detectors to lie within the low-dimensional target subspace containing the samples. This constitutes the fundamental blind spot in random candidate detector generation. For a candidate detector to mature, it must undergo a self-tolerance process to eliminate those recognizing normal samples. Because randomly generated candidate detectors reside in the high-dimensional feature space while samples inhabit a low-dimensional feature subspace, the self-tolerance process removes a vast number of candidates. Consequently, the algorithm fails due to an insufficient number of mature detectors produced.

The hole problem in high-dimensional feature spaces

We first define the concept of holes, then analyze why they readily form at class domain boundaries and demonstrate their inevitability using the Haberman’s Survival dataset. Finally, we explain that the hole problem becomes significantly more serious as the dimensionality of the feature space increases.

Holes are regions within the feature space that remain uncovered by any detector. The negative selection algorithm aims to generate detectors that cover as much non-self space (representing abnormal samples) as possible while avoiding coverage of self space (normal samples). However, due to the inherent irregularity of the boundary separating self and non-self regions, detectors struggle to achieve complete non-self coverage without inadvertently overlapping self areas, thereby creating holes. This problem is particularly pronounced where self and non-self regions intersect and overlap. We illustrate this phenomenon using the Haberman’s Survival dataset.Fig. 3Haberman’s Survival Dataset.

Haberman’s Survival Dataset.

Figure 3(a) shows the sample distribution of Haberman’s Survival dataset. Blue asterisks (*) represent self samples, while red circles (o) represent non-self samples. As illustrated, samples are concentrated in the bottom region, where normal and abnormal samples overlap significantly. In contrast, samples in the top region are sparsely distributed.

Consequently, detectors generated in the top region can utilize larger radii to cover more non-self space, resulting in fewer holes. However, in the dense bottom region, only small-radius detectors can be generated. This leaves substantial non-self space uncovered, leading to more holes.

Figure 3(b) displays the detector distribution generated by the V-Detector algorithm on this dataset. It demonstrates the expected pattern: detectors in the top region have large radii and few holes, while those in the bottom region have small radii and many holes.

The Haberman’s Survival dataset example illustrates that holes tend to form in boundary regions between normal and abnormal samples. This problem becomes particularly severe when these sample categories intersect or overlap. As feature space dimensionality increases, sample distribution becomes sparse, making it increasingly difficult to cover the entire non-self region with a finite number of detectors. Even with more detectors, coverage density may still decrease relative to the exponential growth of space volume.

When covering non-self space with hypersphere detectors in n-dimensional space, the volume V of any given hypersphere is given by the well-known formula:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} V(r)=\frac{\pi ^{n / 2}}{\Gamma \left( \frac{n}{2}+1\right) } r^n \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Where r is the hypersphere radius and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma$$\end{document} denotes the gamma function. If we cover the non-self space with k such hyperspheres, the total coverage volume is kV(r).

To completely cover an n-dimensional unit hypercube (of volume \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1^n = 1$$\end{document}), the following condition must be satisfied:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} kV(r) \ge 1 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Since hypersphere volume decays rapidly with increasing dimensionality n, the number of detectors k required for equivalent coverage grows exponentially. Furthermore, mutual detector overlap makes it prohibitively difficult to cover the entire non-self region with finite detectors in high-dimensional space, inevitably leaving numerous coverage holes.

Our solution

We contend that the randomness-induced inefficiency in candidate detector generation constitutes the primary challenge for detector formation in high-dimensional feature spaces. Randomly generated candidate detectors occupy high-dimensional subspaces, while actual samples reside on lower-dimensional manifolds within this space. This dimensional mismatch prevents most candidate detectors from maturing into functional detectors, causing algorithm failure due to insufficient mature detector counts. To address this, we propose using training-set abnormal samples as candidate detector centers. This approach not only eliminates the blindness of random generation but also yields mature detectors capable of effectively detecting surrounding anomalies, as these abnormal samples encode authentic distributional characteristics of the feature subspace. Crucially, whereas traditional NSAs utilize only normal samples during training, our method incorporates both normal and abnormal samples-aligning with established machine learning methodologies including random forests, K-nearest neighbors, and support vector machines. Thus, our algorithm effectively leverages the previously untapped potential of abnormal training samples ignored by conventional NSAs.

Regarding the hole problem, we contend that the misclassification of abnormal samples within uncovered regions as normal constitutes the primary cause of performance degradation. Since holes remain uncovered by detectors, all contained samples-including abnormal instances-are misclassified as normal. To mitigate this, we reclassify samples in uncovered regions based on the class labels of their nearest neighbors. This approach addresses the fundamental nature of hole formation: holes predominantly emerge in boundary regions between normal and abnormal classes, with severity exacerbating where these classes intersect or overlap. KNN leverages local similarity principles to generate naturally adaptive decision boundaries in class-overlap regions, circumventing the geometric constraints that cause structural deviations in linear models on complex frontiers; as established in machine learning literature, “the local decision characteristics of KNN enable exceptional performance in class overlap regions, particularly under highly nonlinear decision boundaries45,” with the method intrinsically deriving classification decisions from neighboring sample distributions rather than predetermined geometric separators-yielding superior robustness in high-ambiguity regions where traditional boundary-dependent classifiers exhibit significant performance degradation.Fig. 4Proposed Algorithm Flowchart.

Proposed Algorithm Flowchart.

The algorithm flowchart is depicted in Figure 4. Our approach comprises two sequential stages: NSA classification and KNN classification. During the NSA stage, we utilize abnormal training samples as candidate detector centers for generating mature detectors; candidates not covered by existing mature detectors or normal samples ultimately mature into detectors. These detectors collectively form the detector set for test evaluation: samples uncovered by any detector are labeled normal, while covered samples are classified as abnormal. Since holes remain uncovered by detectors, all contained samples-including anomalies-are misclassified as normal. To mitigate this, we introduce KNN reclassification for NSA-labeled normal samples. Our algorithm employs abnormal training samples as candidate detector centers for generating mature detectors through a dual-tolerance process: First, candidate detectors undergo tolerance with existing mature detectors, where any candidate covered by mature detectors is eliminated to prevent coverage redundancy; second, remaining candidates undergo tolerance with normal samples, eliminating those matching normal patterns to avoid false positives. Experimental evaluation

To validate the effectiveness of our proposed method, we conducted experiments on the NSL-KDD38 and UNSW-NB1539,40 datasets.

Evaluation metrics

The six evaluation metrics used in the experiment are: detection rate (DR), false alarm rate (FAR), accuracy, F1-score, number of mature detectors (ND), detector training time, and detection time.

Detection Rate (DR), also known as True Positive Rate (TPR) or Recall, evaluates intrusion detection system (IDS) performance by measuring the proportion of correctly identified attacks among all actual attacks. Formally, it is defined as the ratio of true positives (correctly detected attacks) to the total number of actual attacks.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D R=\frac{T P}{T P+F N} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The False Positive Rate (FPR), or probability of false alarm, quantifies an intrusion detection system’s (IDS) tendency to misclassify normal activities as attacks. Formally, FPR is defined as the ratio of false positives (normal activities incorrectly flagged as attacks) to the total number of normal activities (i.e., false positives plus true negatives).11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F A R=\frac{F P}{F P+T N} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The accuracy of an Intrusion Detection System (IDS) measures its overall correctness in distinguishing between attacks and normal activities. It is defined as the ratio of correct predictions-the sum of true positives (correctly flagged attacks) and true negatives (correctly identified normal activities)–to the total number of predictions made, which includes true positives, true negatives, false positives (normal activities incorrectly flagged as attacks), and false negatives (attacks missed).12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text{ Accuracy } =\frac{T N+T P}{T P+F N+F P+T N} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The F1-score is a performance metric used to evaluate classification models, particularly in binary classification tasks. It represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a single balanced measure of both metrics. The formula for calculating the F1-score is:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F1=\frac{2*precision*recall}{precision + recall} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where precision is calculated using the following formula:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text{ precision } =\frac{T P}{T P+F P} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Table 2 in Section 3.2.2 defines the terms TP, TN, FN, and FP as used in Equations (10)-(14).

Conclusion

Traditional NSAs exclusively utilize normal samples for training, omitting anomaly samples. In this study, we leverage these previously unused anomaly samples as candidate detector centers to train detectors, mitigating high-dimensional detector generation challenges. Unlike traditional NSAs that employ blind random generation – which struggles to produce effective detectors in low-dimensional feature subspaces where samples reside – our approach uses training-set anomaly samples as detector centers. These provide authentic feature-space distribution information, enabling targeted detector generation in sample-dense subspaces. Critically, generated detectors effectively identify surrounding anomalies. In traditional NSAs, uncovered samples within sparse regions (“holes”) are classified as normal, leading to anomaly misclassification. To overcome this limitation, we assign classification based on the nearest neighbor’s category for hole-resident samples, reducing false normalization of anomalies. Comparative evaluation against eight baselines on benchmark intrusion detection datasets NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15 validates our algorithm’s superiority.
